Facial phenotypes in Alzheimer’s disease: from neurobiology to artificial intelligence
Authors: Wenshan Sun, Rong Xing, Jian Zhou, Yong Li, Gelin Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFacial analysis is increasingly being explored as a source of scalable behavioral signals relevant to Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and AD-related cognitive impairment. In this narrative review, informed by a structured literature search, we summarize current evidence on the biological and behavioral basis of facial alterations in AD, with particular emphasis on affective expressivity, neuropsychiatric manifestations, and dynamic facial behavior. We also review representative artificial intelligence-based facial analysis methods, including commonly used datasets, feature representations, and modeling strategies, ranging from facial landmarks and texture descriptors to spatiotemporal video models, multimodal fusion, and language-enhanced frameworks. Current evidence remains limited by small and largely single-center cohorts, heterogeneity in acquisition settings and outcome definitions, inadequate control of confounding factors, limited external validation, poor calibration reporting, and persistent concerns regarding interpretability and clinical specificity. Within the evolving biomarker-based diagnostic framework of AD, facial analysis is better viewed as a candidate, non-specific, and context-dependent tool for auxiliary risk stratification, triage support, and longitudinal monitoring rather than as stand-alone diagnostic tests. Future progress will depend on standardized data acquisition, integration with clinical and biomarker data, improved explainability, and prospective real-world validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13195-026-02129-x.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, Facial phenotype, Neuropsychiatric symptoms, Artificial intelligence, Affective computing

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia and represents an escalating public health and socioeconomic challenge. With global population aging, the number of people living with dementia is projected to increase markedly by 2050, and AD-related costs are expected to impose a substantial long-term economic burden [1, 2]. Despite advances in biomarker-based diagnosis, conventional dementia assessment still relies on specialist clinical evaluation, cognitive testing, laboratory or biomarker assays, and neuroimaging when clinically indicated. These procedures can create financial and logistical barriers, particularly in community and resource-limited settings. As a result, a large proportion of dementia cases remain undiagnosed worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [3]. These challenges highlight the need for accessible, low-burden, and non-invasive digital behavioral tools that may complement existing clinical and biomarker-based pathways, particularly for risk stratification, triage, and longitudinal monitoring in real-world settings.

The identification of amyloid-β (Aβ), tau, and neurodegeneration-related biomarkers has reshaped the diagnostic framework of AD and shifted the field toward biologically defined diagnosis and staging [4–7]. More recently, blood-based biomarkers (BBMs) have made biomarker-informed clinical pathways increasingly feasible, particularly in symptomatic individuals [8–10]. However, BBMs still require careful clinical interpretation and should not be viewed as stand-alone diagnostic or population-wide screening tools [7, 9–11]. At the same time, the biomarker era has drawn attention to the interval between biological disease detection and clinically observable impairment: AD-related pathological changes may be detectable before overt cognitive symptoms, whereas conventional symptom-based assessment may miss subtle behavioral, affective, or neuropsychiatric changes [4, 12, 13]. This has motivated interest in objective, low-burden digital behavioral signals that can capture real-world functional changes relevant to AD [14, 15].

Facial analysis may capture one candidate class of digital behavioral signals, as facial behavior can reflect affective expressivity, neuropsychiatric manifestations, social communication, and dynamic response patterns in low-burden assessment settings [16–18]. Emerging studies suggest that alterations in emotional responsiveness, facial dynamics, and task- or conversation-related behavior may accompany cognitive and behavioral changes along the AD clinical continuum [19–21]. With support from artificial intelligence (AI) and affective computing, facial analysis may serve as a complementary behavioral phenotyping tool within clinical and biomarker-informed pathways. A realistic auxiliary workflow may involve home-based or primary-care-adjacent behavioral observation, followed by BBM-supported triage or referral decisions in appropriate symptomatic individuals, and specialist evaluation with positron emission tomography (PET) or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) testing when clinically indicated.

In this review, we examine the biological and behavioral mechanisms that may underlie facial alterations in AD, together with the emerging role of AI-based facial analysis methods in capturing and quantifying these changes. We discuss representative datasets, facial feature representations, and modeling strategies, while critically evaluating current limitations related to sample size, cohort heterogeneity, recording variability, confounding, limited external validation, poor calibration reporting, interpretability, and clinical specificity. We also outline future directions, including multimodal integration, improved temporal and spatial explainability, prospective real-world validation, and more standardized analytical frameworks. Rather than presenting facial analysis as an independent diagnostic test, we evaluate facial phenotypes as candidate, non-specific, and context-dependent digital behavioral signals with potential roles in auxiliary risk stratification, triage support, symptom monitoring, and longitudinal follow-up.

Literature search strategy and scope of the review

This review was informed by a structured literature search of PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, IEEE Xplore, and Google Scholar from database inception to 31 May 2026. Searches combined terms related to AD, dementia, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), cognitive impairment, facial analysis, facial expression, facial behavior, facial dynamics, computer vision, artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, affective computing, and multimodal analysis. For AI- and algorithm-focused studies, we primarily considered publications from the past 10 years, while earlier studies were retained when they provided important historical, clinical, or methodological context. We primarily included English-language, peer-reviewed original studies examining facial, affective, or visually derived behavioral features in AD, and related cognitive impairment. Selected methodological papers, benchmark datasets, and adjacent-domain studies were also included when they provided foundational context for facial feature modeling, affective computing, or digital behavioral phenotyping. Given the substantial heterogeneity in study populations, acquisition paradigms, outcome definitions, analytical methods, and validation quality, we undertook a narrative synthesis rather than a quantitative meta-analysis. Details of the search strategy are provided in Supplementary Table S1, while representative studies and a descriptive evidence summary of AI/machine learning (ML) validation characteristics are provided in Table 1 and Supplementary Table S2.

Biological and behavioral rationale for facial alterations in AD

The human face is a complex and information-rich structure that not only conveys individual identity and biological traits, but also reflects emotional states, health status, and dynamic behavioral cues. In clinical research, facial phenotypes have been used to support the diagnosis of rare genetic syndromes, cardiovascular conditions, neurodegenerative diseases, and psychiatric conditions [22–25]. In AD, facial alterations are likely to reflect the combined effects of neurobiological change, behavioral expression, and neuropsychiatric symptoms [16, 26, 27]. The following sections summarize the biological and behavioral processes that may underlie facial alterations in AD.

From pathology to phenotype

AD pathology is increasingly recognized as extending beyond cognition alone [28, 29]. Although memory impairment remains the most familiar clinical manifestation, AD can also be associated with a broader range of non-cognitive changes, including apathy, affective dysregulation, altered social behavior, and other neuropsychiatric symptoms [29, 30]. These manifestations may emerge before overt dementia and should not be regarded solely as secondary consequences of cognitive decline [31]. Mild behavioral impairment (MBI) provides a useful framework for conceptualizing later-life emergent and persistent neuropsychiatric symptoms that represent a clear change from longstanding patterns and may capture a neurobehavioral axis of predementia disease expression [32]. Beyond its prognostic value for cognitive decline and incident dementia, MBI has been linked to core AD-related biological changes, including cerebrospinal fluid amyloid and tau abnormalities, amyloid and tau PET findings, neurodegeneration, and progression to neuropathologically confirmed AD [28, 33–37]. Emerging evidence further suggests that similar associations may also be detectable with blood-based biomarkers, including plasma phosphorylated tau [38, 39]. Collectively, these findings support the biological relevance of MBI and suggest that it may reflect a meaningful neurobehavioral dimension of AD-related disease expression rather than merely a secondary consequence of cognitive decline.

These behavioral changes may also have externally observable correlates, one of which is the face. Within this pathology-to-phenotype framework, facial alterations in AD may be understood as computable phenotypic readouts that reflect the transition from underlying pathology to observable behavioral change [16, 26]. Such changes may involve both dynamic facial behavior, which may more directly reflect behavioral and neuropsychiatric dysfunction, and more static facial traits, whose interpretation is more complex and subject to substantial confounding [17, 40, 41].

Dynamic facial expressivity

Dynamic facial expressivity may be particularly relevant to AD because alterations in nonverbal communication have been linked not only to cognitive decline, but also to neuropsychiatric symptoms, especially apathy [42, 43]. Early clinical work showed that facial expression in AD is influenced by both cognitive deficits and neuropsychiatric burden, with apathetic symptoms being particularly associated with reduced facial expressiveness [16].

In AD, altered facial expressivity is likely to arise from multiple converging mechanisms that disrupt the generation and regulation of emotion [44]. Degeneration within limbic and frontal systems may impair the initiation and modulation of affective signals [26, 27]. In parallel, dysfunction of catecholaminergic pathways, particularly norepinephrine and dopamine systems, may reduce emotional arousal and response intensity, contributing to hypomimia-like or emotionally blunted facial behavior [17]. At the network level, AD-related changes may affect both automatic and socially mediated components of facial expression, including circuits involved in salience detection, motivation, emotion regulation, and facial motor control [26, 27]. Upstream processes such as Aβ and tau accumulation, synaptic dysfunction, and neuroinflammatory change may further destabilize these networks and increase vulnerability to neuropsychiatric symptoms, which can manifest downstream as altered facial expressivity [45, 46].

Apathy is one of the earliest and most extensively studied neuropsychiatric symptoms in AD, frequently preceding the clinical onset of dementia [47].Neurocircuitry-based models implicate dysfunction within frontal-subcortical networks involving the anterior cingulate cortex, orbitofrontal cortex, basal ganglia, and thalamus, supporting the view that motivational and behavioral symptoms can arise from network-level pathology rather than cognition alone [27, 47]. In parallel, multimodal imaging evidence suggests that tau-related changes may contribute to increased mild behavioral impairment through disruption of the salience network, which plays a central role in emotion detection, motivation, and affective salience [48]. These findings support a mechanistic link among AD pathology, neuropsychiatric symptoms, and altered facial expressivity.

The clinical evaluation of neuropsychiatric symptoms still relies largely on subjective rating scales, which are influenced by examiner experience, patient cooperation, and recall bias. By contrast, emotion- and motivation-related brain networks influence facial musculature through motor pathways, suggesting that neuropsychiatric abnormalities may leave measurable traces in facial-emotional-motor output. Dynamic facial changes may therefore provide an objective and potentially scalable readout of cognitive-affective dysfunction, particularly in real-world or low-burden settings such as smartphone- or webcam-based assessment. At the same time, such signals should not be interpreted as disease-specific in isolation, but rather as biologically relevant phenotypic readouts that may complement broader clinical and biomarker-based evaluation.

Static facial traits

Compared with dynamic facial behavior, more static facial traits have a less direct and less specific relationship with AD-related disease expression. Features such as perceived facial aging, skin texture, and facial asymmetry have been explored as potential correlates of cognitive impairment and dementia risk [40, 41, 49]. Some studies suggest that individuals with cognitive impairment or AD may appear older or show greater facial asymmetry than cognitively healthy peers [40, 50]. These observations raise the possibility that static facial characteristics may capture aspects of broader biological aging, frailty, or long-term system integrity rather than AD pathology in any specific sense.

The interpretation of static facial traits remains challenging. Facial aging and structural asymmetry are shaped by multiple non-neurological influences, including chronological aging, ultraviolet exposure, smoking, dentition, body composition, habitual expression patterns, sleep posture, and socioeconomic or environmental factors. In addition, structural facial features may reflect indirect developmental, musculoskeletal, or vascular influences that are difficult to disentangle from neurodegenerative processes [51]. For this reason, static facial traits are best regarded as low-specificity phenotypic correlates rather than disease-defining markers. Their potential value may lie in multimodal models, where they are interpreted alongside more informative dynamic facial, cognitive, behavioral, and biomarker-based measures rather than in isolation.

Differential diagnosis and confounding

A major challenge in interpreting facial abnormalities in AD is their limited disease specificity. Reduced facial expressivity, emotional blunting, psychomotor slowing, and diminished social engagement are not unique to AD, but may also occur in other neurodegenerative conditions, including Parkinsonian syndromes and dementia with Lewy bodies, as well as in primary psychiatric disorders such as depression and social withdrawal [17, 25, 52]. This overlap limits the value of facial phenotype as a stand-alone marker for etiological diagnosis. In Parkinson’s disease (PD), automated video-based analysis has shown that hypomimia is frequent, detectable with good discrimination from healthy controls, and associated with mouth and jaw dysfunction, reduced upper-face variability, nigro-putaminal dopaminergic loss, and bradykinesia/rigidity [53]. Direct comparison studies further suggest overlap in reduced facial emotion expressivity between PD and AD [17]. Preliminary work has even suggested that quantified facial dynamics such as expressiveness, reaction speed, and asymmetry may differ between simulated AD-like and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB)-like conditions, although these findings remain proof-of-concept and require validation in real patient cohorts [54]. In parallel, affective computing studies in depression indicate that facial dynamics can also encode clinically meaningful information in primary psychiatric illness [25]. More broadly, facial signals may be shaped by multiple non-specific influences, including aging, frailty, medication effects, sleep disturbance, pain, fatigue, psychiatric comorbidity, recording conditions, and social context. These factors can alter facial behavior independently of AD pathology and therefore complicate both differential diagnosis and model generalizability.

Nevertheless, limited specificity does not eliminate clinical value. Rather than serving as a stand-alone diagnostic marker, facial analysis may be more useful as an adjunctive behavioral signal that helps identify individuals who may warrant closer cognitive, neuropsychiatric, motor, or biomarker-based evaluation. Thus, the most realistic role of facial phenotype at present lies not in definitive differential diagnosis in isolation, but in its interpretation within a broader clinical framework, alongside syndrome-level features and biomarker evidence. Looking ahead, larger datasets, more standardized task paradigms, and increasingly sensitive deep learning methods for modelling subtle spatiotemporal facial dynamics may help reveal more clinically informative patterns across diagnostic groups.

AI-based Facial Analysis and Digital Behavioral Phenotyping

AI shows early promise for discovering and quantifying facial phenotypes and digital behavioral signals relevant to AD [52]. Advances in ML have enabled automated extraction of static facial landmarks and dynamic facial movements from video data, allowing subtle expression changes to be quantified beyond manual observation [55]. Recent studies have incorporated complementary modalities such as speech, language, eye tracking, and physiological signals, within multimodal learning architectures to enhance the robustness of facial analysis [56]. However, challenges remain, including dataset heterogeneity, confounding factors, limited standardization, poor interpretability, and insufficient prospective validation.

Datasets for facial analysis

Affective computing datasets for expression modeling

Affective computing datasets have served as foundational benchmarks for facial expression analysis by supporting the development of facial expression recognition and Action Unit (AU) detection methods [57]. Widely used datasets such as CK+ provide image sequences annotated with both basic emotions and AUs, whereas more recent resources annotated using the Facial Action Coding System, such as EmotioNet and GFT, have expanded annotation to more diverse and less constrained visual settings [58, 59]. These datasets are valuable for methodological development and benchmarking, but their relevance to AD research remains largely indirect because they generally lack older cognitively impaired participants and dementia-specific clinical characterization.

Micro-expression datasets, including CASME II, SMIC, SAMM, and CAS(ME)², further provide high temporal resolution for capturing subtle and short-duration facial movements [60]. These datasets are useful for modeling fine-grained facial dynamics that could be relevant to affective change, but they are typically limited to small samples of young, healthy participants under laboratory conditions, which restricts both their ecological validity and their direct clinical relevance to AD research.

Clinical and dementia-specific cohorts

Clinical cohort datasets directly target the population of interest and therefore offer high ecological and strong clinical anchoring. Representative examples include the I-CONECT, PROMPT, and CANARY projects, which collect multimodal information, such as facial video, speech, language and eye-tracking, from older adults with MCI or dementia through semi-structured interviews and clinical conversations [61–64]. These datasets are typically aligned with standardized diagnostic procedures and cognitive assessments, enabling supervised learning models grounded in clinical outcomes. Although they are often limited by relatively small sample sizes, single-center designs, and restricted demographic diversity, CIR-AV provides a recent example of larger-scale, community-oriented audio-visual data collection [65]. Nevertheless, these clinical and community-based datasets establish a critical foundation for developing clinically meaningful facial phenotyping tools and digital behavioral signals. Future progress will depend on standardized acquisition and annotation protocols, expanded multicenter collaboration, and the implementation of responsible data-sharing frameworks.

Task-oriented and interactive data collection paradigms

Task-oriented and interactive data collection paradigms represent an important direction in facial analysis for cognitive impairment. By employing standardized scenarios to elicit emotional and cognitive responses, these paradigms may enhance group differentiation and facilitate synchronized multimodal data capture. Representative designs can be categorized into: interactive platforms, such as conversational agents, chatbots, virtual assistants, and cognitive games [19, 21, 64, 66–68], observational protocols, including clinical interview videos and standardized portrait imaging [69, 70], and task-specific experiments, such as emotion elicitation, visuospatial memory tests, virtual reality (VR)-based cognitive tasks, and high-load dual-task paradigms [20, 71–78]. While these designs can elicit context-rich and task-relevant facial behavior, real-world factors such as task familiarity, subjective stimulus interpretation, fatigue, recording duration, and environmental variability may introduce noise and reduce within-subject consistency. Additional sources, including sign language corpora and observational recordings from day-care settings, further enrich dataset diversity but also increase heterogeneity across acquisition contexts [18, 79–81].

In summary, facial analysis research in AD is increasingly being explored but remains limited by data scale, demographic diversity, annotation depth, and longitudinal coverage, which constrain model generalizability and clinical translation. Future progress will require large-scale, multicenter datasets with standardized acquisition, rich annotations, and ethically governed data sharing.

Facial feature representation

Methods for modeling facial features in AD have evolved substantially, transitioning from handcrafted static descriptors to deep learning-based representations that capture spatiotemporal dynamics and affective processes. This progression reflects a conceptual shift toward identifying facial phenotypes that more closely align with real-world behavior, neuropsychiatric symptoms, and clinically meaningful disease processes.

Static and Spatiotemporal Features

Facial feature representation in AD and related cognitive impairment can be broadly understood at two closely related levels: static features, which describe how the face appears at a given moment, and spatiotemporal features, which describe how facial behavior changes over time.

Early studies mainly relied on static features derived from individual images or frame-level observations. These included geometric descriptors, such as facial landmarks, facial mesh coordinates, and spatial relationships between key facial regions, as well as appearance-based descriptors such as facial texture and complexion. Commonly used tools, including Dlib, MediaPipe, and OpenFace, enabled the extraction of landmarks, facial mesh, head pose, and AU-related measures [20, 64, 67, 81–83]. These handcrafted or semi-structured facial feature representations are relatively interpretable and computationally efficient, making them practical for small datasets and controlled settings. Later work increasingly used learned image-based representations to capture richer spatial patterns beyond manually defined descriptors [66, 69, 76, 84, 85]. However, the reliability of static representations is often affected by pose variation, occlusion, and illumination. More fundamentally, static features provide limited information about disease specificity, because changes in facial appearance or baseline expressivity may reflect broader effects of aging or general cognitive decline rather than AD itself.

To address these limitations, more recent work has focused on spatiotemporal representations, which characterize how facial behavior unfolds across video sequences. Early work quantified dynamic texture variation across frames, but these methods were often high-dimensional and sensitive to noise [21]. Subsequent approaches combined spatial and temporal information more explicitly, allowing facial appearance, landmark topology, and motion to be represented jointly over time [65, 70, 78, 86, 87]. Recent spatiotemporal representations focus on extracting fine-grained behavioral tokens to describe complex fluctuations and long-range temporal dependencies [63]. Some studies have also introduced mechanisms to emphasize relatively informative frames or facial regions, such as identifying “attention-rich” behavioral segments in clinical interview videos [21, 87]. However, these spatiotemporal models generally require longer recordings, more stable video quality, and more complex preprocessing. Despite their promise, spatiotemporal methods remain sensitive to recording quality, sequence length, and sample size. Their clinical translation will depend on balancing temporal modeling fidelity with computational efficiency, interpretability, and robustness across real-world settings.

Affective and expression-based features

Emotional expressivity is often altered in AD and MCI, making affective facial phenotyping a potentially informative approach for digital assessment. These representations are typically derived from facial AUs, basic expression categories, smile-related indices, and summary measures of emotional response over time, such as expression intensity, duration, response latency, variability, and valence–arousal patterns [18, 19, 68, 71, 73, 74, 76, 84, 85]. Together, these features provide higher-level representations of emotional responsiveness and expressive behavior. Existing evidence suggests that individuals with cognitive impairment often show attenuated or atypical emotional responses compared with cognitively healthy peers [31, 43, 88]. Recent work has enabled more automated extraction of such affective features from naturalistic facial recordings. For example, models using conversational videos and temporally summarized expression features have achieved promising discrimination of MCI/dementia while retaining interpretability [84]. Moreover, combining facial features with biosignals and/or self-reports may improve sensitivity to subtle or mixed affective states frequently observed in AD [72].

However, these affective facial features are subject to several limitations. They are strongly influenced by task context, social setting, recording quality, as well as inter-individual differences in personality and cultural display rules. Nevertheless, these representations still provide informative high-level features and offer an important basis for symptom-oriented digital phenotyping, such as apathy detection.

Facial features of apathy and related neuropsychiatric symptoms

Apathy is common in early AD and is clinically relevant for prognosis and care planning, yet routine assessment still relies largely on subjective ratings. Recent work suggests that reduced expressivity and altered facial motion patterns may support more objective and scalable phenotyping of apathy-related symptoms [42, 89, 90]. Consistent with the mechanistic links discussed above, apathy-related motivational dysfunction may manifest as emotional blunting and diminished facial reactivity, making facial dynamics a plausible readout for symptom quantification. Face-based models, ranging from pretrained CNN features to multitask learning frameworks, have shown promising results for apathy-related classification, particularly when combined with clinical measures [42, 91]. Multimodal models integrating facial cues with audio, behavioral, or other affect-related signals may further improve performance while supporting more interpretable phenotyping; explainability analyses such as SHAP have highlighted reduced facial actions as candidate apathy markers [20, 89, 90, 92].

Despite this progress, apathy-oriented facial modeling remains challenged by symptom overlap, including co-occurring depression and anxiety, as well as by confounding factors such as medication effects and motor slowing, together with the limited availability of well-annotated clinical datasets. These limitations underscore the need for cohort-tailored tasks, standardized endpoints, and robust external validation. They also reinforce that such facial markers are better understood as clinically informative but non-specific phenotypic signals rather than stand-alone indicators.

Classical machine learning models

In the computational analysis of facial features for AD, classical machine learning represents a foundational feature-driven methodology based on a two-step process. First, predefined features are extracted from facial recordings and converted into structured numerical variables, such as AU intensities, 2D facial landmarks, emotion probabilities, continuous affective measures, and simple kinematic markers including blink rates [21, 64, 67, 73–76, 83, 84]. These variables are then analyzed using relatively conventional classifiers, most commonly support vector machines (SVM), k-nearest neighbors (k-NN), logistic regression, and ensemble methods such as random forests or gradient boosting.

This feature-based approach has been particularly attractive in early-stage exploratory studies, many of which rely on small clinical cohorts, often with fewer than 100 participants and in some cases fewer than 30 [21, 67, 73, 74, 83]. In such data-limited settings, classical machine learning is often more practical than deep neural networks because it requires fewer parameters and can operate on explicitly defined inputs. An additional advantage is interpretability: because input variables are predefined, these models can help identify which features, such as delayed response behavior or reduced emotional valence during standardized tasks, contribute most strongly to classification [74, 83].

At the same time, the current evidence base for classical machine learning has important limitations. Most studies focus on broad binary screening tasks, and reported performance varies substantially depending on cohort composition, task design, and validation methodology. Although some studies report apparently strong discrimination in controlled settings, these estimates are derived almost entirely from internal validation procedures, such as leave-one-subject-out or k-fold cross-validation [21, 83, 84]. Because most cohorts remain single-center and relatively homogeneous, concerns persist that models may partially rely on site-specific artifacts or narrowly defined task conditions rather than disease-relevant facial signals [64]. In addition, performance tends to decline in more clinically nuanced settings, such as multi-group classification or differentiation from other neurodegenerative conditions [21, 67, 75]. Overall, classical machine learning remains useful where feasibility, transparency, and structured feature sets are priorities, but its performance is constrained by the representational limits of manually selected features.

Deep learning models

Deep learning models aim to learn relevant facial representations directly from raw images or video sequences, rather than relying on manually predefined features. In principle, this may help capture subtle and spatially distributed patterns that are difficult to encode using handcrafted descriptors. At the same time, deep learning-based facial models typically require sufficiently large, diverse, and well-annotated datasets, and their performance depends heavily on careful validation and regularization to reduce overfitting and domain shift.

Early deep learning studies in this area mainly focused on static spatial modeling using facial portraits collected under controlled conditions. For instance, established architectures like Xception and ResNet have been optimized to assess facial complexion and regional features (e.g., the lower face), demonstrating that global visual cues can effectively distinguish patients with dementia from cognitively sound controls [69, 72, 85]. Later work introduced more complex spatiotemporal architectures, including multi-stream, recurrent, Transformer-style, and graph-based models, to better capture temporal changes in facial behavior and emotional response [63, 70, 72, 78, 86, 93]. Although such models have reported promising results, especially in controlled datasets, their interpretation remains highly dependent on cohort size, task design, and validation methodology.

Despite these advances, the current evidence base for deep learning remains constrained by the scale and structure of available clinical datasets. As model complexity increases, concerns about overfitting, instability, and reduced generalizability become more pronounced. This is reflected in the contrast between stronger performance in some small, controlled datasets and more modest results obtained in naturalistic conversational settings, such as studies based on the I-CONECT cohort [63, 86, 87]. In addition, deep learning models remain challenging to interpret clinically [94]. Visualization methods, including heatmaps and attention maps, may indicate where the model is focusing, but they do not fully explain why the highlighted patterns are clinically meaningful. For spatiotemporal facial analysis, interpretability should therefore extend beyond spatial localization to temporal and task-context explanations. Clinically meaningful signals may emerge during specific interview phases, emotional elicitation periods, response latency intervals, or recovery dynamics after a stimulus. Future models should report not only which facial regions, action units, or visual tokens contributed to prediction, but also when these signals appeared and under what task context. Such temporal explanations may help link model outputs to clinically interpretable constructs, such as emotional reactivity, apathy, psychomotor slowing, delayed response, or reduced social engagement. Privacy and compliance considerations also remain important when models rely directly on raw facial images, which has encouraged some studies to use more abstract inputs, such as anonymized facial mesh coordinate sequences, for subsequent modeling [82]. Overall, deep learning has substantially expanded the representational scope of facial analysis, but its clinical application still depends on stronger evidence for robustness, interpretability, calibration, and external validity.

Multimodal and language-enhanced frameworks

Recent work has increasingly combined facial analysis with complementary modalities, such as speech acoustics, eye-tracking, self-reported measures, and neuroimaging, in an effort to improve detection of AD and related cognitive impairment [19–21, 65, 68, 71, 72, 79, 81, 85, 86]. Multimodal fusion may occur at the feature level (early fusion), by combining learned representations directly, or at the decision level (late fusion), by integrating modality-specific predictions. Both strategies aim to leverage complementary information and reduce the limitations of any single modality.

Representative studies suggest that multimodal fusion can improve performance relative to face-only models in some settings. For example, remote or web-based platforms that combine facial motion, speech, and self-reported information have identified facial measures such as lip aperture, mouth surface area, and eye dynamics as informative features for distinguishing MCI from healthy controls [66]. Other multimodal frameworks have reported performance gains over unimodal baselines, including models that integrate facial video dynamics with eye-tracking and approaches based on disentangled fusion of facial video and speech [65, 85]. However, the magnitude and robustness of these gains appear sensitive to cohort composition, acquisition conditions, task design, and validation strategy. Results from controlled or same-source settings do not necessarily generalize to larger community-based cohorts or less standardized remote assessments, where performance is often more modest.

More recently, large language models (LLMs) have been explored as a possible way to improve the integration and interpretability of heterogeneous dementia-related data. In this context, LLMs may serve less as stand-alone diagnostic tools than as semantic bridges that map low-level multimodal outputs, such as facial motion patterns, AU dynamics, speech features, and behavioral cues, onto higher-level clinical constructs, including emotional blunting or apathy-related behavior [56, 95]. This remains an emerging direction rather than an established solution, and evidence for consistent performance gains across heterogeneous dementia cohorts and real-world settings is still limited. Real-world deployment will also require careful attention to reproducibility, bias, privacy, and governance, particularly given the sensitivity of facial and conversational data.

Validation terminology, leakage risk, and interpretation of model performance

Although many facial AI studies describe their tasks as AD, MCI, or cognitive impairment “detection”, this term should be interpreted as dataset-specific algorithmic classification rather than clinical or etiological diagnosis. Models may distinguish predefined groups under specific sampling, task, and acquisition conditions, but such performance does not establish AD pathology or exclude alternative explanations such as depression, parkinsonian syndromes, frailty, medication effects, sensory impairment, or recording bias. Validation terminology also requires caution: same-cohort cross-validation or hold-out testing should be considered internal validation, and facial-video studies may be vulnerable to leakage if frames, clips, or segments from the same participant cross training and test sets. More rigorous designs use participant-level splitting, such as participant-level k-fold CV, LOSO-CV, or subject-level hold-out testing, but even these do not establish clinical generalizability without independent validation across sites, devices, languages, cultures, clinical settings, and disease spectra. Across the reviewed literature, most studies relied on internal validation, while calibration, subgroup/fairness analysis, and domain-shift evaluation were rarely reported; detailed validation characteristics are summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

Performance metrics in Table 1 should therefore be interpreted as measures of dataset-specific discrimination rather than diagnostic readiness. AUC summarizes threshold-independent discrimination but does not assess calibration, clinical benefit, or decision consequences. Accuracy may be misleading in imbalanced datasets, whereas precision, recall, and F1 score are influenced by class prevalence and decision thresholds. sensitivity and specificity are particularly relevant to screening and triage contexts, but their clinical value still depends on the intended use, target population, and downstream assessment pathway. model performance should therefore be interpreted alongside class imbalance, calibration, external validation, and clinical utility.

Current challenges and future enablers

Despite growing interest in facial phenotype analysis for AD-related cognitive impairment, clinical translation remains limited by several methodological challenges. First, most published studies are based on relatively small, single-center, and highly selected cohorts, which restrict generalizability and increase the risk that models capture cohort-specific rather than disease-relevant patterns. Second, substantial heterogeneity exists in diagnostic anchoring, outcome definitions, and acquisition paradigms. Studies variably rely on DSM-based diagnoses, NIA-AA criteria, Petersen criteria, screening-based grouping, or incompletely reported clinical definitions, while data collection ranges from standardized photographs to interviews, chatbot interactions, and remote video assessments. This variability makes comparison across studies difficult and weakens reproducibility.

Clinical validity is further challenged by confounding. Facial signals may be influenced by age, sex, frailty, depression and anxiety, medication effects, motor slowing, and comorbid neurological or psychiatric conditions, as well as by technical factors such as lighting, camera placement, recording quality, and task familiarity. In many studies, confounder handling remains limited or incompletely reported. Another major limitation is the heavy reliance on internal validation. Most studies use cross-validation or within-cohort train-test splits, whereas true external validation across independent sites, devices, and populations is rare. In addition, calibration is seldom reported, even though it is important for clinically interpretable risk estimation and deployment.

Several developments may help address these limitations. Standardized datasets, harmonized acquisition protocols, and transparent annotation procedures will be essential for improving comparability and supporting reproducible validation. Larger and more diverse multicenter cohorts, ideally with stronger diagnostic anchoring, biomarker-informed characterization, and longitudinal follow-up, are needed to assess both group-level discrimination and within-person change. Future studies should prioritize participant-level external validation, leakage-control procedures, calibration, subgroup/fairness analysis, and evaluation across sites, devices, languages, cultures, and clinical settings. Self-supervised and foundation-model strategies may reduce dependence on fully annotated datasets, while multimodal frameworks integrating facial dynamics with speech, gaze, movement, physiological signals, and clinical or biomarker information may yield more robust representations. Future work should also advance explainable AI approaches that address spatial, temporal, and task-context interpretability, while adopting privacy-preserving strategies to protect sensitive facial and behavioral data and support trustworthy real-world implementation.

Conclusion

Facial analysis, largely enabled by recent advances in artificial intelligence and computer vision, represents a promising source of digital behavioral signals that may complement existing clinical and biomarker-informed assessment pathways across the AD continuum. Current evidence suggests that alterations in facial expressivity, facial dynamics, and affect-related responses may capture clinically relevant behavioral and neuropsychiatric changes associated with cognitive impairment. However, the field remains at an early stage, and clinical translation is still limited by heterogeneous datasets, variability in real-world acquisition conditions, insufficient control of confounding factors, limited external validation, and persistent challenges related to interpretability, calibration, and disease specificity. Accordingly, facial analysis is best viewed not as a stand-alone diagnostic tool, but as a potentially useful adjunct for prescreening, triage support, risk stratification, and longitudinal monitoring within broader clinical and biomarker-informed pathways. Future progress will depend on standardized acquisition and evaluation protocols, larger prospective and multicenter cohorts, privacy-preserving data governance, improved explainability, and multimodal frameworks that integrate facial phenotypes with complementary behavioral, physiological, clinical, and biomarker information. By outlining these priorities, this review highlights both the promise and the current limitations of AI-based facial phenotyping in AD, as well as the steps needed for more rigorous, reproducible, and clinically grounded development.
