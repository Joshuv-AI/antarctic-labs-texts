Mediating roles of social connection and stress-coping self-efficacy in the association between physical activity and emotional distress among lung cancer survivors: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jinhuan Yang, Rui Bao, Zhonglin Chen, Chenyan Han, Yi Zhao, Xiaomin Niu, Danni Dong, Yaping He, Qiao Chu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAlthough previous studies have examined the psychological benefits of physical activity in cancer survivors, the underlying psychological mechanisms among lung cancer survivors remain poorly understood. This study aims to examine the parallel mediating roles of social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping in the association between physical activity and psychological distress (anxiety and depression) among Chinese lung cancer survivors.DesignA cross-sectional observational study.SettingA tertiary care hospital in Shanghai, China.ParticipantsA total of 588 lung cancer survivors were recruited and assessed physical activity, anxiety, depression, social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping. Path analysis was conducted to examine the parallel mediating effects of social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping in the association between physical activity and psychological distress (anxiety and depression).Outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the examination of the mediating effects of social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping on the relationship between physical activity and psychological distress, assessed using path analysis.ResultsSocial connection (p=0.003 for anxiety; p<0.001 for depression) and self-efficacy in stress coping (p<0.001 for anxiety; p<0.001 for depression) significantly mediated the association between physical activity and psychological distress. Physical activity was positively associated with social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping, both of which were negatively associated with anxiety and depression. After controlling for the mediators, the direct effects of physical activity on anxiety and depression were no longer significant.ConclusionThese findings suggest that social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping may serve as critical psychological mechanisms underlying the association between physical activity and emotional well-being among Chinese lung cancer survivors. Interventions targeting anxiety and depression in lung cancer survivors may benefit from integrating strategies to enhance physical activity while promoting social connection and self-efficacy in coping to optimise psychological outcomes.

Introduction

According to the 2022 Global Cancer Report, lung cancer has the highest incidence and mortality rates in China.1 The prevalence of distress among Chinese lung cancer survivors has been reported to range from 10.1% to 61.29%.2 3 Although extensive research has highlighted the benefits of physical activity for mental health,4 5 the psychological mechanisms through which physical activity influences emotional well-being in cancer survivors remain poorly understood. Therefore, investigating these underlying psychological pathways could provide insights for designing effective physical activity interventions and supportive care strategies to improve mental health outcomes for cancer survivors. The objective of this study is to explore the parallel mediating roles of social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping in the relationship between physical activity and emotional distress (anxiety and depression) among Chinese lung cancer survivors.

Emotional benefits of physical activity in cancer survivors

A growing body of literature highlights the positive psychological outcomes associated with physical activity among cancer survivors. Extensive research has shown that physical activity offers significant emotional benefits for cancer survivors, including reductions in anxiety and depression, improvements in mood and enhanced psychological well-being.68 Cross-sectional studies have shown that higher amounts of moderate physical activity are associated with lower levels of emotional distress among cancer survivors.9 10 A cohort study demonstrated that cancer survivors who reported participating in regular physical activity experienced lower depression and higher quality of life.6 Longitudinal studies have suggested that physical activity alleviates anxiety and depression in cancer survivors. For example, interventions that aimed to promote physical activity or exercise were associated with reduced emotional distress in cancer patients.7 8 A systematic review indicated that physical activity interventions moderately alleviated both short-term and long-term depressive symptoms in cancer patients.11 While the direct benefits of physical activity on emotional distress are well-established, the psychological mechanisms underlying this relationship among lung cancer survivors remain less understood. In the following section, we explore the roles of social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping as potential mediators.

Social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping as mediators

In recent years, significant progress has been made in understanding the psychological mechanisms underlying the impact of physical activity on emotional well-being. Research on cancer survivors remains relatively limited, with most studies focusing on breast cancer survivors.1214 Most of this research, however, has concentrated on breast cancer survivors. There is a pressing need to extend such investigations to lung cancer survivors, who experience a lower survival rate,15 a poor prognosis and a higher prevalence of severe symptom burdens.16 These factors may influence the mechanisms through which physical activity affects emotional distress. Therefore, further investigation is needed to explore the potential mediating roles of social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping among lung cancer survivors. Kandola et al17 proposed a conceptual framework that identifies both biological and psychosocial mechanisms underlying the antidepressant effects of physical activity. According to this framework, engaging in physical activity helps alleviate depressive symptoms by fostering positive changes in social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping. Physical activity can enhance social networks by providing opportunities for interaction and socialisation. Conversely, a lack of social connection contributes to the pathophysiology of depression by limiting opportunities for interaction and emotional disclosure. Moreover, physical activity enhances the perception of physical ability and skill mastery, increasing self-efficacy in stress coping. By enhancing self-efficacy in stress coping, physical activity motivates survivors to pursue achievable goals or tasks, which in turn helps alleviate depressive symptoms.

Previous studies have demonstrated that a higher level of social connection, such as maintaining more frequent contact with loved ones and regularly participating in group activities, has been associated with lower levels of emotional distress in cancer survivors.18 Social connection refers to the subjective sense of belonging and relational embeddedness within one’s social network.19 It is distinct from social support, which entails the functional provision of tangible or intangible resources.20 In this study, we focused on social connection as a mediator, given its distinct role in fostering emotional well-being through engagement in physical activity, independent of active support receipt.21 22 Adequate social connection, such as going out for dinner or online chatting with friends and family, provides a secure environment for survivors to express negative feelings and can temporarily distract their attention from the distressing cancer coping experience, which in turn helps alleviate depression.23 Physical activity practice may be an important way of increasing social interactions and building long-term relationships. For instance, previous interventions with physical activity practice have found that the intervention group has reduced the perception of loneliness when compared with the control group.24 25 Engaging in physical activity provides individuals with opportunities to reconnect with old friends, expand their social networks and build meaningful relationships. These connections, in turn, can reduce feelings of isolation, enhance a sense of belonging and improve emotional well-being.26 Thus, social connection may serve as a key psychological mechanism that links physical activity to emotional well-being among cancer survivors.

According to social cognitive theory,27 self-efficacy in stress coping is defined as confidence in one’s ability to use the necessary skills to cope with stress and mobilise resources to meet situational demands. Positive psychotherapy focused on stress coping styles for cancer survivors promotes survivors’ post-traumatic growth and behaviour modification.28 Research has demonstrated that post-traumatic growth is associated with lower levels of anxiety and depression, as well as greater hope and better quality of life among cancer survivors.29 30 A cross-sectional study indicated that individuals with lower stress-coping efficacy, particularly those with dependent coping, were at a significantly higher risk of developing depression.31 Moreover, home-based exercise interventions can produce changes in the perceived ability to manage stress among cancer survivors,32 suggesting that exercise training has the potential to enhance stress management capabilities in cancer patients. A research synthesis demonstrated that the positive influence of exercise on stress was a result of distraction from negative thoughts and the improved retrieval of positive thoughts.33 Hence, we expected that self-efficacy in stress coping would mediate the association between physical activity and emotional distress among lung cancer survivors.

Present study

Building on prior findings, the current study aimed to examine the mediating effects of social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping on the relationship between physical activity and psychological distress (anxiety and depression) among lung cancer survivors. Based on Kandola’s conceptual framework and empirical evidence, we proposed a parallel mediation model. Specifically, we hypothesised that higher levels of physical activity would predict greater social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping. In turn, these factors would be associated with lower levels of anxiety and depression.

Method

Participants and procedure

The study participants were recruited from the oncology department at Shanghai Chest Hospital, Shanghai, China, between February and December 2023. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the ethics committee at Shanghai Chest Hospital (IS2173).

The inclusion criteria were: (a) diagnosed with lung cancer through cytological or histological examination; (b) age ≥18 years and (c) expected survival time of 3 months or more. The exclusion criteria included: (a) severe mental disorders or cognitive impairments (b) poor language ability with inability to understand the study materials.

On patients’ admission, the research team reviewed their medical records to screen for eligibility. Participants were enrolled using a consecutive sampling method. Patients who met the criteria were approached by the investigators and research assistants in person during their hospital stay, informed about the study and invited to participate. Those who agreed and provided written informed consent were enrolled and completed a cross-sectional survey once during their hospital stay. The questionnaire assessed demographic characteristics, clinical information and variables of major interest. As a gesture of appreciation, participants received small non-monetary gifts after completing the questionnaire.

The required sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1, based on a multiple linear regression model. Following Cohen’s (1988) guidelines,34 a small effect size (f² = 0.02) was assumed, with an alpha level of 0.05 and a power of 0.80. The analysis indicated a minimum sample size of 311 participants. The full study protocol is publicly available via the Open Science Framework (OSF): https://osf.io/jaqxp.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Measures

Physical activity

Physical activity was assessed using the Chinese version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Long Form (IPAQ-LF).35 36 The questionnaire assessed physical activity across various domains, including leisure, transport, work, domestic activities and sitting time over the past week. Participants reported the frequency (days per week) and duration (minutes per day) of walking, moderate-intensity and vigorous-intensity activity in each domain during the past 7 days. Total weekly physical activity (in minutes/week) was calculated according to the official IPAQ scoring protocol.35 In this study, we used the total minutes/week as a continuous variable representing overall physical activity. Higher scores indicate greater levels of physical activity. The IPAQ-LF has demonstrated good validity in Chinese adults.37 The internal reliability was 0.66 in this study.

Social connection

Social connection was assessed using two items that were designed to measure the extent of survivors’ engagement with family, friends, neighbours and colleagues either remotely or face-to-face: (1) ‘How often do you contact family members, friends, neighbours or colleagues via WeChat (a widely used social media and messaging application in China) or phone?’ and (2) ‘How often do you go out for a walk, shopping, dining or exercise with family members or friends?’ Responses were recorded on a 4-point scale, ranging from 1 (almost never), 2 (about once or twice a month), 3 (about once or twice a week) and 4 (about three or more times a week). Total scores were calculated for the two items, with a higher score indicating greater social engagement.

Self-efficacy in stress coping

Self-efficacy in stress coping was assessed using the self-efficacy in stress reduction dimension of the Chinese version of the Strategies Used by People to Promote Health (C-SUPPH) scale.38 39 According to a validation study among Chinese lung cancer patients, the self-efficacy in stress reduction dimension composed of five items: ‘Excluding upsetting thoughts from my consciousness’, ‘Using relaxation techniques to decrease my anxiety’, ‘Finding ways of alleviating my stress’, ‘Doing things that helped me to cope with previous emotional difficulties’ and ‘Practicing stress reduction techniques even when I’m feeling sick.’40 For each item, patients were asked how much confidence they had in doing the behaviour. Responses were given on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (very little) to 5 (quite a lot), yielding a total score ranging from 5 to 10. Higher scores reflected greater self-efficacy in stress coping. The internal reliability of the five-item scale was 0.98 in this study.

Depression

The Ten-Item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10)41 was used to assess the severity of depressive symptoms. Participants were asked to rate the frequency of experiencing the symptoms described in each item during the past week using a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (rarely or none of the time) to 3 (most or all of the time), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 30. Higher total scores indicated more severe depressive symptoms. According to Andresen’s41 classification of depressive symptoms, a score of ≥10 indicates depressive symptoms, and a score of <10 indicates no significant symptoms. The CES-D has demonstrated good validity in older Chinese adults, supporting its applicability in the present study context.42 43 In the study, the internal reliability of the scale was 0.83.

Anxiety

The Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale was used to assess the severity of anxiety symptoms.44 The validated Chinese version of the GAD-7 was used in this study, which has demonstrated good reliability and validity in Chinese populations.45 The scale consists of seven items that evaluate the frequency of emotional experiences over the past week, with responses ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). The total score ranged from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating more severe anxiety. According to Kroenke’s46 classification of anxiety symptoms, a score of <10 indicates no anxiety symptoms, while a score of ≥10 indicates the presence of anxiety symptoms. The GAD-7 has demonstrated acceptable validity in cancer populations, supporting its applicability for assessing anxiety symptoms in this study.47 48 In this study, the internal reliability of the scale was 0.92.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 and Mplus 7.0. Preliminary analyses included descriptive statistics and correlation analyses among the key variables. Pearson correlations were calculated to assess the relationships among key study variables and sample characteristics and to identify potential covariates for subsequent analyses. Structural equation modelling was used to examine the mediation effects. Missing data were handled using maximum likelihood estimation. Model fit was assessed using the chi-square goodness-of-fit statistic, the comparative fit index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), and the root-mean-square error of approximation (RMSEA). A model was considered to exhibit a good fit if the CFI and TLI exceeded 0.95 and the RMSEA was below 0.05.49 Indirect effects were assessed using a bootstrap procedure with bias-corrected 95% CIs.

Results

Descriptive statistics and correlations among variables

A total of 675 participants were invited to participate in this study, of whom 588 survivors consented and completed the questionnaire, yielding a response rate of 87.11%. 62 (10.5%) participants had missing data on the physical activity and were excluded from the analysis. Missing data on other variables were handled using maximum likelihood estimation. Among the 588 participants, 417 (70.9%) were male and 171 (29.1%) were female, with an average age of 63.28 years (±8.78). Of these participants, 60.7% had completed senior high school or higher education, 95.2% were married, and 83.6% had advanced lung cancer (stage III or IV). Path analysis

The hypothesised model showed an adequate fit, χ2(9) = 10.92, p=0.28; CFI=0.997, TLI=0.991, RMSEA=0.020, 90% CI (0.000, 0.055), AIC=10 444.451, BIC=10 551.083. Details of these fit indices and model comparisons are presented in table 3. As shown in figure 1, significant total indirect effects of physical activity on both depression (β=−0.14, SE=0.02, 95% CI −0.187 to −0.097) and anxiety (β=−0.11, SE=0.02, 95% CI −0.151 to −0.072) were observed. For depression, the specific indirect effects of physical activity through social connection (β=−0.08, SE=0.020, 95% CI –0.108 to −0.044) and self-efficacy in stress coping (β=−0.07, SE=0.020, 95% CI −0.098 to −0.034) were significant. Likewise, for anxiety, the specific indirect effects of physical activity through social connection (β=−0.05, SE=0.020, 95% CI −0.082 to −0.023) and self-efficacy in stress coping (β=−0.06, SE=0.01, 95% CI −0.087 to −0.031) were significant. After controlling for the mediators, the direct effect of physical activity on both depression (β=−0.06, SE=0.04, 95% CI −0.125 to 0.015) and anxiety (β=0.04, SE=0.04, 95% CI −0.036 to 0.106) became non-significant. This indicates that the effect of physical activity on emotional distress is fully mediated by social connection and self-efficacy, rather than operating through a direct path. These findings indicate that individuals who engage in more physical activity tend to report lower levels of depression and anxiety, and this relationship appears to be explained by their higher levels of perceived social connection and greater coping self-efficacy.

Although our path model was based on an established theoretical framework, the cross-sectional design allows the testing of alternative models. Following the recommendation of Fiedler et al,50 we tested the reverse mediation models by reversing the roles of the mediators and outcome variables. Specifically, four individual reverse mediation models were tested: (a) physical activity→depression→social connection, (b) physical activity→depression→self-efficacy in stress coping, (c) physical activity→anxiety→social connection and (d) physical activity→anxiety→self-efficacy in stress coping. The results indicated an adequate fit for all four models (TLI >0.80, RMSEA >0.05 for all models). The model fit indices of alternative mediation models are shown in online supplemental table s2. Discussion

The study examined the parallel mediating effects of social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping in the relationship between physical activity and emotional distress (anxiety and depression) among lung cancer survivors. Consistent with our hypotheses, the findings showed that both social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping acted as parallel mediators in the relationship. Specifically, after adjusting for relevant demographic and clinical factors, increased physical activity was associated with enhanced social connection and greater self-efficacy in stress coping, which, in turn, was linked to lower levels of anxiety and depression.

The findings, which indicate that physical activity alleviates symptoms of anxiety and depression by fostering social connection and enhancing self-efficacy in stress coping, align with Kandola’s conceptual model.17 This model identifies social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping as essential mechanisms linking physical activity to emotional health. Social connection may alleviate emotional distress through multiple psychological pathways. Physical activity creates opportunities for survivors to engage in social interactions, receive companionship and gain emotional support, all of which contribute to improved emotional well-being.51 Social connection may also reduce emotional distress by strengthening survivors’ sense of support, belonging and identity, thus reducing feelings of loneliness and social isolation.52 In addition to social connection, self-efficacy in stress coping also plays a critical role in this relationship. A randomised controlled trial showed that yoga interventions improve stress-coping abilities and reduce perceived stress levels in cancer survivors.53 Moreover, cognitive-behavioural stress management interventions for lung cancer survivors, including stress awareness, cognitive adjustment and coping with symptoms, decreased anxiety and depression.54 A review study found that stress management interventions help patients cope with negative emotions, reduce stress and promote stress management, thereby alleviating anxiety and depression.55

After accounting for the mediating effects of social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping, the direct effects of physical activity on anxiety and depression became non-significant. While social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping appear to fully mediate the relationship between physical activity and emotional distress, other factors may also play a role in this complex relationship. For example, previous studies have suggested that factors like body image concerns and post-traumatic growth could mediate the relationship between physical activity and emotional distress.12 14 56 Improved body image through physical activity might boost self-esteem and social confidence, indirectly reducing emotional distress.57 Similarly, post-traumatic growth, which refers to positive psychological changes following adversity, has also been associated with better emotional well-being.58 Future studies should explore these potential mediators and their interactions with social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping to influence emotional health outcomes. The potential mediating roles of body image and post-traumatic growth should also consider differences across cancer types, cancer stages and time since treatment completion in future studies, as these contextual factors may influence the magnitude or direction of mediation effects.

Although the parallel mediation model is grounded in an established theoretical framework, the cross-sectional design of the study limits the ability to infer causal relationships or establish temporal precedence. In particular, we observed an adequate fit of alternative mediation models with anxiety or depression mediating the association of physical activity with social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping. These findings indicate the potential for bidirectional relationships between social connection, self-efficacy in stress coping and emotional distress among lung cancer survivors. Experimental studies are needed to further explore and confirm these possibilities.

Moreover, the fact that the sample was predominantly composed of married males with stage IV lung cancer may limit the generalisability of the findings. Marital status has been associated with stronger perceived social connection59 and better emotional outcomes,60 potentially enhancing the mediating effects of social connection and stress coping self-efficacy. In addition, gender and disease stage may influence coping patterns, symptom burden and engagement in physical activity.61 62

The findings of this study have significant implications for clinical practice and intervention research. They suggest that physical activity interventions for lung cancer survivors should integrate strategies to strengthen social connections and enhance self-efficacy in stress coping. For example, group-based exercise programmes (eg, yoga or walking groups) can provide opportunities for social interaction and emotional support, while mindfulness-based activities (eg, tai chi or meditation) can help survivors develop effective stress-coping skills. An intervention combining stress management and exercise training may more effectively improve stress management than either stress management or exercise training alone.32 These findings inform practical interventions and deepen our understanding of the psychological mechanisms underlying the benefits of physical activity. By highlighting the roles of social connection and stress-coping skills as key mediators, this study provides novel insights into how physical activity can alleviate anxiety and depression among lung cancer survivors. Future interventions should prioritise enhancing social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping through tailored physical activity programmes, thereby maximising the emotional health benefits of physical activity.

Study limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design limits our ability to establish causal relationships. Future research should employ longitudinal or experimental designs to further validate our findings. Second, the measurement of physical activity relies on self-reported questionnaires, which may introduce biases such as recall bias, social desirability bias and common method variance. Future studies should consider incorporating objective measurement tools, such as doubly labelled water (DLW), accelerometers and pedometers,63 and broader, multisite samples to reduce bias and improve external validity. Third, our findings are based on a sample of Chinese lung cancer survivors, which may limit their generalisability to survivors of other cancer types and cultural contexts. Cultural factors may influence how individuals perceive and report social connection and emotional distress,64 which could affect the applicability of our results to other populations. Fourth, social connection was assessed using two brief self-developed items to reduce patient burden. Although pilot testing among lung cancer patients suggested that the items were clear and easy to complete, the absence of formal psychometric validation may have introduced measurement bias. Future research should benefit from conducting a comprehensive validation study of these items to strengthen the reliability and construct validity of the measure. Another limitation is that information on participants’ treatment stage, such as curative stage and palliative stage, was not collected, which may influence physical activity and psychosocial responses. Future studies should include such information to better control for its potential effects on the results. Finally, the self-efficacy scale employed in this study specifically focused on stress-coping efficacy. While relevant to the psychological processes under investigation, it may not encompass other domains of self-efficacy, such as exercise-specific or task-specific self-efficacy, which could also play important roles in influencing physical activity.

Conclusions

In summary, our study indicates that social connection and self-efficacy in stress coping are essential mediators underlying the association between physical activity and anxiety and depression. These findings underscore the importance of addressing both social and psychological factors in interventions aimed at improving mental health outcomes in this population. Future psychological interventions should consider integrating positive psychological services that focus on strengthening social connection and stress-coping skills to help lung cancer survivors better cope with mental health.
