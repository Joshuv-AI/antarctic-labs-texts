Adcyap1r1-driven astrocyte reprogramming attenuates neuroinflammation and promotes dopaminergic neuroprotection in Parkinson’s Disease
Authors: Yang-yang Zheng, Li-rong Hu, Yu Gu, Mei-yun Cai, Xiao-wen Wang, Wen-fang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParkinson’s disease (PD), the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder globally, is characterized by progressive degeneration of dopaminergic (DA) neurons and sustained neuroinflammatory cascades. Strategies that simultaneously suppress neuroinflammation and protect DA neurons are urgently needed, particularly through targeting astrocytes (As). Building on our previous discovery that combined miR-124 and small molecule interventions synergistically suppress As activation and induce their transdifferentiation into dopaminergic-like neurons, this study identified Adcyap1r1 as a pivotal regulator via RNA-seq analysis. Here, we systematically investigated its cAMP-mediated dual functional roles in neuroinflammatory modulation and DA neuron preservation.MethodsTGF-β1-activated neonatal SD rat cortical reactive astrocytes (RAs) were used for Adcyap1r1 overexpression. We assessed astrocyte reactivity, neuroinflammation, neuron-like transition, and cAMP pathway activity in vitro. In MPTP-induced PD mice, AAV-mediated Adcyap1r1 overexpression was targeted to striatal As. Motor function, astrocyte activation, neuroinflammation, and endogenous DA neuron survival in the nigrostriatal system were evaluated through behavioral, histopathological, and molecular analyses.ResultsIn activated RAs, Adcyap1r1 overexpression significantly attenuated reactivity, reduced pro-inflammatory mediator expression (e.g., COX-2, iNOS), activated cAMP signaling, and promoted the acquisition of a tyrosine hydroxylase-positive (TH+) neuron-like phenotype. In MPTP-induced PD mice, astrocyte-targeted Adcyap1r1 overexpression in the striatum effectively inhibited astrocytic activation and neuroinflammation, protected endogenous TH+ neurons in the nigrostriatal system, and alleviated motor deficits, thereby supporting its DA neuroprotective potential.ConclusionsOur study demonstrates that Adcyap1r1 functions through a cAMP-dependent dual mechanism to suppress astrocyte reactivity and neuroinflammatory cascades, and facilitate the transition of RAs into a DA neuron-like phenotype. Background

Parkinson’s disease (PD), the second most common neurodegenerative disorder worldwide, is hallmarked by progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons and abnormal aggregation of α-synuclein in substantia nigra [1–3]. Epidemiological data estimates that PD currently affects over 6 million individuals globally. Projections suggest that this number will exceed 12 million by 2030, marking PD as one of the fastest-growing neurodegenerative diseases. Current clinical interventions, predominantly dopamine replacement therapies (e.g., levodopa) and deep brain stimulation, offer only temporary relief of motor symptoms but show less than 30% efficacy in improving non-motor symptoms [4–6]. Furthermore, long-term utilization of these therapies is often accompanied by complications such as dyskinesias and wearing-off phenomena [7]. Emerging evidence underscores neuroinflammation as a core driver of PD pathogenesis, with its dynamic balance exerting a dual role: moderate inflammation clears abnormal protein aggregates (e.g., α-synuclein), endowing neuroprotection, while the chronic activation of glial cells creates a neurotoxic microenvironment, accelerating neuronal degeneration [8–10]. This “double-edged sword” nature highlights that the development of novel therapeutic strategies capable of both inhibiting excessive inflammation and preserving neurorepair potential may represent a critical breakthrough for overcoming current treatment limitations for PD [11]. Among these potential therapeutic targets, the functional state transition of astrocytes (As) is particularly critical [12]. Under physiological conditions, quiescent As maintain neuronal metabolic homeostasis through the glutamate-glutamine cycle and the secretion of neurotrophic factors (e.g., GDNF/BDNF) [11, 13]. Whereas in the context of PD pathology, As are aberrantly activated into reactive astrocytes (RAs), characterized by cellular hypertrophy, upregulation of markers such as GFAP and S100, and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β [14]. Collectively, these processes create a neurotoxic microenvironment that exacerbates the neurodegenerative progression of PD. Autopsy studies have revealed that RAs accumulate in the substantia nigra of PD patients as well as around amyloid plaques in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [15, 16]. This spatial distribution of RAs correlates closely with the extent of neuronal loss in both neurodegenerative disorders. Thus, precise modulation of As activation to disrupt the vicious cycle of “RAs hyperactivation-neuroinflammation cascade-neuronal damage” holds promise for developing dual-function therapies integrating anti-inflammatory and neuroregenerative effects.

Building upon our prior work, we demonstrated that miR-124 combined with ruxolitinib, SB203580, and forskolin synergistically suppresses rat As activation and induces their trans-lineage transdifferentiation into neuron-like cells [17]. However, multi-component systems face critical bottlenecks in clinical translation, including complex molecular interactions and suboptimal delivery efficiency. To address this, we employed RNA-seq to identify Adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide 1 receptor 1 (Adcyap1r1), a gene significantly upregulated during this process, as a potential key molecular switch governing As functional states and fate transition. Notably, Adcyap1r1 encodes the PAC1 receptor, a G protein-coupled receptor, which activates downstream cAMP signaling upon binding the neuropeptide PACAP, exerting pleiotropic effects in neurodevelopment, synaptic plasticity, and stress responses [18–21]. Studies in PD rat models have shown that reduced Adcyap1r1 expression correlates positively with loss of TH+ neurons in the substantia nigra [22], while research in spinal cord injury models indicates that its activation inhibits apoptosis and promotes post-injury motor function recovery [23]. Moreover, recent evidence further reveals that Adcyap1r1 antagonism exacerbates neuroinflammation by disrupting mitochondrial-calcium signaling homeostasis, highlighting its regulatory importance in neurodegeneration [24]. Despite these insights, whether Adcyap1r1 can achieve the dual effects of “inhibiting As activation and driving neuronal transdifferentiation” through single-gene intervention remains undefined. Addressing this question could overcome the technical limitations of traditional multi-factor strategies, offering a more translatable single-target therapeutic paradigm for PD and other neurodegenerative disorders.

This study aims to systematically dissect the multidimensional regulatory mechanisms of Adcyap1r1 in the pathological microenvironment of PD. Using RA-specific Adcyap1r1 overexpression models, we investigate its roles in suppressing aberrant As activation and promoting the trans-lineage acquisition of multi-subtype neuron-like phenotypes. Combined with the MPTP-induced PD mouse model, we further evaluated its effects on ameliorating motor deficits, inhibiting neuroinflammation, and protecting endogenous dopaminergic neurons. Our findings provide a potential molecular target for PD therapy and mechanistic insights for astrocyte-based neuroregenerative strategies.

Methods

Isolation and culture of rat RAs

Primary cortical as were isolated from postnatal day 1–3 Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (Darenfucheng). Cortical tissues were dissected following decapitation, meningeal removal, and enzymatic digestion with 0.05% trypsin (VivaCell, C3530) in 0.01 M PBS supplemented with DNase I (4 mg/mL; Worthington, LS002006) for 30 min at 37 °C. Subsequently, digestion was neutralized using DMEM/F-12 (VivaCell, C3130) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Cellmax, SA101.02). The cell suspension was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer (Biosharp, BS-40-CS) and cultured in T25 flasks with RA-conditioned medium (RAM): DMEM/F-12 base supplemented with 10% FBS, 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 (Novoprotein, CA59), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Solarbio, P1400), maintained in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator at 37 °C. Then, culture medium replacement commenced on day 5 post-seeding and continued every 48 h. When the cell density reached over 95%, the flask was placed on a shaker at 200 rpm/min at 37 °C. To obtain high-purity RAs, 20 µM cytarabine (Sigma, C1768) was added for 24 h to eliminate proliferative contaminants. The purity of As was validated by GFAP immunostaining (> 95% positive cells) prior to experimental use.

Constructing RAs overexpressing Adcyap1r1

Lentiviral Transfection of RAs with Adcyap1r1 Lentiviral vectors for Adcyap1r1 overexpression (LV-Adcyap1r1) and negative control (LV-NC) were obtained from Shanghai Genechem, with titers of 7.5 × 108 transducing units per mL. Purified RAs were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in astrocyte-conditioned medium (AM). Upon reaching about 70% confluence, the culture medium was replaced with fresh AM containing LV-Adcyap1r1 or LV-NC, and HitransG P transfection reagent. Then, the cells were harvested at specified time points post-transfection for downstream mRNA and protein expression analyses.

Neuronal induction of rat cortical RAs

Following 24-hour transfection of RAs with LV-Adcyap1r1, the culture medium was replaced with neuronal induction medium (NIM) containing DMEM/F-12, 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% N2 supplement, and recombinant growth factors: 5 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF, PeproTech, 400 − 29), 10 ng/mL brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF, PeproTech, 450-02), and 15 ng/mL glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF, PeproTech, 450 − 51). The NIM was renewed every 2 days for 14 days to support differentiation, with cultures processed for immunofluorescence analysis at the endpoint.

Animal experiments

8- to 12-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (SPF grade) were used, housed under specific pathogen-free conditions in the institutional animal facility with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water. All procedures were performed in compliance with the Qingdao University Animal Ethics Committee guidelines (Ethics No.: QDU-AEC-2021134), ensuring minimal animal suffering and adherence to the 3R principles. This study has been reported in line with the ARRIVE Guidelines 2.0. Mice were randomized into four groups (n = 6/group): (1) Control (saline injection); (2) MPTP (saline injection followed by MPTP treatment); (3) MPTP + AAV-Control (striatal injection of AAV-GFAAB1D vector followed by MPTP treatment); (4) MPTP + AAV-Adcyap1r1 (striatal injection of AAV-GFAAB1D-Adcyap1r1 vector followed by MPTP treatment). Bilateral stereotaxic microinjections (1 µL/side) of AAV vectors (1.46 × 1013 vg/mL) or sterile saline were delivered to the striatum. Two weeks post-microinjection, PD models were induced in AAV-NC, AAV-Adcyap1r1, and control saline groups via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of MPTP (15 mg/kg, dissolved in saline, four doses at 2-hour intervals within 1 day). Sham-treated mice received vehicle injections without MPTP.

Striatal stereotactic injections

Mice were deeply anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of a ketamine-xylazine mixture (100 mg/kg + 10 mg/kg). Once fully anesthetized, the scalp was shaved, and animals were mounted on a stereotaxic frame (David Kopf Instruments). Following iodophor (iodine-based antiseptic) disinfection, a midline scalp incision was made to expose the bregma and lambda landmarks. Using a mouse brain stereotaxic atlas (Paxinos and Franklin), the striatal target coordinates were set relative to bregma: anteroposterior (AP) + 1.0 mm, mediolateral (ML) ± 2.1 mm, dorsoventral (DV) -3.5 mm. A cranial drill was used to create a burr hole at the target site. A 10-µL Hamilton syringe fitted with a 33-gauge needle was loaded with AAV solution. Virus was injected at a rate of 200 nL/min, delivering a total volume of 1 µL per hemisphere. After completion, the needle was left in place for 10 min to minimize backflow, followed by slow withdrawal. The same procedure was repeated for the contralateral hemisphere. Wounds were closed with surgical sutures, and mice received postoperative analgesia (buprenorphine, 0.05 mg/kg s.c.) and monitored for recovery.

Mouse behavioral tests

Motor functional performance of mice was evaluated on days 4–5 after MPTP infusion. Subsequent to behavioral testing, mice were euthanized, and brains (with discrete isolation of the substantia nigra and striatum) were collected for downstream experiments. Euthanasia was performed humanely in accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020 edition) to minimize suffering: deep anesthesia was induced via intraperitoneal injection of a mixture of ketamine (10% w/v, 100 mg/kg) and xylazine (1% w/v, 10 mg/kg), with unconsciousness confirmed by the absence of corneal and pedal withdrawal reflexes. Immediately thereafter, transcardial perfusion with cold PBS and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) was conducted prior to brain dissection, and death was verified by the cessation of cardiac activity. For the rotarod test, mice were trained for 2 days before being tested on the third day, with the rotarod accelerating from 4 to 40 rpm for three trials. Rods were cleaned with 70% ethanol between trials to remove odor cues, and the time each mouse spent on the rotarod was recorded and averaged for analysis. The pole test followed similar pre-training, with two criteria: turnaround time and pole-climbing time. The apparatus was a 50 cm high, 1 cm diameter vertical pole with a 2.5 cm spherical top. Mice were placed at the top, and the times to descend and turn around were recorded, with each mouse tested three times and the mean calculated for analysis. Gait analysis was performed using the CatWalk™ XT semi-automated video system, with specific principles and steps detailed in this study, and three valid data points selected for analysis.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Total RNA from cells and striatal tissues was extracted using Trizol reagent (ABclonal, RK30129) following the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA synthesis was performed with a reverse transcription kit (Takara Biotechnology) using 1 µg of total RNA as the template. qRT-PCR was carried out on a CFX96 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad) using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix, with cycling conditions optimized for each primer pair. Relative gene expression was quantified using the 2−△△Ct method, normalizing to GAPDH as the internal control. For data presentation, the mean value of the control group was normalized to 1 as the reference baseline. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Western blotting analysis

Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cells were lysed on ice using RIPA buffer supplemented with a phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktail (1:100, Roche). After 30 min of incubation, cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal protein samples (20 µg) were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes via wet transfer. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies (Table 2) diluted in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. The next day, membranes were washed three times with TBST and incubated with secondary antibodies (Table 2) for 1 h at room temperature. After additional TBST washes, protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescent kit (Millipore) and detected with a ChemiDoc XRS+ imaging system (Bio-Rad). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software, with data normalized to GAPDH loading controls. For rescue experiments, reactive astrocytes were pretreated with the AKT inhibitor MK-2206 (10 µM, TargetMol, T1952) or the CREB inhibitor 666−15 (10 µM, TargetMol, T5318) for 2 h prior to cell lysis.

Histological analysis and immunofluorescence staining

Brains were fixed in 4% PFA for 12 h at room temperature, followed by dehydration in a 20–30% gradient sucrose solution (w/v) until sinking. Dehydrated tissues were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, snap-frozen in isopentane chilled on dry ice, and sectioned into 20-µm-thick coronal slices using a cryostat (Leica). Serial sections were collected into four sets of 10 sections each, including regions of interest (striatum and substantia nigra). For immunohistochemistry (IHC), brain sections were incubated with primary antibodies (Table 2) diluted in blocking buffer (5% normal goat serum in PBS-T) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies (Table 2) for 2 h at room temperature, followed by visualization using a DAB peroxidase substrate kit (ZSGB-BIO, ZLI-9018). The reaction was terminated by rinsing with PBS, ensuring uniform DAB incubation duration across all groups. For immunofluorescence staining, sections were processed similarly but used Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Table 2). Nuclei were counterstained with 4’, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Beyotime, C1002) for 5 min at the end of secondary antibody incubation. All stained sections were mounted with antifade medium and imaged using an Olympus BX53 fluorescence microscope equipped with a digital camera, capturing bright field and fluorescent signals under consistent exposure settings.

CCK-8 kit detection

The experiment involves four time points for transfection: 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. Taking the 24-h transfection as an example, the detailed experimental steps are described as follows: The cell suspension was seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/mL (100 µL per well, with the outer wells left cell-free) and cultured overnight until optimal cell conditions were achieved, followed by lentiviral transduction. After 24 h of transfection, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Univ, abs50003-500T) was added per 100 µL of culture medium, mixed gently to ensure homogeneous coloration (avoiding bubble formation). 100 µL of PBS was added to the outer wells as a blank control, followed by an additional 2-hour incubation. Prior to measurement, the plate was shaken on an orbital shaker for approximately 1 min to ensure uniform color development, and the absorbance at 450 nm was then measured using a microplate reader for subsequent cell viability calculations.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired Student’s t-tests. For multiple group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. For the various analyses, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 and ***P < 0.001 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The purified cultured cells are mainly rat RAs

Following isolation of As from the cerebral cortex of postnatal SD rats, primary cultures were maintained in TGF-β1-enriched medium to induce As into RAs. To ensure that the subsequent experimental cells were RAs, we implemented an optimized dual-depletion protocol combining physical shaking with pharmacological inhibitors to effectively eliminate microglial and oligodendroglial contaminants. Post-purification morphological analysis revealed that most of adherent cells exhibited characteristic star-shaped morphology with multiple cytoplasmic processes, consistent with mature astrocytic differentiation (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, immunophenotypic characterization was performed using immunostaining against the RAs markers GFAP and S100β, neuronal markers DCX and MAP2, microglial marker Iba1, as well as vascular endothelial cell marker CD31. Quantitative analysis demonstrated that over 95% of cells expressed GFAP and S100β (Fig. 1B, C and H), while no positive results were observed for DCX, MAP2, Iba1 or CD31 (Fig. 1D-H), confirming the absence of neuronal progenitors or mature neuronal elements. These results confirm that the cultured cells are mainly rat RAs and have no neuronal contamination, which can be used for subsequent experimental studies.

Construct RAs with Adcyap1r1 overexpressing

To establish Adcyap1r1-overexpressing RAs, we infected RAs with lentivirus carrying the Adcyap1r1 gene, using the corresponding negative control lentivirus for the control group. qPCR analysis demonstrated a marked upregulation of Adcyap1r1 mRNA levels at 24 h post-transduction compared to the control group (Fig. 2A). Western blotting further confirmed enhanced protein expression at 48 h (Fig. 2B and C), confirming successful generation of Adcyap1r1-overexpressing RAs. Subsequently, to investigate the regulatory effects of Adcyap1r1 overexpression on astrocytic reactivity, we performed time-course analyses: qPCR analysis revealed significant downregulation of reactive markers Gfap and S100 mRNA at 24 h compared to the control group (Fig. 2D), paralleled by reduced GFAP protein levels at 48 h (Fig. 2E and F). Immunofluorescence quantification on day 14 further revealed a notable reduction in the number of GFAP-positive cells in the overexpression group (Fig. 2G and H). Additionally, CCK-8 proliferation assays demonstrated progressive suppression of the growth kinetics of Adcyap1r1-overexpressing RAs, with significant attenuation observed at 24 h, 48 h, 72 h and 96 h compared to the control group, along with a progressive decline in proliferative ability over time (Fig. 2I). These multimodal datasets collectively substantiate that Adcyap1r1 overexpression effectively suppresses astrocytic activation.

Overexpression of Adcyap1r1 promotes the conversion of A1-type As to A2-type As

Studies have demonstrated that under pathological conditions of PD, As exhibit a “reactive phenotype,” characterized by a functional imbalance between pro-inflammatory (A1-type) and anti-inflammatory (A2-type) subtypes, which is closely associated with DA neurodegeneration. A1-type As exacerbate neuronal death by releasing pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1β, TNF-α, COX-2, and iNOS, whereas A2-type As exert neuroprotective effects through the secretion of neurotrophic factors like GDNF. qPCR analysis revealed that Adcyap1r1 overexpression significantly downregulated mRNA expression levels of A1-type markers Gbp2 and H2-T23 (Fig. 3A), compared to the control group, while upregulating mRNA levels of A2-type markers S100A10, Tgm1, and Ptx3 (Fig. 3B). To further validate these findings, we assessed the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators. qPCR analysis demonstrated a marked reduction in IL-1β, TNF-α, COX-2, and iNOS mRNA levels in Adcyap1r1-transfected As at 24 h post-transfection relative to the Control group (Fig. 3C). Consistent with transcriptional changes, western blotting analysis at 48 h post-transfection confirmed suppressed protein expressions of COX-2 and iNOS (Fig. 3D-G). Immunofluorescence analysis further revealed the reduced positive detection rates of COX-2 and iNOS compared with control specimens (Fig. 3H and I).

Overexpression of Adcyap1r1 promotes transdifferentiation of rat RAs into neuron-like cells

To further investigate Adcyap1r1’s specific regulatory effects on RAs-to-neuron transdifferentiation, we analyzed neuronal lineage markers. qPCR analysis revealed significant transcriptional activation of neuronal markers in Adcyap1r1-overexpressing RAs compared to controls at 24 and 48 h post-transfection. Early neurogenic markers Tuj1 and Dcx were markedly upregulated, paralleled by elevated expression of mature neuronal marker Map2 and synaptic connectivity marker Syn1 (Fig. 4A-D). Western blotting quantification at 48 h confirmed upregulated DCX protein expression (Fig. 4E and F). In addition, immunofluorescence after 14 days of induction demonstrated the formation of DCX+ neuron-like cells in the Adcyap1r1-overexpressing group (Fig. 4G), whereas no positive signals were detected for the mature neuronal markers MAP2 and NeuN, as shown in the supplementary materials (Fig. S1A and B). Subsequently, to characterize neuronal subtype specification, subtype-specific neuronal markers were also evaluated. qPCR analysis showed that Adcyap1r1 overexpression markedly increased mRNA expressions of dopaminergic neurons markers Th, Nurr1 and Lmx1b (Fig. 4H-J), cholinergic marker Chat (Fig. 4K), GABAergic markers Gad1 and Gad2 (Fig. 4L and M), and glutamatergic markers Vglut1 and Vglut2 (Fig. 4N and O) at both 24 and 48 h post-transfection, with expression increasing in a time-dependent manner. Consistently, immunofluorescence analysis on day 14 revealed the presence of TH+ cells in Adcyap1r1-overexpressing cultures (Fig. 4P), corroborating dopaminergic neurons lineage specification. These findings collectively demonstrate that Adcyap1r1 overexpression promotes the initial neuronal transdifferentiation of RAs into neuron-like cells across multiple subtypes, highlighting its pivotal role in orchestrating early neurogenic programs in glial cells.

Molecular mechanisms underlying Adcyap1r1-mediated regulation of astrocyte activation and transdifferentiation into neuron-like cells

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying Adcyap1r1-mediated regulation of As activation and transdifferentiation into neuron-like cells, potential signaling pathways were screened via the KEGG pathway database. It is well-established that Adcyap1/Adcyap1r1 signaling, activated by Adcyap1, mediates plasma membrane adenylate cyclase (AC) activation, which catalyzes ATP conversion to cAMP, thereby elevating intracellular cAMP levels. We hypothesized that Adcyap1r1 may suppress As activation and pro-inflammatory factor release via modulation of the cAMP-AKT-JNK/IκB pathway, which critically regulates NF-κB nuclear translocation, while promoting RAs transdifferentiation into neuron-like cells through the cAMP-CREB signaling axis. To validate this, Western blotting analysis was conducted to reveal that compared with controls, Adcyap1r1 overexpression significantly enhanced the phosphorylation level of AKT (p-AKT) (Fig. 5A and B), while suppressing the phosphorylation of JNK (p-JNK) (Fig. 5C and D) and IκB (p-IκB) (Fig. 5E and F). Additionally, overexpression of Adcyap1r1 led to a notable increase in the phosphorylation of CREB (p-CREB) (Fig. 5G and H).

To further confirm the causal regulatory roles of these signaling axes in Adcyap1r1-mediated biological functions, we performed pharmacological rescue experiments using specific pathway inhibitors. Inhibition of AKT activity with MK-2206 significantly reversed the inhibitory effect of Adcyap1r1 on JNK phosphorylation (p-JNK) (Fig. 5I and J), and increased the phosphorylation of p65 (p-p65) (Fig. 5K and L), the core functional subunit of NF-κB. In parallel, blockade of CREB signaling with 666−15 markedly abolished the Adcyap1r1-induced upregulation of the neuronal marker DCX (Fig. 5M and N). Collectively, these findings suggest that AKT and CREB are indispensable for the anti-inflammatory and pro-differentiation effects of Adcyap1r1 in RAs, supporting our dual-pathway hypothesis that Adcyap1r1 acts through cAMP-AKT-JNK/IκB and cAMP-CREB signaling to coordinate the suppression of inflammation and promotion of neurogenic differentiation in RAs.

Overexpression of Adcyap1r1 improves behavioral function in MPTP-induced PD mice

To further evaluate the therapeutic potential of As-targeted Adcyap1r1 modulation in PD, stereotactic delivery of AAV-Adcyap1r1 to striatal As preceded MPTP administration by 14 days, ensuring preconditioning of the glial microenvironment via As-specific overexpression. On day 14 of pre-treatment, PD was induced via MPTP injection (Fig. 6A). Molecular validation confirmed successful Adcyap1r1 overexpression, with qPCR revealing a significant downregulation of Adcyap1r1 mRNA in MPTP and MPTP + AAV-Control groups versus Control group, which was reversed by Adcyap1r1 overexpression (Fig. 6B), findings corroborated by western blotting for protein expression (Fig. 6C and D). Then behavioral tests were conducted on the 4th to 5th day after MPTP injection. Pole and rotarod tests revealed prolonged turning time and descent time and reduced rotarod retention in MPTP and MPTP + AAV-Control mice versus the Control group, with Adcyap1r1 overexpression significantly ameliorating these motor deficits (Fig. 6E-G). Gait analysis showed irregular paw prints, increased dwell time, and decreased average speed, step frequency, swing velocity, and stride length in MPTP groups; all parameters were significantly improved in MPTP + AAV-Adcyap1r1 mice (Fig. 6H-P).

Adcyap1r1 overexpression suppresses striatal neuroinflammation and protects dopaminergic neurons in MPTP-induced PD mice

To investigate the impact of Adcyap1r1 overexpression on neuroinflammation and endogenous dopaminergic neurons in the striatum of MPTP-induced PD mice, striatal tissues were collected at day 5 after MPTP induction. We first verified the reactive astrocyte specificity of AAV vectors by immunofluorescence co-labeling. The results showed that AAV signals were predominantly co-localized with GFAP-positive As in the mouse striatum, confirming the high efficiency and specificity of astrocyte targeting (Fig. 7A). Immunofluorescence revealed a marked increase in GFAP+ cells in the striatum of MPTP and MPTP + AAV-Control groups compared to the Control group, which was significantly attenuated by Adcyap1r1 overexpression (Fig. 7B and C). Consistently, Western blotting confirmed a reduction in GFAP protein levels in the striatum of MPTP + AAV-Adcyap1r1 group (Fig. 7D and E), indicating suppressed As activation. To further validate the anti-inflammatory role of Adcyap1r1, pro-inflammatory markers expression was assessed. Both qPCR and Western blotting demonstrated that the upregulated mRNA (Fig. 7F and G) and protein expression (Fig. 7H-K) of iNOS and COX-2 in MPTP-treated mice were effectively reversed by Adcyap1r1 overexpression. Given the reciprocal interplay between neuroinflammation and apoptosis, we also evaluated apoptosis-related proteins. Western blotting demonstrated that MPTP induction led to elevated pro-apoptotic Bax and suppressed anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 expression, while Adcyap1r1 overexpression normalized these changes (Fig. 7L-O), indicating its anti-apoptotic and neuroprotective roles. Given the established anti-inflammatory effects, we next examined the integrity of endogenous dopaminergic neurons. qPCR and Western blotting showed that Adcyap1r1 overexpression prevented the reduction in TH expression and rescued MPTP-induced downregulation of TH mRNA (Fig. 7P) and protein levels (Fig. 7Q and R). Immunohistochemistry further revealed reduced TH+ fiber density in the MPTP group, which was preserved by Adcyap1r1-overexpressing As through neuroprotection (Fig. 7S and T). These results collectively demonstrate that Adcyap1r1 overexpression mitigates striatal neuroinflammation, inhibits apoptosis, and preserves the integrity of endogenous dopaminergic neurons in PD mice, underscoring its dual protective mechanisms in PD pathology.

Striatal astrocytic Adcyap1r1 overexpression attenuates substantia nigra neuroinflammation and preserves dopaminergic neurons in MPTP-induced PD mice

To determine whether targeted Adcyap1r1 overexpression in striatal As similarly suppresses neuroinflammation and protects endogenous dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra of MPTP-induced PD mice along the nigrostriatal circuit, we analyzed substantia nigra tissues collected at day 5 after MPTP induction. Immunofluorescence revealed a significant increase in GFAP+ RAs and Iba1+ microglia, key contributors to neuroinflammatory progression, in the substantia nigra of MPTP and MPTP + AAV-Control groups compared to the Control group (Fig. 8A-C). Striatal Adcyap1r1 overexpression markedly reduced both GFAP+ RAs and Iba1+ microglia, indicating its inhibitory effect on MPTP-induced glial activation. To further validate the anti-inflammatory role of Adcyap1r1, pro-inflammatory markers expression was assessed. Western blotting demonstrated that the upregulated protein expression of COX-2 and iNOS in MPTP-treated mice was effectively reversed by striatal Adcyap1r1 overexpression (Fig. 8D-G). In addition, Western blotting demonstrated that MPTP induction significantly downregulated TH protein levels in the substantia nigra, which was reversed by the above intervention (Fig. 8H and I). Similarly, immunohistochemical analysis further showed reduced numbers of endogenous TH+ neurons in the substantia nigra of MPTP-treated mice, which was improved by striatal astrocytic Adcyap1r1 overexpression via circuit crosstalk-mediated neuroprotection (Fig. 8J and K). These findings collectively establish that Adcyap1r1 overexpression in striatal As also attenuates neuroinflammation and protects endogenous dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra of PD mice, consistent with its local neuroprotective effects in the striatum and reflecting the functional interaction within the nigrostriatal pathway.

Discussion

As, the most abundant and widely distributed glial cells in the CNS, are tightly associated with the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases including PD and AD [25–27]. Dysfunctional As trigger a self-amplifying cascade of pro-inflammatory cytokines via metabolic and immune regulatory abnormalities, accelerating dopaminergic neuronal damage [28, 29]. Notably, upon activation into RAs by disease-associated signals, As undergo characteristic morphological changes (cell body hypertrophy, enhanced proliferation and robust GFAP upregulation) and secrete inflammatory mediators (IL-1β and TNF-α) that drive the formation of a neuroinflammatory microenvironment [30, 31]. Therefore, an in-depth analysis of the molecular switches and intracellular regulatory networks through which As mediate neuroinflammation will provide a new perspective for developing glial cell-targeted treatment strategies for neurodegenerative diseases such as PD [32–34]. It should be noted that conventional primary astrocyte cultures exhibit mild basal activation attributed to trypsin-induced cellular stress and 10% FBS-driven proliferation, a characteristic that warrants consideration.

Our team’s previous research has demonstrated that miR-124 combined with three small molecules can synergistically inhibit the RA-specific markers and induce their transdifferentiation into multi-subtype neuron-like cells, while transcriptome sequencing identified Adcyap1r1 as a significantly upregulated gene in this process. Given the clinical translation challenges of multi-component systems, this study focused on investigating the single-gene regulatory effects of Adcyap1r1, a potential key modulator of astrocyte fate.

Using a TGF-β1-induced RAs model and lentivirus-mediated Adcyap1r1 overexpression system, we uncovered the core dual regulatory role of Adcyap1r1 in RAs: it not only effectively inhibited the expression of RAs activation markers (Gfap and S100) and pro-inflammatory mediators (IL-1β, TNF-α, COX-2 and iNOS), but also reshaped RAs neuroprotective function by downregulating A1 neurotoxic markers (Gbp2 and H2-T23) and upregulating A2 neuroprotective markers (S100A10, Tgm1 and Ptx3). These findings provide novel insights into the targeted modulation of astrocyte phenotypic polarization in the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases such as PD. Accumulating single-cell transcriptomic studies have unveiled substantial region- and disease-specific heterogeneity in RAs, challenging the traditional notion of a uniform glial response [35]. The A1/A2 polarization paradigm, initially defined by Liddelow et al. via in vitro and in vivo models, represents a critical functional dichotomy within this heterogeneous spectrum [36]. Specifically, A1 As are triggered by microglia-derived IL-1α, TNF-α, and C1q, exerting potent neurotoxicity by inducing neuronal and oligodendrocyte death. In contrast, A2 As, enriched in ischemic and reparative contexts, upregulate trophic factors to promote neuronal survival, tissue repair, and anti-inflammatory responses [37]. In PD, post-mortem human specimens and preclinical animal models consistently demonstrate that A1-like neurotoxic As predominate in the nigrostriatal pathway, being tightly associated with progressive dopaminergic neurodegeneration and sustained neuroinflammation [38]. Emerging evidence further indicates that pathological α-synuclein induces A1 polarization of As, which in turn impairs the autophagy-lysosomal pathway and facilitates α-synuclein aggregation and propagation-forming a self-amplifying neurotoxic feedforward loop [39]. Consistent with these recent advances, our results show that Adcyap1r1 overexpression robustly suppresses A1 markers and upregulates A2 markers, acting as a key molecular switch to redirect astrocyte polarization. Collectively, targeting Adcyap1r1 to modulate astrocyte subtype heterogeneity may offer a precision strategy to break the toxic cycle and restore neuroprotective function in PD.

Van et al.'s research found that specific knockout of the Adcyap1r1 gene in the mouse retina leads to abnormal structures of retinal neurons and dendrites, revealing its key regulatory role in neurodevelopment [18]. This aligns with our team’s previous discovery of Adcyap1r1’s upregulation during the transdifferentiation of rat RAs into neuron-like cells. In this study, Adcyap1r1 overexpression significantly upregulated the mRNA expression levels of pan-neuronal markers (Tuj1, Dcx and Map2) and synaptic marker (Syn1), and immunofluorescence further revealed the appearance of DCX+ cells in RAs on the day 14 of induction. Although protein expression of the mature neuronal markers MAP2 and NeuN remained undetectable at this stage, this pattern aligns with the well-documented temporal separation of marker expression during somatic cell reprogramming into neurons. Future optimization of induction conditions, such as extending the induction period or adding neurotrophic factors like BDNF, may facilitate the acquisition of mature neuronal phenotypes and strengthen mechanistic insights into RA-to-neuron transdifferentiation. Notably, Adcyap1r1 overexpression also induced a time-dependent increase in mRNA levels of multiple neuronal subtype markers, including dopaminergic (Th, Nurr1 and Lmx1b), cholinergic (Chat), GABAergic (Gad1 and Gad2), and glutamatergic (Vglut1 and Vglut2) markers, supporting its potential to direct RAs toward multi-subtype neuronal specification at the transcriptional level. However, functional maturation remains to be verified. Once mature neuronal phenotypes are obtained, subsequent studies should comprehensively assess the electrophysiological properties of induced cells using the single-cell patch clamp recording and dynamically monitor the RA-derived transdifferentiation process via Cre-loxP–mediated lineage tracing, thus providing conclusive evidence for cell fate transition [40–42].

To assess the neuroprotective effect of Adcyap1r1 in the pathological microenvironment of PD, we established an adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated overexpression model targeting striatal RAs in MPTP-induced PD mice. Behavioral tests (pole, rotarod, and gait tests) showed that Adcyap1r1 overexpression significantly improved motor coordination in PD mice. Mechanistically, these beneficial effects were accompanied by suppressed glial hyperactivation, attenuated neuroinflammation, and enhanced protection of endogenous dopaminergic neurons in both the striatum and substantia nigra. Notably, Adcyap1r1 overexpression markedly reduced the number of GFAP+ RAs in the striatum, which may involve three complementary processes that collectively contribute to neuroprotection: (1) Adcyap1r1 restrains abnormal RA proliferation by downregulating critical cell-cycle regulators such as Cyclin D1; (2) It redirects RAs toward a neuroprotective A2 phenotype, enhancing the survival and function of endogenous dopaminergic neurons-consistent with our in vivo neuroprotective effects; (3) In line with our in vitro observation of DCX+ immature neuron-like cells, Adcyap1r1 may drive the early-stage transdifferentiation of striatal RAs toward a neuronal lineage, further reducing the RA pool while facilitating cellular repair in the injured striatum. Future EdU/DCX co-localization studies will be performed to directly trace these fate transitions and validate the underlying mechanisms.

At the molecular level, our study unveiled that Adcyap1r1 orchestrates a dual-axis regulation of cAMP-AKT-JNK/IκB and cAMP-CREB pathways to exert synergistic neuroprotective effects. As established, TGF-β1 drives astrocyte-to-RA transition via non-canonical NF-κB and JNK activation, fostering a pro-inflammatory milieu detrimental to dopaminergic neurons. We propose that upon Adcyap1r1 overexpression, the encoded G protein-coupled receptor elevates intracellular cAMP through adenylate cyclase activation, initiating bifurcated signaling cascades: on one hand, it enhances AKT phosphorylation, which in turn suppresses TGF-β1-induced JNK and IκB phosphorylation, thereby blocking NF-κB nuclear translocation and subsequent transcription of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and TNF-α. On the other hand, it enhances CREB phosphorylation, promoting its nuclear translocation and subsequent upregulation of key neurogenic transcription factors including Lmx1b and Nurr1, which drive RAs to transdifferentiate into early neuron-like cells (Fig. 9). These coordinated signaling pathways collectively modulate the phenotypic transition of As toward the neuroprotective A2-like phenotype, and pharmacological interference experiments further confirm that intact AKT and CREB signaling are indispensable for mediating these neuroprotective effects.

Proposed mechanistic model of Adcyap1r1-mediated inflammation suppression and neuronal differentiation in RAs. Adcyap1r1 overexpression in RAs inhibits pro-inflammatory factors production via the cAMP-AKT-JNK/IκB signaling axis, while promoting neuronal differentiation through activation of the cAMP-CREB pathway

It is worth noting that the cAMP-CREB signaling not only promotes the expression of synaptic plasticity-related proteins (e.g., SYN1, PSD95) by inducing BDNF secretion, but also mediates neuronal functional maturation by regulating calcium oscillation frequency. Additionally, studies have reported that AKT phosphorylates MDM2 to promote p53 ubiquitination and degradation, thus relieving p53’s transcriptional inhibition of neuronal differentiation-related genes [43]. This regulatory pattern has also been experimentally confirmed by Jinhong Xu’s team,

who demonstrated that activating the AKT pathway and inhibiting p53 enables efficient transdifferentiation of human As into the neuroectodermal lineage [44]. In this study, Adcyap1r1 overexpression significantly enhanced AKT phosphorylation in RAs, suggesting that it may also drive the transition of RAs fate into early neuron-like cells through the cAMP-AKT-p53 signaling axis. In summary, these findings collectively outline the framework of Adcyap1r1’s role in inhibiting the astrocyte activation and promoting their transdifferentiation into neuron-like cells through the cAMP cascade signaling.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that Adcyap1r1 exerts a neuroprotective effect through a multi-dimensional regulatory mechanism: (1) Inhibiting the activation of As and the cascade reaction of neuroinflammation; (2) Promoting the polarization of As into neuroprotective A2-type As; (3) Facilitating the acquisition of multi-subtype neuron-like characteristics by As. Its uniqueness lies in the ability to simultaneously regulate multiple functional states of As through a single gene intervention, which may offer potential advantages for therapeutic development in PD. Future research should focus on: (1) Extending the induction duration or supplementing neurotrophic factors to promote the maturation and functionality of neuron-like cells; (2) Developing targeted delivery systems to selectively manipulate Adcyap1r1 expression in As in vivo. Collectively, these findings not only provide new insights into the pathological roles of reactive astrocytes in PD but also offer a theoretical basis for exploring glial cell reprogramming as a potential therapeutic strategy for neurodegenerative diseases.
