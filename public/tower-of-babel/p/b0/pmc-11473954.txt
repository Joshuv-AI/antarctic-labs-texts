Adaptive segmentation-to-survival learning for survival prediction from multi-modality medical images
Authors: Mingyuan Meng, Bingxin Gu, Michael Fulham, Shaoli Song, Dagan Feng, Lei Bi, Jinman Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Early survival prediction is vital for the clinical management of cancer patients, as tumors can be better controlled with personalized treatment planning. Traditional survival prediction methods are based on radiomics feature engineering and/or clinical indicators (e.g., cancer staging). Recently, survival prediction models with advances in deep learning techniques have achieved state-of-the-art performance in end-to-end survival prediction by exploiting deep features derived from medical images. However, existing models are heavily reliant on the prognostic information within primary tumors and cannot effectively leverage out-of-tumor prognostic information characterizing local tumor metastasis and adjacent tissue invasion. Also, existing models are sub-optimal in leveraging multi-modality medical images as they rely on empirically designed fusion strategies to integrate multi-modality information, where the fusion strategies are pre-defined based on domain-specific human prior knowledge and inherently limited in adaptability. Here, we present an Adaptive Multi-modality Segmentation-to-Survival model (AdaMSS) for survival prediction from multi-modality medical images. The AdaMSS can self-adapt its fusion strategy based on training data and also can adapt its focus regions to capture the prognostic information outside the primary tumors. Extensive experiments with two large cancer datasets (1380 patients from nine medical centers) show that our AdaMSS surmounts the state-of-the-art survival prediction performance (C-index: 0.804 and 0.757), demonstrating the potential to facilitate personalized treatment planning.

Introduction

Survival prediction is crucial for cancer management as it can provide early prognostic information for personalized treatment planning1. It is a regression task that aims to predict the survival outcomes of patients, such as Progression-Free Survival (PFS) and Recurrence-Free Survival (RFS). As patients may be lost during follow-up, survival prediction often encounters right-censored survival data: it is only known that the patients survived for a period of time, but the exact time of event occurrence (e.g., disease progression) is unclear. This incomplete data incurs difficulties in designing survival models. In addition, the survival outcomes of patients are influenced by complex interactions among a variety of factors, including treatment regimens, clinical demographics, and the underlying disease physiology2. This makes survival prediction an intractable challenge that tends to integrate multi-modality imaging data to optimize survival prediction performance1,2, such as Positron Emission Tomography/Computed Tomography (PET/CT) that provides both metabolic (from PET) and anatomical (from CT) information of tumors. In recent years, survival prediction has gained wide attention1,3, especially for cancers with high incidence or mortality (e.g., Head and Neck (H&N) cancers4. The HEad and neCK TumOR segmentation and outcome prediction (HECKTOR) challenge has been held for three years to facilitate the development of survival prediction methods for H&N cancers from PET/CT images5,6.

Traditional survival prediction methods are usually based on radiomics7, in which high-dimensional handcrafted features are extracted from medical images and modeled by statistical survival models such as Cox Proportional Hazard (CoxPH) model8. Radiomics has been widely employed in survival prediction for its capability to precisely characterize intra-tumor information including tumor texture, intensity, heterogeneity, and morphology9,10. However, as radiomics features are extracted from segmented regions that are often limited to primary tumors11,12, radiomics has difficulties in characterizing the prognostic information outside the primary tumors, such as lymph node metastasis13 and heterogeneity in tumor periphery14. There have been studies that incorporate lymph node segmentation15,16 or peritumoral information17,18 in radiomics analysis; however, lymph node segmentation remains a challenging task and the peritumoral information is limited to manually-defined local surroundings of the tumors. Deep survival models based on deep learning have shown the potential to address this limitation as they can perform end-to-end prediction from medical images without necessarily requiring tumor masks1, thus potentially leveraging the prognostic information throughout the entire image for survival prediction. Unfortunately, performing end-to-end prediction without tumor masks brings in interference from irrelevant backgrounds and incurs difficulties in extracting tumor-specific information. There also exist deep survival models that take tumor masks as input to exclude non-tumor regions19,20, but these models have lost the potential to leverage out-of-tumor prognostic information.

The above dilemma exposes a crucial question for survival prediction: how to capture all relevant prognostic information while minimizing interference from irrelevant information. Recently, deep survival models attempted to answer this question by leveraging Multi-Task Learning (MTL)21 and have demonstrated state-of-the-art survival prediction performance22–25. Tumor segmentation has been shown as an effective auxiliary task for survival outcome prediction in various cancers, including Oropharynx Cancer (OPC)22,24 and Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma (NPC)23,25. Deep survival models, through jointly learning tumor segmentation, can be implicitly guided to extract tumor-related information while not excluding out-of-tumor information in images. However, these models cannot fully leverage the out-of-tumor prognostic information due to the destructive interference26, also known as negative transfer27, intrinsic to MTL: the survival prediction task expects models to encompass out-of-tumor prognostic information, while the tumor segmentation task expects models to focus on tumor regions.

How to leverage multi-modality medical images is another crucial question for survival prediction, as multi-modality images can provide potentially complementary information28,29. For example, PET/CT images can provide complementary anatomical and metabolic information of tumors and have been shown to benefit survival prediction20. However, existing deep survival models are sub-optimal in utilizing multi-modality images. Specifically, empirically-designed fusion strategies (e.g., early/late fusion) were commonly adopted to fuse multi-modality information using task-specific manually-designed networks23,24,28,29. The empirically-designed fusion strategies, however, are inherently limited in adaptability because the possible search space for fusion strategies can be too large to be manually searched and the optimal strategy could vary depending on the data (e.g., different diseases) and the tasks (e.g., segmentation or survival prediction). Recently, gate-based fusion modules were used to adaptively integrate multi-modal information (e.g., image, text, and audio)30,31, demonstrating the potential for data-driven fusion. Nevertheless, data-driven fusion strategies have not been investigated for survival prediction from multi-modality medical images.

To cope with these two crucial questions for survival prediction, we present an Adaptive Multi-modality Segmentation-to-Survival model (AdaMSS) for survival prediction from multi-modality PET/CT images. The AdaMSS introduces a novel Segmentation-to-Survival Learning (SSL) strategy, where the model is first pretrained for tumor segmentation and then fine-tuned for survival prediction in two training stages (Fig. 1). This learning strategy is free from the destructive interference in MTL and enables the AdaMSS to initially focus on tumor regions and then gradually expand its focus to include other prognosis-related regions. Moreover, the SSL strategy also provides flexibility to incorporate conventional radiomics features as an enhancement for survival prediction, where handcrafted image features can be extracted from the AdaMSS-segmented tumor regions and then integrated into the AdaMSS through cooperative training and inference (Fig. 1). The AdaMSS further introduces a data-driven strategy to fuse multi-modality information, which realizes adaptive optimization of fusion strategies based on training data during training. To realize this, multi-modality information is allowed to be fused at many potential network locations and then a fusion gate module is proposed to control how to fuse information at each of these locations, which makes a fundamental shift from existing empirically-designed strategies to a data-driven strategy. With the SSL and data-driven fusion strategies, the AdaMSS is designed as an adaptive model consisting of an adaptive focus (Ada-focus) decoder and an adaptive fusion (Ada-fusion) encoder, which enables self-adaptation of its focus regions and fusion strategy for different training targets in the two-stage segmentation-to-survival learning process. Results

Patents and datasets

Our AdaMSS was evaluated with two multi-center cancer datasets including patients with Oropharynx Cancer (OPC) and Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma (NPC). The OPC dataset contains 488 histologically proven locally advanced oropharyngeal H&N cancer patients, which were derived from the HECKTOR 2022 challenge6 and acquired from seven clinical centers. All patients underwent pretreatment PET/CT and have clinical indicators including age, gender, weight, tobacco/alcohol consumption, Human Papilloma Virus (HPV) status, performance status (Zubrod), and therapy regimens (radiotherapy with chemotherapy and/or surgery). RFS labels (time-to-event in days and censored-or-not status) were provided as ground truth for survival prediction, while manual annotations of primary tumors and metastatic lymph nodes were provided as ground truth for segmentation.

The NPC dataset contains 892 histologically proven locally advanced NPC patients from the Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (FUSCC) and Shanghai Proton and Heavy Ion Center (SPHIC). All patients underwent pretreatment PET/CT and have clinical indicators including age, gender, Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) antibody status, Body Mass Index (BMI), Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH), histology type, and TNM stage. PFS labels (time-to-event in months and censored-or-not status) were provided as ground truth for survival prediction, while manual annotations of primary tumors were provided as ground truth for segmentation.

Survival prediction performance

Our AdaMSS was compared with seven existing survival prediction methods, including two traditional radiomics-based methods, two single-task deep survival models, and three multi-task deep survival models. The included traditional methods are Lasso-CoxPH32 and ICARE33. For the traditional methods, radiomics features were extracted from the ground-truth tumor regions using the same feature extraction procedure as23, which is detailed in the “Methods” section. The included single-task deep survival models are MDSN34 and DeepMTLR-CoxPH35. The included multi-task deep survival models are TMSS24, DeepMTS23, and Radio-DeepMTS36. The DeepMTLR-CoxPH, ICARE, and Radio-DeepMTS achieved top performance in the well-benchmarked HECKTOR 2021/2022 challenges, which serve as state-of-the-art survival prediction methods.

We also performed survival analysis for patient group stratification, where patients were stratified into high- or low-risk groups based on the predicted survival outcomes. The Kaplan-Meier curves of the high- and low-risk patient groups are also shown in Fig. 3. For comparison with our AdaMSS, a commonly used CoxPH model based on clinical indicators was also used for patient stratification. Discussion

In this study, we present an Adaptive Multi-modality Segmentation-to-Survival model (AdaMSS) for survival prediction from PET/CT images. The AdaMSS introduces a Segmentation-to-Survival Learning (SSL) strategy for model training and a data-driven strategy for multi-modality image information fusion, which have shown superiority over the existing single- and multi-task learning strategies and the existing empirically-designed fusion strategies. With the SSL and data-driven fusion strategies, the AdaMSS is endowed with the adaptability to optimize its focus regions and fusion strategy during training, thus enabling it to achieve consistent improvements in both survival prediction and tumor segmentation. In addition, unlike the existing common deep survival models developed and evaluated for a single disease22–24,28,29,34,37–39, the AdaMSS was evaluated with two large multi-center cancer datasets and showed good generalizability to different diseases (OPC and NPC).

Survival prediction is crucial for personalized treatment planning as it can stratify patients into groups with different prognoses, and accordingly, clinicians could adopt customized treatment regimens for each patient group. For example, according to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines40, induction chemotherapy (IC) and concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) are recommended as 2A-level evidence for NPC patients. However, it is still a controversy as a portion of locoregionally advanced NPC patients do not benefit from IC. Qiang et al.41 developed a survival prediction system to explore whether different patient groups can benefit from IC + CCRT or CCRT only. Zhong et al.42 developed a deep learning-based nomogram to predict the prognosis of NPC patients and accordingly recommended optimized treatment regimens. These studies demonstrated the necessity of survival prediction to optimize treatment regimens. Our experiments with two large multi-center cancer datasets show that our AdaMSS can outperform the state-of-the-art survival prediction methods (Table 1) and can stratify patients into groups with significantly different prognoses (Fig. 3). These findings suggest that the AdaMSS could serve as a powerful tool for the prognosis of cancer patients and demonstrates the potential to facilitate personalized treatment planning with accurate survival prediction.

Survival prediction is a challenging task that has attracted wide attention1. The CoxPH model8 is the most widely used statistical survival model and has been extended to many variants, such as the Lasso-CoxPH32 that uses Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression for feature reduction. Recently, Individual Coefficient Approximation for Risk Estimation (ICARE) model33 was proposed and achieved one of the top performance in the HECKTOR 2022 challenge. However, as these statistical survival models cannot be directly applied to image data, handcrafted radiomics features are necessary for traditional survival prediction methods. Deep survival models have been developed to perform end-to-end survival prediction from medical images1. For example, Jing et al.34 proposed a Multi-modality Deep Survival Network (MDSN) for survival prediction in NPC. This model takes images covering the whole nasopharynx as input, which enables it to access complete image information but unavoidably brings in interference from irrelevant backgrounds. Recently, multi-task deep survival models were proposed and achieved state-of-the-art performance22–25. Saeed et al.24 proposed a Transformer-based Multimodal network for Segmentation and Survival prediction (TMSS) using a hard-sharing Visual Transformer (ViT)43. In our previous studies23,44, we proposed a Deep Multi-Task Survival model (DeepMTS) that uses a hybrid multi-task framework for joint tumor segmentation and survival prediction. In the comparison among our AdaMSS and existing survival prediction methods (Table 1), our AdaMSS outperformed all the comparison methods in both OPC and NPC datasets, which suggests that our AdaMSS has achieved state-of-the-art survival prediction performance. The widely-adopted Lasso-CoxPH achieved the poorest performance and was outperformed by the recent deep survival models, which is likely attributed to the fact that radiomics features have difficulties in representing the prognostic information outside the segmented tumor regions. However, this does not mean radiomics features are unhelpful. The recent radiomics-based method, ICARE, outperformed the single-task deep survival models (MDSN and DeepMTLR-CoxPH) and performed comparably with the state-of-the-art multi-task deep survival models (TMSS and DeepMTS), which suggests that radiomics features are still promising for the capabilities to represent intra-tumor information.

Deep survival models can be leveraged along with radiomics features and clinical indicators. We previously attempted to enhance DeepMTS with radiomics features and developed a radiomics-enhanced DeepMTS (Radio-DeepMTS)36, which was placed 2nd in the HECKTOR 2022 challenge with a negligible performance difference from the 1st place (0.00068 in C-index with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P$$\end{document}P > 0.05). As shown in Table 1, by integrating the DeepMTS and radiomics features, the Radio-DeepMTS outperformed all other comparison methods, which is consistent with the finding in our previous study36 and further demonstrates the potential to leverage radiomics features to improve survival prediction. We also evaluated the contributions of radiomics features and clinical indicators to our AdaMSS (Table 1) and found that adding radiomics features and/or clinical indicators all contributed to statistically significant improvements in survival prediction. However, compared to the improvements of Radio-DeepMTS over DeepMTS, radiomics features caused larger improvements in our AdaMSS. There are two possible explanations for the larger improvements. Firstly, our AdaMSS achieved better segmentation performance than the DeepMTS, enabling radiomics features to represent intra-tumor information more precisely. Secondly, our AdaMSS integrated radiomics features through cooperative training and inference, while the Radio-DeepMTS integrated radiomics features merely at the decision level and did not consider the synergies during training. Through the SSL strategy, our AdaMSS decouples tumor segmentation and survival prediction to different training stages, which provides the feasibility to incorporate radiomics features (from Stage-I) into joint training with survival prediction (in Stage-II), thus achieving closer cooperation between radiomics and deep learning. In addition, even without using radiomics features, our AdaMSS still achieved better survival prediction and tumor segmentation performance than all the comparison methods, which implies that the AdaMSS itself has superior learning capability. We attribute this superiority to the use of the SSL strategy and data-driven fusion strategy, which can be evidenced by ablation studies.

The benefits of jointly learning tumor segmentation and survival prediction have been well demonstrated in previous studies22–25, but few studies explored the intrinsic destructive interference between them. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that identifies this destructive interference and hence introduces the SSL strategy as an improvement. In the learning strategy analysis (Table 2), compared to using the STL strategy, the MTL strategy improved survival prediction but degraded tumor segmentation, which indicates the existence of potential destructive interference. Our SSL strategy overcame this destructive interference by decoupling tumor segmentation and survival prediction to different training stages, thereby surpassing the MTL strategy in both survival prediction and tumor segmentation. The SSL strategy is based on transfer learning45, a widely used learning strategy to leverage shared knowledge between tasks. There have been studies comparing MTL and transfer learning46, but no study explored this for survival prediction and tumor segmentation. In addition, despite the simplicity of transfer learning, many efforts have been made to enable it to work. For segmentation-to-survival learning, a unified model applicable to two distinct tasks should be designed. To achieve this, we made two key designs in the AdaMSS, rendering it as a model that can adaptively fit into two-stage segmentation-to-survival learning. The Ada-focus decoder serves as a key design for the AdaMSS to enable adaptive adjustment of its focus regions during training. As shown in Fig. 4, the attention maps produced in the Ada-focus decoder were changing during segmentation-to-survival learning and ultimately included both the tumor and its peripherical regions after the training in Stage-II. This can partly explain the superior performance of our AdaMSS: it can capture more prognostic information within and around the tumors while getting less interference from irrelevant backgrounds. The captured information around tumors may represent the heterogeneity in the tumor periphery14 or the potential adjacent tissue invasion17,18.

Another key design to facilitate segmentation-to-survival learning is the Ada-fusion encoder that employs data-driven fusion. Multi-modality images have been widely used for survival prediction. Early and late fusion are the most common strategies, where multi-modality images are concatenated as multi-channel inputs (early fusion)2,22–24,34,36 or enter multiple independent encoders with resultant features fused (late fusion)28,29,37. However, early fusion has difficulties in extracting intra-modality information due to concatenated images for feature extraction, while late fusion has difficulties in extracting inter-modality information due to fully independent feature extraction. Recently, intermediate fusion was leveraged for survival prediction and showed better utilization/integration of multi-modality images38, where multi-modality images were fed into multiple encoders with intermediate features fused at multiple scales via sophisticated fusion modules. However, as mentioned above, existing fusion strategies were empirically designed and inherently limited. In the fusion strategy analysis (Table 3), the empirically-designed fusion strategies showed inconsistent performance in tumor segmentation and survival prediction, which suggests that these two tasks have different requirements for fusion strategies and the optimal fusion strategy should vary depending on the tasks. With the data-driven strategy, our AdaMSS can adaptively optimize its fusion strategy during training, thus enabling it to achieve the best performance in both survival prediction and tumor segmentation. For survival prediction, the early and late fusion strategies failed to outperform using PET alone, which is consistent with the findings in Wang et al.’s study39. The intermediate fusion strategy allowed information to be fused at multiple scales and thus achieved better performance. Nevertheless, the intermediate fusion strategy is still limited to manually defined prior knowledge and was defeated by our data-driven fusion strategy. In Table 4, we attempted to interpret the data-driven fusion strategy and found that the AdaMSS adapted its fusion strategies for different training stages and datasets, which demonstrates the adaptability and generalizability of our data-driven fusion strategy to different tasks and data. According to our literature review, this is the first study that introduces a data-driven fusion strategy for survival prediction.

Our study has a few limitations. First, by using a data-driven fusion strategy, we expect that our AdaMSS can search over a large search space to find the optimal fusion strategy based on training data. Therefore, despite the current search space being vast and including commonly adopted fusion strategies, it could be further expanded to include the latest fusion operations in the future. Second, this study focused on using PET/CT for survival prediction in OPC and NPC. Our AdaMSS can be further validated with other diseases (e.g., brain gliomas, lung cancer) or other imaging modalities (e.g., multi-parametric MRI, PET/MRI). Moreover, in addition to performing patient stratification in the entire population, subgroup stratification analyses on different TNM stages are also insightful. However, as the OPC dataset does not provide information on the TNM stage, we cannot perform stratification analyses in the subgroups of OPC patients; the NPC dataset contains patients with TNM stage III and IVa, which were typically regarded as one group (named locoregionally advanced NPC) in the clinical trials47–49.

Methods

Problem definition

This study aims to predict the survival outcomes of patients from pretreatment PET/CT images with the available clinical indicators (e.g., age, gender, TNM stage). For the OPC dataset, the prediction target is RFS, which is defined as the time from treatment to disease recurrence (including local, regional, and distant metastasis) or death. For the NPC dataset, the prediction target is PFS, which is defined as the time from treatment to disease progression or death.

Data acquisition and preprocessing

The OPC dataset was derived from the HECKTOR 2022 challenge and its acquisition details are provided in ref. 6 The training dataset of HECKTOR 2022 was adopted in this study, while the testing dataset was excluded as its ground-truth labels were not released to the public. All patients were acquired from seven medical centers (CHUP, CHUS, MDA, HGJ, HMR, CHUM, and CHUV). As shown in Fig. 2, the patients from two medical centers (CHUM and CHUV) were used for testing and the patients from the remaining five medical centers were used for training, which followed the same data split settings used in the previous study50 and split the data into 386/102 patients as training/testing sets.

The NPC dataset was acquired from FUSCC and SPHIC between May 2009 and May 2019. All patients received concomitant systemic treatment with intensity modulated radiotherapy (IMRT). After completion of treatment, each patient was followed up every 3 months in the first 2 years, then every 6 months in the third to fifth year, and annually thereafter. The median follow-up time is 50 months (ranging from 44 to 120 months) for FUSCC and 49 months (ranging from 44 to 97 months) for SPHIC. FUSCC and SPHIC Ethical Committee approved this study with informed consent obtained from all included patients. PET/CT images were obtained on a Siemens biograph 16HR PET/CT scanner (Knoxville, Tennessee, USA), and the PET/CT acquisition procedure was detailed in Supplementary Note 1. As shown in Fig. 2, the patients from FUSCC and SPHIC were used for training and testing respectively, which split the data into 657/235 patients as training/testing sets.

For both OPC and NPC datasets, we performed univariate and multivariate Cox analyses on the clinical indicators of the training sets, so as to screen out the prognostic indicators with significant relevance to RFS/PFS (P < 0.05) and leverage them for survival prediction. PET/CT images were resampled into isotropic voxels, where 1 voxel corresponds to 1 mm3, and then cropped to 160 × 160 × 160 voxels with the tumor located in the center. PET images were converted into Standardized Uptake Value (SUV) maps based on body mass and then were individually standardized using Z-score normalization, while CT images were first clipped to range [−1024, 1024] and then mapped to range [−1, 1].

Adaptive multi-modality segmentation-to-survival model (AdaMSS)

Our AdaMSS is an adaptive model for both tumor segmentation and survival prediction. The Ada-focus decoder adopts attention gate modules51 to adaptively focus on task-related target regions. Segmentation-to-survival learning

Radiomics feature extraction

With the tumor segmentation masks, we extracted handcrafted radiomics features from PET/CT images via Pyradiomics52. The extracted radiomics features include 19 features from First Order Statistics (FOS), 24 features from Grey-Level Cooccurrence Matrix (GLCM), 16 features from Grey-Level Run Length Matrix (GLRLM), 16 features from Grey-Level Size Zone Matrix (GLSZM), 5 features from Neighboring Grey Tone Difference Matrix (NGTDM), and 16 features based on 3D tumor shape. The shape-based features were extracted from the tumor segmentation mask, while other features were extracted from PET and CT images separately. The PET/CT-derived features were also recomputed for different wavelet decomposition of PET/CT. Performing low-pass (L) or high-pass (H) wavelet filter along x, y, or z directions resulted in eight decompositions (LLL, LLH, LHL, LHH, HHH, HLL, HHL, and HLH). Consequently, we derived a total of 1456 radiomics features. All radiomics features were standardized using Z-score normalization. The redundant features with Spearman’s correlation >0.7 were eliminated, followed by LASSO regression32 for further feature selection. Note that the feature standardization and selection processes were performed on the training sets and then directly applied to the testing sets.

Training and inference procedure

Our AdaMSS was implemented using PyTorch on a 12GB NVIDIA Titan V GPU. The Adam optimizer with a batch size of 2 was used for both pretraining (Stage-I) and fine-tuning (Stage-II). Each training batch included a censored sample and an uncensored sample. The AdaMSS was pretrained for 10,000 iterations in Stage-I and then fine-tuned for another 10,000 iterations in Stage-II. In Stage-I, the learning rate was set as 1e-4 initially and then reset to 5e − 5, 1e − 5 at the 4000th, 8000th training iteration. In Stage-II, the learning rate was set as 5e-5 initially and then reset to 1e−5, 1e−6 at the 4000th, 8000th training iteration. We performed data augmentation in real-time during training to minimize overfitting, including random affine transformations and random cropping to 112 × 112 × 112 voxels. For both OPC and NPC datasets, models were trained and cross-validated using 5-fold cross-validation within the training set, and then were evaluated in the testing set. Validation was performed after every 200 training iterations and the model weights achieving the best validation results in Stage-I and Stage-II were preserved. For inference, the weights preserved in Stage-I were used for tumor segmentation and radiomics feature extraction, while the weights preserved in Stage-II were used for the final survival prediction. For a fair comparison, the compared deep survival models, including MDSN34, DeepMTLR-CoxPH35, TMSS24, DeepMTS23, and Radio-DeepMTS36, were trained by adopting their official open-source codes under the same cross-validation training procedure as ours and then were evaluated in the testing set.

Performance evaluation and statistical analysis

The Concordance index (C-index) and Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) were used to evaluate the survival prediction and tumor segmentation performance, which are the commonly used evaluation metrics in related studies15,16,22–24,34,35 and also are the standard evaluation metrics for related challenges/competitions5,6,56. For statistical analysis, a two-sided \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P$$\end{document}P value < 0.05 is considered to indicate a statistically significant difference. All the statistical tests were performed using R packages (version 3.6.3, http://www.R-project. org). We adopted Wilcoxon tests to compare the segmentation performance and adopted the compareC package (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/compareC/index.html)57 to compare the survival prediction performance.

Patient stratification

Nomograms based on AdaMSS were built to predict the individual conditional risk of 5-year recurrence for OPC and NPC patients. The calibration plot comparing the predicted versus observed probability was used to assess the accuracy. Survival analyses based on Kaplan–Meier method were performed for patient group stratification. Patients were stratified into high- or low-risk groups based on their predicted survival outcomes. The threshold values were chosen based on the Receiver Operator Characteristic (ROC) curves on the training sets and then applied to the testing sets. Two-sided log-rank tests were applied to measure the differences between the survival outcomes of the two patient groups, where a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P$$\end{document}P value < 0.05 indicates that there exist statistically significant differences. The Hazard Ratio (HR) was used to evaluate the stratification performance, where a higher HR indicates that the high-risk group has a larger hazard rate (i.e., the rate of disease progression or death) relative to the low-risk group.
