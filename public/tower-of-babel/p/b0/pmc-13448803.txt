Whole genome sequencing-based characterization of Escherichia coli isolated from raw beef in selected butcher shops in Addis Ababa and Burayu, Ethiopia
Authors: Negga Asamene Abera, Eyasu Tigabu Seyoum, Rajiha Abubaker Ibrahim, Tolewak Sura, Desta Ayana, Chala Basha, Abraham Ali, Melak Getu, Gebremedhin Gebremichael, Eleni Kidane, Abebe Aseffa Negeri, Gemechu Tadesse, Aklilu Feleke Haile, Getachew Tollera, Atsbeha Gebreegziabxier Weldemariam, Tadesse Eguale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEscherichia coli (E. coli) is a commensal bacterium commonly found in the intestines of humans and animals. Certain pathogenic E. coli strains can lead to severe food poisoning. While different studies have been conducted in Ethiopia with regard to the contamination of food of animal origin with E. coil, there are limited studies that tend to characterize E. coli at a genomic level. The objective of this study was to assess the diversity, virulence profile, pathotypes and antimicrobial resistance markers of E. coli isolated from raw beef samples. A total of 16 E. coli isolates were subject to whole genome sequence analysis. The genomes were analyzed using different bioinformatics tools including AMRFinderPlus, and PlasmidFinder that were used to identify Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes and plasmids, respectively. The two major serotypes identified were O157:H7 (25%) and O82:H8 (12.5%) accounting for 37.5% of all the isolates sequenced. The sequenced E. coli isolates were classified into 13 sequence types (ST) of which ST11 was the predominant one and it was exclusively isolated from the beef samples from Burayu butcher shop. Seven (43.8%) of the E. coli were diarrheagenic E. coli pathotypes (Enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC), n = 4, and Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC), n = 3). Two STEC isolates were assigned to ST101. Two major virulence genes were detected in both pathotypes-eae (4/16) and stx1(3/16). Antimicrobial resistance genetic markers carried on mobile genetic elements such as aph (3’’)-Ib (n = 3), aph(6)-Id (n = 2), sul2 (n = 2), tet(B) (n = 3), and blaTEM-1 (n = 3) were frequently detected in the study isolates. In conclusion, this study revealed that E. coli isolates obtained from meat samples were clonally related within the specified geographic location and certain sequence types appear to be found in different location indicating potential cross contamination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54207-7.Keywords: STEC, EPEC, raw beef, Whole-genome sequencing, Virulence gene profiles, Antimicrobial resistance, EthiopiaSubject terms: Genetics, Microbiology, Molecular biology

Introduction

E. coli strains are commensals inhabiting the intestinal tract of humans and other warm-blooded animals, and they rarely cause diseases. However, some strains of E. coli, maintain pathogenicity that implicate them as one of the most frequent causes of several common bacterial infections in humans and animals1.

Diarrheagenic E. coli isolates are classified into six groups: Shiga toxin (Stx)-producing (STEC), enterotoxigenic (ETEC), enteropathogenic (EPEC), enteroaggregative (EAEC), enteroinvasive (EIEC), and diffusely adherent (DAEC). Over 200 serotypes of E. coli have been known to carry stx genes and these genes confer the ability to produce Shiga toxin (Stx)-one of the most potent bacterial toxins known2. Depending on biochemical and molecular characteristics, Shiga toxins are grouped into two types, stx1 and stx2. The gene coding for these toxins is carried by lambdoid bacteriophages integrated into the E. coli chromosome3. Despite high degree of amino acid sequence similarity (95% to 98.3%), Stx1 is classified into four distinct sub-groups (stx1a, stx1c, stx1d, and stx1e). However, Stx2 toxins are considerably more heterogeneous (50–60% amino acid identity), and as a result fifteen Stx2 subtypes (stx2a to stx2o) have been reported so far. These different stx subtypes are reported to differ in their host distribution characteristics and in the severity of diseases associated with these genes4.

Shiga toxin producing E. coli O157:H7 is one of the most pathogenic strains of Shiga toxin producing E. coli. This pathotype was first recognized as a pathogen in 1982 during investigations of an outbreak of hemorrhagic colitis in Michigan and Oregon, USA5. Currently, Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC) have more than 470 serotypes6. About a quarter of countries in Africa have reported STEC O157:H7 from humans, animals, food or the environment7.

While Enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC) is considered as one of the major causes of diarrhea in infants in developing countries, this important zoonotic pathogen has also been recently reported in the developed parts of the world. The development of proper therapeutic interventions as well as improvement of sanitary condition were credited for the decline of EPEC outbreaks in the early 2000s. Despite such commendable success stories, recent food-borne disease outbreak related surveillance data suggests that EPEC appears to re-emerge, once again, as a significant human pathogen8.

Available reports indicate that there are eight major phylogroups (A, B1, B2, C, D, E, F, G, ) and cryptic Escherichia clades (I to V) in E. coli with each phylogroup characteristically differing in their phenotypic and genotypic features, ecological niches, ability to cause disease, and tendency to harbor antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes. These phylogroups also tend to vary in their transmission dynamics and host range. For example, humans most commonly harbor phylogroups A, B2, D while phylogroup B1 is prevalent in animals and the environment9. Diverse sequence types (ST) of E. coli including ST131, ST38, ST648, and ST354, have been reported from chicken meat, pork, and human infection samples. E. coli ST131, belonging to phylogroup B2, is reported to be the dominant ST in human samples, while ST38, belonging to phylogroup D, are most prevalent in meat samples10.

The global epidemiology depicts that antimicrobial resistance is commonly detected from commensal bacteria isolated from food-producing animals. This phenomenon has triggered critical queries to document the implications of antimicrobial use in animals and the possible transmission of resistant bacteria to humans through the food value chain11. The use of antimicrobials that are critically important in human medicine for treatment and prevention of diseases in animal husbandry is believed to contribute to the emergence and spread of new strains of multidrug-resistant foodborne bacteria, such as extended spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing E. coli11.

The use of whole genome sequence data has made it possible to have an increased understanding of the different types of virulence factors, mechanisms of antimicrobial resistance and underlying species diversity and the tracking of foodborne disease outbreaks12. A few studies have been conducted in Ethiopia13–16 with regard to the contamination of food of animal origin with E. coli; however, there is limited study that tends to characterize E. coli at a genomic level17. Therefore, this study aims to fill this gap in knowledge and provide information that would help design appropriate intervention to control the spread of pathogenic and antimicrobial resistant E. coli in the food value chain in Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

A cross-sectional study was conducted from August 2022 to December 2022, in Addis Ababa city, and Burayu town Ethiopia where, different information and carcass swab samples were collected from conveniently selected butcher shops. The sequenced isolates were obtained from a total of 30 isolates and sample collection and bacterial identification was conducted based on standard protocol as indicated in the previously published paper18.

DNA extraction, library preparation and whole-genome sequencing (WGS)

The confirmed E. coli isolates were sub-cultured on blood agar plates (BAP) to obtain fresh colonies. DNA was then extracted from these pure colonies using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

The concentration of the extracted DNA was measured using High Sensitivity (HS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and Qubit™FlexFluorometer, following the manufacturer’s standard instruction. DNA libraries were prepared using the Illumina DNA Prep Kit (Illumina, Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, bacterial genomes underwent tagmentation using bead-linked transposons and tagmentation buffer 1 (TB 1), which were simultaneously fragmented and tagged the DNA with adapter sequences. Unique dual-index adapters were attached to the tagmented DNA fragments to allow multiplexing, and library cleanup was performed using Illumina Purification Beads (IPB) to selectively retain DNA fragments of optimal size for sequencing. The final library concentration was measured using the Qubit™ 1X dsDNA High Sensitivity (HS) Assay kit. The libraries were then normalized and pooled in equimolar amounts, and subsequently diluted to the starting loading concentration according to the Illumina NextSeq 550 standard protocol. The pooled library was denatured using freshly prepared 0.2 N sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and diluted with hybridization buffer (HT1) to a final concentration of 1.3 pM. The library was then spiked with 1% by 1.3 pM PhiX control, prepared at the same concentration, to monitor run quality. A final volume of 1300 µL library was loaded onto the Illumina NextSeq 550 platform and sequenced using 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads.

Bioinformatic analysis

Assembly

The generated FASTQ reads were quality-checked using FastQC (https://github.com/Morgan-Feuz/fastqc), and low-quality bases and adapter sequences were trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.39) (https://github.com/usadellab/Trimmomatic). High-quality reads were assembled de novo using the SKESA assembler (v2.4.0) (https://github.com/ncbi/SKESA). The draft genomes exhibited an average GC content of approximately 50%, consistent with reference E. coli genomes. Overall assembly quality was strong, with most assemblies displaying good contiguity, typically comprising 125–470 contigs, and only two genomes exceeding this range. Total genome sizes ranged from approximately 4.6 to 5.7 Mb, which falls within the expected genome size range for E. coli. Assemblies meeting quality thresholds were retained for downstream analyses. Assembly completeness was further evaluated using BUSCO (v5.7.1) (https://github.com/Rowena-h/BUSCOfilter), which demonstrated that the majority of assemblies achieved > 99% completeness, confirming their suitability for comparative and functional genomic analyses. Species confirmation was performed using GAMBIT taxonomic classification (v1.0.0) to verify the assignment of isolates as E. coli.

Antimicrobial resistance and virulence

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes were identified using AMRFinderPlus (v4.0.23) with data base version 2025-06-03.1(https://github.com/ncbi/amr), based on the NCBI Reference Gene Catalog19. In addition, Plasmid-associated replicons were identified using PlasmidFinder (v2.1.6) ( https://github.com/genomicepidemiology/plasmidfinder), supporting the investigation of mobile genetic elements that contribute to the dissemination of antimicrobial resistance and virulence traits20.

Molecular serotyping, phylogrouping, and sequence typing

Genome-based molecular serotyping was performed by identifying O-antigen and H-antigen biosynthesis genes from whole-genome sequence data, enabling assignment of isolates to established E. coli serotypes. Phylogenetic grouping was determined using the Clermont typing scheme, classifying isolates into major E. coli phylogroups. Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) was conducted in silico to assign sequence types (STs) and corresponding clonal complexes based on allelic profiles.

Pathotype determination

To assess pathogenic potential, genomes were screened for key virulence genes associated with major E. coli pathotypes. In this study, pathotype assignment focused on detection of eae and stx genes. Isolates lacking these defining markers were classified as non-typable.

Phylogenomic analysis of Escherichia coli isolates

Whole-genome phylogenetic relationships were inferred using kSNP4, a k-mer based single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) (https://github.com/USDA-VS/nvsl_bioinformatic_training/blob/main/docs/ksnp.md)21. Genome assemblies were analyzed with kSNP4 to identify core SNPs across all isolates. The optimal k-mer size was determined using the built-in Kchooser utility. Core SNPs were used to construct a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree. Core-genome SNP-based phylogenetic tree was constructed using kSNP4 based on 43,840 SNPs in all the study isolates. The resulting tree was midpoint-rooted and visualized using iTOL (Interactive Tree of Life) for annotation and presentation.

Results

Overall genome characteristics and serotypes of E. coil isolates

In the current study, a total of 30 confirmed E. coli isolates recovered from raw beef meat were sequenced of which 16 passed sequence quality check. The two major serotypes identified were O157:H7 (n = 4) and O82:H8 (n = 2) accounting for 37% of all the isolates that passed sequence quality check (n = 16).

All assembled genomes had an average GC content of approximately 50%, consistent with E. coli reference genomes. The number of contigs ranged from 1,126 to 87,969, across isolates. The total genome lengths were within the expected size range for E. coli genomes, although two assemblies showed lower completeness. Of the total isolates analyzed, 16 strains were confirmed as E. coli, and no ambiguous (non-ATCG) nucleotides were detected in the assemblies (supplementary Table 1).

Distribution of diarrheagenic pathotypes, sequence types, phylogroup and diversity of E. coli strains

Of the 19 pathotypes that were previously identified using PCR, 42.1% of them were consistently detected by WGS pathotyping (Unpublished data). Two major pathotypes were identified, EPEC (4/16) and STEC (3/16). The sequence analysis resulted in 13 sequence types (STs) and 6 clonal complexes (CCs). Two out of the three STEC isolates belong to clonal complex ST101CPlx with a sequence type assignment to ST101 and ST101-1VL (Table-1). In the case of EPEC, the predominant sequence type was ST 11 (n = 3) of which one isolate was assigned to ST11-3VL. Majority of the isolates belonged to Phylogroup B1(10/16; 62.5%) whereas 4 out of 16 (25.0%) and 2 out of 16 (12.5%) were phylogroup E and A respectively (Table 1).

Distribution of pathotypes among study sites and abattoirs

The sequence analysis of the studied isolates depicts that sequence type eleven (ST11) was exclusively identified from meat samples collected from Burayu butcher shop. This dominance was also observed for isolates recovered from Gulele sub-city of Addis Ababa that tend to cluster into the same sequence type-ST11 (Fig. 1). Similarly, out of the four EPECs identified, three of them were ST11 isolated from Burayu butcher shops. Isolates from Kera abattoir (Addis Ababa) originated meat samples tends to be characteristically diverse in their sequence type. The exception to this finding is the two STEC isolates from Kera abattoir origin meat that were assigned to similar ST101.

Distribution of virulence genes among the sequenced pathotypes

Two major virulence genes were detected among the pathotypes, with eae present in 4 of 16 isolates and stx1 in 3 of 16 isolates. The gene eae was exclusively associated with EPEC while stx1 was specifically found in all the STEC pathotypes. Of the three STEC isolates, three of them had stx1a subtype (Table 2). The pathogenicity islands iha virulence gene was also found in (7/16) isolates. In addition, various Inc-type plasmids were found in the sequenced isolates. The most common identified Inc type plasmid was IncFIB (AP001918) (8/16) followed by IncI1-I(Alpha) (6/16); IncFII (6/16) and IncX1 (3/16). The plasmid type IncX1 was mostly carrying tet (A) and bla TEM-1 genes.

Antimicrobial resistance gene distribution among the different pathotypes of the isolates

Antimicrobial resistance genes associated with antibiotic classes were screened from the whole-genome sequences of the 16 E. coli isolates. None of the isolates have the genes that confer resistance to carbapenems, trimethoprim, rifampicin, macrolides, colistin, and glycopeptides. A single STEC isolate harbored aph (3’’)-Ib gene while two isolates (both neither of the pathotypes) had gene combination (aph(6)-Id; aph(3’’)-Ib) that is known to be associated with resistance to the aminoglycoside and streptomycin. On other hand, a single isolate was found to harbor six genes (aph (3’’)-Ib, qnrD1, sul2, tet(J), catA1, blaTEM-1) that confer resistance to a range of antimicrobial classes while none of the EPEC isolates carry any of AMR genes (Table 3). One isolate, designated as E. coli O113:H40 by serotype, has exhibited a mutation soxS_A12S encoding for multidrug resistance phenotype. This resistance gene was associated with Col(pHAD28) Inc type plasmid. Moreover, all the isolates harbored genes conferring efflux and beta-lactam resistance (Table 4).

Discussion

While a number of studies have reported the occurrence of E. coil and E. coli O157:H7 in different sample types in Ethiopia13–16, to the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the very few studies17 conducted in Ethiopia that reported the use of whole genome sequencing method to document clonal diversity of E. coli isolates with their AMR and other virulence gene profile from food of animal origin.

Shiga toxin producing E. coli are well-documented zoonotic bacterial pathogens, accountable for significant foodborne disease outbreaks around the world. Bovines are asymptomatic carriers of STEC, responsible for their spread22. Here, we report higher prevalence of EPEC (25%) and STEC (18.8%) compared to a study in South Africa23. Our finding on STEC is comparable to another recent study from Ethiopia24 which was (14.3%).

In this study, an attempt was made to explore the clonal diversity of E. coli pathotypes that were isolated from raw beef meat from Addis Ababa and Burayu town. Whole genome sequence analysis of these isolates revealed that there were two major pathotypes, STEC and EPEC, potentially circulating between abattoirs and butchers in the study sites. In addition, from the total isolates, 11 sequence types and 6 CCs were detected indicating high degree of clonal diversity. These two pathotypes have distinct disease manifestations with STEC being characterized by haemolytic uremic syndrome, bloody diarrhea, and haemorrhagic colitis while the EPEC pathotype, with the eae gene, is known for causing severe diarrhea in children in developing countries23. With regard to STEC from the current study, the majorly identified sequence type was ST101 with clonal complex ST11CPlx. Sequence type 11 is reported as a common sequence type circulating between humans and animals25. In the present study, the second frequently identified sequence type was ST 101 which is common causes of urinary tract infections in human. A population-based study from USA that aimed to understand the possible transmission dynamics of uropathogenic E. coli between clinical cases and retail meat have reported that ST101 was among the common sequence types in urine from patients and meat samples26. Similarly, this ST was reported in Iran from inpatients with UTIs27 and blood stream infection in Brazil28. In addition, ST101 has also been reported to be among the major outbreak strains within the Danish broiler production system29. These evidences indicate that ST101 is of greater public health importance because of its ability to cross link between humans and animals. In contrast, a study in Ethiopia reported that the most common sequence types included ST10, ST3576, ST155, and ST227 to which none of our isolates belongs to24. One possible explanation for the observed variation could be the source of samples: while our samples were taken from raw beef, those of Chekole et al.24 originated from diarrheic calves only.

None of the STEC from the present study belonged to ST11. In contrast, recent study from Brazil identified E. coli O157:H7 (STEC) from ground beef that belonged to ST1130. Regardless of the level of contamination, occurrence of STEC can lead to foodborne illness outbreaks, especially considering the virulence and severity of E. coli O157:H7 ST11 lineage30. Interestingly, all of the EPEC pathotypes in our study belonged to ST11 and phylogenetic group E1 while all of the STEC are clustered under group B1 suggesting that these isolates are closely related evolutionarily. The sequence analysis of these isolates revealed that certain sequence types tend to clonally cluster within the same geographic area which indicate potential cross-contamination and transmission of the bacteria during meat processing and handling and this is a significant public health concern31.

Characteristically, STEC and EPEC are known to have their major virulence factors to be carried on mobile genetic elements (MGE). Genes that encode stx are generally carried by lambdoid prophages, which are frequently inducible and transmissible32. In this regard, the current study revealed that the two major virulence genes detected in both pathotypes were eae and stx1. The gene eae was exclusively associated with EPEC while stx1 was specifically found in all the STEC pathotypes. All the STEC isolates in this study did not have eae gene. While the eae gene (encoding intimin) is a key virulence factor in many Shiga toxin-producing E. coli strains, the absence of this gene does not normally make them less virulent since they can still cause severe infections through other mechanisms of intestinal attachment such as efa1 (enterohemorrhagic E. coli factor for adherence), paa (porcine attaching and effacing associated), iha (Vibrio cholerae IrgA homolog), ompA (outer membrane protein A), lpfA (long polar fimbriae), fimA (type 1 fimbriae), and hes (hemagglutinin from Shiga toxin-producing E. coli)33,34. In addition, of all the virulence genes carried by the current study isolates, espx1 (in 14 isolates) and fdeC (in all isolates) occurred at higher frequencies. A recent systematic review from Africa focusing on exploring virulence gene profile of E. coli indicated that none of the studies in Africa used whole genome sequence methods to determine virulence profile. These studies used PCR and some of the virulence genes reported include saa, iha, ipfA0113, katP and espP which we also reported in the current study35.

The resistance analysis of sequence data revealed the absence of antimicrobial resistance genes that confer resistance to certain clinically important antibiotic classes such as carbapenems, trimethoprim, rifampicin, macrolides, colistin, and glycopeptides. However, some of the isolates in the present study harbored a range of resistance genes for some critical antibiotics. Most importantly, the presence of aph (6)-Id and aph (3’’)-Ib genes would predict the ability of some of the isolates to show resistance to aminoglycosides. The use of aminoglycosides in human medicine have resulted in the emergence of bacteria that are resistant to these classes of antibiotics. Aminoglycoside-Modifying Enzymes (AMEs) are notably the most relevant resistance mechanisms in clinical setting36. They are usually categorized into aminoglycoside N-acetyltransferases (AACs), aminoglycoside O-nucleotidyltransferases (ANTs), and aminoglycoside phosphotransferases (APHs)36. The genes, aph (6)-Id and aph (3’’)-Ib, are among the many resistance genes that have been reported to be highly prevalent in enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC) isolates in previous studies36. In addition, three out of the 16 isolates from the present study harbored bla TEM-1 which is of high public health relevance. Recent study conducted in Ethiopia also revealed that genes encoding for acquired resistance of ampicillin, amoxicillin, cephalothin, piperacillin, and ticarcillin (blaTEM-1B) were detected from E. coli isolates recovered from lake water, fish and humans14.

Resistance to sulfonamides is normally encoded in gene like the sul1and sul2. Mobile genetic elements can facilitate the transfer of such genes from commensal bacteria to humans through ingestion of meat-based products23. Our study showed that only two isolates harbored sul2 gene (10.5%) while 6% was reported in a study from south Africa23. Although lower level of resistance genes (sul2) is reported in the current study, sulfonamides resistance genes are commonly reported to exist at higher level in association with domesticated animals, humans and in environments23.

A relatively higher level of tetracycline resistance genes (25%) in our study may imply over use of these antibiotics in animal husbandry in the study area. In line with this, previous study documented that oxyteracycline is the highest consumed antibiotics in Ethiopia37.

While the AMR gene profile aligns with the phenotypic antimicrobial resistance profile of most the isolates18 in the current study, the presence of a single isolate with six different antimicrobial resistance genes is of great concern. Interestingly, one isolate, designated as E. coli O113:H40 by serotype, has exhibited the soxS_A12S -a multidrug resistant gene that is known to confer resistance to ampicillin, chloram- phenicol, quinolone, rifampin, and tetracycline. Similar gene was also reported in E. coli isolates form humans and animals38,39.

Plasmid replicon typing revealed a diverse range of incompatibility (Inc) groups among the E. coli isolates, reflecting a rich mobilome contributing to antimicrobial resistance (AMR) dissemination and adaptation. The most frequently observed Inc group was IncFIB (AP001918), which often co-occurred with IncFII and IncI1-I(Alpha). IncF-type plasmids are narrow-host-range, conjugative plasmids highly adapted to E. coli and are key drivers of clinically relevant AMR gene dissemination, including tet(A), bla TEM-1, and bla CTX-M-15, and other extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs)40,41. Other important plasmid types detected in six of our study isolates (N10S31, N14S35, N16S37, N17S38, N18S39 and N20S41) is IncI1-I(Alpha), which has a broader host range and facilitates horizontal transfer of resistance genes between Enterobacterales, and IncX1, which has been linked to carbapenemase and colistin resistance gene mobilization. A significant public health implication of E. coli with IncI1 plasmids is their potential to harbor antimicrobial resistance genes that confer resistance to critically important antimicrobials used to treat severe cases of enteric infections, including the third-generation cephalosporins42. IncB/O/K/Z plasmids were also detected, often co-carried with IncFIB and IncX1, indicating multi-replicon carriage that can stabilize resistance determinants. This plasmid is reported to have been associated with resistance to azithromycin and mediates transmission of virulence plasmid in Klebsiella pneumoniae43.

Additionally, several small Col plasmids (Col(pHAD28), Col3M, Col156) were identified, which may encode toxin-antitoxin systems, virulence factors, or additional resistance genes. Interestingly, some isolates (n = 5) were found to have no detectable plasmid replicons, suggesting that their resistance phenotype (if present) may be mediated by chromosomally encoded mechanisms or plasmids below the detection threshold of the replicon typing assay. The detection of multiple co-existing plasmid replicons within individual isolates highlights the potential for co-selection and persistence of multiple ARGs and also the survival rate of strains carrying such plasmid tends to improve44. Overall, the plasmid diversity observed underscores the role of plasmid-mediated horizontal gene transfer in maintaining and spreading AMR among E. coli populations in the study isolates.

In conclusion, the whole genome sequence analysis of E. coli isolates recovered form raw beef revealed that the isolates are clonally related within the specified geographic locations and existence of specific sequence types-ST11 in both Burayu town and Addis Ababa city indicates potential cross contamination. While all of the EPEC isolates tend to lack the targeted AMR genes and one isolate with multiple resistance genes was found, more than a quarter of other non-O175:H7 serotypes were found to harbor a range of AMR genes including one isolate with multidrug resistance gene prediction. Educating butchers on hygienic handling of carcass would help reduce the transmission of pathogenic E. coli in the meat value chain.
