Species of Fusarium and Neocosmospora associated with citrus branch diseases in China
Authors: XE Xiao, MJ Cheng, F Zheng, YJ Ao, JR Deng, PW Crous, C Jiao, HY Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpecies of Fusarium and related fusarioid genera are widely distributed and commonly associated with diverse plant hosts, either as saprobes, endophytes, or pathogens. Although fusarioid fungi have been implicated in citrus diseases worldwide, their diversity and pathogenic potential on citrus in China remain poorly characterized. To investigate their species diversity, extensive field surveys were conducted in several citrus-producing regions of China between 2021 and 2024. Symptomatic tissues exhibiting dieback, necrosis, gummosis, canker, and wood decay were collected from twigs, branches, and trunks. Based on multi-locus phylogenetic analyses and morphological characteristics, 336 fusarioid isolates from eight provinces were assigned to six Fusarium species complexes and the genus Neocosmospora, comprising 21 known species (15 Fusarium spp. and six Neocosmospora spp.) and three novel species, namely F. gummosum, N. reticulatae and N. unshiu. Among the known species, 13 are reported from Citrus spp. for the first time, and two represent new records for China. Pathogenicity tests revealed that most fusarioid isolates were pathogenic to citrus branches, with N. unshiu and F. asiaticum being the most aggressive, inducing gummosis and cortex and xylem necrosis, consistent with symptoms observed in the field. Notably, N. unshiu was found to be the dominant pathogen causing citrus trunk canker in Hanzhong City, Shaanxi Province, where it is widely distributed and causes significant damage to citrus. INTRODUCTION

The genus Fusarium represents one of the most ecologically and economically significant groups within the kingdom Fungi. With a broad host range and cosmopolitan distribution, Fusarium species occupy diverse ecological niches, functioning as saprobes, endophytes, and, most notably, aggressive plant pathogens (Leslie & Summerell 2006, Aoki et al. 2014). The genus has attracted considerable attention in the scientific community, ranking as the fourth most cited fungal genus (Bhunjun et al. 2024). It is also listed among the top 10 most important fungal plant pathogens globally (Dean et al. 2012), underscoring its dual importance in both fundamental research and agriculture. Fusarium species are responsible for a wide spectrum of destructive plant diseases, including maize stalk rot, wheat head blight, bean root rot, tomato wilt, and Panama disease of banana, many of which have caused substantial losses in global crop production (Maryani et al. 2019, Srinivas et al. 2019, Han et al. 2023, Erima et al. 2025).

Fusarium was first described by Link (1809) and was originally characterized by producing elongated, fusoid, multiseptate conidia. Early taxonomic classifications were based primarily on morphological features, including cultural characteristics and the microanatomy of the asexual morph (Gerlach & Nirenberg 1982, Nelson et al. 1983). However, due to its high morphological plasticity and the lack of standardized diagnostic criteria, the taxonomy of Fusarium remained problematic (O’Donnell 1993). A major breakthrough occurred in 1935, when Wollenweber & Reinking (1935) proposed a comprehensive morphological classification system that divided the genus into multiple sections and species based on detailed conidial morphology and cultural characteristics. Over the following decades, the morphological classification system of Fusarium was refined, leading to the development of multiple classification frameworks (Leslie & Summerell 2006). With the advent of molecular techniques, more reliable and objective methods for classification have emerged, but debates over the broad versus narrow genus concept of Fusarium persist (Gräfenhan et al. 2011, Schroers et al. 2011, Geiser et al. 2013, 2021, O’Donnell et al. 2020, Crous et al. 2021). Subsequent phylogenomic studies have, however, shown that the morphologically defined genera are in fact separate entities that correlate with their ecology, lifestyle, biochemistry, and distinct sexual morphs (Hill et al. 2022, Chen et al. 2023, Gomez-Chavarria et al. 2024, Ulaszewski et al. 2025).

Citrus spp. are fruit crops of global importance, playing a crucial role in agricultural economies as well as international trade. Previous studies have demonstrated the widespread occurrence of Fusarium and related fusarioid genera in Citrus spp., where they are associated with a range of symptoms, including dry root rot, wilting, gum exudation, vascular discolouration and general decline (Ezrari et al. 2022, Badiwe et al. 2025). Surveys conducted in major citrus-growing regions of Greece, Italy and Spain identified 11 Fusarium species from tissues affected by dry root rot, including four novel taxa. Among these, F. sarcochroum, F. oxysporum, and N. solani were frequently isolated, and inoculation assays confirmed the pathogenicity of two additional novel species F. citricola and F. salinense (Sandoval-Denis et al. 2018). In South Africa, similar investigations recovered 11 Neocosmospora species, with N. citricola, N. ferruginea, and N. solani among the most common. Four new species were described; however, pathogenicity assessments were not performed (Guarnaccia et al. 2021). In Morocco, widespread citrus tree decline was reported, and four fusarioid fungi were identified. Neocosmospora solani again proved to be the most prevalent, and inoculation trials revealed that all but F. equiseti were highly aggressive to citrus seedlings (Ezrari et al. 2021). Despite these advances, the diversity and pathogenic potential of fusarioid fungi associated with citrus diseases remain insufficiently characterized in many regions. This highlights the need for continued investigation into their species composition, virulence, and potential impact on citrus health.

China is considered one of the primary centres of origin for Citrus spp., with a documented cultivation history spanning over 4000 years (Deng et al. 2008, Wu et al. 2018). Citrus spp. are extensively cultivated in southern China, with a total production of 64.33 M tons in 2023 (National Bureau of Statistics, https://data.stats.gov.cn/adv.htm?m=advquer&cn=C01, accessed 26 May 2025). Among the various diseases affecting Citrus spp. in the region, branch-related symptoms such as twig blight, branch dieback, bark rot, canker, cracking, and gummosis are commonly observed, particularly in regions where environmental stress factors such as frost and sunburn frequently occur. The diversity and pathogenic composition of fungal taxa such as Botryosphaeriaceae, Colletotrichum and Diaporthe associated with these symptoms have been previously studied (Huang et al. 2013a, b, Xiao et al. 2021, 2023). During our investigation, fusarioid fungi were frequently detected; however, their species diversity and roles in citrus branch diseases in China remain poorly understood. Early investigations reported 11 Fusarium species associated with Citrus spp., although all were isolated from fruits and roots and identified based on symptomatology and morphological characteristics (Dai et al. 1958, CAAS 1960). In recent years, phylogenetic identification of Fusarium species from citrus twigs and fruits in China has been conducted, but such studies remain limited and sporadic (Zhu et al. 2012, Liu et al. 2019, Zhao et al. 2022, Ma et al. 2024).

During 2021–2024, a series of surveys targeting citrus branch diseases were conducted across major citrus production regions in China. The objectives of this study were to: a) identify Fusarium species and related fusarioid genera associated with citrus branch diseases in China based on morphological traits and phylogenetic analyses; b) determine the dominant species associated with citrus branch diseases; and c) evaluate their pathogenicity to assess the potential risk these fungi pose to citrus production.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sampling and isolation

From 2021 to 2024, diseased citrus samples showing dieback, canker and gummosis of twigs, branches and trunks, and root rot, were collected from the several citrus-producing regions in eight provinces of China, including Fujian, Guangxi, Hubei, Hunan, Jiangxi, Shaanxi, Sichuan and Zhejiang. The investigated citrus species included C. aurantium, C. junos, C. limon, C. maxima, C. reticulata, C. sinensis, C. unshiu and citrus hybrid. Isolation of fungal strains was conducted following the protocol of Xiao et al. (2021). Type specimens of new species were deposited in the Mycological Herbarium, Institute of Microbiology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Beijing, China (HMAS), and ex-type living cultures were deposited in the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Centre (CGMCC), Beijing, China.

DNA extraction, PCR amplification and sequencing

Isolates used for DNA extraction were cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA, 200 g potatoes, 20 g glucose and 15 g agar/L water) and incubated at 28 °C. Genomic DNA was extracted from axenic cultures using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method (Van Burik et al. 1998). Isolates selected for DNA amplification were chosen based on criteria described by Han et al. (2023). In total, 336 Fusarium isolates were assigned to six Fusarium species complexes and the genus Neocosmospora. Different gene loci were targeted depending on the species complex and genus. Partial fragments of the calmodulin (CaM), RNA polymerase II largest subunit (rpb1), RNA polymerase II second largest subunit (rpb2) and translation elongation factor 1-alpha (tef1) genes were amplified for the F. fujikuroi species complex (FFSC) and F. oxysporum species complex (FOSC); CaM, rpb2 and tef1 for the F. incarnatum-equiseti species complex (FIESC); rpb2, tef1 and beta-tubulin (tub2) for the F. lateritium species complex (FLSC); rpb1, rpb2 and tef1 for the F. sambucinum species complex (FSAMSC) and F. tricinctum species complex (FTSC); and internal transcribed spacer region of the nrDNA (ITS), 28S large subunit of the nrDNA (LSU), rpb2 and tef1 for Neocosmospora. The primer pairs and PCR procedures followed the protocols described by Crous et al. (2021) and Han et al. (2023), and are listed in Table 1. All amplification reactions were performed in a total volume of 25 μL mixture consisting of 12.5 μL of 2 × Taq Master Mix (Dye Plus; Vazyme), 9.5 μL ddH2O, 1 μL each of the forward and reverse primers, and 1 μL of DNA template. The PCR products were sent to Shangya Biotechnology (Hangzhou, China) for Sanger DNA sequencing. The nucleotide sequences were assembled and edited with MEGA v. 7.0.26 (Kumar et al. 2016). All sequences obtained in this study were deposited in the GenBank nucleotide database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov; Table 2).

Phylogenetic analyses were performed separately for each species complex and genus. Bayesian inference (BI) analyses were conducted using MrBayes v. 3.2.7 (Ronquist & Huelsenbeck 2003). MrModeltest v. 2.4 (Nylander 2004) was used to determine the best nucleotide substitution model for each partition (Table 4). The Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analyses were run with four simultaneous chains starting from random trees for 100 M generations, with trees sampled every 10 generations. Analyses were automatically terminated when the standard deviation of split frequencies fell below 0.01. The burn-in fraction was set to 0.25, after which the 50 % majority-rule consensus trees and posterior probability (PP) values were calculated.

Genealogical concordance phylogenetic species recognition (GCPSR)

To assess the level of recombination between the newly proposed species and their closely related taxa in this study, pairwise homoplasy index (PHI) tests were conducted on the concatenated multilocus data (Bruen et al. 2006). The analyses were performed using SplitsTree v. 6.3.41 (Huson & Bryant 2006) as described by Quaedvlieg et al. (2014), and a PHI value below 0.05 (Фw < 0.05) was considered indicative of significant recombination within the dataset.

Morphological observations were conducted following the recommendations of Crous et al. (2021). Sporulation was induced on carnation leaf agar (CLA, Fisher et al. 1982) and synthetic nutrient-poor agar (SNA, Nirenberg 1976) by incubating cultures at 25 °C under a 12/12 h near-ultraviolet light/dark cycle for 7–21 d. Micromorphological characteristics were examined and documented with water as the mounting medium under a compound microscope (Eclipse Ni, Nikon, Japan) equipped with differential interference contrast (DIC) optics, alongside a dissecting microscope (SMZ800, Nikon, Japan). Measurements of fungal structures were made using Fiji-ImageJ software (Schindelin et al. 2012). For each fungal structure, at least 30 randomly selected elements were measured. Results are presented as (minimum–) (mean – standard deviation) – (mean + standard deviation) (–maximum).

Colony characteristics were observed on PDA and oatmeal agar (OA, 30 g oatmeal and 15 g agar/L water), with colours determined based on the colour charts of Rayner (1970). Growth rates were evaluated by transferring 5 mm diam. agar plugs from actively growing culture margins to the centre of 90-mm-diam. Petri dishes containing PDA or OA. Cultures were incubated in the dark at five temperature intervals ranging from 5–40 °C. Five replicate plates of each representative isolate were incubated at each temperature. Perpendicular colony diameters were measured after 7 d cultivation.

Pathogenicity tests

One or two representative isolates of each identified fusarioid species were selected for pathogenicity testing, except for species with fewer than three isolates, from which all isolates were tested. The tests were conducted on 8-yr-old healthy potted C. reticulata cv. Shatangju plants at the campus of Zhejiang University in Hangzhou, from 30 September 2024 to 16 April 2025, and on 4-yr-old healthy C. reticulata cv. Ponkan plants at the Quzhou Academy of Agricultural and Forestry Sciences in Quzhou, from 17 January to 26 April 2025. During these periods, environmental temperatures ranged from −4 to 34 °C in Hangzhou and from −3 to 35 °C in Quzhou. Each representative isolate, along with the control, was inoculated onto five branches. A single wound was made on each branch using a sterile cork borer (5-mm-diam.). Inoculations were performed by placing a 7-d-old, 5-mm-diam. mycelial plug from the margins of colonies on a wound. The inoculated area was then covered with Parafilm. The negative control treatment was inoculated with sterile OA plugs. Symptom development was monitored regularly, and disease symptoms were assessed and recorded upon manifestation. To confirm Koch’s postulates, the inoculated fungi were re-isolated by cutting small pieces of necrotic tissue from the edges of each lesion and plating them onto PDA plates at 28 °C. The identity of the re-isolated fungi was confirmed based on morphology. Additionally, given the frequent isolation of fusarioid species in the Hanzhong City, Shaanxi Province, two isolates of each of the most frequently recovered species from samples collected in this region were selected for inoculation on C. unshiu trees grown in the local orchard. The experiment was conducted from 24 January to 8 August 2024, under ambient temperatures ranged from −4 to 37 °C. The inoculation procedure followed the same protocol as described above, except that wounds were made by cutting through the bark with a sterilized knife without removing it.

RESULTS

Sampling and isolation

In the surveyed citrus orchards, the affected twigs, branches and trunks exhibited dieback symptoms (Fig. 1A), along with cracking, dark discolouration, and exudation of light brown gum (Fig. 1B–E). Under humid conditions, a white to orange mould layer was observed on the surface of the infected tissues (Fig. 1F). As the disease progressed, the lesions gradually dried up and became sunken (Fig. 1G). In severe cases, the disease caused leaf curling and withering (Fig. 1H), as well as extensive dieback of shoots and branches, and cross-sections of the branch or trunk revealed necrosis in both phloem and xylem tissues (Fig. 1I). Based on survey and isolation results, citrus canker accompanied by gummosis was found to be widespread and severe in citrus orchards in Hanzhong City, Shaanxi Province, which is located at the northern edge of citrus cultivation in China and is frequently affected by frost damage. Consequently, we conducted additional sampling in multiple orchards and randomly assessed disease incidence in 10 orchards, with 50 trees randomly selected and surveyed in each orchard. Most orchards (seven out of 10) showed an incidence rate exceeding 50 %.

Phylogenetic analyses

Sequences of the rpb2 and tef1 loci for all isolates were generated and queried against the GenBank nucleotide database (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi), resulting in the classification of the 336 isolates into six Fusarium species complexes and the genus Neocosmospora. Phylogenetic analyses were conducted respectively for each species complex and genus using different datasets with 114 representative isolates (Table 2). The results of the phylogenetic analyses indicated that the isolates were classified into 21 known species: one species (F. fujikuroi) from FFSC (Fig. 3, S1), three species (F. ipomoeae, F. pernambucanum and F. sulawesiense) from FIESC (Fig. 4, S2), three species (F. citri-sinensis, F. fujianense and F. lateritium) from FLSC (Fig. 5, S3), two species (F. cugenangense and F. nirenbergiae) from FOSC (Fig. 6, S4), two species (F. asiaticum and F. kyushuense) from FSAMSC (Fig. 7, S5), four species (F. acuminatum, F. californicum, F. dendranthematis and F. rosiradicicola) from FTSC (Fig. 8, S6) and six species (N. falciformis, N. macrospora, N. metavorans, N. oblonga, N. paraeumartii and N. solani) from Neocosmospora (Fig. 9, S7). Furthermore, three isolates formed a distinct lineage within the FLSC, while 19 formed two distinct lineages within the genus Neocosmospora. These lineages were phylogenetically separate from other species in the respective complex and genus, and exhibited corresponding morphological differences, as corroborated by the PHI analysis. The PHI values for all these lineages were 1.0 (Fig. 10). Therefore, these three distinct lineages were considered novel species, which are described below.

Identification and new records of fusarioid species

This study identified 21 known fusarioid fungal species from citrus branch disease samples collected across eight citrus-producing regions in China. The isolates comprised 15 Fusarium species (F. acuminatum, F. asiaticum, F. californicum, F. citri-sinensis, F. cugenangense, F. dendranthematis, F. fujianense, F. fujikuroi, F. ipomoeae, F. kyushuense, F. lateritium, F. nirenbergiae, F. pernambucanum, F. rosiradicicola, F. sulawesiense) and six Neocosmospora species (N. falciformis, N. macrospora, N. metavorans, N. oblonga, N. paraeumartii, N. solani). Among these, 13 species are reported from citrus hosts for the first time, namely F. acuminatum, F. asiaticum, F. californicum, F. cugenangense, F. dendranthematis, F. ipomoeae, F. kyushuense, F. nirenbergiae, F. rosiradicicola, N. metavorans, N. oblonga, N. paraeumartii, and F. pernambucanum. Additionally, two species represent new records for China, namely F. californicum and N. macrospora (Table 2).

Co-isolation of multiple fusarioid species from symptomatic citrus tissues

In this study, the co-isolation of multiple fusarioid species from the same symptomatic citrus tissue was commonly observed across various regions. In Hanzhong City, Shaanxi Province, 43 samples yielded F. citri-sinensis and F. lateritium, either separately or together, from the same lesion. Specifically, in 12 of these samples, both F. citri-sinensis and F. lateritium were isolated from the same lesion. In 10 samples, all three species, F. citri-sinensis, F. lateritium, and the highly pathogenic N. unshiu, were co-isolated from the same lesion. Additionally, F. lateritium and N. unshiu were co-isolated in 17 samples, and F. citri-sinensis and N. unshiu were co-isolated in seven samples. Similar co-isolation phenomena were observed in other regions. In samples from other provinces, F. fujikuroi, N. paraeumartii, and other fusarioid species were frequently isolated alongside F. citri-sinensis and F. lateritium from symptomatic lesions (Table S2).

Pathogenicity

In pathogenicity assays conducted via branch inoculations on C. reticulata cv. Shatangju, most of the tested fusarioid isolates induced discoloured and necrotic lesions 6 mo post-inoculation, except for those from FLSC (F. citri-sinensis, F. fujianense, F. gummosum, F. lateritium), which caused no visible symptoms (Figs 14–16). Most isolates caused mild to moderate lesions (mean lesion length < 2 cm), whereas isolates of F. asiaticum and N. unshiu produced the most extensive lesions, with mean lesion lengths exceeding 2 cm. Additionally, isolates of N. oblonga (mean lesion lengths: 1.82 cm), N. paraeumartii (2.12 cm) and N. reticulatae (1.82 cm and 1.92 cm) also demonstrated moderate to high levels of aggressiveness (Fig. 16). During the incubation period, gummosis symptoms were observed on branches inoculated with N. unshiu and F. avenaceum (Fig. 14E, G).

Consistent results were obtained from pathogenicity tests on C. reticulata cv. Ponkan after a 3-mo incubation, with F. asiaticum (4 cm and 4.82 cm) and N. unshiu (3.88 cm and 3.86 cm) again causing the most severe lesions (Figs 14–16). Neocosmospora oblonga (1.54 cm), N. paraeumartii (2 cm) and N. reticulatae (1.24 cm and 1.8 cm) were moderately to highly aggressive, while N. solani (1.84 cm and 2.3 cm) also showed moderate to high levels of aggressiveness on Ponkan branches (Figs 14–16). Early observations revealed gummosis at the points of inoculation with N. unshiu, but due to increased rainfall, white mycelial growth became visible on the lesion surfaces (Fig. 14M).

Based on fungal identification and sample isolation data, F. citri-sinensis, F. lateritium, and N. unshiu were the three most frequently recovered species in Hanzhong City, Shaanxi Province (Table S2). Therefore, isolates of these species were selected for pathogenicity testing. Inoculation trials revealed that only branches inoculated with N. unshiu developed gummosis and necrotic symptoms resembling those observed under natural field conditions, with gummosis becoming evident and severe as early as 3 mo post-inoculation (data not shown) and intensifying further by 6 mo (Fig. 14R–T). No necrotic symptoms were observed on control branches in any of the three citrus cultivars tested (Fig. 14, 15). All inoculated isolates were successfully re-isolated from the symptomatic lesions on each host, fulfilling Koch’s postulates.

DISCUSSION

In this study, a total of 336 fusarioid isolates were obtained from Citrus spp. exhibiting symptoms of dieback, localized necrosis, canker, decay and gummosis on twigs, branches and trunks based on surveys conducted in major citrus-producing regions of China. Through multi-locus phylogenetic analyses combined with morphological features, these isolates were characterized as belonging to six Fusarium species complexes and the genus Neocosmospora, comprising 21 known species and three novel taxa. Among the known species, 13 are reported from citrus hosts for the first time, while two species represent new records for China. These findings highlight the significant diversity of fusarioid species associated with citrus in China.

During our investigation, we observed that citrus canker accompanied by gummosis was prevalent in orchards located in Chenggu County (33°09′25″N, 107°20′02″E), in southern Shaanxi Province. This region represents the northernmost boundary of China’s citrus cultivation zone. Initial sampling from several orchards across Chenggu resulted in many fusarioid isolates, which were preliminarily identified based on microscopic examination and colony morphology. To further clarify the etiology of the gummosisassociated canker disease commonly observed in this area, we conducted intensive sampling across Chenggu and neighbouring orchards. A total of 71 symptomatic branch samples were collected from 12 orchards, yielding 210 fusarioid isolates. Molecular phylogenetic analyses and morphological verification identified these isolates as belonging to 10 fusarioid species. Among them, N. unshiu, a novel species described in the present study, was the most frequently isolated. Pathogenicity assays confirmed its high aggressiveness, indicating that N. unshiu is the predominant pathogen responsible for branch canker disease in the Chenggu region. In contrast, surveys conducted in other major citrus producing areas showed higher isolation frequencies of fungi from the family Botryosphaeriaceae and the genus Diaporthe (Xiao et al. 2021, 2023), likely due to the relatively lower tolerance of these fungal groups to low temperatures. In China, during the winter and spring, cold waves are frequent, and freeze damage is common in citrus-producing regions, especially in northern citrus areas. Previous surveys in southern Shaanxi have shown a close relationship between the outbreak of citrus canker with gummosis and freeze damage (Ao et al. 2022). In other regions prone to freeze damage, fusarioid fungi were also frequently isolated in this study. Growth experiments in this study demonstrated that Neocosmospora species exhibit a higher tolerance to low temperatures than the dominant citrus branch pathogens previously reported in China, including Lasiodiplodia spp. (Botryosphaeriaceae) and D. citri, with the fungus capable of slow growth even at 5 °C (Fang 2017, Xiao et al. 2021). Moreover, the formation of chlamydospores allows fusarioid fungi to persist in soil environments for prolonged periods (Rahman et al. 2021). Previous studies have also indicated that freezing or low-temperature stress can enhance Fusarium-induced diseases by reducing host resistance and facilitating pathogen invasion (Richard et al. 1982, Marek et al. 2013). Therefore, we hypothesize that after freezing damage to the trees, the reduced disease resistance allows fusarioid species to invade through wounds in branches, trunks or roots. The fungus then becomes latent within the tree, and when environmental conditions such as temperature and humidity become favourable, it begins to colonize the host and cause disease.

Pathogenicity experiments revealed that most of the fusarioid isolates tested could induce lesions on citrus branches, except for four species within the FLSC: F. citri-sinensis, F. fujianense, F. gummosum and F. lateritium. Among these, F. gummosum was newly identified in this study. The other three species have been previously reported on Citrus spp., but no studies have confirmed their pathogenicity (Zhao et al. 2022, Costa et al. 2024). Notably, F. lateritium has been frequently associated with asymptomatic woody hosts such as Citrus spp. and Vitis spp., suggesting a possible endophytic or latent lifestyle rather than a primary pathogenic role (Wollenweber & Reinking 1935, Gerlach & Nirenberg 1982, Sandoval-Denis et al. 2018, Costa et al. 2024). However, the true ecological roles of these species remain unresolved and warrant further investigation. In this study, F. citri-sinensis and F. lateritium were widely isolated from symptomatic citrus tissues across multiple provinces and were frequently co-isolated with other fungal species, including F. fujikuroi, N. paraeumartii, N. reticulatae and particularly the highly pathogenic N. unshiu (Table S2). These findings suggest that F. citri-sinensis and F. lateritium may commonly persist within citrus hosts as endophytes or saprobes, rather than as primary pathogens. Moreover, previous studies have highlighted the common occurrence of co-infection within Fusarium spp. (Wang et al. 2022b, Zhang et al. 2022b). For instance, Han et al. (2023) reported that 56 % of cereal disease samples were co-infected by multiple Fusarium species. Consistent with these findings, our study also identified multiple pathogenic fusarioid species isolated from the same lesion (Table S2), further supporting the notion that co-infection among fusarioid species is a common and widespread phenomenon.

Fusarium is a well-known pathogen in cereal crops, and several species recovered in this study, belonging to the FFSC, FIESC, FOSC, FSAMSC, and FTSC, have also been frequently reported from cereals in China (Wang et al. 2019, 2022a, Han et al. 2023). In the FFSC, F. fujikuroi, previously reported to cause leaf rot disease of citrus seedlings in Hubei (Ma et al. 2024), was also isolated here from C. sinensis in Hubei and citrus hybrids in Zhejiang. Pathogenicity tests showed it could induce pronounced necrotic lesions on citrus branches. In the FIESC, F. sulawesiensis, previously recorded from C. reticulata in Hunan (Wang et al. 2019), was recovered from dead twigs of C. sinensis in the same province, and sporodochia were frequently observed on C. maxima branches infested with scale insects in Fujian and Jiangxi. Pathogenicity assays revealed low virulence on Citrus spp. Given prior reports of Fusarium–insect associations (Jiang et al. 2021, Berasategui et al. 2022, 2023), we hypothesize a possible symbiotic relationship between F. sulawesiensis and scale insects. Fusarium pernambucanum, previously associated with fruit rot, leaf spots, or flower dry rot on various fruits (Guo et al. 2021, Liu et al. 2021, Zhang et al. 2022a), is reported here for the first time on Citrus spp. However, it showed minimal to no pathogenicity in our trials. This difference in pathogenicity may be due to variations in experimental conditions, host susceptibility, or fungal isolate differences (Jones & Dangl 2006, Schulze-Lefert & Panstruga 2011). In the FOSC, F. cugenangense, previously unreported on citrus, was isolated from C. unshiu exhibiting gummosis and bark cracking in Shaanxi, and its pathogenicity was confirmed. This species has also been associated with root rot of Camellia sinensis and wilt of Pyrus pyrifolia in China (Li et al. 2024, Yang et al. 2024). In the FSAMSC, F. asiaticum has been documented in cereals, in fruit rot of Cucumis melo and sheath rot of Zizania latifolia in China (Hao et al. 2021, Ma et al. 2023). We recovered it from C. sinensis with twig dieback in Hunan Province. Pathogenicity tests identified it as one of the most aggressive species, capable of causing severe lesions on citrus branches. This is the first report of this species on Citrus spp. Within the FTSC, F. californicum, which is reported here on citrus for the first time, was isolated from dead twigs of C. junos in Zhejiang and caused clear necrotic lesions in inoculation tests. Initially identified on Prunus dulcis with canker disease (Stack et al. 2020), it has rarely been reported and is newly recorded in China. Together, these species demonstrate that members of traditionally cereal-associated Fusarium lineages are capable of colonizing citrus tissues, albeit with differing levels of virulence.

In China, Neocosmospora species have traditionally been reported from woody host plants (Wang et al. 2022a). Several taxa within this genus have also been identified internationally as causal agents of citrus dry root rot (Sandoval-Denis et al. 2018, Guarnaccia et al. 2021). In this study, eight Neocosmospora species were isolated from symptomatic citrus tissues, including two novel taxa, N. reticulatae and N. unshiu. Both were obtained from trees exhibiting trunk canker and gummosis, and pathogenicity tests confirmed their ability to cause disease symptoms on Citrus spp. Neocosmospora oblonga is reported on citrus for the first time in this study, based on its isolation from a cankered trunk of C. sinensis in Hubei Province. Pathogenicity assays confirmed its ability to colonize Citrus spp. as a pathogen. Neocosmospora paraeumartii, originally isolated from decaying basal stems of Solanum tuberosum, was also recovered from citrus trees with canker and dieback symptoms. This study represents the first report of this species on Citrus spp., and pathogenicity assays demonstrated its aggressiveness on citrus branches. Neocosmospora solani, widely reported abroad as a citrus dry root rot pathogen in citrus orchards, was isolated in this study only from blighted seedlings and decayed roots. It was shown to be pathogenic in inoculation tests, although less aggressive than N. unshiu. Limited isolates of N. macrospora (from dead branches), N. metavorans (from trunks with gummosis) and N. falciformis (from rotting roots) were also obtained. These species have previously been reported from citrus dry root rot tissues or orchard soils in other countries (Sandoval-Denis et al. 2018, Guarnaccia et al. 2021). Collectively, these findings broaden the known diversity of Neocosmospora associated with citrus in China and indicate that multiple species within this genus, including some that were previously overlooked, may contribute to citrus branch and root disease.

In conclusion, this study presents the first systematic survey of 24 fusarioid species associated with citrus in eight citrus-producing regions across China. The pathogenicity of most species was evaluated, offering new insights into their potential roles in citrus branch canker and gummosis. Among these species, N. unshiu was identified as one of the most aggressive pathogens. However, its confirmed distribution is currently restricted to citrus orchards in Hanzhong City, Shaanxi Province. Further surveys in neighboring regions and provinces are necessary to determine its broader geographic range and host spectrum. Based on the findings of the present investigation, we propose that fusarioid fungi primarily cause citrus diseases in regions where freeze damage is frequent, particularly near the northern limit of citrus cultivation in China. These pathogens likely enter host tissues through bark fissures caused by freezing injury. Therefore, after freeze events, prompt application of protective measures such as whitewashing or fungicide spraying on damaged trunks is recommended to promote wound healing, prevent fungal infection, and reduce disease incidence.
