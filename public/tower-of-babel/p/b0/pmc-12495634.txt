Explainable prediction of hypothermia risk in laparoscopic surgery: a retrospective cross-sectional study using machine learning
Authors: Wenyu Su, Xiaoli Wang, Huiyu Jia, Wenjing Chang, Shan Jiang, Huaiju Ge, Shihong Dong, Jie Yu, Guifeng Ma, Yingtao Meng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to develop multiple machine learning models for predicting hypothermia risk in laparoscopic surgery and to perform interpretability analysis of the best-performing model. Our goal is to provide robust decision support for clinicians and ensure safe and effective patient care during surgical procedures.MethodsThis study included 1,030 patients who underwent laparoscopic surgery at Shandong Provincial Cancer Hospital, affiliated with Shandong First Medical University, between July 2023 and June 2024. We employed the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) algorithm for feature selection. We explored the performance of five machine learning algorithms—logistic regression (LR), decision tree (DT), random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)—to predict hypothermia risk during laparoscopic surgery. Finally, we conducted an interpretability analysis of the top-performing model using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP).ResultsLASSO identified nine risk factors: BMI, ASA classification, total volume of intravenous fluids, irrigating fluids during the operation, volume of CO2, blood loss, ambient temperature, long-term alcohol consumption, and type of surgery. Performance comparison among the five models revealed that the XGBoost model performed the best, with an accuracy of 0.762 (95% CI: 0.717–0.807) and an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.835 (95% CI: 0.794–0.872). The model achieved a specificity of 0.749 (95% CI: 0.683–0.816) and a sensitivity of 0.773 (95% CI: 0.713–0.824). The F1 score was 0.778 (95% CI: 0.735–0.828). SHAP analysis revealed that the four most influential factors for hypothermia risk during laparoscopic surgery were operating room temperature, rinsing fluids during the operation, volume of CO2, and blood loss.ConclusionsThis study developed an efficient and interpretable predictive model for the risk of hypothermia in patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery. This model supports clinical decision-making and contributes to the overall goal of providing personalized care in the surgical environment.

Introduction

Laparoscopic surgery can reduce postoperative pain, accelerate recovery, and decrease the risk of complications [1]. As a result, laparoscopic techniques are widely adopted in clinical practice. However, despite advances in laparoscopic technology both domestically and internationally, the incidence of hypothermia during anesthesia and surgery remains high, ranging from 50 to 90% [2, 3]. Hypothermia is defined as a core body temperature below 36.0 °C [4]. Research indicates that hypothermia can negatively impact postoperative recovery, delaying wound healing and prolonging recovery time [5]. This not only extends postoperative care and hospital stays but also increases the overall disease burden.

Moreover, even mild hypothermia can result in complications such as coagulopathy [6], surgical site infections [7], and myocardial infarction [8]. Additionally, intraoperative hypothermia increases the risk of cardiac arrest, and ventricular tachycardia, and, in severe cases, can lead to death [9]. Given that laparoscopic surgeries have largely replaced open procedures and differ significantly in their methodologies, it is crucial to study the risk factors for hypothermia specifically within the context of laparoscopic surgery.

Machine learning offers new opportunities for disease prediction and identifying risk factors for adverse postoperative outcomes. Previous studies have employed machine learning to develop predictive models for hypothermia during laparoscopic surgery [10, 11]. However, to our knowledge, no research has focused on using interpretable machine-learning approaches to explore the specific risk factors for hypothermia in this context. Many machine learning models function as “black boxes,” limiting their interpretability. In the healthcare sector, a lack of interpretability in predictive models diminishes their credibility [12], hinders further exploration of risk factors, and subsequently affects confidence in an actual application of clinical decision-making [13]. Research indicates that clinicians prioritize interpretability over mere predictive accuracy when integrating machine learning models into practice [14, 15]. This underscores that interpretability remains a key challenge and limiting factor for the adoption of machine learning tools in clinical settings.

This study employed machine learning algorithms to identify key factors influencing hypothermia during laparoscopic surgery and developed five risk prediction models. By comparing and analyzing these models, we can determine which performs best for specific applications, offering more reliable options for clinical use. Additionally, we applied the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) method to analyze how different factors affect hypothermia occurrence, revealing the significance and contribution of each variable to the model’s predictions.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study adopts a retrospective cross-sectional design. Patients who underwent laparoscopic surgery at Shandong Cancer Hospital from July 2023 to June 2024 were selected as the study population. The inclusion criteria were as follows: ① patients aged 18 years or older; ② complete clinical data; and ③ American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification of I–III. The exclusion criteria were as follows: ① patients whose preoperative core temperature exceeded 37.5 °C for infectious diseases or other reasons; ② patients who required conversion to open surgery after laparoscopic exploration; and ③ emergency surgeries. This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong First Medical University Affiliated Cancer Hospital (approval number: SDTHEC202409003), which also waived the requirement for informed consent from the participants. All information from the enrolled patients was anonymized to ensure that no personal information was disclosed.

Variable selection

Based on a review of the literature [16–18], the following basic information about the patients was collected: age, sex, and body mass index (BMI); health behaviors, such as long-term smoking, long-term drinking, and nutritional status; underlying diseases, including hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and anemia; preoperative variables, such as type of surgery, ASA classification, and basal body temperature; and intraoperative variables, such as operating room temperature, duration of surgery, duration of anesthesia, blood loss, volume of CO2, volume of urine, irrigating fluid during the operation, and total volume of intravenous fluid. The duration of anesthesia was defined as the interval from anesthesia induction to extubation via the tracheal tube. In contrast, the duration of surgery is defined as the time interval from anesthesia induction to the time at which the patient leaves the operating room [18]. Our hospital’s protocol requires measuring the patient’s oropharyngeal temperature every 30 min from anesthesia induction until the end of surgery, and any core temperature recorded at any time during the entire procedure that was < 36 °C was classified as intraoperative hypothermia. This practice aligns with clinical guidelines advocating for continuous temperature monitoring to prevent hypothermia [19, 20]. Importantly, according to the guidelines for preventing hypothermia during the perioperative period, all liquid items, including intravenous fluids and blood transfusions, must be prewarmed via a fluid-warming device. However, the steam the irrigating solution produces during surgery can impair the screen’s clarity and affect the surgeon’s visualization of the surgical site. To balance the heating of fluids with their impact on the surgical view, we implemented only two passive warming measures, warming blankets, and surgical drapes, without standardizing active warming methods.

Data Processing and Feature Selection

Following the recommendations of biomedical research guidelines [21], building machine learning predictive models requires data preprocessing to remove implausible or extreme outlier responses. Therefore, we excluded samples containing impossible or extremely abnormal values. In addition, we standardize continuous variables (Z-score standardization), scaling them to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. This eliminates differences in scale between features, enhances comparability, and improves the stability of the prediction model [22]. The Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) method effectively addresses multicollinearity issues in machine learning. Its core principle involves introducing a penalty function that shrinks some unimportant coefficients toward zero, thereby performing feature selection by effectively eliminating less relevant variables [23]. Consequently, LASSO was employed in this study for feature reduction and selection. The algorithm was implemented using the “glmnet” package in R software (version 4.1-8).

Machine Learning Models

Based on studies that have achieved favorable results in disease prediction using machine learning [24–26], and considering the characteristics of our data, the samples were randomly split into training and testing sets at a 7:3 ratio. Five supervised machine learning models were constructed in the training set: logistic regression (LR), decision tree (DT), random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost). The evaluation of the models utilized the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, F1 score, sensitivity, and specificity obtained from the testing set. Bootstrap resampling was employed to estimate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for these metrics. All modeling and evaluation processes were conducted using Python’s scikit-learn library (version 1.2.2).

Interpretability Analysis

SHAP (Shapley additive explanations) is an interpretability analysis method that provides insights into the model from global and local perspectives [27–29]. The SHAP feature importance plot illustrates the relative importance of each feature, where the horizontal axis represents the mean absolute SHAP value for each feature. Longer horizontal bars indicate larger absolute SHAP values, reflecting a greater contribution of the feature to the model’s predictions. The SHAP summary plot assesses the contribution and impact of relevant features on the model’s predicted outcomes; each point in the plot represents the SHAP value for an individual patient’s feature. The colors range from red to blue, indicating high to low feature values, respectively. The SHAP dependence plot is primarily used to visualize the importance of specific features and their relationship with the model output. Local explanations can be visualized using force plots, which intuitively show the effect of each feature on individual predictions [30]. In these plots, red indicates positive contributions, blue indicates negative contributions, and the length of the color bar reflects the magnitude of each contribution. In this study, SHAP analyses were implemented in Python using the SHAP version 0.45.0.

Statistical analysis

In this study, categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages, while continuous variables are expressed as medians with interquartile ranges, due to the non-normal distribution of the data. The chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test was employed for continuous variables to compare demographic characteristics between the hypothermia and normothermia groups. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS version 27.0. A two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Basic characteristics

A total of 1,045 patients who underwent laparoscopic surgery met the criteria. We excluded 15 samples containing implausible or extreme outlier values, and ultimately, 1,030 patients were included in this study. Among these, 573 patients (55.6%) experienced hypothermia. The baseline body temperatures of the patients ranged from 35.1 °C to 37.1 °C, and their ages varied from 22 to 89 years. Of the 573 hypothermic patients, 319 (55.7%) were male. There were no statistically significant differences (P > 0.05) between the normothermic and hypothermic groups in terms of age, duration of surgery, sex, nutritional status, hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, or anemia. Feature selection

The LASSO regression model identified 12 potential factors that could influence intraoperative hypothermia. We used ten-fold cross-validation to select the optimal lambda (λ), specifically the λ.1se criterion (Fig. 1). Factors unrelated to intraoperative hypothermia had their coefficients shrunk to zero and were consequently excluded from the model. Ultimately, as shown in Table 2, nine variables were retained.

Model Building and Evaluation

In this study, variables selected through LASSO regression were combined with five machine learning algorithms—LR, DT, RF, SVM, and XGBoost—to construct predictive models, as shown in Table 3; Fig. 2. The internal validation results from the testing set indicated that the XGBoost model achieved the highest accuracy (0.762, 95% CI: 0.717–0.807) and AUC value (0.835, 95% CI: 0.794–0.872). It also demonstrated the best specificity (0.749, 95% CI: 0.683–0.816) and sensitivity (0.773, 95% CI: 0.713–0.824). Furthermore, the RF model exhibited the highest sensitivity (0.773, 95% CI: 0.720–0.819). Since the F1 score balances both precision and recall, it is particularly suitable for evaluating imbalanced datasets. Therefore, we further compared the performance of the models using the F1 score, and again, the highest F1 score was observed with the XGBoost model (0.778, 95% CI: 0.735–0.828). Considering all factors, this study selected the XGBoost model for subsequent analyses.

Model Explanation

The SHAP feature importance for the XGBoost model is shown in Fig. 3, where the four most influential features are operating room temperature, rinsing fluid during surgery, volume of CO₂, and blood loss. The SHAP summary plot for the XGBoost model (Fig. 4) illustrates the impact of these features on the predictions. Higher SHAP values for particular features indicate an increased likelihood of patients developing hypothermia. For example, increases in blood loss, the volume of CO₂, and the amount of irrigation fluid used during surgery are associated with a higher probability of hypothermia. Conversely, higher operating room temperatures decrease the risk. Compared to patients classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status grade 1, those classified as grades 2 and 3 are more likely to experience hypothermia. Additionally, long-term alcohol consumption is associated with an increased risk of hypothermia.

The SHAP dependence plot (Fig. 5) provides a detailed view of how various features influence the model’s predictions. Patients undergoing hepatobiliary and pancreatic surgery exhibit higher SHAP values, indicating a greater likelihood of intraoperative hypothermia. In contrast, patients undergoing colorectal surgery tend to have a lower risk. The SHAP values for kidney, adrenal gland, and prostate surgeries are minimal and consistently non-negative, suggesting a weak association with hypothermia. For gynecological surgery, SHAP values cross zero, indicating no clear significant relationship. Furthermore, as the raw values for blood loss, irrigating fluid, and volume of CO₂ increase, the SHAP values transition from negative to positive, implying that higher values of these variables are often linked to hypothermia. Conversely, increases in operating room temperature and BMI are associated with decreases in SHAP values, indicating a reduced risk of hypothermia.

SHAP dependency plot for model features. Note: SHAP (Shapley additive exPlanations) values explain how each feature contributes to the model’s predictions, highlighting its impact on the predicted risk

The SHAP method was applied to explain the individual predictions of two patients and to assess the contribution of features to each patient’s prediction in the XGBoost model. Figure 6 displays the SHAP force plots for a normothermic patient (Example A) and a hypothermic patient (Example B). In Example A, except for the total volume of intravenous fluid (2200 ml) and the type of surgery (gynecological surgery), the values for the volume of CO₂ (300 L), non-long-term alcohol consumption, blood loss (90 ml), irrigating fluid during surgery (400 ml), and BMI (22.03) contributed to decreasing the model’s prediction from the baseline value (0.446) to a lower value (−1.31), suggesting that this patient would not develop hypothermia. Conversely, in Example B, the values for operating room temperature (22.1 °C), volume of CO₂ (800 L), and irrigating fluid during surgery (1000 ml) pushed the model’s prediction above the baseline (0.446). Meanwhile, blood loss (88 ml), type of surgery (colorectal surgery), and long-term alcohol consumption negatively impacted the prediction. Considering all these factors, the model’s output was elevated from the baseline (0.446) to 2.45, indicating a high likelihood that this patient would experience hypothermia.

Discussion

In this study, the XGBoost model constructed using hypothermia-related risk factors selected via LASSO regression demonstrated superior performance. We employed the SHAP method to perform an interpretive analysis of the model, identifying operating room temperature, volume of CO₂, blood loss, irrigating fluid during surgery, type of surgery, total volume of intravenous fluids, and BMI as key predictors of intraoperative hypothermia in patients undergoing laparoscopic procedures. While previous studies [3, 11] have utilized machine learning to predict hypothermia in laparoscopic patients, interpretative analyses of these models remain limited. This study’s application of SHAP provides a comprehensive understanding of the global impact of these factors, elucidating their roles in hypothermia risk prediction. Such insights can aid healthcare professionals in optimizing perioperative care to mitigate hypothermia risk. For instance, the analysis suggests that when the volume of irrigating fluid exceeds 750 ml, the risk of hypothermia increases, indicating that controlling this variable within an optimal range is advisable. Furthermore, in cases where larger volumes of irrigating fluid are necessary, adjusting the operating room temperature upward could help reduce hypothermia incidence during surgery. Local interpretative analysis, which explains individual predictions made by the XGBoost model, offers valuable insights into how specific variables influence hypothermia risk for individual patients. This personalized understanding can facilitate tailored interventions, ultimately enhancing patient safety and outcomes during surgical procedures.

Most of the identified risk factors in our study align with findings from previous research. Variables such as operating room temperature [31, 32], volume of CO₂ [33–35], irrigating fluid during surgery [36], and BMI [18] have consistently been recognized as key determinants in the risk of hypothermia during laparoscopic procedures. According to China’s “Guidelines for Surgical Nursing Practice,” the operating room temperature should be maintained between 21 °C and 25 °C. Our results indicate that when the room temperature drops below 23 °C, the likelihood of hypothermia increases. Although elevating the operating room temperature is an effective measure to prevent hypothermia, relying solely on warming strategies may not sufficiently reduce its incidence below certain thresholds [37]. Previous studies on laparoscopic hypothermia included alcohol consumption as a variable but reported no significant difference between normothermic and hypothermic patients regarding alcohol intake [11]. In contrast, both our univariate analysis and LASSO regression demonstrated a close association between long-term alcohol consumption and increased hypothermia risk. Furthermore, SHAP analysis of the XGBoost model supported this finding, indicating that long-term alcohol use elevates the probability of intraoperative hypothermia. This discrepancy might stem from differences in the criteria used to assess alcohol consumption; some literature suggests that alcohol can induce skin vasodilation, which facilitates heat loss and lowers core temperature [38]. However, to date, there are no specific studies evaluating the impact of long-term alcohol consumption on laparoscopic hypothermia. Additionally, our analysis revealed that when the total volume of CO₂ used during pneumoperitoneum exceeds 400 L, the risk of hypothermia increases. During laparoscopic procedures, an extended duration of pneumoperitoneum and increased insufflation volume can cause a gradual widening of the temperature gap between the core and intra-abdominal temperature [17], potentially leading to a significant decrease in core temperature.

This study employed machine learning techniques combined with the SHAP interpretability tool to develop an interpretable risk prediction model for hypothermia during laparoscopic surgery. This approach offers a comprehensive strategy to facilitate collaboration among multidisciplinary teams—including anesthesiologists, nurses, and surgeons—aimed at maintaining appropriate patient temperatures during procedures. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the risk factors influencing intraoperative hypothermia are diverse. Although our dataset included patients’ basic information, health behaviors, preoperative variables, and intraoperative factors, there may still be relevant risk factors that were not sufficiently captured. Second, while the SHAP tool provides valuable insights into the model’s output and supports clinical decision-making, the feature values do not imply causal relationships with the prediction outcomes. Third, as the study sample was derived solely from patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery at a tertiary A hospital in Shandong Province, the findings are limited to this specific clinical context. Moreover, due to the limited sample size, our model was only internally validated, lacking external validation, which restricts our ability to assess its generalizability and applicability to other populations.

Looking forward, we plan to incorporate additional key features into the model, expand our collaborative network to multiple centers, and increase the sample size. These steps will enable a more comprehensive exploration of hypothermia risk factors and help develop a reliable tool to support clinical decision-making.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that utilizing machine learning and SHAP interpretability tools is a feasible approach to exploring risk factors for laparoscopic hypothermia. The findings indicate that when blood loss exceeds approximately 100 ml, the irrigating fluid volume reaches about 750 ml, and the CO₂ volume surpasses roughly 600 L, the SHAP values transition from negative to positive. These critical thresholds highlight important points for clinical intervention. Notably, patients undergoing hepatobiliary and pancreatic surgery should prioritize temperature maintenance strategies as a key aspect of perioperative management. Additionally, the study recommends maintaining the operating room temperature between 23 °C and 25 °C to reduce hypothermia risk.
