Brain and central nervous system cancer burden in children across Asia
Authors: Weiqin Zhang, Wenwen Lv, Rui Chen, Taixiang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBrain and central nervous system cancers are a major cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality in children. However, evidence on long-term trends and inequalities across Asia remains limited. Using Global Burden of Disease 2023 data, we analyzed the burden among children aged 0–14 years in Asia from 1990 to 2023 and projected trends to 2050. Age-standardized incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life year (DALY) rates were lower in 2023 than in 1990, but no significant regional trends were observed. Substantial geographical heterogeneity persisted, with higher burdens in Central and Western Asia. Infants bore the highest burden, and boys had higher incidence and mortality than girls. Inequality analyses indicated persistent concentration of burden in lower-socio-demographic index settings. Projections suggested a continued decline in overall burden to 2050 but with considerable uncertainty. These findings highlight persistent regional and socio-economic disparities and require strengthened early diagnosis, treatment access, and survivorship care in resource-limited settings.Subject areas: health sciences, medicine, medical specialty, internal medicine, oncology, public health

Introduction

Brain and central nervous system (CNS) cancers are a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality in children worldwide.1 Despite notable advances in diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, these malignancies remain a significant challenge due to their aggressive biology, complex management, and substantial long-term sequelae such as neurocognitive impairments and endocrinopathies.2,3 Importantly, pediatric CNS tumors are biologically and clinically distinct from adult tumors, with differences in tumor spectrum, developmental context, and therapeutic vulnerability. Some tumor types arising in early life can be particularly aggressive, while treatment is constrained by the vulnerability of the developing brain, underscoring the need for child-specific epidemiological assessment. The disease and its long-term sequelae impose a sustained burden not only on survivors but also on families and health systems.4

The distribution and evolution of this burden are strongly influenced by disparities in healthcare access, diagnostic capacity, treatment infrastructure, and survivorship care.5 Asia provides a particularly informative setting for this analysis because it encompasses a broad spectrum of demographic, socio-economic, and health system contexts within which these disparities can be examined. Existing evidence in Asian children remains fragmented and is derived largely from single-country or short-term reports, which insufficiently capture the marked heterogeneity in incidence, mortality, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and prevalence across the region.6,7,8 A region-wide analysis is, therefore, needed to characterize geographical variation, quantify socio-demographic index (SDI)-related inequalities, and inform resource allocation and childhood cancer control strategies across diverse settings. To address these gaps, we used data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2023 study to systematically analyze the burden of brain and CNS cancers among children aged 0–14 years in Asia from 1990 to 2023. Our objectives were to characterize geographical heterogeneity, examine age- and sex-specific disparities, assess health inequalities, and project the burden to 2050 using a Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model.9

Result

Overall trends

From 1990 to 2023, the number of incident cases of brain and CNS cancers among children aged 0–14 years in Asia decreased from 29,580 (95% uncertainty interval [UI]: 19,144–39,435) to 23,111 (15,531–34,120) (Tables 1 and S1–S3). Over the same period, the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) was numerically lower in 2023 than in 1990 (2.09 vs. 2.77 per 100,000 population), but the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) of −0.95% (95% confidence interval [CI]: −11.84 to 11.29) indicated no statistically significant temporal change. The number of deaths decreased from 16,842 (95% UI: 10,965–21,984) to 10,728 (7,752–14,948), and the age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) was numerically lower in 2023 than in 1990 (0.97 vs. 1.58 per 100,000), although the EAPC of −1.61% (95% CI: −12.43 to 10.55) indicated no statistically significant temporal change. Total DALYs fell from 1,422,877 (95% UI: 925,055–1,858,977) to 897,565 (647,945–1,249,908), and the age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR) was numerically lower in 2023 than in 1990 (81.23 vs. 133.38 per 100,000), whereas the EAPC of −1.62% (95% CI: −12.44 to 10.54) suggested no statistically significant temporal change. By contrast, prevalent cases increased slightly (from 109,173 to 113,513), while the age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) remained stable (EAPC: 0.00%, −10.99 to 12.36). Throughout the study period, boys had higher ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR than girls, and both sexes showed broadly similar temporal patterns.

Geographical heterogeneity

Despite apparent regional improvements in mortality- and DALY-related burden, marked geographical heterogeneity in childhood brain and CNS cancers persisted across Asia (Tables 1 and S1–S3; Figures 1 and S1–S3). In 2023, the highest ASIRs were observed in Iraq (5.86 per 100,000, 95% UI: 4.12–8.35), Azerbaijan (4.94, 2.96–6.91), Uzbekistan (4.70, 3.91–5.76), Tajikistan (4.37, 3.01–5.89), and Turkmenistan (4.03, 3.28–4.94). Although some of these countries, particularly Iraq and Turkmenistan, had numerically higher ASIRs in 2023 than in 1990, the corresponding EAPCs were imprecise and should be interpreted cautiously. Similar apparent patterns were observed for mortality. The highest ASDRs in 2023 were recorded in Azerbaijan, Uzbekistan, and Turkmenistan, whereas higher ASPRs were observed in Iraq, South Korea, and Iran. By contrast, consistently lower burdens across all indicators were seen in the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Malaysia, and Kuwait.

Age- and sex-specific burden trends

From 1990 to 2023, the burden of brain and CNS cancers among children aged 0–14 years in Asia exhibited distinct age- and sex-specific patterns (Figures 2 and S4–S6). Age-specific incidence rates were generally lower in 2023 than in 1990, with infants (<1 year) consistently experiencing the highest ASIRs, decreasing from 5.13 (95% UI: 2.82–7.18) to 2.91 (95% UI: 1.85–4.61) per 100,000 (Figure 2A). Incidence rates also declined among children aged 2–4 years and 5–9 years, whereas incidence rates among those aged 10–14 years remained largely stable, decreasing slightly from 1.92 (95% UI: 1.25–2.59) to 1.88 (95% UI: 1.20–2.87 per 100,000), with a slight increase observed after 2010. In 2023, incidence rates were comparable between sexes, at 2.84 (95% UI: 1.69–4.86) per 100,000 in infant boys and 3.00 (95% UI: 1.60–5.30) per 100,000 in infant girls, declining to 1.94 (95% UI: 1.15–3.08) and 1.82 (95% UI: 0.97–3.20) per 100,000, respectively, among children aged 10–14 years (Figure 2B).

Age-specific prevalence rates remained relatively stable overall. Infants had the highest prevalence rates, which declined from 21.31 (95% UI: 10.92–33.72) to 14.19 (95% UI: 7.98–25.17) per 100,000 (Figure S4A). In contrast, prevalence rates increased after 2010 among children aged 5–9 years (9.41, 95% UI: 5.32–15.59 per 100,000) and 10–14 years (9.47, 95% UI: 5.14–16.00 per 100,000), in 2023. In 2023, prevalence rates were consistently slightly higher in girls than in boys across all age groups, including among infants (14.86 [95% UI: 7.11–27.64] vs. 13.57 [95% UI 7.63–24.70] per 100,000) (Figure S4B).

Age-specific mortality rates were lower in 2023 than in 1990 across all age groups, with the largest reduction observed among infants (from 2.69 [95% UI: 1.50–3.68] to 1.31 [95% UI: 0.89–1.96] per 100,000) (Figure S5A). In 2023, mortality rates remained highest among infants (1.31 per 100,000 in both sexes) and lowest among children aged 10–14 years (0.92 [95% UI 0.58–1.45] in boys and 0.78 [95% UI 0.46–1.25] per 100,000 in girls) (Figure S5B).

Age-specific DALY rates declined across all age groups. Infants consistently bore the highest burden, with DALY rates decreasing from 243.09 (95% UI: 135.60–332.21) to 118.63 (95% UI: 80.32–177.57) per 100,000 (Figure S6A). In 2023, DALY rates were similar between infant boys (118.67 [95% UI: 72.43–201.95] per 100,000) and girls (118.59 [95% UI: 68.41–202.51] per 100,000) and lowest among children aged 10–14 years (72.44 [95% UI: 46.13–113.40] in boys and 60.94 [95% UI: 36.43–98.10] per 100,000 in girls) (Figure S6B).

Inequality analyses

Inequality analyses based on the slope index of inequality (SII) ranked by the SDI showed that, for incidence, the SII among children aged 0–14 years with brain and CNS cancers in Asia was 0.51 per 100,000 (95% UI: −0.32 to 1.33) in 1990 and 0.30 per 100,000 (−0.69 to 1.28) in 2023. Corresponding concentration index estimates were consistent, decreasing slightly from 0.02 (95% UI: −0.05 to 0.09) to 0.01 (−0.07 to 0.08) (Figure 3). For prevalence, the SII increased from 4.10 per 100,000 (95% UI: 0.38–7.81) in 1990 to 6.32 per 100,000 (0.81–11.84) in 2023, with concentration index estimates rising from 0.11 (95% UI: 0.02–0.20) to 0.13 (0.03–0.22) (Figure S7). For mortality, the SDI-ranked SII shifted from 0.02 per 100,000 (95% UI: −0.46 to 0.50) in 1990 to −0.32 per 100,000 (−0.66 to 0.02) in 2023, accompanied by a decline in concentration index from −0.03 (95% UI: −0.10 to 0.03) to −0.10 (−0.17 to −0.02) (Figure S8). For DALYs, the SII decreased from 1.56 per 100,000 (95% UI: −38.27 to 41.38) in 1990 to −27.33 per 100,000 (−55.94 to 1.27) in 2023, while concentration index estimates declined from −0.03 (95% UI: −0.10 to 0.03) to −0.10 (−0.18 to −0.02) (Figure S9).

BAPC projections

Based on the BAPC model, the burden of brain and CNS cancers among children aged 0–14 years in Asia is projected to decline steadily up to 2050 (Figures 4 and S10–S12). In terms of absolute numbers, the annual number of incident cases is expected to decrease from 2023 onwards, reaching 5,411 cases (95% UI: −425 to 11,246) by 2050. The number of prevalent cases is projected to decline from 108,694 in 2023 to 16,055 (−5,752 to 37,861) by 2050. Similarly, the number of deaths is expected to decrease to 2,758 (242–5,275), and total DALYs to 185,505 (−12,046 to 383,056). In parallel, all age-standardized rates (ASRs) are projected to decline. By 2050, the ASIR is expected to decrease to 0.70 per 100,000 (−0.05 to 1.45) and the ASPR to 2.07 per 100,000 (−0.74 to 4.89). Correspondingly, the ASMR and ASDR are projected to decline to 0.36 (0.03–0.68) and 23.94 (−1.55 to 49.44) per 100,000, respectively. Sensitivity analyses across outcomes showed directionally consistent projected trends under alternative model specifications. Retrospective validation similarly indicated that predicted trajectories were broadly consistent with observed patterns over the validation period (Figures S13–S24).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the burden of brain and CNS cancers in children aged 0–14 years across Asia, using data from the GBD 2023 study. Our findings indicate that the ASMRs and ASDRs were numerically lower in 2023 than in 1990, whereas the trend in ASIRs was comparatively stable and should be interpreted with caution. However, disparities persist, particularly in regions with lower SDI scores, where some countries show increasing trends in incidence and mortality. Age- and sex-specific analyses revealed higher disease burdens among infants and boys, consistent with global patterns, highlighting the need for region-specific healthcare strategies to address these disparities.10

The temporal patterns in incidence and mortality should be interpreted as reflecting distinct underlying processes. In the present analysis, the downward trends in ASMRs and ASDRs are more plausibly related to improvements in clinical management and supportive care, whereas the trend in ASIRs was found to be comparatively stable and does not support a strong inference of declining disease occurrence. Observed incidence is shaped largely by case ascertainment, registry completeness, diagnostic access, and classification practices.11,12 Improved diagnostic capabilities, including wider availability of neuroimaging and more refined tumor classification, may increase the detection of previously unrecognized cases, rather than reducing incidence per se. By contrast, therapeutic advances—including safer maximal surgical resection,13 conformal radiotherapy,14 risk-adapted chemotherapy,15 and improved perioperative and supportive care—are more likely to have reduced mortality and disability, thereby contributing to the decline in ASMRs and ASDRs. The centralization of care in specialized pediatric neuro-oncology centers has probably further strengthened these gains by facilitating multidisciplinary treatment, better supportive care, and more consistent implementation of complex protocols.

However, the apparent improvement in disease burden does not reflect equal progress across all regions or age groups.16 While the ASIRs, ASMRs, and ASDRs were lower in 2023 than in 1990 across Asia, healthcare access and treatment remain significant challenges in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).17,18 These regions face a multitude of barriers including delayed presentation and diagnosis, limited access to pathology and imaging, scarcity of trained specialists (neurosurgeons, pediatric oncologists, and radiation oncologists), and insufficient supportive care infrastructure.19,20 The stable or increasing prevalence despite variable incidence trends may suggest that where survival rates are improving, a growing cohort of long-term survivors is emerging.21 This underscores the critical need for sustained focus on survivorship care. Long-term sequelae, including neurocognitive impairments, endocrine dysfunction, and secondary cancers, continue to pose substantial challenges for survivors and healthcare systems, particularly in settings with underdeveloped follow-up care programs.3,22

Geographical disparities remain a key feature of the disease burden across Asia, mirroring the global childhood cancer divide.1,23 The apparent upward patterns observed in some countries, particularly Iraq and Turkmenistan, should be interpreted cautiously. Broader contextual factors, including healthcare disruption in conflict-affected settings, environmental exposures, and limitations in diagnostic infrastructure, may influence both underlying disease burden and its measurement; similar challenges have been highlighted in other global burden analyses of conflict-affected regions.24 Within the GBD framework, these estimates may reflect not only the underlying epidemiological variation but also the temporal changes in cancer registration completeness, diagnostic access, and case ascertainment, which cannot be fully disentangled from modeled estimates alone. Incidence and prevalence should also not be expected to change in parallel because prevalence is shaped by case detection, survival duration, and mortality; accordingly, apparently stable prevalence despite rising incidence should not be regarded as a true epidemiological contradiction. Countries with higher SDI scores have seen greater improvements in healthcare access, diagnostic capacity, and treatment delivery, which are more likely to have translated into lower mortality and disability, rather than unequivocal reductions in incidence.25 In contrast, countries with lower SDI scores continue to face profound systemic challenges.26 Limited access to healthcare services, especially in rural areas, delays diagnosis and treatment, contributing to worse outcomes.27 The scarcity of pediatric cancer centers, trained personnel, and financial resources further exacerbates this burden, while limited access to advanced imaging and molecular testing constrains timely diagnosis and the implementation of precision medicine.28 These findings should also be considered alongside Global Cancer Observatory estimates, which are more directly anchored to registry-based cross-sectional data, whereas GBD provides modeled longitudinal estimates across incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs. Differences between the two frameworks, therefore, likely reflect methodological distinctions, rather than true inconsistency, and are best viewed as complementary perspectives on childhood CNS cancer burden.29

The socio-economic disparities in disease burden reflect broader determinants of health, including healthcare infrastructure, education, and access to public health programs. High-SDI countries have benefited from substantial investments in pediatric oncology, including specialized treatment centers and the availability of targeted therapies and immunotherapies, which have significantly improved survival outcomes.30,31 In contrast, countries with lower SDI scores require multifaceted strategies to enhance healthcare systems, improve early detection, and ensure equitable access to treatment.32,33 International collaborations, including the sharing of resources, expertise, and technology, are essential to bridging the gap between high- and low-income regions and addressing the inequalities in childhood cancer care.34,35

Age- and sex-specific trends offer insights into disease biology and risk. The relatively high burden observed in infants (<1 year) should be interpreted cautiously. Although some tumor entities presenting in the first year of life, including atypical teratoid/rhabdoid tumors and certain gliomas, are biologically distinct and often aggressive,10 pediatric oncology literature has more commonly reported peak incidence in early childhood rather than strictly in infancy. This pattern may, therefore, reflect both genuine biological heterogeneity and methodological features of the GBD framework, including age stratification, data sparsity, and modeled estimation. Tumors arising in infancy also present particular diagnostic and therapeutic challenges because treatment options may be constrained by toxicity concerns in the developing brain.36,37 The male predominance in incidence and mortality is a well-documented phenomenon in pediatric CNS cancers.38 This disparity is supported by studies suggesting sex differences in genetic susceptibility (e.g., mutations in TP53 and ACVR1), epigenetic regulation, and immune microenvironment, though the exact mechanisms remain an active area of research.39,40

In older children, the burden pattern differs from that observed in infants, and the underlying tumor spectrum is likely to be heterogeneous. Improved outcomes in this group might partly reflect greater tolerance of multimodal therapy and better delivery of age-appropriate supportive care.41 The growing prevalence of survivors underscores a paradigm shift: success must be measured not only by survival but also by the quality of survivorship.42 In practical terms, this requires structured long-term follow-up systems, including specialized survivorship or late-effect clinics, risk-based surveillance, and multidisciplinary assessment of neurocognitive, endocrine, neurological, and psychosocial sequelae.43 Emerging models from high-SDI settings suggest that coordinated survivorship pathways, survivorship care plans, and integration of rehabilitation and educational support can improve continuity of care and functional outcomes, although these approaches must be adapted to local workforce and health system capacity. Effective management of long-term complications, therefore, depends not only on survival gains but also on the development of sustainable follow-up infrastructure, particularly in resource-limited settings where survivorship services remain underdeveloped.29,44

The inequality analysis, based on the SII and CI, quantifies the steep socio-economic gradient in childhood CNS cancer burden.45 Notably, the SII for DALYs shifted from positive values in 1990 to markedly negative values in 2023, indicating a reversal in the distribution of disease burden from higher- to lower-SDI settings. The concentration of higher burden in lower-SDI countries is a consequence of structural inequities46 and reflects a clear epidemiological transition in which the burden of childhood CNS cancers has progressively shifted toward socio-economically disadvantaged populations. While slight improvements suggest the potential impact of public health initiatives, the slow pace highlights the need for sustained, targeted investment. This widening inequality likely arises because high-SDI countries have achieved substantial reductions in mortality and disability through advances in early diagnosis, specialized care, and treatment delivery, whereas comparable gains have not been realized in many low- and middle-SDI settings. Proven strategies include policy reforms for universal health coverage, training of mid-level providers, and adapted treatment protocols that maximize outcomes within existing constraints,33,47 alongside efforts to strengthen diagnostic capacity, referral systems, and access to multidisciplinary pediatric neuro-oncology care.

The BAPC projections suggest that future reductions in burden are likely to remain uneven across settings. These projected improvements should be interpreted primarily in relation to mortality and disability, rather than as unequivocal declines in disease occurrence. Although continued advances in diagnosis and treatment may further improve outcomes, their benefits will not be distributed equitably without deliberate investment in health system capacity.48 Achieving meaningful reductions in LMICs requires not only the translation of global innovations into locally feasible solutions but also sustained strengthening of core health system pillars, including timely diagnosis, referral pathways, specialized treatment access, supportive care, and long-term follow-up.35,49 Ultimately, reducing the burden of childhood CNS cancers in Asia demands a dual commitment: advancing the frontiers of science and ensuring that the foundations of equitable care are in place for every child.50

Limitations of the study

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the GBD study provides extensive standardized estimates, these remain dependent on the availability and quality of underlying country-level data; in settings with sparse or unreliable cancer registry data, burden may be underestimated. This challenge is particularly relevant in low-SDI settings, where incomplete surveillance and greater reliance on model-based estimation may limit the capture of long-term disease dynamics, as noted in other recent GBD-based analyses.24 Second, the GBD cancer framework relies on topography-based International Classification of Diseases (ICD) coding, rather than morphology-based or molecular classification, and excludes neoplasms with benign or uncertain behavior. As a result, histologically distinct pediatric CNS tumor entities, including high-grade and low-grade tumors, could not be distinguished, and overall clinical burden may be underestimated because some non-malignant tumors in children can still cause substantial morbidity and require intensive treatment. This limitation also prevented us from determining whether apparent improvements in outcomes reflect therapeutic advances for more aggressive tumors or, in part, increased detection of lower-grade tumors through improved imaging and diagnostic access over time; mortality- and survivorship-related trends should, therefore, be interpreted with caution. Third, the use of secondary health system data means that emerging influences, such as environmental change or newly recognized cancer risk factors, might not have been fully captured. Fourth, projections from the BAPC model were based on extrapolation of historical trends and did not explicitly account for abrupt demographic or healthcare shocks, and these should, therefore, be interpreted cautiously. Finally, the focus on ASRs may obscure important variation within vulnerable subpopulations, including children with limited access to care.

Method details

Study design and data sources

This study used data from the GBD 2023 study to examine temporal trends in the burden of brain and CNS cancers among children aged 0–14 years across Asian countries from 1990 to 2023.51 A population-based analytical framework was applied to estimate incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs, with stratification by age, sex, geography, and socio-demographic development.

The GBD 2023 study provides internally consistent and comparable estimates across locations and time by synthesising multiple data sources, including population-based cancer registries, vital registration systems, hospital records, verbal autopsy data, and published studies. Data sparsity and heterogeneity were addressed using established GBD modeling approaches, including spatial Gaussian process regression and the Cause of Death Ensemble model (CODEm).52 Mortality estimates were derived from cause-specific mortality models, whereas prevalence and DALYs were estimated within the non-fatal disease modeling framework. All results are reported with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs), reflecting both data and model uncertainty.

Case definition and study population

Brain and CNS cancers were defined according to the GBD 2023 cause hierarchy and identified using ICD-10 codes C70–C72 and ICD-9 codes 191–192. Only malignant neoplasms were included, consistent with the GBD cancer framework.51 Although this approach may underestimate the total clinical burden by excluding benign and uncertain-behaviour tumors, it ensures comparability across locations and over time. Because GBD classification is based on tumor topography rather than morphology, histological subtype and tumor grade could not be distinguished.

The study population comprised children aged 0–14 years in Asian countries and territories as defined by the GBD regional classification. Age was stratified into four predefined groups (<1, 1–4, 5–9, and 10–14 years), consistent with the GBD pediatric framework. These categories reflect clinically meaningful developmental stages, during which disease biology, diagnostic patterns, and treatment tolerance differ substantially. Analyses were conducted by sex and at both national and regional levels.

Outcome measures

Age-standardised rate

Age-standardised rates (ASR) were calculated to adjust for differences in the age distribution across the populations in the study. This ensures that comparisons between regions are not confounded by variations in age structure. In this study, age-specific rates for incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) were weighted and summed to derive the age-standardised incidence rate (ASIR), age-standardised prevalence rate (ASPR), age-standardised mortality rate (ASMR), and age-standardised DALY rate (ASDR), respectively. ASRs were primarily used to assess temporal trends and to facilitate comparisons across populations with differing demographic structures. In contrast, age-specific rates were used to characterise variations in disease burden across predefined pediatric age groups (<1, 1–4, 5–9, and 10–14 years). Temporal trends in disease burden were quantified using the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) in ASRs. EAPC was calculated to measure the relative change over time for each outcome, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) used to assess statistical significance. A positive EAPC indicated an overall upward trend, whereas a negative EAPC indicated an overall downward trend. However, trends were interpreted as statistically significant only when the 95% CI did not include 0; otherwise, they were considered stable or non-significant.

Inequality analysis

Inequality in the burden of brain and CNS cancers was assessed using the Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and the Concentration Index.45 These indices were calculated for each outcome (incidence, mortality, DALYs, and prevalence), stratified by the SDI, to assess socio-economic disparities in disease burden. The SII and concentration index were calculated by ranking all Asian countries/territories based on their SDI values. The population-weighted regression approach was employed to ensure that the indices reflect the gradient of disease burden across the entire socio-economic spectrum.45

Bayesian Age-Period-Cohort model

To project the burden of brain and central nervous system cancers to 2050, we used a Bayesian age–period–cohort (BAPC) model implemented in R with the BAPC package and integrated nested Laplace approximations.9 Counts were modeled within a Poisson framework, and age, period, and cohort effects were specified as smooth functions. We used the default model settings of the BAPC package, in which age, period, and cohort effects were modeled with second-order random walk priors and overdispersion was specified using an independent and identically distributed random effect. Projections were therefore based on extrapolation of the historical age–period–cohort structure under the RW2 smoothing assumption. Because the model did not incorporate explicit terms for sudden demographic or health-system shocks, projected estimates should be interpreted as trend-based forecasts conditional on the continuation of historical patterns rather than as scenario-based predictions under abrupt future disruption. To assess the robustness of the projections, we conducted sensitivity analyses under alternative model specifications, including different random walk structures (rw1 vs. rw2), second-difference settings, and stronger smoothing priors. We also performed retrospective validation by fitting the model to data from 1990 to 2013 and comparing predicted estimates with observed values for 2014–2023. These alternative specifications were selected to assess whether projection results were sensitive to the choice of random walk order, the inclusion of second-difference penalties, and the strength of smoothing assumptions.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.2. Statistical significance was defined using 95% confidence intervals, and all estimates were derived in accordance with standard GBD analytical procedures.
