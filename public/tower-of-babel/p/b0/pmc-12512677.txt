Implementation of the Noncommunicable Disease Capacity Assessment and Planning Process in Iraq, Jordan, and Pakistan
Authors: Randa K Saad, Meredith H Kruse, Patricia Richter, Lara Kufoof, Zakir Hussein, Yousef Khader
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAddressing noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) necessitates coordinated, country-tailored responses. The NCD Capacity Assessment and Planning (N-CAP) Process is a tool that strengthens public health functions and promotes multisectoral collaboration to achieve more sustainable and impactful progress. This case study describes the piloting and implementation of the N-CAP Process in the Eastern Mediterranean Region.MethodsThe N-CAP Process includes three sequential, facilitator-led activities: stakeholder mapping, a Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats Workshop (SWOT), and an N-CAP Workshop. Jordan, Iraq, and Pakistan were identified as countries with ministries of health with sufficient resources to implement the N-CAP Process and its recommendations. The N-CAP Process was piloted in Jordan (2021), revised for Iraq (2022), and implemented in its final version in Pakistan (2023). Qualified local facilitators and recorders were trained in each country prior to use. Stakeholder mapping was conducted in coordination with the ministries of health to identify multisectoral, technical, and policy stakeholders to participate in the activities. The SWOT Workshops convened stakeholders to assess the current landscape and identify critical NCD public health functions. The N-CAP Workshops used discussion guides to facilitate assessment and planning exercises that helped participants determine how to strengthen capacity within the critical NCD area.ResultsThe N-CAP Process led stakeholders to assess their country’s efforts across six public health domains (strategic direction, systems, resources, quality, engagement, and impact) and create prioritized recommendations or a plan to reach a higher level of functioning to better address NCDs. Participants in Jordan, Iraq, and Pakistan used the N-CAP Process to focus on data-to-action, coalition strengthening, and surveillance, respectively. While each country’s outcomes were unique and context-specific, common themes emerged, including health workforce, health information, health financing, and leadership and governance, all of which contribute to health system strengthening in different ways.ConclusionsThe N-CAP Process effectively creates structured, multisectoral recommendations and actionable plans to increase national capacity to address public health threats, such as NCDs. By facilitating comprehensive assessments and fostering governmental and multisectoral engagement, the N-CAP Process serves as an adaptable resource for countries and other regions grappling with similar public health challenges.Keywords: Noncommunicable diseases, Public health, N-CAP process, Multisectoral, Assessment and planning, Iraq, Jordan, Pakistan

Background

Noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), including cardiovascular diseases, cancer, diabetes, and chronic respiratory diseases, are the leading cause of death globally, responsible for 41 million deaths annually [1]. During decades of significant decline in the global burden of communicable diseases, some regions of the world began to see NCDs surpassing communicable diseases as the leading causes of death [2]. Following this upward trend, in 2019, the Eastern Mediterranean saw high rates of NCDs that resulted in costs of nearly US$50 billion in Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries [2, 3]. The burden of NCDs in the Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMR) continues to increase, and, if current patterns persist, as many as 2.4 million deaths due to NCDs are projected for 2025 [4]. In Jordan, NCDs already account for more than 78% of all deaths [5], while in Iraq and Pakistan the figures are approximately 55% [6] and 57% [7], respectively, underscoring the significant national contributions to the regional burden.

Globally, countries have responded by adopting regional frameworks and political pledges, developing national NCD guidelines, and establishing ministerial-level NCD departments [1, 8]. Despite this visible commitment, only two of the ten progress indicators that track achievements made in the prevention and control of NCDs are being met by at least half of the 176 countries that adopted the World Health Organization’s (WHO’s) Sustainable Development Goals [9]. To provide countries with new tools to focus and strengthen their efforts to address this costly and growing public health threat, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) (Atlanta, Georgia, United States), in collaboration with the International Association of National Public Health Institutes (IANPHI) (Atlanta, Georgia, United States), and Global Health Development | Eastern Mediterranean Public Health Network (GHD|EMPHNET) (Amman, Jordan) developed a country driven tool that helps countries to assess, prioritize, and create a plan to improve national capacity to address NCDs and respond more effectively to public health threats.

The NCD Capacity Assessment and Planning (N-CAP) Process aligns efforts to strengthen public health functions and promotes collaboration to achieve more sustainable and impactful progress [10]. Trained facilitators lead participants through in-depth conversations that assess the current level of progress within their country and establish the steps needed to reach a preferred level. The N-CAP Process uses discussion guides and a maturity model conceptual framework to describe current and preferred levels of progress within a critical NCD area. The result is a prioritized plan of next steps, which can be used to develop more comprehensive national plans. The N-CAP Process puts ministries of health in a lead position to encourage country ownership and implementation of the plan and engages multisectoral stakeholders to promote collaboration for greater impact.

This case study reports the process and outcomes of piloting the N-CAP Process in Jordan (2021) and Iraq (2022) and implementing it in Pakistan (2023). The three countries represent different burdens of disease and governance models. Jordan has a centralized Ministry of Health (MoH), while Iraq has a dual system including the Kurdish Regional MoH, and Pakistan has a federal structure with authority delegated to provinces.

Methods

A formal conceptual framework was not used for reporting outcomes; reporting was structured systematically around the study objectives and the sequential N-CAP Process steps.

The N-CAP process

The N-CAP Process includes three sequential, facilitator-led activities: stakeholder mapping, a Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats (SWOT) Workshop, and the N-CAP Workshop, Fig. 1. In line with the N-CAP Standard Operating Procedures, commercial interests that sell products proven to cause NCDs and/or inconsistent with healthy lifestyles (e.g., tobacco and alcohol industries) are explicitly excluded from participation. Stakeholder mapping is conducted in coordination with the MoH to identify multisectoral, technical, and policy stakeholders to participate in the subsequent activities. The SWOT Workshop convenes stakeholders to assess the current landscape and identify critical NCD public health functions. The N-CAP Workshop includes assessment and planning exercises of the identified public health function. Discussion guides are used to facilitate and describe the level of progress (beginning, progressing, advanced, and leading edge) within a country and six public health domains (strategic direction, systems, resources, quality, engagement, and impact). Procedures, roles, and responsibilities on how to conduct the N-CAP Process have been previously described [11].

Overview of the N-CAP process. Adapted from [Saad et al., 2024], which provides a detailed description of the process [11].

Country selection

Given the leading role of the MoH, conversations are held with MoH representatives to determine if sufficient resources are available for the successful implementation of the N-CAP Process and its recommendations. Jordan, Iraq, and Pakistan were selected by GHD|EMPHNET, a technical expert in the EMR, as countries that met these criteria.

In 2020, the Royal Health Awareness Society (RHAS) in Jordan, engaged in conversations with the CDC about how to better address NCDs in the country. The CDC’s Office of Global NCDs (Global Health System Strengthening Team as of October 2023) partnered with the CDC’s National Public Health Institute Program and IANPHI to adapt the Staged Development Tool into an assessment and planning resource that could be applied to the prevention and control of NCDs [12]. The CDC and RHAS collaborated with GHD|EMPHNET in the adaptation of the tool and implementation of the activities. The N-CAP Process was subsequently piloted in Jordan in 2021 [11].

In 2022, GHD|EMPHNET identified similar interest from the MoH in Iraq, which had a dedicated NCD department and updated its National NCD Strategy in 2018 to focus on engaging stakeholders for multisectoral collaboration [13]. A second pilot was conducted in Iraq in 2022.

The success of the pilots generated interest from other EMR countries, including Pakistan. In 2023, the N-CAP process was implemented at the sub-national level in Azad Jammu and Kashmir (AJK), aligning with Pakistan’s decentralized health system, where provinces and territories are primarily responsible for health service delivery and planning. The Chief of the Field Epidemiology and Disease Surveillance Division at the National Institute of Health in Islamabad chose these two provinces, based on their need, interest, and capacity to take an active leadership and decision-making role in the activities and outcomes. GHD|EMPHNET initiated official communication with the Director General of Health of AJK and the N-CAP Process was subsequently implemented. It was the first substantial NCD-related activity in the region.

Facilitator/Recorder identification and training

Before implementation, qualified local facilitators and recorders were identified and trained. The N-CAP Process Facilitator and Recorder Training Course is available to countries and other interested organizations as an e-learning [14].

To maintain neutrality and avoid potential conflicts of interest, the identified facilitators were not part of the Ministries of Health in the respective countries. This approach was intentional, as external facilitation was considered essential to ensure objectivity, foster open discussion among participants, and strengthen the credibility of the process.

In Jordan, RHAS and GHD|EMPHNET Headquarters teams were selected as facilitators and recorders and received virtual training from the CDC and IANPHI. In Iraq, GHD|EMPHNET enlisted a local consultant recommended by Iraq’s Field Epidemiology Training Program Director with experience working on NCDs and leading and facilitating high-level meetings to be the facilitator. GHD|EMPHNET’s Pakistan Country Office Manager was chosen as the facilitator for AJK based on his extensive experience in facilitating health discussions and high-level meetings. Facilitators in Iraq and Pakistan received training and technical guidance from GHD|EMPHNET Headquarters in collaboration with the CDC.

Jordan, 2021

In lieu of stakeholder mapping, GHD|EMPHNET, a founding member of the Jordan NCD Alliance (JNCDA), updated a stakeholder analysis conducted as part of the JNCDA’s 2019 NCD Advocacy and Communication Strategy (unpublished). The stakeholders identified were primarily representatives from health-related sectors as well as organizations from nongovernmental and academic sectors. No representatives from the commercial or industry sector were involved in the Jordan N-CAP Process activities. This highlighted an opportunity to broaden multisectoral engagement in future applications of the process.

Facilitators also reviewed the JNCDA’s SWOT analysis and the 2015–2019 National Strategy for the Health Sector in Jordan to identify critical NCD areas [15]. Results were discussed in January 2021 with Jordan’s Assistant Secretary General of Primary Health, the Director of the NCD-Directorate, the Head of the Cardiovascular Disease Department at the MoH, and the WHO’s NCD coordinator in Jordan. NCD data collection was determined as a priority. Accordingly, a Data-to-Action Discussion Guide was developed by CDC, IANPHI, and GHD|EMPHNET.

The N-CAP Workshop was held September 28-29th, 2021 in Amman, Jordan, and attended by 16 participants (Table 2). Participants assessed the country’s overall level of progress related to NCD data-to-action and each of the six domains as “beginning” or “progressing” and agreed the preferred levels of progress for each were “progressing” to “advanced” (Table 3).

Participants acknowledged there were NCD policies and numerous NCD meetings and workshops had been held in Jordan. However, efforts to tackle NCDs were fragmented. Participants identified challenges related to national NCD data priorities, health information systems (HIS), and human and financial resources. A list of recommendations to address these gaps was developed.

All participants noted several shortcomings in the 2015–2019 National Strategy for the Health Sector [15]. These included the absence of an action plan for multisectoral collaboration, clear delineation of sector roles and responsibilities, specificity on achieving outlined targets for NCDs, and adequate prioritization of national NCD data collection. The majority of participants decided that greater emphasis was needed on developing coordinated action plans and robust national-level data collection for NCDs. MoH representatives suggested a high-level governmental directive be issued to identify NCD priorities, encourage collaboration, and allocate resources. Participants proposed establishing a steering committee led by the MoH to update and execute a multisectoral national strategy for NCDs with defined data priorities and targets that would facilitate coordination among stakeholders, streamline efforts, and share resources to more effectively address NCDs.

MoH participants discussed the lack of a unified HIS across public and private sectors and an integrated system for NCD data that included processes for data validation and quality assurance. This limited data availability and utilization. Data analysis at ministerial units was described as basic and descriptive. Non-MoH participants felt that published NCD reports were inadequate and lacked recommendations, future projections, work plans, and cost-benefit analyses. These gaps made the reports less actionable for policymakers. Participants recommended that the MoH lead efforts to revamp the HIS and establish an integrated system with quality assurance and data validation mechanisms for more efficient NCD data collection, management, and dissemination. They also spoke about incentivizing centers and data collectors to encourage improvement and submit higher-quality data.

All participants reported an insufficient number of qualified experts within the HIS field. They noted that data collectors, distributed across governmental health centers, lacked essential qualifications, contributing to low-quality NCD data collection. MoH participants shared how these staff were overworked, inadequately trained, and not involved in the development of data collection tools. They also underscored a notable shortage of data analysis specialists and statisticians, and suggested the MoH host ongoing training programs for data collectors to improve their skills in data input and tool development. Additionally, MoH participants proposed bolstering the workforce capacity by recruiting statisticians and data specialists or establishing a dedicated unit within the MoH to improve data interpretation, visualization, and dissemination.

The majority of participants emphasized how, occasionally, funds were diverted to projects with limited impact on NCD prevention and control. They agreed to advocate for increased budget allocation for NCDs and to urge policymakers to prioritize sustainable health initiatives over short-term, ineffective projects. Partnerships with external donors that prioritized sustainable NCD projects and aligned with Jordan’s national NCD priorities were also encouraged.

The N-CAP Workshop’s outcomes were published by GHD|EMPHNET as a policy brief that informed the development of the MoH’s “Roadmap for Strengthening Primary Care in Jordan” and “NCD Strategy and Action Plan,” which are currently being developed [16].

Iraq, 2022

Based on lessons learned in Jordan, the N-CAP Process was updated to include standardized operating procedures and a library of seven Discussion Guides [10]. Adjustments were made to engage the MoH at an earlier stage and include a Participant Alignment session during the N-CAP Workshop to help orient participants to one another before the assessment and planning exercises [10]. A detailed description of these revisions has been published [11]. The updated N-CAP Process was piloted in Iraq.

In April 2022, facilitators and the MoH mapped key NCD stakeholders. Stakeholders were sent a questionnaire from GHD|EMPHNET’s Stakeholder Mapping and Analysis Toolkit to solicit information and guide engagement strategies [17]. Results were shared with the MoH to identify organizations to participate in the N-CAP Process activities.

On August 22nd, 2022, facilitators and the MoH conducted a SWOT Workshop with 12 participants in Baghdad, Iraq (Table 2). The outcomes revealed a need to strengthen the existing coalition that helped develop the National Strategy for Prevention and Control of NCDs in 2013 [18]. The Coalition Discussion Guide was selected.

The N-CAP Workshop was held October 19-20th, 2022 in Baghdad, Iraq, and included 27 participants (Table 2). All participants were members of the MoH-led NCD coalition. During the Workshop, participants assessed the country’s overall level of progress related to coalitions and the majority of the six domains as “progressing,” and the preferred level of progress for most domains was “advanced” (Table 3).

All participants agreed that there were internal challenges within the coalition related to strategic planning and communication. Then, they considered external factors that impacted the effectiveness of the coalition’s work and determined that resources, quality standards and measurements, and policies were key areas for improvement. A plan for next steps to address these gaps was developed.

Participants acknowledged that the existence of an NCD coalition suggested there were collaborative efforts to address NCDs in Iraq. However, not all members had strategic plans for NCD prevention and control, highlighting the need for coordinated priorities and strategies within the coalition. Communication issues within the coalition led to inefficiencies, fragmentation, and duplicative efforts across sectors. To improve effectiveness, participants agreed on a preliminary communication plan, led by the MoH, and endorsed by the General Secretariat of the Council of Ministers of Iraq to ensure compliance. The plan included hosting regular meetings with monitored attendance and establishing an official communication platform within 3–6 months after the Workshop.

According to the majority of participants, resource management was a critical issue that affected their work. The NCD coalition did not have dedicated or sustained funding. Activities were supported on an ad hoc basis, often relying on in-kind contributions from the MoH or temporary external donor-funded projects, which limited the coalition’s ability to maintain continuity and expand its reach. Financial constraints and a shortage of skilled human resources were major obstacles to NCD program implementation and sustainability. They proposed that the coalition address the Ministry of Finance to advocate for specific NCD funding in ministry budgets and seek alternative funding sources by pursuing international requests for proposals. Additionally, participants suggested creating a training program led by the Ministry of Education and Higher Education to enhance professional skills in NCD prevention.

Quality measurements and evaluation of NCD programs were recognized as areas requiring attention. Most participants reported inconsistent quality standards and a lack of guidelines to assess NCD programs. In this discussion, the concept of quality standards was applied to the delivery of healthcare services, implementation of preventive measures, adherence to legal regulations, and overall quality of interventions. Participants highlighted the need for clear frameworks from relevant ministries that include metrics to assess performance, safety, consistency, and effectiveness of interventions. Leveraging technology, such as digital monitoring and online platforms, was recommended to streamline monitoring and evaluation procedures and enforce legal compliance.

Participants emphasized the pivotal role of legislation in the coalition’s efforts. They reported weak adherence to laws and policies related to physical activity and nutrition due to resource shortages, inadequate training of enforcement agencies, and insufficient political will. Participants discussed how knowledge gaps of existing laws and policies often led to unintentional non-compliance. They also commented on the lack of systems to monitor and evaluate adherence to the laws. To address this, the participants agreed the coalition would work to review, update, and enforce laws and policies. They also committed to engage legislative authorities, such as the House of Representatives and the General Secretariat of The Council of Ministers, to prioritize and enact laws with legislative and monitoring measures that align with global NCD best practices.

All participants decided the coalition would conduct a continuous review and reassessment of the aforementioned points within 6–12 months and use ongoing communication to monitor and assess progress made in implementing the proposed action plan.

Pakistan, 2023

The version of the N-CAP Process piloted in Iraq was used in AJK in Pakistan. In March 2023, the facilitators and the Director General of the AJK Health Directorate identified key NCD stakeholders by using GHD|EMPHNET’s Stakeholder Mapping and Analysis Toolkit to assess the attributes of each stakeholder [17]. The results helped the AJK Health Directorate select organizations to participate in the N-CAP Process activities (Table 2). While this approach may have limited the inclusion of community-based perspectives and voices outside formal health structures, it provided clear alignment with government priorities and facilitated strong institutional ownership of the process.

The SWOT Workshop was held in collaboration with the AJK Health Directorate and 18 participants on June 12th, 2023, in Muzaffarabad, Pakistan (Table 2). NCD surveillance was identified as a critical NCD area and therefore, the Surveillance Discussion Guide was chosen.

The N-CAP Workshop was conducted August 24-25th, 2023 in Muzaffarabad, Pakistan. A total of 18 stakeholders attended (Table 2). Participants assessed the region’s overall level of progress related to NCD surveillance and the six domains as “beginning” to “progressing,” and the preferred level of progress was “advanced” (Table 3).

During the Workshop, participants listed several challenges related to NCD surveillance strategy and tools, resources, stakeholder engagement, data monitoring and quality assurance mechanisms, and integration of NCD surveillance within existing structures. Based on the assessment discussion, a plan of next steps was created to address the prioritized challenges.

A central issue was the need for a strategic plan to oversee NCD surveillance. Participants discussed the lack of data on NCDs and their risk factors and how a central program or policy to prevent and control NCDs in AJK did not exist. They agreed that a new dedicated steering committee led by the AJK Health Directorate would be formed and tasked with establishing a comprehensive strategic plan within a proposed six-month timeline. This plan would be integrated into the regional action plan for NCDs in AJK to strengthen the surveillance system both at the regional and district levels.

Resource constraints, such as access to essential laboratory and diagnostic tools, staff capacity building, and technological support, were found as significant challenges impacting NCD surveillance efforts. Participants considered how some areas in AJK lacked access to necessary resources due to its geography. To address this, the AJK Health Directorate planned to conduct an equipment needs assessment and develop a procurement plan to secure financial resources from the government and donors. This would enable health units to enhance their capacities for NCD diagnosis and data collection. Together, participants also outlined a 1–2-year timeframe to develop a comprehensive training plan to increase surveillance expertise within the workforce.

Most participants highlighted data quality concerns and communication gaps among health units in AJK, hindering effective surveillance and policymaking. They identified issues such as non-standardized data entry and outdated paper-based data collection methods. In response, the AJK Health Directorate offered to lead the development of digital data reporting systems and standardized coding procedures, like the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition (ICD-10), to improve real-time data entry and accessibility across health facilities. Participants also planned to create quality assurance mechanisms within the surveillance framework with regular site visits by the AJK Health Directorate and annual data quality assessments. They decided to establish an accountability framework to define performance expectations, ensure transparency, enhance accountability, and facilitate decision-making.

Participants underscored the lack of a comprehensive NCD surveillance system to inform policies and programs. They noted the existing diabetes surveillance efforts were limited and did not encompass all NCDs. Participants discussed aligning specialized programs, like the diabetes program, with NCD surveillance. Some participants also suggested conducting a baseline NCD survey for data gathering and regular impact assessments using surveillance data. They concluded this approach would enable evidence-based evaluations of NCDs to guide informed decisions for future health policies.

Participants agreed to follow a 12-month timeline for the execution of the abovementioned priorities. Follow-up meetings involving all relevant stakeholders were scheduled to monitor and track progress of the implementation.

Discussion

The three sequential activities of the N-CAP Process led stakeholders to assess their country’s efforts across six public health domains and create a prioritized list of recommendations or a plan of how to reach a higher level of functioning to address NCDs. EMR countries used the N-CAP Process to support their NCD response, with participants in Jordan, Iraq, and Pakistan, focusing on data to action, coalitions, and surveillance, respectively. While each country’s outcomes were unique and context-specific, common themes across the countries aligned with the WHO’s health system building blocks [19, 20], including health workforce, health information, health financing, and leadership and governance, all of which contribute to health system strengthening in different ways.

Adequate health workforce and financing are critical to addressing NCDs and were reflected in the systems, resources, and quality assessment discussions and planning by participants in Jordan, Iraq, and Pakistan [21, 22]. All three countries included strategies to increase funding for NCD programs and infrastructure and identified workforce capacity building as an opportunity for improvement. Training needs focused on data collection and surveillance workers, as well as professional healthcare workers.

Central to effective public health is the translation of data to evidence-based policies and programs that facilitate informed decision-making and mitigation of disease burden [23]. This theme most frequently arose during the systems and quality domain assessments. Participants in Jordan and Pakistan discussed the need to enhance electronic HIS to collect and analyze NCD-related data. Participants in Iraq recognized a gap in NCD programs and policy monitoring and evaluation and committed to working with the respective government ministries to create and implement tools for this purpose. Pakistan participants also prioritized developing a communication strategy for data dissemination and education. All countries emphasized the need for integrating quality assurance mechanisms into their data collection and analysis processes.

Leadership and governance are integral parts of the health system that require strategic planning, policy-making, and implementation oversight [24]. These aspects were prominent during the countries’ assessments of the strategic planning and impact domains. Participants in Jordan and Pakistan committed to creating comprehensive NCD action plans or strategies that included priorities, targets, and implementation guidelines. Jordan participants also focused on engaging multisectoral stakeholders in the strategy. In Pakistan, plans were made to leverage NCD data to inform policies. Iraqi participants prioritized evaluating and overseeing NCD-related programs and policies.

While there are similarities in recommendations or action plans across countries, the N-CAP Process was tailored to reflect the specific priorities and commitments of each government and stakeholders. For example, in Iraq, revitalizing an existing coalition was a priority for the Health Directorate for NCDs. Therefore, the discussion guide was adapted to help plan the coalition’s future activities rather than solely assess the internal processes of the coalition itself. Likewise, in Jordan and Pakistan, the outcomes of the N-CAP Workshops required strong governmental leadership; political restructuring in Jordan required a transfer of recommendations to incoming officials, whereas in Pakistan, the Health Directorate of AJK was motivated and willing to take on the majority of the responsibility for planning and implementation.

Implementation of the N-CAP process across different governance structures highlighted important contextual variations. Notably, definitions of what constitutes an NCD and the scope of essential NCD service packages were not uniform across countries. In Pakistan, the emphasis was primarily on the “big four” NCDs (cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancer, and chronic respiratory disease), while in Jordan and Iraq, mental health or injuries were explicitly incorporated into the national discourse. These differences in definitions shaped the priorities that emerged, and the interventions considered feasible. Notably, in Iraq, the Kurdish Regional Ministry of Health was not engaged in this pilot, which may have limited the inclusiveness of perspectives.

Moreover, the notion of “progressing” carried different meanings across contexts. For some stakeholders, progression was defined as piloting new service delivery models or introducing previously absent interventions; for others, it signified advancing institutionalization, coalition governance, or alignment with broader health system reforms. Recognizing this variation is important for interpreting the outcomes of the N-CAP Process and for ensuring that future applications of the model allow flexibility while maintaining comparability across countries.

Iraq’s use of the N-CAP Process provided a unique approach, as it was implemented within the existing NCD coalition. Participants were already familiar with one another, shared a history of collaboration, and could align the process with an established structure, which facilitated consensus building. At the same time, relying exclusively on coalition members carried the risk of insularity, as perspectives from outside the coalition, such as community groups, were not directly represented.

Likewise, the implementation of the N-CAP Process at the subnational level in AJK (Pakistan) differed in important ways from the national-level processes conducted in Jordan and Iraq. At the subnational level, health system governance and decision-making authority were more decentralized, which facilitated rapid engagement of local health authorities and adaptation of priorities to reflect provincial needs. This allowed for stronger ownership and faster alignment with local service delivery structures. By contrast, the national-level process in Iraq involved broader coalitions with multiple ministries and partners, requiring more time to achieve consensus, but resulting in strategies with wider political legitimacy and potential for national-scale resource mobilization. While subnational implementation enhanced agility and responsiveness, it also carried limitations, particularly the risk of fragmented priorities if not adequately linked with national frameworks.

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of the N-CAP Process. Its success depends on political stability, stakeholder cooperation, and strong leadership. An important consideration in interpreting the N-CAP Process outcomes is the role of power dynamics in shaping priorities. While the process was designed to be participatory, the composition of participants inevitably influenced which issues gained prominence and which received less attention. In line with Luke’s second dimension of power [25], certain actors, particularly those with institutional authority or technical expertise, were able to frame discussions and steer the agenda toward specific priorities. Although facilitators used structured techniques, such as round-robin input, to balance participation and encourage diverse input, the MoH steered priorities as they often held greater formal authority. During the assessment and planning exercises, stakeholders may not agree on priorities or be willing to take responsibility for action. In these situations, skilled facilitation and MoH leadership are needed to reach a resolution and way forward. Despite these limitations, when used among motivated stakeholders the N-CAP Process can help countries create collaborative strategies for more impactful progress.

Conclusion

As the global burden of NCDs continues to increase, countries need innovative tools to assess and plan how to respond. The N-CAP Process is one such tool. Participants from Jordan, Iraq, and Pakistan each used the N-CAP Process to assess, prioritize, and plan how to strengthen their country’s capacity and efforts in critical NCD areas. The outcomes of each implementation were based on the country’s unique needs and circumstances and reflected the priorities and commitments of the country’s MoH and key NCD stakeholders. The success of the N-CAP Process in the EMR and the design of the materials to align with key principles of health system strengthening demonstrates that the tool can benefit other countries’ efforts to assess and plan how to better address NCDs, a leading public health threat.
