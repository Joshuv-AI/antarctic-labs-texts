Optimizing nursing interventions and identifying prognostic factors for airway complications in pediatric bronchoscopy
Authors: Huahua Tian, Nan Li, Na Li, Junran Shi, Manli Wang, Linyang Xie, Hangxing Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the incidence and severity of post-bronchoscopy airway complications in pediatric patients, assess the impact of targeted nursing interventions, and identify prognostic risk factors, including procedure-related thresholds, influencing postoperative recovery.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on 216 pediatric patients who underwent flexible bronchoscopy at a tertiary referral center from February 2023 to February 2025. Airway complications, including hypoxemia (SpO₂ <90%), bronchospasm, laryngeal edema, aspiration pneumonia, and pneumothorax, were systematically recorded. Nursing interventions such as pre-procedural nebulization therapy, intraoperative oxygen supplementation strategies, and post-procedural airway management were evaluated. Multivariate logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed to identify independent predictors and clinically relevant cut-off values for severe complications.ResultsOf the 216 patients, 76 (35.19%) developed at least one airway complication. The most common events were transient hypoxemia (29.63%, n = 64), bronchospasm (12.50%, n = 27), and laryngeal edema (9.72%, n = 21), while severe outcomes such as aspiration pneumonia (3.70%) and pneumothorax (0.93%) were rare. Pre-procedural nebulization significantly reduced bronchospasm incidence (9.17% vs. 16.67%, p = 0.027). Subgroup analysis revealed no significant difference between salbutamol and budesonide (p > 0.05), though saline aerosols were not evaluated. Patients with laryngeal edema had a markedly higher need for oxygen supplementation compared with those without (85.71% vs. 28.21%, p < 0.001). Multivariate analysis identified younger age (OR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.12–1.77, p = 0.003), pre-existing respiratory disease (OR = 2.18, 95% CI: 1.39–3.42, p = 0.001), and prolonged procedure duration (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.08–1.47, p = 0.004) as independent risk factors. ROC analysis further indicated a cut-off of 7 min for procedure duration, above which the risk of severe complications increased significantly (18.6% vs. 7.2%, p = 0.003).ConclusionsAirway complications following pediatric bronchoscopy are frequent in high-risk populations, with most events being transient but clinically relevant. Individualized nursing interventions—particularly standardized pre-procedural nebulization and optimized oxygenation strategies—can effectively reduce complication rates. Younger age, pre-existing respiratory disease, and prolonged procedure duration (especially beyond 7 min) are key prognostic factors. These findings underscore the importance of risk stratification and structured perioperative nursing protocols to enhance safety and optimize recovery in pediatric bronchoscopy.

Introduction

Pediatric bronchoscopy is a vital diagnostic and therapeutic modality for evaluating and managing various respiratory conditions in children [1]. Its application ranges from the investigation of chronic cough and recurrent pneumonia to the removal of foreign bodies and the assessment of congenital airway anomalies [2, 3]. Despite its clinical utility, pediatric bronchoscopy carries a heightened risk of adverse events due to children’s unique anatomical and physiological attributes, including narrower airways, higher oxygen consumption, and comparatively underdeveloped alveolar structures [4, 5]. Consequently, postoperative complications such as hypoxemia, bronchospasm, laryngeal edema, and aspiration pneumonia can substantially prolong hospitalization and compromise clinical outcomes [6].

In recent years, procedural advancements and improved imaging techniques have contributed to safer bronchoscopy in both adult and pediatric populations [7]. However, children remain more susceptible to perioperative and postoperative respiratory complications [8]. Given the potential for airway compromise, robust perioperative management is essential, emphasizing precise anesthesia strategies and comprehensive nursing care [9]. Nursing interventions, specifically pre-procedural nebulization therapy and postoperative airway monitoring, have been recognized as critical in preventing complications and facilitating recovery [10]. Nonetheless, limited empirical evidence has systematically evaluated the efficacy of these interventions in reducing specific airway complications and improving prognoses in pediatric bronchoscopy recipients [11].

Several knowledge gaps persist regarding the optimal approach to mitigate postoperative airway complications in pediatric bronchoscopy. Although the incidence of common complications (e.g., transient hypoxemia and bronchospasm) is well documented, there is a lack of detailed data on how targeted nursing measures influence these outcomes [4, 6]. Moreover, while some risk factors—such as younger age and prolonged procedural duration—have been preliminarily associated with increased complication rates, few studies have examined these variables within a comprehensive analytical framework [12]. In particular, the direct effect of pre-procedural nebulization regimens, different modes of intraoperative oxygen supplementation, and systematic postoperative care protocols has not been robustly quantified [10, 12]. As a result, pediatric respiratory units lack standardized, evidence-based nursing protocols to effectively reduce the likelihood and severity of airway complications in susceptible patient groups [11].

In light of these considerations, the present study was designed to address the following objectives: (1) To assess the incidence and severity of airway complications—including hypoxemia, bronchospasm, laryngeal edema, and aspiration pneumonia—among pediatric patients undergoing flexible bronchoscopy; (2) To evaluate the impact of nursing interventions—specifically pre-procedural nebulization therapy, intraoperative oxygen supplementation strategies, and post-procedural airway management—on the occurrence of these complications; (3) To identify prognostic risk factors influencing postoperative outcomes, with a particular focus on patient demographics, pre-existing respiratory conditions, and procedural variables. Through a systematic examination of clinical and nursing records, as well as targeted statistical analyses, this investigation seeks to elucidate the multifaceted interplay between nursing interventions and procedure-related risks. The ultimate goal is to contribute evidence-based recommendations that may inform future standardized care protocols, minimize adverse events, and enhance the safety profile of pediatric bronchoscopy.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was a retrospective cohort analysis conducted at the Department of Pediatric Pulmonology in a tertiary pediatric hospital with a high volume of bronchoscopy procedures. The study period spanned from February 2023 to February 2025. Ethical approval was obtained from the hospital’s Institutional Review Board (IRB), and all procedures complied with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived; however, strict confidentiality protocols were followed, and all data were anonymized before analysis. Pediatric patients who underwent flexible bronchoscopy during the study period were systematically reviewed to assess the incidence of post-procedural airway complications, the effectiveness of specific nursing interventions, and potential prognostic factors influencing clinical outcomes.

Patient population

During the study period, a total of 243 pediatric patients underwent flexible bronchoscopy. Among them, 27 patients were excluded: 8 with severe congenital heart disease, 6 with chronic neuromuscular disorders affecting respiratory function, 5 with coagulopathies (platelet count < 50 × 10⁹/L or prothrombin time > 3× control value), 4 who required emergency endotracheal intubation during or immediately after bronchoscopy, and 4 who were lost to follow-up within the first 48 h post-procedure. After these exclusions, 216 patients fulfilled the inclusion criteria (aged 6 months to 12 years, with complete perioperative and postoperative nursing records) and were included in the final analysis. This final sample was used for all subsequent statistical analyses.

Data collection

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), underlying respiratory conditions such as asthma, recurrent pneumonia, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, or congenital airway anomalies, and the indications for bronchoscopy, were extracted from electronic medical records. Procedural details were recorded, including bronchoscopy duration, the type of intervention performed (such as bronchoalveolar lavage, biopsy, or foreign body removal), the sedation and anesthesia protocols used (including agents like propofol, dexmedetomidine, or sevoflurane), and the type of intraoperative oxygen supplementation (categorized as high-flow nasal oxygen, low-flow nasal cannula, or no supplemental oxygen).

Nursing interventions were systematically evaluated. Pre-procedural nebulization therapy was not applied uniformly to all patients; instead, it was selectively administered within 30 min before bronchoscopy to children considered at higher risk of peri-procedural bronchospasm (e.g., those with a history of reactive airway disease, recurrent wheezing, or foreign body aspiration). In emergency cases, nebulization could not always be performed due to time constraints. Salbutamol (0.15 mg/kg, maximum 2.5 mg) was the preferred agent, while budesonide (1 mg) was occasionally used in children with prominent airway inflammation or when β₂-agonists were contraindicated. Subgroup analyses were performed to compare outcomes between salbutamol and budesonide; isotonic saline nebulization was not included as a preventive measure, as it is not considered equivalent to bronchodilators or corticosteroids in preventing procedure-related bronchospasm.

Intraoperative oxygen supplementation strategies and post-procedural airway management were also documented, including humidified oxygen therapy, post-procedural nebulization, early mobilization, and airway clearance techniques. The primary outcomes assessed were the incidence and severity of post-bronchoscopy airway complications, including hypoxemia (defined as SpO₂ <90% for more than 30 s), bronchospasm (diagnosed based on clinical wheezing or increased airway resistance requiring bronchodilator administration), laryngeal edema (identified by post-procedural stridor, retractions, or increased work of breathing), aspiration pneumonia (confirmed by radiographic evidence of pulmonary infiltrates within 48 h post-procedure), and pneumothorax (diagnosed via bedside ultrasonography or chest X-ray when clinically indicated). Secondary outcomes included length of hospital stay, the need for intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and reintubation due to airway compromise.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics and procedural details. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were described as absolute counts and percentages.

Between-group comparisons were conducted to evaluate both the impact of nursing interventions and the comparability of patient subgroups. Specifically, baseline characteristics (age, sex, indication for bronchoscopy, and pre-existing respiratory comorbidities) were compared between patients who did or did not receive pre-procedural nebulization, and between those who did or did not require post-procedural oxygen supplementation. Independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests were applied for continuous variables, and χ² tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate.

To identify independent risk factors for severe airway complications, variables with P < 0.10 in univariate analyses, along with clinically relevant covariates, were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Multicollinearity was examined using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and model calibration was assessed by the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

To further explore procedure duration as a risk factor, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the optimal cut-off value for predicting severe complications, based on the Youden index. Patients were stratified according to this threshold, and complication rates were compared. Sensitivity analyses were performed to test the robustness of the findings, including subgroup stratification by age (< 3 years vs. ≥3 years) and by the presence of pre-existing respiratory disease. Internal validation was conducted using bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations). All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 216 pediatric patients were included in the study. The mean age of the cohort was 4.72 ± 1.83 years, with 115 males (53.24%) and 101 females (46.76%). The primary indications for bronchoscopy were suspected foreign body aspiration in 91 patients (42.13%), recurrent pneumonia in 63 patients (29.17%), chronic cough in 43 patients (19.91%), and other indications in 19 patients (8.79%).

When stratified by subsequent subgroup analyses, baseline characteristics including age, sex distribution, indication for bronchoscopy, and pre-existing respiratory comorbidities were comparable between patients who did and did not receive pre-procedural nebulization therapy, as well as between those who did and did not require post-procedural oxygen supplementation (all p > 0.05). Incidence of airway complications

Out of the 216 patients, 76 (35.19%) developed at least one airway complication following bronchoscopy. Transient hypoxemia was the most frequently observed complication, occurring in 64 patients (29.63%), followed by bronchospasm in 27 patients (12.50%) and laryngeal edema in 21 patients (9.72%). Aspiration pneumonia was diagnosed in 8 patients (3.70%), and pneumothorax was identified in 2 patients (0.93%). These findings are summarized in Table 2.

When stratified by indication for bronchoscopy, the overall complication rates were 36.3% in patients undergoing foreign body removal (n = 91), 34.9% in recurrent pneumonia (n = 63), 32.6% in chronic cough (n = 43), and 36.8% in other indications (n = 19). The incidence of bronchospasm was slightly higher in the foreign body removal group (14.3%) compared with recurrent pneumonia (12.7%), chronic cough (11.6%), and other indications (10.5%); however, these differences did not reach statistical significance (χ² test, p = 0.64). Thus, although a numerical trend toward more bronchospasm in foreign body cases was observed, the data did not support routine neuromuscular blocker use in this setting.

Effect of nursing interventions

The effect of nursing interventions was evaluated by subgroup analyses. Among the 216 patients, 120 received pre-procedural nebulization therapy, while 96 did not. The incidence of bronchospasm was significantly lower in the nebulization group, with 11 patients (9.17%) experiencing bronchospasm compared to 16 patients (16.67%) in the non-nebulization group (p = 0.027).

Regarding post-procedural airway management, the requirement for oxygen supplementation was strongly associated with the presence of laryngeal edema. Specifically, in the group with laryngeal edema (n = 21), 18 patients (85.71%) required supplemental oxygen, whereas only 55 of 195 patients (28.21%) without laryngeal edema required oxygen therapy (p < 0.001).

Subgroup comparison of nebulization agents showed that among the 120 patients receiving therapy, 102 were treated with salbutamol and 18 with budesonide. The incidence of bronchospasm was 8.8% in the salbutamol group and 11.1% in the budesonide group, with no statistically significant difference (p = 0.72). Given the small sample size in the budesonide subgroup, these results should be interpreted with caution. Collectively, these findings suggest that standardized pre-procedural nebulization reduces bronchospasm risk, while laryngeal edema is a strong predictor of post-procedural oxygen supplementation. Prognostic risk factors for severe complications

Multivariate logistic regression identified three independent prognostic factors associated with severe airway complications. Younger age was significantly correlated with increased risk (OR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.12–1.77, p = 0.003). Pre-existing respiratory diseases also strongly predicted complications (OR = 2.18, 95% CI: 1.39–3.42, p = 0.001), and prolonged bronchoscopy duration was associated with higher risk (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.08–1.47, p = 0.004).

Other covariates, including baseline SpO₂, CRP levels, airway anomalies, number of suctioning events, and sedation type, were included in the regression model but did not reach statistical significance (all p > 0.05). The full regression results are presented in Table 5.

In addition to the per-minute effect of procedure duration, ROC curve analysis was conducted to identify a clinically meaningful cut-off. The median bronchoscopy duration was 6 min (IQR: 4–9 min). ROC analysis indicated that a cut-off of 7 min provided the best discrimination for predicting severe complications, with an AUC of 0.71 (95% CI: 0.63–0.78). Patients with procedure times longer than 7 min experienced significantly more severe complications than those with shorter durations (18.6% vs. 7.2%, p = 0.003).

Discussion

This study identified a 35.19% incidence of airway complications following pediatric bronchoscopy, with hypoxemia (29.63%), bronchospasm (12.50%), and laryngeal edema (9.72%) being the most common. Severe complications such as aspiration pneumonia (3.70%) and pneumothorax (0.93%) were rare. Younger age, pre-existing respiratory diseases (OR = 2.18, 95% CI: 1.39–3.42, P = 0.001), and prolonged procedure duration (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.08–1.47, P = 0.004) were independent risk factors. Further ROC analysis revealed a clinically relevant cut-off of 7 min, above which the risk of severe complications increased significantly (18.6% vs. 7.2%, p = 0.003), providing a more practical threshold for clinical risk stratification. Pre-procedural nebulization therapy significantly reduced bronchospasm (9.17% vs. 16.67%, P = 0.027), and post-procedural humidified oxygen therapy was effective in decreasing laryngeal edema severity and oxygen requirement (P < 0.001). These findings emphasize that younger children and those with underlying lung disease require closer monitoring, and that specific nursing interventions can effectively reduce complications.

Our results align with prior studies, where pediatric bronchoscopy complication rates range from 10% to 35%, with hypoxemia consistently the most common event [13]. Carvalho et al. reported the persistence of desaturation even with prophylactic atropine in airway management, emphasizing the challenge of hypoxemia in pediatric airways [13]. Conversely, Chen et al. documented a 33% transient hypoxemia rate during rigid bronchoscopy, similar to our findings, suggesting that desaturation is frequent but generally well-managed [14]. Severe complications in our study, such as aspiration pneumonia (3.70%), were consistent with previous reports (2–5% incidence), confirming that while serious events are rare, they remain clinically relevant [15]. The relatively high overall complication rate observed in our center may be attributed to its role as a tertiary referral hospital managing a high proportion of younger and more complex patients, combined with stringent definitions and continuous monitoring that captured transient events often underreported elsewhere. Importantly, most complications were transient and self-limited, and severe outcomes remained uncommon.

The strong association between younger age and increased complication risk is well supported in the literature. Chen et al. found that children under 5 had significantly elevated hypoxemia risk during bronchoscopy, and Midyat et al. demonstrated a higher prevalence of airway abnormalities in infants, aligning with our observation of higher risk in younger children [16]. We further analyzed the relationship between bronchoscopy indications and peri-procedural complications. Although bronchospasm occurred somewhat more frequently in patients undergoing foreign body removal (14.3%) compared to other indications (11.1–12.7%), this trend did not reach statistical significance. Therefore, our data do not support routine use of neuromuscular blocking agents during foreign body removal procedures. Importantly, most cases were managed effectively with sedation alone, allowing preservation of airway protective reflexes. Nevertheless, in selected high-risk cases—such as repeated failed attempts at retrieval or patients with known reactive airway disease—the anesthesiologist may consider muscle relaxant use on an individualized basis.

Our finding that pre-existing respiratory disease significantly increased complication likelihood (OR = 2.18, P = 0.001) aligns with previous reports linking chronic lung conditions and syndromic anomalies to worse bronchoscopy outcomes [17, 18]. There is limited research on the impact of nursing interventions in pediatric bronchoscopy. In our cohort, pre-procedural nebulization was selectively administered to higher-risk children or withheld in emergency situations, which explains why not all patients received it. Among those treated, salbutamol was most frequently used, with budesonide applied in selected cases; subgroup analysis showed no significant difference between the two agents (p >0.05), although the small sample size in the budesonide group limited statistical power. These results suggest that both agents can be beneficial, but larger studies are needed to confirm comparative efficacy. Importantly, isotonic saline nebulization was not included, as current evidence does not support its equivalence to bronchodilators or corticosteroids in preventing procedure-related bronchospasm. Our finding that pre-procedural nebulization reduced bronchospasm incidence by nearly half supports its integration into standard care, particularly for patients with reactive airways. A meta-analysis by Ho et al. further supported the role of nebulized agents in improving bronchoscopic tolerance [19]. Post-procedural oxygen therapy has been suggested as beneficial in preventing hypoxemia-related complications, and our study provides quantitative evidence for its role in reducing laryngeal edema severity (P < 0.001) [20]. Together, these findings reinforce that nursing care strategies can significantly influence post-bronchoscopy outcomes and highlight the need for standardized perioperative nursing protocols [21, 22].

Our findings support targeted interventions to reduce airway complications. Pre-procedural risk assessment is essential, particularly for infants and children with respiratory diseases, who may benefit from bronchodilator therapy and optimized oxygenation strategies. Continuous intraoperative monitoring remains critical, as hypoxemia was the most frequent adverse event, requiring prompt oxygen supplementation and airway management. Post-procedural humidified oxygen therapy should be considered standard care for patients at high risk of laryngeal edema to reduce complications and oxygen requirements. The cut-off analysis further suggests that procedures exceeding 7 min warrant heightened vigilance, proactive airway management, and potentially limiting procedure duration in high-risk patients. Implementing structured nursing protocols for these interventions could further enhance patient safety.

This study’s retrospective, single-center design limits generalizability, and reliance on medical records may lead to underreporting of minor complications. While our sample size is moderate, larger multicenter studies could provide greater statistical power to analyze risk factors across different patient populations. Additionally, unmeasured confounders such as operator experience, sedation technique, and bronchoscope size could have influenced outcomes. Future research should include prospective studies to validate identified risk factors, multicenter trials to assess variation in practice patterns, and interventional studies to evaluate the efficacy of structured nursing care protocols, such as standardized nebulization therapy and post-procedural oxygen supplementation, in reducing complication rates. By refining perioperative nursing strategies and integrating clinically relevant cut-off thresholds, future studies can improve pediatric bronchoscopy safety and optimize patient outcomes.
