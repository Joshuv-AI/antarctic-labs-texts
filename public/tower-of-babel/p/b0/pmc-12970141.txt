Antibiotic-associated dysbiosis and bispecific antibody outcomes in multiple myeloma
Authors: Magdalena Corona, Roberto García-Vicente, Adolfo Jesús Saez-Marin, Raquel Ancos-Pintado, Alba Rodríguez-Garcia, Andrés Arroyo, Alberto Blanco, Ajai Chari, Thomas Martin, Jeffrey Wolf, Beatriz Rey-Bua, Maria-Victoria Mateos, K Martin Kortüm, Christine Riedhammer, Luis E Tamariz-Amador, Evelyn Valencia, Paula Rodríguez-Otero, Jesús San Miguel, Gladys Ibarra, Albert Oriol, Maria Teresa Cedena, Nieves López-Muñoz, Rafael Alonso, Maria Calbacho, Jose María Sanchez-Pina, María Linares, Joaquín Martínez-López
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe gut microbiota plays a critical role in regulating immune homeostasis and modulating responses to cancer immunotherapies. However, the impact of antibiotic-induced dysbiosis in patients with multiple myeloma (MM) treated with bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) remains unexplored. This multicenter, international study investigated whether antibiotic exposure prior to BsAb initiation alters the gut microbiome and affects clinical outcomes in patients with relapsed or refractory MM.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 237 adult patients with MM treated with CD3-engaging BsAbs across six academic institutions. Antibiotic exposure was defined as the administration of any broad-spectrum, non-prophylactic antibiotic within 30 days before BsAb initiation. Clinical outcomes included overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), and cumulative incidence of relapse, evaluated using Kaplan-Meier estimates, log-rank tests, and multivariable Cox and competing-risk regression models. Additionally, in a subset of 24 patients, peripheral blood samples were collected prior to BsAb infusion for immunophenotyping, cytokine profiling, and serum short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) quantification, while stool samples for 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) sequencing were collected in a subset of 19 patients.ResultsBroad-spectrum antibiotic exposure prior to BsAb therapy was associated with significantly inferior 1-year OS (60% (95% CI 44% to 81%) vs 77% (95% CI 71% to 83%), p=0.004) and PFS (26% (95% CI 14% to 47%) vs 53% (95% CI 46% to 61%), p<0.001), and higher relapse incidence (68% (95% CI 48% to 82%) vs 43% (95% CI 36% to 50%), p=0.004). In multivariable analyses, antibiotic exposure remained independently associated with poorer OS, PFS, and higher relapse risk. These associations were also observed within the subgroup of patients treated with CD3/B-cell maturation antibody-targeted BsAbs (n=155). Immunoprofiling revealed lower CD4+ T-cell counts (p=0.017) and reduced circulating cytokine levels among antibiotic-exposed patients. 16S rRNA sequencing demonstrated a marked depletion of SCFA-producing genera, including Roseburia and Eubacterium, accompanied by lower serum SCFA concentrations. Moreover, microbiota composition before BsAb treatment correlated with therapy response and treatment-related toxicity.ConclusionsAntibiotic-induced dysbiosis prior to BsAb therapy is associated with impaired immune reconstitution and inferior clinical outcomes in MM. These findings underscore the importance of antibiotic stewardship and suggest that microbiota-preserving strategies could enhance the efficacy of BsAb therapy in MM.Keywords: Multiple Myeloma, Bispecific T cell engager - BiTE

Background

Patients with multiple myeloma (MM) are highly susceptible to infections due to both the disease itself and immunosuppressive treatments. Disease-related factors contributing to this susceptibility include immunodeficiency from hypogammaglobulinemia,1 bone marrow suppression leading to B-cell dysfunction, and functional abnormalities of dendritic cells, T cells, and natural killer cells.2 Treatment-related factors further heighten this risk. For instance, chemotherapy, particularly high-dose melphalan prior to autologous stem cell transplantation, can induce prolonged neutropenia and lymphopenia.3 Corticosteroids also suppress the immune system, thereby increasing infection susceptibility.4 Additionally, targeted therapies and immunomodulatory drugs, such as bortezomib, lenalidomide, and thalidomide, can further compromise immune function.5 6 Consequently, patients with MM exhibit a significantly elevated risk of bacterial infections, up to seven times greater than healthy individuals of comparable sex and age.7 The frequency and types of these infections are influenced by the specific treatment regimen. For example, bacterial infections have been reported in 14% of patients receiving lenalidomide and dexamethasone, 30% of those treated with pomalidomide plus low-dose dexamethasone,8 and as high as 40% of patients undergoing B-cell maturation antibody (BCMA)-targeted chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T-cell therapy.9 Notably, infections were reported as a major cause of mortality in 22% of patients with MM.7 Prophylactic immunoglobulin replacement therapy has not proven effective in reducing infection rates among newly diagnosed patients with myeloma, who also exhibit suboptimal antibody responses to several vaccines.2 Accordingly, antibiotic treatment remains the primary strategy for managing or reducing infection risk10; however, this does not consistently translate to reduced mortality.11 Furthermore, antibiotic use may negatively impact progression-free (PFS) and overall (OS) survival in patients undergoing certain MM treatments, such as autologous stem cell transplantation,12 or increase the risk of acute graft-versus-host disease in allogeneic stem cell transplantation.13

These observed effects may be partially attributed to the negative impact of antibiotics on the gut microbiota,14 a factor increasingly recognized for its role in MM.15 The gut microbiota is essential for immune system development and function,15 and it also influences hematopoiesis.16 Gut microbiota dysbiosis can lead to heightened activation of inflammatory pathways, including interleukin (IL)-6 and NF-κB, potentially driving MM progression.17 For instance, Prevotella heparinolytica can induce the differentiation of T helper cell 17 (Th17) cells. These cells can then migrate from the gut to the bone marrow, where they contribute to MM progression.18 Furthermore, dysbiosis alters the production of microbiota-derived metabolites, which are critical regulators of host physiological and immune functions.15 19 Notably, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), including acetate, propionate, and butyrate, have shown potential in delaying the progression of monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance to MM.20 Moreover, the gut microbiota has been proposed as both a predictive biomarker and a potential modulator of the immune system, capable of enhancing immunotherapy efficacy.21 Specifically, it may influence responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors,22 hematopoietic stem cell transplantation,23 24 and CAR-T-cell therapy.25 26

Bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) represent a novel immunotherapeutic approach for the treatment of MM, achieving high response rates.27,29 While promising, preliminary findings indicate a potential for elevated infection risk compared with conventional MM therapies.30 Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the influence of antibiotic administration on the gut microbiota and its subsequent effect on the therapeutic efficacy of BsAbs in MM.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective multicenter study analyzed 237 adult patients with relapsed or refractory MM treated with BsAbs across six European and North American centers, from November 2017 to March 2024. Three patients who previously received a myeloma-directed BsAb were excluded, as were 38 patients lacking detailed information on antibiotic exposure during the 30 days prior to BsAb initiation.

Patients were grouped based on their antibiotic exposure prior to BsAb treatment. Those receiving any non-prophylactic oral or intravenous antibiotics within 30 days before the BsAb priming dose escalation were classified as having “antibiotic exposure”. Cefepime, meropenem, piperacillin-tazobactam, and ceftazidime-avibactam were considered “broad-spectrum exposure” due to their antipseudomonal activity and well-documented capacity to disrupt the gut microbiome.26 Antibiotic treatment was administered at the discretion of the treating physician, guided by confirmed or suspected infections, patient risk factors, and previous history of multidrug-resistant bacterial isolates.

Prophylactic infection prevention measures varied by center and included (but not limited to) intravenous or subcutaneous immunoglobulins, antibacterial prophylaxis, herpes prophylaxis, and Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis.10 30

Clinical definitions

Patients were classified as having high-risk cytogenetic abnormalities if they presented with at least one of the following: del(17p), t(4;14) or t(14;16). Overall response to BsAb treatment was assessed within 3 months, using the International Myeloma Working Group response criteria.31

Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) were graded according to the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy criteria.32 Additional clinical definitions are provided in online supplemental Material.

Characterization of T cells and serum analysis

T cell subpopulations from peripheral blood (PB) samples were analyzed using flow cytometry on a BD FACSCanto II cytometer (RRID:SCR_018056, BD Life Sciences, San Jose, California, USA). PB samples were collected at baseline, within the month preceding BsAb therapy initiation, with a median of 19 (IQR 26–13) days prior to BsAb treatment. The fluorochrome-labeled monoclonal antibodies used for this analysis are listed in online supplemental table S1. Serum samples were obtained from PB by centrifugation. The concentrations of 12 circulating cytokines were then measured using the LEGENDplex HU Th Cytokine Panel (catalog no. 741028, BioLegend, San Diego, California, USA) and SCFAs were quantified by gas chromatography and mass spectrometry (GC/MS) (RRID:SCR_019415, Agilent, Santa Clara, California, USA). Further details are provided in online supplemental material.

Fecal sample collection and microbiome sequencing

A subset of 19 patients from the study underwent prospective fecal microbiota analysis. Baseline stool samples were collected prior to the first BsAb dose, within the month preceding treatment initiation, with a median of 8 (IQR 17–4) days before the first infusion. Each patient provided a stool sample in a sterile container. In addition, in five patients, a second fecal sample was collected in a paired manner 3 months after BsAb initiation for longitudinal analyses. Immediately after delivery to the hospital, samples were aliquoted and stored at −80°C. Microbial DNA was extracted from 200 mg of fecal content using the AllPrep PowerFecal DNA/RNA Kit (cat. 80244, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and DNA concentration was determined using the Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer with the dsDNA HS Assay (cat. Q32851, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). 16S ribosomal RNA gene-targeted sequencing was performed to characterize the fecal microbiota, employing the Ion 16S Metagenomics Kit (cat. A26216, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Detailed procedures and bioinformatics analysis are provided in online supplemental material.

Statistical analysis

A centralized database was established to collect patient data from participating centers. This database included demographic information, disease characteristics, prior treatments, details of specific BsAbs administered, observed toxicities, and clinical outcomes. Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics, such as frequencies, medians, and IQR. Comparisons between groups were performed using Fisher’s exact or χ² tests for categorical variables, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables. OS and PFS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, compared using log-rank tests, and modeled using Cox proportional regression hazard models. Relapse or progression was evaluated using cumulative incidence methods.

Duration of antibiotic exposure, age, BsAb product, cytogenetic risk, and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, were specified a priori for inclusion in multivariable Cox regression models for the primary endpoints (PFS and OS), as well as in a multivariable competing risk regression model for the cumulative incidence of relapse. A subgroup analysis of OS, PFS, and relapse incidence was also performed in patients treated with CD3/BCMA-directed BsAbs.

Results

Patients’ baseline characteristics

We included 237 patients from six centers; among these, 58 (24%) had antibiotic exposure, and 36 (15%) received broad-spectrum antibiotics within 30 days before initiating BsAb treatment, with a median duration of antibiotics of 5 (IQR 2–8) and 4.5 days (IQR 2–6), respectively (online supplemental figure S1). Most patients (n=209, 88%) received BsAb treatment as monotherapy. Overall, 67% received a CD3/BCMA-targeted BsAb (n=155), 32% a CD3/GPRC5D-targeted BsAb (n=77), and 1% a CD3/FcRH5-targeted BsAb (n=2). Clinical characteristics are detailed in online supplemental table S2.

Correlation across prior antibiotic exposure and clinical outcomes

Three months after initiating BsAb treatment, the best overall response rate was 73%, with a complete response rate of 47%. With a median follow-up of 17.1 months (IQR: 9.1–32), the 1-year OS probability was 74% (95% CI 68% to 81%) and 1-year PFS was 49% (95% CI 43% to 56%). In univariable Cox regression analysis, antibiotic exposure prior to BsAb treatment was associated with reduced OS (HR 1.81 (95% CI 1.09 to 3.00), p=0.022) and PFS (HR 1.67 (95% CI 1.16 to 2.42), p=0.006) (figure 1A and B).

To further investigate the association between antibiotic exposure prior to BsAb initiation and clinical outcomes, we conducted univariable models for PFS and OS stratifying patients by non-antibiotic exposure (n=179), broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure (n=36), and non-broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure (n=22). Only broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure was associated with an increased hazard of PFS (p=0.001) and OS (p=0.005) compared with no antibiotic use (online supplemental figure S2).

Prior broad-spectrum antibiotic use reduces BsAb treatment efficacy

Patients were categorized into two groups based on broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure. Patients without any antibiotic exposure, or non-broad-spectrum exposure were included in the “non broad-spectrum” group. Median age was 62 years (IQR 54–69) in the broad-spectrum group (n=36) and 66 years (IQR 59–73) in the non-broad-spectrum group (n=201) (p=0.022). A higher proportion of patients in the broad-spectrum group had an ECOG performance status >1 (19% vs 5.5%, p=0.01). Other adverse MM features were comparable across the two groups (table 1). Most patients in both groups were treated with a BsAb as monotherapy: 86% in the broad-spectrum and 89% in the non-broad-spectrum exposure (p=0.8); but CD3/BCM-targeted BsAb, was most frequently administered in the non-broad-spectrum group (50% vs 70%, p=0.021).

The majority of patients in both groups received herpes and Pneumocystis prophylaxis treatment (broad-spectrum vs non-broad-spectrum 86% vs 90%, p=0.6; and 69% and 80%, p=0.14). Bacterial prophylaxis was infrequent in both groups, with 6.1% in the broad-spectrum group and 2.6% in the non-broad-spectrum group (p=0.3).

Incidence of CRS was higher in the broad-spectrum group (86% vs 62%, p=0.005) with a non-significant trend towards a greater rate of severe (grade >1) CRS (31% vs 19%, p=0.092). Additionally, the rate of severe hypogammaglobulinemia during the first 3 months of treatment was higher in the non-broad-spectrum group (90% vs 72%, p=0.012), leading to a numerically higher proportion of patients under periodic immunoglobulins replacement protocol (61% vs 44%, p=0.059). Other early toxicities, including ICANS and infections during the first 3 months of treatment were comparable across the groups (online supplemental table S3).

3 months after initiating BsAb treatment, best overall response rate was 75% in the non-broad-spectrum group and 67% in the broad-spectrum group (p=0.3), with complete response rates of 49% and 33% (p=0.08), respectively. Univariable logistic regression analysis revealed that a longer broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure was associated with a higher risk of not achieving a complete response at 3 months post-BsAb therapy (OR 1.20 (95% CI 1.04 to 1.45), p=0.032) (online supplemental table S4). Association across duration of broad-spectrum exposure and reduced likelihood of achieving a complete response was confirmed in a multivariable logistic regression model adjusted for duration of other antibiotics exposure, age, cytogenetic risk, BsAb product, and ECOG status (OR 1.27; 95% CI 1.04 to 1.67; p=0.043) (online supplemental table S5).

At 1 year, PFS probability was significantly lower in the broad-spectrum group, at 26% (95% CI 14% to 47%), than in the non-broad-spectrum group, at 53% (95% CI 46% to 61%) (p<0.001) (figure 2A). Duration of broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure was associated with reduced PFS in univariable (HR 1.10 (95% CI 1.05 to 1.15), p<0.001) (online supplemental table S6) and multivariable Cox regression analysis (HR 1.09 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.17), p=0.018) (online supplemental table S7). Notably, high cytogenetic risk remained independently associated with PFS (HR 1.96 (95% CI 1.32 to 2.90), p<0.001).

1-year OS probabilities were 60% (95% CI 44% to 81%) in the broad-spectrum group and 77% (95% CI 71% to 83%) in the non-broad-spectrum group (p=0.004) (figure 2B). Increased days of broad-spectrum antibiotic use were associated with decreased OS in univariable (HR 1.08 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.13), p=0.004) (online supplemental table S8) and multivariable Cox regression analysis (HR 1.15 (95% CI 1.03 to 1.28), p=0.011) (online supplemental table S9). High cytogenetic risk (HR 2.27 (95% CI 1.30 to 3.95), p=0.004) was also independently associated with decreased OS in the multivariable Cox regression analysis.

Additionally, 1-year cumulative incidence of relapse or progression was significantly higher in patients with broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure (68% (95% CI 48% to 82%) vs 43% (95% CI 36% to 50%), p=0.004) (figure 2C). Duration of broad-spectrum exposure (HR 1.08 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.15), p=0.010) and high cytogenetic risk (HR 1.78 (95% CI 1.19 to 2.66), p=0.005) were independently associated with increased relapse or progression in a multivariable competing risk regression analysis (online supplemental table S10).

Conversely, no excess of non-relapse mortality (NRM) was observed in patients exposed to broad-spectrum antibiotics (1-year NRM: 5.7% (95% CI 1.0% to 17.0%) in the broad-spectrum group versus 4.3% (95% CI 2.0% to 8.0%) in the non–broad-spectrum group; p=0.90). Death was predominantly attributable to disease progression or relapse in both the broad-spectrum and non-broad-spectrum groups (75% and 73%, respectively).

Given that patients treated with CD3/BCMA-targeted BsAb (n=155) constituted the largest and most homogeneous subgroup within the study population, we performed prespecified subgroup analyses in this cohort. Within this subgroup, 1-year OS and PFS rates were lower in the broad-spectrum group than in the non-broad-spectrum group. Specifically, the 1-year OS probability was 49% (95% CI 29% to 83%) and 80% (95% CI 74% to 88%) (p<0.001), while the 1-year PFS probability was 6.7% (95% CI 1.0% to 43%) and 58% (95% CI 51% to 68%) (p<0.001), respectively. In multivariable Cox regression analysis, longer duration of broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure was associated with inferior OS (HR 1.26; 95% CI 1.08 to 1.47; p=0.003) and PFS (HR 1.17; 95% CI 1.05 to 1.31; p=0.005) (online supplemental figures S3 and S4), (online supplemental tables S11 and S12). Similarly, the 1-year cumulative incidence of relapse or progression was 82% (95% CI 47% to 95%) in the broad-spectrum group and 37% (95% CI 29% to 45%) in the non-broad-spectrum group (p<0.001). Duration of broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure remained independently associated with a higher risk of relapse or progression in the multivariable competing risk regression analysis (HR 1.15; 95% CI 1.06 to 1.24; p<0.001) (online supplemental figure S5), (online supplemental table S13). Further analyses evaluating the impact of antibiotic exposure after BsAb treatment initiation are provided in online supplemental table S14.

Association between prior antibiotic exposure and baseline T-cell populations

To specifically evaluate associations with prior antibiotic administration, we characterized the immune status of 24 patients (figure 3A–B, online supplemental figure S6), of whom 63% were treated with CD3/BCMA BsAb, reflecting the distribution observed in the overall cohort (online supplemental table S15). Patients were grouped based on exposure to non-prophylactic antibiotics within 30 days prior to infusion, and we observed associated alterations in the T cell population of PB samples.

Patients receiving antibiotics, who also showed a higher proportion of penta-refractory disease (online supplemental table S16), exhibited a significantly lower proportion of total T cells (p=0.041) and, specifically, CD4+ T cells (p=0.017) in their PB before BsAb treatment. Further analysis of CD4+ T cell subpopulations revealed a significantly higher proportion of Th2 cells in patients who did not receive antibiotics (p=0.017; figure 3A). Additionally, these patients showed a non-significant trend towards a higher proportion of Th1 cells (online supplemental figure S6A).

We then measured the circulating concentrations of 12 serum cytokines related to T cell function. While no statistically significant differences were observed, patients receiving antibiotics tended to have lower concentrations of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα) and IL-2 (figure 3B; online supplemental figure S6B). Nonetheless, expression of the T-cell activation marker CD25 did not differ between groups (online supplemental figure S6C). No significant correlations were observed between any of these reported parameters and the number of days before treatment on which the samples were collected, indicating that sample timing did not affect the results (online supplemental table S17).

Association between prior antibiotic exposure and gut microbiota

To examine the impact of antibiotic administration within 30 days prior to BsAb treatment on gut microbiome composition and diversity, we conducted an exploratory high-throughput 16S ribosomal RNA sequencing analysis on 19 patient samples (online supplemental table S18), of whom 68% were treated with CD3/BCMA BsAb. No significant differences were found in baseline characteristics based on antibiotic exposure (online supplemental table S19). We first assessed α-diversity in baseline stool samples, calculating richness, diversity, and evenness indices. While no significant differences were observed, patients who received antibiotics showed a trend towards lower average Chao1 richness, Simpson diversity and Pielou evenness indices (figure 3C). None of the α-diversity indices correlated with the day of sample collection relative to treatment initiation (online supplemental table S17). β-diversity was analyzed using the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity metric, revealing a non-significant trend towards dissimilarity between groups (p=0.09, online supplemental figure S7A). We then performed taxonomic assignment of the gut microbiota (online supplemental figure S7B), which identified numerous differentially abundant taxa between patients who had received antibiotics and those who had not. At the family level, patients without prior antibiotic exposure exhibited a higher relative abundance of Streptococcaceae, whereas those who received antibiotics showed increased relative abundances of Oscillospiraceae, Peptococcaceae, Erysipelatoclostridiaceae, Anaerovoracaceae, Hungateiclostridaceae, and Erysipelotrichaceae (figure 3D). At the genus level, numerous differences were also found between the two groups, in particular a greater abundance of Limosilactobacillus, Roseburia and (Eubacterium)_ventriosum_group in the microbiota of patients without prior antibiotic exposure (figure 3E). Notably, these genera are known producers of SCFAs in the large intestine.33,35

Consequently, we quantified circulating serum levels of SCFAs in 24 patients using GC/MS. Consistent with the microbial composition findings, patients without antibiotic exposure exhibited significantly higher concentrations of total SCFAs (p=0.022), particularly acetate (p=0.045) (figure 3F). Moreover, comparable results were obtained when SCFA levels were quantified in fecal samples (online supplemental figure S8).

Association between intestinal microbiota and toxicity and response to treatment

We next explored the relationship between gut microbiota composition and treatment toxicity, response, and survival. Due to a lack of stool samples from patients experiencing ICANS, toxicity analyses were restricted to CRS, and patients were categorized based on CRS development, with comparable baseline characteristics (online supplemental table S20). While no significant differences in overall microbiota diversity were observed, patients with CRS exhibited non-significantly lower Simpson and Pielou indices, which could suggest reduced diversity and evenness (figure 4A). β-diversity analysis was analyzed using the Bray Curtis dissimilarity metric (online supplemental figure S9A). At the taxa level, patients with CRS showed increased levels of families such as Prevotellaceae, Carnobacteriaceae, Rikenellaceae, and Enterobacteriaceae, as well as genera such as Eisebergiella, Dialister, Enterorhabdus, and Odoribacter. Conversely, patients without CRS showed significantly higher relative abundances of the families Bacteroidaceae, Butyricicoccaceae, and Desulfovibrionaceae, as well as the genus Lactobacillus (figure 4B; online supplemental figure S9B). Notably, Butyricicoccus and Ruminococcus are known SCFA producers.36 37

To analyze the impact of the gut microbiota on treatment response, we compared the microbiota of patients who achieved complete response within the first 3 months with those who did not, finding no differences in baseline characteristics between the groups (online supplemental table S21). While α-diversity parameters did not differ between these groups (online supplemental figure S10A), β-diversity analysis revealed a significant difference in overall microbiome composition (p=0.03) (figure 4C). Accordingly, several taxa showed significantly different relative abundances between patients achieving complete response within 3 months and those who did not. Consistent with our findings on CRS, SCFA-producing groups, including Hungatella, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Slackia, and Eubacterium, were associated with achieving complete response within 3 months (figure 4D; online supplemental figure S10B).38,41

Association between gut microbiota and survival

To investigate the effect of the gut microbiota on long-term survival, we classified patients into high or low α-diversity groups using maximally selected rank statistics, and compared their survival rates. Patients with a low Simpson’s diversity index and a low Pielou’s evenness index showed significantly poorer PFS (p=0.034 and p=0.031, respectively) (figure 5A–B).

Similarly, we classified patients based on the relative abundance of gut microbiota taxa. We found numerous taxa with potential prognostic value. Patients with higher relative abundances of the genera Erysipelatoclostridium, Allistipes, and Parabacteroides exhibited worse PFS (figure 5C; online supplemental figure S11A). Conversely, patients with higher relative abundances of Streptococcus, Anaerostipes (figure 5D; online supplemental figure S11B), and the SCFA producers Eubacterium_halii_group, Roseburia and Butyricicoccus, demonstrated significantly improved survival (figure 5E; online supplemental figure S11C).

Comparison of baseline and post-treatment gut microbiota diversity and taxonomic composition

Finally, to investigate the impact of BsAb treatment on gut microbiota diversity, we compared 10 paired samples from five patients collected at baseline and 3 months after BsAbs infusion. We observed a significant decrease in the Pielou evenness index (p=0.019), as well as a non-significant trend towards lower α-diversity indices (online supplemental figure S12A). At the taxonomic level, pretreatment samples showed a higher relative abundance of the order Desulfovibrionales, while samples collected 3 months post-treatment exhibited a greater relative abundance of Staphylococcales (online supplemental figure S12B).

Discussion

This multicenter study is, to our knowledge, the first to comprehensively evaluate the impact of antibiotic exposure on clinical outcomes, immune responses, and gut microbiota composition in patients with MM treated with BsAbs. Our findings demonstrate that prior exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics is associated with profound alterations in gut microbial diversity and composition, accompanied by impaired immune responses and reduced efficacy of BsAb therapy.

Antibiotic exposure negatively affected treatment efficacy. Complete response rates, as well as 1-year PFS, OS, and cumulative incidence of relapse were lower in the broad-spectrum group than in the non-broad-spectrum group. These findings were supported in a subgroup analysis of 155 patients treated with CD3/BCMA-directed BsAbs, further reinforced these findings, revealing even more pronounced differences in clinical outcomes between groups. While associations between gut microbiome and treatment efficacy or toxicity have been documented in other immunotherapy treatments, such as CAR-T-cell therapy,25 26 42 our study suggests, for the first time to our knowledge, that antibiotic exposure—particularly broad-spectrum antibiotics—may compromise the efficacy of BsAb therapies, potentially modulating the host immune response and/or mechanisms of disease progression.43

An exploratory study was conducted to examine the association between antibiotic exposure prior to BsAb therapies and immune parameters, finding that patients who received antibiotics had significantly lower peripheral T cell counts, particularly CD4+ T cells. This reduction might be associated with impaired immune competence during BsAb treatment, potentially impairing their antitumor activity, a phenomenon previously observed with CAR-T therapies in lymphoma.26 Notably, patients not receiving antibiotics showed a shift towards a higher proportion of Th2 cells, along with a trend towards decreased Th1 cells. This observation is particularly relevant given the established interactions between T helper cell populations and the gut microbiota,44 which can be disrupted by antibiotics.45 Interestingly, the use of antibiotics reduces the production of IL-4, which could result in the inhibition of Th2 cell development.46 Additionally, in preclinical models, a close relationship has been demonstrated between Th2 immune inflammation and the gut microbiota,47 which could be altered as a consequence of antibiotic use. Importantly, the panel of T cell markers analyzed was limited; expanding the panel to include additional activation and inhibitory markers in future studies will provide valuable insights. Furthermore, patients exposed to antibiotics displayed lower concentrations of cytokines, including TNFα and IL-2, indicating a potential decline in immune function. Indeed, Lankelma et al demonstrated that systemic mononuclear cells produce lower levels of cytokines, such as TNFα, after antibiotic therapy.48 Collectively, these preliminary results indicate that antibiotic-associated dysbiosis can influence immune function during BsAb treatment, potentially reducing the efficiency of the immune system in myeloma control.

Patients without prior antibiotic treatment exhibited a greater prevalence of SCFA-producing taxa such as Roseburia34. Additionally, these patients exhibited a higher abundance of Streptococcus in their gut microbiota, which has been positively correlated with the expression of T-cell activation genes and T-cell abundance in patients with breast cancer.44 Conversely, antibiotic-treated patients had a greater abundance of Erysipelotrichaceae and Anaerovoracaceae, taxa associated with negative health outcomes.49 50 These shifts in microbiota composition resulted in corresponding changes in SCFA levels, with non-antibiotic-exposed patients displaying significantly elevated serum acetate. Given the vital roles of SCFAs in maintaining gut barrier integrity and modulating immune responses,51 52 these differences may contribute to the improved outcomes in patients without antibiotic exposure.

Microbiota diversity has emerged as a prognostic factor for survival in immunotherapy.53 54 Lower diversity and evenness, as indicated by the Simpson and Pielou indices, respectively, were associated with poorer PFS, while higher diversity correlated with improved survival within this relatively small patient subcohort. Specifically, an increased abundance of SCFA-producing bacteria, such as Eubacterium, Roseburia, and Butyricicoccus, was associated with better survival, highlighting the potential role of the gut microbiota as a modifiable factor influencing treatment efficacy. Notably, a higher abundance of SCFA producers such as Eubacterium, and elevated SCFA levels, have previously been reported as factors associated with minimal residual disease negativity in patients with MM following induction therapy.55 56 In addition, in this study we have not only observed that the microbiota before BsAb treatment is associated with the response and immune status, but also that the BsAb treatment itself also affects the intestinal microbiota. Interestingly, we have detected an enrichment of the order Staphylococcales following BsAb treatment.

While antibiotic exposure significantly alters gut bacterial density and composition, antimicrobial stewardship in hematologic malignancies must always prioritize patient safety due to patient immunosuppression. In clinical settings such as sepsis or febrile neutropenia, prompt initiation of broad-spectrum antibiotics is mandatory, as the immediate risk of infection-related mortality clearly outweighs any potential microbiome-mediated effects on PFS or OS. Broad-spectrum antibiotics therefore remain an essential component of care for immunocompromised patients. However, non-infectious febrile syndromes, such as tumor-related fever or CRS, are also common in this population,57 and several studies have documented prolonged antibiotic use in the absence of microbiological confirmation and clinical stability.58 In this context, recent evidence supports that antibiotic de-escalation or discontinuation can be safe in patients who achieve sustained clinical stability and apyrexia, even in the setting of neutropenia, when infection has been reasonably excluded.59 Accordingly, our findings should not be interpreted as discouraging appropriate antibiotic use, but rather as supporting judicious reassessment and timely de-escalation when clinically feasible, in alignment with established antimicrobial stewardship principles.

The main limitation of this study is its retrospective design, which may underestimate certain variables, particularly in the long-term follow-up setting. Nevertheless, we analyzed a large international cohort with detailed antibiotic exposure data, supporting the robustness of our findings. Second, we lack validated comorbidity or frailty scores data, which could also impact clinical outcomes. Although ECOG performance status was incorporated into both univariable and multivariable models and did not demonstrate a significant effect, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be excluded. A further limitation is the limited number of patients who received broad-spectrum antibiotics prior to BsAb initiation compared with the non-broad-spectrum group, which underscores the need to validate these clinical findings in larger patient cohorts. In addition, antibiotic exposure—particularly the use of broad-spectrum agents—may partially reflect underlying infection severity or clinical complexity, which could also justify immune dysfunction. While we included available clinical data to contextualize this potential confounding, residual overlap between infection-related effects and antibiotic exposure may remain. In relation to the biological analyses, factors such as the modest sample size, the small number of patients who received non-prophylactic antibiotics in the gut microbiota analysis, and variability in antibiotic exposure and sample collection timing should be considered when interpreting the results. However, the comparable clinical profiles across groups strengthen the consistency of our biological observations, which provide a valuable basis for future studies in larger cohorts.

In summary, our findings suggest that antibiotic exposure may be associated with impaired treatment outcomes, altered immune profiles, and changes in gut microbiota composition in patients treated with BsAb. The adverse impact of antibiotics, particularly broad-spectrum, on survival, both OS and PFS, underscores the need for judicious antibiotic use in patients with cancer. Gut microbiota, particularly SCFA-producing taxa, may modulate therapy response and prognosis. While exploratory, these observations support further investigation into microbiome-informed approaches as potential strategies to optimize outcomes in MM.
