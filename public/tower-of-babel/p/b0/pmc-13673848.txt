In-hospital mortality and predictors among hospitalized adults with acute heart failure in Southern Ethiopia: retrospective cohort study
Authors: Sura Markos, Mosa Ebrahim, Betrie Bikamo, Yusuf Haji, Bereket A. Tegene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute heart failure is a leading cause of hospital admissions and mortality in Africa. Data on in-hospital outcomes and predictors of mortality remain limited in southern Ethiopia. Understanding these factors is crucial for improving patient management and resource allocation.ObjectiveTo determine the in-hospital mortality rate and identify factors associated with death among adults admitted with acute heart failure at Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study including 480 adult patients hospitalized with acute heart failure between September 2021 and September 2023. Clinical, laboratory, and treatment data were extracted from medical records using standardized definitions. Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of in-hospital mortality, expressed as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsThe median age of patients was 67 years, and 51% were female. In-hospital mortality was 17%, with most deaths occurring early during hospitalization (median time to death: 4 days; IQR: 2–5). Rheumatic heart disease (31%), cardiomyopathy (20%), and ischemic heart disease (15%) were the most common underlying etiologies. Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction occurred in 64% of patients, whereas reduced ejection fraction was more common among those with cardiomyopathy or ischemic heart disease. Independent predictors of in-hospital death were acute kidney injury (AOR 8.9, 95% CI 4.2–18.8), mechanical ventilator support (AOR 4.4, 95% CI 2.3–8.5), and cardiogenic shock (AOR 2.2, 95% CI 1.4–3.4).ConclusionIn-hospital mortality was high, with most deaths occurring early during admission. Acute kidney injury, cardiogenic shock, and mechanical ventilatory support were strong independent predictors of mortality. These findings support close monitoring and timely management of high-risk patients during hospitalization.

Introduction

Acute heart failure (AHF) is characterized by a rapid onset or worsening of heart failure (HF) symptoms, resulting in cardiorespiratory compromise and urgent hospitalization [1]. Globally, Heart failure remains a major global health burden and is associated with substantial morbidity, mortality, and healthcare utilization across both high-income and low- and middle-income settings [2, 3]. The global burden of heart failure continues to increase, driven by population aging, improved survival after ischemic heart disease, and the rising prevalence of major cardiovascular risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and obesity [2, 4]. In-hospital outcomes vary across regions, with reported mortality rates in high-income settings generally ranging from 4% to 10%, influenced by healthcare access, early risk stratification, and implementation of guideline-directed therapies [3, 5, 6].

In Africa, HF accounts for 3–7% of general medical admissions and over 30% in specialized cardiovascular unit [2, 7, 8]. Patients hospitalized with HF are often younger than their counterparts in developed countries, and rheumatic heart disease remains a prominent etiology, reflecting persistent infectious and valvular disease burdens [6, 8, 9]. In-hospital mortality in sub-Saharan Africa is higher, ranging from 20 to 30%, due to limited healthcare infrastructure, delayed presentation, and restricted access to guideline-directed therapies [3, 5, 8, 10].

In Ethiopia, hospital-based studies report in-hospital mortality rates between 10.6% and 23.8%, with predictors including cardiogenic shock, pulmonary hypertension, acute kidney injury, and adverse drug events [7, 11–13]. Most studies, however, were conducted in central or northern regions, often with small sample sizes, heterogeneous HF populations, and without standardized operational definitions of AHF. These limitations reduce the representativeness of findings and limit the ability to generalize to other regions. Southern Ethiopia, in particular, may differ in patient demographics, prevalence of comorbidities, healthcare-seeking behavior, and access to specialized care, which can significantly influence outcomes.

Despite existing studies, comprehensive region specific data from southern Ethiopia remain limited. Understanding predictors of in-hospital mortality in this region is crucial to guide targeted interventions, optimize resource allocation, and improve patient outcomes. While large scale international registries, such as the European Heart Failure Survey II (EHFS II), OPTIMIZE-HF in the United States, and regional registries like JCARE-CARD in Japan and China-HF, provide valuable benchmarks for heart failure outcomes, their applicability to Ethiopia is limited due to differences in healthcare systems, population characteristics, and available resources [14–17].

To our knowledge, this study represents the largest single-center cohort of patients hospitalized with AHF in Ethiopia. Although previous studies have reported AHF outcomes from other regions of the country, evidence from Southern Ethiopia remains limited. Conducted at Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, a major tertiary referral center serving a large and diverse population in Southern Ethiopia and neighboring regions, this study determined the in-hospital mortality rate and identified factors associated with death among adults admitted with AHF. These findings provide the first systematic assessment of AHF outcomes in Southern Ethiopia and contribute region-specific evidence to the national literature.

Methods and materials

Study area, period, and design

This retrospective cohort study evaluated in-hospital mortality and its predictors among adults admitted with AHF at Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (HUCSH) between September 1, 2021, and September 30, 2023. Retrospective design enabled the analysis of existing clinical records from a large, real-world patient population, allowing efficient evaluation of outcomes and associated factors without the logistical challenges of prospective enrollment. HUCSH, the largest tertiary and teaching hospital in southern Ethiopia, has approximately 500 beds and provides specialized services across cardiology, nephrology, endocrinology, pulmonology, gastroenterology, and neurology. The hospital serves a regional population exceeding 18 million, receiving referrals primarily for complex or severe conditions.

Study population and eligibility

The study included adult patients (≥ 18 years) admitted with a clinical diagnosis of AHF during the study period. AHF was defined according to guideline-based criteria as new-onset or acutely decompensated heart failure requiring urgent hospitalization, supported by documented clinical signs and investigations such as echocardiography, chest X-ray, or elevated natriuretic peptides.

Inclusion criteria required complete documentation of demographics, primary diagnosis, laboratory and imaging results, treatment modalities, and in-hospital outcomes. Records missing any of these critical elements were excluded. Recurrent admissions were also excluded to ensure that each patient contributed a single index hospitalization, minimizing clustering and bias in regression analyses. Patients were not stratified into acute versus chronic decompensation due to inconsistent documentation; all included cases, however, represented acute presentations necessitating hospitalization. All eligible patients admitted during the study period who met the inclusion criteria were included consecutively; therefore, no formal sample size calculation was performed. The final sample represents a census of all eligible cases within the study timeframe. Based on the observed in-hospital mortality rate (17%), 82 outcome events were available, allowing inclusion of up to eight independent variables in multivariable logistic regression in line with the recommended minimum of 10 events per variable to reduce overfitting.

Data collection and procedures

Data were collected using a structured investigator-developed data extraction checklist informed by established heart failure guidelines and prior literature, covering key clinical, laboratory, treatment, and outcome variables. Two trained internal medicine physicians independently reviewed all records, resolving discrepancies through consensus. Inter-rater reliability was assessed in a 10% random sample, yielding a kappa coefficient of 0.87, indicating substantial agreement.

Collected variables included demographics, comorbidities, vital signs, laboratory and imaging results, precipitating factors, treatment modalities, and in-hospital outcomes. Data were managed using Kobo Toolbox with built-in validation rules to minimize entry errors. Missing key outcome or baseline data led to exclusion, whereas partial missingness in other variables was handled via complete-case analysis. Variables with more than 10% missing data were evaluated for potential bias, and sensitivity analyses were planned.

Data management and statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 21. Continuous variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Median and IQR were used because continuous variables were not normally distributed, as assessed by visual inspection of histograms and/or Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables with a p-value < 0.2 in bivariable analysis, as well as clinically important variables based on prior literature, were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. Prior to regression analysis, the distribution of variables and adequacy of cell counts were assessed to ensure model stability. Model assumptions, including multicollinearity (VIF < 2) and overall model fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow test), were evaluated. Interaction terms between major organ dysfunctions were examined but were not significant. Continuous variables were dichotomized only when clinically justified, and sensitivity analyses with continuous forms ensured robustness. Before regression analysis, the distribution of categorical variables was reviewed to ensure adequate cell counts, and sparse categories were merged when necessary to maintain model stability and avoid estimation bias. Adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were reported, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. Patients who left the hospital against medical advice (AMA) were not included in the primary mortality analysis because their final outcomes after discharge could not be reliably determined. Mortality analyses were therefore restricted to patients with documented in-hospital outcomes (death or discharge). To assess potential bias introduced by AMA cases, a sensitivity analysis excluding these patients from the denominator was performed. This approach minimized outcome misclassification and ensured that mortality estimates reflected only confirmed in-hospital events.

Bias mitigation strategies

Potential biases inherent in retrospective studies were addressed through strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, standardized variable definitions, dual independent data extraction, and inclusion of clinically relevant covariates in regression models.

Operational definitions

To ensure consistency, all variables were defined using standardized, guideline-based criteria. Acute heart failure was defined as new-onset or acutely decompensated heart failure requiring urgent hospitalization with clinical and investigational evidence [18, 19]. Cardiogenic shock was defined as sustained systolic blood pressure below 90 mmHg with signs of end-organ hypoperfusion requiring inotropic or vasopressor support [20]. Pulmonary edema was identified by imaging evidence of congestion. Hypertensive heart disease was defined by chronic hypertension with echocardiographic evidence of left ventricular hypertrophy or diastolic dysfunction [21]. Cardiomyopathy was categorized as dilated, hypertrophic, or restrictive [22]. Acute kidney injury (AKI) was defined according to the KDIGO serum creatinine criteria as an increase in serum creatinine by ≥ 0.3 mg/dL within 48 h or an increase to ≥ 1.5 times baseline within 7 days [23]. Urine output criteria were not assessed because these data were not consistently available. Diabetes mellitus was defined as fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, random glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL, or documented physician diagnosis with treatment. Other comorbidities were defined according to international guideline thresholds or documented long-term treatment [24]. Poor drug adherence was defined as documented non-adherence to prescribed medications in the medical record, based on patient or caregiver report as recorded by the treating clinician.

Results

Cohort selection and patient characteristics

Between September 2021 and September 2023, 520 adult patients were admitted with acute heart failure at Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. Forty patients were excluded due to incomplete medical records or missing key outcome data, leaving 480 patients for analysis. The cohort had a female-to-male ratio of 1.04:1, with 245 females (51%) and 235 males (49%). The median age was 67 years (IQR: 58–74), with 216 patients (45%) aged 65–74 years. Most patients were admitted via the Emergency Outpatient Department (n = 264, 55%), followed by medical wards (n = 192, 40%) and the intensive care unit (n = 24, 5%). Two-thirds of patients (n = 312, 65%) had no prior hospitalization for heart failure, while 168 patients (35%) had at least one previous admission. Clinical, laboratory, and precipitating factors

On presentation, vital sign assessment revealed that the majority of patients had normal blood pressure (n = 356, 74%), whereas 124 patients (26%) were hypotensive (SBP < 90 mmHg). Tachycardia was observed in 346 patients (72%), with a median heart rate of 102 beats per minute (IQR: 88–118). Hypoxemia (oxygen saturation < 90%) was present in 206 patients (43%). Pulmonary congestion on chest X-ray was identified in 258 patients (54%), with 141 patients (29%) demonstrating moderate to severe pulmonary edema (grades 2–3), highlighting substantial hemodynamic compromise in a significant proportion of admissions.

Electrocardiographic evaluation showed that 463 patients (96%) had at least one abnormality. The most frequent findings were sinus tachycardia in 178 patients (37%), left ventricular hypertrophy in 134 patients (28%), right ventricular hypertrophy in 88 patients (18%), and atrial fibrillation in 70 patients (15%). Less common abnormalities included STEMI (n = 19, 4%), left bundle branch block (n = 10, 2%), right bundle branch block (n = 10, 2%), sinus bradycardia (n = 10, 2%), and atrial flutter (n = 4, 1%). It should be noted that multiple ECG abnormalities could occur in a single patient, and thus percentages do not sum to 100%.

Laboratory assessments demonstrated that 378 patients (79%) had hemoglobin levels > 9 g/dL. Serum creatinine > 1.2 mg/dL was observed in 180 patients (38%), hyponatremia (< 135 mmol/L) in 132 patients (28%), and potassium derangements in 84 patients (17%). These findings indicate that a substantial subset of patients presented with renal dysfunction and electrolyte imbalances, which are known contributors to acute decompensation and adverse outcomes in heart failure.

Precipitating factors were identified in 472 patients (98%), with multiple factors possible per patient. The most frequent precipitants were pneumonia in 130 patients (27%), poor medication adherence in 120 patients (25%), arrhythmias in 48 patients (10%), and hypertension in 48 patients (10%). Less common factors included anemia, acute kidney injury, thyrotoxicosis, pregnancy, and pulmonary embolism, collectively affecting 50 patients (10%). Etiology and heart failure phenotype

Among the 480 patients, rheumatic heart disease (RHD) was the most frequent underlying etiology, affecting 150 patients (31.3%). Cardiomyopathy was the second most common cause, observed in 96 patients (20%), and followed by ischemic heart disease in 67 patients (13.9%), hypertensive heart disease in 57 patients (11.9%), and cor pulmonale in 54 patients (11.3%). Heart failure phenotypes were classified according to left ventricular ejection fraction. HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) occurred in 307 patients (64%), HFrEF in 129 patients (27%), and HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF) in 44 patients (9%). Among patients with HFrEF, 102 (79%) had underlying cardiomyopathy, 74 (57%) had ischemic heart disease, and 4 (3%) had rheumatic heart disease (RHD).

Comorbidities and treatment modalities

Among the 480 patients, hypertension was the most prevalent comorbidity, affecting 264 patients (55%). Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was present in 86 patients (18%), metabolic syndrome in 48 patients (10%), and type 2 diabetes mellitus in 38 patients (8%). Less frequent comorbidities included malignancy in 8 patients, asthma/COPD in 4, HIV infection in 1, and chronic liver disease in 1 patient. These data highlight that cardiometabolic disorders are the predominant comorbidities in this cohort, consistent with the known epidemiology of heart failure in sub-Saharan Africa.

Pharmacologic management was guided by contemporary heart failure guideline-directed medical therapy recommendations, consistent with the 2021 ESC Guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of acute and chronic heart failure and the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure. During hospitalization, loop diuretics were administered to 470 patients (98%) and spironolactone to 331 patients (69%), while beta-blockers were prescribed to 236 patients (49%). Additional therapies included antibiotics in 235 patients (49%), anticoagulants in 134 patients (28%), and blood transfusions in 44 patients (9%). Mechanical ventilatory support was required in 24 patients (5%). In-hospital outcomes

Of the 480 patients included in the cohort, 321 (67%) improved and were discharged, 82 (17%) died during hospitalization, and 77 (16%) left the hospital against medical advice. Because the outcomes of patients leaving against medical advice could not be reliably determined, these individuals were excluded from the mortality analysis. Mortality estimates and regression analyses were therefore restricted to patients with confirmed in-hospital outcomes. The median time to death was 4 days (IQR: 2–5), indicating that mortality occurred early during hospitalization.

Factors associated with in-hospital mortality

Multivariate logistic regression analysis of 480 patients identified three independent predictors of in-hospital mortality. AKI was associated with a markedly increased risk of death (AOR 8.9, 95% CI 4.2–18.8, p<0.001). While the number of patients with AKI was relatively small, sensitivity analysis using penalized regression confirmed that AKI remains a strong independent predictor, indicating the robustness of this association. Mechanical ventilatory support conferred a 4.4-fold higher risk of mortality (AOR 4.4, 95% CI 2.3–8.5, p<0.001), and cardiogenic shock increased the odds of death 2.2-fold (AOR 2.2, 95% CI 1.4–3.4, p=0.002) (Table 5).

Discussion

This study found a high in-hospital mortality rate (17%) among patients admitted with acute heart failure at a tertiary hospital in southern Ethiopia. Mortality occurred early during hospitalization, with acute kidney injury, cardiogenic shock, and mechanical ventilation identified as strong independent predictors of death. Rheumatic heart disease was the most common underlying etiology, reflecting the local disease pattern. Overall, these findings indicate a high-risk clinical profile and substantial early in-hospital mortality burden in this setting. The observed in-hospital mortality was comparable to that reported from Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (17.2%) and Jimma Medical Center (21.2%), higher than that reported from Gondar (10.6%), but lower than that observed at St. Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College (23.8%) [7, 11–13]. Differences in reported mortality rates may reflect variations in study populations, healthcare settings, and methodological approaches.

Globally, in-hospital mortality is lower, ranging from 4% in the United States [16] to 4.2% in the THESUS–HF survey across sub-Saharan Africa [8], highlighting the influence of healthcare access and resource availability [6, 8, 16]. More than half of our cohort was aged > 65 years. In this population, the management of AHF is often complicated by multimorbidity, frailty, polypharmacy, and atypical clinical presentations, which can adversely affect outcomes. Individualized treatment and multidisciplinary care are therefore particularly important in older adults with AHF [25].

The median age of patients was 67 years (IQR 58–74), with nearly half younger than 65, reflecting a relatively younger population compared with the BREATHE and China-HF registries, and Italian registry cohorts [14, 26–28]. This is consistent with the predominance of RHD, which accounted for nearly one-third of cases, alongside hypertensive heart disease (13.5%) and other cardiometabolic conditions, emphasizing the dual burden of valvular and non-communicable disease in Ethiopia and sub-Saharan Africa [6, 8].

Cardiogenic shock, acute kidney injury (AKI), and mechanical ventilation were strong independent predictors of mortality. Cardiogenic shock increased the risk of death 2.2-fold, consistent with Ethiopian studies and resource-limited settings where hemodynamic instability drives poor outcomes [29–31]. Regional and international data confirm the prognostic significance of shock and multi-organ dysfunction, with patients requiring mechanical ventilation or renal support showing markedly higher mortality [32–34]. AKI was the strongest predictor of in-hospital mortality, conferring an 8.9-fold increased risk (AOR 8.9, 95% CI 4.2–18.8, p < 0.001). This association likely reflects the close interaction between cardiac and renal dysfunction, whereby impaired cardiac output and systemic hypoperfusion contribute to worsening renal function and adverse clinical outcomes. Similar associations have been consistently reported in Ethiopian studies and international heart failure registries, where worsening renal function is associated with substantially higher in-hospital mortality [11, 12, 29, 32, 35, 36]. From a clinical perspective, this finding highlights the importance of routine renal function assessment and early management of kidney injury in patients hospitalized with acute heart failure. Although the number of patients with AKI was relatively small, sensitivity analysis using penalized regression confirmed the robustness of this association. Prior HF hospitalization did not significantly affect mortality, contrasting with some international studies [7, 28], likely due to differences in healthcare access and baseline disease severity.

Rheumatic heart disease was the leading etiology (31.3%), followed by dilated cardiomyopathy (20%) and ischemic heart disease (13.9%), mirroring patterns reported in other sub-Saharan African cohorts [37]. These etiologies are commonly associated with advanced structural heart disease and delayed presentation, which may contribute to more severe clinical status at admission and the higher in-hospital mortality observed in the region [6, 8, 38].

HFrEF was mainly associated with cardiomyopathy and ischemic heart disease, whereas HFpEF predominated in patients with hypertensive heart disease or RHD without severe systolic impairment, consistent with both local [12] and international findings [6, 8, 39, 40].

In-hospital mortality among patients with AHF at HUCSH remains high. Cardiogenic shock, AKI, and the need for mechanical ventilation emerged as the strongest predictors of death. RHD is a leading etiology, particularly among younger patients, while HFrEF is closely linked to structural myocardial injury. These findings highlight the importance of early identification of high-risk patients, timely organ support, and management strategies tailored to the local epidemiology of heart failure.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, it was conducted at a single tertiary hospital, which may overrepresent patients with more severe illness and contribute to higher observed in-hospital mortality than in the general population. Second, the retrospective design relies on medical records, which may introduce misclassification or documentation errors in diagnoses, laboratory results, and treatments. Third, data on socioeconomic factors and health behaviors such as income, education, smoking, and medication adherence were not available, limiting assessment of their impact on outcomes. Fourth, the findings may not be generalizable to rural populations or other regions of Ethiopia with different healthcare access and disease patterns. Approximately 16% of patients left the hospital against medical advice, and their post-discharge outcomes were unknown; while these patients were excluded from mortality analysis to avoid misclassification, this may have introduced a modest selection bias. Finally, although key clinical factors were adjusted for, unmeasured confounders may still influence the results, and causality cannot be inferred. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the characteristics and predictors of mortality among patients with acute heart failure in southern Ethiopia

Conclusions

The findings of our study clearly depict the high in-hospital mortality rate of patients with heart failure at tertiary level care showing the gap in proper patient management. Moreover, cardiogenic shock, AKI and respiratory failure (requiring ventilator support) were found to have statistically significant association with in-hospital mortality. Therefore, patients with acute heart failure should undergo risk stratification, and management should be tailored to address mortality risks.
