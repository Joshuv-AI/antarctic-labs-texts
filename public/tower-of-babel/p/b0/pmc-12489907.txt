Toxoplasma gondii infection in the endangered Amami Woodcock, Scolopax mira (Aves: Charadriiformes)
Authors: Ryotaro Suzuki, Toshihiro Tokiwa, Keiko Ito, Ryouta Torimoto, So Shinya, Makoto Haritani, Masami Yamamoto, Hisashi Yoshimura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Toxoplasma gondii is a parasitic protozoan for which family Felidae serves as the definitive hosts. In regions where native felids are absent, introduced domestic cats (Felis catus) act as reservoirs of T. gondii, posing a threat of infection to various wildlife population. A population of feral cats has been identified on Amami-Oshima Island located in the southern part of the Japanese Archipelago, and molecular examination confirmed that the T. gondii they harbour has spread to endemic mammals. In this study, we aimed to detect T. gondii in the Amami Woodcock (Scolopax mira), a bird species endemic to Amami-Oshima Island. DNA was extracted from the brain tissue of twenty-two birds, and molecular detection of T. gondii was performed using a commercial T. gondii detection kit based on real-time polymerase chain reaction. Two birds (9.1%) tested positive. The determined nucleotide sequences were 100% identical to that of the T. gondii reference sequence. Histopathological examination and ultrastructural analyses revealed terminal colonies in the cardiomyocytes of one bird, which were immunohistochemically confirmed as T. gondii. Here, we report the first record of T. gondii infection in the Amami Woodcock. Given its endangered status, ongoing research on the prevalence and pathogenicity of T. gondii is warranted.

Introduction

Toxoplasma gondii is an obligate intracellular protozoan parasite belonging to the phylum Apicomplexa (Dubey, 2008). Two types of hosts are required for the life cycle of T. gondii. First, members of the family Felidae, such as the domestic cat (Felis catus), serve as definitive hosts where sexual reproduction occurs. Second, a wide range of mammals and birds act as intermediate hosts, where T. gondii undergoes asexual reproduction. T. gondii is believed to have spread worldwide along with domesticated cats, resulting in infections in native wildlife (Lehmann et al., 2006; Deem et al., 2010; Taylor et al., 2023; Work et al., 2016). This parasite can cause mortality and disease in highly susceptible hosts, and poses a serious clinical concern for wildlife conservation. Mortality due to toxoplasmosis, especially in island animals lacking native felids habitats, poses threat to the conservation of endangered species such as the Hawaiian monk seal (Monachus schauinslandi) (Barbieri et al., 2016), Hawaiian Crow (Corvus hawaiiensis) (Work et al., 2000), and Little Penguin (Eudyptula minor) (Campbell et al., 2022).

Amami-Oshima Island, located in the southern part of the Japanese Archipelago, is home to many endemic species, including the Amami rabbit (Pentalagus furnessi) and Amami spiny rat (Tokudaia osimensis), both of which are listed as endangered species (Sugimura et al., 2003; IUCN, 2024a; IUCN, 2024b). Although lacking native felids on this island, many non-native feral cats inhabit the island's forests (Shionosaki et al., 2016). Several studies have demonstrated that predation by cats affects the habitat of endemic species on the island (Shionosaki et al., 2015, 2016; Suzuki and Oumi, 2017). Furthermore, antibody testing for T. gondii conducted in feral cats from Amami-Oshima Island between 2013 and 2017 revealed a seroprevalence of 9.0% (123/1363) (Matsuu et al., 2017) . In addition, a case of toxoplasmosis in an Amami spiny rat has also been reported (Tokiwa et al., 2019). This suggests that T. gondii is transmitted from feral cats to endemic mammals on Amami-Oshima Island. However, the infection status of other endemic species on the island remains unknown.

The Amami Woodcock, Scolopax mira (Aves: Charadriiformes: Scolopacidae) is an endemic species restricted to the Amami Islands (including Amami-Oshima Island) and Okinawa Islands in, Japan (Brazil and Ikenaga, 1987; The ornithological society of Japan, 2024). This species was traditionally considered a resident; however, recent investigations have shown that it migrates between Amami-Oshima and Okinawa Island in, Japan (Torikai, 2023). Due to deforestation and predation by non-native species, it is listed as a “nationally rare species of wild fauna and flora” under Japanese domestic law, and conservation and reproduction programs are being implemented by the Ministry of the Environment (Ishida and Takashi, 1998; BirdLife International, 2024; (Okinawa and Amami Natural Environment Office, Ministry of the Environment, 2024). On Amami-Oshima Island, this species primarily inhabits forested areas (Brazil and Ikenaga, 1987), which overlaps with the habitats of feral cats. In addition, it preys on earthworms and insects in the soil (Okinawa and Amami Natural Environment Office, Ministry of the Environment, 2024), which may make it susceptible to exposure to sporulated oocysts of T. gondii, that contaminate the soil. Thus, they may be strongly affected by T. gondii among the endemic wild birds on this island.

The purpose of this study was to confirm T. gondii infection in Amami Woodcocks on Amami-Oshima Island by detecting T. gondii-specific genes in tissues using molecular biology techniques. In addition, we examined the effects of T. gondii infection on infected birds using histopathological methods.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

In total, twenty-two dead Amami Woodcocks from Amami-Oshima Island, provided by the Ministry of Environment and Veterinary Hospitals, were examined. Birds were collected from the following locations: three from Amami-shi, four from Tatsugo-cho, seven from Yamato-son, two from Uken-son, two from Setouchi-cho (Fig. 1), and four of unknown origin. Of these, seventeen birds were found dead in the field and were recovered by the Ministry of Environment, Japan, between 2022 and 2025. Five birds were brought to veterinary hospitals on Amami-Oshima Island for treatment after traffic accidents or illness; however, all eventually died. Necropsy

Necropsies were conducted at Nippon Veterinary and Life Science University (n = 18) and local veterinary hospitals (n = 4). Each cadaver was externally measured, and gross lesions on the body surface and internal organs, including external injuries, were recorded. A portion of the brain tissue was removed from each cadaver and stored frozen at −20 °C. All sampled organs (heart, lung, liver, kidney, spleen, testis/ovary, proventriculus, gizzard, intestine, brain, and thoracic muscle) were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin.

Molecular analysis

Brain tissue from twenty-two birds was used for molecular analysis. This choice was based on previous studies reporting that tissue cysts of T. gondii are frequently detected in the brains of intermediate hosts (Dubey, 1997). Total DNA was extracted from frozen brain tissue samples using the QIAGEN QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Venlo, Netherlands) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Toxoplasma gondii DNA was detected using real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The QuantStudio 1 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) and Toxoplasma gondii TaqMan PCR Kit (Product No. TM44750, Norgen Biotek Corp., Thorold, ON, Canada) were used for real-time PCR, and the tests were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. QuantStudio Design & Analysis software (Applied Biosystems) was used for quantification and melting curve analysis, and positive or negative results were determined by analysing the amplification curves.

DNA from brain tissue that tested positive using real-time PCR was analysis was subjected to conventional PCR to amplify and sequence the T. gondii SAG3 and GRA6 genes. The primers used for the PCR and the protocol were based on those described by Tokiwa et al. (2019). When agarose gel electrophoresis confirmed the amplification of DNA fragments of the expected length, purification was performed using the QIAGEN PCR & Gel Cleanup Kit (QIAGEN). Purified samples were sent to Eurofins Genomics (Tokyo, Japan) for direct sequencing. The sequences obtained were deposited in the DNA Data Bank of Japan (DDBJ) and subjected to a BLAST search for homology in GenBank.

Histopathological examination

Histopathological examination was performed on all or parts of the sampled organs the necropsy. Formalin-fixed organs were excised, processed into paraffin blocks according to a standard protocol, sectioned into 3 μm slices, and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (HE). The prepared tissue sections were observed under a light microscope, and the presence of T. gondii and pathological changes were recorded. Microscopic examination and Photomicrographs were obtained using a BX53 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and a DP74 (Olympus).

Immunohistochemical staining was performed to detect T. gondii in the tissue sections of birds that tested positive using real-time PCR analysis. A monoclonal anti-T. gondii mouse antibody (Tokiwa et al., 2019) was applied, and chromogenic detection was performed using Histofine (Nichirei Biosciences, Tokyo, Japan). The liver tissue from a common squirrel monkey (Saimiri sciureus) naturally infected with T. gondii was used as a positive control.

Ultrastructural examination

For ultrastructural analysis, the inverted gelatin capsule method was employed for resin embedding (Morecki and Becker, 1968). Briefly, the regions of interest were identified in HE-stained, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded myocardial tissue sections. The sections were rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 90%, 80%, 70%, and 50%), fixed with 1 % osmium tetroxide (OsO4), and then dehydrated through the reverse ethanol series (50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%). Gelatin capsules filled with epoxy resin were inverted onto the regions of interest on the sections, and the polymerisation was carried out at 60 °C. The resin blocks were trimmed, and ultrathin sections (approximately 80 nm thick) were cut using an ultramicrotome. Ultrathin sections were contrasted with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and examined using a transmission electron microscope (JEM-1011, JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Results

Molecular analysis

A real-time PCR screening test revealed a T. gondii prevalence of 9.1% (2/22; 95% confidence intervals [CI]: 1.3–29.0%). Hereafter, these birds will be referred to as birds A (collected in Yamato-son) and B (collected in Uken-son). PCR performed on T. gondii-positive birds resulted in the amplification of two DNA fragments from bird A. Sequences (SAG3: 212 bp, GRA6: 148 bp) were determined using direct sequencing. Both sequences were 100% identical to the T. gondii reference sequence (ex. SAG3: XM_002371878, GRA6: JX044198) and showed no similarity to other tissue cyst-forming coccidia. The determined sequences were registered in the DDBJ under accession numbers, LC885161 (SAG3) and LC885162 (GRA6). Neither SAG3 nor GRA6 genes were amplified from Bird B using conventional PCR.

Histopathological examination

Histopathological examination revealed protozoa, likely T. gondii, in the heart tissue of bird B. The organisms were located within the cardiomyocytes (Fig. 2a) and were presumed to represent terminal colonies based on their morphology. Immunohistochemistry identified protozoa that reacted positively to the anti-T. gondii antibodies in the myocardium of bird B (Fig. 2b). In addition to terminal colonies, structures resembling protozoan cysts were observed in bird B. Immunohistochemistry was also performed on Bird A, but no protozoa were detected in the brain or heart tissues.

Ultrastructural examination

Ultrastructural studies revealed that the parasite in the cardiac muscle of Bird B was a clump of ovoid or crescent-shaped zoites, with no cyst wall surrounding the clump (Fig. 3). This aggregation was considered a terminal colony because no structures corresponding to the cyst walls were observed. Due to prolonged freezing, most of the intracellular organelles of the tachyzoites were not preserved. Discussion

In the present study, 9.1% of birds tested positive for T. gondii. The global prevalence of T. gondii infection in wild birds has been estimated at 16.6% (95% CI: 12.4–21.9), with an average prevalence of 11.0% (95% CI: 5.9–19.41) when estimated using PCR-based methods (Chen et al., 2024). The prevalence of T. gondii estimated in the Amami Woodcock in the present study was within the reported range. The prevalence of T. gondii is influenced by factors such as the frequency of contact with contaminated soil or water and the likelihood of predation on intermediate hosts harboring tissue cysts. For example, Karakavuk et al. (2018) reported an 89.6% prevalence of T. gondii in wild birds in Turkey using a methodology comparable to that used in the present study. Many sampled species were raptors of the orders Accipitriformes and Strigiformes, which are carnivorous birds positioned as apex predators in the food web (Donázar et al., 2016). Apex predators tend to exhibit higher parasite prevalence owing to biomagnification (Hollings et al., 2013; Wilson et al., 2024). In contrast, Amami Woodcocks primarily feed on soil invertebrates such as earthworms (Okinawa and Amami Natural Environment Office, Ministry of the Environment, 2024) and occupy a lower trophic level than raptors. Therefore, the estimated prevalence of T. gondii in the Amami Woodcocks may reflect their position within the ecosystem's trophic structure.

Ground-foraging birds have a high prevalence of T. gondii because they have more opportunities to orally ingest sporulated oocysts present in the soil (Dubey, 2008; Heddergott et al., 2025). Since Amami Woodcocks primarily inhabit forest ground and prey on small soil animals such as earthworms and insects, their exposure to oocysts is likely to be high. Earthworms also serve as transport hosts of T. gondii (Bettiol et al., 2000). With the genus Scolopax, T. gondii infection has been reported in Eurasian Woodcock (Scolopax rusticola) (Konstantinos et al., 2017), in addition to this study. Since Scolopax has an ecology strongly related to soil, these birds may serve as sentinels that reflect the status of environmental contamination by T. gondii oocysts.

In this study, real-time PCR screening of DNA from brain tissue was first performed. The kit used is designed to detect T. gondii-specific DNA; however, since the target gene information is proprietary and undisclosed. Among the two birds that tested positive by real-time PCR, one was also positive by immunohistochemical staining using anti-T. gondii antibodies in the myocardial tissue; however, it is known that cross-reactions with other tissue-cyst-forming coccidia are frequently observed in immunohistochemical staining (Dellarupe et al., 2024). Sequencing by conventional PCR with myocardial DNA was unsuccessful, preventing definitive confirmation that the coccidia observed in this bird was T. gondii. Nevertheless, considering that T. gondii-specific DNA (although the genetic information is unknown) was detected by real-time PCR, the observed terminal colony exhibited morphological features consistent with T. gondii, and no similar coccidia were found in any of the birds negative for T. gondii by real-time PCR, it is highly likely that the terminal colony observed in the heart of this bird represented T. gondii.

No histological lesions were observed in birds with confirmed infections in the present study, suggesting that acute toxoplasmosis did not develop following the infection and that the disease followed a subclinical course. This implied that the genotype prevalent on Amami-Oshima Island is low pathogenic to Amami Woodcocks. T. gondii genotypes are traditionally classified into three types: I, II, and III, and the pathogenicity of each type in mice is known to differ (Abdelbaset et al., 2022). On Amami-Oshima Island, a genotype closely related to Type II has been detected in a Amami spiny rat (Tokiwa et al., 2019). We were unable to identify the genotype of T. gondii that was infecting Amami Woodcocks in this study. However, since the virulence of T. gondii varies depending on various factors, such as the strain of infection, the intensity of infection, and the immune status of the host (Ihara et al., 2024), these results alone do not indicate a low pathogenicity of T. gondii in Amami Woodcocks. In this study, the number of birds that tested positive for T. gondii was limited, making it insufficient to draw pathological conclusions regarding T. gondii infection in this species. Notably, it remains unclear whether infection with T. gondii in this species progresses to the chronic stage (tissue cyst), and further investigations are required.

The results of this study suggest that T. gondii is not an immediate threat to Amami Woodcock populations. However, routes of transmission to wild birds have already been established on Amami-Oshima Island, which may have significant implications. Recent research identified a highly -virulent strain of T. gondii, referred to as the “TgCatJpOk4” strain, on Okinawa Island, located south of Amami-Oshima Island (Ihara et al., 2024). This strain has been frequently detected in livestock and is regarded as a significant threat to animal husbandry owing to its ability to cause fatal infections in pigs. It has also been reported to cause ocular toxoplasmosis in immunocompetent humans. Although there is currently no evidence indicating that this strain is pathogenic to wild birds, its introduction to Amami-Oshima Island could result in high virulence in birds, including the Amami Woodcock. Furthermore, recent studies have confirmed the seasonal migration of the Amami Woodcock between Okinawa and Amami-Oshima Islands (Torikai, 2023). Therefore, the migration of TgCatJpOk4-infected individuals poses a potential risk of introducing this strain into the local Amami Woodcock population on Amami-Oshima Island.

Conclusion

This study revealed that T. gondii infection occurs in the wild in the endangered Amami Woodcock. Since many feral cats inhabit the forests of Amami-Oshima Island, pathogens originating from cats such as T. gondii are spreading to native species. Currently, the Ministry of the Environment, Japan is trapping feral cats in the island's forests, and their numbers are decreasing. Controlling the population of feral cats and other non-native species is thought to be effective in preventing T. gondii infection in native species on Amami-Oshima Island.
