Associations Between Nicotine Metabolites and Serum Neurofilament Light Chain Levels in the General Population
Authors: Qianqian Ji, Erhan Tang, Yueting Liao, Jialei Yang, Yunzhang Wang, Yiqiang Zhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNicotine has been associated with cognitive functions such as memory and attention, with serum neurofilament light chain (sNfL) as a biomarker for neurological diseases such as Alzheimer's disease (AD) and multiple sclerosis (MS). However, the associations between nicotine and its metabolites and sNfL levels remain underexplored. This study aims to investigate the associations of serum and urine levels of cotinine and trans‐3’‐hydroxycotinine (hydroxycotinine) with sNfL levels in a broad population.MethodsEmploying data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2013–2014, this cross‐sectional study applied multivariable linear regression models and restricted cubic splines to examine the links between cotinine, hydroxycotinine (both in serum and urine), and sNfL levels.ResultsA total of 2052 participants were included in the serum analysis (mean age, 46.8 years; SD, 15.3; weighted 52.1% women) and 661 participants in the urine analysis (weighted 49.5% women). sNfL levels were positively associated with both serum and urine concentrations of cotinine and hydroxycotinine. Adjusted analyses revealed increases in sNfL levels in association with these substances, noting nonlinear associations for serum and urine cotinine and hydroxycotinine with sNfL levels.ConclusionThese findings demonstrate robust positive associations between nicotine metabolites and sNfL levels and identify novel U‐shaped associations at lower exposure levels. The results raise the hypothesis that very low nicotine metabolite levels may be associated with lower axonal injury markers, warranting further longitudinal and mechanistic studies to clarify causality.Keywords: cotinine, hydroxycotinine, National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, nicotine, serum neurofilament light chain

Introduction

Tobacco smoking remains a major public health concern, contributing to the deaths of over 5.4 million individuals annually [1], with the potential for this number to rise to approximately nine million by 2030 according to the World Health Organization (WHO) [2]. Extensive research has demonstrated that smoking is a significant risk factor for a multitude of diseases, including lung cancer, ischemic heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and various other forms of cancer [3]. In addition to these well‐documented health implications, chronic exposure to smoking has been linked to respiratory tract infections, asthma in infants, suppressed immune function, and heightened allergic responses [4]. Furthermore, epidemiological studies have associated smoking with a range of neurological disorders, such as Parkinson's disease (PD), Alzheimer's disease (AD), stroke, multiple sclerosis (MS), silent cerebral infarction, and sleep disorders [3, 5, 6]. Notably, habitual smokers are at a two‐fold higher risk of developing AD [7]. Similarly, a strong positive dose‐dependent association has been observed between ischemic stroke and smoking [8], and smoking even one cigarette daily increases the risk of developing a stroke [9]. Furthermore, smoking has been identified as a risk factor for MS [10] and has been linked to sleep apnea, sleep‐disordered breathing, insomnia, and poor sleep quality [11]. However, despite the numerous adverse effects of smoking, an inverse relationship has been found between smoking and the incidence and mortality of PD [12].

In contrast to the complex effects of smoking, nicotine, the principal psychoactive chemical in tobacco smoke, has been reported to have distinct associations with neuronal processes. Studies have indicated that nicotine acts as an agonist of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) in both the central and peripheral nervous systems [13]. Given the widespread presence of nAChRs throughout the brain and the critical role of the central cholinergic system in cognitive function restoration, nicotine, and its analogs have been linked to improved cognitive performance [14]. Nicotine has been associated with various cognitive functions, including attention, learning, and memory [15], and has been linked to improved cognitive impairment in disease‐induced cognitive disorders, such as AD, PD, age‐associated memory impairment, schizophrenia, stroke, autism spectrum disorder, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder [16]. Moreover, nicotine has been associated with a reduced risk of PD [17] and has exhibited potential for the treatment of this condition [18]. Additionally, nicotine has been linked to improvements in attention, working memory, fear memory, spatial memory, and social behavior in nonsmoking adults [14]. However, paradoxical effects have been reported, likely reflecting its inverted U‐shaped dose–response relationship, in which low doses improve cognition, whereas chronic or heavy smoking is associated with cognitive impairment [19]. Furthermore, high‐dose nicotine treatment has been associated with spatial memory deficits, anxiety‐like and depressive behaviors, and cognitive decline in animal models [20, 21].

Neurofilament light chain (NfL) has emerged as a promising biomarker for various neurological diseases. Neurofilaments, comprising light, medium, and heavy chains, are neuron‐specific type IV intermediate filament heteropolymers that play a crucial role in the neural cytoskeleton [22]. Following neuro‐axonal damage, they are released into the extracellular space and have been proposed as potential biomarkers for neuro‐axonal injury in neurological diseases [22, 23]. Notably, elevated levels of NfL have been associated with several neurological disorders, including MS, AD, PD, stroke, Huntington's disease, motor neuron disease, and traumatic brain injury [24, 25, 26, 27]. Additionally, NfL levels have been linked to disease severity, aging, and body mass index (BMI) [28, 29].

Despite the substantial evidence regarding the associations between smoking and neurological diseases, there is a notable gap in research investigating the associations between nicotine in body fluids and serum neurofilament light chain (sNfL). This study aimed to investigate whether concentrations of nicotine metabolites in body fluids are associated with sNfL levels, with a particular focus on whether lower metabolite levels correspond to reduced sNfL. Given the extremely short elimination half‐life of nicotine, cotinine and trans‐3’‐hydroxycotinine (hydroxycotinine) are being considered as preferred biomarkers due to their higher concentrations and significantly longer elimination half‐lives [30]. The estimated elimination half‐life of cotinine is approximately 15–20 h, whereas the half‐life of nicotine is only 0.5–3 h. Similarly, the half‐life of hydroxycotinine is about 5–6 h, but when generated from cotinine, its elimination half‐life becomes similar to that of cotinine [30]. To this end, this study utilized cotinine and hydroxycotinine to examine their associations with sNfL in a nationally representative US population.

Methods

Study Population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a comprehensive cross‐sectional survey conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) biennially to evaluate the health and nutritional status of the US noninstitutionalized civilian population. Data collection involves a multistage sampling design integrating in‐person questionnaires, physical measurements, and laboratory tests. The survey protocols and procedures were approved by the NCHS Ethics Review Board, and all participants provide written informed consent. All study procedures adhered to relevant ethical guidelines and regulations. Detailed information and survey data of the NHANES have been extensively documented elsewhere [31].

For this study, data from NHANES 2013–2014 were utilized. The following exclusion criteria were applied: age < 20 years, unavailable data on sNfL, serum or urine cotinine and hydroxycotinine, demographic information (including age, sex, race/ethnicity, education level), BMI data, or self‐reported smoking status. Ultimately, 2052 participants were included in the serum cotinine and hydroxycotinine analyses, and 661 participants were included in the urine cotinine and hydroxycotinine analyses (Figure 1). To assess potential selection bias, we compared demographic characteristics between participants with and without urine metabolite data (Supplementary Table S1).

Flowchart of sample size screening.

Cotinine and Hydroxycotinine Measurements

Serum and urine specimens were collected and stored until testing. The detailed sample collection procedures and laboratory methods are available on the NHANES website. Specifically, serum cotinine and hydroxycotinine were quantified using the isotope‐dilution high‐performance liquid chromatography coupled with atmospheric pressure chemical ionization tandem mass spectrometric (ID HPLC‐APCI MS/MS) method. Internal standards, methyl‐D3‐cotinine and methyl‐D3‐hydroxycotinine, were added to serum samples. Following basification and extraction, the residue was injected into a C18 HPLC column, and the eluent was monitored by APCI‐MS/MS. For urine specimens, two separate isotope dilution HPLC‐MS/MS methods were employed. The urine sample was spiked with an internal standard mixture and incubated with β‐glucuronidase to hydrolyze the conjugated analytes before analysis by electrospray ionization HPLC‐MS/MS. The lower limit of detection (LLOD) for serum and urine cotinine and hydroxycotinine was 0.015 ng/mL and 0.03 ng/mL, respectively. Specimens below LLOD included: 627 participants for serum cotinine, 1020 for serum hydroxycotinine, 13 for urine cotinine, and 15 for urine hydroxycotinine. Values below the LLOD were imputed as LLOD divided by the square root of two for all analytes.

sNfL Measurement

Serum samples were collected from NHANES participants aged 20–75 years in 2013–2014 who consented to specimen storage for future research. The sNfL measurement was conducted using a highly sensitive immunoassay method, where the products of the reaction between sNfL and acridinium‐ester (AE) labeled antibodies were captured by paramagnetic particles (PMP), and a chemiluminescent reaction occurred. The results were measured and read by an automated platform (Attelica). The lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) of the assay was 3.9 pg/mL, with 36 participants having sNfL below this threshold. Values below the LLOQ were imputed as LLOQ divided by the square root of two for all analytes.

Covariates

Sociodemographic variables included age, gender, race/ethnicity (categorized as non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, and other races), and education level (categorized as below high school, high school, and some college or higher). BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Smoking status was defined as current smoker (self‐reported current cigarette use) versus nonsmoker.

Statistical Analysis

Sample weights, clustering, and stratification were incorporated to account for the complex survey design of NHANES in both full sample and urine subsample analyses. Descriptive statistics were calculated for study participants based on gender, serum, and urine cotinine and hydroxycotinine. Categorical variables were expressed as number and percentage, while continuous variables were reported as weighted means, standard deviations, and medians. sNfL, serum, and urine cotinine and hydroxycotinine concentrations were log‐transformed to ensure a normal distribution due to right‐skewed distribution. Multivariable linear regression was performed to explore associations between serum and urine cotinine and hydroxycotinine and sNfL. Covariates were selected based on prior knowledge that might confound or modify the relationship between sNfL and nicotine metabolites. Three models were constructed, with progressive adjustments for age, sex, race/ethnicity, education level, BMI, and smoking status. The nonlinear associations of serum and urine cotinine and hydroxycotinine with sNfL were examined using restricted cubic spline analysis with 5 knots placed at the 5th, 27.5th, 50th, 72.5th, and 95th percentiles of the exposure distribution, as determined by Akaike information criterion (AIC) minimization. Subgroup analyses were conducted, and log‐likelihood ratio tests were performed to estimate the significance of interactions. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with a two‐sided p < 0.05 threshold for statistical significance.

Results

The basic characteristics of the study participants by different body fluid samples are presented in Table 1. For the analysis of serum cotinine and hydroxycotinine levels, involving 2052 adults, the mean (SD) age was 46.8 (15.3) years, with 1069 women (weighted, 52.1%) and 983 men (weighted, 47.9%). The median serum cotinine and hydroxycotinine levels were 0.040 and 0.015 ng/mL, respectively. The median sNfL level was 12.3 pg/mL, with values ranging from 2.8 to 498.0 pg/mL. In the analysis of urine cotinine and hydroxycotinine levels, involving 661 adults, the mean (SD) age was 46.8 (15.3) years, with 327 women (weighted, 49.5%) and 334 men (weighted, 50.5%). The median cotinine level was 0.509 ng/mL, and the median hydroxycotinine level was 0.890 ng/mL. The median sNfL level was 11.6 pg/mL, with values ranging from 2.8 to 208.0 pg/mL. More than half of the participants had completed high school or higher education, and approximately 45% reported being non‐Hispanic Whites. The associations of serum cotinine and hydroxycotinine with urine cotinine and hydroxycotinine are depicted in Figure 2.

After multivariable adjustment, urine cotinine (β = 0.02; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.01–0.04) and urine hydroxycotinine (β = 0.02; 95% CI: 0.01–0.04) were both significantly positively associated with sNfL (Table 2). Nonlinear associations were also observed between urine cotinine and hydroxycotinine and sNfL (both p for nonlinearity < 0.01). The splines showed clear U‐shaped patterns, with β coefficients significantly decreasing below zero around the 27.5th percentile and then increasing steadily up to the 72.5th percentile and higher levels.

Sensitivity analyses employing 4‐knot restricted cubic splines confirmed the robustness of the observed curvilinear associations, with persistent U‐shaped and inverted U‐shaped associations for respective metabolites (Figure S1). Consistent associations were observed across most subgroups (Table 3). A significant interaction was found between serum cotinine and age with sNfL (p = 0.05 for interaction). In stratified analyses, the β coefficient for sNfL was 0.01 (95% CI: −0.01 to 0.02) among participants < 60 years of age and 0.03 (95% CI: 0.01–0.05) among those ≥ 60 years. No significant interactions were found between serum hydroxycotinine, urine cotinine, or urine hydroxycotinine and strata variables in the subgroup analyses.

Discussion

In this cross‐sectional study of US adults, we observed significant positive associations between serum/urine cotinine and hydroxycotinine with sNfL levels using multivariable linear regression. The comparison between participants with and without urine metabolite data did not reveal significant differences in key demographic characteristics (Supplementary Table S1), suggesting minimal selection bias. Importantly, restricted cubic spline analyses revealed significant nonlinear relationships between serum cotinine, serum hydroxycotinine, urine cotinine, and urine hydroxycotinine and sNfL levels. Notably, we identified, for the first time, a U‐shaped association between urine cotinine and hydroxycotinine and sNfL at low levels in the general population, suggesting that lower levels of nicotine metabolites may be associated with attenuated increases in sNfL. Furthermore, our subgroup analyses demonstrated the robustness of these findings.

Previous research has emphasized the influence of various pathological and physiological factors on sNfL levels. Pathologically, sNfL levels have been shown to be elevated in multiple neurological disorders, including MS, AD, PD, inherited peripheral neuropathies, stroke, Huntington's disease, motor neuron disease, and traumatic brain injury [24, 25, 26, 27]. Physiologically, sNfL levels rise with age even in neurologically healthy individuals, with a marked acceleration after approximately 60 years of age [29]. Additionally, sNfL levels may be influenced by hemodilution related to body size, with BMI identified as a major determinant of sNfL levels in individuals younger than 60 years [29]. Although the observed β coefficients in our study were modest, previous research suggests that even small increases in sNfL may reflect early neuroaxonal injury, particularly in preclinical or subclinical stages of neurological diseases such as MS [32]. In this context, advances such as artificial intelligence–assisted rehabilitation approaches may further enhance the clinical translation of biomarkers like sNfL [33].

The existing research on the relationship between nicotine or its metabolites and sNfL is limited. To our knowledge, only one prior study, based on data from NHANES 2013–2014, has examined the association between serum cotinine levels and sNfL levels [34]. That study demonstrated a significant association between higher serum cotinine levels and elevated sNfL levels, which is consistent with our findings. However, our study extends this study by observing a nonlinear association between serum hydroxycotinine and sNfL levels, revealing an inverted U‐shaped pattern for the β of sNfL with serum hydroxycotinine at low concentrations. Furthermore, significant U‐shaped associations were also identified between urine cotinine and hydroxycotinine and sNfL at low levels in the general population.

The potential mechanisms underlying the association between low nicotine levels and sNfL levels may be partially explained by SIRT6‐mediated pathways. SIRT6 is a member of the sirtuin family, which promotes apoptosis in various cell types, including neurons. And nicotine can accelerate its degradation to provide neuroprotection [35]. In addition, studies indicated that nicotine may mediate neuroprotection both in vitro and in vivo through activation of central α7 nAChRs, leading to upregulation of high‐affinity nerve growth factor receptors (TrkA) [36]. Furthermore, nicotine can activate α4 subunit‐containing nAChRs in vivo, resulting in significant inhibition of neurodegeneration [37].

Our study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first study to concurrently examine the associations of both serum and urine cotinine and hydroxycotinine with sNfL in a general population sample. Moreover, the use of NHANES, a nationally representative database, allowed for appropriate application of sample weights and adjustment for multiple potential confounders, enhancing the generalizability of our findings.

Nevertheless, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference, and reverse causation cannot be excluded—for example, individuals with early neuroaxonal injury may alter their nicotine exposure. Second, although major demographic and physiological covariates were adjusted for, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., comorbidities, environmental exposures) may remain. Third, nicotine exposure was assessed using single‐point serum and urine levels of cotinine and hydroxycotinine, which reflect recent exposure and may not capture long‐term or cumulative effects. Additionally, we did not differentiate between sources of nicotine (e.g., combustible cigarettes, e‐cigarettes, nicotine replacement therapy), nor account for other potentially neurotoxic constituents of tobacco smoke. Despite these limitations, our study provides novel, population‐based evidence on the relationship between nicotine metabolites and sNfL, and our findings should be considered hypothesis‐generating and a basis for future longitudinal and mechanistic research.

In conclusion, our study revealed significant and positive associations between serum and urine cotinine and hydroxycotinine and sNfL in US adults. Notably, we identified a U‐shaped association between urine cotinine and hydroxycotinine and sNfL at lower exposure levels, suggesting that very low nicotine metabolite concentrations may be associated with attenuated sNfL elevations. Further research is warranted to clarify these associations and explore underlying mechanisms.
