Digital twins in oncology: where we are and where we hope to go
Authors: Matthew McCoy
Published: 2025

The rapidly advancing frontiers of high-throughput data collection and computational simulation are enabling the development of digital twins (DT) to support clinical decision-making in oncology.1 The exact definition of a DT depends on the context of its application, but in general, encompasses a virtual simulation that is customised using real-world data, as well as the methodology to provide specific, actionable insight into how a patient will respond to alternative therapeutic interventions. However, their use for cancer treatment faces technical and ethical challenges that do not apply to other industries that have successfully applied a DT to optimise complex systems (aerospace, automotive, manufacturing). Current DT use cases in oncology include using models to inform clinical protocols, conduct virtual clinical trials, conduct training, and predict responses based on biomarkers.2 However, the application to clinical decision support is typically limited to a single iteration of a DT cycle rather than dynamically informing longitudinal decision making in response to tumour evolution. The data needed to model tumour growth and predict an optimal intervention are noisy and/or incomplete, and their collection must minimise the negative impact on the patient’s quality of life. The computational simulations must be trustworthy and quantify the uncertainty of the predicted response in ways that inspire confidence from the clinical decision makers. These, along with numerous other constraints, must be overcome before the DT can be used to optimise and fully personalise the treatment of cancer.

The specific models used to represent a patient’s disease depend on the cancer type, but in general they fall into two categories: mechanistic models, which simulate the relevant physiological processes, and statistical models, which are trained on prior data. In the case of the former, the mathematical formulations that underpin a mechanistic model must expose variables that can be defined through diagnostic assays. And even when the technology exists to measure those parameters, noise and error in the data will introduce uncertainty to the prediction outcome. A compounding challenge in cancer comes from the diversity of environments and evolutionary pressures present within a single tumour. Models must account for this subclonal heterogeneity when predicting the response to various treatment strategies. Rather than creating an exact mapping between a patent’s data and the corresponding model inputs, the data will need to be deconvoluted into distributions of the values that influence subclonal tumour properties. The number of parallel simulations needed to quantify the uncertainty in predicted outcomes increases exponentially with the number of patient inputs; therefore, models should capture all the relevant mechanisms as simply as possible in an attempt to minimise the patient-definable parameters.3 Statistical models have their own unique sources of uncertainty that must be quantified and contextualised in different ways. The ‘black-box’ nature of their predictions and sensitivity to biases in their training data requires additional assurances that subsequent therapeutic recommendations apply to the specific patient’s disease.4 Future efforts to understand and interpret the ‘rationale’ of these artificial intelligence models will increase confidence in their predictions, leading to their increasing use as DT models.

The DT connects the real-world system to its virtual representation in an iterative cycle, where patient data are collected, simulations are run, a clinical intervention is made, more patient data is collected, simulations are run and so on. In practice, the DT is implemented as a set of nested loops that provide a rigorous analysis of the simulation output and enrich the predicted outcomes of various intervention strategies, providing clinicians with the information needed to make informed decisions.5 An inner loop is where the simulation of the patient’s tumour generates outcomes for various treatment strategies. This inner loop runs many times, as dictated by an outer loop of validation, verification and uncertainty quantification.6 The outer loop is used to assess how noise and uncertainty in patient data impact the simulated outcomes. For example, a population of patient DTs that represent the range and uncertainty in the input data can be enrolled in virtual clinical trials to predict the benefits of every therapeutic option. Finally, validation metrics and other comparative analyses across combinations of possible parameter values are presented to clinical decision-makers in the outer-outer loop. As treatment is applied, new data are collected from the patient to initiate another cycle of DT simulations.

In addition to the constraints on constructing, parameterising and interpreting a personalised model of cancer, implementing a DT in a clinical decision-making workflow faces some other general challenges. The nature of the DT may require multiple rounds of data collection, involving procedures that can diminish the patient’s quality of life. Minimising that impact will require the development of less invasive methods for collecting high-quality patient data at regular intervals, as well as optimising the computing infrastructure to provide the clinical recommendation with minimal turn-around time. But with the rapid progress in understanding and modelling cancer progression and the ever-increasing pace of technology development, the use of DTs will become increasingly personalised. They will expand beyond biomarker-based interventions into models that incorporate longitudinal data collection and dynamically treat an evolving tumour.
