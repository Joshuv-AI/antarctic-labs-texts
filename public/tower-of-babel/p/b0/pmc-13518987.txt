Adding flexibility to payments for environmental services enhances cost-effectiveness in the Brazilian Amazon
Authors: Gabriela Demarchi, Julie Subervie, Cauê D Carrilho, Thibault Catry, Antoine Pfefer, Philippe Delacote
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPayments for Environmental Services (PES) are widely used to protect tropical forests, but rigid pricing may fail when landowners’ opportunity costs fluctuate. Inflexible incentives can lead to contract breaches, reducing environmental benefits. We test whether flexible PES contracts improve outcomes by allowing partial payments when deforestation remains below a threshold. Using a Becker-DeGroot-Marschak auction within a randomized controlled trial, we compare a flexible PES contract, a zero-deforestation contract, and a control group. Although the zero-deforestation contract had greater potential to avoid deforestation, high breach rates reduced realized conservation. The flexible PES contract, while more costly due to higher compliance, achieved greater forest protection per hectare: participants deforested 31% less than under the stringent contract in the peak period (95% CI [2%, 60%], d = 0.12). These findings suggest that flexible PES contracts can improve the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of conservation programs.Subject terms: Environmental economics, Climate-change policy, Social sciences

Introduction

Payments for environmental services (PES) use direct incentives to improve the environmental impacts of farmers’ land use decisions1,2. As deforestation and forest degradation account for approximately 10–15% of global greenhouse gas emissions, PES has emerged as a critical tool for climate change mitigation by promoting forest conservation and sustainable land management practices3. With annual transactions exceeding USD 40 billion as of 2017, PES now plays a central role in global efforts to combat deforestation and biodiversity loss4. Yet despite this widespread uptake, growing evidence questions the effectiveness of PES in achieving additional conservation—particularly in contexts where landholders face high and volatile opportunity costs or where monitoring and enforcement are weak5–8. Recent reviews underscore that program design is a critical determinant of PES outcomes to be investigated9,10, in particular regarding the level of technical sophistication that can be implemented11–13.

In this context, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) can be a powerful tool to assess the outcome of forest conservation programs. For example14, implemented an RCT in Mexico to emphasize the role of compulsory versus partial land-enrollment on additionality and avoided deforestation, finding that full-enrollment contracts reduced deforestation by 41% and significantly improved cost-effectiveness. Another recent RCT with a PES to curb crop residue burning in India showed that offering partial upfront payments can improve both compliance and cost effectiveness15. RCTs have also been advocated as a way to accurately assess the additionality and environmental integrity of carbon credits, particularly in the context of forest conservation interventions16, while17 offer a typology and guidelines for the implementation of RCTs for the evaluation of forest conservation programs.

A key challenge is that PES contracts typically do not consider the fact that landowners’ opportunity costs and liquidity constraints may experience important temporal variability. In practice, however, smallholders operating in dynamic markets often face climatic and economic shocks, as well as fluctuating incentives, that undermine compliance18. Economic theory suggests that under such uncertainty, landowners place value on maintaining flexibility in their land-use decisions, which rigid conservation contracts often fail to acknowledge19. When unforeseen expenses occur or when market returns from alternative land uses rise, landowners often breach contracts once the fixed PES payment no longer compensates for the lost income. Together, these findings suggest that the effectiveness of PES programs depends not only on whether incentives are offered, but on how contracts are designed to anticipate real-world constraints and behavioral responses. Yet despite conceptual advances in PES theory, head-to-head empirical comparisons of alternative contract structures under identical socio-ecological and budgetary conditions remain rare, particularly in high-deforestation frontiers such as the Brazilian Amazon.

In this study, we provide experimental evidence on PES contract design by evaluating two alternative contract structures that differ in conservation stringency and penalty mechanisms, both requiring full-enrollment of eligible forest areas. We implemented a pilot PES program with volunteer households in the Western Brazilian Amazon—a region facing high deforestation pressure due to cattle expansion. Households were randomly assigned to either: (A) a zero-deforestation contract, under which any detected forest loss voided the payment; or (B) a threshold-based contract, where penalties were applied based on the extent of deforestation (20% penalty for deforestation below 0.5 ha; 50% penalty for 0.5–1 ha; no payment for deforestation above 1 ha). A third group received no PES offer and served as a control. In both treatment arms, payments were made conditionally, at the end of a twelve-month contract period, and forest conservation was monitored using high-frequency Sentinel-1 Synthetic-Aperture Radar (SAR) imagery to ensure robust detection regardless of cloud cover20,21.

To assess participants’ valuation of each contract type and to inform policy trade-offs, we embedded two experimental components. First, we implemented a Becker-DeGroot-Marschak (BDM) auction to elicit participants’ minimum required compensation, thereby capturing heterogeneity in opportunity costs5,22,23. Second, we tracked actual deforestation and compliance, which could be full or partial in the case of the flexible PES contract, over the contract period. This period coincided with a regional spike in deforestation, offering a unique opportunity to test how each contract performs under adverse market conditions.

By aligning with the recent literature on contract innovations, such as full-enrollment14 and liquidity-sensitive payment structures15, our study provides empirical insights into how small but strategic changes in PES design can enhance environmental effectiveness and cost-effectiveness in practice. Our results offer direct guidance for improving PES schemes in high-deforestation settings, particularly where policymakers face constrained budgets and imperfect enforcement capacity.

Results

The results presented below rely on comparisons of average outcomes across the three treatment groups (A, B, and controls), whose participants are directly comparable on average due to random assignment, which ensures independence between treatment assignment and potential outcomes24,25. PES contract allocation through the BDM auction mechanism ensures that the assignment of contracts involves a random component: while lower bids increase the probability of receiving a PES contract, the actual cutoff is determined by chance, making assignment quasi-random. For this reason, our analysis uses an intention-to-treat (ITT) estimator, comparing average outcomes across the three randomized groups (A, B, and control), irrespective of whether participants in A or B ultimately received a contract.

Our sample consisted of 456 landowners, with 154 in the control group, 150 in treatment arm A, and 152 in treatment arm B. The study was conducted in the state of Acre, a region characterized by a humid tropical climate, extensive forest cover, and increasing pressures from agricultural expansion. As shown in Fig. 1, the plots enrolled in the experiment were geographically dispersed across the forest frontier zone, where land-use change dynamics are particularly active. On average, participants owned 56 ha of land, including 32 ha of forest (see Table S2 in the SI Appendix). They lived approximately 40 km from the nearest village and regularly exploited their forests, typically clearing between 1 and 2 ha annually.

Landowners consistently demanded a higher price for the stringent PES

Each of the 302 landowners assigned to arm A or arm B participated in the BDM auction. Their characteristics are detailed in Table S1 in the SI Appendix. The auction was designed to ascertain the landowner’s genuine willingness to accept (WTA) by disclosing the minimum compensation required to fully preserve their forest for a twelve-month period under each of the offered contracts. Each contract type had its own urn calibrated to reflect the available program budget and to ensure that the proportion of signed contracts remained consistent between treatment groups, set at about two-thirds. The assignment of contract type was determined in advance through randomization but remained undisclosed to both participants and surveyors until after both bids were submitted. Once the assigned contract was revealed, the participant drew a token from the corresponding urn to determine whether their bid qualified for a PES offer. Participants were informed of the outcome immediately after the draw. As each participant posted one bid per contract, we obtained a balanced panel dataset of 604 observations (see full details in the Materials and Methods section).

Table S9 in SI Appendix presents the summary statistics of the two auctions: the mean (median resp.) bid for the more stringent contract was 9284 Brazilian reais (BRL) (4500 BRL resp.), which is roughly equivalent to eight (four resp.) times the minimum wage in Brazil in 2021. The mean bid (median resp.) for the flexible PES contract was significantly lower, at 6692 BRL (3500 BRL resp.).

Figure 2 displays the BDM demand curves for each of the two PES contracts. Regardless of the percentile group considered, we observe that the bid for the flexible PES contract (B) is always lower than the bid for the stringent PES contract (A). Table S10 in SI Appendix moreover provides the results of two tests that compare the distributions of the bids, using both the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney rank-sum and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. In both cases, the distribution under the contract A treatment is significantly different from the contract B treatment at the 1 percent level. This is consistent with theoretical predictions, as the more demanding contract—implying zero deforestation—is associated with higher compliance costs. The compensation offered for contract A is therefore expected to be higher than that offered for contract B. The difference between the two bids can be seen as an indicator of how much landowners value the flexibility offered by contract B.

The stringent PES recorded a larger number of contract breaches

In group A, any deforestation constituted a breach of contract resulting in no payment, whereas in group B, a breach was only recorded when deforestation had exceeded 1 hectare, with smaller areas of deforestation resulting in penalty adjustments to the payment rather than complete cancellation. Consequently, breaches of contract were expected to be more frequent in group A. The question was therefore to measure the extent to which this had been the case in the context studied. Figure S3 in the SI Appendix shows the compliance status of all participants. We ran an ordinary least squares (OLS) regression that gives the impact of being assigned to a PES group on having breached the contract and found that the default was indeed much higher in group A than in group B (Column 2 in Table 1). This difference is statistically significant at the 1 percent significance level.

We then looked at the extent to which this difference in default rates affected the relative costs of the two groups. We reconstructed the total costs of the program for both treatment groups, accounting not only for the direct payments (PES), but also for implementation expenses, including salaries and logistical costs, which were incurred for every participant, regardless of whether they signed a PES contract or complied with its terms. Due to higher average bids, projected PES costs under full compliance were greater in the stringent PES contract (group A). Yet, because fewer landowners complied, actual disbursements were lower than in the flexible PES contract (group B): about 2180 BRL per participant in group A compared to 2750 BRL per participant in group B (Column 4 in Table 1). Including implementation costs, the total average cost for contract A was around 3000 BRL per participant, whereas contract B averaged 3580 BRL per participant (Column 6 in Table 1), or 583 and 695 USD, respectively, using the average 2022 rate. The gap between projected and actual payments was markedly larger under the more stringent contract, reflecting both higher bids and lower compliance (Figure S1 in the SI Appendix).

The flexible PES contract saved more forest than the stringent PES

We assessed differences in forest clearing between treatment and control groups using high-resolution SAR data, observing periods before, during, and after the contract. Figure 3 displays mean forest loss over time across the three groups. Prior to the intervention and up to period 7 (June 2022), deforestation trends were nearly identical across groups. Starting in period 8 (August 2022), however, we observe a clear divergence: deforestation in the PES groups declined relative to the control, consistent with the program’s conservation objective. During period 8, participants under any PES contract conserved 0.265 ha more forest than those in the no-PES group (95% CI [0.102, 0.429]), corresponding to a Cohen’s d effect size of 0.22 standard deviations (Table S4, Column 1 in the SI Appendix). These estimates are quite similar in magnitude to those found for other PES-based forest conservation programs run in Brazil26 or elsewhere in Latin America27,28. From period 9 onward, point estimates are smaller in magnitude but a formal trend test over the post-payment periods does not provide evidence of a systematic declining trend (Table S11 in the SI Appendix). These results do not rule out more gradual weakening of conservation impacts once payments end, particularly given the short post-intervention window.

To compare the effectiveness of the two PES contracts, we estimated group-specific treatment effects using a fixed-effects panel regression (Table 2 and Column 2 of Table S4 in the SI Appendix). Contract B showed consistent and statistically significant reductions in deforestation from August 2022 onwards, while contract A yielded smaller and generally insignificant effects despite its stricter conditions. The difference is particularly pronounced in period 8 (August 2022), where participants in group B deforested 0.144 ha less than those in group A—corresponding to an effect size of approximately 0.12 standard deviations—and the null hypothesis of no difference between contracts is rejected for almost all periods. Full panel regressions are displayed in Tables S4 and S5 in the SI Appendix. Given the limited power of our design to detect differences between contract designs (Figs. S6–S8 in the SI Appendix), statistically significant A–B contrasts may be upward-biased, and non-significant contrasts cannot be interpreted as evidence of no difference between contracts. We conducted a series of robustness checks to validate these findings. First, the results remain consistent when reanalyzed using Global Forest Change (GFC) data29, confirming both the direction and relative magnitude of treatment effects (Table S12, SI Appendix). Second, we addressed potential interference between experimental units. Comparisons with broader municipal trends (Fig. S9, SI Appendix) rule out the possibility that the PES program triggered a regional increase in deforestation, supporting the assumption of no general equilibrium effects that would violate the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA) and would bias our estimate of the pure impact of the PES. Third, matched comparisons with nearby non-participant farms (Fig. S10 and Table S13, SI Appendix) indicate that control group behavior was not influenced by proximity to treated farms, further supporting SUTVA. Finally, we found no evidence of spatial spillovers beyond property boundaries based on analyses of surrounding buffer zones, suggesting that the environmental gains observed within participating properties were not offset by increased deforestation nearby (Table S14, SI Appendix). Full details of these robustness checks are provided in Section A.3 of the SI Appendix.

Cost-effectiveness and cost-benefit analysis

While the flexible PES contract (PES B) incurred higher average costs per participant, it also achieved significantly greater forest conservation; these results naturally lead us to examine the relative cost-effectiveness of the two contracts. We first calculated the cumulative forest savings per landowner by summing the period-specific treatment effects from Table 2: PES A conserved approximately 0.5 ha per participant, while PES B conserved nearly 0.9 ha—almost twice as much. Importantly, this gap is likely underestimated, since we used the point estimates, even when the associated confidence interval did not exclude zero as a possible value for the true impact of PES A. We then assessed the cost per hectare of forest saved using the average payment disbursed per participant from Table 1: 2180 BRL in group A and 2750 BRL in group B. Results show that PES B achieved better cost-effectiveness ratios: 3056 BRL per hectare saved, compared to 4360 BRL for PES A. This suggests that the flexible PES contract delivered more environmental impact per unit of expenditure.

Building on these comparisons, we conducted a cost-benefit analysis from the perspective of the implementer, considering program expenditures as costs and a global social cost of carbon (SCC) as benefits (Table 3). We converted avoided deforestation into avoided CO2 emissions by using the estimates of average biomass stored in forests in our study zone30, and calculated the monetary value of these benefits using the SCC published by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency31,32. Using these values, we found that PES B avoided approximately 76,040 tCO2eq, compared to 41,882 tCO2eq under PES A. When considering PES payments alone, the benefit-cost ratio for PES B ranges from 2.4 to 5.6, depending on the value of the SCC used, while PES A ranges from 1.8 to 4.1. Including implementation costs, the ratios remain higher for PES B: 1.8 to 4.2 versus 1.3 to 3.0 for PES A. These results confirm that the flexible PES design not only achieved greater forest conservation but also delivered higher climate benefits relative to costs. Full methodological details are provided in the “Materials and Methods” section.

For context, we compare these cost metrics with other evaluations of PES programs. The implied cost per ton of avoided CO2 is approximately US$1.0–1.4 for PES B and US$1.5–2.0 for PES A. These values are of the same order of magnitude as the experimental PES in Uganda, which achieved about US$0.46 per tCO2 and a benefit-cost ratio of 2.4 under standard SCC assumptions6, and far below the average cost reported for Costa Rica’s national PES program (around US$71 per tCO233).

Marginal impact of price incentives

Taken together, the results indicate that group B outperformed group A in avoiding deforestation, largely driven by a notably low rate of contract compliance among participants in group A. However, given that contract A offers higher remuneration, we would like to know to what extent payment levels influenced the decision of landowners not to clear the forest in each group. To investigate this, we estimated the causal effect of receiving an additional 1000 BRL in each treatment arm. We were able to do so because, as part of the BDM experimental auction, participants received a random price above their reservation price (i.e., their final bid), enabling causal inference analysis (see full details in the “Materials and Methods” section). We thus estimated a panel regression model that includes the amount of payment actually received, controlling for the reservation price, as well as individual and period fixed effects (Table S6 in the SI Appendix). Results show no difference between the two contracts: a higher payment of 1000 BRL had a marginal effect of around 0.1 ha in both groups.

Discussion

Our results suggest that, in certain contexts at least, contract flexibility can significantly enhance both the effectiveness and efficiency of forest conservation incentives. Specifically, we have seen in the field that landowners generally demand lower payments for more flexible contracts. This finding aligns with economic theory, which suggests that landowners value optionality under uncertainty2. The field experiment also confirmed the theoretical prediction that a more restrictive contract would be broken more often. In our study, this led to a smaller area of forest being conserved on average in the stringent PES contract group.

Furthermore, although low compliance mechanically reduces expenditure, we observed that the area conserved per real expenditure was ultimately higher in the flexible PES contract group. The flexible PES contract group therefore proved to be both the most effective in conserving forest and the most efficient in doing so at a lower cost per hectare. Finally, our data showed that the effect of price incentive was similar in both groups, which highlights that payment levels alone are insufficient to improve PES effectiveness. The structure of contracts, especially the degree of flexibility embedded in conditionality, thus plays a crucial role in shaping conservation outcomes. As forest-based climate mitigation strategies are scaled up, how we pay may matter as much as how much we pay.

While short (5-year) renewable PES contracts, such as those in Mexico and Costa Rica (e.g.,34,35), limit long-term risk, such horizons remain long relative to rapid shifts in deforestation incentives, as seen in 2022. Our results suggest that, beyond contract length, incorporating within-contract flexibility can further strengthen PES schemes’ resilience to abrupt changes in opportunity costs. Our findings moreover contribute to the broader debate on whether PES schemes should prioritize technical sophistication—through more refined, incentive-compatible contract designs—or local legitimacy—through simpler, more acceptable and contextually grounded rules11–13. They show that greater sophistication, allowing for graduated penalties, can enhance program performance under certain circumstances. At the same time, flexibility may also promote local legitimacy, as participants are likely to perceive graduated sanctions as fairer and more reasonable, allowing them to remain in the program after minor infractions. In this sense, flexible PES designs may help reconcile the goals of technical efficiency and local acceptability.

However, several factors limit the generalizability of our results. First, the coincidence of our experiment with the 2022 deforestation spike is central to interpreting our results. The end of President Bolsonaro’s term raised expectations of renewed enforcement by IBAMA, making deforestation unusually profitable in the short run. This likely created conditions favorable for the flexible PES contract, which, as shown in the theoretical model provided in the SI appendix A5, tends to outperform mainly when most individuals experience a large shock. Under more typical or low-deforestation conditions, it is thus unlikely that we would have been able to demonstrate that the flexible PES contract could outperform the stringent one.

Second, we used a BDM auction to elicit participants’ true opportunity costs. Although tractable in a pilot setting involving a few hundred people, this approach would be much more complex to implement at scale. Accurately estimating opportunity costs in large-scale programs thus remains a key challenge for policy design36,37.

Moreover, while we do not find statistical evidence of a significant declining trend in conservation effort once payments stopped, post-payment point estimates are smaller and suggest weaker conservation impacts. Although it remains uncertain whether the effect will eventually disappear, this pattern suggests that lasting forest protection may require ongoing financial incentives. This interpretation aligns with broader findings in the conservation literature documenting the often temporary nature of PES-induced behavior changes once incentives end10,38,39. Our study does not allow us to infer how participants would have responded to permanent payments; both their reservation prices and conservation decisions could have been different under such conditions.

Finally, we conducted Monte Carlo simulations to assess the statistical power of our experiment, calibrated to effect sizes comparable to those typically reported for traditional PES schemes40 (see Section A.2 in the SI). These are conservative benchmarks: our setting was explicitly designed to generate larger improvements than traditional PES schemes. The experiment is well powered to detect the average effect of PES on deforestation relative to no intervention, and moderately powered to detect differences in contract default rates. However, our experiment is underpowered to detect differences in deforestation outcomes between alternative contract designs. Therefore, our statistically significant point estimates may be upward-biased, and non-significant contrasts between contracts A and B on deforestation outcomes should not be interpreted as evidence of no difference between contract designs

Methods

The experiment was conducted in three phases: (1) enrollment of participants from eligible villages and preliminary testing of the experimental protocol with a small number of volunteers; (2) random assignment of individuals who agreed to participate between the three treatment arms—the two PES groups and the control group, baseline data collection including a socio-economic survey and the BDM auction run from individuals assigned to PES groups; and (3) follow-up, deforestation monitoring using satellite data from all groups, and disbursement of PES payments to individuals who have won a contract through the auction and have fulfilled it.

Study area and village selection

The study was carried out in the Tarauacá-Envira watershed, focusing on rural households located along the BR-364 highway, within the municipalities of Tarauacá and Feijó. These municipalities lie in a frontier region of agricultural expansion and deforestation.

Landholdings, generally smaller than 100 ha, are composed of roughly 60% forest and 40% pasture, based on analysis of MapBiomas data41 and illustrated in SI Appendix Fig. S2. Agricultural practices involve slash-and-burn techniques to grow maize, manioc, bananas, and vegetables, with one cropping cycle per year aligned with the rainy season. Forest is usually cleared during the dry season (May–July), followed by burning (August–October). Clearing practices include both primary and secondary vegetation. After cultivation, land is either converted to pasture or left fallow to regenerate.

Villages were selected using GFC data to identify settlements with recent deforestation activity, and priority was given to locations with adequate road or river access and a high density of households registered in Brazil’s rural environmental registry (Cadastro Ambiental Rural, CAR). Eligibility criteria for participation included: (i) owning a property of less than 500 ha; (ii) CAR registration; (iii) active engagement in agricultural or livestock production; and (iv) historical deforestation recorded at least twice between 2017 and 2020. We selected six settlements (or villages) for the study and then conducted door-to-door visits to the eligible farms to present the program and enroll the farmers interested in participating.

Recruitment and informed consent

Between May and August 2021, we visited each eligible household to explain the objectives and structure of the PES experiment, including the randomized nature of treatment assignment. Willing participants signed an informed consent form indicating their understanding that they might not receive a PES contract and that their property would be monitored regardless of group assignment.

Property boundaries were verified using high-resolution satellite imagery and corrected where necessary in collaboration with the landowners. These corrections were important due to inconsistencies in the CAR database. The final step in recruitment involved digitizing farm boundaries manually using PlanetScope mosaics42.

A pre-test with 20 households was conducted in August 2021. It revealed comprehension challenges related to contract thresholds and penalty structures. These insights informed revisions to the contracts and the BDM procedure. The experimental design and analysis plan were pre-registered in the AEA RCT Registry prior to data collection under trial number AEARCTR-000819343. Detailed descriptions of the pre-test and protocol adaptations are provided in SI Appendix A.2.

Random assignment and treatments

We conducted stratified randomization by village to ensure a balanced distribution of the treatment according to the observable characteristics available to us before collecting data from the participants, i.e. the information from the freely available satellite imagery: landholding size, 2021 forest cover (based on manually digitized PlanetScope imagery), historical deforestation (over 2015–2019 based on GFC data), distance to the main road, and distance to the nearest market or village. Strata were defined by six settlements. Within each stratum, we randomly assigned the volunteer households to three groups.

Two groups were offered a PES contract that included different forest conservation thresholds: contract A with a zero deforestation target and contract B with deforestation threshold penalties. Under both contracts, conditional payment for the full conservation of forest cover was made at the end of the twelve-month contract period. Under contract A, if any deforestation had been detected through satellite imagery during that time, the landowner received no payment. Under contract B, if satellite images had indicated that deforestation had exceeded certain thresholds, the final payment was adjusted according to a system of penalties. More precisely, a 20-percent penalty was applied if a deforestation area of less than 0.5 ha had been detected. A 50-percent penalty was applied if a deforestation area of between 0.5 and 1 ha had been detected. Finally, no payment was issued if a deforestation patch greater than 1 ha had been detected. Individuals assigned to group C were not offered any PES and were used as controls.

PES contracts were designed for the entire forested area of the property, not on a per-hectare basis. Of the 305 eligible landowners assigned to a PES treatment, only three declined participation in the BDM auction. Balance tests on baseline covariates confirmed comparability across groups (SI Appendix Table S2).

Becker-DeGroot-Marschak auction procedure

Each of the 302 PES-assigned landowners participated in a BDM auction designed to elicit their minimum willingness-to-accept (WTA) payments for complying with each contract. Participants submitted one bid per contract type. They were informed that only one of these bids would be taken into account in the real world for the possible granting of a PES, which would be determined by a random drawing. To ensure balance between treatment arms, the random assignment of type contract (A or B) was performed in advance. However, this information was concealed from both participants and surveyors until after the bids had been submitted. This double-blind approach preserved the integrity of the bidding process by eliminating strategic behavior based on contract assignment.

After the bids were collected, the assigned contract was revealed to the participant, who was then invited to draw a token from an urn corresponding to the assigned contract. If the drawn price met or exceeded the participant’s bid, a contract was offered. Otherwise, the participant received a small in-kind compensation. Each urn was calibrated to reflect the available program budget and to ensure a similar contract award rate between groups, with approximately two-thirds of participants receiving offers. The range of possible prices was not disclosed in advance. For contract A, tokens ranged from 4500 to 6100 BRL; for contract B, from 3900 to 5500 BRL, both in 100 BRL increments.

No participant refused a PES contract once offered. The final contract signatories included 108 in group A and 109 in group B. Summary statistics and distributional comparisons of the bid data are presented in SI Appendix Tables S8–S10. Auction design details, calibration process, and participant training materials are available in SI Appendix A.2.

Follow-up, monitoring and payments

The first PES contracts were signed in September 2021 and the last in December 2021. Since forest clearing occurs predominantly during the dry season (between April and October), we implemented a follow-up procedure to maintain participants’ engagement in the program, verify bank details, and reinforce contract commitments. During this period, our team also assisted participants without bank accounts in opening one to ensure they could receive payments. Reminders were sent regularly until December 2022 via phone calls, text messages, and short videos.

Deforestation monitoring was conducted using satellite imagery. We relied on cloud-free PlanetScope optical mosaics provided by Norway’s International Climate and Forest Initiative (NICFI) to visually identify and delineate deforested areas. By comparing mosaics captured at the beginning and end of the dry season, we manually detected changes in forest cover for each of the 217 plots under contract. This visual assessment, detailed in Fig. S4 (SI Appendix), was used to verify participant compliance and cross-check automated detection results.

Landowners who fulfilled the contract terms received payment via bank transfer shortly after the end of the 12-month monitoring period. In cases of detected deforestation, plots were carefully reviewed on a case-by-case basis to prevent erroneous sanctions due to potential classification errors.

Deforestation data

To evaluate changes in forest cover before, during, and after the PES contracts, we constructed a panel dataset with eleven observations per individual: six during the pre-contract phase, three during the contract phase, and two post-contract. These data were derived from 60 Sentinel-1 Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) images collected between October 2020 and August 2023. Unlike optical imagery, radar images are unaffected by cloud cover, allowing consistent and frequent (every 6–12 days) monitoring.

We applied the Cumulative Sum (CuSum) technique20,21 to detect structural changes in backscatter values (in VV and VH polarizations), which signal deforestation events. This method identifies statistically significant breaks in the signal, corresponding to abrupt canopy loss, as illustrated in Fig. S5 (SI Appendix). The algorithm distinguishes changes in the radar signal associated with new clearings and estimates the date of forest loss by detecting deviations from the mean backscatter. Satellite-based deforestation monitoring, including Sentinel-1 SAR image preprocessing and deforestation detection using the CuSum algorithm, was conducted in Python 3.9.

The radar-based data were validated against manually delineated deforestation polygons extracted from PlanetScope mosaics, ensuring the robustness of the detection results. In accordance with PES contract requirements, we restricted our analysis to primary forest areas, following the official classification by the Brazilian National Institute for Space Research (INPE)44, and considered only full canopy removal at the pixel level as deforestation.

A preliminary validation study in the region demonstrated that excluding the wet season improved the precision of detection by 19% and reduced false positives by 3.8%. Therefore, our analysis focused on three dry-season intervals: April–June, June–August, and August–October. Table S3 in the SI Appendix shows the average clearing patterns across the three experimental groups over the eleven monitoring periods.

Carbon benefits calculation methodology

To calculate avoided CO2 emissions, we considered the average above-ground biomass (AGB) in our study zone (315 metric tons)30, assuming 48% carbon content45. Cost-benefit analysis: analytical perspective

Our cost-effectiveness and cost-benefit analyses are conducted from the implementer’s perspective, where avoided deforestation is valued using the global SCC to express the program’s potential contribution to global climate mitigation, while the costs are solely those incurred in implementing the programme (payments, monitoring, administration, etc.). This is what most PES studies do (e.g., 6).

If one were to adopt a full social cost-benefit perspective, the analysis would ideally include not only the implementation costs but also the opportunity costs borne by participants, as well as potential externalities or broader administrative costs46,47. In our case, these additional social costs are likely small or already internalized by the program, since the PES payments were designed to cover participants’ opportunity costs—otherwise, participation would have been unlikely. Hence, our cost-benefit analysis is not far from what a full social CBA would yield.

Regarding the choice of the SCC, we used the global estimate to express the program’s benefits in terms of avoided climate damages. Using a country-specific SCC, such as one based on national-level damage estimates (e.g.,48), would instead reflect the social perspective of Brazil, valuing only the domestic share of global climate benefits. This would be appropriate if the objective were to assess the national welfare implications of the program. However, because forest conservation in the Amazon generates global public goods, using the global SCC provides a more comprehensive measure of the climate value of avoided deforestation.
