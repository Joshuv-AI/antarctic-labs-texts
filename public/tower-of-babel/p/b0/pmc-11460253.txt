ATP‐binding cassette transporter TaABCG2 contributes to Fusarium head blight resistance by mediating salicylic acid transport in wheat
Authors: Ya‐Zhou Zhang, Jie Man, Lan Wen, Si‐Qi Tan, Shun‐Li Liu, Ying‐Hui Li, Peng‐Fei Qi, Qian‐Tao Jiang, Yu‐Ming Wei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractATP‐binding cassette (ABC) transporters hydrolyse ATP to transport various substrates. Previous studies have shown that ABC transporters are responsible for transporting plant hormones and heavy metals, thus contributing to plant immunity. Herein, we identified a wheat G‐type ABC transporter, TaABCG2‐5B, that responds to salicylic acid (SA) treatment and is induced by Fusarium graminearum, the primary pathogen causing Fusarium head blight (FHB). The loss‐of‐function mutation of TaABCG2‐5B (ΔTaabcg2‐5B) reduced SA accumulation and increased susceptibility to F. graminearum. Conversely, overexpression of TaABCG2‐5B (OE‐TaABCG2‐5B) exerted the opposite effect. Quantification of intracellular SA in ΔTaabcg2‐5B and OE‐TaABCG2‐5B protoplasts revealed that TaABCG2‐5B acts as an importer, facilitating the transport of SA into the cytoplasm. This role was further confirmed by Cd2+ absorption experiments in wheat roots, indicating that TaABCG2‐5B also participates in Cd2+ transport. Thus, TaABCG2‐5B acts as an importer and is crucial for transporting multiple substrates. Notably, the homologous gene TaABCG2‐5A also facilitated Cd2+ uptake in wheat roots but did not significantly influence SA accumulation or FHB resistance. Therefore, TaABCG2 could be a valuable target for enhancing wheat tolerance to Cd2+ and improving FHB resistance.Keywords: Cd2+ , Fusarium head blight, importer, salicylic acid, transporter

INTRODUCTION

As one of the largest protein families in plants, ATP‐binding cassette (ABC) transporters have been shown to transport various substances across membranes, including lipids, heavy metals and phytohormones (Kang et al., 2011; Kretzschmar & Burla, 2011). Therefore, ABC transporters play an important role in plant growth, development and responses to abiotic and abiotic stresses (Kang et al., 2011). Each ABC transporter comprises four core domains: two nucleotide‐binding domains (NBDs) and two transmembrane domains (TMDs), forming a modular structure (Higgins, 1992). Based on domain organization and phylogenetic analysis, eukaryotic ABC proteins are classified into eight major subfamilies (Dean & Annilo, 2005; Verrier et al., 2008). In particular, G‐type ABC transporters have been shown to mediate the transport of various plant hormones, such as auxins, cytokinins and abscisic acid (ABA) (Borghi et al., 2015). For instance, in Arabidopsis thaliana, AtABCG25 and AtABCG40 are high‐affinity ABA transporters, whereas AtABCG14 mediates the transport of cytokinins (Zhao et al., 2023). In wheat, the G‐type ABC transporter TaLr34 facilitates ABA uptake, with two gain‐of‐function mutations in the first TMD exerting opposite effects on wheat leaf rust resistance (Krattinger et al., 2013, 2019).

Salicylic acid (SA) plays a crucial role in wheat's defence against Fusarium graminearum, a major causative agent of Fusarium head blight (FHB) (Qi et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2023). Owing to the impact of global warming and genetic diversity of pathogens, FHB poses a serious threat to global wheat production (Zhang et al., 2023). During F. graminearum infection, wheat induces SA accumulation, which activates systemic acquired resistance and triggers key morphological and chemical defences against FHB (Kang et al., 2008; Zhou & Zhang, 2020). Recent studies have shown that SA prevents F. graminearum infection through an additional immediate mechanism (Huang et al., 2019; Joshi et al., 2016), wherein it directly inhibits the conidial germination and hyphal growth (Qi et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2016). Given the importance of these early defence responses, the rapid transport of SA to infection sites is critical. However, the transporter responsible for SA transport remains unknown.

Cadmium (Cd) is a toxic, nonessential heavy metal that poses a severe threat to plants and animals, leading to a major issue in agricultural production (Genchi et al., 2020; Haider et al., 2021). Cd2+ absorption affects SA accumulation and plant resistance to fungal pathogens (Chu et al., 2022; Li, Cao, et al., 2024; Li, Li, et al., 2024). Recent studies have demonstrated that G‐type ABC transporters, such as AtABCG36 in A. thaliana and OsPDR9/OsPDR20 in rice, are involved in the transport and detoxification of Cd2+ (Fu et al., 2019; Li, Cao, et al., 2024; Li, Li, et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2019). However, the G‐type ABC transporters responsible for Cd2+ transport in wheat are yet to be elucidated.

We previously identified that the G‐type ABC transporter TraesCS5B02G449500 is induced by SA (Qi, Jiang, et al., 2019). In the current study, we functionally characterized TraesCS5B02G449500, demonstrating that it mediates the uptake of SA and Cd2+ in wheat. This study expands our understanding of SA and Cd2+ transport and may aid in breeding wheat cultivars with enhanced FHB resistance and reduced Cd2+ accumulation.

RESULTS

Bioinformatics analysis of TaABCG2

Bioinformatics analysis of TraesCS5B02G449500 using Ensembl BLAST indicated that it belongs to the G‐type ABC transporter family, is located on chromosome 5B and is named TaABCG2‐5B. TaABCG2 homologues were identified across A, B and D genomes of various wheat accessions. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the protein sequences of TaABCG2‐5B and TaABCG2‐5D are conserved in both hexaploid and tetraploid wheat accessions (Figure 1a). However, TaABCG2‐5A alleles are less conserved, sharing only 64%–99% sequence similarity across different wheat accessions. Sequence alignments and 3D structure analysis showed higher diversity in the TMD1 of TaABCG homologues (Figure 1b,c), resulting in functional differences among them.

Reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) analysis in the Triticum turgidum ‘Kronos’ background revealed that the expression levels of TaABCG2‐5A and TaABCG2‐5B were significantly upregulated by SA treatment, with TaABCG2‐5B showing significantly higher expression levels than TaABCG2‐5A (Figure 1d).

Isolation of TaABCG2 ‐5A and TaABCG2 ‐5B mutants

By screening the Kronos TILLING population, we identified two premature stop codons in TaABCG2‐5A (T4‐2323, Q502*) and TaABCG2‐5B (T4‐2173, Q724*) (Figure 2a,b). These mutations were confirmed through Sanger sequencing, resulting in the loss of >50% of TaABCG2‐5A and TaABCG2‐5B.

Characterization of TaABCG2‐5A and TaABCG2‐5B in mutant lines. (a) Schematic representation of wild‐type (WT) and mutant TaABCG2‐5A and TaABCG2‐5B lines. (b) Sanger sequencing results showing mutation sites in TaABCG2‐5A and TaABCG2‐5B. (c) Restriction analysis of PCR products amplified via CAPS and dCAPS markers in four homozygous genotypes. (d) Phenotypes of different mutants at Zadok growth stage 65. (e) Effects of TaABCG2‐5A and TaABCG2‐5B mutations on thousand‐kernel weight (TKW). Ten lines were used to measure TKW for each homozygous genotype. Bars represent the mean values with standard errors. Asterisks indicate significant differences (*p ≤ 0.5, **p ≤ 0.05).

To minimize background mutations introduced by chemical mutagenesis, the homozygous mutants ΔTaabcg2‐5A (loss‐of‐function mutation of TaABCG2‐5A) and ΔTaabcg2‐5B were backcrossed twice to the wild‐type (WT) Kronos line. These backcrossed lines were then crossed to generate the double mutant ΔTaabcg2‐5AB. We developed cleaved amplified polymorphic sequences (CAPS) and derived CAPS (dCAPS) markers to identify homozygous single and double mutants of TaABCG2‐5A and TaABCG2‐5B (Figure 2c). Four homozygous lines—WT (Kronos), ΔTaabcg2‐5A, ΔTaabcg2‐5B and ΔTaabcg2‐5AB—were selected from BC2F6 populations for further analysis.

To evaluate the effect of these mutations on plant development, we monitored the plant length and measured the thousand‐kernel weight (TKW). Although plant length did not significantly differ between the WT and mutant lines (Figure 2d), TKW was significantly reduced in all mutant lines (Figure 2e).

Involvement of TaABCG2 ‐5B in FHB resistance

In the greenhouse, we infected the WT and three mutant lines with F. graminearum. Phenotyping and fungal biomass analysis revealed that ΔTaabcg2‐5B and ΔTaabcg2‐5AB lines were more susceptible to F. graminearum than WT (Figure 3a–d). However, ΔTaabcg2‐5A line showed a similar response to F. graminearum as the WT line. These results confirm that TaABCG2‐5B plays an important role in wheat FHB resistance.

To further validate this, TaABCG2‐5B was reintroduced into the ΔTaabcg2‐5B line. Overall, 10 transgenic plants were identified using ubiquitin (UBI) promoter‐specific primers. RT‐qPCR analysis demonstrated that three overexpression lines—OE‐TaABCG2‐5B‐2, OE‐TaABCG2‐5B‐4 and OE‐TaABCG2‐5B‐16—exhibited significantly higher expression levels of TaABCG2‐5B than the WT line (Figure 3e). As expected, FHB resistance testing indicated that OE‐TaABCG2‐5B‐1 line restored the WT phenotype and that OE‐TaABCG2‐5B‐2, OE‐TaABCG2‐5B‐4 and OE‐TaABCG2‐5B‐16 lines displayed significantly increased FHB resistance in transgenic plants (Figure 3f).

TaABCG2‐ 5B is essential for SA accumulation

We measured SA accumulation in the spikelets of mutant lines and transgenic plants. The results showed that OE‐TaABCG2‐5B‐4 and OE‐TaABCG2‐5B‐16 lines had significantly higher SA content than the other lines (Figure 4). Following F. graminearum inoculation, the SA content was reduced in ΔTaabcg2‐5B and ΔTaabcg2‐5AB spikelets but significantly increased in noninoculated spikelets (Figure 4). The ΔTaabcg2‐5A line did not show a significant difference compared with the WT line (Figure 4). Conversely, OE‐TaABCG2‐5B‐4 and OE‐TaABCG2‐5B‐16 lines exhibited the opposite pattern (Figure 4). These findings indicate that TaABCG2‐5B acts as an SA transporter involved in SA accumulation in inoculated spikelets but is not responsible for long‐distance transport.

TaABCG2‐5B localizes to the cell membrane

TaABCG2‐5B was predicted to localize to the plasma membrane (Figure 5a). To test this, the coding sequence of TaABCG2‐5B (excluding the stop codon) was fused to the 5′‐terminus of GFP in the 1300‐GFP vector, under the control of the UBI promoter. Scanning confocal microscopy revealed that the control group expressing UBI::GFP exhibited fluorescence throughout the cells, whereas green fluorescence was observed specifically in the membrane of UBI::TaABCG2‐5B‐GFP transformed cells in wheat protoplasts and Nicotiana tabacum, aligning with the position of the cell membrane localization marker (Figure 5b,c). These findings prove that TaABCG2‐5B is localized to the cell membrane.

TaABCG2‐5B mediates SA transport into cells

To assess whether TaABCG2‐5B effectively mediates SA transport, we first generated a time‐course curve of intracellular SA content in protoplasts. The results showed a significant increase in SA accumulation after 12 h of cocultivation with 1 mM SA (Figure 6a). Thus, protoplasts from WT, ΔTaabcg2‐5B, OE‐TaABCG2‐5B‐4 and OE‐TaABCG2‐B‐16 lines were cocultivated for 12 h with SA. As expected, the intracellular SA content was significantly reduced in ΔTaabcg2‐5B line, whereas it was restored in OE‐TaABCG2‐5B‐4 and OE‐TaABCG2‐5B‐16 lines (Figure 6b, Table S2).

Yeast cultures plated as serial 10‐fold dilutions, starting from OD600 = 0.6. Representative results from three independent experiments. Three biological replicates were performed for each sample. To further validate this, the coding sequence of TaABCG2‐5B was cloned into the PYC54 vector under the control of the T7 promoter. We then overexpressed TaABCG2‐5B in yeast cells (Saccharomyces cerevisiae). The results showed that yeast cells with TaABCG2‐5B overexpression did not show increased sensitivity to 3 mM SA compared with those transformed with an empty vector (Figure 6c). This phenomenon might be attributed to the fact that SA only has an inhibitory effect on yeast (Figure 6c) (Kodo et al., 2013).

TaABCG2‐5B acts as an importer involved in Cd2+ transport

The expression level of TaABCG2‐5B was analysed across various wheat tissues, including flag leaf, palea, lemma, glume, awn and stem. The RT‐qPCR results revealed a significantly higher expression of TaABCG2‐5B in the root compared to other tissues (Figure 7a). RNA was extracted from the root, stem, leaf and spikelet of WT plants at the Zadok growth stage 65 to explore whether the TaABCG2‐5B transporter complex is involved in Cd2+ uptake. As expected, the ΔTaabcg2‐5B line exhibited about a 32% decrease in Cd2+ accumulation in the root, whereas the OE‐TaABCG2‐5B‐16 line showed about a 106% increase in Cd2+ accumulation (Figure 7b). S. cerevisiae cells with TaABCG2‐5B overexpression showed increased sensitivity to 40 μm Cd2+ compared with those transformed with an empty vector (Figure 6c). Notably, the ΔTaabcg2‐5A line exhibited a similar approximately 30% decrease in Cd2+ accumulation in the root (Figure 7b), and the double mutant of TaABCG2‐5A and TaABCG2‐5B (ΔTaabcg2‐5AB) displayed an additive effect compared with the single mutants (Figure 7b). These results confirm that TaABCG2‐5B encodes an importer ABCG transporter and plays a key role in Cd2+ uptake.

DISCUSSION

ABCG transporters are classified as either exporters (which transport substances out of the cytoplasm) or importers (which transport substances into the cytoplasm) based on the energy derived from ATP hydrolysis across the membrane (Saurin et al., 1999). Recent studies have suggested that some plant ABCGs act as importers, challenging the traditional view that eukaryotic ABC transporters are exclusively ‘exporters’ (Kang et al., 2010; Krattinger et al., 2019). In this study, we identified TaABCG2‐5B as an SA importer in protoplasts, which was further confirmed by Cd2+ absorption experiments (Figures 6 and 7b). Our findings reinforce the notion that ABC transporters can act as importers.

Plants have the largest number of ABC transporter proteins among eukaryotes, and these proteins have been linked to the synthesis and deposition of secondary metabolites. In our study, the deletion of TaABCG2‐5B significantly reduced the accumulation of both SA and Cd2+ in all mutant lines (Figures 6 and 7b), indicating that TaABCG2‐5B acts as a multifunctional transporter. A recent study revealed that the heavy metal transporter OsNRAMP1 enhances reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in rice by reducing Cd2+ absorption, thereby conferring broad‐spectrum resistance to fungal pathogens (Chu et al., 2022). Similarly, Li, Cao, et al. (2024) and Li, Li, et al. (2024) identified AhNramp25 as a transmembrane transporter responsible for Cd2+ and SA absorption; moreover, it plays a role in mediating resistance to Ralstonia solanacearum in peanuts. Therefore, TaABCG2‐5B may contribute to FHB resistance by regulating Cd2+ and SA homeostasis, potentially inducing wheat disease resistance through ROS.

In this study, we observed a significant reduction in SA accumulation in the ΔTaabcg2‐5B mutant line and an increase in its accumulation in the overexpression lines (Figure 4). Additionally, FHB resistance was associated with SA levels (Figure 3). Further analysis of the expression of SA metabolism‐related genes under F. graminearum infection revealed that TaICSB, the gene encoding the key enzyme for SA synthesis, showed significantly increased expression in the ΔTaabcg2‐5AB line and reduced expression in overexpression lines (Figure S2). This suggests that the loss of TaABCG2‐5B function may result in insufficient SA transport into cells, prompting infected cells to upregulate SA synthesis and further enhancing wheat's defence against F. graminearum infection. SA directly inhibits F. graminearum spore germination and mycelial growth and is metabolized by salicylate hydroxylase (encoded by FgNahG) (Qi et al., 2012, 2019b). It is plausible that more SA must be transported from noninfected sites to infected sites. Previous studies have shown that SA treatment of wheat roots and leaves can enhance resistance to F. graminearum (Ameye et al., 2015; Makandar et al., 2012). Interestingly, we found that SA content decreased in inoculated spikelets of ΔTaabcg2‐5B and ΔTaabcg2‐5AB lines but significantly increased in noninoculated spikelets (Figure 4). Lim et al. (2020) showed that SA is translocated from primary infected tissues to distal tissues through the apoplast. Based on the findings that F. graminearum punctures wheat cells and induces SA accumulation at infection sites (Brown et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2024), we suggest that transporting SA into cells using TaABCG2‐5B can facilitate the inhibition of F. graminearum growth in wheat (Figure 8), as supported by Joshi et al. (2016).

Schematic diagram of TaABCG2‐5B mediating the transport of Cd2+ and salicylic acid (SA).

In this study, the deletion of both TaABCG2‐5A and TaABCG2‐5B resulted in >30% reduction in Cd2+ uptake in wheat roots (Figure 7b). However, TaABCG2‐5A did not significantly influence SA accumulation in wheat spikelets (Figure 4). Importers typically rely on substrate‐binding proteins (SBPs) to deliver substrates to the core of the transporter, facilitating the uptake of ions, sugars, amino acids and other molecules (Davidson & Chen, 2004). Locher (2009) reported that the TMD of ABC proteins can recognize transport substrates and bind to specific substrates with the aid of SBPs. For example, the ABCG transporter TaLr34 mediates ABA uptake, and the gain‐of‐function mutations in the TMD1 of LR34res and LR34sus proteins exert opposite effects on disease resistance (Krattinger et al., 2013, 2019). By comparing the protein sequences of TaABCG2‐5A, TaABCG2‐5B and their wheat relatives, we observed that TaABCG2‐5A and TaABCG2‐5B are highly conserved in wheat but differ at the end of one of the TMD2 domains (Figure 1b,c). Further investigation revealed a frameshift mutation of TaABCG2‐5B between amino acid positions 1395 and 1402 in other wheat varieties, coinciding with the above‐mentioned position (consistent with the above results) (Figure S3). Thus, we hypothesized that these sequence differences play a crucial role in determining the specific roles of these ABC transporters in Cd2+ and SA transport (Figure 8). However, it remains unclear whether this mutation decreases Cd2+ and SA accumulation or reduces FHB resistance.

The homologous gene TaABCG‐5A is not significantly involved in SA transport or pathogenicity (Figures 2e and 3). However, it notably reduces Cd2+ absorption, which may be a valuable trait even if some beneficial phenotypes are compromised. Additionally, there are considerable variations in TaABCG‐5A across wheat varieties (Figure 1a), and these variations may help differentiate traits such as TKW and Cd2+ accumulation. Therefore, TaABCG is an important target for breeding wheat cultivars with enhanced resistance to FHB and reduced Cd2+ accumulation.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

Plant materials and growth conditions

All mutants used in this study were derived from durum wheat, T. turgidum subsp. durum ‘Kronos’ (AABB, 2n = 4× = 28), which is moderately susceptible to F. graminearum (Figure S1). Early termination mutants of TraesCS5B02G449500 (Triticum_aestivum_Kronos_EIv1_scaffold_025337, T4‐2173, ΔTaabcg2‐5B) and its allelic gene (Triticum_aestivum_Kronos_EIv1_scaffold_050163, T4‐2323, ΔTaabcg2‐5A) were obtained from ethyl methane sulfonate (EMS)‐mutagenized population of Kronos (Uauy et al., 2009). Transgenic plants were developed as described by Wang et al. (2022). The T1 lines were verified using the primer pair Ubi1899F/Bar496R (Table S3). Phenotypic analyses were conducted using the T2 generation, and the results were confirmed using the T3 generation. All plants were grown in greenhouses under a 16/8 h day/night cycle at 25/16°C and were watered and fertilized as needed with 15:15:15 (N:P:K) after sowing.

Sequence analysis and primer design

Nucleotide sequences of TaABCG2‐5A and TaABCG2‐5B were obtained from the Jorge Dubcovsky Lab database (https://dubcovskylab.ucdavis.edu/). Their coding sequences were predicted based on the T. turgidum Svevo.v1 database (http://plants.ensembl.org/index.html) and confirmed through Sanger sequencing. Primer Premier 5.0 (Premier Biosoft) software was used to design the PCR primers (Table S3). Deduced amino acid sequences were aligned using MEGA v. 5 (Tamura et al., 2011). Neighbour‐joining trees (10,000 replicates) for the classification of deduced proteins were constructed using MEGA software, based on Poisson correction and complete deletion of gaps. DNAMan v. 9.0 (LynnonBiosoft) software was used to conduct multiple sequence alignments.

FHB resistance assay

Based on the Zadoks cereal development scale, the Z65 stage of wheat spikelets was used for the FHB resistance assay. All experiments were conducted using F. graminearum PH‐1, a highly virulent strain. Two flowering florets from the central spikelet of each head were inoculated with 103 conidia. The inoculated heads were sprayed with water, covered with plastic wrap and incubated at 25°C for 48 h. Wheat plants were then placed in a controlled environment at 25°C. FHB symptoms were assessed 10 days after inoculation. Twenty plants were used per line, and all lines were grown in greenhouses under a 16/8 h day/night cycle at 25/16°C with humidity maintained at ≥60%. For in vitro FHB resistance assays, the inoculated spikes were cut from the plants and placed in a high‐humidity environment (≥85%). Ten lines of each homozygous mutant and transgenic line were tested, with 10 spikes used per line. Fungal biomass was estimated by measuring the expression levels of FgGAPDH, as described by Zhang et al. (2016).

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from fresh wheat spikelet powders using E.Z.N.A. Total RNA Kit I (Omega Bio‐Tek) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA was then reverse‐transcribed using PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with genomic DNA Eraser (TaKaRa), following the manufacturer's protocol. The reference genes w‐GAPDH (Ta.66461), Aox (Ta.6172) and hn‐RNP‐Q (Ta.10105) were used for qPCR of the T. turgidum samples (Qi et al., 2012). The relative amount of F. graminearum was estimated by measuring the level of FgGAPDH in inoculated spike tissue at 48 h using qPCR, as previously described (Qi et al., 2019a). qPCR was conducted using the MyiQ real‐time PCR detection system (Bio‐Rad). All primers are listed in Table S3.

Quantification of SA in wheat protoplasts and spikes

Protoplasts were prepared following the method described by Shan et al. (2014). A total of 106 protoplasts were inoculated into W5 solution (77 mM NaCl, 250 mM CaCl2, 10 mM KCl, 4 mM MES, pH 5.7) with or without 1 mM SA as a control. Protoplasts and external solution were collected at 0, 6, 12, 18, 24 and 30 h, and the protoplasts were washed twice with W5 solution. Each treatment was analysed in triplicate.

For wheat spike samples, two florets from each fully developed spikelet at the mid‐anthesis stage were inoculated with 103 conidia, and humidity was increased to 80%. The inoculated wheat plants were treated similar to FHB resistance assay as described above. After 48 h, inoculated spikes with significant differences in SA content were harvested and ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen (Qi et al., 2019b). Analyses were conducted with three biological replicates per treatment.

SA content was measured using monoclonal Antique‐based ELISA kit (#RXJ1401587PL, Ruixin Biological Technology) according to the manufacturer's instructions. To avoid interference from the W5 solution, SA levels in protoplasts and external solutions were confirmed through high‐performance liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (HPLC–MS), as previously reported (Fragnière et al., 2011). Chromatographic results were analysed using an Agilent C18 Eclipse column (5 μm, 2.1 × 150 mm) and OpenLab CDS software (Agilent Technologies).

Subcellular localization

Cell‐Ploc v. 2.0 (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/Cell‐PLoc‐2/) was used to predict using subcellular localization. The TaABCG2‐5B cDNA was cloned into the 163‐hGFP vector (Pro BUI:TaABCG2‐5B‐GFP) through an LR reaction (Invitrogen) and transformed into Kronos protoplasts, as described by Shan et al. (2014). Additionally, TaABCG2‐5B cDNA was cloned into the pCAMBIA1302 vector (Pro CaMV35S:TaABCG2‐5B‐mGFP5) through an LR reaction (Invitrogen) and transformed into N. tabacum leaves following the method described by Zhao et al. (2023). Localization of TaABCG2‐5B was observed under a Leica microscope.

Heterologous expression of TaABCG2 ‐5B in S. cerevisiae

For stress analysis in S. cerevisiae, TaABCG2‐5B cDNA was cloned into the PYC54 vector (Pro T7:TaABCG2‐5B) using an LR reaction (Invitrogen). The vector was then transformed into competent cells of S. cerevisiae Y1H Gold (Weidi) according to the manufacturer's instructions. A control strain was developed by transforming competent cells with the empty PYC54 vector. Transformants were selected on plates containing SD − Ura medium (Coolaber) following incubation at 29°C for 2–3 days. Positive colonies (OD600 = 0.6) were diluted to different concentrations, spotted onto SD − Ura plates supplemented with 3 mM SA or 40 μM Cd2+ and incubated at 29°C for >3 days.

Cd2+ content analysis

To measure Cd2+ concentration in root tissues, WT and transgenic wheat seedlings were exposed to 2 mM CdCl2 (Sigma‐Aldrich) for 5 days, as described by Du et al. (2023). Root tissues were collected, rinsed with double‐distilled water and dried at 60°C. Cd2+ content was quantified using an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (Agilent 5110). The analysis was quality‐controlled following relevant national standards (GB 5009.268‐2016).

Statistical analysis

Prism 8 (GraphPad) software was used to examine the significance of differences between the average numbers of infected spikelets, SA content and gene expression levels. The least significant difference test was used at a significance threshold of p ≤ 0.05. To minimize errors, two independent tests were performed, and the average value was used as the final result.
