Predictors of spinal cord injury in patients with traumatic spinal fractures: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Farhad Heydari, Mehdi Nasr Isfahani, Farzaneh Afzali, Amir Bahador Boroumand, Neda Al-Sadat Fatemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpinal cord injury (SCI) following traumatic spinal fractures substantially elevates the risk of morbidity and mortality; however, reliable early prediction is difficult, particularly in resource-limited clinical environments. This study aimed to identify early admission predictors of SCI in patients with spinal fractures and to explore factors associated with in-hospital mortality within this cohort. In this prospective cohort study, consecutive adult (≥ 18 years) polytrauma patients with clinical signs of spinal injury were enrolled at two tertiary trauma centers in Isfahan, Iran (2023–2024). Among 1000 screened patients, 422 had radiologically confirmed spinal fractures and formed the primary cohort. Admission demographics, injury characteristics, vital signs, and Injury Severity Score (ISS) were recorded. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of SCI. Of the 422 patients with spinal fractures, 85 (20.1%) sustained SCI. The model identified higher ISS (OR 1.16, 95% CI 1.12–1.20, p < 0.001) as an independent admission predictor of SCI, while ICU admission (OR 3.33, 95% CI 1.94–5.70, p < 0.001) and surgical fixation (OR 3.67, 95% CI 2.10–6.50, p < 0.001) were clinical factors significantly associated with SCI. Cervical fracture level was protective (OR 0.64, 95% CI 0.47–0.87, p = 0.005). In an exploratory univariable analysis, non-survivors (n = 18) had significantly lower Glasgow Coma Scale scores, higher ISS, higher heart rate, lower oxygen saturation, and lower educational attainment (all p < 0.05). Higher ISS independently predicts SCI at admission, while ICU admission and surgical fixation are strongly associated with SCI, offering a practical early risk-stratification framework. Exploratory mortality analyses identified associated physiological and injury-severity factors, highlighting avenues for future prognostic research. Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) represents a critical challenge in trauma care, imposing profound and enduring social, functional, and economic burdens on individuals and healthcare systems1. While disproportionately affecting younger adults, SCI remains a significant global health concern across all age groups. Spinal injuries are distinct from other trauma due to their potential for permanent neurological deficit, their frequent occurrence within the context of multiple trauma (polytrauma), and the associated risk of life-threatening injuries to thoracic and abdominal viscera.

The epidemiology of SCI underscores its substantial impact. In the United States, approximately 273,500 individuals live with SCI, with nearly half of new injuries occurring between ages 16 and 302. Annual incidence averages about 40 cases per million, though significant regional variation exists. Etiology varies by age: motor vehicle collisions, violence, and sports are predominant in younger populations, while falls represent the leading cause among the elderly. Global demographic aging is driving an increased prevalence of geriatric spinal injuries, further elevating the worldwide burden of SCI.

Clinical management of spinal trauma is complex. The decision between nonoperative and surgical intervention is particularly nuanced in patients without neurological deficits or major concomitant injuries. This complexity is compounded by the high baseline prevalence of degenerative spinal pain in adults, which can obscure post-traumatic pathology3. Accurate prognostic stratification is therefore essential, as it informs treatment strategies, guides rehabilitation planning, and supports shared decision-making.

Acute cervical SCI presents especially formidable challenges, frequently accompanied by cardiovascular and respiratory complications that significantly increase morbidity4. While advances in critical care, diagnostics, and surgical techniques have improved outcomes2, predicting long-term results remains difficult. Prognosis is influenced by a complex interplay of biomechanical, physiological, and systemic factors. Although a substantial body of literature explores outcome prediction5, inconsistencies persist regarding the value of specific early clinical parameters. Reliable, early prediction models are needed to optimize acute management, stratify patients for clinical trials, and allocate resources efficiently.

Most existing prognostic models are derived from high-income country (HIC) data. Evidence from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where trauma systems face distinct challenges such as resource constraints, prehospital delays, and varying care pathways, remains scarce. This gap limits the global generalizability of current prediction tools. Simple, admission-based prognostic factors that are readily available in all settings could be particularly valuable for early risk stratification in LMIC trauma centers.

Therefore, this prospective study aimed to identify early admission predictors of spinal cord injury in patients with traumatic spinal fractures within a polytrauma cohort from an LMIC setting. Secondarily, we explored factors associated with in-hospital mortality. By clarifying these prognostic relationships, our findings seek to enhance early clinical decision-making and contribute to a more globally representative evidence base for spinal trauma outcomes.

Methods

Study design and setting

This prospective observational cohort study was conducted from January 2023 to December 2024 at two tertiary trauma referral centers in Isfahan, Iran: Kashani Hospital and Al-Zahra Hospital. Both centers provide specialized emergency and neurosurgical care for patients with spinal and multiple traumatic injuries.

Study population

Consecutive adult patients (≥ 18 years) presenting with multiple trauma and clinical suspicion of spinal injury (e.g., localized pain, tenderness, or neurological symptoms) were prospectively enrolled via an institutional trauma registry. All enrolled patients underwent standard radiographic evaluation (spinal X-ray with CT or MRI as clinically indicated).

From this initial cohort, patients with a radiologically confirmed spinal fracture constituted the primary analytic cohort. Spinal cord injury (SCI) was defined as a new neurological deficit attributable to the spinal trauma, confirmed by clinical examination and advanced imaging, and identified within the fracture cohort.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) radiologically confirmed traumatic spinal fracture, and (2) admission to either study hospital during the enrollment period. Exclusion criteria were: (a) emergency transfer directly to the operating room precluding complete initial assessment, (b) death within 24 h of injury, or (c) transfer to another facility before initial evaluation was complete. The sample size was determined pragmatically based on consecutive admissions over the study period, reflecting the relative rarity of SCI in polytrauma settings.

Ethical considerations

The study received ethical approval from the Research Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUI.MED.REC.1403.014) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from patients or legal guardians when feasible. For incapacitated patients, anonymized data were used in compliance with ethical standards for observational research. Confidentiality was maintained by de-identifying patient records and restricting data access to the investigative team.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome was the occurrence of spinal cord injury (SCI) among patients with spinal fracture. The secondary outcome was in-hospital mortality within the same cohort; mortality analyses were pre-specified as exploratory.

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp.). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range) based on distribution, assessed via the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Comparisons between SCI and non-SCI groups utilized independent samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables.

Primary analysis: Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of SCI. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariable analysis were entered into the model. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and Nagelkerke R2.

Exploratory analysis: Given the limited number of in-hospital mortality events (n = 18), a formal multivariate model was not constructed to avoid overfitting. Mortality findings are presented as descriptive, univariable comparisons between survivors and non-survivors. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Study cohort derivation

From the 1000 consecutively enrolled patients with multiple trauma and clinical suspicion of spinal injury, 422 (42.2%) had a radiologically confirmed spinal fracture and formed the primary study cohort (Fig. 1). Within this spinal fracture cohort, 85 patients (20.1% of fractures) were diagnosed with spinal cord injury (SCI). Consequently, all subsequent predictive analyses pertain to the cohort of patients with confirmed spinal fractures (n = 422). The mean age was 39.5 ± 18.8 years, with no significant difference between SCI and non-SCI groups (36.1 ± 19.1 vs. 39.8 ± 18.8 years, p = 0.080). The cohort was predominantly male (77.3%). Road traffic accidents were the leading injury mechanism (62.8%).

SCI patients had significantly higher rates of ICU admission (64.7% vs. 36.9%, p < 0.001), surgical fixation (65.9% vs. 27.8%, p < 0.001), and in-hospital mortality (12.9% vs. 3.8%, p < 0.001). Multivariable predictors of spinal cord injury

A multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to identify independent predictors of SCI within the spinal fracture cohort. In the final adjusted model (Table 2), higher Injury Severity Score (ISS) (aOR 1.16, 95% CI 1.12–1.20, p < 0.001) was identified as an independent admission predictor of SCI. ICU admission (aOR 3.33, 95% CI 1.94–5.71, p < 0.001) and surgical fixation (aOR 3.67, 95% CI 2.10–6.41, p < 0.001) were clinical factors significantly associated with SCI, though these reflect post-admission clinical decisions rather than purely admission-based parameters. Cervical fracture level (compared to thoracic/lumbar) was associated with significantly lower odds of SCI (aOR 0.64, 95% CI 0.47–0.87, p = 0.005). The model demonstrated acceptable calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow test, p = 0.47) and explained approximately 32% of the variance in SCI occurrence (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.32).

Discussion

This prospective cohort study from a low-middle income country (LMIC) identified higher Injury Severity Score (ISS) as a robust independent admission predictor of spinal cord injury (SCI) in polytrauma patients with traumatic spinal fractures. Additionally, ICU admission and surgical fixation were clinical factors strongly associated with SCI, though these variables reflect clinical decisions made after recognition of injury severity or neurological deficit rather than purely admission-based parameters. Cervical fracture level demonstrated a protective association. These findings provide a practical early risk-stratification framework for emergency settings where advanced diagnostics may be limited.

Our core finding—that ISS is a robust predictor of SCI—reinforces the fundamental link between overall trauma burden and spinal cord compromise. This aligns with the pathophysiology of high-energy trauma, where greater force transfer increases the likelihood of both vertebral column failure and neural element injury. The strong association of SCI with both ICU admission and surgical fixation likely reflects clinical decision-making in response to injury severity, instability, or neurological deficit—factors that themselves are hallmarks of significant spinal trauma. The protective association of cervical fracture level is intriguing and contrasts with literature often citing higher cervical morbidity6. This may indicate differences in injury mechanism, kinetic forces, or treatment pathways in our cohort that merit investigation in larger, multicenter studies to delineate the complex interaction between fracture location and cord injury.

Our results are consistent with systematic reviews identifying physiological derangements (e.g., low GCS, hypoxia) and injury severity as key determinants of trauma outcomes7,8. However, most such evidence originates from retrospective studies in high-income countries. By prospectively confirming the utility of simple admission parameters in an LMIC polytrauma cohort, our study adds novel, pragmatic data. In settings with prehospital delays and resource constraints, these immediately available metrics—ISS, GCS, vital signs—offer critical, actionable intelligence for triage and initial management, potentially amplifying their prognostic value9,10.

Compared to previous LMIC studies, our findings complement the work of Derakhshanrad et al.9 who emphasized epidemiological patterns of SCI in Iran, but extend it by providing a predictive model based on acute admission variables. Similarly, while Jacob et al.11 focused on early mortality predictors in South Africa, our study specifically isolates SCI within polytrauma and identifies predictors of the injury itself, rather than its mortality outcome.

In our exploratory, descriptive analysis of in-hospital mortality, we observed expected associations with physiological compromise (lower GCS, tachycardia, hypoxia) and higher ISS. While consistent with global trauma literature7,8, these findings are derived from univariable comparisons due to the limited number of mortality events (n = 18), which precluded stable multivariate modeling. Therefore, they should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating observations that underscore the critical nature of initial resuscitation and highlight candidates for validation as independent predictors in future, larger LMIC cohorts. Our observation that heart rate and oxygen saturation are associated with mortality aligns with recent machine learning studies in critical care trauma populations, which have validated the importance of these vital signs in short-term outcome prediction12.

Strengths and limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the low number of mortality events restricts the analysis of this outcome to exploratory, univariable associations. Second, data on comorbidities, detailed treatment variables (e.g., operative timing, transfusion requirements), and SCI severity grading (e.g., ASIA scale) were unavailable, limiting direct comparability with established prognostic models like the Geriatric Trauma Outcome Score (GTOS)13 and depth of analysis. Third, ICU admission and surgical fixation—while significantly associated with SCI—reflect clinical decisions made after recognition of injury severity or neurological deficit, rather than purely admission-based parameters. A sensitivity analysis restricted to admission variables (ISS, vital signs, GCS, demographics) was not possible given the study design; therefore, these factors should be interpreted as clinical correlates rather than early predictors. Fourth, the absence of long-term functional and survival follow-up confines our conclusions to the in-hospital phase. Future multicenter studies integrating these elements are essential to validate and refine early prognostic tools for spinal trauma in LMIC settings.

Conclusion

This prospective cohort study demonstrates that higher Injury Severity Score (ISS) independently predicts spinal cord injury (SCI) at admission in patients with traumatic spinal fractures, while cervical fracture level is associated with reduced odds of SCI. ICU admission and surgical fixation are clinical factors strongly associated with SCI, reflecting post-admission clinical decisions. In exploratory analyses, physiological markers such as Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), heart rate, and oxygen saturation were associated with in-hospital mortality. This SCI prediction framework provides a pragmatic approach to early risk stratification for trauma centers, with particular relevance for resource-constrained settings. Key limitations include the absence of comorbidity data, the post-admission nature of ICU and surgical variables, and the exploratory, non-predictive status of the mortality analysis. Future multicenter studies that integrate comprehensive clinical data with long-term functional outcomes are essential to validate and refine these prognostic tools for global application.
