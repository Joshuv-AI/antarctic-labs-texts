Lactate–albumin ratio measurements at multiple time points and their association with outcome in pediatric trauma: a PICU-based study
Authors: Kazim Zararci, Hatice Feray Ari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood trauma is one of the leading global causes of morbidity and mortality. Due to the trauma-triggered systemic inflammatory response, simple laboratory parameters are important for rapid assessment in pediatrics. Lactate is an indicator of hypoxia, serum albumin is a negative acute phase reactant, and the lactate-to-albumin ratio (L/A) has been reported to be associated with trauma severity and mortality. The aim of this study was to evaluate the association between L/A measurements at 0, 24, and 48 h and PICU mortality in pediatric trauma patients. Secondary outcomes were recorded and described but were not included as primary analytical endpoints.MethodsThis retrospective observational cohort study included patients aged 1 month–18 years admitted to a tertiary PICU due to trauma between May 2023 and September 2025. Only cases with complete paired data at 0, 24, and 48 h were analyzed. Demographic and clinical variables, laboratory parameters, clinical scores, and Pediatric Risk of Mortality Score III (PRISM III), Pediatric Trauma Score (PTS), Shock Index (SI), Shock Index, Pediatric Age-Adjusted (SIPA), and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) were recorded. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis assessed the ability of L/A measured at 0, 24, and 48 h, together with comparator measures, to discriminate mortality.ResultsAmong 115 patients, mortality was 11.3%. L/A values at 0, 24, and 48 hours were significantly higher in non-survivors than survivors. ROC analysis showed good discriminatory performance for both lactate and L/A at 24 and 48 hours. Although L/A demonstrated numerically higher area under the curve (AUC) values, no formal statistical comparison between lactate and L/A was performed.ConclusionL/A measurements at 24 and 48 h showed good discriminatory performance and were associated with mortality in pediatric trauma. L/A may serve as a complementary biomarker alongside established clinical scoring systems, although its incremental value over lactate alone remains uncertain.Keywords: Pediatric intensive care, Trauma, Lactate-to-albumin ratio, Mortality

Introduction

Childhood trauma remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, particularly in children older than one year [1, 2]. Early risk stratification in pediatric trauma is challenging due to age-related physiological variability and the complex interplay between hemodynamic instability and systemic inflammatory response [3–5].

It has been suggested that combining clinical and laboratory parameters improves prognostic assessment in critically ill children [6]. Lactate, as a marker of tissue hypoxia, and serum albumin, as a negative acute-phase reactant, have both been associated with trauma severity and mortality [7]. L/A, which combines lactate and albumin measurements, has been investigated as a potential biomarker associated with outcomes in pediatric critical illness [7].

In parallel, clinical scoring systems such as the PRISM III, PTS, SI, and SIPA are widely used to assess disease severity and predict outcomes [8–10]. However, these tools may have limitations when used individually, and integrating biochemical and physiological parameters may provide a more accurate assessment of prognosis. Most previous studies have focused on single time-point L/A measurements at admission. In contrast, this study evaluates L/A at multiple predefined time points (0, 24, and 48 h) and compares its performance with established clinical scoring systems within the same cohort.

The objectives of this study were threefold: to evaluate the association between L/A measurements at 0, 24, and 48 h and PICU mortality in pediatric trauma patients; to compare the discriminatory performance of L/A at these predefined time points with established clinical scoring systems, including PRISM III and PTS; to describe selected secondary clinical outcomes in relation to L/A measurements, although these were not included in primary predictive analyses.

Materials and methods

This was a retrospective observational cohort study conducted in the PICU of a tertiary university hospital. All patients aged 1 month to 18 years admitted to the PICU due to trauma between May 2023 and September 2025 were screened for eligibility. Trauma mechanisms were classified as motorcycle accidents, in-vehicle and out-of-vehicle traffic accidents, falls from height, drowning, penetrating injuries, and bicycle accidents, based on the documented mechanism of injury in the patients’ medical records. Patients who did not meet the age criteria, those treated in the PICU for non-traumatic reasons during the same period, and those without complete paired lactate and albumin measurements at all predefined time points (0, 24, and 48 h) were excluded.

The study data were retrospectively recorded from the hospital electronic record system into a structured case report form. Demographic and clinical data including age, gender, mechanism of trauma, length of PICU stay, need for surgery and blood product transfusion, presence of cardiac arrest, development of brain death, and mortality status were recorded. Laboratory tests obtained at admission included complete blood count, C-reactive protein (CRP), and blood gas analysis. Serum albumin levels were measured at 0, 24, and 48 h. L/A was calculated at 0, 24, and 48 h based on corresponding lactate and albumin measurements.

Additionally, clinical scoring systems such as PRISM III, PTS, SI, SIPA, and GCS were recorded using the patients’ clinical and laboratory data. Clinical scoring systems were calculated using standard definitions. PRISM III score was calculated based on physiological and laboratory variables obtained within the first 24 h of PICU admission. PTS was calculated using six parameters including weight, airway status, systolic blood pressure, level of consciousness, presence of fractures, and presence of wounds, with lower scores indicating more severe trauma. SI was calculated as the ratio of heart rate to systolic blood pressure at admission, with SI ≥ 1.0 considered high risk. SIPA was evaluated using published age-specific cut-off values (e.g., ≥ 1.2 for ages 1–3 years, ≥ 1.0 for ages 4–6 years, ≥ 0.9 for ages 7–12 years, and ≥ 0.8 for ages 13–16 years), as previously defined. Although SIPA has been primarily validated in selected pediatric trauma populations, particularly in specific age groups and injury types such as blunt abdominal trauma, it has also been used more broadly as a practical indicator of hemodynamic instability. In this study, SIPA was included for comparative purposes rather than as a standalone predictive tool. GCS was recorded at admission and analyzed as a dichotomous variable (GCS > 8 vs. ≤ 8) for statistical purposes. All clinical scores were calculated based on data obtained at the time of PICU admission.

The primary outcome was PICU mortality. Secondary outcomes were evaluated descriptively and were not included in the primary predictive analyses.

All analyses were performed using the SPSS 25.0 program. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and histograms/Q-Q plots. According to the Shapiro–Wilk test, most continuous variables, including age, length of stay, PRISM III score, PTS, and laboratory parameters, did not show normal distribution (p < 0.05) and were therefore presented as median (IQR). Only a limited number of variables demonstrated normal distribution; therefore, parametric tests were applied only to these variables where appropriate. Specifically, a small subset of laboratory variables demonstrated approximate normal distribution. Variables with normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical variables were reported as n (%). For comparisons between two groups according to mortality status, the independent samples t-test was used for normally distributed continuous variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test; Fisher’s exact test was applied when the expected cell count was < 5. Measurements at 0, 24, and 48 h were separately compared between mortality groups at each time point (Mann–Whitney U). Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to assess the discriminatory performance of L/A at 0, 24, and 48 h, as well as comparator laboratory and clinical parameters, for mortality prediction. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

The Aydın Adnan Menderes University Clinical Research Ethics Committee approved this study (Approval No: 2025/263, Date: 18 September 2025). All procedures performed in this study involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized patient data, the requirement for informed consent to participate was waived by the Ethics Committee in accordance with national regulations. Therefore, informed consent from parents or legal guardians was not required.

Results

A total of 115 pediatric trauma patients were included in the study. During the study period, all eligible trauma patients were screened. Only three patients were excluded because lactate and/or albumin measurements were unavailable at one or more predefined time points, all of whom died early before completion of follow-up sampling. No patients were excluded due to early discharge. The cohort was predominantly male, and the overall clinical profile reflected moderate to severe trauma. Motorcycle accidents and falls from height were the most frequent mechanisms of injury, and multiple trauma was observed in the majority of cases. Approximately one-third of patients required surgical intervention, and more than half received blood product transfusions. Overall PICU mortality was 11.3%. All deaths occurred in patients who fulfilled the clinical criteria for brain death. The demographic and baseline clinical characteristics of the cohort are summarized in Table 1.

ROC curve analysis showed that PRISM III had good discriminatory performance, with an AUC of 0.97, although this should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of events. Importantly, L/A measured at 24 and 48 h also showed good discriminatory performance (AUC = 0.97 and 0.96, respectively; p < 0.001 for both), with numerically similar AUC estimates to PRISM III; however, no formal statistical comparison between ROC curves was performed. Both lactate and L/A showed higher AUC values at later time points than at baseline. However, because no formal longitudinal or within-patient trend analysis was performed, these findings should be interpreted as time-point-specific discriminatory performance rather than evidence that repeated measurements improved prognostic accuracy. Discussion

Mortality among pediatric trauma patients was found to be associated with trauma severity scores, acid–base imbalance indicators, and elevated L/A measured at 0, 24, and 48 h. This pattern likely reflects persistent tissue hypoperfusion and an ongoing systemic inflammatory response. Although L/A measured at 24 and 48 h showed good discriminatory performance, the stronger performance observed at later time points may reflect progression of physiological deterioration rather than early risk stratification ability.

Higher PRISM III scores in non-survivors support its role as a prognostic indicator in pediatric trauma [12, 13].

Trauma mechanisms were broadly consistent with previous reports and are not discussed in detail, as they are not central to the primary objective of the study [14–16]. The relatively high rate of motorcycle-related injuries may reflect local characteristics and should be interpreted with caution [17].

Severe trauma and neurological involvement were prominent in non-survivors, consistent with previous reports [15]. The absence of surgical intervention among non-survivors likely reflects injury severity and treatment selection, as these patients may have had non-survivable or rapidly deteriorating conditions, particularly severe neurological injury, precluding surgical intervention.

SI and SIPA were associated with mortality in our cohort, supporting their relationship with hemodynamic instability in pediatric trauma, as previously reported [18, 19].

Elevated inflammatory, hepatic, renal, and coagulation markers in non-survivors reflect progressive organ dysfunction and are consistent with previous studies linking L/A to mortality and multiple organ dysfunction [20].

In a study by Durak et al., elevated L/A, procalcitonin, and neutrophil–lymphocyte ratios were associated with mortality in pediatric trauma patients [11]. However, evidence on the prognostic value of L/A in pediatric trauma, particularly at multiple time points, remains limited. This highlights the need for further investigation. Previous studies have demonstrated the association of SI and SIPA with mortality and adverse outcomes; SIPA, in particular, may provide improved discrimination due to its age-adjusted structure [9, 18]. However, their performance may be limited when used as standalone predictors, as highlighted in recent meta-analyses [19]. In this context, our study evaluated physiological scores together with a biochemical marker. However, the present study was not designed to determine whether L/A provides incremental prognostic value beyond its individual components.

Severe neurological impairment (GCS < 8) was strongly associated with mortality, consistent with previous studies [15, 21, 22].

Lower pH and base excess (BE) values at all time points in non-survivors indicate persistent metabolic acidosis, consistent with previous studies linking acid–base disturbances to mortality in trauma [23]. Lower PCO₂ levels at 24 h in non-survivors may reflect the effects of controlled mechanical ventilation and targeted ventilatory strategies in critically ill patients rather than improved physiological status.

L/A has been increasingly investigated as a biomarker associated with mortality in pediatric trauma and critical illness [11, 24–26]. In our cohort, PRISM III (AUC = 0.969) and PTS (AUC = 0.946) demonstrated good discriminatory performance. L/A measurements at 24 and 48 h showed numerically similar discrimination estimates to PRISM III, although no formal statistical comparison between ROC curves was performed. However, L/A should not be interpreted as a replacement for established clinical scoring systems. Instead, it may serve as a simple adjunctive biomarker that complements physiological scores and supports clinical assessment. Unlike PRISM III, which reflects early cumulative severity within the first 24 h of PICU admission, L/A may reflect ongoing metabolic deterioration during PICU follow-up. Furthermore, comparisons between PRISM III and admission L/A should be interpreted cautiously, as PRISM III is derived from cumulative physiological and laboratory data collected during the first 24 h of PICU admission, whereas admission L/A represents a single time-point measurement. Therefore, these measures do not reflect equivalent temporal windows of assessment. The lactate–albumin ratio is derived from its individual components, and the present study does not formally evaluate its incremental prognostic value over lactate or albumin alone. Although L/A showed numerically higher AUC values than lactate at all evaluated time points, the statistical significance of these differences remains unknown. In addition, the higher discriminatory performance observed at later time points may reflect temporal proximity to the clinical outcome rather than a true increase in prognostic accuracy. As patients approach clinical deterioration or death, laboratory abnormalities may become more pronounced, which can lead to improved discriminatory performance. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Further studies are needed to clarify its independent contribution. However, given the retrospective design of the study, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causal or independent predictive relationships.

Although our study has several limitations, including its single-center, retrospective design and the inclusion of a heterogeneous trauma population, these factors may limit the generalizability of our findings. The retrospective nature of the study may also be associated with incomplete data capture and unmeasured confounders, which could influence the observed associations. In particular, the single-center design may restrict direct comparability with previously reported multicenter studies, where patient diversity, trauma patterns, and management strategies may differ significantly. Moreover, the relatively small sample size and limited number of mortality events may have reduced the statistical power of the analyses and increased the risk of overfitting, particularly in exploratory comparisons of biomarkers and scoring systems. The very small number of mortality events may also have resulted in optimistic estimates of discriminatory performance. In particular, confidence intervals approaching 1.000 for several ROC analyses should not be interpreted as evidence of near-perfect discrimination, but rather as a reflection of limited event numbers and sampling variability. Furthermore, formal statistical comparison of ROC curves between lactate and L/A (e.g., DeLong analysis) was not performed. Therefore, although L/A demonstrated numerically higher AUC values than lactate, it remains uncertain whether the addition of albumin provides significant prognostic improvement beyond lactate alone. These limitations should be carefully considered when interpreting the discriminatory performance of L/A and physiological scoring systems, as the observed accuracy may not be fully generalizable to broader or more diverse pediatric trauma populations. Therefore, larger multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate these findings. In addition, although several secondary outcomes were recorded, they were not analyzed in relation to L/A measurements and were presented descriptively; therefore, conclusions regarding these outcomes should be interpreted with caution. Although patients with drowning and penetrating injuries were not the primary focus of the analytical cohort, these cases were included in the descriptive characteristics to reflect the real-world trauma spectrum of our PICU, and a degree of heterogeneity remains. Drowning and penetrating injuries constituted a small proportion of the cohort (n = 7, 6.1%) and were unlikely to have materially influenced the overall findings. A substantial proportion of mortality in our cohort was associated with severe neurological injury, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other mechanisms of death in pediatric trauma. Importantly, inclusion in the 24- and 48-hour analyses required survival until those predefined time points and availability of complete paired measurements. Consequently, patients who died early before completion of follow-up sampling were necessarily excluded from these later analyses. This introduces survivorship (temporal selection) bias and may have systematically overestimated the discriminatory performance of L/A measured at 24 and 48 h, as the most rapidly deteriorating patients were not represented in these time-point-specific analyses. Therefore, the stronger discriminatory performance observed at later time points should not be interpreted as evidence of superior early prognostic utility, but rather as a finding potentially influenced by temporal proximity to outcome and cohort selection.

Despite these limitations, our study has several important strengths. First, all patients were managed and followed by the same clinical team, ensuring consistency in clinical decision-making and reducing inter-observer variability. Second, the availability of L/A measurements at multiple time points allowed comparison of L/A values across predefined time points rather than relying solely on baseline measurement. Third, the simultaneous evaluation of physiological scoring systems (SI, SIPA, PRISM III, and PTS) together with a biochemical marker within the same cohort enabled a comprehensive and integrative analysis of prognostic performance. Finally, as our unit is the only PICU providing pediatric trauma care in the region, the study reflects real-world clinical practice and minimizes referral-related selection bias, thereby strengthening the internal validity of the findings. Overall, our study contributes to the literature by providing a structured and clinically contextualized evaluation of L/A across predefined time points within the same cohort, rather than introducing a novel biomarker. This approach provides a more clinically applicable framework for interpreting L/A measurements in pediatric trauma.

In conclusion, L/A measured at 24 and 48 h showed good discriminatory performance and were associated with mortality. The observed performance trends appeared numerically similar to those of established clinical scores such as PRISM III; however, formal comparative ROC testing was not performed. Given the retrospective single-center design and the limited number of mortality events, these findings should be interpreted with caution. L/A may represent a potentially useful adjunctive biomarker; however, its incremental prognostic value over lactate alone could not be determined in the present study and requires confirmation in larger prospective studies.
