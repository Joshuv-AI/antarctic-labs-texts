Revolutionizing Mg-based guided bone regeneration mesh derived from endogenous dentoalveolar bone augmentation
Authors: Shiyuan Li, Mingyu You, Hao Zheng, Yinchuan Wang, Bozun Miao, Jia Pei, Meiping Xiong, Guangyin Yuan, Liang Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ideal regeneration of alveolar bone remains challenging due to its irregular morphology, complex structure, and unique biological functions. Advances in computer-aided design (CAD) and additive manufacturing (AM) have facilitated the development of customized bone grafts, making the repair of complex alveolar defects feasible. Magnesium (Mg) alloys with biodegradability, excellent osteogenesis and mechanical properties have been in high demand for guided bone regeneration (GBR) applications. However, the clinical application of Mg alloys is hindered by rapid degradation. Thus, this study developed a customized Mg-alloy GBR mesh via digital modeling and laser powder bed fusion (LPBF), with a strontium-doped octacalcium phosphate (SrOCP) coating to regulate degradation and osteogenesis. Sustained release of Mg2+ and Sr2+ activated the PI3K/Akt pathway, significantly promoting endogenous bone regeneration. In beagle models, the mesh achieved effective repair of alveolar defects (15 mm × 8 mm × 5 mm) without extra collagen membranes or bone substitutes. It showed controlled degradation (92.16% volume retention at 4 weeks, >80% degradation at 12 weeks) and strong osteogenic capability (new bone volume fraction 86.89% at 12 weeks). Collectively, a promising GBR strategy with biodegradable LPBF Mg-alloy mesh to facilitate the alveolar bone regeneration is proposed.

Introduction

Alveolar bone defects, frequently caused by trauma, tooth extraction, or bone diseases, can severely impair both masticatory function and facial aesthetics [1]. Furthermore, insufficient alveolar bone volume leads to inadequate stability of dental implants and reduces their long-term survival rates [2]. Although current bone repair techniques, such as autologous bone grafting and bone substitute materials [3], have shown some success in animal experiments and clinical studies [4,5], ideal bone regeneration in alveolar regions remains challenging due to the highly irregular anatomical morphology of alveolar bone and the complexity of bone regeneration in the oral microenvironment [1].

Guided Bone Regeneration (GBR), currently the most effective clinical technique for alveolar bone augmentation [6], is based on the fundamental principle of placing a barrier membrane over the bone defect area after filling it with artificial bone powder, which prevents soft tissue invasion and ensures sufficient space for the growth of newly formed bone tissue [7]. Collagen membranes, such as Bio-Gide®, are the most widely used GBR membranes in clinical practice [8]. However, it is difficult for collagen membranes to maintain a stable space for bone formation due to their rapid degradation in blood and saliva. While titanium mesh exhibits superior mechanical stability relative to collagen membranes and has demonstrated acceptable clinical efficacy in bone repair [9], it requires intraoperative shaping to conform to the specific profile of the alveolar bone. Bending the mesh inevitably generates sharp edges, which can irritate the mucosa and lead to exposure of the titanium mesh [10]. Notably, reports indicate that the exposure rate of titanium mesh reached 33.3% [11,12], significantly increasing the risk of secondary infection and bone augmentation failure. Recent advances in additive manufacturing have created new possibilities for improving GBR materials. Currently, customized titanium meshes, fabricated via digital modeling and additive manufacturing techniques (such as laser powder bed fusion (LPBF)), are clinically used [13,14]. These customized meshes precisely conform to the alveolar bone anatomy, eliminating the need for intraoperative manual shaping and reducing complications such as mesh exposure and bone resorption [15]. Nevertheless, titanium meshes are non-resorbable, and they must be removed in a second surgery, which causes additional trauma to patients. Hence, designing next-generation GBR membranes with biodegradability, suitable mechanical properties, and biocompatibility has become a key strategy to overcome the current clinical limitations in alveolar bone defect reconstruction.

Magnesium and its alloys, as a new generation of biodegradable materials, offer significant advantages for bone repair. Firstly, the elastic modulus of pure magnesium (45 GPa) closely matches that of cortical bone tissue (12-30 GPa), effectively reducing stress shielding effects induced by mechanical mismatches in traditional metal implants such as titanium mesh [16]. Secondly, their complete degradability avoids the secondary surgical removal. More importantly, Mg2+ ions released during magnesium degradation have been demonstrated to promote osteoblast adhesion, proliferation, and bone matrix mineralization by activating the PI3K/Akt and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways [[17], [18], [19]], thereby exhibiting distinct osteoinductive and osteogenic capabilities. Recent studies have further validated the clinical potential of magnesium in alveolar bone regeneration. For instance, Rider et al. implanted pure magnesium membranes (NOVAMag®) into the alveolar bone defects of beagles [20]. They observed that at 16 and 52 weeks postoperatively, the magnesium membrane group exhibited a significantly higher average percentage of bone area than the collagen membrane group. This confirmed that the magnesium membrane was capable of promoting alveolar bone regeneration.

Based on the above findings, we fabricated a customized magnesium alloy mesh using laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) for this study. The advantages of LPBF Mg-based GBR mesh are listed as follows: (1) LPBF Mg-alloy GBR mesh suitable for the defect site can be precisely designed based on computed tomography (CT) data of the specific alveolar bone, enabling anatomical adaptation at the implant-bone interface; (2) the Mg-alloy GBR mesh not only acts as a barrier membrane providing mechanical support but also effectively promotes bone regeneration in the defect area due to the osteogenic effect of magnesium ions.

However, the clinical translation of LPBF customized Mg-based GBR mesh still faces three major challenges: (i) The low boiling point and high vapor pressure of magnesium lead to powder splash during the manufacturing process, resulting in distortion or blockage of pores on the surface of magnesium mesh. (ii) Rapid in vivo degradation of LPBF Mg-based GBR mesh will lead to the release of excessive Mg2+, which may activate osteoclasts and impair osteogenic processes [21]; (iii) Hydrogen gas accumulates during overly rapid degradation, forming subcutaneous cavities that induce the exposure of the Mg mesh and significantly increase the risk of secondary infection and bone augmentation failure [22]. To address these challenges, optimizing additive manufacturing processes to match Mg-based GBR mesh and developing appropriate coatings for GBR mesh to regulate degradation are regarded as potential solutions [[23], [24], [25]].

It is well known that the primary inorganic component of bone and tooth enamel is calcium phosphate (CaP) [26], which has been extensively studied for its biological functions. CaP has been shown to participate in cell adhesion and signaling, regulating the osteogenic differentiation of stem cells and bone metabolic homeostasis by releasing Ca2+ ions [27,28]. Studies have demonstrated that the CaP coating effectively inhibits the corrosion of the magnesium alloy matrix and significantly reduces its degradation rate [29]. Furthermore, it activates the osteogenic signaling pathways through the sustained release of Ca2+, PO43−, and Mg2+ ions, thereby markedly enhancing the bone integration properties of the material. However, the oral environment is harsher than conventional bone implantation sites, posing higher demands for coating protection and functional adaptability, which are limitations that traditional CaP coatings struggle to address. To overcome this, we developed a Sr-doped octacalcium phosphate (SrOCP) coating based on traditional CaP technology, which offers two critical advantages. Firstly, in terms of corrosion protection, Sr doping modulates OCP crystal growth, reducing the grain aspect ratio while improving the coating's kinetic stability and crystallinity [30], further enhancing coating density and barrier performance. Secondly, in terms of osteogenic capacity, Sr2+ exerts a dual regulatory effect on bone metabolism by enhancing osteoblast activity and new bone formation while inhibiting osteoclast function and bone resorption [31,32], and the synergistic release of Sr2+ (from the coating) and Mg2+ (from the Mg alloy matrix) amplifies osteogenic differentiation [33].

Recently, our research group has overcome the aforementioned challenges and successfully developed a customized, degradable LPBF Mg-alloy mesh for alveolar bone defect reconstruction. A three-wall bone defect measuring 15 mm in length, 8 mm in width, and 5 mm in height was created in the alveolar bone of beagles. We successfully restored these large defects in 3 months using magnesium meshes alone—without artificial bone powder or collagen membranes. Concurrently, as the magnesium mesh degrades within approximately 4 months, the need for secondary surgery is avoided, demonstrating immense clinical potential. To our knowledge, this represents a significant breakthrough and milestone event in the field of alveolar bone defect reconstruction.

Methods

Fabrication of LPBF Mg-alloy GBR mesh and SrOCP coating

First, cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) scanning was conducted on adult beagle dogs (10-15 kg) to acquire digital imaging and communications in medicine (DICOM) data. The DICOM datasets were imported into Avizo software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction of the mandible. Second, the STL file of the mandible was imported into Magics software (Materialise, Belgium) for refined processing and topological repair. Guided by the refined anatomical geometry, virtual surgical planning was conducted to delineate the precise boundaries of the bone defect and identify the optimal implantation sites. A conformal mesh structure was then architecturally designed to fully cover the defect area. Based on this model, the customized magnesium mesh (designated as CMM) was fabricated via laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) using a ProX DMP 320 system (3D Systems, USA) with Mg-Nd-Zn-Zr alloy powder (JDBM) [23]. The LPBF customized magnesium meshes (designated as LPBF-CMM) were prepared using the following key parameters: the laser power was 80 W, the scan speed was 400 mm/s, the layer thickness was 20 μm, the hatching space was set as 60 μm, and the adopted scanning strategy was bidirectional exposure with a rotation angle of 90° between adjacent layers. Additionally, we fabricated LPBF-customized titanium meshes (designated as LPBF-CTM) that served as a control group for subsequent experimental characterization.

Before coating, the LPBF-CMMs were electrochemically polished to remove unmelted powder particles adhering to the surface. The polishing electrolyte consists of 50% phosphoric acid and 50% absolute ethanol; the polishing time is 8 min. After polishing, the samples were ultrasonically cleaned in absolute ethanol three times (3 min each) and then dried for subsequent use. Subsequently, a precursor dicalcium phosphate dihydrate (DCPD) coating was deposited on the polished LPBF-CMMs, following the method described in our previous report [29]. A hydrothermal conversion process was then performed by immersing the DCPD-coated LPBF-CMMs into an optimized aqueous solution containing the following reagents: calcium disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetate hydrate ((C10H12N2O8)CaNa2·xH2O, Ca-EDTA, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), tetrasodium ethylenediaminetetraacetate ((C10H12N2O8)Na4, Na-EDTA, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), sodium dihydrogen phosphate (NaH2PO4, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), and strontium nitrate (Sr(NO3)2, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), and the pH was adjusted to 6.5 with 1 M NaOH. The compositions and formulas of the hydrothermal reaction solution used in the preparation process are according to our previous work [34].

XRM analysis

X-ray micro-tomography (XRM, Zeiss Xradia 520 Versa) was employed to scan the LPBF magnesium alloy mesh. The obtained slice data were imported into the Avizo software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to analyze and evaluate the forming quality of the printed parts.

Coating characterizations

The surface morphology and microstructure were observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, MIRA3, Czech Republic), equipped with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, Aztec X-MaxN80, Czech Republic, and EDS, INCA X-Act, USA) for elemental mapping. Phase composition analysis was conducted using X-ray diffraction (XRD, D8 Advance, Germany) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at 40 kV and 40 mA, scanning 2θ from 3° to 80° at 5°/min.

Mechanical property testing

The bending strength of Mg-based GBR mesh was measured by the three-point bending test according to the GB/T 232-2010 bending standard, as specifically shown in Fig. S1. A load was applied perpendicular to the mesh plate at a rate of 1 mm/min until the mesh plate specimen failed, with three replicates.

Coating adhesion test

Coating adhesion was evaluated via the tape test (ASTM D3359-09) and static tensile test. For the tape test, a 1 mm-spaced orthogonal grid was scribed through the coating to the substrate on Mg alloy discs (Φ14 × 3 mm). A 3M 600 tape was applied and rapidly peeled off at 60°, and coating peeling was graded under magnification per the standard scale: Grade 0 (no peeling), Grade 1 (≤5% peeled area), Grade 2 (5%–15% peeled area), Grade 3 (15%–35% peeled area), Grade 4 (35%–65% peeled area), and Grade 5 (more severe than Grade 4 peeled area).

The static tensile test was conducted by Shanghai Lingsheng Medical Device Technology Co., Ltd., following GB 23101.4-2008, using a 100 kN floor-standing tester (Tinius Olsen) equipped with a 10 kN force sensor and a displacement sensor. Φ20 mm × 3 mm coating discs were bonded to fixtures with adhesive, cured at 120 °C for 16 h, and cooled to room temperature. Samples were mounted with the long axis aligned to the tensile load; after preloading to 20 N at 1 mm/min, stretching proceeded at 2.5 mm/min until complete separation. Five parallel samples were tested, and the average value was calculated.

Immersion tests

The samples were immersed in α-modified Eagle's medium (α-MEM, Gibco, Invitrogen), supplemented with 1% (v/v) penicillin and streptomycin (Gibco, Invitrogen), and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 21 days. The ratio of sample surface area to solution volume was 1.25 cm2/mL, in accordance with the in vitro standards outlined in ISO 10993-5. The culture medium was refreshed every three days, and the concentrations of Mg2+ and Sr2+ ions in the extracts were analyzed using an inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometer (ICAP6300, USA). Cellular experiments

Preparation of extracts

The samples, including LPBF-CMMs, SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMMs, and LPBF-CTMs, were sterilized by ultraviolet irradiation for 1 h and then immersed in α-MEM containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin mixture, according to ISO 10993-5. After 72 h of immersion, the extracts were obtained. Then, the extracts were filtered through a 0.22 μm pore size membrane (Millipore, USA) to remove potential metal particles and corrosion products, and stored at 4 °C until use.

Cytocompatibility assessment

The mouse osteoblastic cell line (MC3T3-E1, National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures, China) at passages 3-5 and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs, National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures, China) at passages 3-5 were cultured in alpha-modified Eagle medium (α-MEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin (Gibco, USA). MC3T3-E1 cells at passages 3-5 maintain stable osteogenic potential, which is essential for evaluating the material's osteogenic compatibility. HUVECs, as a classic model of vascular endothelial cells, were selected based on the close coupling of osteogenesis and vascularization in alveolar bone regeneration. For the cell proliferation assay, MC3T3-E1 cells and HUVECs were seeded at 2000 cells per well in 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h. The culture medium was then replaced with 100 μL of sample extracts containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) per well, and wells containing α-MEM served as negative controls. On days 1, 3, and 5 of culture, the existing culture medium was substituted with 100 μL of CCK-8 working solution (Cell Counting Kit-8, Beyotime Biotech, China), prepared by mixing fresh α-MEM and CCK-8 reagent at a volume ratio of 10:1. After 2 h of incubation at 37 °C, the optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Gen5, BioTek, USA).

For live/dead cell staining assays, cells were co-cultured with extracts from various samples at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well and incubated for 1 and 3 days. At the end of each period, the medium was discarded, and the cells were gently rinsed three times with PBS. Next, the live/dead staining working solution was prepared according to the instructions of the Live/Dead Staining Kit (Solarbio, China). 10 μL of the calcein-AM solution and 30 μL of the propidium iodide (PI) solution were added separately to 10 mL of PBS. Finally, live cells (stained green) and dead cells (stained red) were observed using fluorescence microscopy (IX71, Olympus, Japan). Immunofluorescence staining with phalloidin and DAPI was performed following the manufacturer's instructions. After 48 h of co-culture with extracts, the medium was discarded. The cells were gently rinsed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, and then sequentially stained in the dark with Alexa Fluor 488 phalloidin (Invitrogen, USA) for 30 min and DAPI (Invitrogen, USA) for 10 min. Cytoskeletal morphology was imaged using fluorescence microscopy (IX71, Olympus, Japan).

Cellular adhesion morphology was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). MC3T3-E1 cells and HUVECs were directly seeded on the samples and incubated for 24 h. After incubation, the samples were rinsed twice with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min, and then dehydrated through a gradient of ethanol (30%, 50%, 70%, 85%, 95%, and 100% v/v), with each step maintained for 10 to 15 min. Following air-drying, the samples were sputter-coated with gold and imaged under SEM (MIRA3, Czech Republic) to evaluate cell morphology and cellular adhesion.

In vitro osteogenic properties

The extracts used to evaluate osteogenic properties were obtained by incubating samples in osteogenic induction medium (Procell, China), following the same procedure described in section 2.7.1. MC3T3-E1 cells were co-cultured with osteogenic extracts for 3 and 7 days to assess alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity. For ALP staining, the cells were stained using the BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Color Development Kit (C3206, Beyotime, China). For the quantitative measurement of ALP activity, the Alkaline Phosphatase Assay Kit (P0321S, Beyotime, China) was used according to the manufacturer's instructions. The total protein content of each sample was quantified with a Micro BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

After 21 days of co-culture with osteogenic extracts, mineralized calcium nodules in MC3T3-E1 cells were stained using Alizarin Red S Solution (OriCell, China). To quantify mineralization, stained calcium nodules were dissolved in cetylpyridinium chloride solution, and the optical density (OD) was measured at 567 nm with a microplate reader (Gen5, BioTek, USA).

After osteogenic induction for 3 and 7 days, the expression levels of osteogenic-related genes, including ALP, Runx2, Col1α1, and OCN, were measured using quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) following this protocol: First, total RNA was extracted with the EZ-press RNA Purification Kit (EZBioscience, USA), and RNA concentration was assessed with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Next, reverse transcription was performed using a T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, UK) to synthesize cDNA from the RNA. Subsequently, a mixture of ddH2O, cDNA, SYBR Green master mix, and gene-specific primers was added to 96-well plates. qRT-PCR analysis was conducted on a CFX Connect Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, UK). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the comparative threshold cycle (ΔΔCt) method, with GAPDH as the housekeeping gene. The primer pairs for qRT-PCR are shown in Table S1.

RNA-sequence analysis

The culture and osteogenic induction methods for BMSCs were the same as those for MC3T3-E1. After 7 days of culture, the culture medium was aspirated. The cells were rinsed twice with DPBS (Dulbecco's Phosphate-Buffered Saline), then TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was added. RNA extraction, transcriptome sequencing, and subsequent analysis were performed by Shanghai OE Biotech Co., Ltd. RNA purity and quantification were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, USA). RNA integrity was evaluated with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA). The library was constructed using the TruSeq RNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, USA).

Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten. Low-quality data were filtered using Trimmomatic. The FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of Transcript per Million Mapped Reads) expression levels of genes were quantified via Cufflinks software. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq R package. Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs) were selected based on the criteria of q-value <0.05 and fold change >2, and hierarchical clustering analysis was conducted on these DEGs. Finally, GO (Gene Ontology) and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) enrichment analyses were performed on DEGs to identify the primary biological functions or signaling pathways affected by the differentially expressed genes.

Western blotting

To verify the molecular mechanism by which SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM promotes osteogenic differentiation, protein was extracted from BMSCs seeded in 6-well plates. After 1 day of culture, the medium was replaced with osteogenic induction medium supplemented with extracts of SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM samples, and refreshed every 72 h. Cell samples were collected and lysed in RIPA lysis buffer containing 1% phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, China), and samples were denatured by heating at 100 °C for 5 min. 20 μg of protein was separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membrane was blocked with a quick blocking solution (Beyotime, China) at room temperature for 15 min. Then it was incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Autoradiograms were obtained using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) western blotting substrate (Tanon, China). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA), with GAPDH as the internal reference. The primary antibodies used were as follows: anti‐GAPDH antibody (1:5000; Abcam, UK), anti-phospho-Akt (Ser473) (1:5000; Abcam, UK), anti-Akt (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, USA), and anti-PIK3R3 (1:1000; ABclonal, China).

Animal model and implantation process

The in vivo experiments used 12 adult male beagles (10-15 kg), with bilateral alveolar bone defects constructed in each dog's mandible (2 defects per dog). This yielded 24 defects total, allocated to 4 experimental groups (2 time points: 4 weeks and 12 weeks; 3 biological replicates per group per time point): (1) LPBF-CTM (n=3), (2) LPBF-CTM/Bio-Oss/Bio-Gide (n=3), (3) SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM (n=3), (4) sham operation (blank control) (n=3). To minimize experimental bias, defect locations (left/right mandible) and group assignments were randomized using a computer-generated random number table.

Tooth extraction in beagles

The third and fourth premolars of the mandible, along with their corresponding maxillary counterparts, were extracted. Postoperative infection was prevented with intramuscular ceftiofur sodium for one week. At 12 weeks post-extraction, complete healing of the gingiva and alveolar bone was confirmed, and the animals were prepared for guided bone regeneration surgery.

Guided bone regeneration surgery

A horizontal incision was made along the alveolar ridge crest in the edentulous area, followed by vertical incisions distal to the second premolar and mesial-buccal to the first molar. After flap elevation, the mandible was exposed. Using a low-speed fissure bur, a three-walled bone defect measuring 15 mm in length, 8 mm in width, and 5 mm in height was created in the mandible of beagles, guided by a pre-fabricated template. Then, the customized magnesium and titanium meshes were positioned to cover the defect, with each mesh secured with two titanium screws on the buccal side. The soft tissue flap was repositioned and closed with interrupted sutures. Postoperatively, vital signs were closely monitored. The oral cavity was irrigated daily with saline solution for 7 days, and intramuscular ceftiofur sodium was administered for 5 days to prevent infection.

Surgical diet and care protocols

To support optimal soft tissue healing and alveolar bone regeneration, a stage-specific diet was implemented for all experimental beagles from post-operation day 1 to the end of the study. Week 1 (Liquid Diet): Mix the dog food with water at a weight ratio of 1:5. Weeks 2 to 4 (Semi-liquid Diet): Mix the dog food with water at a weight ratio of 2:5. From the fifth week, unsoaked dog food was fed to encourage natural chewing.

For surgical site protection, all beagles were housed individually in spacious cages (1.5 m × 1.0 m) to prevent aggressive interactions, such as biting or scratching, that could damage the surgical site. The surgical site was examined daily for signs of trauma or infection. Oral irrigation with sterile saline was performed twice daily (morning and evening) to remove food debris, and a 0.12% chlorhexidine gluconate mouth rinse (diluted 1:10 with water) was applied once daily for the first 14 days to reduce the oral bacterial load, thereby balancing infection prevention with minimal mucosal irritation. From the 15th day, no special oral health care was provided.

Micro-CT

Animals were euthanized at 1 and 3 months post-surgery. Mandibles were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Micro-CT analysis was performed to evaluate alveolar bone regeneration. Three-dimensional bone morphology was reconstructed in Dragonfly software, and parameters such as bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) were quantified using CTAn.

Histological analysis

Following Micro-CT, the fixed bone samples were embedded in polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA), dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and sectioned. Sections were stained with methylene blue-acid fuchsin and toluidine blue, and representative coronal sections illustrating new bone formation were selected for analysis.

Double fluorescence labeling

Fluorescent dyes were administered intraperitoneally 14 and 7 days before euthanasia: alizarin red S (30 mg/kg), followed by calcein (20 mg/kg). Histological sections were prepared post-harvest and scanned under a TissueFAXS Plus fluorescence microscope (TissueGnostics GmbH, Vienna, Austria) to capture panoramic images at wavelengths specific to calcein and alizarin red S.

Biosafety assessment

To assess potential organotoxicity induced by the graft materials, the hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, and kidneys of beagles were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h. Tissues were embedded, sectioned into 5 μm-thick slices, and subjected to hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining for histological examination.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was analyzed using SPSS 20.0. Student's t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were employed to determine significance levels. P-value <0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Design and optimization of biodegradable LPBF Mg-alloy GBR mesh structure

Structure design of LPBF Mg-alloy GBR mesh

We conducted forming experiments on magnesium alloy mesh structures using LPBF technology, designing and fabricating planar mesh plate specimens with five structural configurations: triangle, quadrilateral, hexagon, circle, and circular honeycomb. The CAD models and LPBF samples are shown in Fig. S2. The results indicated that different geometric configurations significantly influence the forming accuracy and defect distribution of the Mg-alloy meshes. For both triangular and quadrilateral mesh specimens (Fig. S2a and b), the edges exhibited distinct discontinuities. Locally, there were spheroidized protrusions caused by dynamic fluctuations in the molten pool, which partially obstructed the meshes at the acute edges. This phenomenon occurred because the triangular and quadrilateral specimens contain many regions with acute angles, where localized heat accumulation exacerbates the molten pool's instability, significantly affecting forming accuracy. Additionally, the circular (Fig. S2d) and circular honeycomb (Fig. S2e) mesh specimens exhibited significant printing inaccuracies. Specifically, complete blockage was observed in the pores of both specimen types. A large number of incompletely melted Mg-alloy powder particles adhered to the surfaces of the pore walls. Some circular meshes displayed noticeable dimensional deviations and lack-of-fusion defects. The forming issues of circular meshes mainly stem from their poor self-supporting capability. During laser melting, the edges of circular pores lack sufficient structural support. The molten material flows downward under gravity, and its surface tension is insufficient to maintain the circular pore shape, ultimately leading to deformation at the pore edges. In contrast, the hexagonal meshes (Fig. S2c) demonstrated excellent forming consistency without mesh discontinuities or blockages, well preserving the structural integrity of the designed model.

Mechanical properties of LPBF Mg-alloy GBR mesh

The mechanical properties of Mg-alloy GBR mesh specimens with different structural configurations were evaluated via the three-point bending method, as shown in Fig. S1. The calculated bending strength and bending stiffness are shown in Table 1. Specimens with hexagonal and circular configurations exhibited significant mechanical advantages; their bending strengths reached 122.6±4.6 MPa and 128.4±7.4 MPa, respectively, and their bending stiffness reached 1694.6±97.7 N mm2 and 1756.8±162.4 N mm2, respectively. In contrast, specimens with triangular and circular honeycomb configurations exhibited inferior bending performance, with bending strengths of 88.3±5.0 MPa and 84.3±2.2 MPa, respectively, which were only about 70% of the strength of the hexagonal mesh specimens. Considering the printing accuracy of Mg-alloy meshes with different configurations, the hexagonal mesh emerged as the optimal choice, offering a balance between forming precision and mechanical performance. Its geometric configuration not only facilitates LPBF fabrication but also maintains high structural integrity and design consistency during manufacturing. Design model and morphology of LPBF Mg-alloy GBR mesh

Based on the results in sections 3.1.1, 3.1.2, the hexagonal mesh was determined to be the optimal choice for preparing LPBF Mg-alloy GBR meshes. Thus, in this section, regular hexagons served as the fundamental unit to fabricate customized magnesium alloy meshes adapted to the anatomical morphology of the mandibular alveolar bone in beagles. A schematic diagram of the alveolar bone defect restoration and CAD design model of LPBF-CMM with detailed dimensions are shown in Fig. 1, which exhibit a fully open-cell structure with uniformly distributed regular hexagonal pores. The length and thickness of the mesh were 20.32 mm and 0.5 mm, respectively, and the circumcircle diameter of the regular hexagonal pores was 3 mm. Several fixation holes were designed along the edge of the mesh, enabling secure fixation to the alveolar bone defect site using fixation screws. We characterized the printing quality of LPBF-CMM by X-ray microscopy (XRM), Optical Micrographs (OM), and SEM images, with representative results displayed in Fig. S3 and Fig. S4. Fig. S3 shows XRM cross-sectional images of LPBF-CMM before and after electrolytic polishing. Only a few surface powder adhesions (a1, a2) are present, and these small amounts of powder adhesion can be easily removed after electrolytic polishing (b1, b2). Design and characterization of SrOCP coating on the surface of Mg-alloy GBR mesh

Fig. 2a shows the macroscopic images of the LPBF-CMM and SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM. Before polishing, a small amount of unfused Mg-alloy powder still adhered to the mesh pore walls, resulting in a relatively rough surface. After polishing, the unmelted powder generated during LPBF fabrication was effectively eliminated, making the mesh's overall structure consistent with the design model. The SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM exhibits a grayish-white appearance. The microscopic morphology of the coating is shown in Fig. 2b and c. At low magnification (Fig. 2b), the coating exhibits uniform, intact adhesion to the mesh surface, with a structure composed of micrometer-scale spherical particles. High-magnification SEM micrograph (Fig. 2c) revealed that the coating consisted of nanoscale lamellar grains. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis (Fig. 2d) confirmed that the primary elements of the coating were calcium (Ca), phosphorus (P), strontium (Sr), and oxygen (O). The cross-section of the coated Mg-alloy is shown in Fig. 2e. It can be clearly observed that the coating exhibited a uniform, dense, and continuous morphology, with an average thickness of 30 μm. The XRD pattern of the coating displayed two characteristic peaks of octacalcium phosphate (OCP) at 2θ angles of 4.8° and 31.8° (Fig. 2f). Notably, the peak intensity at 2θ=4.8° decreased due to Sr doping, indicating that the coating is primarily composed of Sr-doped octacalcium phosphate (hereafter denoted as the SrOCP coating). In vitro degradation behavior

Fig. 3a shows SEM surface morphologies and cross-sectional micrographs of LPBF-CMM and SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM after a 21-day immersion test. The results indicated that the surface of SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM remained intact, with no significant changes in the microstructure. In contrast, the surface of the uncoated LPBF-CMM formed plate-like degradation products with deep cracks, indicating poor corrosion resistance. Moreover, SEM cross-section images revealed that uncoated LPBF-CMM developed a layer of degradation products thicker than 50 μm after 21 days of immersion. Fig. 3b–e shows the results of ion release (Mg2+, Sr2+) in the immersion solution, the calculated corrosion rates, and the changes in pH. Based on Mg2+ release rates and calculated corrosion rates, the SrOCP coating significantly reduced Mg2+ release over 21 days. Notably, during the first 7 days, the uncoated LPBF-CMM exhibited rapid Mg2+ release, with a cumulative release of 4926.9 ppm, while the SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM showed a much lower cumulative Mg2+ release of 175.5 ppm, almost one-thirtieth of the uncoated samples. From day 10 to day 21, the concentration of Mg2+ released stabilized in both uncoated and SrOCP-coated groups. Meanwhile, the release rate of Sr2+ from SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM decreased gradually over the 21 days. The corrosion rates of different GBR mesh groups calculated based on the Mg2+ concentration are shown in Fig. 3d. The corrosion rates of the uncoated LPBF-CMM and SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM were 1.081 mm/year and 0.059 mm/year, respectively. Additionally, the pH of extracts from the SrOCP-coated group remained consistently lower than that from the uncoated group. The pH (Fig. 3e) changes exhibited fluctuations owing to the uncoated LPBF-CMM's intense initial reaction with water, which released abundant OH− and caused a rapid pH increase before stabilization via corrosion product formation. In contrast, the SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM group maintained a stable, weakly alkaline pH during the initial period due to the protective barrier provided by the coating. To better simulate the oral physiological environment, in vitro immersion experiments in artificial saliva were supplemented. The results showed that the Mg2+ release kinetics of the LPBF Mg-alloy GBR mesh in artificial saliva were consistent with those in α-MEM, as shown in Fig. S6. Notably, the corrosion rate of the mesh in artificial saliva was slightly lower than that in α-MEM, indicating good corrosion resistance under saliva-mimicking conditions.Fig. 3Results of in vitro immersion test. Osteogenic effect in vitro

Further experiments were performed to evaluate the osteogenic properties of the LPBF meshes. In this section, the LPBF-CMM group was excluded from testing because its extract inhibited cell proliferation due to excessive Mg2+ release, as evaluated in the earlier cytocompatibility assessment. The osteogenic differentiation potential of SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM was evaluated by monitoring alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and mineralized matrix formation in MC3T3-E1 cells. ALP serves as a biomarker of early osteogenesis, while calcium deposition is a critical process in osteoblast differentiation and maturation. ALP staining images taken on days 3 and 7 showed that the purple-stained area gradually increased in both size and density with increasing incubation time (Fig. 5a). On day 3, the purple color of cell colonies in the SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM group was slightly darker than that in the control and LPBF-CTM groups. On day 7, the SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM group exhibited the most intense purple staining among the three groups. The quantitative analysis of ALP activities was consistent with the staining results (Fig. 5b). On day 7, the ALP activity in the SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM group was significantly 1.45-fold higher than that in the control group and 1.56-fold higher than that in the LPBF-CTM group. Meanwhile, no significant difference in ALP activity was observed between the control and LPBF-CTM groups. For mineralization capacity, after 21 days of osteogenic induction, the area of deep red-stained mineral nodules in the SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM group (Fig. 5c) was significantly larger than that in the control and LPBF-CTM groups. Consistently, quantitative analysis of Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining dissolution showed that the calcium deposition amount in the SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM group was the highest (Fig. 5d). In vivo evaluation of Mg alloy meshes degradation

In this study, the in vivo degradation of SrOCP-coated LPBF magnesium alloy meshes was systematically evaluated using micro-CT. Fig. 10a and b revealed that the mesh retained 92.16±4.18% of its original volume at 4 weeks post-implantation, localized degradation and partial reduction in rod diameter were observed only at the contact area with titanium screws, while the main structure remained intact, showing no significant differences from its shape at the initial implantation stage. The slow degradation of SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM conferred a distinct advantage over traditional collagen membranes, as it provides robust mechanical support for early bone regeneration. Meanwhile, a considerable amount of new bone formation was observed and the BV/TV value achieved 56.12%, as shown in Fig. 7. By 12 weeks, the remaining volume had decreased to 18.81±2.26%, indicating that over 80% of the mesh had fully degraded. As illustrated in Fig. 10a, the mesh had lost its original structure, leaving small amounts of magnesium alloy fragments and corrosion products. Based on this calculation, the complete degradation time of the SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM is approximately four months, which meets the clinical requirement that GBR implant materials should degrade within six months. The SrOCP-coated magnesium alloy meshes that degraded in vivo for 4 and 12 weeks were characterized using SEM and EDS techniques (Fig. 10c). At 4 weeks post-implantation, the magnesium alloy mesh was found to be closely attached to newly formed bone tissue, with excellent integration. Additionally, elemental mapping analysis of strontium revealed a continuous distribution across the coating domain, confirming structural integrity of the SrOCP layer. The coating maintained a uniform thickness of approximately 25 μm with dense morphology. As for specimens explanted at 12 weeks, residual magnesium alloy persisted locally. Advantages of additively manufactured Mg-based GBR mesh versus manually shaped Mg-based GBR membrane.

Some researchers recognized the degradable and osteogenic properties of Mg-based materials and tried to fabricate biodegradable Mg-based GBR membranes by manually shaping thin Mg sheets [20]. This process has at least two disadvantages: (1) manual bending of thin Mg plates fails to achieve a precise match with the contour of the alveolar bone; (2) manual bending introduces stresses into the Mg plates, particularly significant tensile stress on the outer side of the sheet. For Mg-based materials, tensile stress will accelerate degradation rates exponentially [35]. On the one hand, rapid degradation might cause premature fracture of the outer edge of the curved surface and result in the loss of its essential physical support. On the other hand, it leads to rapid local release of Mg2+ ions and the activation of osteoclasts [21], ultimately affecting the alveolar bone repair process.

In contrast, the LPBF technology employed in this study enables the fabrication of Mg-alloy meshes customized to the shape of alveolar bone defects, based on computed tomography (CT) scans. The entire preparation process is completed before surgery, significantly reducing operative time and improving surgical efficiency. Additionally, the mesh conforms precisely to the defect site, eliminating the need for intraoperative adjustments. Unlike the manually bent magnesium meshes reported in the literature [20], the additive-manufactured meshes are free from significant internal stresses induced by bending deformation, thereby avoiding the drawback of stress-accelerated corrosion in Mg-based membranes. Thus, by integrating biodegradable Mg-alloy materials with additive manufacturing, this study demonstrates significant advantages and promising application potential in the field of customized alveolar bone defect reconstruction.(2)The synergistic effect of Mg2+/Sr2+ controlled release on osteogenic mechanism.

The synergistic effect of Mg2+/Sr2+ controlled release on osteogenic mechanism.

Different concentrations of Mg2+ released from the degradation of Mg-alloys have been confirmed to exert distinct effects on osteoblast-related cells and osteoclasts, and they also have varying impacts on the osteogenic differentiation capacity of osteoblast-related cells [36]. In this study, SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM released Mg2+ within a relatively low concentration range (2.5 mM–5 mM)—a range conducive to promoting the osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) [37,38]. This range is also biologically effective for oral-derived stem cells, as supported by studies showing 1–10 mM Mg2+ enhances odontogenic differentiation of human dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) and 0.5–5 mM Mg2+ promotes periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) proliferation and osteogenic differentiation [39,40]. In contrast, a high-Mg2+ environment significantly inhibited osteogenic differentiation when the Mg2+ concentration reached 20 mM [38]. The results of this study showed that this concentration range effectively promotes osteoblast proliferation, increases alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity, and upregulates the expression of osteogenesis-related genes (Runx2, OCN, ALP, Col1α1). Additionally, previous research by our group has demonstrated that Sr2+ and Mg2+ exhibit a synergistic effect, which can regulate the TLR4/PI3K/Akt signaling pathway in osteoblasts and promote the proliferation and differentiation of MC3T3-E1 cells [33]. RNA-seq results in the present study further confirmed that SrOCP significantly enhances the osteogenic differentiation capacity of BMSCs, leading to the high expression of genes such as Spp1 and Fgfr3 in the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, as shown in Fig. 6. Other studies have indicated that Mg2+ generated from Mg-alloy mesh degradation can activate additional signaling pathways, such as the Wnt signaling pathway, which induces BMSCs differentiation into the osteoblast lineage [19]. Sr2+ can also activate multiple signaling pathways to induce osteogenesis: for instance, it activates the FAK/MAPK pathway to promote osteogenic differentiation [41], and the non-canonical Wnt5a signaling pathway to maintain the stemness of stem cells and regulate their differentiation capacity [42]. Based on the above statements, it can be concluded that SrOCP-coated LPBF-CMM mainly releases the appropriate concentrations of Mg2+ and Sr2+, which synergistically activate signaling pathways such as PI3K-Akt, regulate the expression of osteogenesis-related genes, further promote the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and the enhancement of osteoblast function, and ultimately achieve efficient induction of endogenous bone regeneration.(3)Why can Mg-based GBR meshes achieve alveolar bone defect regeneration in beagles without collagen membranes?

(3)Why can Mg-based GBR meshes achieve alveolar bone defect regeneration in beagles without collagen membranes?

Why can Mg-based GBR meshes achieve alveolar bone defect regeneration in beagles without collagen membranes?

The core principle of traditional GBR technology is to construct a physical barrier using collagen membranes to isolate soft tissue invasion and reserve sufficient space for osteoblast proliferation [43]. However, collagen membranes have inherent limitations: their mechanical strength is weak, making them prone to collapse under soft tissue pressure or intraoperative manipulation. Moreover, they degrade too rapidly in saliva and blood environments, failing to maintain their barrier function throughout the entire process [44]. Additionally, the adherence of collagen membranes to the edges of bone defects depends on the surgeon's operational proficiency. Poor adherence easily creates gaps, which become breeding grounds for bacteria and increase the risk of infection [45].

A key phenomenon observed in this study—the thin fibrous membrane naturally formed on the surface of Mg-based meshes one week after implantation—provides a core basis for replacing collagen membranes. This fibrous membrane is a natural stress response of the body to foreign implant placement [46], and its formation mechanism is closely related to the biological properties of Mg-based materials: Mg2+ released by magnesium degradation can activate the proliferation and migration of fibroblasts, promote collagen fiber secretion and cross-linking, and further form a naturally derived barrier with a dense structure and excellent adherence. Compared with traditional collagen membranes, this fibrous membrane offers two advantages: first, it has stronger mechanical stability and relies on the rigid support of Mg-based meshes to avoid collapse or displacement; second, it has better biocompatibility, as it is derived from autologous tissue secretion without the risk of immune rejection. Future research should focus on elucidating the histological characteristics of the fibrous membrane and its formation mechanism. This will require sampling of the Mg mesh and adjacent tissues at multiple time points post-implantation, followed by comprehensive histological and immunohistochemical analyses so as to uncover the underlying mechanism of fibrous membrane replacing collagen membranes.

In previous studies, although the pure magnesium membrane (NOVAMag®) reported by Rider et al. did not require a second surgery, it still relied on precise intraoperative fixation to maintain its barrier function [20]. In contrast, the Mg-based meshes in this study further simplified the surgical procedure through the dual effects of “self-rigid support + natural fibrous membrane barrier”. Furthermore, traditional titanium meshes lack bioactivity and thus cannot induce the orderly formation of fibrous membranes. Their sharp edges easily irritate the mucosa, resulting in exposure and subsequent complications [11]. However, the weakly alkaline degradation environment (pH 8.0-8.5) of Mg-based meshes can inhibit the proliferation of oral pathogenic bacteria, reduce mucosal irritation and infection risks, and further ensure the integrity of the natural barrier.(4)Why can Mg-based GBR meshes achieve alveolar bone defect regeneration in beagles without bone substitute materials?

(4)Why can Mg-based GBR meshes achieve alveolar bone defect regeneration in beagles without bone substitute materials?

Why can Mg-based GBR meshes achieve alveolar bone defect regeneration in beagles without bone substitute materials?

In traditional GBR technology, bone substitute materials (e.g., Bio-Oss) primarily function to fill bone defect spaces and provide osteogenic scaffolds, but they have several limitations in clinical applications. First, bone substitute particles tend to occupy the core space required for osteoblast proliferation, resulting in newly formed bone tissue only growing around the particles and failing to form a continuous trabecular structure. Results from the supplementary control group (titanium mesh + Bio-Oss + collagen membrane) in this study also confirmed that at 12 weeks, there remained a clear boundary between bone powder particles and newly formed bone, with no effective osseointegration achieved (Fig. S7–S8). Second, some bone substitute materials degrade extremely slowly; studies have shown that Bio-Oss particles remain undegraded in the human body even 20 years after implantation, and long-term residues may trigger chronic inflammation or affect bone tissue remodeling [47]. Third, the osteogenic activity of bone substitute materials depends on their integration with autologous bone, and their insufficient osteoinductive capacity becomes more prominent in large-area bone defects.

The SrOCP-coated Mg-based meshes used in this study completely eliminated the reliance on bone substitute materials through an endogenous osteogenesis strategy based on “ion synergistic induction + microenvironment regulation”. From the perspective of ion effects, Mg2+ released by magnesium mesh degradation and Sr2+ released by SrOCP coating form a synergistic effect: Mg2+ can activate osteogenic signaling pathways such as PI3K-Akt and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways, promoting the differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells into osteoblasts [19]; Sr2+ enhances the mineralization ability of bone matrix by replacing Ca2+ in hydroxyapatite and simultaneously inhibits osteoclast activity to reduce bone resorption [48,49]. Under the synergistic effect of these two ions, the proliferation activity, ALP activity, and ability to form mineralized nodules of osteoblasts are significantly improved, as shown in Fig. 4, Fig. 5, enabling efficient osteogenesis without the need for exogenous bone substitute materials to provide scaffolds.

Compared with previous studies on Mg-based materials, the advantage of this study lies in the precise regulation of degradation rate: uncoated magnesium materials degrade too rapidly, and excessively high local Mg2+ concentrations can inhibit osteoblast activity [36]. In contrast, the SrOCP coating reduces the corrosion rate to 0.059 mm/year, maintaining the Mg2+ release concentration within the optimal osteogenic range. In addition, the hexagonal pore structure (circumcircle diameter of 3 mm) of Mg-based meshes provides natural channels for osteoblast migration and vascular infiltration. Its porosity and connectivity are superior to the particle accumulation structure of traditional bone substitute materials, further promoting endogenous bone regeneration.(5)Limitations and future prospects of this study

(5)Limitations and future prospects of this study

Limitations and future prospects of this study

Despite the significant progress made in this study, there are still certain limitations. Firstly, the follow-up period of the animal experiment was 12 weeks. Although obvious bone regeneration effects were observed, the complete degradation time of the Mg mesh, the long-term (e.g., 1-2 years) stability of bone tissue, and the long-term survival rate of implants after placement still need further verification. Secondly, although the beagle alveolar bone defect model used in this study is close to clinical scenarios, the human oral environment is more complex (e.g., the impact of periodontitis, diabetes, and other systemic diseases), and more comprehensive toxicological and preclinical studies are required before clinical translation. Thirdly, although the degradation rate of the meshes has been significantly reduced, their degradation behavior in different oral microenvironments (e.g., acidic saliva, infected wounds) has not been clarified.

Future research can be carried out in the following directions: firstly, optimize the composition ratio of the SrOCP coating to further regulate the release rates of Mg2+ and Sr2+, making them more accurately match the healing cycles of different types of bone defects; secondly, conduct multi-center, large-sample clinical trials to verify the applicability of the meshes in different clinical scenarios (e.g., socket preservation, maxillary sinus elevation, periodontitis bone defect repair); thirdly, explore the synergistic application scheme of meshes and implants, and develop an integrated “mesh-implant” implantation system to further simplify the surgical procedure and improve the treatment effect.

Conclusion

In response to the urgent clinical need for alveolar bone reconstruction and augmentation through guided bone regeneration technology, this study developed a biodegradable, customized LPBF Mg-alloy mesh coated with a strontium-doped calcium phosphate (SrOCP). In vitro experiments demonstrated that the SrOCP-coated LPBF Mg-alloy mesh exhibits controlled degradation behavior, excellent cytocompatibility, and remarkable osteogenic capacity. In vivo, the SrOCP-coated LPBF magnesium alloy GBR mesh showed favorable biosafety and osteogenic activity in beagle alveolar bone defects. After 12 weeks, 80% of the mesh had degraded, while the defect was fully filled with new bone, positioning it as an ideal GBR material with significant potential for clinical application.
