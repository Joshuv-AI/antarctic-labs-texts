Comparative analysis of Pharmaceutical Care practice in rural and urban community pharmacies in Valencia, Spain: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Salvador Gutiérrez-Igual, Rut Lucas-Domínguez, José Sendra-Lillo, Isabel Romero Crespo, M Carmen Montesinos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDrug-Related Problems (DRPs) pose a significant Public Health concern affecting patient safety in both urban and rural settings. Ensuring the Rational Use of Medicines is a central activity of Pharmaceutical Care (PC). This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of Pharmacist-led Intervention (PLI) in reducing DRPs and to analyze differences between rural and urban settings. The influence of sociodemographic variables in PLI acceptance was also estimated.MethodsA prospective quasi-experimental study was conducted with a pre-post PLI approach from August 2023 to June 2025 in Valencia (Spain). Patients whose medication was reviewed in community pharmacies were included and classified according to pharmacy location, categorized into rural (less than 5,000 inhabitants) and urban areas. This allowed a comparative analysis in each setting. Main variables included total number of DRPs, DRPs categorization, and sociodemographic factors in each location.ResultsPLI resulted in a notable reduction in general DRPs (p < 0.05) in both urban and rural settings. Significant differences were observed for precautions for use, contraindications, and START criteria (p < 0.05) between urban and rural settings. Furthermore, locations with more than 600 inhabitants evidenced a higher number of DRPs (p < 0.05). Additional analysis demonstrated that acceptance of PLI was significantly associated with region, urban/rural area, number of health centers, DRPs category, pharmacists’ recommendations, and category of medical intervention (p < 0.05). Further analysis indicated that a higher number of medications and living in areas with more than one health center (urban areas) were associated with an increased likelihood of intervention acceptance.ConclusionThis study confirms the positive impact of PLI on reducing drug-related problems. However, particular differences between rural and urban settings require adjustment of intervention strategies to optimize PC.Keywords: Medication review, Clinical pharmacy services, Drug-related problems, Potentially inappropriate prescriptions, Rural health

Background

Patient safety is one of the primary challenges faced by healthcare systems worldwide, and ensuring it is imperative at every stage of the healthcare process [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), patient safety involves reducing unnecessary risks of harm associated with healthcare. Among the factors that compromise safety, medical incidents are a particular concern due to their significant impact, which can range from medical errors, adverse events, and hospitalizations to, in extreme cases, death [2].

Medication errors are estimated to be related to 4.7% to 5% of hospital admissions [3–5]. Up to 50% of these errors could be prevented through safe medication practices, highlighting the crucial role of pharmacists in preventing such incidents through Pharmaceutical Care (PC) [4]. Furthermore, the WHO estimates that the global cost of these errors is approximately 42 billion dollars annually, representing around 1% of worldwide health expenditures [5].

Common medication errors are one of the leading causes of adverse events in healthcare. They include Drug-Related Problems (DRPs), such as errors in prescribing, administration, and dispensing, as well as drug-drug interactions, inadequate dosages, or non-adherence to therapeutic regimens [6]. Factors such as polypharmacy, particularly among elderly patients, lack of knowledge about the proper use of medications, and self-medication, further increase the risk of these adverse events [2, 7].

The early detection of potential or unresolved DRPs can result in avoiding unnecessary health center visits, hospital admissions, and long-term care [8, 9]. Although most of them are classified as preventable, if they are not identified and prevented in time, they can be fatal. In the hospital setting, from 1.3% to 41.3% of admissions are related to DRPs [10–13]. In the United States, DRPs are one of the main causes of death after hospital admission (3–6%), while in neighboring countries such as Portugal, approximately 43,000 patients per year are hospitalized because of DRPs [6].

The prevalence and management of drug-related problems can also vary significantly between urban and rural settings [14]. In urban areas, the greater availability of technological resources and the presence of a greater number of specialized professionals facilitate the implementation of safety protocols and the monitoring of medication therapy [15]. In contrast, in rural settings, limited infrastructure, less access to technologies, and a shortage of highly specialized personnel can hinder both early detection and prevention of DRPs [16]. Whereas in US the 17.8% of community pharmacies are located in small rural municipalities, in Spain they represent approximately 28% of the total [17, 18].

Pharmacist-led Intervention (PLI) through PC has demonstrated a positive impact on the prevention and resolution of DRPs in both settings, as well as in the optimization of the patient’s pharmacotherapy through the detection and resolution of Potentially Inappropriate Prescriptions (PIPs) [19–21]. In this line, the implementation of new technologies that help to quickly and easily identify DRPs and PIPs, as well as the standardized protocol for PLI, which guided pharmacists in identifying DRPs and proposing recommendations, appears to be an effective tool in both community pharmacies and Hospitals settings [19, 22].

Although PLI has demonstrated its potential to reduce DRPs, evidence comparing the effectiveness of these interventions across different community pharmacy settings remains limited. In particular, little is known about whether contextual factors such as rural or urban environments influence the implementation and acceptance of pharmacists’ recommendations in daily practice.

Therefore, we hypothesize that differences in the prevalence and resolution of DRPs between rural and urban pharmacies may arise due to variations in healthcare infrastructure, pharmacist workload, and access to technology and specialists.

The aim of this study was twofold: (a) to evaluate the effectiveness of Pharmacist-led Interventions in reducing Drug-Related Problems, analyzing the differences in clinical variables between rural and urban settings; and (b) to assess the influence of sociodemographic variables of each setting on the acceptance or rejection of the PLI.

Methods

A prospective, multicenter, non-randomized experimental study (quasi-experimental) with a pre-post Pharmacist-led Intervention design was conducted by the name InPRESC. Community pharmacies were selected using a stratified convenience sampling technique. The study design was based on the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines, a list of 22 essential points to improve the communication and presentation of observational studies.

Study design and variables

InPRESC study was structured in two different phases, each corresponding to a specific objective of the research. This phased approach was integrated into the overall pre-post design, allowing for the sequential development and evaluation of the PLI:

Pre-post Pharmacist-led Intervention phase. A quasi-experimental pre-post study was designed to evaluate the general impact of PLI on the reduction of DRPs by community pharmacists. The impact of the interventions was evaluated by comparing the number of DRPs identified during the baseline medication review with those identified after the PLI. Pharmacists documented all identified DRPs and the corresponding recommendations during the review process. The change in the number of DRPs between the pre-intervention and post-intervention periods was used as the primary outcome measure.Additionally, differences in the number of DRPs between urban and rural settings were analyzed, as well as the influence of clinical variables in both areas, including sex, DRPs type (drug interaction, duplicity, contraindication, precaution of use, and STOPP “Screening Tool of Older Person’s Prescriptions” / START “Screening Tool to Alert doctors to Right Treatment” criteria, health departments, and population size. All DRPs were identified using standardized criteria according to the Pharmaceutical Care Network Europe (PCNE) classification V9.1, a European association that promotes research and development in Pharmaceutical Care in Europe, seeking to improve the quality of life of patients by optimizing the use of medicines.

Pre-post Pharmacist-led Intervention phase. A quasi-experimental pre-post study was designed to evaluate the general impact of PLI on the reduction of DRPs by community pharmacists. The impact of the interventions was evaluated by comparing the number of DRPs identified during the baseline medication review with those identified after the PLI. Pharmacists documented all identified DRPs and the corresponding recommendations during the review process. The change in the number of DRPs between the pre-intervention and post-intervention periods was used as the primary outcome measure.

Additionally, differences in the number of DRPs between urban and rural settings were analyzed, as well as the influence of clinical variables in both areas, including sex, DRPs type (drug interaction, duplicity, contraindication, precaution of use, and STOPP “Screening Tool of Older Person’s Prescriptions” / START “Screening Tool to Alert doctors to Right Treatment” criteria, health departments, and population size. All DRPs were identified using standardized criteria according to the Pharmaceutical Care Network Europe (PCNE) classification V9.1, a European association that promotes research and development in Pharmaceutical Care in Europe, seeking to improve the quality of life of patients by optimizing the use of medicines.

Influence of sociodemographic factors in the intervention acceptance phase. In this phase, the impact of sociodemographic and clinical factors was estimated on the acceptance or rejection of the PLI recommended by the community pharmacist, following the same study design. The factors analyzed included sex, age, setting (urban/rural), health departments, population size, total number of health conditions, and total number of initially prescribed medications.

Influence of sociodemographic factors in the intervention acceptance phase. In this phase, the impact of sociodemographic and clinical factors was estimated on the acceptance or rejection of the PLI recommended by the community pharmacist, following the same study design. The factors analyzed included sex, age, setting (urban/rural), health departments, population size, total number of health conditions, and total number of initially prescribed medications.

Study settings and participants

InPRESC study was developed in 45 community pharmacies from Valencian province (Spain), representing all health departments of this province. Twenty-four of these are located in rural areas (under 5,000 inhabitants) and twenty-one in urban areas. Each participating pharmacy had, at least, one pharmacist responsible for delivering the intervention. Pharmacists recruited eligible patients attending their pharmacies who met the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate in the study, after providing written informed consent.

The term “Rural Municipality” was obtained from Law 45/2007, of December 13, 2007, for the sustainable development of the rural environment [23]. For descriptive and analytical purposes, an additional stratification by population size was applied, categorizing pharmacies into three groups: 0–600 inhabitants (compromised economic viability pharmacies), 601–5,000 inhabitants (rural), and > 5,000 inhabitants (urban). The total duration of the project was from August 2023 to February 2025.

Candidates were selected from participating pharmacies based on the following criteria: 55 years or older and taking five or more chronic treatments. Additionally, patients with more than three chronic conditions or those with renal and/or hepatic failure were given special priority for PC service.

The exclusion criteria were patients receiving chronic treatment with hospital-dispensed medications and those with incomplete clinical histories or who did not adhere to the established procedures. Furthermore, to minimize potential bias in analyzing the outcomes of the PLI, patients who did not receive a response from their physician were excluded.

All participating pharmacists, regardless of setting, were trained with the same protocol and used the same digital tool (Revisem®) for DRPs or PIP identification and intervention documentation, ensuring procedural uniformity. Patients constituted the unit of analysis. Each participant received an individual medication review and Pharmacist-led Intervention, and all analyses were performed at the patient level.

The sample size was calculated a priori using G*Power version 3.1.9.7 software to detect differences between two independent means in two separate groups using a two-tailed Student’s t-test statistics. Losses during the study were estimated at 5%. To ensure a representative sample, a conventional effect mean size (d) of 0.5 was used, with a 1:2 sampling ratio to increase statistical power in this underrepresented rural population, a type I error (α) of 5%, and a statistical power (β) of 0.8.

Applying the above parameters, a total sample size of 185 patients was estimated: comprising 106 urban and 79 rural patients. Given the multicenter nature of the study, a larger number of patients were ultimately recruited (n = 365). After applying inclusion/exclusion criteria and excluding cases without physician response, the final analytical sample consisted of 315 patients.

Pharmacist-led Intervention through pharmaceutical care

The 45 participating pharmacies were previously trained and qualified to provide pharmaceutical interventions, specifically in medication review service by Revisem® digital software, a program developed by Muy Ilustre Colegio Oficial de Farmacéuticos de Valencia to facilitate Pharmaceutical Care Services and patient management. Training included standardized instruction on patient data collection, identification, and classification of drug-related problems, formulation of pharmacist recommendations, and documentation of outcomes.

Pharmacists performed comprehensive medication reviews for patients, identifying DRPs and PIPs (STOPP/START). Since these criteria are designed for older adults, the screening using STOPP/START was applied only to patients aged 65 years or older.

This process involved the systematic collection of patient demographic information, clinical conditions, and complete medication lists. Interventions were implemented to address the identified issues, and outcomes were analyzed to assess the effectiveness of PLI in improving medication safety in rural settings. DRPs were identified using predefined criteria integrated into the software and classified according to the PCNE V9.1 classification.

After reviewing the medication of patients included in the study using Revisem®, pharmacists could recommend a PLI for clinically relevant DRPs that were identified. It is noteworthy to point out that Revisem® automatically generates a report containing the information gathered by the pharmacist, detailing the type of DRP detected, the associated health condition and medication, and the recommended pharmacist’s intervention to resolve the DRP. Thereafter, the patient communicated this report to their primary care physician or specialist, and medical intervention was subsequently documented by the pharmacist in Revisem®, along with the final resolution of the identified DRP and/or changes made.

Data collection

Patient data, recorded by pharmacists participating in the study, were systematically and consistently collected using Revisem®. The primary variables analyzed were: sex, age, setting, health department and population size of the municipality, total number of initial and final medications prescribed, number of health conditions, total number of initial and final DRPs identified, DRPs type based on PCNE V9.1 criteria [6], number of completed PLI, number of medications involved in DRP detected, and number of modifications or variations made to medical prescriptions following incident reports.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics V25 computer package (INC. Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to obtain the mean as a measure of central tendency and the standard deviation (SD) as a measure of dispersion of quantitative variables. Before inferential analysis, the existence of extreme values was verified (outliers), as well as the specific assumptions of each statistical technique used, such as normal distribution, homogeneity of variances, and independence.

For the inferential analysis of the first objective, t-test and ANOVA tests were used. Comparisons between groups, such as differences in the number of DRPs according to setting (rural or urban/CEV) or sex, were performed using t-tests. Although the number of DRPs constitutes a count variable, exploratory data analysis showed an approximately symmetric distribution in the study sample. Given the relatively large sample size and the exploratory nature of the study, parametric tests were used to compare mean values across groups, consistent with commonly reported approaches in previous pharmaceutical care studies. To analyze differences between multiple groups, such as DRP type, health departments or number of inhabitants, ANOVA was used.

The second study objective was analyzed using multinomial logistic regression to assess the influence of sociodemographic variables on the acceptance or rejection of PLI. A Poisson regression model was also used to explore factors associated with the acceptance of pharmacists’ recommendations. For the analysis of qualitative variables, the Chi-square test was used. Type I error was set at 5% (p < 0.05) and power at 0.8.

Results

The total project sample consisted of 365 patients, 61.9% female. After applying the inclusion criteria, 315 patients (86.3%) were analyzed, of whom 217 (68.9%) were rural patients and 98 (31.1%) were urban patients.

The average age of the depurated sample was 76.2 (12.6) years. On average, patients were taking 9.9 (4.0) medications, and the mean number of drug-related problems per patient was 10.0 (7.5).

Based on these results, the variation in the number of DRPs before and after the PLI was explored across different population size categories. The analysis shows a significant interaction (p < 0.05) between time (pre vs. post PLI) and municipality size, suggesting that the magnitude of the intervention effect (and the number of final DRPs) varies depending on the population size (Fig. 1a).

Additionally, differences in the initial number of DRPs detected between rural and urban areas were analyzed (Fig. 1b-c). Although the mean values differed [10.1 (7.7) in rural areas and 9.6 (6.8) in urban areas] these differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Similarly, no significant differences were found in the final number of DRPs detected. The same results were obtained when compared to the influence of PLI in the reduction of drug-related problems in both settings (p > 0.05).

The total number of detected DRPs was also descriptively analyzed according to population size by classifying pharmacies into three groups: (1) Compromised Economic Viability (CEV) pharmacies, serving populations of 0 to 600 inhabitants; (2) rural pharmacies, serving 601 to 5,000 inhabitants; and (3) urban pharmacies, serving more than 5,000 inhabitants (Table 1b).

The results showed that pharmacies located in municipalities with 601–5,000 inhabitants showed fewer DRPs than both CEV and urban pharmacies (p < 0.05). In both cases, the findings indicate a significantly lower prevalence of DRPs in rural pharmacies. Additionally, when results were stratified by sex, no statistically significant differences were found in DRP prevalence between male and female patients in either urban or rural settings. However, a slight trend was observed, with female patients in urban areas showing a slightly higher mean number of DRPs compared to male patients (Table 1b).

Finally, DRPs types classified by setting were evaluated. The results showed significant differences in the number of precautions of use [t (363) ≈ 3.24, p < 0.05, r = 0.17, CI95% 0.36/1.48)], contraindications [t (363) ≈ -3.94, p < 0.05, r = 0.20, CI95% (-0,34/-0.11)], and START criteria [t (363) ≈ 3.15, p < 0.05, r = 0.16, CI95% (0.11/0.47)], with a higher prevalence of contraindications in rural settings. However, no significant differences were found in drug interactions [t (363) ≈ -1.35, p > 0.05, CI95% (-0.02/0.21)], duplicities [t (363) ≈ 1.56, p > 0.05, CI95% (-1,72/0.32)], or STOPP criteria [t (363) ≈ 0.99, p > 0.05, CI95% (-0,22/0.66)] between rural and urban areas. Factors influencing the intervention acceptance phase

In addition to the previously described analyses, the acceptance of PLI has also been evaluated based on various sociodemographic and intervention-related factors.

Regarding sex, no significant association was observed between patient sex and PLI acceptance [Χ² (3) = 3.80, p > 0.05]. In contrast, age was significantly related to acceptance [Χ² (6) = 12.57, p < 0.05]. Similarly, municipality proved to be a significant factor, with a strong association between the specific municipality and PLI acceptance [Χ² (69) = 153.63, p < 0.05]. Moreover, when comparing urban and rural zones, a significant difference was also found [Χ² (3) = 8.52, p < 0.05]. Specifically, PLI acceptance was higher among patients aged 65 and above, in rural areas compared to urban ones.

Analysis of the number of inhabitants reveals a significant association with PLI acceptance [Χ² (3) = 7.97, p < 0.05]. Additionally, the number of health centers showed a significant relationship with PLI acceptance [Χ² (6) = 19.53, p < 0.05]. Further, the type of DRP was significantly associated with acceptance [Χ² (18) = 40.36, p < 0.05] but not the type of pharmacists’ recommendations [Χ² (21) = 26.32, p > 0.05]. Specifically, higher PLI acceptance was observed in municipalities with small populations.

Communication with patients and physicians was also evaluated. While the communication way used by the pharmacist to interact with the patient did not significantly affect PLI acceptance [Χ² (6) = 9.42, p > 0.05], the communication method used to convey the intervention to the physician did show a significant association [Χ² (15) = 53.03, p < 0.05]. Specifically, PLI acceptance was higher when the intervention was communicated to the physician through a written message conveyed by the patient or direct phone calls.

When all variables were analyzed collectively using a multinomial logistic regression (including the number of medications and the inhabitant range/setting) the model was significant. Interventions were more likely to be accepted when the municipality had more than one health center, as having only one center was strongly associated with lower acceptance. Additionally, female patients tended to show a higher likelihood of accepting the intervention compared to male patients [OR = 2.32, p = 0.055]. Moreover, a greater number of initial medications was associated with a higher probability of acceptance, as fewer initial medications increased the chances of unknown outcomes [OR = 0.79, p = 0.010].

Moreover, to identify factors influencing the total initial count of potential drug-related problems, a Poisson regression was performed incorporating predictors such as sex, area, health department, inhabitant range, age, health problems, and initial medication. The analysis revealed that the health department, inhabitant range, health problems, and initial medication were significant predictors of the initial DRPs count, whereas sex, area, and age did not exhibit a significant effect.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of Pharmacist-led Interventions (PLI) in reducing DRPs within community pharmacies in Valencian province, Spain, while comparing outcomes between urban and rural settings and exploring the influence of certain factors such as sociodemographic factors on the acceptance of these interventions. The findings of this study contribute to the growing research that supports the value of PLI in enhancing patient safety and optimizing medication management, particularly in the context of community pharmacies.

The study population was intentionally restricted to older adults with polypharmacy, as this group represents the primary target population for pharmaceutical care and medication review services. While this enhances internal validity and clinical relevance, it limits the generalizability of the findings to younger or less complex patient populations. Therefore, the results should be interpreted within the context of patients at higher risk of drug-related problems.

Our results indicate that overall, PLI significantly reduced the average number of DRPs per patient from 9.1 to 8.2. Although the absolute reduction in the mean number of DRPs per patient may appear modest, the decrease was mainly driven by categories with clear clinical relevance. In particular, pharmacist-led interventions resulted in the identification and resolution of contraindications, precautions for use, and START-related problems, which are directly associated with patient safety and the risk of adverse drug events. Additionally, this paper summarizes the main variables and factors that must be considered in each area (urban/rural) and the influence of them in the acceptance of PLI recommended, emphasizing in number of medications and residing in municipalities with fewer than six hundred inhabitants.

Comparison with existing literature

In agreement with previously national and international published articles, our study showed that PLI reduces significantly DRPs detected by community pharmacies [19, 24]. However, these prior studies failed to compare the PLI between rural and urban areas, and this is of relevance given that specific differences in Pharmaceutical Care must be contemplated between rural and urban patients [25, 26]. Nevertheless, most of the existing evidence is either reviews or based on pharmacists’ perceptions and not on concrete patient-level data in specialized PC. Therefore, our prospective study fills a critical gap by providing quantitative comparisons of medication review interventions and patient outcomes in rural and urban settings.

Finally, municipality, area, number of health centers, type of DRP, pharmacists’ recommendations, and type of medical intervention are related to PLI acceptance [27]. However, the study of Rianne J Zaal et al.27, was conducted in a single urban center without assessing rural–urban differences, limiting its generalizability to non-urban settings.

Despite the overall reduction in DRPs, the comparative analysis between urban and rural areas did not show significant differences in the total number of them. This finding may reflect the similarity in pharmaceutical care practices across both settings. Similar results have been reported in previous studies such as Helen D. Howarth et al.26, which found no major differences in pharmacy services between rural and urban environments, although it highlighted the need for further research.

However, our data revealed a significant interaction between the timing of the intervention (pre vs. post) and the size of the municipality. This means that the effect of the intervention varied depending on how large the municipality was. In other words, the PLI had a greater or lesser impact depending on the population size. One possible explanation is that access to health services and response times differ across municipalities in Spain, which may influence how quickly and effectively the intervention can be implemented [28].

Further supporting the relevance of taking into consideration rural/urban patients, the group analysis presented here showed that specific categories of DRPs, specifically precautions for use, contraindications, and START criteria, occurred more frequently in rural settings. These results may reflect underlying differences in healthcare infrastructure, resource availability, and patient management practices between urban and rural communities [29, 30].

Additionally, our results revealed a significant difference in the total number of detected DRPs between the diverse types of pharmacies classified according to the population size. These findings suggest that the setting in which pharmacies conduct PC significantly influences the prevalence of DRPs, which raises important implications for the safety and quality of medication dispensing [25]. One of the main factors that could explain the higher prevalence of DRPs detected in rural pharmacies is the lower availability of personal and technological resources compared to urban areas [31].

The analysis of sociodemographic variables provided additional insights into the acceptance of PLI. Factors such as sex and age were not significantly associated with intervention acceptance. However, variables including municipality, urban/rural classification, the number of health centers, DRP category, and the type of pharmacists’ recommendations did exhibit significant associations [27, 32, 33]. This finding is relevant, indicating that acceptance of PLI may depend more on contextual than individual factors such as physicians’ relationships or PLI relevance.

This study found that the method pharmacists use to communicate with patients does not impact acceptance, while the way they convey recommendations to physicians significantly influences acceptance rates. This finding suggests that physician engagement with pharmacist recommendations is influenced by how the information is delivered [34]. Optimizing communication strategies with physicians could therefore play a key role in improving the acceptance and effectiveness of pharmaceutical interventions [35]. Future research should explore which specific communication approaches are most effective in enhancing collaboration and ensuring better patient outcomes.

Notably, our results revealed that a higher number of medications increased the likelihood of non-acceptance of the intervention. Nevertheless, having more than one health center (urban area) increases the probability of PLI acceptance. These findings suggest that polypharmacy and geographic isolation may act as barriers to the acceptance of pharmacist interventions, potentially due to limited access to healthcare information, lower health literacy, or reduced trust in unfamiliar health services. This underscores the need for tailored strategies to engage patients in sparsely populated areas and those with complex medication regimens [27, 36].

Importantly, the findings presented herein reinforce the need to implement risk prevention strategies specific to each type of pharmacy. In the case of rural pharmacies, it is essential to improve access to ongoing training, technology development and supervisory systems to reduce the incidence of medication errors. Similarly, it could be beneficial to establish regulatory guidelines adapted to the reality of these pharmacies, ensuring that they have the necessary support to operate in a safe and efficient manner.

Strengths

Our findings are in line with previous research that has emphasized the effectiveness of Pharmaceutical Care in reducing medication errors [19, 24, 26]. The observed differences between urban and rural settings further highlight the need for tailored intervention strategies that consider the unique challenges faced by rural healthcare systems, such as limited access to technology and specialized personnel. By addressing these disparities, future initiatives could further optimize PLI and enhance overall patient safety.

Limitations

The quasi-experimental design and non-randomized sampling may introduce potential biases, and the small sample size (especially in urban settings) could limit the generalizability of our findings. Given the exploratory nature of the study, multiple comparisons were performed to examine differences across sociodemographic factors, settings, and DRP categories. No formal adjustment for multiple testing was applied. Therefore, these additional exploratory analyses should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Additionally, the classification of rural pharmacies was based solely on national legal criteria, without considering other potentially relevant classification methods, which may affect the interpretation and applicability of the results. Future research should aim to include larger, randomized samples and extend the follow-up period to validate and expand upon these results.

Finally, although patients were recruited from multiple pharmacies, potential intra-pharmacy correlation cannot be completely ruled out and should be considered in future studies using hierarchical analytical approaches.

Conclusion

The results of the InPRESC project indicate that, while PLI effectively reduces DRPs overall, its impact is influenced by local sociodemographic factors and differences between urban and rural settings. These findings emphasize the importance of designing context-specific strategies to maximize the benefits of Pharmaceutical Care, contributing to improved patient outcomes and enhanced safety in the medication use of patients.
