Optimizing patient flow logistics: strategic challenges, tactical solutions, and future directions
Authors: Hamed Zamani, Fereshteh Parvaresh, Mehdi Nasr Isfahani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study explores patient flow logistics (PFL) from strategic and tactical viewpoints, introducing a decision-making framework to systematically categorize key challenges. Through a systematic literature review, the research analyzes articles published between 2010 and 2023 in 17 top-tier journals within Operations Research (OR) and Management Science (MS), using SCOPUS and healthcare-specific keywords (e.g., “Capacity Planning,” “Resource Allocation,” “Pandemic,” “Hospital,” “Patient”) to retrieve relevant studies. After applying publication year, journal quality, and optimization technique filters—including mathematical programming and stochastic optimization—66 articles were selected for analysis. Findings reveal an increasing reliance on stochastic models, greater adoption of metaheuristic over heuristic algorithms, and stronger integration of real-world data in decision-making frameworks. Nevertheless, gaps persist, particularly in maximization-based methods addressing service rates and equity in epidemic resource allocation. The proposed classification framework offers actionable insights for academics, policymakers, and healthcare professionals by distinguishing pandemic and non-pandemic logistics. Future research should explore cross-sector hospital cooperation, integrated patient management strategies, and the incorporation of disease spread models in predictive resource planning.

Background

Efficient Patient Flow Logistics (PFL) is essential for modern healthcare delivery, ensuring timely and coordinated patient transitions while maximizing use of limited medical resources. Studies have shown that structured patient flow interventions can reduce average hospital length of stay from 11.5 to 4.4 days and decrease emergency department boarding time by 90%, leading to significant improvements in bed turnover and financial savings [1].

The importance of PFL in healthcare

PFL frameworks have been linked to better clinical outcomes by reducing overcrowding, improving communication across departments, and facilitating ongoing care coordination. Health Information Systems (HIS), including real-time dashboards and push-notification systems, have proven effective in enhancing access to decision-relevant data, decreasing patient wait times, and reducing staff workload [2].

Moreover, dashboards allow hospital administrators to monitor key performance indicators, identify delays, and respond rapidly to systemic bottlenecks. A systematic review highlights that dashboards significantly improve patient safety and operational efficiency when they offer real-time alerts, patient tracking, and staff reminders [3].

In high-pressure environments such as emergency departments, umbrella reviews of patient flow strategies across population inflow, care throughput, and discharge output phases have confirmed that these interventions can mitigate ED crowding and improve overall responsiveness of healthcare systems [4].

One of the most critical benefits of PFL is its role in optimizing resource utilization. Hospitals operate under constrained resources, and managing the availability of beds, specialized equipment, and skilled professionals is essential to maintaining operational efficiency. PFL strategies ensure these resources are allocated effectively, reducing unnecessary waste and operational costs while enhancing patient care quality [5]. Additionally, minimizing patient wait times is a priority for improving healthcare experiences. Delays in receiving treatment can lead to patient dissatisfaction and worsened health outcomes. A well-implemented PFL system improves access to medical services, particularly specialist consultations, ensuring patients receive timely interventions that contribute to their recovery and overall well-being [6].

Beyond daily operations, PFL is especially critical during crises such as pandemics or natural disasters. During periods of heightened demand, hospitals must quickly adapt their capacity to accommodate surging patient numbers. Without effective PFL strategies, healthcare systems risk overcrowding, increased wait times, and diminished service quality. A robust and flexible approach ensures uninterrupted care by efficiently reallocating personnel and resources where they are needed most.

Real-world examples demonstrating PFL relevance

Several real-world scenarios have illustrated the critical importance of efficient PFL systems. For instance, during the COVID-19 pandemic, hospitals in countries such as Italy and the United States experienced severe patient surges. Institutions that had pre-established PFL frameworks—such as centralized bed tracking systems and dynamic staffing models—demonstrated greater resilience and lower mortality rates. One prominent example is the Cleveland Clinic, which utilized predictive analytics and patient flow dashboards to dynamically reallocate staff and ICU beds during COVID-19 surges, thereby maintaining continuity of care [7]. Another case is Singapore General Hospital, which implemented a real-time location system (RTLS) to monitor patient movement, equipment usage, and staff deployment. This system allowed for enhanced coordination, reduced patient wait times, and a 12% improvement in discharge efficiency. These examples validate the theoretical foundations of PFL and illustrate its real-world applicability in improving patient outcomes, especially under resource-constrained conditions [8].

Contribution of this study

This systematic review contributes to the academic literature on PFL by addressing specific gaps that remain unaddressed in existing reviews. While several recent studies have examined operations management in healthcare—particularly in domains such as cancer care and surgical workflow optimization—many of these reviews are either bibliometric in nature or focus on very specific application areas without offering a structured decision-level classification of PFL problems. For example, Hadid et al. [9] conducted a bibliometric analysis of cancer care operations management, identifying major research trends but without a detailed taxonomy of decision-making levels in patient logistics. Similarly, Fhoula et al. [10] reviewed home cancer care literature using keyword clustering and visualization techniques, yet their study did not explore broader hospital logistics frameworks or planning strategies across different care settings. Another comprehensive review by Hadid et al. [11] focused on optimization approaches in outpatient chemotherapy but remained limited to a specific service domain rather than the full spectrum of patient flow across healthcare systems. Methods

This section defines the scope and methodology used in the study, outlining the selection process for relevant research papers and the analytical approaches applied to evaluating PFL.

Scope

This research examines strategic and tactical PFL challenges, focusing on medical resource allocation and capacity planning in both pandemic and non-pandemic contexts. Given the complexity of healthcare decision-making, the study reviews work that incorporate OR techniques, including optimization models such as mathematical programming, simulation-based approaches like discrete event simulations, and queuing theory applications such as stochastic optimization. By emphasizing these methodological frameworks, the study aims to identify patterns in healthcare logistics decision-making.

To ensure consistency and methodological rigor, empirical studies and purely computational analyses that do not incorporate explicit OR-based decision models are excluded. This approach allows for a more precise assessment of how OR techniques contribute to optimizing patient flow and hospital resource management.

Research methodology

The study leverages SCOPUS, a premier database of peer-reviewed literature, ensuring high-quality journal selection. Papers published between January 2010 and February 2023 were screened, while conference proceedings, book chapters, and technical reports were excluded to preserve academic integrity. A structured keyword search was conducted across 17 top-tier OR/MS journals, using specific terms related to capacity planning, resource allocation, and pandemic-related healthcare logistics.

In this study, we have focused exclusively on articles centered on optimization models. This encompasses a wide range of models, categorized as follows:

Studies that did not employ OR methodologies and tools were systematically excluded from our analysis. For instance, simulation studies lacking a mathematical programming framework or an optimization algorithm were not considered. Our focus is on quantitative solutions and mathematical modeling for decision-making problems in the relevant domain.

The selection of journals was also based on the same OR/MS criteria. We limited our scope to core OR/MS journals, ensuring that the selected articles align methodologically with the primary objective of our study—analyzing optimization models. While some interdisciplinary journals may also publish relevant papers, we relied solely on core OR/MS journals to maintain focus and quality.

In the first step of the research, we conducted a keyword search in the Scopus database using the following Boolean combination applied to the title, abstract, and keyword fields: (“Capacity planning” OR “Resource Allocation” OR “Location-Allocation” OR “Pricing”) AND (“Healthcare” OR “Hospital” OR “Patient” OR “Inpatient” OR “Epidemic” OR “Pandemic”).

In the second step, we restricted the publication period to articles published between 2010 and 2023 to focus on recent developments. In the third step, we applied a filter to include only articles published in selected top-tier OR/MS journals, ensuring methodological relevance to Operations Research. In the final step, we screened the full texts of the remaining articles and excluded those that were irrelevant to the field of PFL, focused solely on operational-level issues such as patient scheduling, or did not employ OR-based methodologies.

Figure 1 illustrates the number of articles retained after each filtering step. Ultimately, 66 relevant articles were selected for in-depth analysis, ensuring comprehensive coverage of both routine and pandemic-related healthcare logistics from a strategic and tactical decision-making perspective.

At the macro level, strategic and tactical PFL decisions are examined to understand how hospitals and healthcare networks manage long-term planning and mid-term resource allocation. Healthcare-related terms

To ensure consistency in terminology, several key healthcare concepts are central to PFL and hospital operations. Emergency Departments (EDs) function as 24/7 urgent care facilities, handling acute medical conditions that require immediate attention [15]. Intensive Care Units (ICUs) provide specialized critical care for patients in life-threatening conditions, requiring dedicated monitoring and advanced medical support [16]. Patients receiving treatment are categorized as either outpatient (OPD) or inpatient (IPD), with OPD cases requiring no hospitalization, while IPD involves extended hospital stays [17].

Healthcare facilities also manage elective and emergency surgeries, where elective procedures are scheduled in advance, while emergency surgeries address urgent medical needs [18]. Some patients transition through Step-Down Units (SDUs), which serve as intermediate care areas between general wards and ICUs, offering recovery support before full discharge [19]. Operating Theatres (OTs) are sterile environments designed for surgical procedures, equipped with specialized tools and medical monitoring devices [20]. Hospitals must also account for surge capacity, enabling rapid scalability of resources during mass casualty events or health crises [21, 22]. Additionally, no-show patients, individuals who miss scheduled appointments without prior notice, pose a challenge to healthcare efficiency by disrupting planned resource allocation [23].

Patient flow in healthcare systems

PFL is studied across two primary levels of hospital operations. Hospital networks optimize inter-hospital patient transfers, ensuring specialized care is accessible and reducing congestion in overburdened facilities [24]. Within individual hospitals, patient movement is carefully structured based on their treatment needs. ED patients are classified into three distinct pathways: those ready for immediate discharge, those requiring ICU or general ward (GW) admission, and those scheduled for surgery. Following surgical intervention, patients are directed to either ICU, GW, or SDU, depending on their recovery requirements [25].

Planning levels & PFL categorization

Healthcare logistics decisions in PFL are structured across three distinct planning horizons (Fig. 5). Strategic decisions focus on long-term hospital infrastructure and resource distribution, ensuring sustainable healthcare capacity. Single-facility capacity planning determines how resources are allocated within individual hospitals, while network capacity planning coordinates multi-hospital systems to optimize patient care. Location-allocation models identify ideal facility placements, ensuring accessibility for diverse populations.

Tactical decisions address mid-term healthcare operations, focusing on capacity planning and resource allocation. This includes planning elective admissions, ensuring hospital occupancy levels remain balanced [26], and allocating medical assets efficiently to prevent shortages. Pricing strategies influence healthcare affordability and patient accessibility.

Operational decisions handle daily hospital workflows, such as managing patient entries and discharges to optimize bed turnover and scheduling consultations to minimize service delays. Research on admission, discharge, and appointment scheduling is excluded from this study since prior works have extensively covered these topics [27, 28].

Strategic decisions

Single-facility capacity planning

Strategic capacity planning within hospitals focuses on optimizing bed availability, staffing levels, and department layouts to enhance efficiency. Managing these aspects effectively ensures hospitals can accommodate patient demand while maintaining quality care.

Breuer et al. [29] developed a Mix Integer Linear Programming (MILP) model to optimize nurse staffing and bed allocation, minimizing both costs and unadmitted patients. Their approach accounted for uncertainty, leading to an 85% reduction in unadmitted patients in the budgeted model and 100% in the data-driven approach. Kanani & Takagi [30] explored neonatal inpatient capacity using patient-day and bed utilization rate metrics, employing a queuing model at the University of Tsukuba Hospital in Japan for long-term hospital planning. Rau et al. [31] investigated outpatient physiotherapy bottlenecks in Taiwan using discrete event simulation, identifying Intermittent Pelvic Traction as a major source of delays. Implementing a therapist pooling strategy significantly reduced patient waiting times.

Nguyen et al. [32] optimized physician allocation in outpatient clinics through a stochastic model, minimizing capacity requirements and validating their approach with data from the urology department. Armony et al. [19] examined Step-Down Unit (SDU) capacity, showing that SDU expansion can reduce ICU demand, but optimal SDU size depends on patient volume and facility constraints (tested at a New York hospital). Carew et al. [33] improved elective surgery scheduling through a Markov Decision Process (MDP) model, reducing waiting times by 14.3%, tested at British Columbia Children’s Hospital.

Veneklaas et al. [34] introduced a decision support system to optimize admission lounge efficiency, employing the Erlang Loss model to refine bed allocation strategies. Their findings confirmed feasibility in both existing hospitals and new department designs.

Network capacity planning

Strategic capacity planning across healthcare networks ensures coordinated patient care by optimizing resources in hospitals, long-term care (LTC) facilities, and multi-service units.

Hospital Networks involve optimizing bed distribution and patient transfers to prevent regional disparities. Ben Abdelaziz & Masmoudi [35] used multi-objective stochastic optimization to evaluate bed shortages across 157 Tunisian hospitals, revealing a 75% deficit in western & southern regions. Nezamoddini & Khasawneh [24] designed an integer programming model to manage patient transfers, reducing waiting lists by 35% and hospital resource demands by 10%.

LTC Networks focus on alleviating emergency department congestion and improving patient transitions. Patrick [36] applied an MDP model, demonstrating that reducing patient LoS could render existing capacity sufficient. Zhang et al. [37] used a multi-server queuing system to optimize annual bed allocation, avoiding linear increases in capacity demands via simulation-based optimization. Zhang & Puterman [38] expanded LTC planning strategies, validating a comparison-based simulation approach with Vancouver Island Health Organization data. Cardoso-Grilo et al. [39] incorporated equity-driven LTC planning, using a multi-objective stochastic optimization model alongside Morphol-KMG scenario reduction, tested in Great Lisbon.

Multi-Service Networks manage capacity across multiple healthcare providers, integrating efficiency models. Bidhandi et al. [40] created a multi-stage network model, integrating queuing theory and integer programming to minimize costs, validated through a six-stage network simulation. Bravo et al. [41] developed a linear optimization model for multi-site healthcare networks, maximizing net income while refining resource distribution.

Pandemic Testing Facilities require dynamic capacity planning during outbreaks. Liu et al. [42] introduced a multi-period MILP model to identify testing facility locations and capacities, minimizing total operational costs using a Lagrangian relaxation algorithm. Their approach outperformed CPLEX solvers, particularly in large-scale pandemic scenarios.

Location-allocation

The location-allocation problem in healthcare focuses on optimizing facility placement and resource distribution to enhance accessibility and efficiency. Ensuring equitable healthcare availability requires strategic planning to determine the best locations for medical services, particularly in community-based settings and epidemic response scenarios.

In community-based healthcare, Khodaparasti et al. [43] developed a multi-objective MILP model to optimize community-based organizations (CBOs), counseling centers (CCs), and addiction treatment clinics (ATCs) within a two-tier system. Their fuzzy goal programming analysis revealed significant service gaps in Shiraz, Iran, indicating infrastructure inadequacies that hinder healthcare accessibility. During epidemic resource allocation, Anparasan & Lejeune [44] applied integer linear programming to optimize Cholera Treatment Centers & Units during Haiti’s 2010 cholera outbreak. Their methodology incorporated valid inequalities, improving solution efficiency and identifying nurse and physician availability as critical factors in patient care delivery.

Pandemic-driven challenges necessitate precise planning for testing facilities and plasma banks. Devi et al. [45] proposed an MILP model to optimize temporary testing lab (TTL) locations during influenza outbreaks, reducing travel times and logistical costs, validated with Maharashtra, India case study data. Similarly, Manupati et al. [46] developed a bi-objective MILP model optimizing plasma bank locations during COVID-19, effectively minimizing transportation time and total costs using heuristic methods.

For vaccination centers and field hospitals, strategic decisions play a crucial role in improving emergency healthcare resilience. Bertsimas et al. [47] employed an epidemiological model with quadratic optimization to strategically locate vaccination centers, reducing COVID-19 deaths and exposure rates by up to 20% in the United States. Ardakani et al. [48] designed an MILP model for COVID-19 healthcare resilience, categorizing hospitals into accessible, backup, and field hospitals. Their approach optimized costs, patient satisfaction, and nurse allocation, leading to a significant reduction in fatalities—from 37% to under 5%—following field hospital integration.

Observations & summary

Strategic healthcare research spans three key domains: hospital capacity planning, healthcare network optimization, location-allocation modeling. Recent trends highlight the continued dominance of exact methods, though heuristic and Approximate Dynamic Programming (ADP) models are increasingly being adopted for their flexibility and efficiency (Table 3). Multi-objective models have gained traction as researchers emphasize the need to balance efficiency, cost, and equity in healthcare systems. Minimization objectives significantly outweigh maximization approaches, indicating a predominant focus on cost reduction rather than profit optimization. However, an overlooked aspect of strategic planning is the issue of overutilization and underutilization, particularly regarding how employee overtime affects efficiency and service quality. Addressing these gaps through integrated decision-making models can lead to more effective resource allocation, improved patient outcomes, and enhanced hospital performance.

Tactical decisions

Tactical capacity planning

Tactical capacity planning is essential for ensuring efficient resource allocation and managing elective patient admissions while balancing healthcare demand with available resources [26].

In general hospitals, Hulshof et al. [26, 49] developed MILP and ADP models to optimize elective patient admissions and medical resource distribution, resulting in fairer resource allocation and improved hospital performance. Andersen et al. [50] applied Markov models to enhance bed allocation during periods of overcrowding, achieving an 11.7% reduction in patient relocations at a Danish hospital. Batista et al. [51] introduced a bi-objective stochastic model to refine capacity allocation across five patient groups, outperforming existing hospital practices in Chile.

For special-purpose wards, Malik et al. [52] utilized NSGA-II genetic algorithms to optimize surgical scheduling, leading to reduced waiting times and lower overtime costs. Dellaert and Jeunet [53] designed a MILP model aimed at minimizing resource underuse, validated with data from a Dutch heart center. Anjomshoa et al. [54] developed a multi-objective MILP model to decrease overdue surgeries, waitlists, and tardiness, demonstrating effectiveness in Melbourne hospitals.

In clinic and outpatient settings, Izady [23] employed bulk service queue models to optimize specialty clinic appointment capacity, minimizing delays for non-urgent patients. Aslani et al. [55] applied robust optimization methods for outpatient physician capacity planning, significantly enhancing cost savings and responsiveness to demand fluctuations, validated at Tan Tock Seng Hospital in Singapore.

For pandemic-specific challenges, Bozkir et al. [56] developed a two-stage stochastic optimization model for dialysis patient cohorting, effectively optimizing machine allocation in COVID-19 emergency response scenarios.

Resource allocation during an outbreak

In pandemics, healthcare systems must allocate medical resources—including beds, personnel, and equipment—strategically to prevent overwhelming hospitals and ensure continued patient care.

Regional resource sharing plays a crucial role in mitigating shortages and improving response efficiency. Arora et al. [57] optimized antiviral distribution for influenza outbreaks, demonstrating that smaller cities benefit most from bilateral aid, as tested in California and Arizona. Liu & Zhang [58] developed a four-phase logistics model integrating SEIR forecasting and MILP planning, refining epidemic control strategies through dynamic resource prediction adjustments.

Bed allocation remains a key challenge during outbreaks. Long et al. [59] applied a dynamic transmission model to optimize Ebola epidemic bed distribution, confirming that the Myopic policy is the most effective approach, validated with West Africa 2014 Ebola data. Yin & Büyüktahtakın [60] designed a multi-stage stochastic programming model to optimize Ebola treatment center (ETC) bed distribution, integrating equity constraints to ensure fair access to resources, tested in Guinea, Sierra Leone, and Liberia.

Post-outbreak strategies require strategic resource reallocation. Melman et al. [61] analyzed three pandemic resource allocation strategies and found that limiting elective surgery closures improves patient outcomes while reducing total casualties, as demonstrated in a UK hospital study. Ma et al. [62] developed an MDP model for COVID-19 bed allocation, incorporating patient subsidization to lower treatment costs for elective cases.

Vaccine and testing distribution also demand optimized allocation strategies. Fadaki et al. [63] introduced a multi-period MILP model to enhance vaccine distribution, minimizing travel distances and reducing wastage, validated with Victoria, Australia data. Jahani et al. [64] proposed a Non-Linear Programming (NLP) model for hospital vaccine inventory, ordering, and staffing, using NSGA-II heuristics for improved optimization. Thul & Powell [65] developed a reinforcement learning-based MDP model for vaccine and test kit distribution, demonstrating its effectiveness across 51 U.S. states and Nevada.

Resource allocation in non-pandemic conditions

In normal healthcare conditions, efficient bed allocation, staffing, and resource distribution are essential for ensuring equitable access and operational effectiveness. Research has explored various optimization strategies to address these challenges across different hospital scales and specialties.

Bekker et al. [66] evaluated four bed allocation policies, concluding that threshold-based approaches work best for small-scale hospitals, whereas high flexibility proves ineffective in larger systems. Zhou et al. [67] developed an equity-driven resource allocation model, optimizing hospital revenue and patient fairness using EMI metrics, validated in Shanghai public hospitals. Wang et al. [68] introduced a Mix Integer Non-Linear Programming (MINLP) model that refines bed distribution and patient acceptance rates, proving effective across hospitals of various scales.

Addressing patient overflow and blocking is crucial for maintaining smooth hospital operations. Izady & Mohmed [69] optimized overflow ward strategies, minimizing patient rejection rates and nursing costs using CDOS heuristics, validated in a teaching hospital. Gong et al. [70] presented a stochastic optimization model improving bed allocation and equitable access, based on a case study in Shanghai. Bretthauer et al. [71] investigated bed blocking dynamics, concluding that downstream unit adjustments significantly reduce bottlenecks. Wu et al. [72] developed a loss-queuing model to minimize blocking probability, validated at a Tianjin tertiary hospital. Zychlinski et al. [73] applied fluid modeling to optimize geriatric ward allocation, addressing under-utilization of beds.

Emergency departments require dynamic optimization to prevent overcrowding and enhance patient care efficiency. Feng et al. [74] applied multi-objective stochastic programming to optimize ED resource allocation, improving patient length-of-stay (LoS) in a Taiwan hospital study. Chan et al. [75] developed a queue-based optimization model for flexible nurse allocation, significantly enhancing patient flow, tested in a New York hospital.

In demand prediction and long-term resource planning, Ordu et al. [76] introduced a three-stage forecasting and optimization model, improving strategic hospital planning for patient demand and bed allocation.

Pricing & budget allocation

Pricing strategies and budget al.location are critical yet underexplored areas in healthcare research. Understanding cost adjustments, subsidies, and optimal resource distribution enhances financial sustainability and accessibility across health networks. In healthcare networks, Lee & Choi [77] introduced a quadratic programming model to optimize hospital budget al.locations. Their analysis compared two methods: a utility-based algorithm, which improved patient satisfaction, and a flow-based approach, which was more cost-effective, validated with Seoul hospital data.

For telemedicine pricing and subsidies, Wang et al. [78] examined pricing and capacity planning for for-profit telemedicine firms (TFs) and subsidized general hospitals (GHs). Their game model revealed that subsidies enhance patient utility and reduce hospital wait times, offering insights into government-supported virtual healthcare models. HIV resource distribution presents unique budget challenges. Lasry et al. [79] designed a nonlinear optimization model balancing HIV screening and risk-reduction funding, recommending a 100% budget al.location to at-risk populations over five years, based on U.S. data. Alistar et al. [80] expanded this approach, dividing funds between prevention and treatment using a SIT model, validated with Uganda and Russia epidemic data to optimize infection control strategies.

Observations & summary

Tactical healthcare studies generally fall into two broad categories: hospital tactical planning, which includes elective admissions, resource allocation, and overflow ward optimization, and medical resource allocation during outbreaks, covering aspects such as bed distribution, vaccine and testing allocation, and equitable access strategies.

Recent research trends indicate a growing reliance on stochastic optimization, reflecting the need to incorporate uncertainty into decision-making processes (Table 4). While exact methods remain dominant, metaheuristic algorithms are becoming increasingly popular, offering computational efficiency compared to traditional heuristic and ADP models. Additionally, real-world data is utilized in nearly all studies, enhancing practical applicability and ensuring that optimization models align with actual healthcare system constraints. However, there remains a gap in studies using maximization objectives, particularly regarding service rate optimization and equity considerations, which is especially relevant in epidemic resource allocation scenarios.

Multi-level decision-making

Certain studies span strategic, tactical, and operational healthcare decisions, integrating long-term planning with short-term adaptability.

In healthcare system optimization, Garg et al. [81] developed a Markov chain model that integrates resource planning and patient scheduling, applied to elderly care systems in London. Maenhout & Vanhoucke [82] introduced a branch-and-price algorithm to optimize nurse staffing and scheduling, significantly improving staff satisfaction and cost efficiency, validated with Belgian hospital data. Sun et al. [83] designed three optimization models for hospital patient allocation during influenza outbreaks, tested in Metro Louisville, Kentucky.

Integrated scheduling and resource allocation help refine hospital capacity management. Liu et al. [84] developed an MDP model optimizing elective and emergency surgery scheduling, considering capacity constraints in OPRs and ICU beds. Intrevado et al. [85] proposed a multi-period MILP model for LTC facility planning and patient assignment, revealing high costs associated with home care substitution, validated in Canada.

Blood supply chain management involves efficient donor scheduling, collection center placement, and hospital inventory optimization. Kenan & Diabat [86] examined a three-layer blood product supply chain, using two-stage stochastic modeling to determine collection center locations and donor scheduling. Hamdan & Diabat [87] developed a multi-objective RO model optimizing the blood supply chain amid disruptions, concluding that 15 of 30 proposed blood collection centers were sufficient, validated in Jordan. Kamyabniya et al. [88] designed a stochastic optimization model addressing victim triage and blood logistics following an earthquake in Tehran, emphasizing resource-sharing benefits.

Hospital expansion and pandemic response require dynamic strategies to optimize facility locations and patient care delivery. Eriskin et al. [89] introduced a multi-objective MILP model to optimize new hospital site selection, resource distribution, and patient assignments during the COVID-19 pandemic, validated in Wuhan, China.

Recent advancements in multi-level healthcare decision models indicate increasing integration of strategic, tactical, and operational factors. MILP models dominate research, though stochastic and robust optimization techniques are gaining momentum due to their ability to address uncertainty in healthcare environments. While exact methods remain prevalent, heuristics and metaheuristic algorithms are increasingly favored for improving computational efficiency. Additionally, resource-sharing strategies significantly enhance healthcare network efficiency, particularly in disaster response scenarios, highlighting the importance of collaborative facility planning and dynamic patient allocation systems. Table 5 illustrates the modeling, solution approach, data type, and objective functions for such papers.

Pandemic vs. pandemic-free situations

Research in PFL is categorized into two distinct groups: pandemic-free studies, which analyze hospital operations without epidemic considerations, and pandemic-related studies, which address resource distribution and healthcare strategies during outbreaks (Table 7).

In pandemic-free situations, intensive care units (ICUs) receive more attention than other hospital departments due to the complexity of critical care conditions and specialized equipment needs (Table 6). However, relatively few studies focus on hospital networks, as most research is confined to single-hospital scenarios, highlighting a gap in understanding broader healthcare coordination. Additionally, patient classification in these studies is predominantly based on health status, while future research could benefit from incorporating age, gender, and socioeconomic factors into healthcare planning.

This suggests untapped potential for broader adoption, particularly in operational decision-making. Unlike non-pandemic studies, hospital networks are commonly analyzed, reflecting the necessity of regional coordination in healthcare emergencies. Before 2021, most studies examined single-patient categories, whereas post-2021 research has expanded to include diverse patient types, acknowledging the complexity of simultaneous healthcare demands. Conclusion

This paper systematically reviewed PFL research, focusing on studies that employ OR techniques in healthcare decision-making. A three-tier decision framework—covering strategic, tactical, and operational approaches—was introduced to categorize 66 papers from leading journals, analyzing both pandemic and non-pandemic healthcare scenarios. Research was assessed based on modeling techniques, optimization approaches, data sources, objective functions, and key assumptions, providing a structured analysis of healthcare logistics advancements.

Based on observed gaps, emerging trends, and feasibility, several underexplored areas warrant further investigation.

Public-Private Hospital Cooperation could significantly enhance healthcare efficiency by optimizing patient distribution and resource utilization, particularly in crisis scenarios. Simultaneous pandemic and non-pandemic patient management demands strategic planning to ensure balanced bed allocation and personnel distribution without disrupting routine care. No-show patient behavior impacts healthcare efficiency, and addressing it through automated reminders and behavioral analysis could enhance resource utilization and reduce wait times.

Incorporating equity criteria into resource allocation models ensures fair healthcare access, minimizing disparities based on geographic and socioeconomic factors. LoS-dependent treatment costs should be analyzed to optimize hospitalization strategies, reducing unnecessary expenses while improving patient flow efficiency. Despite their importance in outbreak response, disease spread models remain underutilized, and integrating them into PFL research could enhance epidemic forecasting and optimize healthcare resource allocation.

Gaps and future research directions

Despite the increasing sophistication of optimization techniques in healthcare planning, several critical gaps persist that limit the practical and theoretical advancement of the field. These gaps can be broadly categorized into those explicitly identified in prior studies and additional underexplored areas revealed through our synthesis.

Identified research gaps from literature

A recurring theme in the literature is the limited incorporation of dynamic and real-time modeling. While some studies, such as Nguyen et al. [32], address uncertainty through stochastic approaches, few integrate adaptive control mechanisms or real-time data updates that reflect the evolving nature of healthcare systems. This omission restricts the responsiveness of models to emergent conditions, particularly in crisis scenarios like pandemics or natural disasters.

Another notable gap lies in the fragmentation across decision-making levels. Most optimization models focus exclusively on strategic, tactical, or operational planning, with rare exceptions—such as Liu et al. [84] and Garg et al. [81]—that attempt to bridge these layers. The lack of integrated frameworks impedes holistic decision-making and fails to capture the cascading effects of choices made at one level on others.

Equity in resource allocation remains an underrepresented objective. Although studies like Cardoso-Grilo et al. [39] and Yin & Büyüktahtakın [60] introduce equity metrics, the dominant focus across the literature is on cost minimization and efficiency maximization. This narrow lens overlooks systemic disparities and limits the capacity of models to support inclusive and just healthcare delivery.

Furthermore, the modeling of public-private collaboration is virtually absent. Given the growing interdependence between public and private healthcare providers, especially during large-scale health emergencies, the lack of joint planning models represents a significant oversight. Future research should explore mechanisms for coordinated resource sharing, joint investment strategies, and policy alignment across sectors.

Finally, the integration of disease transmission models into operational planning is limited. Although epidemiological modeling is central to pandemic response, only about 30% of related studies incorporate such models into logistical decision-making. This disconnect hinders anticipatory planning and weakens the ability of healthcare systems to proactively allocate resources in response to projected disease spread.

Underexplored areas from our synthesis

Our review also highlights several under-investigated themes that warrant deeper exploration. One such area is the modeling of patient no-shows. In real-world settings, missed appointments—particularly in outpatient clinics and elective procedures—lead to resource wastage, idle staff, and increased wait times. Despite its operational significance, few optimization models account for no-show probabilities or develop adaptive scheduling systems. Future research could integrate behavioral modeling, automated reminder systems, and overbooking strategies within robust and stochastic frameworks to enhance service utilization.

Another overlooked dimension is the trade-off between overutilization and underutilization of healthcare resources. Existing models often assume full or near-full utilization, prioritizing efficiency and cost-effectiveness. However, excessive workloads can result in staff burnout, medical errors, and compromised care quality, while underutilization implies inefficiency and waste. There is a pressing need to formally model this trade-off, incorporating nonlinear workload-performance relationships, fatigue constraints, and safety thresholds to support balanced and sustainable operations.

Length-of-stay (LoS)-dependent costing also remains insufficiently addressed. While LoS significantly influences hospital costs and resource availability, most models treat it as a fixed or exogenous variable. This simplification overlooks the complex interplay between discharge timing, bed occupancy, and readmission risks. Few studies, such as Patrick [36], explicitly model this interaction. Future work should endogenize LoS within planning frameworks to enable nuanced evaluations of extended versus shortened stays under varying policy and demand scenarios.

Moreover, healthcare delivery often spans multiple services and facilities, yet most optimization models focus on single-site planning. This narrow scope fails to capture interdependencies across emergency departments, surgical units, rehabilitation centers, and long-term care facilities. Only a handful of studies, such as Bidhandi et al. [40], explore multi-stage or multi-site coordination, and even fewer validate their models with real-world data. There is a critical need for integrated network optimization approaches that reflect patient flows, referrals, transfers, and shared resource pools across interconnected facilities.

Lastly, the inclusion of demographic variables in healthcare planning models is notably sparse. While clinical urgency and care type are commonly used for patient categorization, attributes such as age, gender, income, ethnicity, and comorbidity profiles are often excluded. These factors profoundly influence healthcare access, risk exposure, and resource needs—particularly in pandemic response and equity-focused interventions. The absence of demographic granularity limits the ability of models to support personalized care and address systemic disparities. Future research should embed demographic segmentation within multi-objective frameworks that balance efficiency, fairness, and public health outcomes.
