Removal of Mercury from Flue Gas by Adsorption on Mechanochemically Modified Coconut-Shell Activated Carbon Enriched with CCl4
Authors: Jun Zhang, Chunbao Zhao, Qiang Zhou, Wei Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Industrial waste coconut-shell activated carbon enriched with CCl4 (SACCl) was employed for the removal of elemental mercury (Hg0) from flue gas via mechanochemical modification. The results demonstrated that the mechanochemically modified SACCl (denoted as SACCl-M20, with a ball-milling duration of 20 min) exhibited excellent Hg0 adsorption performance, with the Hg0 breakthrough rate remaining nearly 0 even after 1 h of continuous adsorption. In the presence of 600 ppm of SO2, the Hg0 removal efficiency of SACCl-M20 decreased from 99.3% to 93.8%. However, this inhibitory effect was attenuated with the introduction of 6% O2, with the efficiency recovering to 97.6%. A slight further improvement in the removal efficiency to 98.2% was achieved by the additional introduction of NO. Thermogravimetric analysis confirmed no release of substances from SACCl-M20 below 150 °C. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis revealed that chloride ions (Cl–) were generated on the SACCl surface via mechanochemical modification, which promoted the oxidation and adsorption of mercury. The main mercury adsorption products on the SACCl-M20 surface were identified as HgCl2 and Hg2Cl2, which could be converted into HgS via mechanochemical ball milling with Na2S. The gas-phase mercury adsorption process on both pristine and mechanochemically treated SACCl surfaces was governed by external diffusion and chemisorption. Mechanochemical modification enhanced the chemisorption of mercury on the SACCl surface but reduced the mass transfer rate of external diffusion.

Introduction

Mercury, a typical toxic pollutant emitted from coal-fired flue gas, has attracted widespread attention due to its high toxicity, bioaccumulation, and long-range atmospheric migration properties. There are three main forms of mercury in coal-fired flue gas: oxidized mercury (Hg2+), particulate mercury (Hgp), and elemental mercury (Hg0). Among them, Hg2+ and Hgp can be effectively captured by existing air pollutant purification devices (e.g., wet flue gas desulfurization devices, electrostatic precipitators, and filter dust controllers) owing to their high water solubility and particulate affinity. However, Hg0 is the dominant form in flue gas and is difficult to eliminate using conventional treatment equipment due to its chemical inertness and high volatility. − Therefore, converting Hg0 into HgP or Hg2+ for co-removal with existing flue gas pollution control devices has become a promising strategy for mercury abatement in coal-fired flue gas.

Activated carbon injection (ACI) is a well-recognized technology that can effectively convert Hg0 into Hgp in flue gas, and the combined application of ACI with flue gas dust removal devices is regarded as the most promising mercury removal technologies. , Nevertheless, competitive adsorption occurs between Hg0 and flue gas components (e.g., SO2 and NO) on the activated carbon surface, leading to low adsorbent utilization and high mercury removal costs. , To address these issues, researchers have attempted to improve the mercury adsorption capacity of unit mass activated carbon and reduce mercury removal cost through various modification methods, such as sulfur doping , and chlorine impregnation. − However, traditional chemical solution impregnation methods suffer from complex preparation processes, high production costs, and long durations, resulting in elevated adsorbent preparation costs.

Mechanochemical modification can regulate the physical and chemical properties of adsorbent surfaces and induce solid-state chemical reactions by leveraging the friction, shear, and impact forces generated from solid–solid surface interaction. − Compared with traditional chemical solution impregnation, mechanochemical modification has attracted significant research interest due to its advantages of simple operation, low energy consumption, facile chemical modification, and industrial scalability. Bisson et al. found that mechanochemical modification of biomass ash with liquid bromine significantly improved its mercury removal efficiency. Xiao et al. confirmed that although mechanochemical bromination slightly reduced the specific surface area of petroleum coke, it remarkably increased the number of mercury adsorption active sites, leading to a 60–70% enhancement in mercury removal efficiency. Zhang et al. and Geng et al. reported that mechanochemical bromination modification increased the active sites and functional groups on fly ash surface, thereby improving mercury oxidation and removal capabilities, with HgBr2 and HgO formed as the main adsorption products on the fly ash surface. While these studies validated the effectiveness of mechanochemical modification in enhancing mercury oxidation and adsorption, the added modifiers (e.g., Cl or Br) increase adsorbent preparation costs and pose potential secondary pollution risks.

Industrial waste coconut-shell activated carbon enriched with CCl4(SACCl) is a byproduct of CCl4-containing organic waste gas treatment processes, where CCl4 primarily interacts chemically with the activated carbon surface to form stable C–Cl covalent bonds (confirmed by XPS analysis in Section ). This chemical binding of CCl4 distinguishes SACCl from materials with physically adsorbed CCl4 and provides a unique foundation for the subsequent generation of active Cl– for Hg0 oxidation via mechanochemical modification. In this work, the waste coconut-shell-activated carbon enriched with CCl4(SACCl) was used as the raw material to fabricate highly efficient and low-cost mercury removal adsorbents via mechanochemical ball-milling modification. Specifically, the effects of modification duration and adsorption conditions on mercury removal efficiency were systematically investigated, and the underlying mechanism of Hg0 adsorption on mechanochemically modified SACCl was elucidated through a combination of adsorption experiments and material characterization techniques.

Experimental Section

Sorbent Preparation

Honeycomb-shaped industrial waste coconut-shell activated carbon containing CCl4 (SACCl) was initially crushed using a high-speed crusher at a rotational speed of 3400 rpm for 2 min. The crushed product was then sieved through a standard double-layer sieve (60 mesh upper sieve and 80 mesh lower sieve) under continuous mechanical vibration (frequency: 50 Hz) to collect the 60–80 mesh fraction, which was sealed and stored as a raw material for preparing mercury removal adsorbents.

An omnidirectional planetary ball mill was employed for the mechanochemical modification of SACCl. As illustrated in Figure , four freely rotatable agate jars were fixed to the mill’s base plate. When the base plate rotated (revolved), the four agate jars performed a 360° reverse rotation along their orbital paths. Based on the research by Geng et al., the modification parameters were set as follows: agate ball diameter = 6 mm, ball milling speed = 300 r/min, and mass ratio of agate balls to SACCl = 20:1 (100 g agate balls and 5 g SACCl). To investigate the effect of the mechanochemical modification time on mercury removal performance, the ball-milling duration was varied as 10, 20, 40, 60, and 80 min, and the resulting modified adsorbents were denoted as SACCl-M10, SACCl-M20, SACCl-M40, SACCl-M60, and SACCl-M80, respectively.

Schematic diagram of an omnidirectional planetary ball mill.

Mercury Removal Test

The mercury adsorption performance of the adsorbent was evaluated by using a fixed-bed experimental setup (Figure ), which consisted of a simulated flue gas distribution system, mercury vapor generation system, fixed-bed adsorption reactor, online mercury analysis device, and tail gas treatment system.

Mercury adsorption experimental device.

A mercury permeation tube was sealed in a U-shaped high-borosilicate glass tube and immersed in a thermostatic water bath. High-purity N2 was introduced into one end of the U-shaped tube to carry a constant concentration of mercury vapor, which was then mixed with balanced N2, O2, SO2, or NO to form simulated mercury-containing flue gas. The mercury concentration was controlled by adjusting the water bath temperature, and the total flow rate of the simulated flue gas was maintained at 2 L/min. The flow rate of the mercury-carrying N2 was precisely controlled at 200 mL/min using a mass flow meter (MFC). For each experiment, 200 mg of the sorbent sample was placed in the fixed-bed reactor. The simulated mercury-containing flue gas passed through the adsorbent bed, and the mercury concentration in the outlet gas was measured in real time using a VM-3000 online mercury analyzer (Mercury Instrument, Germany). The exhaust gas was purified by activated carbon adsorption before being discharged.

The mercury removal performance of the adsorbent was characterized by the mercury breakthrough rate (η, %) and mercury adsorption capacity per unit mass of adsorbent (q, μg/g), as follows 1 η=c2c1×100% 2 q=c1∫0tQ(1−c2c1)dtm where Q is the flow rate of the simulated flue gas, m3/min; m is the adsorbent mass (g); t is the adsorption time (min); and c 1and c 2 are the mercury concentrations in the simulated flue gas at the inlet and outlet of the fixed bed, μg/m3, respectively.

Sorbent Characterization

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific 250Xi) with a monochromatic Al X-ray source (pass energy 50.0 eV, Spot Size 500 μm) was used to analyze the chemical bonding configurations of chlorine and oxygen on the adsorbent surfaces. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms were obtained on a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 analyzer at an adsorption temperature of 77 K, with degassing at 393 K for 480 min. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) specific surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution of the adsorbents were calculated accordingly. The stability of SACCl-M20 was evaluated using a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA) at a heating rate of 20 K/min in N2 atmosphere.

Adsorption Kinetics Model

Pseudo-First-Order Kinetic Model

The kinetic model of pseudo-first-order is mainly based on the mass balance equation and describes the diffusion and mass transfer processes of gaseous adsorbates to the external surface of adsorbent. The model equation is expressed as follows 3 dqtdt=k1(qe−qt) In this expression, q t refers to the amount of adsorbate adsorbed per unit mass adsorbent at time t, q e represents its equilibrium value, and k 1 is the rate constant of the pseudo-first-order equation.

Pseudo-Second-Order Kinetic Model

The pseudo-second-order kinetic model is a quantitative method for describing the adsorption rate of adsorbates on the solid material surface, which involves the chemisorption process of adsorbates on the active sites of the adsorbent surface. The model equation is given as follows 4 dqtdt=k2(qe−qt)2 where the definitions of q t and q e are the same as those in eq , and k 2 is the rate constant of the pseudo-second-order equation.

Elovich Model

The Elovich kinetic model is based on the isotherm equation of Temkin adsorption and is used to describe the chemical process of gas molecule adsorption on solid surfaces. The model equation is expressed as follows 5 dqtdt=a·e−bqt where a is the initial adsorption rate, and b is a constant related to surface coverage and activation energy.

Weber–Morris Kinetic Model

The Weber–Morris kinetic model is also based on the mass transfer balance and describes the intraparticle diffusion process of the adsorbate from the external surface to the internal pores of the solid adsorbent. The model equation is expressed as follows 6 qt=kidt1/2+C where k id is the rate constant of the intraparticle diffusion model, and C is the constant related to the boundary layer effect of adsorption.

Results and Discussion

Mercury Removal Performance

Effect of Mechanochemical Modification

To investigate the effect of mechanochemical ball-milling modification on mercury removal performance of SACCl, pristine coconut-shell activated carbon (SAC), SACCl, and SACCl-M10 were tested in a fixed-bed adsorption system, and their Hg0 breakthrough curves are shown in Figure (a). The Hg0 breakthrough curves of SACCl and SACCl-M10 exhibited an “L” shape, with the breakthrough rates stabilizing at approximately 22% and 10%, respectively. In contrast, the Hg0 breakthrough curves of pristine SAC increased rapidly, exceeding 60% after 60 min of adsorption. These results indicated that the mercury removal performance of SAC was significantly enhanced after enrichment with CCl4, as chlorine species in CCl4 promoted the oxidation and adsorption of Hg0. Furthermore, mechanochemical modification further improved the mercury adsorption efficiency compared to unmodified SACCl.

Hg0 breakthrough curves for (a) different adsorbents and (b) different ball-milling times.

Ball-milling time significantly affects the physical and chemical properties of adsorbent surfaces, thereby influencing mercury adsorption. , The Hg0 breakthrough curves of SACCl modified with different ball-milling durations are presented in Figure (b). All curves exhibited an “L” profile, with a rapid decline in the initial adsorption stage, followed by gradual stabilization. The adsorption performance first improved and then deteriorated with increasing ball-milling time. SACCl-M20 and SACCl-M40 maintained a nearly zero breakthrough rate after 1 h of continuous adsorption. Compared with the 70–95% mercury adsorption efficiency of activated carbon impregnated with KI or HCl solution, , SACCl-M20 and SACCl-M40 achieved nearly 100% Hg0 removal efficiency. The preparation cost of KI/HCl-impregnated activated carbon is approximately 8000–12,000 RMB per ton, whereas the cost of SACCl-M20 and SACCl-M40 is only about 20% of this value. However, when the mechanochemical ball-milling time was extended to 80 min, the breakthrough rate of Hg0 increased to over 15%, which may be attributed to the destruction of the SACCl surface pore structure caused by excessive milling. This observation is consistent with the findings of Zhao et al. on mercury removal using mechanochemically sulfur-modified biomass coke.

Effect of Adsorption Condition

The effect of the inlet Hg0 concentration on the mercury removal performance of SACCl-M20 at 150 °C is shown in Figure (a). When the inlet mercury concentration was below 40.3 μg/m3, the Hg0 breakthrough rate remained nearly zero after 60 min adsorption; however, it increased to approximately 5% when the concentration was elevated to 52.1 μg/m3. This result indicates that the inlet Hg0 concentration had a relatively small impact on the mercury removal efficiency of SACCl-M20 within the tested concentration range.

Hg0 breakthrough curves of different adsorption conditions with (a) inlet concentration of Hg0 and (b) adsorption temperature.

Figure (b) presents the Hg0 breakthrough curves of SACCl-M20 at different adsorption temperatures with an initial concentration of 31.9 μg/m3. At 100 °C, the Hg0 breakthrough rate exceeded 40% after 60 min adsorption; in contrast, it remained nearly zero at 150 °C, indicating complete Hg0 removal. The mercury removal efficiency of SACCl-M20 was approximately 10% higher than that of mechanochemically bromine-modified activated coke reported in the literature. However, the breakthrough rate increased with further increases in the temperature. This suggests that Hg0 adsorption on SACCl-M20 is dominated by chemisorption, which is enhanced at elevated temperatures. Nevertheless, mercury chloride (the main adsorption product) begins to decompose above 200 °C, leading to increased Hg0 penetration at temperatures exceeding 150 °C. Additionally, the desorption of physically adsorbed CCl4 from SACCl-M20 may contribute to the decreased mercury removal efficiency at higher temperatures.

Effects of SO2 and NO

The effects of SO2 and NO on mercury adsorption performance of SACCl-M20 under 150 °C with an initial Hg0 concentration of 31.9 ± 0.2 μg/m3 are shown in Figure . The Hg0 removal efficiency of SACCl-M20 decreased from 99.3% to 93.8% when the simulated flue gas contained 600 ppm of SO2, which is consistent with the results reported in the literature. , This phenomenon was attributed to the competitive adsorption between SO2 and Hg0 for the active site of oxygen-containing functional groups on the surface of activated carbon. However, the Hg0 removal efficiency recovered to 97.6% when 6% oxygen was introduced into the simulated flue gas, and the inhibitory effect of SO2 on mercury adsorption on the SACCl-M20 surface was significantly attenuated. It is speculated that oxygen is adsorbed on the surface of SACCl-M20 and forms new oxygen-containing functional groups that facilitate mercury adsorption, thereby offsetting the adverse effects caused by the competitive adsorption of SO2. Compared with the condition of 600 ppm of SO2 with 6% O2, the Hg0 removal efficiency of SACCl-M20 was slightly improved to 98.2% after the additional introduction of NO.

Surface Chemical Properties Analysis

XPS was used to analyze the C 1s and Cl 2p spectra of pristine SAC, SACCl, and SACCl-M20 to investigate the effect of mechanochemical modification on the surface chemical properties of the adsorbents. As shown in Figure (a),(c),(e), the C 1s spectra of all samples were deconvoluted into four peaks: the dominant peak at 284.8 eV (C–C), weak peaks at 285.1 eV (C–O) and 288.99 eV (CO), and a small peak corresponding to COOH/C­(O)–O–C groups.

XPS spectrogram of SAC, SAC–CCl4, and SAC–CCl4-M20.

The Cl 2p spectra (Figure (b),(d),(f)) exhibited three distinct characteristic peaks: a peak at 198.12–198.52 eV (ionic chlorine, Cl–) and two larger peaks at 200.6 ± 0.2 eV and 202.4 ± 0.5 eV (covalently bonded chlorine, C–Cl groups). After mechanochemical modification, the relative content of C–Cl groups decreased from 89.37% (SACCl) to 77.72% (SACCl-M20), while the relative content of Cl– increased from 10.63% to 22.28%. This increase in the Cl– content is considered to be the key factor promoting the adsorption of gas-phase Hg0.

It can be inferred that the high-energy impacts generated by ball milling break partial C–C bonds in the carbon matrix, generating abundant surface defects of SACCl (e.g., edge sites, dangling bonds, and lattice distortions), which possess unpaired electrons with high reactivity. The C–Cl bonds of the SACCl surface interact with these defect sites, leading to the weakening of C–Cl bond strength and the initiation of an electron transfer process, which results in the heterolytic cleavage of C–Cl bonds. The C atom retains the electron (forming a negatively charged carbon defect), while the Cl atom acquires a net negative charge, thus generating free Cl– ions on the adsorbent surface.

Thermogravimetry Analysis

The thermal stability of SACCl-M20 was evaluated by a Thermal Gravimetric Analyzer at a heating rate of 20 K/min under a N2 atmosphere. The thermogravimetric (TG) and derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) curves are shown in Figure . The TG curve indicates that SACCl-M20 began to lose weight at 168.5 °C, with no significant mass loss observed at temperatures below 150 °C, confirming that no substances were released during mercury adsorption at 150 °C. The DTG curve showed a maximum weight loss rate at 221.6 °C, and the mass of SACCl-M20 remained constant at temperatures above 360 °C. The total mass loss rate was 13.1%, which was attributed to the release of bound water and chlorine-containing substances from the adsorbent surface at elevated temperatures.

Hg-TPD Analysis

Temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) experiments were conducted to investigate the mercury species formed during the adsorption process. Samples of SACCl and SACCl-M20 (0.5 g each) after mercury adsorption for 1 h were heated in a pure N2 atmosphere at a rate of 5 °C/min, and the desorbed mercury concentration was detected in real time. The Hg–TPD curves are shown in Figure . The TPD curve of SACCl was deconvoluted into two characteristic peaks at 270 and 350 °C, corresponding to the decomposition of HgCl2 and HgO, respectively. , In contrast, the Hg–TPD curve of SACCl-M20 showed a broad desorption range (60–520 °C) with three deconvoluted peaks at 201.7 °C, 258.3 °C, and 284.7 °C. The peak at 284.7 °C (49.1% of total desorption) was attributed to HgCl2, the peak at 201.7 °C (37.2%) to Hg2Cl2, and the peak at 258.3 °C (13.7%) to an intermediate species between Hg2Cl2 and HgCl2. The total mercury desorption amount of SACCl-M20 was 2.3 times that of SACCl via the integration of the desorption curve, which is consistent with the mercury adsorption performance results shown in Figure (a). To investigate the conversion of mercury adsorption products, SACCl-M20 after 1 h of mercury adsorption was ball-milled with Na2S (mass ratio = 10:1) for 30 min, followed by Hg-TPD analysis. The results are listed in Figure . Compared with SACCl-M20, the Hg-TPD curve of Na2S-treated sample (SACCl-M20-Na2S) exhibited a narrow desorption range (200–420 °C) with a single peak at 300 °C, which was attributed to the decomposition of HgS. This result indicated that the mercuric chloride (HgCl2 and Hg2Cl2) adsorbed on SACCl-M20 can be converted into HgS via mechanochemical ball milling with Na2S. Since HgS is insoluble in water and chemically stable, this conversion step eliminates the potential risk of secondary mercury release during the disposal or utilization of the spent adsorbent.

Adsorption Kinetics Analysis

Adsorption kinetics is an important method for investigating the mechanisms of the adsorption processes. It is well established that the adsorption of gaseous Hg0 on solid adsorbent surfaces involves three consecutive steps: (1) diffusion of Hg0 molecules from the gas phase to the external surface of the adsorbent (external diffusion); (2) intraparticle diffusion of Hg0 molecules through the adsorbent pores to the internal active sites, and (3) adsorption of Hg0 molecules at active sites on the adsorbent surface (chemisorption/physical adsorption). The overall adsorption rate is determined by the slowest step (rate-limiting step). In this study, pseudo-first-order, pseudo-second-order, Elovich, and Weber–Morris kinetic models were used to fit the experimental adsorption data to investigate the adsorption mechanism of Hg0 on SAC, SACCl, and SACCl-M20.

The experimental mercury adsorption capacity data were nonlinearly fitted using eq , and the fitting results are shown in Figure (a). The calculated parameters of q e and k 1 are listed in Table . The correlation coefficients (R 2) of all samples exceeded 0.99, indicating that the pseudo-first-order model describes the experimental data. This suggests that Hg0 adsorption on the surfaces of SAC, SACCl, and SACCl-M20 is closely related to external diffusion, which is consistent with the findings of Zhang et al. on gaseous Hg0 adsorption by nonthermal plasma-modified activated carbon. The rate constant k 1 of SACCl and SACCl-M20 was significantly higher than that of pristine SAC, which was attributed to the increased average pore diameter of SACCl after ball milling (Table ), which enhanced the driving force for external diffusion mass transfer.

The nonlinear fitting results of the pseudo-second-order model are shown in Figure (b), and the calculated parameters are listed in Table . The R 2 values of all samples exceeded 0.99, indicating that the pseudo-second-order model also well describes the experimental data. This suggests that chemisorption is an important process in Hg0 adsorption on the sorbent surfaces. Hubbe et al. noted that the pseudo-second-order model cannot fully explain the entire chemisorption process (as it does not account for diffusion effects), but it is suitable for adsorption systems with low adsorbate concentrations or stable equilibrium concentrations, where the collision between mercury molecules and surface active sites is the rate-limiting step. As shown in Figure , the outlet Hg0 concentration after adsorption by SACCl-M20 was extremely low and stable, which is consistent with the basic assumption of the pseudo-second-order model. The rate constant k 2 of SACCl-M20 was significantly higher than that of pristine SAC, which may be attributed to the increased number of chlorine-containing active sites on the modified adsorbent surfaces via mechanochemical modification.

The nonlinear fitting results of the Elovich model are shown in Figure (c), and the calculated parameters are given in Table . The R 2 values of all samples were above 0.99, which is very close to the pseudo-second-order model. This indicates that Hg0 adsorption on the active sites of the adsorbents follows the Temkin adsorption isotherm and is dominated by chemisorption, which further confirms the important role of chemisorption in the Hg0 adsorption process.

The fitting results of the Weber–Morris model are shown in Figure (d), and the calculated parameters are listed in Table . The R 2 values of all samples were below 0.96, and the fitting curves did not pass through the origin. This suggests that intraparticle diffusion contributes to the Hg0 adsorption process but is not the sole rate-limiting step. The nonzero intercept C indicates the presence of a significant boundary layer effect, which further verifies the important role of external diffusion in the adsorption process.

As shown in Figure and Table , the pseudo-first-order, pseudo-second-order, and Elovich models provided better fits to the experimental data than the Weber and Morris models (higher R 2 values). This confirmed that the gaseous Hg0 adsorption processes on the SAC, SACCl, and SACCl-M20 surfaces are jointly governed by both external diffusion and chemisorption. The equilibrium adsorption capacity q e of SACCl was significantly higher than that of SAC in the pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order models, indicating that CCl4 adsorption enhanced the Hg0 adsorption capacity of activated carbon, which is consistent with the experimental results shown in Figure (a). The q e values obtained from the pseudo-second-order model for SACCl-M20 were higher than those from the pseudo-first-order model, which suggests that the Hg0 adsorption on the SACCl-M20 is dominated by chemisorption. In contrast, the q e values from the pseudo-first-order model decreased after ball milling, which was attributed to the destruction of the surface pore structure of SACCl caused by mechanochemical modification. The rate constant k 1 was much lower than those of k 2 and k id, indicating that the external diffusion is the rate-limiting step for Hg0 adsorption on SACCl-M20. Mechanochemical modification enhanced the chemisorption of Hg0 by increasing the number of chlorine-containing active sites on the SACCl surface but reduced the external diffusion mass transfer rate due to pore structure damage. This trade-off effect led to a first increase and then a decrease in mercury removal efficiency with increasing ball-milling time, which is consistent with the results shown in Figure (b).

Conclusion

1.The industrial waste coconut-shell activated carbon enriched with CCl4 (SACCl) was realized for high-value utilization, which successfully transformed a waste byproduct from organic waste gas treatment into a high-efficiency, low-cost adsorbent for elemental mercury (Hg0) removal from flue gas. The SACCl modified via mechanochemical treatment for 20 min exhibited exceptional Hg0 removal performance, maintaining nearly 100% removal efficiency for 1 h at 150 °Ceven when the initial Hg0 concentration reached 52.1 μg/m3, with an overall removal efficiency exceeding 95% under simulated flue gas conditions. This excellent performance was attributed to the friction and shear forces generated by ball milling, breaking stable C–Cl covalent bonds on the SACCl surface and producing reactive chloride ions (Cl–) that serve as key active sites for Hg0 oxidation.2.Hg-TPD analysis identified HgCl2 and Hg2Cl2 as the main mercury adsorption products on SACCl-M20 surface that are formed via the oxidation of Hg0 by surface Cl–.
