Innovation in clean energy from man-made wind and small-wind generation
Authors: Isabel C. Gil-García, Ana Fernández-Guillamón, Álvaro H. Montes-Torres
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The need to reduce global emissions leads us to look for various sources of clean energy. In recent decades, wind technology has advanced significantly, enabling large-scale power generation in both marine and terrestrial environments, as well as the development of mini-wind solutions. However, we often underestimate the capacity of certain human activities and production processes to generate clean energy, wasting their true potential. This work focuses on using artificially generated wind gusts to transform them into clean electricity through small wind turbines. The proposal is developed in four phases: (1) identify activities that generate wind, (2) collect data on wind speed and direction, (3) perform a descriptive statistical analysis of the wind resource, and (4) select the appropriate technology to calculate the electricity generation. The proposal is evaluated using the air flow produced by the air conditioning systems of a data center in Colombia. The results are analyzed from technical, economic, environmental, and political perspectives. Through small wind power, an annual production of approximately 468 MWh is estimated, avoiding the emission of 300 metric tons of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{CO}}_2$$\end{document}CO2.

Introduction

The climate change has encouraged most countries to start using clean energy, reducing the consumption of fossil fuels and, consequently, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{CO}}_2$$\end{document}CO2 emissions1. Among the different energy sectors, the electrical one is extremely important, as it can be derived from various primary energy sources and, consequently, can be easily decarbonized by the use of renewable energy sources (RES) like wind2. In fact, one of the main advantages of this RES, is that, apart from large installations for grid integration, it can generate electricity at small scales installations through distributed generation, which have significant benefits3.

Recently, many studies have focused on the potential or feasibility of small wind power around the world. Elnaggar et al.4 conducted a feasibility study of the wind energy potential in Gaza to feed a small wind turbine (WT) of 5 kW installable on the roof of residential buildings. They estimated an expected value of 2695 kWh of electricity generation at 10 m, but it can be increased at higher altitudes. An analysis of 88 regions of Iran was carried out in5, trying to identify the most affordable conditions for investing; they concluded that these WTs are profitable approximately in 30% of the cases, where wind speed is higher than 5 m/s. Rodriguez et al.6 studied the techno-economic feasibility of 28 small WTs models at 18 locations in the Valley of Mexico Metropolitan Area to satisfy in situ electrical consumption. Comparing the annualized net present value, only two WTs and location models provided positive values. A similar study was carried out in7 to light the Banyak Island Tourism Area (Indonesia) with a 20 kW WT, which could produce 115,154 kWh/year and turbine. Recently, four Polish cities were analysed in8, considering two 3 kW WTs. In this case, the wind potential at the study locations was insufficient to economically justify the installation of a WT. Also, some high wind speed areas in New Zealand were considered as very convenient for small-scale wind installations9. Other authors have explored utilizing the sea breeze for microgeneration, finding it to be a promising wind energy resource in peri-urban coastal areas where the implementation of large-scale wind power plants is not feasible10.

While traditional wind energy harnesses natural wind driven by atmospheric pressure variations and the Coriolis force caused by the Earth’s rotation11, this paper explores an unconventional yet promising wind resource generated by human activities and systems. Specifically, this research analyses whether the airflows produced by heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) systems can be effectively harnessed to generate clean electrical energy using small WT. These HVAC systems use fans to expel the warm air from indoor environments to the outdoors12. This expelled air represents a consistent and potentially untapped wind resource for electricity conversion. Consequently, the novelty of this contribution lies in the systematic analysis of man-made airflows as a viable wind energy resource, which can transform these systems from mere energy consumers into contributors to sustainable energy production.

While the concept of capturing wind energy from natural sources is well-established, this paper pioneers the exploration of artificial wind generated by human activities. The objectives of this paper are twofold: To assess the feasibility of harnessing the wind resource produced by HVAC systems for small-scale wind energy generation. This includes analysing the characteristics of the airflow, such as speed, consistency, and direction, to determine its suitability for driving small wind turbines.To quantify the potential electrical energy that can be generated from this man-made wind resource. By doing so, the paper aims to establish a new perspective on sustainable energy generation within urban and industrial environments, where HVAC systems are prevalent.This work contributes to the field by not only proposing a novel source of wind energy but also by providing a framework for its practical application, thereby expanding the possibilities for renewable energy generation in built environments. Specifically, this contribution is focused on the wind resource that comes from the air expelled due to the HVAC equipment of a data centre, which requires a considerable number of fans using large amounts of energy13. A real data centre located in Colombia is considered as a case study. Search activities that generate a wind flow

Firstly, it is crucial to identify the characteristics that an air flow must have to be considered as potentially viable for energy generation. In everyday life and with the advancement of technology, several activities generate artificial wind flows that can be analysed for this purpose. To determine which activities are most suitable, the continuity of the air flow should be considered. The air flow must be continuous or, at the very least, occur at regular intervals throughout the day. This regularity and persistence are essential to ensure the reliability and consistency of the energy generation process. Examples of potential air flows are:Transportation infrastructure: Air flow generated by passing trains and subways in a tunnel can be substantial and regular due to the pressure and suction caused by their movement. This flow, although intermittent, can be predictable, making it an interesting14.Maritime vessels in motion: Vessels in motion generate an air flow due to the movement of the boat through the water. This flow is continuous during navigation, with a variable intensity depending on the speed and size of the vessel and the atmospheric and maritime conditions. The study of these flows can be relevant for the aerodynamic design and energy efficiency of vessels15.HVAC systems: These systems, used to regulate the temperature in buildings, generate significant air flows that can be harnessed. They generate a constant and controlled air flow, making them an excellent case study for analysing energy efficiency, climate control, and air distribution in inhabited spaces. This is the case study of the current research.

Search activities that generate a wind flow

The rapid technological advancements of the last decade have frequently altered the way we perform various activities. Today, there are several social networks, banking networks, streaming platforms, games, and online shopping available anywhere in the world. In addition, there are platforms that allow the creation and sharing of content almost in real time. These technological developments require that the computing equipments that host these applications and platforms have high power, storage, and speed capacities, in addition to having adequate cooling to ensure their efficient operation. Technology selection and calculation of electricity generation

Currently, there is a wide variety of both horizontal and vertical axis small wind turbines. Based on the measurements made with the meteorological station, it was observed that the direction of the wind produced by the equipment remains constant during almost the entire operating time of the chiller. However, it could be seen that the wind generated by the environment can cause the weather station vane to change in direction, which sometimes causes the wind to become turbulent. Considering this behaviour, it is decided to use a vertical axis wind turbine, which is more appropriate for turbulence conditions.

Taking into account that the wind turbines will be placed on top of the chillers, their dimensions, weight, and power factors are of great importance. The goal is to find a vertical axis wind turbine that minimizes dimensions and weight, while maximizes electricity generation. Based on these criteria, the “Tesup V7”27 wind turbine was selected, with a nominal power of 9 kW. This model was chosen because of its compact dimensions and low weight, that present a significant advantage for installation. In addition, it is one of the mini wind turbines that offers the greatest power in relation to its size. Discussion

Energy balance

With 2 chillers always in operation, the system is equivalent to 6 wind turbines generating 513.82 MWh. The 16 fans of the chillers (8 per chiller, with a nominal power of 2.4 kW) have a consumption of 336.39 MWh annually. Estimating losses of 9% due to unplanned availability, unavailability due to scheduled maintenance etc.28, the net electricity generated by the wind turbines would be 467.6 MWh, sufficient to cover the consumption of the fans, and having a surplus of 131.2 MWh. This surplus energy can be used for other electrical needs within the data centre, or can be injected into the electrical grid, providing additional benefits. Conclusions and future research

The integration of small WT into air conditioning equipment represents a tangible opportunity to move towards a more sustainable future, aligning with several Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 7, 9 and 13). This study demonstrates the feasibility of utilizing artificially generated wind from data centre cooling systems to produce clean energy through small WTs. Based on the data centre under analysis, which is located in Colombia, this work estimates an annual clean energy production of approximately 468 MWh, avoiding about 300 metric tons of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{CO}}_2$$\end{document}CO2 emissions. As there is potential for widespread application of this study in similar cooling systems globally, collaboration between governments, companies, and energy organizations is essential to harness this potential and turn it into a reality, which could have substantial cumulative environmental and economic impacts. By adopting proactive policies and encouraging innovation, we can significantly reduce our environmental impact, save energy, and move towards a more sustainable world for future generations. Furthermore, authors would like to emphasize that this solution can be scalable to other industries with similar air flow generation. As possible future works, multi-criteria decision-making strategies could be used to determine the optimum WT model.
