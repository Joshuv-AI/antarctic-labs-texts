A conserved sequence insert of fibrillins is involved in plastoglobule association and lipid binding
Authors: Kiran-Kumar Shivaiah, Roselane Kithan-Lundquist, April K Kaneshiro, Duncan M Boren, Febri A Susanto, Andres Herrera-Tequia, Josh V Vermaas, Peter K Lundquist
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlastoglobule lipid droplets of chloroplasts serve complex roles affecting plant development, stress tolerance, and photosynthesis. They harbor a set of ~40 proteins that collectively dictate plastoglobule functions. Due to the monolayer structure of plastoglobules, which encompasses a neutral lipid core, these proteins must associate monotopically on the plastoglobule surface. However, targeting determinants have not been identified for plastoglobule proteins, and the protein–membrane interaction mechanisms that establish the plastoglobule proteome remain unclear. Here, we demonstrate that most plastoglobule-localized fibrillins (FBNs) harbor an extra sequence insert with a predicted amphipathic helical structure placed at the lip of the β-barrel. Deletions of the insert in two plastoglobule-targeted FBNs disrupts their localization, indicating that the inserts are necessary for effective plastoglobule association. Molecular dynamics simulations support the specific interaction of the amphipathic helical insert of AtFBN1a with membranes rich in lipid-packing defects, which are expected to be especially prevalent on the tightly curved surface of plastoglobules. Proteomic analyses indicate that AtFBN1a influences the plastoglobule proteome through outcompeting and recruiting specific proteins. We also demonstrate that the plastoglobule-localized FBNs, AtFBN1a and AtFBN7a, bind unsaturated fatty acids (FAs), particularly C18:1, and that elimination of the amphipathic helical inserts suppresses FA binding in AtFBN1a but promotes FA binding in AtFBN7a. Predicted amphipathic helices can be identified on two-thirds of plastoglobule proteins, which indicates that the use of amphipathic helices may be a general mechanism by which proteins selectively associate with plastoglobules.Keywords: amphipathic helix, fibrillin, lipid droplet, lipocalin, plastoglobule, plastoglobulin

Introduction

Plastoglobules are lipid droplets (LDs) of plant chloroplasts, present in all photosynthetic organisms (Lundquist et al. 2020). As LDs, they are characterized by a monolayer polar lipid surface enclosing a neutral lipid core (Austin 2nd et al. 2006). Plastoglobules serve as a crossroads of metabolic activity within the chloroplast and appear to be essential for plant survival and stress resilience (Youssef et al. 2010, Lundquist et al. 2013, Martinis et al. 2013, Van Wijk and Kessler 2017, Espinoza-Corral et al. 2019, Lundquist et al. 2020, Pralon et al. 2020, Espinoza-Corral et al. 2021b, Arzac et al. 2022, Espinoza-Corral and Lundquist 2022, Torres-Romero et al. 2022, Kim et al. 2022a). Based on mutant phenotypes and gene coexpression patterns, it has been suggested that plastoglobules serve as regulatory and enzymatic hubs of chloroplast membrane remodeling and adaptation, crucial for plants to thrive within constantly fluctuating environments (Lundquist et al. 2012, 2020).

Through the use of mass spectrometry–based proteomics, immunoblotting, and confocal microscopy, a set of 40 proteins have been identified as bona fide plastoglobule-localized proteins in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana (Vidi et al. 2006, Ytterberg et al. 2006, Lundquist et al. 2012, Shanmugabalaji et al. 2020, Espinoza-Corral et al. 2021b). Consistent with the lipid-rich nature of plastoglobules, many of these proteins hold characterized or putative functions in lipid metabolism, including tocopherol cyclase, carotenoid cleavage dioxygenase 4, and allene oxide synthase. The most abundant proteins of the plastoglobule are seven members of the fibrillin (FBN) family, comprising ~50% of the A. thaliana plastoglobule protein mass (Lundquist et al. 2012, Espinoza-Corral et al. 2021b). FBNs share a lipocalin-like domain characterized by a β-barrel that forms a hydrophobic cavity capable of binding small hydrophobic ligands (Lakshmi et al. 2015, Schiefner et al. 2015, Malnoe et al. 2018) and are believed to provide a structural role for the plastoglobule (Deruere et al. 1994, Simkin et al. 2007, Singh and Mcnellis 2011). Investigation of mutant lines in plastoglobule-targeted FBNs implicate FBNs in stress tolerance (Yang et al. 2006, Youssef et al. 2010, Singh and Mcnellis 2011, Torres-Romero et al. 2020, 2022, Kim et al. 2022a) as well as in the recruitment of proteins (such as the jasmonate biosynthetic pathway or carotenoid metabolism) to plastoglobules (Youssef et al. 2010, Lundquist et al. 2013, Gamez-Arjona et al. 2014, Torres-Romero et al. 2022, Kim et al. 2022a, Zhou et al. 2023, Li et al. 2023a, 2023b).

Despite the apparently essential nature of plastoglobules in photosynthetic organisms, the molecular mechanism(s) of protein targeting to plastoglobules remains unclear. Nearly all of the ~3000 chloroplast-localized proteins—including all 40 plastoglobule proteins—are nuclear encoded. N-terminally encoded chloroplast transit peptides (cTPs) direct these proteins to the translocon at the outer chloroplast envelope membrane and translocon at the inner chloroplast envelope membrane protein import machinery, which translocates the proteins across the chloroplast double envelope membrane. But once a plastoglobule protein has been imported, and cleaved of its cTP, how can it distinguish between the various membrane subcompartments of the chloroplast?

Attempts to identify conserved sequence features among plastoglobule-localized proteins, through sequence alignments or hidden Markov models, have failed to identify common sequence features. Confocal microscopy of serially truncated AtFBN7a indicated that nearly the entire polypeptide may be necessary for proper plastoglobule targeting (Vidi et al. 2007, Shanmugabalaji et al. 2013). In contrast, closely related isoforms of Zea mays phytoene synthase either separately localize to the plastoglobule or dually localize on bilayer membranes and aqueous stroma, indicating that subtle sequence differences may underlie plastoglobule association (Shumskaya et al. 2012). Consistent with the latter observation, a 19-residue hydrophobic region of rice phytoene synthase, OsPSY2, could direct cargo protein to plastoglobules when localized at the C-terminus (Yu et al. 2023).

Protein targeting to cytosolic LDs is better understood than their targeting to plastoglobules, and it occurs through two broad targeting pathways: the first, known as the ``Class I'' (or ERTOLD) pathway, targets proteins to the endoplasmic reticulum membrane through a hydrophobic hairpin loop, which then migrate laterally to physically connected LDs; in the second pathway, known as the ``Class II'' (or CYTOLD) pathway, proteins are directly localized to LDs from the cytosol, often through an amphipathic helix (AH) (Kory et al. 2016, Dhiman et al. 2020, Olarte et al. 2022). Because plastoglobules are physically connected to the thylakoid membrane, the Class I targeting pathway, in which proteins are targeted first to the thylakoid membrane before lateral migration to the plastoglobule, could be employed. Alternatively, proteins could employ a Class II targeting pathway in which AHs or lipid modifications (for example) drive proteins directly to associate with the plastoglobule.

The use of AHs in LD association is logical. The monolayer structure of LDs is expected to foster the formation of lipid-packing defects, defined as gaps in the polar headgroup surface through which underlying neutral lipid becomes exposed to the aqueous solvent (Drin and Antonny 2010, Bacle et al. 2017, Prevost et al. 2018). The lipid-packing defects favor interaction of AHs through hydrophobic interactions between the exposed neutral lipid and the hydrophobic face of the AH, as well as polar or charged interactions between the lipid headgroups or aqueous solvent and the hydrophilic face of the AH. In the context of cytosolic LDs, AHs typically harbor bulky hydrophobic side chains in their hydrophobic face that are critical for effective LD interaction, seemingly by promoting initial anchoring to the LD surface (Monje-Galvan and Klauda 2018, Prevost et al. 2018, Dhiman et al. 2020, Kim et al. 2021, Zouiouich et al. 2022). In addition, the positioning of uncharged polar residues near the interface between hydrophobic and hydrophilic faces is important for membrane curvature sensing in many AHs (Drin et al. 2007, Drin and Antonny 2010). However, unlike LDs of the cytosol, which are composed of phospholipids, the monolayer of plastoglobules is comprised of galactolipids. Moreover, the lipid core of plastoglobules is rich in prenyllipids and their derivatives, such as plastoquinone and xanthophyll esters (Lundquist et al. 2013, Espinoza-Corral et al. 2021b, Morelli et al. 2023), in contrast to the lipid core of cytosolic LDs, characterized by triacylglycerol (TAG) and sterol esters Lundquist et al. 2020, Gao et al. 2019, Kim et al. 2022b. Plastoglobules are also substantially smaller in size than cytosolic LDs, often as small as ~70 nm in diameter (Van Wijk and Kessler 2017). Thus, it is not clear to what extent the targeting principles that apply to cytosolic LDs can be translated to plastoglobules.

Here, we report that FBNs employ a unique AH insert within their lipocalin-like domain that drives effective plastoglobule targeting. Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy indicates a stable helical structure in the AH while molecular dynamics (MD) simulations support the interaction of the AH sequence specifically with membrane surfaces enriched in lipid-packing defects. We also demonstrate that FBNs bind fatty acids (FAs) and that the efficacy and ligand preference are affected by the presence/absence of the AH insert. Predicted AHs are enriched in plastoglobule-localized proteins—found on two-thirds of all plastoglobule proteins—indicating that the use of AHs may represent a general mechanism of plastoglobule targeting.

Results

Predicted AHs are a common feature of plastoglobule proteins

To test whether AHs may be involved in association of proteins to plastoglobules, we used the HeliQuest web-based software tool to screen for predicted AHs among Gautier et al. (2008) A. thaliana proteins that have been demonstrated to localize to plastoglobules in various proteomic experiments (Vidi et al. 2006, Ytterberg et al. 2006, Lundquist et al. 2012, Espinoza-Corral et al. 2021b). We screened a total of 40 plastoglobule proteins for AHs of α-helical structure. HeliQuest allows customization of multiple search parameters, including the net charge state, the hydrophobic moment, and the maximal number of charged residues. To first optimize the parameter settings, we ran the screen with six proteins known to contain AHs [Homo sapiens ADP-ribosylation factor GTPase-activating enzyme (Bigay et al. 2005); H. sapiens Golgi microtubule-associated protein of 210 kDa (Magdeleine et al. 2016); H. sapiens perilipin 1, 3, and 4 (Rowe et al. 2016, Copic et al. 2018, Ajjaji et al. 2019); and Penicillium chrysogenum peroxin-11 (Opalinski et al. 2011)], as well as eight soluble stromal chloroplast proteins that are not expected to hold AHs (A. thaliana caseinolytic protease R1, AtClpP5, and AtClpP6, thioredoxin X, copper chaperone for SOD1, Rubisco activase, and Chlamydomonas reinhardtii thioredoxin H), serving as positive and negative controls, respectively. While multiple AHs were predicted within each of the positive controls, no AHs were predicted from any of the soluble stromal proteins (Supplementary Table S1). This suggests that with the selected search parameters we were able to effectively identify AHs with a low false-positive identification rate.

All the gene models of the 40 plastoglobule proteins were analyzed using the HeliQuest search program, totaling 57 distinct protein sequences from all the possible gene models. The search predicted at least one AH in 36 of the 57 plastoglobule protein sequences (63.2%), and in 27 out of the 40 plastoglobule proteins (67.5%), including all 6 of the ABC1 kinases and 4 of the 7 FBNs (Table 1; Supplementary Table S1; Supplementary Data File S1). Plastoglobule proteins lacking predicted AHs tended to be less abundant s of the plastoglobule proteome (e.g. the UbiE-like methyl transferases and flavin reductase–like proteins) (Lundquist et al. 2012), or those recruited to the plastoglobule under unique conditions (e.g. the allene oxide cyclase isomers) (Lundquist et al. 2013). To compare the rate of AH prediction of plastoglobule proteins peripherally associated with the other chloroplast membrane systems, we applied our HeliQuest search against the complete list of Plant Proteome Database-annotated peripherally associated inner envelope and peripherally associated thylakoid proteins (Sun et al. 2009). Predicted AHs were found in 39 of 155 total gene models (25.2%) of peripherally associated thylakoid proteins and 10 of 23 total gene models (43.5%) of peripherally associated inner envelope proteins (Supplementary Fig. S1). Thus, while a substantial portion of peripherally associated proteins at the inner envelope and thylakoids may comprise AHs, plastoglobule proteins contain predicted AHs at a substantially higher rate.

FBN proteins of plastoglobules harbor a unique helical insert sequence with amphipathic character

To better understand their structural context, we developed structural models of all 13 FBN proteins encoded in the A. thaliana genome, generated using AlphaFold2 (Jumper et al. 2021, Jumper and Hassabis 2022) and RoseTTAFold (Baek et al. 2021). The models from the two programs were highly similar and thus we relied on the AlphaFold2 models for our analyses (Supplementary Fig. S2). All the FBN proteins comprised an eight-stranded β-barrel structure with a conserved GxW motif positioned near the bottom of the β-barrel, the Trp side chain facing inside the barrel. This modeled structure is consistent with crystal structures of other lipocalin domains such as Escherichia coli Blc (Flower et al. 2000, Campanacci et al. 2004). Upstream of the β-barrel (i.e. near the N-terminus) was found a crisscrossing α-helical pair of varying length. While the helical pair and β-barrel formed the shared core features of all 13 A. thaliana FBNs, a unique helical insert was additionally found within all but one of the plastoglobule-localized FBNs and was absent from all but one of the thylakoid/stroma-localized FBNs (Fig. 1a; Supplementary Fig. S2). This helical insert occurred between the fifth and sixth strands of the β-barrel [strands E and F according to the lipocalin domain nomenclature (Flower 1996)], spanned 16–27 residues in length, and was usually flanked by short β-strand inserts not present in the core FBN structure (Fig. 1b–d and Supplementary Fig. S2). The helical insert was also sometimes predicted by AlphaFold2 to form a kinked conformation, with an interruption to the helical structure of two or more residues (Fig. 1e). Notably, temperature-induced lipocalin proteins (TILs) use a hydrophobic, proline-rich insert sequence in this same location, between β-strands E and F, for peripheral membrane association at the plasma membrane (Hernandez-Gras and Boronat 2015). Furthermore, the placement of the FBN helical insert along the side of the β-barrel opening was reminiscent of the cryo-EM structures of the human and fly seipin lumenal domains, in which a hydrophobic helix (i.e. the ``central helix'' or ``membrane-anchored helix'') lies near the opening of an eight-stranded β-sandwich (Supplementary Fig. S3). The hydrophobic helix of seipin embeds within the endoplasmic reticulum membrane and is important for its oligomerization, function, and localization (Sui et al. 2018, Yan et al. 2018, Salo et al. 2020).

Because the presence of a helical insert closely coincided with plastoglobule localization among the FBNs, we considered how the helical inserts may drive interaction with plastoglobules. The HeliQuest-predicted AHs of the FBNs corresponded to the helical inserts (AtFBN1a and AtFBN1b) and in other instances with portions of the helical pair (AtFBN1b and AtFBN4). As noted above, AtFBN7a, AtFBN7b, and AtFBN8 did not have a HeliQuest-predicted AH. However, by independently analyzing each helical insert of the seven plastoglobule-localized FBNs, we found that they all held an amphipathic character to varying degrees, with hydrophobic moments (μH) ranging from 0.414 to 0.591, except for AtFBN8, whose helical insert is interrupted by eight residues that are predicted by AlphaFold2 to lack a helical structure (Fig. 1d). Large hydrophobic residues (Trp, Phe, and Tyr) were conspicuously absent from the hydrophobic faces of the helical inserts, except for AtFBN5 (which does not localize to the plastoglobule) and AtFBN8 (Fig. 1d and e). This contrasts sharply with the predicted AHs of the inner envelope and thylakoid peripheral proteins (Supplementary Data File S2), as well as the known AHs of cytosolic LD proteins that frequently contain large hydrophobic residues with prominent roles in mediating LD localization (Chorlay and Thiam 2020, Chorlay et al. 2021, Kim et al. 2021, Zouiouich et al. 2022).

Plastoglobule-localized FBN proteins require the AH insert for proper plastoglobule association

To investigate the role of the helical inserts in chloroplast localization, we selected two plastoglobule-localized FBNs, AtFBN1a and AtFBN7a, with a helical structure predicted to be continuous or interrupted, respectively. We developed a series of three deletions within the AtFBN1a sequence, which removed: (i) the downstream two-thirds of the helical insert and five downstream flanking residues (AtFBN1aΔ17α), (ii) all of the insert plus 10 upstream flanking residues (AtFBN1aΔ30α), and (iii) all of the insert and 15 flanking residues upstream and downstream (AtFBN1aΔ45α) (Fig. 2a). Similarly, a series of three deletions within the AtFBN7a sequence were developed, which removed: (i) the upstream portion of the helical insert (AtFBN7aΔ17α), (ii) the downstream portion of the helical insert (AtFBN7aΔ10α), or (iii) all of the insert plus two flanking residues upstream and downstream (AtFBN7aΔ27α) (Fig. 2a). AlphaFold2 structure prediction of deletion variants, followed by structural alignments to the wild-type variant, indicated that the deletions would not impact the core structure of the proteins, including the β-barrel and α-helical pair (Fig. 2b).

Sequences were tagged and transiently transformed individually into tobacco plants (Nicotiana benthamiana), driven by the strong tobacco mosaic virus 35S constitutive promoter. Forty-eight hours after transformation, plastoglobule and thylakoid subcompartments were isolated from leaf tissue and immunoblotted (Fig. 2c). We also performed immunoblots using the total leaf protein samples from which subcompartments were isolated, allowing us to normalize the protein levels in the subcompartments to the total accumulation of each construct. As expected, the wild-type variants, AtFBN1aWT-yellow fluoresecent protein (YFP) and AtFBN7aWT-cyan fluorescent protein (CFP), were associated with the isolated plastoglobules. Each deletion construct of AtFBN1a substantially reduced the association of the protein to the plastoglobules, a phenomenon that was not replicated in the thylakoids (Fig. 2c and d). In contrast, only the Δ17α deletion of AtFBN7a led to reduced association levels with plastoglobules. Thus, while deleting the upstream portion of the predicted AH in AtFBN7a strongly impaired its plastoglobule association, deletion of the downstream portion did not, and a combined deletion of both the upstream and downstream portions appeared to mostly restore plastoglobule localization, pointing to a complex relationship between these portions of the AH in plastoglobule association (Fig. 2c and d). To validate our immunoblotting results, we used LC–MS/MS proteomics with label-free quantification (LFQ) to estimate the relative abundance of the wild-type and the Δ17α variants of AtFBN1a and AtFBN7a on isolated plastoglobules (Supplementary Table S2). Consistent with the immunoblotting results, both Δ17α variants were reduced to about one-third the abundance of their respective wild-type variants (Fig. 2d).

MD simulations reveal differential interactions with thylakoid and plastoglobule surfaces

MD simulations were used to develop molecular models of AtFBN1aWT interaction with membrane surfaces corresponding to plastoglobules or thylakoids. The plastoglobule surface was simulated using a trilayer system comprising an 8-nm-thick neutral lipid core sandwiched between two glycerolipid monolayers, as used in MD simulations with other LDs (Fig. 3a) (Gonzalez-Rubio et al. 2011, Bacle et al. 2017, Kim et al. 2022c). To mimic a plastoglobule in our simulations, we utilized a galactolipid monolayer composition of equal parts monogalactosyl diacylglycerol (MGDG) and monogalactosyl monoacylglycerol (MGMG). Inclusion of MGDG with saturated acyl chains reflects the dominant levels of this galactolipid, and the enrichment of saturated acyl chains, in isolated plastoglobules (Devadasu et al. 2025a, 2025b), while inclusion of MGMG is intended to mimic the effects of the strong membrane curvature on the plastoglobule surface, which is anticipated to introduce lipid-packing defects and intercalation of neutral lipid into the monolayer membrane (Bigay and Antonny 2012, Bacle et al. 2017). Alternative arrangements, such as a micelle or vesicle structure, would have been too large to simulate effectively. Thus, MGMG was added to facilitate the disrupted lipid packing that would be expected on the curved plastoglobule surface. A thylakoid bilayer membrane model comprising MGDG, digalactosyl diacylglycerol (DGDG), and sulfoquinovosyl diacylglycerol (SQDG) (Fig. 3a) was used as control to identify changes in binding. A hybrid ``thylakoid trilayer'' membrane, with an inner lipid core matching the plastoglobule simulations, sandwiched between outer leaflets matching the simulated thylakoid membrane, was also used to gauge the importance of the inner layer to the FBN binding pose.

Six unique simulation systems (replicas) were created with each membrane system using the AlphaFold2 structural model of AtFBN1aWT (lacking the 68-residue predicted -cTP) rotated to face each side of a cube (Supplementary Fig. S4), creating independent starting configurations to minimize bias. These starting configurations were run for 1.0 μs using NAMD 3.0a12 (Phillips et al. 2020) and CHARMM36 or CHARMM36m force fields (Klauda et al. 2010, Vanommeslaeghe et al. 2010, Huang et al. 2017).

Membrane contact points along the protein sequence were identified and mapped (Fig. 3b). Highly distinct binding poses emerged in the presence of simulated plastoglobule and simulated thylakoid membranes, while the trilayer thylakoid membrane resulted in a variety of intermediate poses. In the presence of the plastoglobule trilayer, membrane contacts primarily arose along the AH insert and the downstream 15 residues (residues 195–209 of the full-length sequence): a binding pose which emerged in five of the six replicas (Fig. 3b and c). No other regions consistently contacted the plastoglobule across multiple replicas. In contrast, in the presence of the thylakoid bilayer, contacts were identified at the N-terminus of the mature protein sequence (residues 1–10) that contained a bulky hydrophobic side chain (4Phe), the downstream α-helix of the helical pair, upstream of β-strand E (β-E) and Loop 6 (βF–βG loop), following the nomenclature established for lipocalins (Flower 1996), while the AH insert only contacted the thylakoid surface in two of the six replicas (Fig. 3b–d). Hybrid membrane simulations using a thylakoid trilayer showed inconsistent behavior across each of the simulation replicas (Supplementary Fig. S5). For example, in three of the six replicas that were run, the N-terminus makes little or no membrane contact, while in the other three it is the highest contact region of the protein. The AH also shows inconsistent contact across thylakoid trilayer replicas. The inconsistent results with the hybrid thylakoid trilayer membrane indicate that the binding poses observed with simulated plastoglobule and thylakoid membranes are a function of both the bilayer composition and the presence/absence of an underlying neutral lipid core.

Notably, the binding depth of the protein did not substantially penetrate below the polar headgroups in the plastoglobule or thylakoid simulations, with the exception of the N-terminus on the thylakoid bilayer (Fig. 3d). This suggests that either the 1.0 μs trajectories were not long enough for residues to penetrate more deeply into our plastoglobule mimic or that plastoglobule curvature is required for residue intercalation. Further investigation with substantially larger plastoglobule LDs would facilitate discrimination between these two hypotheses. Nonetheless, our MD simulation results support a central role for the AH insert in plastoglobule interaction via the unique plastoglobule lipid composition and structure.

Accumulation of exogenous FBN selectively disrupts the plastoglobule proteome

Localization of certain proteins on cytosolic LDs is sensitive to protein crowding, in which proteins are outcompeted by stronger-binding proteins (Kory et al. 2015, 2016, Prevost et al. 2018). Furthermore, plastoglobule localization of certain proteins may be dependent on protein–protein-mediated recruitment (Torres-Romero et al. 2022, Kim et al. 2022a, Li et al. 2023a, 2023b). To investigate whether AtFBN1a may influence the plastoglobule proteome through protein crowding or protein recruitment, we characterized the quantitative proteome of plastoglobules using the LC–MS/MS analyses of isolated plastoglobules from the N. benthamiana transient transformations described above. Isolated plastoglobules from the cTPAtRbcS-YFP-accumulating tissue represent the negative control and provide a baseline proteome of N. benthamiana plastoglobules that has not been previously reported in the literature. Our objective was not to identify novel plastoglobule-localized proteins, but to identify and quantify the expected plastoglobule-localized proteins of N. benthamiana and how they are changed in abundance. Therefore, we focused our identification and quantification on orthologs of the A. thaliana plastoglobule proteins identified from either unstressed or light-stressed conditions (Lundquist et al. 2012, Espinoza-Corral et al. 2021b). Most of the orthologs were identified in the isolated N. benthamiana plastoglobules of the negative control, comprising 20 distinct proteins or protein groups (hereafter, proteins) (Table 1; Supplementary Table S2). An additional four proteins were identified in either the AtFBN1aWT-YFP or AtFBN1aΔ17α-YFP plastoglobules. Protein orthologs not identified in any N. benthamiana plastoglobule isolation were limited to esterase/lipase/thioesterase 4, allene oxide synthase, aldo/keto reductase, and several proteins with unannotated function (Lundquist et al. 2012, Espinoza-Corral et al. 2021b). These proteins are among the lowest abundant proteins of A. thaliana plastoglobules and, in some cases, only localized to A. thaliana plastoglobules under light-stress conditions (Lundquist et al. 2012, Espinoza-Corral et al. 2021b). This indicates that they may be present on N. benthamiana plastoglobules, albeit only under stressed conditions or below the limit of detection of our experimental setup.

Broadly speaking, the baseline plastoglobule proteome, represented by the cTPAtRbcS-YFP-accumulating samples, is similar to that of unstressed A. thaliana, characterized by several abundant FBN homologs, particularly FBN4, and a number of less abundant enzymes, including several homologs of the ABC1K family (Fig. 4a; Table 2). However, it is notable that the proportion of the N. benthamiana plastoglobule proteome dedicated to FBNs was substantially greater than that seen in A. thaliana (68% in N. benthamiana vs 44% in A. thaliana). Additionally, plastoglobular protein of 18 kD (PG18), SOUL heme-binding protein 4 (SOUL4), and carotenoid cleavage dioxygenase 4 (CCD4) were considerably more abundant in A. thaliana plastoglobules (1.0%, 1.0%, and 0.6% in N. benthamiana vs 5.2%, 9.6%, and 4.9% in A. thaliana, respectively).

We found that over half of the N. benthamiana endogenous proteome changed in abundance when either AtFBN1aWT-YFP or AtFBN1aΔ17α-YFP was localized on plastoglobules (Table 2). We considered proteins as putatively recruited to plastoglobules by AtFBN1a if they were increased in abundance in AtFBN1aWT-YFP samples but increased to a lesser extent (or not at all) in AtFBN1aΔ17α-YFP samples. Conversely, we considered proteins as putatively outcompeted on the plastoglobules by AtFBN1a if they were decreased in abundance in AtFBN1aWT-YFP samples but decreased to a lesser extent (or not at all) in AtFBN1aΔ17α-YFP samples. According to these guidelines, we identified two proteins that were putatively outcompeted by AtFBN1a: (i) UbiE-like methyltransferase 2a (NbUbiMT2a) and (ii) UbiE-like methyltransferase 2b (NbUbiMT2b) (Fig. 4b, Table 2). Likewise, we identified five proteins that were putatively recruited to the plastoglobule by AtFBN1a: (i) fructose bis-phosphate aldolase 2 (NbFBA2), (ii) NbCCD4, (iii) NbFBN7a, (iv) NbABC1K9, and (v) NbSOUL4 (Fig. 4c and Table 2). The strongest recruitment appeared to be for NbFBA2, which was increased in abundance three-fold specifically in the presence of AtFBN1aWT-YFP. Notably, our results provided some support for possible recruitment of NbABC1K3 as well, although the low abundance of the protein meant the difference between AtFBN1aWT-YFP and cTPAtRbcS-YFP was not statistically significant. Curiously, our results indicated that NbPG18 was lost from plastoglobules when expressing AtFBN1aΔ17α-YFP but unchanged when expressing the wild-type variant. It is not clear what relationship the deletion variant of AtFBN1a may have to NbPG18 localization. Collectively, our proteomic investigation uncovered evidence that ectopic expression of AtFBN1a alters the plastoglobule proteome, at least in part via protein recruitment and competition.

FBN proteins display lipid-specific binding that is affected by loss of the AH insert

The lipocalin-like domain, characteristic of the FBN family, is capable of binding a wide array of small hydrophobic compounds, particularly free FAs, and may function in lipid transport between plastoglobules and the other plastid membrane systems (Charron et al. 2005, Grzyb et al. 2006, Lakshmi et al. 2015, Schiefner and Skerra 2015, Herrera-Tequia et al. 2025). While the lipocalin-like sequence of FBNs does not share all the characteristic motifs of the canonical lipocalin domain (Flower 1996)—for example, the large loop L1 is absent in FBNs—the eight-stranded β-barrel structure is reproduced in the AlphaFold2 and RoseTTAFold structural models and the GxW motif is conserved at the bottom of the barrel at the beginning of the βA strand. To investigate the general lipid-binding characteristics of AtFBN1a and AtFBN7a, and the impact of the Δ17α sequence variants lacking the core of the predicted AHs, we employed in vitro assays using heterologously expressed protein with the P-96 polarity-sensitive fluorescent probe (Li et al. 2019). P-96 fluoresces strongly in a hydrophobic environment, provided by the interior of the AtFBN β-barrels. Competitive binding by an interacting lipid species will dislodge the P-96, reducing the fluorescence signal and giving a quantitative readout of lipid-binding efficacy. Based on the binding specificities of other lipocalin domains that are typically characterized by the binding of FAs, we assayed a series of free FAs, including saturated and unsaturated acyl chains of varying length, as well as phosphatidic acid and diacylglycerol (DAG) species.

The secondary structure of heterologously expressed proteins was validated using CD spectroscopy. The experimentally calculated secondary structure of each protein closely aligned with prediction based on the AlphaFold2 models (Table 3). Further, the deletion variants (Δ17α of both proteins) showed the expected reduction in proportion of the helical structure. Because CD spectra were collected in aqueous solution in the absence of liposome, we conclude that the AHs adopt a stable helical structure even in the absence of membrane. AtFBN1aWT showed a clear preference for unsaturated FAs, with especially effective binding of C18:1 (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S6). Notably, the binding efficacy of lipids was impaired in the absence of the AH. AtFBN7aWT also displayed a clear preference for C18:1, although its binding efficacy was less than that of AtFBN1aWT. Strikingly, the loss of the AH from AtFBN7a promoted the binding of lipids, the opposite effect of AH deletion in AtFBN1a.

Discussion

The mechanism(s) by which specific proteins associate with plastoglobules remains obscure. In light of the plastoglobule’s monolayer structure enclosing a neutral lipid core, transmembrane helices cannot be accommodated; rather, associated proteins should presumably interact in a monotopic fashion, either peripherally or via hairpin loop. But how can proteins distinguish between plastoglobules and the other membrane systems of the chloroplast: the extensive internal thylakoid membranes and the encompassing inner envelope membrane? In the present work, we have demonstrated the significance of a conserved AH in driving plastoglobule localization of two members of the FBN family.

Our discovery that the AH insert of AtFBN1a is responsible for direct interaction with plastoglobules is consistent with our previous study, in which we used protease shaving of isolated plastoglobules to map exposed and protected regions of the protein (Espinoza-Corral et al. 2021a). This work suggested a tryptic cleavage site between the α-helical pair and the β-barrel, which, together with phosphosites on either side of the α-helical pair identified from publicly available phosphoproteomic datasets, led us to propose that the α-helical pair is positioned distal to the plastoglobule surface and the AH insert in contact with the plastoglobule. In work by Vidi et al. (2007), confocal microscopy of a series of truncated variants of AtFBN7a indicated that nearly the entire protein sequence was necessary for plastoglobule association (residues 1–290 of the full-length AtFBN7a) (Vidi et al. 2007). Shorter variants that still retained the AH insert (sequences 224–250 of the full-length AtFBN7a) appeared to aggregate within the chloroplast and failed to associate with plastoglobules. From our AlphaFold2-modeled structure, we can see that those variants would have disrupted the β-barrel, likely leading to the observed aggregation. Thus, the truncation study is consistent with our reported significance of the AH inserts and indicates that a targeted deletion of the AH and flanking sequence is necessary to abolish plastoglobule localization while maintaining the overall protein structure and preventing aggregation.

There is precedent for the use of AHs to target proteins to cytosolic LDs (Kory et al. 2016, Mirheydari et al. 2016, Rowe et al. 2016, Copic et al. 2018, Prevost et al. 2018). However, there are a number of important distinctions between plastoglobules and cytosolic LDs that may confound the direct exchange of targeting principles (Lundquist et al. 2020). First, the polar lipid composition of plastoglobules is comprised of neutral galactolipids (Hansmann and Sitte 1982, Steinmuller and Tevini 1985, Tevini and Steinmuller 1985, Devadasu et al. 2025a, 2025b), in contrast to the phospholipid-rich nature of cytosolic LDs, which are abundant in phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine, while less abundant anionic components such as phosphatidylinositol and phosphatidylserine confer negative charge to the membrane (Tauchi-Sato et al. 2002, Krahmer et al. 2011, Penno et al. 2013). Interactions between the negative charges of the cytosolic LD surface and positively charged residues located at the hydrophobic–hydrophilic interface of AHs can be important for AH interaction with cytosolic LDs (Drin et al. 2007). However, such interactions would seemingly not be significant on the plastoglobule surface with their only limited negative charge from negligible levels of SQDG or phosphatidylglycerol (Devadasu et al. 2025a, 2025b). Second, the composition of the neutral lipid core is highly distinct between cytosolic LDs and plastoglobules. Whereas cytosolic LDs are dominated by TAG and/or sterol ester compounds (Gao et al. 2019, Lundquist et al. 2020, Kim et al. 2022b), plastoglobules lack sterols, and are enriched in phytyl esters and prenyllipids (Gaude et al. 2007, Lundquist et al. 2013, Van Wijk and Kessler 2017, Devadasu et al. 2025b). Specific interactions between protein and TAG can promote protein association on cytosolic LDs (Caillon et al. 2020, Chorlay and Thiam 2020); different protein–lipid interactions may drive targeting to plastoglobules. Third, the smaller size of the plastoglobules (often ≤70 nm in diameter) compared to that of cytosolic LDs (sometimes >1000 nm in diameter) introduces differing degrees of curvature to LD surfaces, which can affect the magnitude and nature of the lipid-packing defects. Thus, the underlying neutral lipids may be particularly susceptible to emergence on the plastoglobule surface.

Consistent with the multiple distinctions between plastoglobules and cytosolic LDs, bulky hydrophobic side chains are conspicuously absent from the AHs of plastoglobule-targeted FBNs. This contrasts with the AHs of cytosolic LD proteins in which bulky hydrophobic side chains are typically present and are important for proper association (Monje-Galvan and Klauda 2018, Prevost et al. 2018, Dhiman et al. 2020, Kim et al. 2021, Zouiouich et al. 2022). Importantly, our HeliQuest analyses of peripheral envelope membrane proteins are also enriched in predicted AHs (Supplementary Fig. S1). However, as with cytosolic LDs, we find that all the predicted AHs of the peripheral envelope proteins contain at least one—and typically multiple—bulky hydrophobic side chains (Supplementary Data File S2). This indicates that the absence of bulky hydrophobic side chains within AHs may be the distinguishing feature of AH-mediated plastoglobule association. On the other hand, FBN AHs share an enrichment of charged polar side chains, particularly near the hydrophobic–hydrophilic interface (Fig. 1), as seen with the ALPS-like AHs found in curvature-sensing proteins of cytosolic LDs (Drin et al. 2007, Drin and Antonny 2010). This indicates that a similar principle may be used for curvature sensing between cytosolic LDs and plastoglobules. Collectively, the sequences of the FBN AHs and the distinct size and composition of plastoglobules underline the likelihood that biophysical principles differ to some extent between plastoglobules and cytosolic LD protein association.

Our use of MD simulations supports the direct interaction of the AH inserts with the plastoglobule, and the importance of lipid-packing defects on the membrane surface to foster this interaction. However, there are important caveats that must be taken into consideration when drawing conclusions from these simulations. To model a large and curved surface efficiently, we approximate it as a planar bilayer containing the monoacyl MGMG, which mimics the presumed lipid-packing defects that would arise in a curved surface. This is seen directly in the simulations, where the AH insert makes more contacts in the defect-rich plastoglobule mimic compared to the planar thylakoid membrane (Fig. 3b). The presence of nonnatural MGMG lipids may have unexpected secondary effects on the behavior of AtFBN1a, although we predict these will be minor, as the monogalacto headgroup that directly interacts with AtFBN1a is identical to native lipids. The atomic representation for the neutral lipid core is another key limitation, as a quantitative view of the neutral lipid core is still lacking. Plastoglobules are thought to contain TAG, DAG, phytyl esters, and xanthophyll esters, among other possible lipid species. However, the relative proportions of the various neutral lipids, and how these proportions change across environmental or developmental conditions, are unknown. We elected to create a simplified core comprising solely TAG and DAG that better aligns with MD simulation data from cytosolic LDs and establishes a foundation from which the influence of differing neutral lipid compositions can subsequently be systematically explored (Kim et al. 2022c, Sapia and Vanni 2024, Whitehead et al. 2025). We also must note that even with multiple simulation replicates, contact profiles are not perfectly consistent, as shown in Fig. 3b. This indicates that 1 μs is not enough to fully sample rotational degrees of freedom for protein–membrane contacts. Longer simulation times may be necessary to establish stabilizing interactions between the protein and membrane, including the putative specific polar and hydrophobic interactions. This would be valuable in understanding the molecular details of AH interactions on the plastoglobule surface by establishing the roles of specific residues, hypothesizing effects from the introduction of bulky hydrophobic side chains, and estimating the strength of hydrophobic interactions in AH insertions.

The position of the AH insert near the opening of the β-barrel may hold implications for protein function and specific lipid binding. Our specific lipid-binding assays indicate that the presence or absence of the AH insert affects the specific lipid-binding strength, albeit in opposing directions among the two tested proteins. This puzzling result may lie in the different AlphaFold2-predicted structures of the AH inserts of AtFBN1a, which has a single contiguous AH, and AtFBN7a, which has two noncontiguous AHs. The tested deletion constructs remove all of the AtFBN1a AH but only the upstream portion of the AtFBN7a AH. It may be that the AH inserts represent lids covering the opening of the hydrophobic cavity of the β-barrel that prevent specific lipid binding until interaction with the membrane causes the AH insert to move. This would present the AH insert as a sort of gate regulating specific lipid binding within the hydrophobic cavity that is only opened when the AH lid interacts with the proper membrane surface. The specific lipid-binding capacity of the FBNs also raises the question of what purpose specific lipid binding may serve, including possible involvement in plastoglobule targeting. Arguing against a role in plastoglobule targeting, we show that Δ17α variants of AtFBN1a and AtFBN7a hold opposing effects on the lipid-binding strength (Fig. 5) but similarly impair plastoglobule association (Fig. 2), observations inconsistent with a role for lipid binding in plastoglobule targeting. More plausibly, FBNs may function as lipid transporters, shuttling specific lipid species between membrane systems of the chloroplast, as proposed previously (Singh and Mcnellis 2011). Thus, we argue that plastoglobule targeting of AtFBN1a and AtFBN7a is solely mediated by AH interaction along the plastoglobule surface and does not involve any specific lipid-binding activity.

Ectopic targeting of the AtFBN1a construct allowed us to investigate protein-binding competition and recruitment at the plastoglobule. In particular, the two N. benthamiana UbiE methyltransferases appeared to be outcompeted by AtFBN1a (Fig. 4). Notably, these two proteins were among the few plastoglobule proteins without a predicted AH. While the mechanism by which the UbiE methyltransferase interacts with plastoglobules is unknown, the absence of an AH may account for its ability to be outcompeted. Alternative methods of plastoglobule targeting may be utilized by proteins lacking a suitable AH, such as protein–protein interactions (Torres-Romero et al. 2022, Kim et al. 2022a, Li et al. 2023a, 2023b). Conversely, accumulation of AtFBN1a was associated with increased levels of FBA2. This aligns with a recent omics investigation of Z. mays plastoglobules that identified a correlation between ZmFBN1a/ZmFBN1b and ZmFBN2 (Devadasu et al. 2025a), which, combined with our observations described here, suggests a physical or functional connection between these proteins that spans monocots and dicots.

Materials and Methods

Transient expression in N. benthamiana

The full-length FBNs were cloned into either pEARLEYGate101 or pEARLEYGate102 vectors to transiently express each protein with a C-terminal fluorescent protein (FP) tag. The chloroplast transit peptide (cTP) of AtRbcL and the full-length and truncated AtFBN1a variants, including their cTPs, were cloned as coding sequences into pEARLEYGate101, which provided a C-terminal enhanced yellow fluorescent protein (EYFP) tag driven by the 35S promoter. AtFBN7a and its mutated variants, including their cTP, were cloned as coding sequences into the pEARLEYGate102 vector, which provided a C-terminal enhanced cyan fluorescent protein (ECFP) tag driven by the 35S promoter. Constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 cells. Different combinations of constructs were infiltrated into 4–5-week-old N. benthamiana plants grown on Arabidopsis Mix soil at 25°C, 100 μmol m−2 s−1, and a 16-/8-h light/dark photoperiod. Infiltration of whole N. benthamiana plants was performed by hand syringe or applying vacuum, as described in Bibik et al. (2022).

Subcellular fractionation and immunoblotting

From N. benthamiana plants expressing AtFBN1a, and AtFBN7a variants, plastoglobules and thylakoid membranes were isolated according to Shivaiah et al. (2022). Briefly, plastoglobules were separated using 0.2 M sucrose in Medium R solution (50 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES)-KOH pH 8.0 and 5 mM MgCl2 with phosphatase and protease inhibitors) after sonicating thylakoid fractions. The floating plastoglobules were further purified using a sucrose gradient ranging from 0.7 M to 0 mM sucrose. Purified plastoglobules were normalized by measuring the OD700.

For isolations of total leaf and thylakoid, samples were normalized based on total chlorophyll content and estimated using acetone extraction and spectrophotometry as described in Lichtenthaler (1987). For plastoglobule isolations, samples were normalized based on OD700 values. For total leaf, thylakoid, envelope, and stroma isolation, samples were normalized based on total protein content, estimated using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, USA). Equal amounts of sample were added to Laemmli sample buffer before heating at 65°C for 15 min. Samples were then run on a 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) gel and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (Cytiva, Amersham, UK). Membranes were incubated with appropriate primary antibodies and then visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA).

Protein structure modeling and molecular dynamics simulations

Alphafold2 was used to generate a protein structure from the amino acid sequence of AtFBN1a (Jumper et al. 2021). This protein model was placed above a membrane model (in which each leaflet contained 400 lipids) in six different orientations and simulated using NAMD 3.0a12 to capture binding interactions (Phillips et al. 2020). The bound states were analyzed to quantify the interactions between AtFBN1a and the membrane using VMD (Humphrey et al. 1996), highlighting the amino acids most responsible for their interaction.

Three distinct protein–membrane combinations were built to compare the protein–membrane interactions depending on the biological context: (i) AtFBN1a and plastoglobule trilayer, (ii) AtFBN1a and a thylakoid bilayer, and (iii) AtFBN1a and a ``thylakoid trilayer'' consisting of the bilayer composition from (ii) with a TAG and DAG inner core as in (i). The thylakoid bilayer was composed of a combination of MGDG, DGDG, and SQDG (Supplementary Table S4) to align with the literature on thylakoid membrane composition (Gounaris et al. 2001). Initial models of the thylakoid membrane were overly ordered and rigid. To improve fluidity, SQDG and saturated MGDG lipids were given 12 carbon acyl tails. The trilayer outer leaflet was composed of a mixture of MGDG and MGMG (Supplementary Table S4). MGMG was selected to increase packing defects in the plastoglobule membrane surface, to mirror the conditions of the curved plastoglobule membrane. A neutral lipid core was also included, consisting of TAG and DAG. For each of these combinations, six unique simulation systems were created by rotating the protein in 90-degree increments. Systems were solvated in water using Python scripts built for the system. After solvation, the system contained between ~370 000 (bilayer) and 300 000 (trilayer) atoms. The dimensions of the bilayer simulation box are 158.8 Å × 158.8 Å × 144.4 Å. The trilayer simulation box was 128 Å × 128 Å × 200 Å. The simulation box was sized to permit realistic protein dynamics unconstrained by periodic boundary effects while reducing the simulation cost. The system was briefly minimized and equilibrated using NAMD 2.14 to eliminate bad contacts (Phillips et al. 2020). The simulations were performed using NAMD 3.0a12 (Phillips et al. 2020) using explicit solvent and CHARMM36 carbohydrate, lipid, and protein force fields (Klauda et al. 2010, Vanommeslaeghe et al. 2010, Huang et al. 2017). In keeping with CHARMM standards, a 12-Å cutoff was used, together with the TIP3 water model (Jorgensen et al. 1983). Long-range electrostatics were handled using the particle mesh Ewald method with 1.2-Å grid spacing (Essmann et al. 1995, Kuhne et al. 2020). The system was simulated in an NPT ensemble using a Langevin thermostat and barostat (Bussi et al. 2007). To enable 2-fs time steps, we used the SETTLE algorithm to fix hydrogen bond lengths (Miyamoto and Kollman 2004). The end simulation time for bilayer replicas was 1.5 μs and for trilayer replicas it was 1.0 μs. The simulations were carried out on the NCSA’s Delta computing cluster.

Analysis was conducted using Python VMD scripts built for the system to leverage NumPy (Harris et al. 2020) for efficient numerics and Matplotlib (Hunter 2007) for plotting. Most of the analysis was conducted by analyzing intermolecular contacts present in the simulation, which measure the number of times protein residues approached the membrane within a certain cutoff distance. Depending on the cutoff distance selected, contact counts can change significantly. For our analyses, we used a weighted contact definition that allows for fractional contacts at distances near the selected cutoff. The exponentially weighted native contact definition from Sheinerman and Brooks (Sheinerman and Brooks 3rd 1998) has been used in prior studies to assess membrane–protein contacts (Vermaas and Tajkhorshid 2014, 2017, Vermaas et al. 2018). LC–MS/MS proteomics and data analysis of plastoglobules

Isolated plastoglobules were prepared and analyzed by LC–MS/MS proteomics essentially as in Espinoza-Corral and Lundquist (2022). Lyophilized plastoglobules in triplicate (three biological replicates) were resuspended in 50 μL of 125 mM Tris, pH 6.8, 4% SDS, 20% glycerol, heated to 60°C for 5 min, and electrophoresed into a 12% Tris–HCl Criterion gel from BioRad at 50 V for 20 min or until the dye front moved just below the bottom of the sample wells. Electrophoresis was stopped and the gel was stained with Coomassie Blue protein stain until bands appeared. Protein bands were excised from the gel and digested according to Shevchenko et al. (1996), with modifications. Briefly, gel bands were dehydrated using 100% acetonitrile and incubated with 10 mM dithiothreitol in 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate, pH 8, at 56°C for 45 min, dehydrated again, and incubated in the dark with 50 mM iodoacetamide in 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate for 20 min. Gel bands were then washed with ammonium bicarbonate and dehydrated again. Sequencing-grade modified trypsin was prepared to 0.01 μg/μL in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate and 100 μL of this was added to each gel band so that the gel was completely submerged. Bands were then incubated at 37°C overnight. Peptides were extracted from the gel by water bath sonication in a solution of 60% acetonitrile/1% trifluoroacetic acid and vacuum-dried to 20 μL. Injections of 5 μL were automatically made using a Thermo (www.thermo.com) EASY-nLC 1200 onto a Thermo Acclaim PepMap RSLC 0.1 mm × 20 mm C18 trapping column and washed for 5 min with buffer A. Bound peptides were then eluted over 65 min onto a Thermo Acclaim PepMap RSLC 0.075 mm × 500 mm resolving column with a gradient of 8% B to 40% B in 54 min, ramping to 90% B at 55 min, and held at 90% B for the duration of the run (buffer A = 99.9% water/0.1% formic acid; buffer B = 80% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid/19.9% water) at a constant flow rate of 300 nL/min. The column was maintained at a constant temperature of 50°C using an integrated column oven (PRSO-V2, Sonation GmbH). Eluted peptides were sprayed into a ThermoScientific Q-Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (www.thermo.com) using a FlexSpray spray ion source. Survey scans were taken in an Orbitrap (60 000 resolution, determined at m/z 200), and the top 15 ions in each survey scan were then subjected to automatic higher-energy collisional dissociation with fragment spectra acquired at 7500 resolution.

The resulting MS/MS spectra were converted to peak lists using MaxQuant v2.4.5.0 and searched against a protein database using the Andromeda search algorithm, a part of the MaxQuant environment (Cox and Mann 2008, Cox et al. 2011). LC–MS/MS .raw files from samples expressing each protein variant were searched separately using a protein database containing all Nicotiana tabacum protein sequences available from UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb?query=nicotiana+tabacum; 82 358 proteins) with common laboratory contaminants and the respective AtFBN protein sequence appended. Oxidation of methionine, deamidation of asparagine, and N-terminal and lysine acetylation were set as variable modifications; carbamidomethylation was set as a fixed modification. Digestion mode was trypsin/P with a maximum of two missed cleavages. The MS/MS tolerance of the first search was 20 ppm, and that of the main search was 4.5 ppm, with individualized peptide mass tolerance selected. The false discovery rate (FDR) at peptide spectrum match and protein levels was set as 0.01, estimated using the reverse decoy method (Elias and Gygi 2007). The LFQ intensity was determined as the total sum of the peak of Unique and Razor peptides with Fast LFQ selected. The best gene model for each protein group was identified as having the highest number of Unique + Razor Peptide counts when compiled across all the experiments. In the case of a tie, the lowest gene model number was selected, regardless of whether the protein group contained gene models from one or more gene loci.

The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/pride/) (Perez-Riverol et al. 2022) in MIAPE-compliant format with the dataset identifier PXD045458.

Bacterial expression and purification of FBN proteins

Open reading frames of the mature sequences of AtFBN1a and AtFBN7a, without predicted N-terminal cTPs, were amplified by PCR and then cloned into pSMT3 vector, which provided an N-terminal 6X-His tag followed by a SUMO tag. Details of restriction sites and primers are provided in Supplementary Table S3. The plasmid constructs were transformed into BL21 (DE3) strain. An initial starter culture was grown at 37°C overnight and then 5 mL starter culture was added into 500 mL of Luria broth (LB) culture. The culture was induced using 0.1 mM IPTG (final concentration) at an OD600 of 0.6–0.8 and incubated at 16°C or 22°C for 16 h. The harvested cells were resuspended using the lysis buffer 50 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.6, 250 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 0.5% Triton X-100, and 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) and lysed using sonication. The lysed sample was centrifuged at 18 000 rpm for 30 min at 4°C. The supernatant was filtered using a 0.45 μm nitrocellulose filter and loaded onto the Nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) resin, which was pre-equilibrated with buffer A containing 50 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 8.3, 250 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT. Consecutively, the resin was washed using buffer A containing 20, 40, and 60 mM imidazole. The tagged proteins were eluted using buffer A containing 250 mM imidazole. The eluants were dialyzed against buffer containing 50 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 8.3, 250 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 10% (w/v) glycerol, 5% (w/v) glycerol, and 1 mM EDTA. The proteins were concentrated using Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filters (Millipore).

P-96 fluorescence-based lipid-binding assays

The lipid-binding capability of FBNs was evaluated by monitoring the displacement of the fluorescently labeled FA probe, P-96, i.e. 1-pyrenedodecanoic acid (Naylor et al. 1991). The experiments were conducted with a Varian Cary Eclipse Fluorescence Spectrophotometer at a temperature of 25°C in a quartz fluorometer cell (Starna Cells Inc., Atascadero, CA, USA). The excitation and emission wavelengths were set at 342 and 377 nm, respectively. We first estimated the Kd of P-96 with each protein variant by titrating increasing amounts of P-96 in a measurement buffer containing 10 mM MOPS at pH 7.2 (Supplementary Fig. S6). Estimation of Kd was performed in the GraphPad Prism software v. 10.0.2.

Competition assays with each tested ligand were then performed using an amount 10 times greater than the calculated Kd of P-96. The lipids used for the experiments were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, USA, Avanti Polar Lipids, USA, and Cayman Chemical, USA. To determine the binding capability, the fluorescence of P-96 was measured in two scenarios using a measurement buffer containing 175 mM mannitol, 0.5 mM K2SO4, 0.5 mM CaCl2, and 5 mM MES at pH 7.0, which was found to give a lower background fluorescence (Buhot et al. 2004): first, without any proteins present (Q0) and, secondly, with the addition of 0.5 μM of protein (Qp). Subsequently, P-96 was incubated with FAs for 1 min before measuring fluorescence (F0). Then, 0.5 μM of protein was added, and the fluorescence was measured after an additional 2 min (F). The results were expressed as the percentage of fluorescence from the protein–P96 complex, calculated as (F − F0)/(Qp − Q0).

Circular dichroism spectroscopy

Protein concentrations were determined employing the Pierce™ BCA protein quantification kit (Thermo Scientific, IL, USA). Subsequently, 300 μL of a 6 μM protein solution in a 10 mM phosphate buffer at pH 7.3 was introduced into a Hellma 1 mm QS cuvette, sealed with a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) stopper. A CD spectrum was then recorded using a JASCO J-815 CD Spectrometer, with the wavelength range set between 190 and 260 nm. Data were collected in increments of 1-nm wavelength, with an averaging time of 1.5 s per wavelength point. Protein secondary structure was determined by analyzing the data derived from an average of five scans using the K2D3 server (http://cbdm-01.zdv.uni-mainz.de/∼andrade/k2d3/).
