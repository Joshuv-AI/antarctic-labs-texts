Identifying Bias at Scale in Clinical Notes Using Large Language Models
Authors: Donald U Apakama, Kim-Anh-Nhi Nguyen, Daphnee Hyppolite, Shelly Soffer, Aya Mudrik, Emilia Ling, Akini Moses, Ivanka Temnycky, Allison Glasser, Rebecca Anderson, Prathamesh Parchure, Evajoyce Woullard, Masoud Edalati, Lili Chan, Clair Kronk, Robert Freeman, Arash Kia, Prem Timsina, Matthew A Levin, Rohan Khera, Patricia Kovatch, Alexander W Charney, Brendan G Carr, Lynne D Richardson, Carol R Horowitz, Eyal Klang, Girish N Nadkarni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate whether generative pretrained transformer (GPT)-4 can detect and revise biased language in emergency department (ED) notes, against human-adjudicated gold-standard labels, and to identify modifiable factors associated with biased documentation.Patients and MethodsWe randomly sampled 50,000 ED medical and nursing notes from the Mount Sinai Health System (January 1, 2023, to December 31, 2023). We also randomly sampled 500 discharge notes from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV database. The GPT-4 flagged 4 types of bias: discrediting, stigmatizing/labeling, judgmental, and stereotyping. Two human reviewers verified model detections. We used multivariable logistic regression to examine associations between bias and health care utilization, presenting problems (eg, substance use), shift timing, and provider type. We then asked physicians to rate GPT-4’s proposed language revisions on a 10-point scale.ResultsThe GPT-4 showed 97.6% sensitivity and 85.7% specificity compared with the human review. Biased language appeared in 6.5% (3229 of 50,000) of Mount Sinai notes and 7.4% (37 of 500) of Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV notes. In adjusted models, frequent health care utilization (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 2.85; 95% CI, 1.95-4.17), substance use presentations (aOR, 3.09; 95% CI, 2.51-3.80), and overnight shifts (aOR, 1.37; 95% CI, 1.23-1.52) showed elevated odds of biased documentation. Physicians were more likely to include bias than nurses (aOR, 2.26; 95% CI, 2.07-2.46); GPT-4’s recommended revisions received mean physician ratings above 9 of 10.ConclusionThe study showed that GPT-4 accurately detects biased language in clinical notes, identifies modifiable contributors to that bias, and delivers physician-endorsed revisions. This approach may help mitigate documentation bias and reduce disparities in care.

Biased language in clinical documentation compromises care and perpetuates systemic bias.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 It enters electronic health records (EHRs) through providers’ implicit biases and subjective assessments. It can stigmatize patients—especially those from marginalized groups—with negative descriptors such as noncompliant, aggressive, or resistant.3,6, 7, 8 These terms are perceived as undesirable by patients, leading to feelings of being judged and disrespected.9, 10, 11, 12 Furthermore, their use perpetuates negative attitudes among providers, influencing decision making and leading to suboptimal care.6,13 This exacerbates disparities among marginalized populations and erodes patient trust, a concern heightened by increased patient access to EHRs under the 21st Century Cures Act.10,14

Addressing this issue requires scalable detection across settings.15,16 Manual review limits prior work.17,18 Large language models (LLMs) such as OpenAI’s generative pretrained transformer (GPT)-4 can offer a solution by enabling automated detection of biased languages.19, 20, 21, 22, 23 The emergency department (ED), as a primary point of patient contact involving diverse conditions and populations, offers an opportunity to study explicit biases in documentation.24,25 Factors such as overnight shifts, clinical presentation, and frequency of ED utilization may influence how providers document these encounters.18,26,27

In this study, we aimed to evaluate whether GPT-4 can detect and revise biased language in ED notes. Using this pipeline, we identified modifiable factors associated with bias in notes.

Patients and Methods

Study Design

We used the Mount Sinai Health System (MSHS) biased language codebook, developed by the FAIR Documentation Workgroup, to categorize documentation into discrediting, stigmatizing/labeling, judgmental, and stereotyping language (Supplemental Figure 1, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). We applied GPT-4 to identify these categories at scale. Supplemental Figure 2 (available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/) shows the progression from codebook to AI implementation. The study received approval from the institutional review board of the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai.

We prespecified 5 research questions: whether GPT-4 accurately identifies biased language relative to human-adjudicated gold-standard labels in a validation subset, reporting sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) with 95% CIs; whether model-suggested revisions reduce biased language while preserving clinical meaning, based on clinician ratings; the prevalence of biased documentation and its distribution across categories in the 50,000 MSHS ED notes; which patient, encounter, and documentation features are associated with biased documentation; and whether performance and category distribution generalize to the deidentified MIMIC-IV corpus.

Study Population

We used 50,000 random adult MSHS ED notes from 2023. We also validated on a public data set using 500 Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC-IV) discharge summaries.28,29

For the MSHS data set, we started with 424,427 ED visits, and after applying exclusion criteria (detailed in Supplemental Appendix, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/), retained 230,967 unique patient records from which we randomly sampled 50,000 patients. One ED note was randomly selected for each unique patient. For the MIMIC-IV data set, 500 discharge summaries were randomly selected from 145,915 unique patient records.

LLM Access and Protected Health Information Handling

Notes were extracted from the MSHS Epic EHR and processed on secure institutional servers under Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act. Further, GPT-4 (0613) with default hyperparameters was accessed via an enterprise endpoint with encrypted transport and access controls; no study data were retained outside institutional systems, and vendor training use was not permitted. The full prompt appears in Supplemental Table 1 (available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). MIMIC-IV analyses used a deidentified data set. Full infrastructure and extract, transform, and load details are provided in Supplemental Appendix (available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/).

Prompt Refinement

We engineered prompts by assigning GPT-4 the role of an emergency physician specialized in recognizing biased documentation. We included definitions of the 4 bias categories: discrediting, stigmatizing/labeling, judgmental, and stereotyping—based on biased language established in prior research and institutional policies.1,4,7,11,12,30

We evaluated GPT-4 for identifying and categorizing bias and suggesting unbiased alternatives. We made iterative refinements by reviewing GPT-4 performance on 100 EHR sentences, split evenly between biased and unbiased text. These examples were solely used for prompt optimization and were not used in the final cohort. We compared prompt outputs with assessments from human reviewers with high interrater reliability (≥0.65). We quantified concordance using Cohen κ, Fleiss κ, and percentage agreement. The prompt underwent 3 rounds of iteration until GPT consistently performed comparably with that of reviewers (Supplemental Figure 3, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). We then applied the final prompt to MSHS ED notes and MIMIC-IV discharge summaries. Further, GPT-4 was used to flag potential biases in clinical notes. For final prompt text, see Supplemental Table 1.

We used the GPT-4 application programming interface to identify and categorize bias in clinical notes, prompting it to flag bias and its subtypes. Application programming interface responses in JSON format were parsed to tabulate biases. Details of data processing and model specifications are provided in the Supplemental Appendix.

Ground Truth and Annotation

To create gold-standard labels for evaluation, 2 clinician reviewers (A.Moses., I.Temnycky.) independently annotated sentence– or phrase-level spans using the MSHS codebook; disagreements were resolved by discussion to consensus, and consensus labels served as ground truth. Label quality was assessed before adjudication using Cohen κ and percentage agreement (Supplemental Figure 4, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). In the final annotation round, discrediting showed the highest reliability (κ=0.81), while stereotyping, a rarer and more context-dependent class, had κ of 0.35 with 89% percentage agreement. During prompt iteration, human reviewer interrater reliability was ≥0.65 across categories.

Manual Validation

To validate GPT-4 output, 200 MSHS, and 50 MIMIC-IV charts were randomly selected, evenly split between no bias and with bias labels. Reviewers (A.M. and I.T.) independently evaluated the output for accuracy, resolving disagreements through discussion (Supplemental Figure 4). They also assessed GPT-4’s language improvement suggestions on a 10-point scale (scores: 0, much worse; 5, no change or similar; 10, much improved) (Supplemental Table 2, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). Consensus human labels were used as the gold-standard to compute GPT-4 sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV in the validation subset.

Modifying Feature Selection

We identified potentially modifiable features associated with documentation bias through literature review and institutional experience.6,7,11,13,31,32 These included clinical factors (chief symptom, timing of ED presentation), utilization patterns (frequency of ED visits), and provider characteristics (role and shift timing). Additionally, we collected data on clinical conditions and substance use history that prior research has shown may influence documentation practices.33, 34, 35 The complete list of features analyzed is available in Supplemental Table 3 (available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/).

Statistical Analyses

For the MSHS cohort, we conducted univariate analyses using χ2 tests to identify potential associations between flagged biases and clinical features. Multivariate logistic regression was then performed to evaluate these associations, adjusting for confounders selected based on clinical relevance and prior literature.2,6,13,16,17,27,33 Key independent variables included clinical factors (eg, chief symptom and timing of ED presentation), health care utilization patterns (eg, frequency of ED visits), and provider characteristics (eg, role and shift timing). To assess GPT-4’s performance against human reviewers, we calculated sensitivity, specificity, PPVs, and NPVs with Wilson score 95% CIs, which account for the asymmetric nature of proportions near boundaries and are more accurate for small sample sizes. All analyses were conducted using Python 3.9, with statistical significance set at a 2-sided P value of <.05. Details of the feature selection process and included variables are provided in Supplemental Table 3. For multivariable logistic regression models, we assessed goodness of fit using the Hosmer-Lemeshow χ2 test with 10 groups and report McFadden pseudo-R2 and the Brier score. Model discrimination is summarized with area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve and 95% CIs; model-specific diagnostics are provided in Supplemental Appendix.

Data Set Characteristics and Overall Bias Detection

Analysis of 50,000 MSHS ED notes revealed bias in 3229 (6.5%) notes. The most common types were discrediting language (1989, 4.0%), stigmatizing/labeling (1678, 3.4%), judgmental language (1593, 3.2%), and stereotyping (281, 0.6%) (Supplemental Figure 5, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). A similar analysis of 500 MIMIC-IV discharge notes showed comparable results with an overall bias rate of 7.4% (37 notes) (Supplemental Figure 6, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/).

Performance Metrics by Bias Type at MSHS

The GPT-4 demonstrated strong performance, against the human gold-standard, with an overall sensitivity of 97.6% (82/84; 95% CI, 91.7%-99.3%) and specificity of 85.7% (143/167; 95% CI, 77.4%-91.3%). Performance varied by bias type, with judgmental bias showing the highest sensitivity (43/44; 97.7%; 95% CI, 87.7%%-99.6%) and strong specificity (132/140; 94.3%, 95% CI, 86.6%-97.7%). Positive predictive values ranged from 42.9% (6/14; 95% CI, 15.8%-75.0%) for stereotyping to 82.4% (42/51; 95% CI, 69.3%-90.7%) for judgmental bias, whereas NPVs remained high across categories (97.1%-99.3%; 95% CI, 89.3%-99.9%) (Table 2).

Sociodemographic Features

In examining sociodemographic features, housing status emerged as one of the strongest predictors of biased language in documentation. Undomiciled patients faced substantially higher rates of bias in univariate analysis (622 of 2303 [27.0%] vs 2607 of 47,697 [5.5%], an association that remained robust after multivariate adjustment (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 2.37; 95% CI, 2.10-2.67; P<.001). Insurance type also demonstrated significant associations, with commercially insured patients (730 of 12.87 [5.7%]) having lower odds of experiencing biased language than those with Medicaid coverage (1650 of 18,531 [8.9%]) (aOR, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.51-0.65; P<.001). Male patients (2006 of 22,977 [8.7%]) showed significantly higher odds of experiencing bias than female patients (1223 of 27,019 [4.5%]) (aOR, 1.58; 95% CI, 1.41-1.77; P<.001), whereas married patients showed lower odds (327 of 11,378 [2.9%]) than single patients (2378 of 28,788 [8.3%]) (aOR, 0.60; 95% CI, 0.53-0.68; P<.001). Although other demographic factors showed significant associations in univariate analysis, these relationships were largely attenuated after multivariate adjustment, suggesting that modifiable socioeconomic factors may be more influential in documentation bias than immutable demographic characteristics.

Health Care Utilization

In univariate analysis, ED utilization showed the strongest gradient effect with documentation bias. Patients with over 100 previous visits experienced a 21.0% bias rate (104 of 405) compared with 5.2% (1872 of 36,024) for those with 0 to 3 visits (P<.001). This relationship remained robust in multivariate analysis, with high-utilization patients showing nearly 3 times the odds of experiencing bias (aOR, 2.85; 95% CI, 1.95-4.17; P<.001). The strength of this association increased steadily with visit frequency, suggesting a dose-response relationship that persisted after adjustment for clinical and demographic factors. The primary model showed adequate fit (Hosmer-Lemeshow χ2[df=8], 6.80; P=.03; McFadden pseudo-R2=0.148; Brier=0.055; and area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve=0.78; 95% CI, 0.78-0.79); see Supplemental Table 8 (available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/) for all models.

Clinical Presentation

Initial univariate analysis revealed elevated bias rates for certain chief symptoms: substance use disorder (237 of 736 [32.2%]), psychiatric conditions (301 of 1204 [25.0%]), and glucose metabolism disorders (11 of 65 [16.9%]), compared with that for general medical symptoms (1111 of 44,341 [2.5%]; P<.001). These relationships maintained similar patterns in multivariate analysis, although with some attenuation: substance use disorder (178 of 553) (aOR, 3.09; 95% CI, 2.51-3.80), glucose metabolism (30 of 178) (aOR, 2.07; 95% CI, 1.35-3.17), and psychiatric conditions (198 of 1192) (aOR, 1.53; 95% CI, 1.29-1.81). Notably, the relative ranking of these associations remained consistent between univariate and multivariate analyses, suggesting robust relationships independent of other factors.

Health Care Delivery

Provider type showed significant associations in both analyses. In univariate analysis, physician notes contained higher rates of bias than nursing documentation (2350 of 24,979 [9.4%] vs (1299 of 25,021 [5.2%]; P<.001). This difference was amplified in multivariate analysis (aOR, 2.26; 95% CI, 2.07-2.46; P<.001). Similarly, overnight shifts showed elevated bias in both univariate (1441 of 15,010 [9.6%] vs 1788 of 33,750 [5.3%] for day shifts; P<.001) and multivariate analyses (aOR, 1.37; 95% CI, 1.23-1.52; P<.001), suggesting that care delivery timing impacts documentation practices independently of other factors.

Bias Subtypes

Analysis of specific bias categories revealed association with clinical presentations and care contexts. Stigmatizing/labeling language appeared most frequently in substance use disorder presentations (181 of 736 [24.6%]). Judgmental language predominated in notes with glucose metabolism disorders (9 of 65 [14.0%]). Discrediting language showed stronger associations with patterns of health care utilization, particularly among patients with increased ED visits. After multivariate adjustment, these subtype-specific associations remained significant but showed some attenuation, suggesting partial mediation through other clinical and systemic factors. Housing status emerged as a key predictor in both analyses. Univariate analysis showed that undomiciled patients faced higher bias rates 27.0% (622/2303) vs 5.5% (2607/47,697) for domiciled patients; P<.001. This relationship remained strong in multivariate analysis (aOR, 2.37; 95% CI, 2.10-2.67; P<.001), suggesting that housing status impacts documentation practices independent of other clinical and demographic factors.

Discussion

In this study, we assessed GPT-4’s ability to detect biased language in clinical documentation, examined the prevalence and modifiable factors of that bias, and evaluated physician acceptance of GPT-4’s suggested revisions.6,7 Prior work documented bias but relied on a manual review.3,7,12,13,36 We demonstrated that an LLM can effectively detect documentation bias with sensitivity and specificity comparable with human reviewers, offering a pathway to comprehensive bias surveillance, building on recent work showing LLMs’ potential for medical information extraction.20,36,37 Our analysis reveals that documentation bias occurs most frequently in modifiable contexts—during overnight shifts, in cases of high ED utilization, and encounters involving stigmatized conditions.12,17,18,25 Moreover, when bias is detected, GPT-4 can generate alternative, unbiased language that physicians rate as highly effective. These capabilities create new opportunities for both monitoring and improving documentation practices.20, 21, 22,31

This scalable strategy addresses a significant gap in large EHR data set bias detection and reveals clear patterns in health care delivery associated with biased documentation.24,36,38 Health care, specifically ED utilization, was the strongest predictor. Patients with increased ED visits experienced nearly 3 times the odds of bias. The timing of care delivery is also important, with overnight shifts associated with increased bias. This aligns with studies on provider fatigue and documentation quality.31,34,39 These temporal and utilization-based patterns suggest that system stressors may contribute to documentation bias more than previously recognized.40

Particularly concerning was the clustering of bias around substance use and psychiatric presentations, echoing stigma reported in mental health documentation.18,33,41 Thus, documentation bias in the EHR likely stems from implicit bias present in clinical encounters, which reflects and codifies structural and societal biases.21,42

Our study suggests that AI approaches could serve as accurate tools for documentation bias, enabling identification of patterns and trends in biased language use.36,37 This approach will allow health systems to strategically address documentation bias at a systemic level through targeted interventions.12,21,31,43 For example, our findings suggest that enhanced staffing and support during overnight shifts, specialized training for providers caring for high-utilization patients, and focused education around substance use and psychiatric presentations could meaningfully reduce documentation bias.11,24,31,34,35

Beyond detection, GPT-4 excelled in generating alternative, unbiased language for clinical documentation. It consistently received high ratings from physician reviewers across all bias categories. The model proved particularly effective at recasting judgmental and stigmatizing language while preserving essential clinical information. For instance, when encountering notes with stigmatizing language about substance use, GPT-4 suggested objective, person-first alternatives that maintained clinical accuracy but removed potentially harmful descriptors.4,22,42 This capability represents a significant advance over previous approaches that could identify bias but not offer improvements or scalability.6, 7, 8,13,21

However, we emphasize that the goal of automated debiasing suggestions is not to directly alter clinical notes but, rather, to provide clinicians with examples of more equitable documentation practices following recommendations from recent studies on reducing bias in clinical communication.16,21,22,31,40,43 Although LLMs can assist in identifying biased language, the subjective nature of bias detection means that these tools should be implemented within a framework that combines technological capabilities with human expertise.31,44 Such an approach acknowledges that technology alone cannot fully capture the nuances of bias in health care but, rather, serves to augment and support human judgment in promoting more equitable care delivery.45 By exposing providers to alternative language options during training, this approach could help reshape institutional communication norms while preserving clinical autonomy.16,22,23,33,45,46 The high acceptability ratings from physicians suggest that such suggestions could be readily integrated into clinical workflow.

It is important to acknowledge that LLMs may themselves harbor inherent biases derived from their training data.47, 48, 49, 50 In the context of detecting clinical bias, the LLM might inadvertently reflect these biases. Phenomena such as sycophancy bias—where models align their responses with presumed user expectations—can impact the LLM’s performance.47 Furthermore, the subjective nature of bias definitions and the complexity of clinical communication mean that a model trained at 1 time point might not fully capture evolving language norms. For instance, certain terms deeply embedded in EHRs, such as symptoms in chief symptom, were not flagged in this study despite their potentially problematic nature. Additionally, our approach did not assess the severity or frequency of bias within individual notes, likely underestimating the overall impact of biased language in clinical documentation.

From an implementation perspective, our study focused on EDs within New York City, potentially limiting generalizability to other health care settings. The predominance of English-speaking patients and overrepresentation of Medicare and Medicaid beneficiaries may not reflect the experiences of other populations. It also echoes concerns raised about representation in health care AI development.51,52 Furthermore, we could not fully account for all potential confounders in the complex relationship between health care delivery and documentation bias, a challenge noted in previous studies of clinical documentation practices.6,16,17,53 Variables such as provider experience, patient acuity, and department-specific documentation practices may influence bias patterns in ways our study could not capture.5

Additionally, missing data across key demographic variables limited our ability to draw conclusions about certain patient populations, particularly those with nonbinary gender identities or nonheterosexual orientations. Taken together, these limitations suggest that although LLMs offer promising tools for bias detection and mitigation, their implementation requires careful monitoring and ongoing validation.

Conclusion

Generative pretrained transformer 4 accurately detects biased language in clinical notes, identifies modifiable contributors to that bias, and delivers physician-endorsed revisions. This approach may help mitigate documentation bias and reduce disparities in care.
