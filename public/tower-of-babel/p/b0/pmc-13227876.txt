Plastid genomes and phylogenomic relationships in Cheilosoideae, a poorly known early-diverging subfamily of Euphorbiaceae
Authors: Zhao-Hui Ran, Hager R. Belal, Kenneth J. Wurdack, Ya-Lin Feng, Paul M. Kirika, Elijah Nyakudya, Mohamed Hamdy Amar, Sheng-Wei Wang, Neng Wei, Qing-Feng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cheilosoideae is the smallest and earliest-diverging of the four subfamilies of Euphorbiaceae, with seven species grouped in two morphologically similar paleotropical genera, Cheilosa and Neoscortechinia. The subfamily is distinguished by its unusual echinate pollen but their relationships remain poorly understood, with limited taxon sampling in prior evolutionary analyses. The present study, using newly generated whole plastome and nuclear ribosomal DNA (nrDNA) datasets, provides new insights into their molecular and morphological evolution. Our findings on the seven assembled Cheilosoideae plastomes reveal typical tetrad structure and length variation, with sizes ranging from 160,197 bp to 163,210 bp. Structural variations among plastomes generated distinct hotspots of repetitive sequences, particularly within tandem repeats and SSRs. Our phylogenetic analyses based on the plastome dataset resolved the four subfamilies (Cheilosoideae, Acalyphoideae, Crotonoideae, and Euphorbioideae) of Euphorbiaceae in successive sister relationships, although this result was partly inconsistent with an alternative topology based on the nrDNA dataset. Our study provides the first species-level phylogenetic framework for the Cheilosoideae. Both plastid and nuclear analyses strongly support an unexpected, slightly nested position of Cheilosa within a paraphyletic Neoscortechinia. Biogeographic analyses infer that Cheilosoideae probably originated from Southeast Asia and diversified since Middle Miocene (ca. 12.77 Ma). These results offer novel perspectives on the evolutionary history and biogeographic origins of Cheilosoideae within the Euphorbiaceae.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08350-y.

Introduction

Euphorbiaceae, comprising about 235 genera and 6,500 species, is one of the largest families of flowering plants and is the largest of the 36 families grouped in the order Malpighiales [1, 2]. They are distributed worldwide, especially in the tropics, with species adapted to a variety of climates and habitats. The family exhibits remarkable morphological diversity, encompassing a wide variety of growth forms (including trees, lianas, succulents, and herbs), floral forms, and reproductive ecologies [3]. The present narrow circumscription of Euphorbiaceae sensu APG IV [1] based on molecular phylogenetic evidence includes four subfamilies, Acalyphoideae, Cheilosoideae, Crotonoideae, and Euphorbioideae, and reflects the removal of five clades (formerly subfamilies or tribes) that are now each recognized as other families of Malpighiales. Among these five clades only Peraceae, formerly subfamily Peroideae [2], remains closely associated with Euphorbiaceae and the others are more distantly related. Rafflesiaceae is presently inferred as the sister-group to Euphorbiaceae but the phylogenetic placement of that highly modified holoparasitic family remains controversial [4]. If Rafflesiaceae are resolved elsewhere in Malpighiales [5], then Peraceae becomes the closest relative of Euphorbiaceae and could be reinstated as subfamily Peroideae.

Cheilosoideae, the focus of this study, were previously classified with Acalyphoideae in the early-diverging tribe Cheiloseae [6–8] until molecular phylogenetic evidence indicated they were separate from that subfamily and instead represented an early-diverging lineage for the entire family [2, 9–11]. The inclusion of poorly known Cheilosoideae in early broad molecular phylogenies was enabled by a high-quality DNA sample of Neoscortechinia kingii (Hook.f.) Pax & K. Hoffm. from a cultivated plant in Bogor Botanical Gardens (Indonesia). Given the phylogenetic position of Cheilosoideae, they have potential to improve our understanding of what is plesiomorphic in Euphorbiaceae in regard to both morphology and molecules.

Cheilosoideae is the smallest of the four subfamilies of Euphorbiaceae, comprising a total of seven species in two Southeast Asian and Malesian genera, Cheilosa and Neoscortechinia. Cheilosa includes only one species, C. montana Blume, while Neoscortechinia comprises six species: N. angustifolia (Airy Shaw) Welzen, N. forbesii (Hook.f.) S. Moore, N. kingii, N. nicobarica (Hook.f.) Pax & K. Hoffm., N. philippinensis (Merr.) Welzen, and N. sumatrensis S. Moore [3, 6]. Most of the species are distributed across West Malesia (Nicobar Islands, Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, Sulawesi, Java, Borneo, and the Philippines) in ranges that variously overlap, and only N. forbesii is isolated in East Malesia (Solomon Islands and New Guinea) [6, 7]. They are non-descript, dioecious tropical forest trees with mostly unspecialized features including latex absent, alternate, simple leaves, apetalous flowers, imbricate, pentamerous perianths, low stamen number (5–10), 2–3 locular ovaries, and capsular fruits. Among characters of taxonomic significance, their distinction as a subfamily lies in pollen with an echinate (spiny) exine that is unique in Euphorbiaceae, and their seed structure has an unusual red fleshy exotesta (sarcotesta) [2]. Generic differences include stamen and locule numbers, style morphology, and the presence or absence of bract glands and floral discs. At the species level Neoscortechinia spp. are morphologically very similar, making identifications challenging, and distinctions among them mostly involve quantitative characters, indument differences, and biogeography. Revisions of both genera have clarified their taxonomy and documented significant morphological variation within some species [12, 13].

While the placement of Cheilosoideae within Euphorbiaceae is well-established in family-wide molecular phylogenies, the relationships among its constituent species are poorly known because prior taxon sampling rarely included more than a single exemplar of each genus. A morphology-based phylogeny based on 10 characters of all species is poorly resolved and provides a limited framework for considering character evolution [13]. This highlights the need for phylogenomic studies to gain deeper insights into Cheilosoideae combined with a re-evaluation of morphology in an evolutionary framework. We report, here, seven new plastomes of Cheilosoideae which are the first to be generated and characterized for the subfamily. Together with 17 other species of Euphorbiaceae, and three outgroups (one of them is newly generated here), we performed analyses to (1) reconstruct a robust phylogenomic framework for Cheilosoideae, (2) examine their plastome structure and evolution, and (3) infer their biogeographic history and patterns in morphological character evolution.

Materials and methods

Taxon Sampling, DNA Extraction, and Sequencing

Our taxon sampling contained 27 accessions, including eight with newly generated data and 19 from GenBank (Table 1). The plant materials were derived from dry specimens deposited at the Kew Herbarium. Total genomic DNA was extracted from leaves using the Mag-MK Plant Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Sangon Biotech Co., Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The integrity and quality of the extracted DNA were evaluated through 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and further verified using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, CA, United States). For sequencing, short DNA fragments of approximately 350 base pairs were prepared to construct paired-end libraries with insert sizes of 150 base pairs. Library preparation was performed using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, MA, United States), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Three independent replicate libraries were prepared and sequenced using the HiSeq 2000 platform (Illumina, CA, United States) at the Beijing Genomics Institute (Shenzhen, China).

Assembly and annotation of plastome and nrDNA sequences

Following the removal of low-quality reads and adapter sequences, the resulting high-quality clean data were assembled using GetOrganelle v.1.7.4 under default settings, with k-mer values set to 21, 45, 65, 85, 95, 105, 117, and 127 [14]. The assembled data were subsequently visualized and filtered using Bandage 0.80, which yielded complete circular plastomes [15]. The plastomes were annotated using the Plastid Genome Annotator (PGA) [16] and GeSeq online tool [17]. The annotations were validated in Geneious Prime v.2025.0.2, using Ricinus communis as a reference sequence [18]. The circular gene maps were generated using Chloroplot online [19]. The nrDNA repeat sequences, encompassing the 18S, ITS1, 5.8S, ITS2, and 26S ribosomal RNA genes were assembled using GetOrganelle v.1.7.4 with default settings and annotated using Geneious Prime. All newly assembled sequences have been deposited in GenBank, and their corresponding accession numbers are provided in Table 1.

Characterization of repetitive sequences

Microsatellite markers (SSRs) were identified across the seven plastomes utilizing the web-based application MISA v.3.0 [20]. Minimum repeat thresholds were established for SSR motif identification: 10, 6, 4, 3, 3, and 3 for mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotide types. Tandem repeats were identified with Tandem Repeats Finder v.4.0.9 [21], using the parameters described by Xu and Wang [22] for the maximum period size (500), minimum alignment score for repeat reporting (80), and maximum size of the tandem repeat array (2 mbp), as well as the alignment values for match (2), mismatch (7), and indels (7).

Phylogenetic informativeness

The phylogenetic informativeness (PI) of protein-coding genes was evaluated using PhyDesign [23]. DNA sequences were analyzed with the HyPhy substitution rates algorithm [24], under default settings to minimize phylogenetic noise and account for gene length variation. Maximum likelihood (ML) trees were constructed from concatenated alignments of 78 shared coding DNA sequences (CDSs) from 24 Euphorbiaceae accessions. These ML trees were converted into rooted ultrametric trees using the chronos function in the ape R package, with the root set to 1 and tips to 0. Both the concatenated CDSs alignment and the ultrametric tree were used as inputs in PhyDesign to determine phylogenetic informativeness values (PIV).

Selection pressure analysis

A total of 78 CDSs were extracted from the plastomes of seven Cheilosoideae species to assess substitution rates within the group. The sequences were extracted using Phylosuite v.1.2.2 [25], with stop codons manually removed. The resulting sequences were aligned using MAFFT v.7.22 [26] and gaps were eliminated using trimAl v.1.2 [27] in automated1 mode, both integrated into Phylosuite v.1.2.2, and the aligned files were manually converted and saved in AXT format. Non-synonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates for the 78 CDSs were calculated using Ka/Ks Calculator v.2.0, employing the Ms method (selecting the model with the smallest AICc among 14 candidates) under the standard genetic code [28].

Phylogenetic analysis

Our phylogenetic analyses used plastome and nrDNA datasets (GenBank-retrieved and seven newly sequenced) of 27 accessions, including 24 Euphorbiaceae and three other Malpighiales outgroups (i.e., Linum pallescens, Pera arborea, and Phyllanthus amarus) (Table 1). Plastid CDSs and nrDNA were extracted and processed using Phylosuite v.1.2.2. The data matrices were aligned using MAFFT v.7.22 under default settings and trimmed using trimAl v.1.2 in automated1 mode [27]. The trimmed sequences were subsequently visually inspected in Geneious v.8.0.2 [18] and manually adjusted where necessary using a similarity criterion. Under the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), the best-fit model (GTR+F+I+G4) was estimated in ModelFinder [29] for the nrDNA dataset. PartitionFinder2 [30] was used to identify the best partitioning schemes and evolutionary models for CDSs dataset, applying the corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc) as the model selection criterion. We reconstructed the phylogenetic relationships under a ML optimality criterion with IQ-TREE v.1.6.8 [31] and branch support was estimated with 10,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates [32, 33]. Bayesian inference (BI) analyses for the same datasets were conducted using MrBayes v.3.2.6 [34]. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulations were performed for 10 million generations and sampled every 1,000 generations. The first 25% of the trees were discarded as burn-in, and the remainder used to generate majority-rule consensus trees. The iTOL tool [35] was utilized to visualize and manipulate the final phylogenetic trees.

Molecular divergence dating estimation

Phylogenetic inferences were performed using the CDSs dataset within the Bayesian evolutionary framework implemented in BEAST v.1.8 [36]. Our analysis used two calibrations including the fossil inflorescence, Hippomanoidea warmanensis, within Euphorbioideae [37], and one secondary calibration point based on previously estimated crown node ages for Euphorbiaceae [38]. Considering the minimum age of the Hippomanoidea warmanensis fossil (41.03 Ma, million years ago), we set this as the lower age constraint for the clade containing Triadica sebifera, Hippomane mancinella and Excoecaria agallocha, with its prior modelled using a log-normal distribution. Additionally, we specified a normal prior for the secondary calibration (the crown node of Euphorbiaceae), with the mean value of 89.9 Ma (HPD: 81.2–97.4 Ma) [38]. Using ModelFinder [29], we determined the GTR+I+G substitution model to be the most appropriate for the analysis. A birth-death process, a random starting tree, and a lognormal uncorrelated relaxed clock were all adopted [39]. The MCMC was conducted for a total of 500 million generations and sampled every 1000 generations. Effective sample size (ESS) and convergence were evaluated for each MCMC run using Tracer v.1.7.1 [40]. We displayed the post-burn-in trees and produced a maximum clade credibility using TreeAnnotator v.1.10.4 [36]. To visualize the tree and display mean divergence times with 95% HPD intervals, we used Figtree v.1.4.4 [41].

Ancestral area reconstruction

Ancestral area reconstruction was performed with the R package BioGeoBEARS [42] using tree files generated by BEAST. BioGeoBEARS employs ML methodologies that incorporate the core principles and assumptions of three prominent models (i.e., DEC, DIVALIKE, and BAYAREALIKE) in historical biogeography. In addition, all three models were evaluated following the incorporation of the founder-event speciation parameter (+ J). The model exhibiting the highest Akaike Information Criterion cumulative weight (AICc_wt) was selected as the most appropriate model, with the p-value from the likelihood ratio test determining the acceptance or rejection of the null hypothesis concerning the model with the + J parameter [43]. Biogeographic distribution regions were categorized as follows: (A) Europe; (B) North America; (C) South America; (D) Africa; (E) Australasia; (F) Central Asia; (G) Northern Asia; (H) East Asia; (I) South Asia; (J) Southeast Asia; (K) Western Asia. Distribution information was obtained by consulting online databases, including the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF; https://www.gbif.org) and Plants of the World Online (POWO; http://www.plantsoftheworldonline.org).

Results

Complete plastomes of Cheilosoideae

The assembly and annotation of plastomes among seven Cheilosoideae are summarized in (Figure S1, Table 2). The circular plastomes displayed a typical conserved quadripartite structure, consisting of one large single-copy (LSC), one small single-copy (SSC), and a pair of inverted repeat (IR) regions. Their total lengths ranged from 160,197 bp (in C. montana) to 163,210 bp (in N. angustifolia), and GC content ranged from 36.0% to 36.2%, respectively. The LSC region exhibited notable size variation among species, with C. montana possessing the shortest LSC (87,488 bp) while N. sumatrensis having the longest (89,471 bp). Similarly, the SSC region varied in length, with C. montana having the shortest SSC (18,671 bp) while N. angustifolia possessing the longest (19,002 bp). The IR regions were more conserved in size with slight variation, ranging from 53,290 bp in N. kingii to 54,892 bp in N. angustifolia. A total of 127 to 129 distinct genes were annotated, showing variation in CDSs, transfer RNA (tRNA) genes, and ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes, along with occurrences of gene duplication, loss, and pseudogenization. Functional annotation of the genes revealed that 73 genes were involved in self-replication processes, while 45 genes were associated with photosynthetic functions, and four genes were classified as hypothetical open reading frames (ORFs) with unknown functions (Table S1).

Repetitive sequence characterization

The plastomes of seven Cheilosoideae exhibited significant variation in the abundance and types of repetitive sequences (Fig. 1A). Simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were identified, encompassing mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotide motifs (Fig. 1B and C). Mononucleotide repeats were the most abundant, ranging from 108 in C. montana to 78 in N. nicobarica, with A/T and C/G motifs being predominant. Dinucleotide repeats ranged from four in N. philippinensis to 17 in N. sumatrensis, mainly consisting of AG/CT and AT/AT motifs. Trinucleotide repeats were most abundant in N. kingii, with common motifs including AAT/ATT, AGC/CTG, and ATC/ATG. Tetranucleotide repeats were least in N. kingii (7 repeats), with motifs including AAAG/CTTT, AAAT/ATTT, AATT/AATT, and AGAT/ATCT. Pentanucleotide repeats varied from N. angustifolia (1 repeat) to N. kingii (5 repeats), with motifs AATA/ATATT, ATATC/ATATG, and AAAAT/ATTTT. Notably, the hexanucleotide repeat sequence was rarer, occurring only in N. sp. (3 repeats) and N. sumatrensis (1 repeat), with motifs AATATG/ATATTC (Tables S2 and S3). For tandem repeats, a total of 199 repeats were identified, showing significant variation across all species, including C. montana (18 repeats), N. kingii (22 repeats), N. nicobarica (21 repeats), N. sumatrensis (34 repeats), N. angustifolia (45 repeats), N. philippinensis (30 repeats), and N. sp. (29 repeats) (Fig. 1D; Tables S4).

Phylogenetic informativeness

Phylogenetic informativeness (PI) analysis was performed using 78 CDSs from the 24 plastomes of Euphorbiaceae. The ycf1 gene exhibited the highest per-site PI, followed by ndhF, rpoC2, matK, rpoB, ycf2, rpoC1, psaA, psaB, and psbB (Figure S2). Gene length did not consistently correlate with high per-site PI values. For example, ycf2, despite having the longest coding sequence, displayed relatively low per-site PI. Similarly, psaA and psaB, which also possess extensive coding regions, showed low per-site PI. These findings suggest that factors other than gene length significantly influence the phylogenetic informativeness of coding regions.

Selective pressure analysis

The rates of non-synonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitutions were analyzed across 78 CDSs from Cheilosoideae. The highest Ka value was observed in the rpl33 gene (1.63148), while the psbF gene exhibited the highest Ks value (1.11191). To evaluate selective pressure on specific genes, we calculated the Ka/Ks ratio. Twenty genes, including atpH, cemA, clpP, clpP1, ndhB, petA, petG, petN, psaI, psbJ, psbD, psbF, psbI, psbL, psbN, rpl2, rpl33, rps12, rps14, and ycf3, exhibited a Ka/Ks ratio > 1, revealing positive selection (adaptive evolution). In contrast, genes with a Ka/Ks ratio < 1 (purifying selection) were more prevalent, suggesting evolutionary pressure to maintain ancestral function (Figure S3). Our findings revealed significant variability in Ka/Ks ratios among the analyzed CDSs.

Phylogenetic analyses

The plastome and the nrDNA datasets were analyzed separately using ML and BI optimality criteria and most nodes were strongly supported (ML bootstrap values (BS) = 100; BI posterior probabilities (PP) = 1.0; only a single node with < 80% BS which was in the plastid tree) (Fig. 2), including the monophyly of Cheilosoideae. Relationships within Cheilosoideae were mostly strongly supported, including Cheilosa montana as nested within Neoscortechinia by both plastid and nrDNA data. With plastid data, the four subfamilies of Euphorbiaceae were recovered as strongly supported monophyletic groups (BS = 100; PP = 1.0) and Cheilosoideae formed the earliest-diverging lineage (Fig. 2). The nrDNA phylogeny, had several supported differences in the overall topology compared to the plastome-based phylogeny, including the recognition of paraphyletic Crotonoideae and species placement differences among accessions in Neoscortechinia.

Biogeographical history of Euphorbiaceae

A molecular dating approach estimated the divergence times of the Cheilosoideae within Euphorbiaceae, using the plastome dataset to calibrate the analysis and determine its relative age compared to other subfamilies. The origins of Euphorbiaceae were estimated at 105.70 Ma (mean age of stem group; 95% HPD: 90.00–127.56), and diversified (crown group, node 1) at approximately 88.88 Ma (95% HPD: 81.08–96.83 Ma), corresponding to the Late Cretaceous period. Crotonoideae (node 2) diversified at approximately 79.18 Ma (95% HPD: 69.37–88.64 Ma), also aligning with the Late Cretaceous. Acalyphoideae (node 3) diversified at approximately 55.07 Ma (95% HPD: 37.70–70.28 Ma), corresponding to the Late Cretaceous to Late Eocene. Euphorbioideae (node 4) diversified at approximately 53.36 Ma (95% HPD: 47.51–62.00 Ma), corresponding to the Paleocene, and Middle Eocene. Cheilosoideae (node 5) diversified at approximately 12.77 Ma (95% HPD: 6.07–23.59 Ma), during the Miocene epoch (Fig. 3). This divergence dating analysis provides a detailed evolutionary timeline for Euphorbiaceae, emphasizing the relatively recent crown group diversification of the Cheilosoideae relative to the other subfamilies.

Ancestral area reconstruction

The Reconstruct Ancestral State in Phylogenies (RASP) method is a widely utilized tool in biogeography for estimating the ancestral geographic distributions of species or lineages. The BAYAREALIKE + J model, implemented within the BioGeoBEARS framework, demonstrated the strongest empirical support (Table S5). Ancestral area reconstruction using this model suggests that the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of Euphorbiaceae likely originated in a broad geographic region identified as Tropical Southeast Asia (region J). This reconstruction is notably influenced by the Cheilosoideae subfamily, whose entire distribution is confined to Tropical Eastern Asia, strongly shaping the ancestral distribution. However, the current distribution of extant species across Euphorbiaceae subfamilies exhibits considerable variation (Fig. 4): (1) In Acalyphoideae, species are found across a wide range of regions, including Europe, South America, Africa, Australasia, Central Asia, East Asia, South Asia, and Southeast Asia; (2) In Euphorbioideae, species are distributed in North America, South America, Africa, Australasia, East Asia, South Asia, and Southeast Asia; (3) In Crotonoideae, species are primarily found in North America, South America, East Asia, South Asia, and Southeast Asia. These patterns reflect the diversification of the family across distinct ecological zones, highlighting the role of both historical and environmental factors in shaping their current distribution. Cheilosoideae is inferred to have originated in Southeast Asia (SEA) and diversified during the Middle Miocene, a period marked by significant climatic and geological changes that likely influenced speciation and dispersal patterns.

Discussion

Plastome structural and repetitive sequence variation

Previous comparisons of Euphorbiaceae plastomes have focused on other subfamilies and this study presents the first analysis of the plastome structure of Cheilosoideae which is relevant to understanding broader molecular evolution across the family. Although its overall architecture exhibits the typical conservatism of angiosperm plastomes [44], a detailed comparative analysis of the plastomes within this subfamily offers new insights into their structural organization and molecular evolution. Our analysis revealed size variation among Cheilosoideae plastomes, ranging from 160,197 bp to 163,210 bp, with differences observed across the entire genome including the LSC, IRs, SSC regions. Length variation is primarily contributed by the LSC and SSC regions, while the IR regions remain relatively conserved, consistent with previous findings [44]. Their differences reflect many types of structural changes, including gene inversions, repeat sequence amplification, gene loss or pseudogenization events, as well as expansion or contraction of the inverted repeat (IR) regions [22]. Repetitive sequences are key drivers of plastome rearrangement and differentiation. Although plastid genomes are generally conserved, the abundance of simple sequence repeats (SSRs/microsatellites) varies significantly among species [45]. These repeats play crucial roles in genome reorganization and serve as genetic hotspots, with their high sequence variation holding substantial value for population genetics, evolutionary, and biogeographical studies [46, 47]. We found that their size distribution is non-random, with significant interspecific variation in the number of both SSRs and tandem repeats (identifying 122–149 polymorphic SSR loci in total). Mononucleotide A/T and C/G repeat motifs were the most prevalent, consistent with patterns observed in land plants [48, 49]. The majority of repeats are located in non-coding regions, a pattern consistent with reports in other Euphorbiaceae and higher plants [49, 50].

While angiosperm plastomes are often thought to have a universal gene and intron content, there is variation and related structural change in many lineages including other Euphorbiaceae and Malpighiales [44, 51, 52]. We identified three plastid genes (infA, rpl32, and rps16) associated with gene loss or pseudogenization in Cheilosoideae and related subfamilies. Within Cheilosoideae, the infA gene is conserved in all sampled species, whereas in other Euphorbiaceae subfamilies (except for Excoecaria agallocha within Euphorbioideae) it shows consistent loss or pseudogenization. This distribution pattern suggests an evolutionary specificity of infA loss, most likely resulting from functional transfer to the nuclear genome and subsequent functional redundancy [44, 53, 54]. The loss of plastid genes is often linked to functional evolution. In the case of infA, its loss in some lineages may reflect compensation by nuclear-encoded genes, a common phenomenon in plants where organellar genes are transferred to the nuclear genome to maintain essential functions [55, 56]. This functional redundancy is likely a key driver of plastome evolution, especially in response to ecological pressures or the need for efficient energy use in certain lineages [57]. Similarly, the rps16 gene exhibits the pattern of frequent gene loss or pseudogenization among sampled Euphorbiaceae species. Previous studies have shown that the loss of rps16 has been linked to structural rearrangements and sequence degeneration in specific taxonomic lineages of plastid genomes [51, 55]. Therefore, the discontinuous distribution pattern observed in this study is more consistent with lineage-specific late loss events. In contrast, the rpl32 gene has been lost only in certain species, which may result from independent, recurrent functional transfers to the nucleus and subsequent lineage-specific evolutionary trajectories [55, 56]. Furthermore, the loss of plastid genes and their transfer to the nuclear genome may be part of a broader trend of genome reduction in plants, particularly in species adapting to specialized ecological niches. Such gene transfers are often facilitated by mechanisms like non-homologous end joining and recombination, which can be influenced by environmental stressors, leading to genetic shifts that enhance survival in challenging environments [58]. These processes of gene transfer and genome restructuring are integral to the dynamic evolution of plastid genomes, reflecting the broader adaptive landscape in plant evolutionary biology.

Selective pressure and gene evolution

The Ka/Ks ratio, which measures the rates of non-synonymous (Ka) substitutions and synonymous (Ks), is a key metric for assessing nucleotide evolution and natural selection [59]. Typically, Ka/Ks ratios are less than one, as synonymous substitutions occur more often than non-synonymous ones [60]. This ratio provides insights into evolutionary pressures, including neutral, purifying, or positive selection [61–63]. Genes with high Ka/Ks variability are potential barcodes for species differentiation and beneficial in phylogenetic studies. Our results confirm that some genes had Ka/Ks ratios below one, highlighting the significance of understanding Ka/Ks dynamics in the context of purifying and positive selection [64]. Additionally, the Ka/Ks ratios greater than 1 were observed in only 20 regions (atpH, cemA, clpP, clpP1, ndhB, petA, petG, petN, psaI, psbJ, psbD, psbF, psbI, psbL, psbN, rpl2, rpl33, rps12, rps14, and ycf3), indicating that these genes have undergone positive selection, which aligns with previous studies on Euphorbiaceae [65]. Notably, members of the ndh and pet gene complexes identified here may be involved in the regulation of energy metabolism, positioning them as candidate targets for investigating adaptive evolution within Cheilosoideae. The Ka/Ks analysis revealed that the majority of genes are under strong purifying selection (Ka/Ks < 1), which favors the maintenance of their core biological functions. This finding is consistent with the conserved nature of plastid genes and existing phylogenetic results [66]. The 20 genes with Ka/Ks > 1 identified in this study (e.g., clpP, ycf3, and ndhB) may have experienced adaptive evolution in response to specific environmental pressures.

Phylogenetic relationships and morphology

Reconstructing evolutionary relationships within Euphorbiaceae especially at higher taxonomic levels (i.e., tribes and subfamilies) remains challenging, and even phylogenomic approaches have shown conflict among loci and low support values at some deep nodes [5]. Our study does show incongruence between complete plastid CDSs and nrDNA datasets, notably with the latter resolving a paraphyletic Crotonoideae and a slight internal difference among Neoscortechinia spp. Despite the topological conflicts, nuclear and plastid genomic data show strong congruence on three key findings: (1) the monophyly of Cheilosoideae; (2) its status as the first-diverging lineage of Euphorbiaceae; and (3) the slightly nested placement of C. montana within a paraphyletic Neoscortechinia.

Although prior molecular phylogenetic studies sampled only 1–2 Cheilosoideae, the monophyly of the subfamily was always considered likely due to its unique echinate pollen. Their unusual seed morphology [67] may also be unique but needs further study. While fleshy structures that aid in seed dispersal are widespread across Euphorbiaceae (especially as caruncles), those that envelop seeds are relatively rare, and their development and homologies are mostly poorly known. At least 20 genera [68] of Euphorbiaceae, spanning all four subfamilies, have fleshy exotestas (sarcotestas) but most differ in details of form and color from Cheilosoideae which have a bright red to orange outer surface and are yellow internally. In Cheilosoideae the fleshy layer (sometimes confusingly referred to as an aril) is a true sarcotesta which simulates an aril, and the seeds are known to be consumed and dispersed by vertebrates, especially primates and birds [69, 70]. Underneath the sarcotesta is a hard black mechanical layer with a palisadal exotegmen structure typical of other Euphorbiaceae [3, 67]. Details of seed coat anatomy such as layer thickness and vascularization are also unusual but likely correlate with its modified fleshy nature. The relatively large fruit (1–4 cm in dia.) of Cheilosoideae are usually 1-seeded by abortion (sometimes 2–3 seeds in Cheilosa) and their dehiscence is atypical in being tardily loculicidally dehiscent, rather than explosively loculicidal and septicidal as in much of the rest of Euphorbiaceae. As with the seeds, this fruit modification is likely adaptive for dispersal strategy, and reflects changes in lignification and lack of differentially-oriented fibre layers within the fruit walls which can be thick, especially in Cheilosa (1–6 mm).

The taxonomy of the two genera has been questioned before, but their morphological differences are considered to outweigh similarities [12, 13]. Here Cheilosa is resolved as slightly nested within Neoscortechinia but if the genera are united, Cheilosa would have nomenclatural priority as the older generic name and all six Neoscortechinia species would need to be transferred to the former. The identity of the fruiting collection used for our Cheilosa data is confirmed by a combination of morphological characters (i.e., leaves without basilaminar glands and large globose fruits described as having three seeds) that excludes it from Neoscortechinia. Our data also agrees with that of the four genes in the phylogeny of Tokuoka [3] which sampled a different specimen of the same collection (Coode et al. 7842, L). Cheilosa has some plesiomorphic features including laminar and bract glands absent, and a pistil typical for Euphorbiaceae with a 3-locular (rarely 4) ovary, short-connate styles, and long, papillose stigmas; Neoscortechnia spp. are more specialized in usually having laminar and bract glands, and the pistils are 2-locular, lack defined styles and have 4 small stigmatic lobes lacking papillae. However, Cheilosa has other features that appear more derived including an annular disc, stamens 9–10 in 2 whorls, and anthers glandular-apiculate; Neoscortechinia spp. have dissected discs, stamens (4)5–9 in a single whorl, and anthers muticous. The nested placement of Cheilosa would indicate that its plesiomorphic features are potentially reversals. Compelling morphological characters that could serve as synapomorphies for the few internally resolved clades are not apparent as differences among Neoscortechinia species are slight. However, the sister relationship between Cheilosa and N. kingii is supported by their lack of adaxial basal laminar glands which are otherwise present on the other Neoscortechinia spp.

Resolving the deepest nodes of Crotonoideae has proven challenging, with few-gene studies showing weak support and/or potential polyphyly [2, 3], although monophyly is now emerging with phylogenomic data [5]. Our study reveals incongruence between plastid and nuclear data regarding the monophyly of Crotonoideae. While plastid data strongly supports the monophyly of Crotonoideae, the nrDNA dataset suggests a paraphyletic relationship within this group. Further investigations, including more extensive taxon sampling and the integration of additional molecular markers, could help clarify whether these discrepancies have biological or analytical causes, and provide a more complete understanding of the evolutionary processes shaping the Crotonoideae lineage.

Given the many unusual morphological features (i.e., pollen and fruit/seed) of Cheilosoideae it remains unclear how much the group informs on our understanding of early evolution of Euphorbiaceae except for establishing plesiomorphic character states for very general features that are also shared with Peraceae (i.e., woody habit, latex absent, alternate, simple leaves, apetalous flowers, imbricate, pentamerous perianths, low stamen number, 2–3 locular ovaries, separate styles, and capsular fruits). The subfamily may inform on ancestral breeding systems as dioecy is present in Cheilosoideae and most Peraceae. The impact of dioecy on diversification has been debated with disparate hypotheses of its effects ranging from promoting diversification to being an evolutionary dead-end unless escaped by transitions to other breeding systems [71, 72]. While diversification rates were not explicitly tested here, we observe that Cheilosoideae are not very species-rich and there is clearly asymmetric diversification with that group of seven species sister to the rest of the family, which is massive diversification of nearly 6500 species where monoecy is more frequent than dioecy.

Divergence time and biogeography

Divergence time estimates facilitate a wide range of downstream evolutionary and comparative analyses [73]. Although our limited taxon sampling and the small number of calibrations limit accuracy, several deeper nodes fall within the broad time range reported by previous plastid-based dating studies in Euphorbiaceae/Acalyphoideae (e.g., Eocene origins of major Acalyphoideae lineages) [74, 75]. However, discrepancies of tens of millions of years can occur for some genus-level stem ages (e.g., Ricinus), likely reflecting differences in taxon sampling, loci, and calibration strategies among studies [76]. Cheilosoideae have the youngest diversification at 12.77 Ma which is ca. 40–66 million years later than the other three subfamilies. This relatively young age may be reflected in their tiny species-richness, limited molecular and morphological differentiation, and limited geographic range. Their diversification coincided with the Middle Miocene (ca. 11.6–16.0 Ma), a period marked by gradual climatic shifts in SEA [77, 78], which began to show early signs of drying and the development of stronger seasonal monsoon patterns. These changes likely influenced dispersal across the many islands that dominate the species’ ranges. Infraspecific morphological variation has been noted in some species (i.e., C. montana, N. kingii, and N. nicobarica) and previously given some taxonomic recognition as varieties, and to some degree corresponds with geography [12–13]. Given this variation and problems with identifications in some species complexes (e.g., slight distinctions between N. sp., N. nicobarica, and N. philippinensis), population genetics sampling is warranted and can use the hotspots of genomic variation identified here. Cheilosoideae all occupy similar tropical forest environments with varying degrees of habitat breadth (swamp forests to hills) and substrate (sand, clay, or limestone). Additionally, N. philippinensis with the widest species distribution (Sumatra to the Philippines) appears to be the most ecologically tolerant and with widest elevation range (0–1500 m) [69].

Conclusions

This study presents the first phylogenomic framework for subfamily Cheilosoideae, using complete plastome and nuclear ribosomal DNA (nrDNA) sequences from nearly all species to address critical knowledge gaps previously constrained by the scarcity of phylogenetic data for this group. We characterized the features and variation patterns of Cheilosoideae plastomes and identified valuable molecular markers such as Simple Sequence Repeats (SSRs) and tandem repeats. Phylogenomic analyses confirmed the monophyly of Cheilosoideae, with Cheilosa resolved as nested placement within a paraphyletic Neoscortechinia. The relationships resolved among its mostly similar appearing constituent species have little morphological support. Biogeographic and divergence dating analyses indicate an origin of the subfamily in SEA during the Middle Miocene. These findings offer new insights into the evolutionary history and biogeographic origins of Cheilosoideae, the earliest-diverging lineage (basal position) within the Euphorbiaceae.
