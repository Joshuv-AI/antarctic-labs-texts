Achromobacter sp. FB-14 mediated morpho-physio-biochemical and molecular improvements in cotton subjected to salt stress
Authors: Sidra Batool, Khadija Rafiq, Muhammad Saeed, Atyia Naeem, Muhammad Shahid Munir Chohan, Muhammad Sohail Akram
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCotton is considered moderately salt-tolerant, yet the degree of tolerance can vary significantly among different genotypes.Methods and resultsThe current study examined the effects of plant growth-promoting bacterium “Achromobacter sp. FB-14” in two cotton genotypes (FH-326 and FH-941). Under 150 mM salt stress, FH-941 showed a higher accumulation of malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) i.e. 24.4 and 8.20 µmol g− 1 FW, respectively. Contrarily, FH-326 exhibited higher catalase (CAT) and peroxidase (POD) activities (48.82 & 27.81 Umg− 1 protein, respectively). FB-14 inoculated plants exhibited an increase in chlorophyll a content by 10% (FH-326) and 8% (FH-941). FH-326 exhibited better ion (Na+, K+, Ca+ 2, Mg+ 2) uptake and root-shoot translocation patterns than FH-941. Imposition of salt stress, without FB-14 inoculation, resulted in higher reduction in NHX1, SOS1, and HAK5 gene expression in FH-941 compared to FH-326. While elevated expression of HKT1 was recorded in FH-326 compared to FH-941. A down-regulation of LHCB, GhWRKY3 and GhWRKY34 was recorded in both genotypes except GhWRKY34 was up-regulated in FH-326. Additionally, FB-14 inoculation in FH-941, under salt stress, led to up-regulation of LHCB and GhWRKY3 and GhWRKY34.ConclusionsThe FH-326 maintained optimum H2O2 and MDA content through POD/CAT activities, balanced ion uptake and gene regulation. The growth of salt-sensitive FH-941 was facilitated by FB-14 inoculation through modulated patterns of ion homeostasis, expression of ion transporter and transcription factors. Further studies involving more cotton genotypes and salt-related molecular markers under bacterial inoculation may assist in devising better strategies to achieve potential yield of cotton.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07183-5.

Introduction

The demand for natural fibers is expected to rise with the increasing world population. Cotton is serving as a major source of textile fiber and its sustainable production underpins the textile industry, contributing an estimated $600 billion annually to the world economy [1]. Pakistan plays a critical role in the global textile market as world’s second-largest cotton exporter. Despite being among top five cotton-producing countries globally, Pakistan’s cotton industry faces persistent challenges; including pest infestations, plant diseases, and soil salinity [2]. One of the major factors limiting cotton production, both globally and in Pakistan, is the degradation of fertile land caused by salinity. Approximately, 230 million hectares of land worldwide is considered fertile, yet about 20% of this area has been affected by salinity [3]. Saline soils are commonly identified by an electrical conductivity (EC) greater than 4 dS m− 1, an exchangeable sodium percentage (ESP) below 15%, and a pH less than 8.5 [4]. This growing issue affects large swathes of agricultural land [5], necessitating the adoption of innovative agronomic practices to sustain and enhance cotton productivity in the country, particularly in salt-affected regions.

The harmful effects of soil salinity are closely linked to the physiological stress it imposes on plants. Arable soil experiences ion imbalances due to buildup of sodium (Na+) and chloride (Cl−) concentrations, which badly impact cellular enzyme activity, stomatal function and plants photosynthetic rate. Salinity delays germination and reduces root elongation, further hampering plant establishment and early growth [4]. As plants absorb excess Na+ and Cl− ions, hypertonic stress increases leading to overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS). The resulting rise in ROS levels further damages cell membranes, causing the breakdown of lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids [5, 6].

To counter these detrimental effects, plants have evolved several mechanisms to regulate sodium levels and maintain ion homeostasis. Plants mitigate excessive Na+ accumulation in their shoots by limiting its entry or enhancing its removal. This process operates at organ, tissue, and cellular levels, involving various Na+ transporters that help maintain ion balance [7, 8]. Key transporters include members of the SOS pathway, NHX family, and HKT family. These transporters help reduce Na+ loading into the xylem, restrict its movement within the plant, and regulate its sequestration into vacuoles [9]. The HKT gene family comprises Na+ transporters (class I) and Na+/K+ transporters (class II) across different plants. NHX1, located on the tonoplast, sequesters excess Na+ into vacuoles, while SOS1, situated on the plasma membrane, pumps Na+ into the apoplast [10].

In addition to these intrinsic mechanisms, beneficial soil microbes such as plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) can further assist plants in coping with salt stress. PGPR colonize plant roots and can assist plants growth and development through multiple pathways. These PGPR may reduce salt uptake in plants by trapping cations within their exopolysaccharide matrix and altering root architecture through extended rhizosheaths [11, 12]. They also play a key role in maintaining K+/Na+ balance and ion homeostasis by limiting Na+ accumulation in leaves and enhancing Na+ exclusion through roots via up-regulation of ion transporters [13]. A study revealed that Azotobacter lipoferum and A. chroococcum significantly improved K⁺ levels, maintained K⁺/Na⁺ ratio, and reduced Na⁺ concentrations in maize plants suffering from salinity stress [14]. Bharti et al. [15] observed that PGPR Dietzia natronolimnaea regulated the expression of SOS pathway-associated genes and ion transporters such as TaNHX1, TaHAK, and TaHKT1, thereby enhancing tolerance of wheat plants to salt stress. In Arabidopsis thaliana, plants inoculated with Burkholderia phytofirmans PsJN demonstrated enhanced tolerance to salt stress. Transcriptomic analysis indicated changes in the expression of several ion transporters, including SOS1, AKT1, NHX2, and HKT1, which likely contributed to enhanced salt tolerance [16].

Achromobacter species are gram-negative aerobic bacteria commonly found in moist environments and exhibit notable versatility and resilience. Various Achromobacter species have shown a strong capacity for promoting plant growth, enhancing metal uptake in contaminated soils, robustness under various abiotic stress which hints its efficacy in stress-prone agricultural systems [17]. For instance, Achromobacter xylosoxidans has been reported to increase shoot and root length and biomass production, while producing beneficial compounds such as indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), phosphate-solubilizing enzymes, and catalase; all these contribute to improved plant nutrition and stress mitigation [18]. These functional attributes make Achromobacter species particularly useful in supporting plant growth, especially in challenging environments such as saline soils. Similarly, A. insolitus strain MRBPUMBE has shown high efficacy in boosting plant growth and increasing shallot yield under field conditions. More notably, the strain Achromobacter sp. FB-14 used in the present study was isolated from sandy clay soil characterized by high electrical conductivity and elevated pH levels, indicating its natural adaptation to saline-alkaline environments. Earlier investigations revealed that FB-14 not only promotes plant growth but also plays a crucial role in alleviating salt stress by enhancing the expression of stress-responsive CIPK genes in rice [19]. Thus, FB-14 offers a combined advantage of both physiological support (i.e. nutrient solubilization and oxidative stress reduction) and possible activation of molecular entities/transcription factors involved in salt stress tolerance.

In a preliminary lab experiment, conducted in Petri-plates, we identified FH-326 cotton genotype as salt-tolerant compared to FH-941 which showed more sensitivity to imposed salt stress. The current study was designed to further explore plant-microbe interactions and objectives of the study were; (i) to compare physio-biochemical modulations in FH-326 and FH-941 grown in natural soil with imposed salt stress (ii) to assess the impact of Achromobacter sp. FB-14 on cotton growth, oxidant as well as anti-oxidant status and (iii) to evaluate the effect of FB-14 on expression patterns of key genes responsible for ion transport and salt-induced plant responses. The study results facilitate to understand PGPR’s role in enhancing salt tolerance as well as may provide an eco-friendly and sustainable solution to enhance crop resilience and productivity with less dependency on chemical interventions.

Materials and methods

Screening of cotton genotypes for salt tolerance

Seeds of five cotton genotypes (FH-152, FH-326, FH-941, FH-942, FH-444) were obtained from Ayub Agricultural Research Institute (AARI), Faisalabad. In a preliminary screening experiment, conducted in Petri-plates (using Hoagland’s nutrient solution), FH-326 was categorized as salt-tolerant and FH-941 as salt-sensitive on the basis of germination percentage and plant growth patterns (data not shown).

Cultural condition of Achromobacter FB-14

A potential plant growth-promoting rhizobacterium Achromobacter sp. strain FB-14 [19] was grown in LB broth while incubating at 37 °C for 10 h (cell density of 108 CFU mL− 1). Afterwards, bacterial culture was centrifuged, the pellet was washed using dH2O and re-suspended in dH2O to obtain a concentration of OD600 = 1.0.

Experimental soil

The experimental soil was obtained from agricultural fields of AARI, Faisalabad. It was categorized as clay loam and had 0.7 mg kg− 1 nitrogen, 7.7 mg kg− 1 phosphorous, 109 mg kg− 1 potassium with 1.34% organic manure and 7.4 soil pH.

Plant material, FB-14 inoculation and imposition of salt stress

Seeds of both cotton genotypes (FH-326 and FH-941) were surface sterilized using sodium hypochlorite followed by rinsing thrice with dH2O. Afterwards, seeds were inoculated using FB-14 inoculum or dH2O for 20 min. Meanwhile, the soil was pre-inoculated by mixing 7 mL of FB-14 inoculum (inoculated soil) or dH2O (un-inoculated soil) per 100 g of soil. The FB-14 inoculated seeds were sown in inoculated soil while un-inoculated seeds were sown in un-inoculated soil. Initially, seeds were sown in small plastic bags (Fig. 1) but later, after germination, plants along with soil were shifted into earthen pots and four seedlings per pot (5 kg soil) were maintained. Afterwards, salt stress was imposed using 500 mL of 150 mM NaCl solution in three periodic intervals i.e. 100 mL, then after 10 days 200 mL and, finally, 200 mL after 10 days interval of 2nd application making ~ 4383 mg total NaCl per pot. Hence, the experiment was consisted of four treatments (i) control (ii) salt-stressed (iii) FB-14 inoculation, and (iv) FB-14 inoculation plus salt-stressed. Canal water (pH 6.5; EC values less than 0.5 ds mol− 1) was used for irrigation as per plants requirement. The plants were allowed to grow for 30 days after imposition of first salt stress (suppl. Table 1).

Plant biomass

Fresh weight of cotton roots and shoots was recorded at the day of plant harvesting. The dry weight was measured after heat drying of plant samples in an oven at 110 °C for 48 h.

Stomatal conductance and rate of photosynthesis

Stomatal conductance and rate of photosynthesis of the growing plants were recorded (two days before harvesting) in maximum natural sunlight between 9:00–11:00 h at 25 °C using an infra-red gas analyser (IRGA) model SB-MK2, Analytical Development Company, Hod-desdon, UK.

Chlorophyll and total phenolic content

Fresh leaves (0.5 g) were crushed and homogenized using 80% acetone. The mixture was centrifuged for 15 min at 8,000 g and absorbance of the supernatant was observed using Shimadzu UV-1601 spectrophotometer at three different wavelengths i.e. 663, 645, and 470 nm [20]. For phenolics determination, 0.1 mL supernatant was mixed with water (2 mL) and folin ciocalteu’s reagent (1 mL). Sodium carbonate (20%) was added to maintain the mixture volume up to 10 mL. The absorption spectrum was recorded at 750 nm [21] and presence of phenolics was estimated by comparing the spectrum with the standard curve line.

Relative membrane permeability (RMP)

The leaves of cotton plants were cut and placed in 20 mL of deionized dH2O in test tubes. The tubes were vortexed for 5 s. Initial electrical conductivity (EC0) of the solution was recorded and test tubes were kept at 4 °C for 24 h. The EC1 was recorded, samples were autoclaved (120 °C for 10 min) and EC2 was recorded. Finally, RMP was calculated as described earlier [22].

Estimation of lipid peroxidation, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and proline content

Plant exposed to thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reaction to determine level of malondialdehyde content (MDA), an indication of membrane lipid peroxidation. The shoots were homogenized with 0.1% trichloroacetic acid (TCA), centrifuged and the supernatant was diluted with 20% TCA and 0.5% TBA. The mixture was then placed in an incubator (for 40 min) at 95 °C. After ice quenching, absorption of the supernatant was recorded at 532 and 600 nm [23]. For determination of H2O2 content, 0.5 g leaves were macerated in 0.1% TCA and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant (1 mL) was mixed with potassium iodide (2 mL) and phosphate buffer (1 mL). The absorption of the reaction mixture was monitored at 390 nm [24]. For determination of proline content, leaves (0.5 g) were homogenized with 3% (w/v) sulfur salicylic acid and filtered. The filtrate (2 mL) was mixed with ninhydrin reagent (2 mL) and glacial acetic acid (2 mL). The mixture was incubated for 60 min at 100 °C. It was cooled down and 4 mL of toluene was added [25]. The absorption spectrum at 520 nm was measured using the lower layers containing toluene.

Total soluble proteins

The fresh leaves were mixed with Bradford reagent followed by homogenization and centrifugation. The mixture was then incubated for 20 min [26] and absorbance (at 595 nm) was recorded using a spectrophotometer. Total soluble proteins were calculated by comparing with a standard curve of Bovine serum albumin (BSA).

Determination of peroxidase and catalase activity

For peroxidase (POD) activity, plant samples were grounded in phosphate buffer (pH 7.8) and centrifugation of samples was done for 20 min at 10,000 g. Absorbance of the reaction mixture (oxidation reaction of Guiacol and H2O2) was recorded at 240 nm, for 2 m with 20 s interval, using a spectrophotometer. Peroxidase activity was defined as a change in absorbance of 0.01 units per minute [27]. For catalase activity, plant samples (0.5 g) were homogenized in phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.0) and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min. Catalase activity was determined by using the supernatant. The degradation of H2O2 indicated the activity of catalase [28].

Determination of plant nutrient/ion content

Plant samples (roots and shoots, separately) were kept in an oven for 24 h at 120 °C. For digestion, 0.5 g sample was taken in a flask having 5 mL H2SO4 and incubated overnight at room temperature. A 35% (v/v) H2O2 solution was added in flasks and set over a hot plate at 350 °C making the mixture transparent [29]. Following cooling, the solution was filtered by adjusting volume up to 150 mL. The N, P, K, Ca, and Mg content of dried shoot (and root) samples were determined by atomic absorption spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Model 7JO-8024, Tokyo, Japan).

Analysis of ion transporter and transcription factor gene/s expression

Gene expression analysis was carried out using three biological as well as technical replicates. The Hot Borate method [30] with some modifications was used for isolation of RNA. Traces of genomic DNA were degraded using RNase-free DNase and complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using a standard kit from Thermo Fischer Scientific. Polymerase chain reaction was performed using cDNA (2 µg), 200 nM gene-specific forward and reverse primers (Suppl. Table 2; [31–33]) for each target gene along with 10 µL Maxima SYBR Green Master Mix. The qPCR was performed using a 96 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Scientific Piko-Real) with reaction temperature conditions set as 95 °C for 30 s (initial denaturation), 95 °C for 10 s (40 cycles), and 58 °C for 30 s [13]. As an internal reference control, UBQ7 transcript expression was monitored and its expression was compared to target genes for normalization. Melting-curve analysis was used to determine the amplification specificity. The relative transcript abundance of the target gene/s was analyzed by employing the Livak method [34].

Statistical analysis

There were four replicates for each treatment (total 32 pots for the two genotypes) and experiment was conducted following a completely randomized design. The statistical program ‘CoStat’ was used to analyze the obtained data for analysis of variance (ANOVA). The data was also computed for least significant differences (p ≤ 0.05) among treatment means. Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed using XLSTAT software (version 2016.1).

Results

Shoot fresh and dry weight

With 150 mM NaCl treatment, shoot fresh weight was reduced by 8% and 26% in FH-326 and FH-941, respectively, compared to their respective control plants (Table 1). In absence of salt stress, FB-14 inoculation resulted in a considerable increase in shoot fresh weight (i.e. 19.3% in FH-326 and 57.3% in FH-941) in contrast to respective plants grown with salt stress. In FH-941, a 32% decrease was observed in shoot dry weight under 150 mM NaCl stress while only a 9% reduction was seen in FH-326 when each compared to their respective control group (Table 1). Without salt stress, a 14% increase in shoot dry weight of FH-941 was recorded by FB-14 inoculation compared to control group while it was 68.69% higher compared to plants grown with salt stress. The FB-14 inoculation, in absence of salt stress, induced more increase in shoot dry weight (14% higher) in FH-941 compared to FH-326. However, in presence of salt stress, FH-326 exhibited higher shoot dry weight when grown with FB-14 inoculation (Table 1).

Root fresh and dry weight

Under NaCl stress, a significant increase in root fresh weight of 29% was observed in FH-941 as compared to control (Table 1). The FH-326 showed 15% increase while FH-941 exhibited 18% decrease in root fresh weight by inoculation with FB-14 compared to respective plants grown with salt stress alone. The inoculation with FB-14 in presence of NaCl increased the root fresh weight in FH-941 as compared to control (50%) as well as plants grown with salt stress alone (16%). In absence of FB-14 inoculation, a decrease in root dry weight of 38.4% was recorded in FH-941 with 150 mM salt stress. While salt-stressed FH-326 exhibited almost similar root dry weight as recorded for control plants. In absence of salt stress, FB-14 inoculation brought an increase in root dry weight in both genotypes though higher (44%) in FH-941 when each was compared to its control plants. However, when FB-14 inoculated plants were compared with salt-stressed plants, FH-941 exhibited a significant (133%) increase in root dry weight.

Plant chlorophyll content, stomatal conductance and photosynthetic rate

Under NaCl stress, without FB-14 inoculation, FH-326 showed a 5% reduction in chlorophyll a content compared to control plants (Fig. 2a) while a 15% reduction was evidenced in FH-941. Plants treated with FB-14, in absence of NaCl, had a considerable (15.42% and 26.78%) increase in chlorophyll a content in FH-326 and FH-941, accordingly, when compared with respective salt-stressed plants. The FB-14 inoculation in presence of salt resulted in 10% (FH-326) and 18% (FH-941) higher chlorophyll a content when each was compared to plants grown with NaCl stress alone. Without FB-14 inoculation, chlorophyll b content was reduced in both FH-326 (7%) and FH-941 (21%) under NaCl stress in comparison to respective control (Fig. 2b). Conversely, a 10% and 16% elevation was noted in chlorophyll b content in FH-326 and FH-941, respectively, by inoculation of FB-14 in absence of NaCl stress. The FB-14 inoculated plants, in presence of stress, exhibited 16% (FH-941) and 10% (FH-326) higher chlorophyll b content compared to salt stress alone.

Under salt stress, stomatal conductance was reduced in both (FH-326 and FH-941) by 18% and 35%, respectively, as compared to their respective control plants (Fig. 2c). In presence of salt stress, plants grown from FB-14 inoculated seeds showed a considerable (11% in FH-326 and 23% in FH-941) increase in stomatal conductance compared to respective plants grown with NaCl stress alone (Fig. 2c).

Without FB-14 inoculation, salt stress imposed maximum reduction (47%) in FH-941 while FH-326 plants exhibited comparatively less (21%) reduction in rate of photosynthesis as compared to respective control plants (Fig. 2d). However, FB-14 inoculation resulted in enhancement in rate of photosynthesis in both genotypes, though it was more pronounced in FH-941 (43%) compared to FH-326 (16%) when each was compared with plants grown under NaCl stress alone (Fig. 2d).

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and malondialdehyde (MDA) content

The FH-326 genotype showed less oxidative stress in terms of H2O2 as only an 11% increase was recorded after NaCl imposition. However, a 27% increase in H2O2 was recorded in FH-941 compared to control group (Fig. 3a). With FB-14 inoculation, in absence of NaCl, FH-326 maintained H2O2 content with slight non-significant reduction (6%). The 27% increase in H2O2 content (with salt stress alone) in FH-941 was reduced to 2% when plants were grown from FB-14 inoculated seeds.

Under 150 mM NaCl stress, a 50% increase in MDA level was recorded in FH-941 while FH-326 exhibited almost similar MDA content (6.29 nmol g− 1 FW) when was compared to respective control plants (Fig. 3b). Further, FB-14 inoculation assisted plants of both genotypes (grown in absence or presence of NaCl) in maintaining MDA levels comparable to respective control plants (Fig. 3b).

Relative membrane permeability (RMP)

The result indicated that relative membrane permeability (RMP) was significantly increased by 6% in FH-326 and 22% in FH-941 in presence of NaCl stress without FB-14 inoculation when compared with respective control plants (Fig. 3c). The plants (FH-326 or FH-941) grown with FB-14 inoculation together with imposition of NaCl stress exhibited similar RMP level as that of their respective control group/s. However, FB-14 inoculation reduced RMP up to 8.1% (FH-326) and 24.72% (FH-941) when same was compared to RMP exhibited by salt-stressed plants.

Total phenolics

In comparison to control plants, FH-941 showed a 9% increase in phenolic content under NaCl stress while there was no effect on FH-326 plants. A 14% decrease (compared to control) was recorded for phenolic content in FH-941 when seeds were subjected to FB-14 inoculation in absence of salt stress (Fig. 3d). A 20–23% decrease in phenolic content was recorded after FB-14 inoculation in absence or presence of salt stress when was compared to phenolic content in plants grown with salt stress alone. The FH-326 exhibited very little variation in phenolic content in response to stress when subjected to FB-14 inoculation.

Total proline, total soluble proteins (TSP), and antioxidant enzyme activities

The two genotypes showed opposite response with imposition of salt stress where FH-326 exhibited highest (9.89 µmole g− 1 FW) while least proline content was recorded in FH-941 (Fig. 4a). Inoculation of seeds using FB-14 resulted in increased proline content in FH-941 in absence (25% higher) and presence (30.5% higher) of salt when compared to plants grown with salt stress alone. The imposition of salt stress decreased total soluble protein content by 2% and 11% in FH-326 and FH-941, respectively (Fig. 4b). A non-significant variation in TSP was recorded in FH-326 across all the treatments. FB-14 inoculation induced an increase in TSP content in FH-941 both in absence and presence of salt stress. (Fig. 4b).

The FH-326 genotype maintained an optimum peroxidase (POD) activity and exhibited very little variation with imposition of salt stress and/or FB-14 inoculation (Fig. 4c). However, POD activity was increased up to 23.8% in FH-941 after imposition of salt stress compared to plants grown without salt stress (control). In addition, FH-941 displayed 11% less POD activity when plants were grown from FB-inoculated seeds compared to un-inoculated seeds. The pattern of catalase (CAT) activity, with various treatments, was similar to POD activity pattern in both genotypes (Fig. 4d).

Ionic content of roots and shoots

In presence of NaCl stress, without FB-14 inoculation, an increase of 31% and 68% in root Na+ content was observed in FH-326 and FH-941, respectively, compared to respective control plants (Fig. 5a). The FB-14 inoculation increased root Na+ in FH-326 by 2%; however, FH-941 had a 2% decrease in root Na+ when each was compared to respective control. The application of NaCl stress and FB-14 inoculation resulted in both varieties (FH-326 and FH-941) showing an elevation in root Na+ content by 6% and 19%, respectively (Fig. 5a; Suppl. Table 2a). A 7% and 53% increase in Na+ content was recorded, after addition of 150 mM NaCl in shoot samples of FH-326 and FH-941, respectively, compared to respective control plants (Fig. 6a). Seed inoculation using FB-14 assisted plants (of both genotypes) to circulate Na+ or restrict in roots as shoot Na+ content in inoculated plants (without or with salt stress) were almost similar as that of Na+ content in plants grown without salt and FB-14 inoculation.

In comparison to control plants, there was a decrease in root K+ uptake by 7% in FH-326 and 33% in FH-941 at 150 mM NaCl. In absence of NaCl stress but with FB-14 inoculation, a significant reduction in root K+ was observed in both varieties FH-326 and FH-941 i.e. 4% and 10% respectively, compared to control plants. The FB-14 inoculation and salt stress decreased root K+ in FH-326 (11%) and FH-941 (12%) as compared to control (Fig. 5b). Without FB-14 inoculation and imposition of salt stress, FH-941 exhibited more shoot K+ content in comparison to FH-941. The imposed NaCl stress reduced shoot K+ content by 15% and 35% in FH-326 and FH-941, respectively, as compared to control plants (Fig. 6b). Plants of both genotypes were able to maintain shoot K+ content as compared to control group.

The calcium ions (Ca2+) content in roots was decreased in NaCl stressed plants resulting in a 7% and 20% decline in root Ca2+ content in FH-326 and FH-941, respectively, as compared to their respective control plants (Fig. 5c; Suppl. Table 2a). When both varieties were treated with FB-14, FH-326 showed an increased (1%) root Ca2+ content but FH-941 exhibited 3% less root Ca2+ as compared to control plants. Plants inoculated with FB-14 along with salt stress showed 3% and 14% reduced root Ca2+ in FH-326 and FH-941, respectively, compared to un-inoculated plants (Fig. 5c). It was recorded that shoot Ca2+ of both genotypes were not significantly altered by FB-14 inoculation and/or salt stress imposition as evident in Fig. 6c.

Without FB-14 inoculation, 150 mM NaCl addition resulted in 16% and 31% less root Mg2+ content in FH-326 and FH-941 respectively, in comparison to respective control plants (Fig. 5d). Inoculation of seeds using FB-14 inoculation produced plants (of both genotypes) having root Mg2+ content comparable to Mg2+ present in roots of plants of a control group, irrespective of NaCl absence or presence. The least shoot Mg2+ content was recorded in FH-941 with imposition of 150 mM NaCl stress which was 25% less in comparison to control group. Under similar salt stress, FH-326 plants exhibited less reduction (8%) when compared to a respective control group (Fig. 6d).

Gene expression analysis

Ion transporters play a key role in maintaining ionic homeostasis, particularly Na+/H+ transporters i.e. NHX1 (tonoplast Na+/H + antiporter), SOS1 (a plasma membrane Na+/H+ antiporter), HKT1 (high-affinity Na + transporter) and HAK5 (high-affinity K+ transporter) contribute significantly [31, 32]. Therefore, expression of the stated few genes was examined in the current study. Under salt stress (150 mM), down-regulation of NHX1 (non-significant in FH-326) and SOS1 expression was observed in both genotypes (Fig. 7a and b) and this reduction was more prominent in FH-941 compared to FH-326. The HKT1 expression was recorded maximum in un-inoculated FH-941 plants subjected to salt stress. With FB-14 inoculation, in absence of salt stress, plants of both genotypes maintained an HKT1 expression comparable to control group (Fig. 7c). The FH-326 plants exhibited similar HAK5 expression patterns as that of control plants despite imposition of 150 mM NaCl stress while expression of this gene was significantly down-regulated with salt stress induction in FH-941 (Fig. 7d). Plants grown from FB-14 inoculated seeds (without or with salt stress) exhibited comparably better HAK5 expression than plants grown with NaCl stress alone (Fig. 7d).

Photosynthetic apparatus is affected by abiotic stress and a variety of coping mechanisms have been developed in plants [35]. The most important gene that has net effect on the photosynthesis process is light-harvesting chlorophyll a/b-binding (LHCB) proteins. Two gene families (LHCA and LHCB) in higher plants are responsible to encode proteins that constitute light-harvesting complex of photosystem I and II [36, 37]. Light harvesting chlorophyll a and b binding proteins help in photo protection under stress conditions. They quench harmful chlorophyll triplet states. These transcription proteins prevent dissemination of photo energy by excitation of electrons. In the current study, gene expression of light-harvesting chlorophyll a/b-binding protein (LHCB) was examined. The maximum LHCB expression was recorded in FH-326 when grown from FB-14 inoculated seeds in absence of salt stress while least expression was observed in FH-941 plants subjected to 150 mM NaCl (Fig. 8a). Compared to NaCl stress alone, plants of both genotypes exhibited better relative expression of LHCB when grown from FB-14 inoculated seeds despite presence of salt stress.

A specific transcriptional factor (TF) superfamily of higher plants “WRKY” is involved in numerous biological processes, the most prominent is defense against biotic and abiotic stresses. The function of WRKY protein is to combine/interact with W-box element of target protein and to inhibit/activate the transcription of downstream target genes [38]. In the current study, WRKY3 gene expression was down-regulated in both genotypes (FH-326 and FH-941) under salt stress (Fig. 8b). The WRKY34 gene expression was contrasting in the two genotypes where up-regulation was observed in FH-326 while FH-941 exhibited down-regulation of the same (Fig. 8c). The FB-14 inoculated plants (of both genotypes) exhibited optimum WRKY3 and WRKY34 gene expression (though slightly less than the expression recorded in the control group) in absence and presence of salt stress.

Pearson correlation

Pearson correlation indicated that root Na+ content were significantly correlated (positive or negative) with all the studied physio-biochemical attributes. Expression of NHX1, SOS1, HAK5, LHCB, WRKY3 and WRKY34 genes was negatively correlated but HKT1 was positively correlated with root Na+ (Suppl. Table 2a). However, a non-significant correlation was found between root Na+ and shoot fresh weight as well as root Na+ and proline content. All the studied genes have a negative correlation with antioxidant enzymes (CAT and POD) except HKT1 (Suppl. Table 2b). The proline content exhibited a non-significant correlation with the nutrient ions except shoot Na+ where proline was significantly negatively correlated (Suppl. Table 2a). Chlorophyll a, b, stomatal conductance, and rate of photosynthesis were highly negatively correlated with root/shoot Na+, but were positively correlated with root/shoot K+. Plant phenolic content were negatively correlated with expression of NHX1, SOS1, HAK5, LHCB, WRKY3 and WRKY34 genes but positively correlated with HKT1 expression (Suppl. Table 2b). Root and shoot Ca2+ content exhibited a strong correlation with stomatal conductance, rate of photosynthesis but a less significant correlation with chlorophyll a while a non-significant correlation with chlorophyll b. Root and shoot Mg2+ content were significantly correlated with chlorophyll a, b, stomatal conductance and rate of photosynthesis (Fig. 9). Discussion

Salt stress profoundly diminishes crop yield by disrupting natural growth and development of plants [39]. Therefore, various strategies have gained substantial consideration to counteract the adverse impacts of salt stress. Utilization of salt-tolerant plant growth promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) holds prominence strategy in this regard [40]. In this context, the current study was intended to discern the effects of Achromobacter sp. FB-14 inoculation on two cotton genotypes i.e. salt-tolerant FH-326 and salt-sensitive FH-941. The main factor contributing towards negative impact of salt stress on plant growth is the ionic osmotic effect which disrupts cell function and osmotic potential. The disruption in water intake leads to several adverse impacts. In the current study, root fresh weight of both genotypes increased where sensitive one (FH-941) exhibited higher fresh weight. However, shoot fresh weight was less affected in FH-326 (tolerant genotype) compared to FH-941. This indicated that FH-941 roots attempted to minimize salt-induced toxicity by higher water uptake as root-shoot water transport remain disturbed by presence of higher salt concentrations in soil-root interface. The stated possibility was further supported by less root and shoot dry weight of FH-941 compared to FH-326. Further, it was revealed that FB-14 inoculation demonstrated an increase in plant fresh as well as dry matter accumulation in both genotypes where FH-941 exhibited better response than FH-326. It seems likely that FB-14 stimulated more water uptake, root development and assisted in mitigation of salt-induced negative effects [13, 41].

Following the assessment of biomass, chlorophyll content was evaluated as biomass accumulation is inherently linked to photosynthetic efficiency, which is primarily governed by chlorophyll levels. Under salinity stress, plants exhibit a significant reduction in chlorophyll content which can be associated with less synthesis and/or increased activity of chlorophyllase that causes disintegration of chlorophyll [42]. The same was observed in the current study with more reduction of chlorophyll content in FH-941. However, chlorophyll content (chl a and b) was found to be enhanced with exogenous application of Achromobacter sp. FB-14 when compared with the un-inoculated plants. It has been found that the enhancement in chlorophyll content was associated with nutritional availability (uptake as well as root-to-shoot translocation of nutrient ions particularly K+ stimulated by PGPR inoculation). Furthermore, FB-14 inoculated plants demonstrated an increase in stomatal conductance and photosynthesis. Probably, these changes then potentially influenced source-sink interactions and modulated plant growth accordingly [41, 43]. Taken together, these changes suggest that FB-14 inoculation helps mitigate the negative impacts of salinity on photosynthesis. Maintaining chlorophyll content helps sustain photosynthetic activity under salt stress, while the buildup of osmoprotectants (e.g. proline) supports cellular balance by regulating osmotic pressure and protecting cell structures. Hence, proline accumulation was also evaluated as a key osmoprotective response to salinity stress [44]. In the current study, both cotton cultivars (FH-326 and FH-941) demonstrated improved proline levels in FB-14 inoculated plants thereby mitigating adverse effects of salinity stress. Beyond osmolyte accumulation, salinity causes a significant decrease in cellular protein content by impairing enzymes involved in protein degradation and synthesis. Our results indicated that higher sodium toxicity possibly triggered the pathways for protein denaturation, though more severely in FH-941 genotype [45].

Plants need to precisely coordinate the production and scavenging of ROS to identify and respond to stress signals [6]. Lipid peroxidation is utilized as a marker of oxidative damage under oxidative stress triggered by salt stress. It has been found that malondialdehyde (MDA) is a main degradation product, and that increasing salt concentrations promote oxidative damage due to the generation of ROS, which leads to membrane damage in plants [46].

In the current work, it was found that cotton genotype FH-941 exhibited increased levels of MDA when subjected to salt stress, indicating higher membrane damage. While FH-326 genotype exhibited low MDA level representing less membrane damage. The ability of FH-326 to maintain MDA content can be linked to enhanced nutrient absorption under bacterial inoculation that helps plants to maintain H2O2 content, reduce synthesis of ROS, and thus better membrane stability [5, 13]. In un-inoculated cotton plants, higher MDA content was also associated with higher H2O2 levels. Higher cellular H2O2 concentrations can interfere with important plant metabolites and bio-molecules as well as may likely disturb electron transport chain and photosynthetic pigments [47]. In the present work, H2O2 levels were found to be minimum in FB-14 inoculated plants of both genotypes and the current findings were in accordance with previous studies [40, 41] which state that PGPR inoculation could potentially be an important strategy for crop improvements under hostile environmental conditions [11]. The regulation of H2O2 levels is closely tied to the activity of antioxidant enzymes, as these enzymes play a central role in scavenging excess ROS and maintaining redox balance under stress conditions [46]. In the present work, peroxidase (POD) and catalase (CAT) activity was reduced in both cotton genotypes (FH-326 and FH-941) under salt stress compared to the plants grown with FB-14 inoculation.

Plants under stress often experience ionic imbalances, which can lead to nutrient deficiencies. Salinity reduces nitrogen, potassium, and phosphorous levels in cotton plants, primarily due to elevated Na+ and Cl− ions in the soil [48]. The influx of Na+ disrupts intracellular ion balance, inhibiting the uptake of K+, Ca2+, and Mg2+ and their utilization. Sodium and chloride ions also disrupt root and shoot absorption structures, altering the selectivity of semi-permeable membranes for ions [46]. Inoculation of cotton with FB-14 resulted in enhanced K+ absorption in roots but reduced root-shoot translocation compared to un-inoculated controls. In the current work, Ca2+ absorption and translocation were enhanced in both root and shoot of FH-326 as compared to salt-sensitive FH-941 genotype. This indicated that Ca2+ signaling has pivotal role in growth patterns of salt-tolerant plants under stress environments [49]. A balanced cellular Mg2+ concentrations are important for photosynthetic machinery, synthesis of DNA/RNA and proteins. So, Mg2+ is very important component of plant growth and development [50]. Our finding demonstrated that FB-14 inoculation enhanced Mg2+ content in cotton shoots in FH-326 genotype. The increased Mg2+ content assisted plants, under salt stress, by maintaining optimum photosynthetic activity [15].

Plant cells use three different strategies to address the Na+/K+ imbalance under salt stress conditions. These include (i) Na+ sequestration in vacuoles, (ii) efficient Na+ efflux, and (iii) transfer of Na+ from shoots to roots [51]. NHX1 is a Na+/H+ antiporter, located in the tonoplast, the main function is to compartmentalize excess Na+ into vacuoles [13, 32]. A non-significant change in NHX1 gene expression, under salt stress, in FH-326 indicated a possible role of this gene towards salt-tolerance. This was further validated by a low NHX1 expression in salt-sensitive FH-941. However, FB-14 inoculation up-regulated NHX1 gene expression in FH-941 genotype that may helped salt-sensitive cultivar to prevent Na+ toxicity, regulate the Na+/K+ ratio in the cytoplasm, manage the osmotic potential, and maintain vascular sequestration of Na+ under induced salt stress [51]. Another approach adopted by plants to reduce cytoplasmic Na+ toxicity involves excluding excess Na+ from inside the cell. A Na+/H+ antiporter (SOS1) present in the plasma membrane plays a key role in this process. The SOS1 gene expression was modulated in wheat under salt stress by Azospirillum brasilense Sp7 inoculation [52]. Under salt stress, inoculation of plants using FB-14 resulted in up-regulation of SOS1 gene expression in both genotypes compared to salt-stressed plants. Inoculation with FB-14 appeared to assist salt-sensitive FH-941 genotype in maintaining Na⁺ homeostasis by coordinating activities of plasma membrane and tonoplast ion transporters. A similar effect has been reported earlier with inoculation of Staphylococcus sciuri SAT-17 in salt-stressed maize [53]. Either modulated gene expression assisted FH-326 plants to restrict (or compartmentalize) Na+ or better K+ uptake up-regulated the salt-responsive genes need to investigate further [1, 53]. Plant lines deficient in HAK5 demonstrated decreased root elongation on low-K+ plates as well as decreased K+ uptake in the high-affinity range of concentrations [54]. In the current study, under salt stress, FB-14 inoculation up-regulated HAK5 gene expression in salt-sensitive FH-941 genotype. HAK5 expression is directly linked to K+ uptake. Potassium deprivation is a limiting factor for cotton yield and HAK5 gene is expressed, particularly under K+ deficiency, in plant roots. Its activity is enhanced under K+ deficiency by the transcriptional induction of the AtHAK5 gene [54]. However, PGPR-facilitated salt tolerance is a multifaceted mechanism comprising of modulation in nutrient uptake, ion transporter (and SOS pathway) patterns, and anti-oxidant status [15]. Salt-tolerant FH-326 plants exhibited an up-regulation of HAK5 under salt-stressed environment. Possibly, presence of high salt reduced K+ uptake (in an initial phase), resulting in imbalanced Na+/K+ ratio, leading to higher gene expression of HAK5 that in turn provoked more uptake of K+ as recorded in the current study [53]. Similarly, Wang et al. [31] highlighted that a better root K+ absorption (instead of Na+) may be achieved through PGPR induced modulated expression of SOS1, AKT1, and HAK5. Collectively, these results demonstrated that FB-14 inoculation supports the regulation of ion transporters and stress-related genes, assists to maintain ionic balance, enhance potassium uptake, and improve salt tolerance in the salt-sensitive FH-941 genotype under saline conditions.

This regulation of ionic homeostasis not only helps mitigate the harmful effects of salt stress but also plays a crucial role in maintaining plant growth and productivity by supporting efficient photosynthetic processes. Since photosynthesis is directly linked to light capture, maintaining an efficient light-harvesting system is vital for the plant’s overall stress tolerance. Light-harvesting chlorophyll a/b-binding (LHCB) proteins are few of the prominent players affecting the rate of photosynthesis. In the current work, under salt stress, FB-14 inoculation resulted in up-regulation of LHCB gene expression in FH-941 genotype, thus probably enhancing salt tolerance potential of FH-941 genotype.

In addition to enhancing LHCB expression, salt stress also modulated key transcriptional regulatory pathways. Transcription factors, such as GhWRKY3, are central to orchestrating plant responses under salt stress by regulating genes involved in ion homeostasis, antioxidant defense, and stress signaling pathways [32]. In the current study, GhWRKY3 gene expression was up-regulated by FB-14 inoculation in both genotypes grown under salt stress in comparison to un-inoculated salt-stressed groups. In a previous study, it was reported that WRKY3 gene enhanced tobacco plants tolerance against salt stress. Moreover, WRKY3 transgenic tobacco exhibited improved salt tolerance by raising ABA hormone susceptibility [55]. Similarly, GhWRKY34 may help plants respond to salt stress by regulating stress-related genes during growth and development. In the current study, FB-14 inoculation induced up-regulation of GhWRKY34 in FH-941 genotype, indicating its potential role in salt tolerance. It was earlier highlighted that overexpression of GhWRKY34 in Arabidopsis improved plant capacity to take up Na+ and K+ selectively and maintain a low Na+/K+ ratio in the roots and leaves that conferred salt tolerance [33]. Together, increased expression of LHCB, GhWRKY3 and GhWRKY34 may have contributed to improving salt tolerance in the FH-941 genotype.

Conclusions

The current study highlights the cellular and growth differences between two cotton genotypes “FH-326 and FH-941”. The genotype “FH-326” showed greater resilience to imposed salt stress through higher antioxidant enzyme activities and by maintaining cellular concentrations of important ions. Inoculation of cotton with Achromobacter sp. FB-14 assisted salt-sensitive FH-941 to maintain chlorophyll a content, photosynthetic efficiency and root/shoot ion concentrations by reducing oxidative stress through anti-oxidant machinery as well as modulated expression of salt-responsive genes. The findings suggest that FB-14 inoculation could be a useful strategy for improving cotton yield in saline soils and for strengthening crop resilience in challenging environmental conditions. Further transcriptome studies, involving more cotton genotypes, are suggested for deep deciphering of FB-14 mediated plant responses under high salt concentrations.
