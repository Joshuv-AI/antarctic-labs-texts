Distinct distributed neural dynamics predict pallium-dependent social approach
Authors: Imri Lifshitz, Asia Prag, Netta Livneh, Maayan Moshkovitz, Abeer Karmi, Lilach Avitan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A key component of social behavior is approach, where animals move toward social partners to maintain group cohesion and coordinate actions. While social cues are continuously encoded across modalities, it remains unclear whether a distinct neural process underlies social approach. We developed an experimental assay in which a head-fixed, tail-free zebrafish interacts with a freely swimming conspecific, enabling precise behavioral quantification alongside large-scale functional imaging at cellular resolution. We demonstrate that approach movements are more likely to be temporally coupled with conspecific movements, highlighting the interplay between spatial positioning and temporal coordination. Notably, distinct distributed neural activity emerges seconds before approach movements, characterized by increased activity in pallial neurons and reduced activity in midbrain and hindbrain populations. These coordinated dynamics reliably predict upcoming approach movements across regions and account for individual differences in social behavior. Moreover, we show that these neural processes are specific to the social context and rely on pallial activity. Together, our findings uncover a distributed yet coordinated neural mechanism underlying social interaction.

Introduction

Group living is a widespread strategy among animals, rooted in a fundamental social affiliative drive that motivates individuals to approach conspecifics1–3. This affiliative behavior supports critical social interactions essential for survival, reproduction, and resilience to stress4–8. While progress has been made in identifying brain regions and sensory modalities mediating this behavior, key questions remain unanswered9–12. Specifically, it is unclear how visual cues from conspecifics are processed to regulate social distance, how brain-wide neural dynamics mediate the approach toward conspecifics, and whether these dynamics differ from those underlying non-social behavior.

Several challenges hamper a comprehensive understanding of the neural processes underlying approach behavior toward a conspecific. First, the circuits driving this behavior are distributed across multiple brain regions, and capturing brain-wide neural activity with high spatiotemporal resolution remains technically unfeasible in mammals13. Second, the interactive and dynamic nature of social behaviors makes it difficult to isolate the influence of the self and the other on individual behavioral and neural responses. To address these challenges, we used the zebrafish as a model system. Its vertebrate brain organization and genetic homology to mammals make it well-suited for uncovering conserved neural processes14,15. Additionally, its optical transparency enables whole-brain imaging at cellular resolution, allowing precise tracking of neural activity underlying social behavior. Zebrafish also exhibit visually driven social behaviors early in development, and their discrete movements allow natural segmentation of the behavior, facilitating the study of dynamic social interactions16–20. Together, these advantages establish the zebrafish as a powerful system for dissecting the neural mechanisms underlying social approach behavior.

By two weeks of age, zebrafish maintain a short distance from conspecifics by actively approaching them17,20. This behavior is abolished in the dark20, suggesting a strong reliance on vision, although mechanosensory21–23 and olfactory24 contributions have also been reported. The approach toward conspecifics is known to be influenced by multiple spatial variables, including their relative position17,18,20,21,25 and depth26. In addition, temporal coordination plays a critical role: freely swimming zebrafish exhibit synchrony with movements of conspecifics16,19,23,27 and attraction to their movement statistics, even in the absence of reciprocal interaction18,28,29. These findings indicate that spatial dependencies in approach behavior are expressed within specific temporal regimes of conspecific movements. Yet, how approach behavior is jointly shaped by the relative position of a conspecific, the timing of its movements, and longer-timescale changes in movement direction remains unclear.

Our understanding of the neural circuits underlying fish social behavior has primarily relied on two complementary approaches. One uses synthetic social cues combined with functional imaging to isolate how specific cue features are encoded in the brain29–32. The other captures natural social behavior but relies on post hoc proxies of neural activity33–36. Although approaching conspecifics is central to social interaction, the lack of experimental paradigms that capture naturalistic social actions during brain-wide functional imaging has left the real-time neural dynamics of approach behavior largely unexplored. In addition, zebrafish, like other social species, exhibit individual variability in social behavior, with some individuals displaying strong affiliative tendencies while others engage minimally with conspecifics16,34. Whether and how this variability is reflected in neural activity remains an open question.

Here, we combined large-scale neural recording at single-cell resolution with an experimental assay to identify neural signatures of social approach. To preserve the natural complexity of social cues, we introduced a freely swimming conspecific to a head-fixed, tail-free focal fish. This assay not only reproduced spatial and temporal features previously observed in freely shoaling fish, but also provided new insights into the behavioral and neural processes underlying social approach. Specifically, focal fish were more likely to elicit approach movements when temporally coupling their movements with those of conspecifics. These approach movements were consistently preceded by distinct neural processes in the midbrain, hindbrain, and telencephalon, enabling the decoding of upcoming movements toward a conspecific and accounting for individual variability in social behavior. Finally, targeted pallial ablation demonstrated that this region is necessary for social approach and its associated neural dynamics. These findings provide a comprehensive understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying social approach behavior.

Results

An experimental assay for simultaneous recording of social interaction and large-scale neural activity

To overcome the challenge of recording neural activity from a fish that is engaged in social interaction with a real conspecific, we developed an experimental social assay. In this assay, a 15–17 days post fertilization (dpf) head-fixed, tail-free focal fish was embedded in the center of an inner transparent plate, observing an age-matched, freely swimming conspecific placed in an outer plate (n = 44 fish pairs). The physical separation between the two plates ensured that the responses of the focal fish to the conspecific were driven solely by visual information. The conspecific could swim freely within an arc spanning 230° of the visual field of the focal fish (extending 115° to each side). While the behavior of both fish was captured with a high-speed camera, we recorded large-scale neural activity of the focal fish using volumetric two-photon microscopy (Figs. 1A, B and S1A, B and Supplementary Movies 1–4, see Methods). To rigorously quantify the behavior of both fish, we preprocessed the raw camera frames and tracked each fish separately using a custom-developed tracking system. For each fish, we extracted the contour and the tail midline from the swim bladder to the tail tip. These extracted features enabled tracking of the angular position and direction of motion of the conspecific relative to the focal fish, as well as its segments of movement throughout the experiment (Fig. 1C, see Methods). Using the tail dynamics of the focal fish, we identified segments of movement and inferred the intended turn angle for each movement. This inference mechanism was trained and tested on recordings of freely swimming fish where tail dynamics and turn angles were measured, and our prediction could be validated (Fig. S2, see Methods). During the experiment, conspecifics spanned the entire arc area of the outer plate, and movements of the focal fish were elicited when the conspecific was at varying angular positions (Fig. 1D). Across all fish, conspecifics spent 75% of their time between −50° and 50° relative to the focal fish (Fig. 1E), where the predicted turn angle of 95% of focal fish movements fell within this range (Fig. 1F). While the focal fish elicited movements at a substantially lower rate than the conspecific, the movement rates of both fish remained stable throughout the 25 min of recording in the light (Fig. S1C). Together, this assay allowed accurate tracking of the fine details of two behaving fish where the focal fish observes a natural social cue.

Freely swimming 14 dpf larval zebrafish approach conspecifics by turning toward them, with a higher likelihood of turning right when conspecifics are in their right visual field and a lower likelihood when they are in their left17,20. We examined whether this tendency is conserved in our assay by calculating the probability of the focal fish turning right as a function of the angular position of the conspecific. Consistent with previous results, fish turned toward their respective conspecifics, with approach probability increasing at larger angular positions (Fig. 1G). The strength of this effect was comparable to that observed in freely swimming fish20. Moreover, in line with previous findings, the tendency to approach a conspecific in our assay was abolished in the dark (Fig. 1H)20, and was independent of the direction of motion of conspecifics (Fig. S1D)37,38. Hence, the behavior in our assay reproduced spatial findings previously observed in freely shoaling fish.

We next sought to verify that spatial information of the conspecific was encoded in tectal neural activity of the focal fish. To do so, we divided the visual field of the focal fish into four sections ranging from −50° to 50°, each covering 25° (Fig. 1Ii). Using four regressors indicating the onset of conspecific movements within each section, we identified neurons whose activity correlated with the angular position of the conspecific (Fig. 1J, top, see Methods). These neurons were topographically organized (Fig. 1Iii–iii). In addition to position, tectal neurons were correlated with the direction of motion of the conspecific (Figs. 1J, bottom and S1E), yet they did not exhibit a systematic spatial order (Fig. S1F), in agreement with previous reports39. This confirms that the focal fish observed the natural social cue and encoded its physical properties, such as position and direction of motion, in tectal neural activity. Together, these findings support the use of our assay as a platform for studying the neural basis of social approach behavior.

Approach behavior is jointly shaped by spatial and temporal information from conspecifics

By two weeks of age, freely shoaling larval zebrafish synchronize their movements by maintaining a short time lag relative to movements of conspecifics16,19,23. We examined whether focal fish in our assay similarly relied on temporal information provided by their conspecifics to elicit movements. Focal fish did not elicit movements at random timings, but tended to move shortly after conspecific movement onset, mostly during the glide phase, when angular velocity decreases (Fig. 2A). To assess this relationship, we aligned the onset of each focal fish movement to time zero and examined conspecific behavior within a 1.2-s window before and after movement onset. Focal fish were most likely to move approximately 150 ms after conspecific movement onset (Figs. 2B and S3A). This time lag between fish movements was significantly shorter than expected by chance (Figs. 2C and S3B). The empirical distribution of time lags between fish movements was further captured by a simple model of two Poisson processes when inducing a fraction of coupled movements, but not when the processes were fully independent, confirming that focal fish tended to synchronize their movements with those of conspecifics (Fig. S3C, D see Methods). Next, we asked whether spatial and temporal factors interact to modulate fish approach behavior. Specifically, we investigated whether focal fish movements occurring within the characteristic time lag following conspecific movements (locked movements) differed in their spatial relation to the conspecific compared to movements occurring outside this time lag (unlocked movements) (Fig. 2D, see Methods). For each movement group, we examined the relationship between the angular position of the conspecific at movement onset and the predicted turn angle of the focal fish. We found a significant relationship for locked movements, but not for unlocked movements, with a significant interaction effect, suggesting that these relationships differ between movement types (Fig. 2E). This indicates that movement synchrony is associated with an increased likelihood of turning toward a conspecific.

To further assess the relationship between synchrony and approach behavior across fish, we calculated the fraction of approach movements (defined as movements directed toward the conspecific, see Methods) for both locked and unlocked movements of each focal fish. Locked movements were significantly more likely to be approach movements than unlocked movements (Fig. 2F). These locked movements were associated with higher conspecific movement frequencies, closer to the characteristic frequency of 1.5 Hz at this age18 (Fig. S3E). Furthermore, the mean time lag between focal fish and conspecific movements was shorter for approach movements than for non-approach movements (Fig. 2G). These results indicate that approach behavior is jointly shaped by the relative position of conspecifics and the timing of their movements.

Freely swimming larval zebrafish exhibit considerable variability in their social behavior, with approximately 30% of 2–3-week-old larvae showing little to no social interaction16,34. Consistent with these reports, 35% of fish in our dataset displayed low approach probabilities (Fig. S3F). Supporting the validity of this measure, a separate dataset (n = 11 fish) revealed that approach probability in our assay was positively correlated with the social preference of the same fish during free interaction (Fig. S3G, see Methods), demonstrating that our assay captures meaningful individual differences in social behavior. We then asked whether this variability in fish approach behavior was related to their tendency to synchronize movements. To quantify synchrony level of each focal fish with its conspecific, we measured the distance between the empirical time lag distribution and a chance level time lag distribution (using Kullback-Leibler divergence, DKL, see Methods), with a larger distance indicating a stronger tendency to synchronize movements. We found that synchrony level varied across individuals and was correlated with approach probability (Fig. 2H), suggesting that individual differences in movement synchrony contribute to variability in social approach behavior.

In addition to short-timescale synchrony, we examined whether focal fish movements also relate to longer timescale structures in conspecific behavior. Larval zebrafish have been shown to swim in alternating sequences of rightward and leftward turns, lasting up to 20 s40. Similarly, conspecifics in our assay exhibited rightward and leftward movement sequences relative to the focal fish (median duration: 9.5 s), with each sequence beginning when the conspecific changed its direction of motion (Fig. S3Hi). The onset of these conspecific sequences occurred across the full range of angular positions with no bias toward particular angles (Fig. S3Hii). Focal fish tended to move early in these sequences (Figs. 2I and S3I), with approximately 60% of their movements occurring within the first half of a conspecific sequence. This suggests an additional temporal relationship, operating on a longer timescale, that links focal fish movements to changes in the direction of motion of conspecifics.

Distinct neural processes predict upcoming approach movements on a single-trial level

The established social assay enabled us to record activity from over 12,000 neurons across the brain of the focal fish during social interaction (Fig. 3A, see Methods). While neural encoding of various sensory features of conspecifics, such as shape31 and swim frequency29 was studied, we examined whether distinct neural processes are associated with approach behavior toward conspecifics. We first characterized the neural activity preceding and following focal fish movements, using two regressors representing activity before and after each movement onset (Fig. S4A, see Methods). Thousands of neurons across the brain showed increased activity following focal fish movements, whereas an order of magnitude fewer neurons displayed ramping activity prior to the movement which decreased afterward (Fig. 3B). We next examined whether neural activity linked to approach movements of the focal fish differs from that associated with non-approach movements, and whether such differences, if present, emerge before or after the movement. To address these questions, we first focused on the midbrain, a region known to be involved in fish social behavior14,31. We calculated the mean population neural activity for approach and non-approach movements at each time point within a 10-s window before and after the movement. We then computed the cosine distance between the corresponding activity patterns (Fig. 3Ci, see Methods). To control for baseline differences between arbitrary divisions into two movement groups, we repeated this analysis for two randomly selected groups. Distance values were higher before movement onset than after, both for approach vs. non-approach movements and in the control comparison (Fig. 3Cii). This suggests a general phenomenon where neural activity diverges before fish movements and becomes more similar after movements. Interestingly, before movement onset, the distance between approach and non-approach movements exceeded that of the control groups, with this neural distinction emerging several seconds before the movement. Across fish, this distinction was evident in the midbrain and hindbrain but not in the telencephalon and diencephalon (Fig. S4C), suggesting that distinct neural processes in these regions precede approach movements toward a conspecific.

We then asked whether distinct neural processes were evident at the level of single movements. To test this, we first measured the distance between the population activity of each single movement of the focal fish and the mean population activity of both approach and non-approach movements (calculated on a leave-one-out basis, Fig. 3Di). Population neural activity of single approach movements more closely resembled the mean activity of approach movements than that of non-approach movements, with this distinction evident only before the movement (Figs. 3Dii and S4D). To further examine whether single approach movements are associated with similar neural patterns, we averaged the population neural activity over 5 s preceding each movement and computed pairwise similarity between all pairs of population vectors (Fig. 3Ei), generating a similarity matrix. Hierarchical clustering of this matrix revealed two distinct clusters that largely corresponded to approach and non-approach movements (Fig. 3Eii), but not to right and left turns (Fig. S4E). Moreover, approach and non-approach movements did not differ in other kinematic features, including predicted turn angle, inter-bout interval, movement duration, tail amplitude, or tail frequency (Fig. S4F), suggesting that these clusters reflect social approach rather than differences in movement kinematics. Together, these results suggest that individual approach movements share a common neural signature emerging seconds before movement onset.

To examine whether the prolonged neural distinction emerging seconds before approach and non-approach movements reflects the long-timescale structure of conspecific behavior, we asked whether the emergence of this distinction depends on the duration of conspecific movement sequences. Conspecifics often moved in sequences of rightward and leftward movements (Figs. 2I and S3H), with substantial variability in the time between the onset of such sequences and a focal fish movement (Fig. S4G). We divided both approach and non-approach movements into two groups based on whether they followed a long or short sequence of conspecific movements (median threshold of 7.6 s between short and long sequences across fish, see Methods). In long sequences, distinct neural processes for approach and non-approach movements emerged approximately 10 s before movement onset, whereas in short sequences, this divergence emerged closer to movement onset (Figs. 3F and S4H). This suggests that the observed distinction in neural activity preceding approach and non-approach movements is related to the time elapsed following a change in the direction of motion of conspecifics.

Given the neural distinction before approach and non-approach movements, we asked whether the upcoming movement identity (approach or non-approach) could be decoded from the preceding population activity. We identified the neurons that contributed most to the distance between population responses preceding approach and non-approach movements (see Methods), focusing on the midbrain and hindbrain, regions where this distance was significantly evident across fish (Fig. S4C). These neurons were spatially distributed throughout the midbrain and hindbrain (Fig. S4I). Based on the mean activity of these neurons over the 5 s before movement onset, we decoded movement identity with accuracy significantly above chance in both regions (Fig. 3G, see Methods). This confirms that fish approach behavior is represented by distinct neural activity in the midbrain and hindbrain and can be accurately predicted several seconds before movement onset.

A notable inter-individual variability in decoding performance was observed (Fig. 3G), and while some fish showed higher performance than chance, others did not, presumably because the behavior was not fully established in all fish at this age (35% non-social fish). Similarly, the neural distinction preceding approach and non-approach movements (represented by the area between the curves, see Fig. 3D) was greater than chance and showed considerable inter-individual variability (Fig. S4J). Therefore, we asked whether these neural differences between individuals can explain the observed variability in approach behavior. Indeed, we identified a correlation between the area between the curves and approach probability across fish (Fig. 3H). This effect was not related to a bias in the fraction of right vs. left turns between approach and non-approach movements (Fig. S4K). Taken together, these findings indicate that distinct neural dynamics in the midbrain and hindbrain predict approach behavior and account for its inter-individual variability.

Coordinated distributed neural dynamics precede approach behavior

Our results demonstrate that upcoming approach and non-approach movements can be predicted from midbrain and hindbrain neural activity preceding the movement based on the geometric distance between the respective population responses. We asked what pattern of neural activity determines this geometric distance and drives the distinction between these responses. To address this question, we examined the neurons that contributed most to this distance, and found that they exhibited reduced activity before approach movements (Fig. 4A, top) and increased activity before non-approach movements (Fig. 4A, bottom). Across fish, this distinction was evident before movement onset and gradually diminished after the movement (Fig. 4B). To confirm that this neural pattern was related to the visual cue, we repeated the analysis using the same identified neurons for approach and non-approach movements elicited in the dark. Since the neurons distinguishing approach from non-approach movements were suppressed before approach movements, we asked whether another set of neurons might be active, potentially responsible for their suppression. Therefore, we searched for neurons with increased activity before approach movements and reduced activity before non-approach movements using an approach regressor obeying this pattern (Fig. 4Ci, see Methods). We identified neurons across the brain that correlated with this activity pattern. Projecting the highly correlated neurons from different fish onto a reference brain (see Methods), we found a dense concentration of neurons in the telencephalon, with fewer neurons sparsely distributed across other brain regions (Fig. 4Cii). These telencephalic neurons demonstrated the expected pattern of increased activity before approach movements and reduced activity before non-approach movements (Fig. 4D, E), a distinction that was consistent across angular positions of the conspecific (Fig. S5B). Importantly, neither these neurons nor the midbrain and hindbrain neurons with the opposite activity pattern encoded the angular position of the conspecific (Fig. S5C). As in the midbrain and hindbrain, the distinction in telencephalic neural activity disappeared in the dark (Fig. S5D) and was robustly evident at the level of single movements (Fig. S5E). These results suggest that approach movements are distinguished from non-approach movements also by the activity of a specific subset of telencephalic neurons, with a pattern opposite to that observed in the midbrain and hindbrain.

The observed distinction in telencephalic neural activity before approach and non-approach movements suggested that the identity of the upcoming movement could also be decoded from this region. To test this, we applied a logistic regression decoder, as previously used in the midbrain and hindbrain. For this analysis, we used neurons highly correlated with the approach regressor (Fig. 4C, see Methods). The decoder predicted the upcoming movement identity based on telencephalic activity significantly better than chance (Fig. 4F), with a higher effect size compared to other brain regions (telencephalon: Cohen’s d = 1.25, diencephalon: d = 0.83, midbrain: d = 1.06, hindbrain: d = 0.85). Interestingly, decoding performance in the telencephalon correlated with the above-mentioned decoding in the midbrain and hindbrain (Fig. 3G), with high decoding accuracy in the telencephalon associated with high accuracy in the midbrain and hindbrain (Fig. 4G), despite the use of distinct neuronal populations in each region. This highlights a distributed neural mechanism, with opposing activity patterns preceding approach behavior (Figs. 4H and S5F). Together, these results indicate that telencephalic activity contributes to the prediction of upcoming movements as part of a coordinated network.

Pallial neurons are necessary for socially specific approach behavior

The neural signature preceding social approach movements raised the question of whether this activity is specific to social approach or reflects a more general mechanism engaged whenever the focal fish moves toward an object. To address this, we conducted an experiment with two counterbalanced 30 min sessions on the same focal fish (n = 7). In one session, we recorded neural activity and behavior while the focal fish observed a real conspecific. In another session, it observed a projected dot that reproduced the trajectory of the conspecific but moved continuously (Fig. 5A, see Methods). On average, approach probability did not differ between the sessions (paired t-test, p = 0.1). However, distinct neural activity preceding approach vs. non-approach movements emerged only when focal fish observed a real conspecific (Fig. 5B). Moreover, the opposing patterns of reduced midbrain and hindbrain activity alongside increased telencephalic activity before approach were present only in the conspecific condition (Fig. 5C). Since social approach movements were preceded by increased activity in a small telencephalic population, we sought to localize this population within the telencephalon. Registering these neurons to a 21 dpf brain atlas41 revealed that they were predominantly located in the pallium (Fig. S6A, see Methods). To test whether these neurons were necessary for social behavior, we performed targeted two-photon laser ablations of pallial neurons, and measured social preference16 within individuals (n = 9) before and 24 h after ablation (Fig. 5D, see Methods). On average, 46 neurons were ablated per fish across both hemispheres, spanning a depth of 40 μm. Ablation was confined to the targeted neurons (Fig. 5Ei), and neighboring neurons remained functionally active when assessed 24 h post-ablation (Fig. 5Eii). Prior to ablation, fish displayed a wide range of social preference levels, with most individuals preferring proximity to conspecific cues. Following ablation, fish exhibited a significant reduction in social preference compared to their pre-ablation baseline (Fig. 5F, G and Supplementary Movie 5). Importantly, following ablation, movement kinematics remained unaltered (Fig. S6B), and visual function remained intact (Fig. S6C, see Methods), excluding gross motor or sensory deficits. To test for nonspecific effects of the ablation or embedding procedure, we performed two control experiments: sham ablations in the olfactory bulb, which is not required for visually guided social behavior in this assay (Fig. 5G, n = 10 fish), and embedding without ablation (Fig. S6D, n = 7 fish). Neither control produced a change in social preference (see Methods). These results indicate that pallial neurons are necessary for social approach.

Lastly, we asked whether ablation of pallial neurons also affected the distinct neural activity preceding approach movements. To address this, a subset of ablated fish (n = 5) was retested 24 h post-ablation in our assay with simultaneous two-photon calcium imaging and behavioral tracking. Repeating the decoding analysis of approach and non-approach movements based on the preceding neural activity, we were unable to decode movement identity in the telencephalon (Fig. 5H), despite the ablation of only a small population of pallial neurons. We next examined the cosine distance between mean population vectors of approach and non-approach movements in the midbrain and hindbrain, and found that this distance did not differ from chance following ablation (Fig. 5I). Together, these results indicate that pallial activity is not only required for social approach behavior, but also crucial for the distinct neural signature that precedes it.

Discussion

Social interactions are dynamic, complex, and high-dimensional, presenting major experimental and computational challenges for uncovering their behavioral principles and underlying neural mechanisms. We developed an experimental assay for simultaneous recording of large-scale neural activity and social interaction in zebrafish, revealing fundamental behavioral and neural principles of social approach. This assay presents naturalistic visual input from a real conspecific under stable imaging conditions, providing a complementary and technically simple alternative to virtual-reality approaches, which can replay and systematically manipulate realistic conspecific stimuli. Using this assay, we found that approach movements are driven by the interplay between spatial and temporal features of conspecifics. These approach movements were preceded by distinct neural activity patterns in the midbrain and hindbrain, as well as in a small population of pallial neurons, revealing a socially-specific signature emerging seconds before approach behavior. These distributed patterns enabled accurate decoding of upcoming social actions within each region, and accounted for inter-individual variability in approach behavior. Furthermore, targeted ablation of pallial neurons reduced social behavior and abolished the neural signature preceding approach movements, indicating that the pallium is required for both social approach and its predictive neural dynamics. By combining large-scale neural imaging and ethologically relevant social stimuli in an experimental assay that captures social actions, this work provides a mechanistic account of the neural processes underlying social approach behavior at single-cell resolution.

Brain-wide, interconnected circuits regulating social behavior are an evolutionarily conserved feature across diverse taxa, from fish to birds and mammals14,15,42. Consistent with this framework, our results reveal that the neural processes underlying social approach behavior are not confined to a single brain region but are distributed across multiple areas. Our work extends this understanding by identifying distinct neural dynamics within specific telencephalic, midbrain, and hindbrain populations, which exhibit opposing activity patterns before approach movements (Fig. 4H). Interestingly, we observed prolonged neural dynamics emerging several seconds before the onset of a social movement, aligning with sustained signals reported to predict upcoming decisions43–45. Our results suggest that these long-timescale dynamics encode the nature of the upcoming movement and are linked to changes in conspecific motion direction (Figs. 3F and S4H).

The ventral telencephalon, including the ventral nucleus of the ventral telencephalon (Vv), has been implicated in social behavior in zebrafish24,33,35,36 and is considered evolutionarily related to the mammalian basal ganglia13. Our results further reveal a key contribution of the dorsal telencephalon and its distinct neural signature during social behavior. Specifically, we identified a small subset of neurons in the pallium, a limbic structure considered homologous to the hippocampus and amygdala46–48 and associated with social behavior49–52. These neurons exhibited a characteristic activity pattern that distinguished approach from non-approach movements (Fig. 4E) and was specific to the social context (Fig. 5C). Targeted ablations revealed that this pallial population is necessary for social behavior, as removal of these neurons reduced social preference (Fig. 5G). Following ablation, the predictive neural dynamics preceding approach movements were abolished in the telencephalon, as well as in the midbrain and hindbrain (Fig. 5H, I). Notably, the ablated pallial neurons were identified based on their anatomical location rather than functional involvement in social behavior, and their molecular identity, neurotransmitter content, and connectivity remain to be determined. Nevertheless, the loss of predictive dynamics following pallial ablation suggests that intact pallial processing is required for the distributed neural signature preceding social approach behavior.

The thalamus sits at the entry point of vertebrate social decision circuits, integrating multimodal social cues and projecting to action-regulating nodes53. Across species, the dorsal thalamus carries a conserved, frequency-tuned code that tracks species-typical temporal structure22,29. Consistent with this, a small subset of diencephalic neurons in our social assay exhibited increased activity at the characteristic frequencies of conspecific movements18 (Fig. S6E). However, these neurons did not distinguish approach from non-approach movements (Fig. S6F), suggesting a primarily integrative sensory role upstream of action selection. The absence of distinct neural activity in the thalamus preceding approach behavior, despite its presence in the pallium, aligns with teleost anatomy, where direct thalamo-pallial projections are limited and thalamic outputs primarily target the subpallium47,54. Since our recordings focused on the dorsal thalamus, we cannot exclude ventral thalamic contributions to approach discrimination, and future work should test this with targeted recordings and perturbations.

Our data reveal substantial inter-individual variability in approach behavior, consistent with previous reports16,34. By analyzing this variability, we identified both behavioral and neural factors that may account for these differences. From a behavioral perspective, we found that focal fish with stronger temporal coupling between their movements and those of conspecifics were more likely to approach conspecifics (Fig. 2H). This suggests that the ability to synchronize with social partners is a key component of social behavior. From a neural perspective, we observed that the more distinct the neural activity patterns preceding approach and non-approach movements, the higher the probability of approach behavior (Fig. 3H). This finding suggests that the formation of shoaling behavior may arise from a progressive refinement of these neural distinctions. Further research is required to uncover how this neural distinction between approach and non-approach movements is shaped by development16–18,20,31, prior social experience21,31,34,55, neuropeptides22,24,28,35,36,56,57, genetics58–60, and internal states24,61,62. Our work established a robust framework for dissecting the contribution of each factor at the functional neural level.

Social interactions range from simple, reflexive, and unidirectional behaviors to complex, reciprocal, and cognitively demanding exchanges. Despite this diversity, they share fundamental features: actions that are temporally coupled and spatially oriented with respect to a conspecific. These principles are evident across species, from collective odor responses in flies63 to parental behavior in mice10 and to gaze following in humans64. In mammals, coordinated social actions rely on interactions between prefrontal circuits, subcortical reward systems, and motor areas9. Our findings indicate that zebrafish engage similar structures during social approach despite their simpler neural architecture. Moreover, we identify a pallium-dependent signature preceding social approach, which is robust enough to predict whether an upcoming movement will be directed toward a social partner. Whether prefrontal activity modulates downstream reward and motor circuits using similar activity profiles remains an open question. Zebrafish offer a unique opportunity to study social behavior at the cellular level across the entire vertebrate brain, advancing our understanding of brain-wide neural dynamics mediating social behavior.

Methods

Zebrafish maintenance

Nacre zebrafish (Danio rerio) embryos expressing elavl3:H2B-GCaMP6s65 were collected and raised according to established procedures66 and raised at 27 °C with a light and dark cycle of 14/10 h. Fish were fed live rotifers (Brachionus plicatilis) daily from 5 dpf. Experiments took place when zebrafish were 15–17 dpf. At this age, the sex of the fish cannot be determined. All procedures were performed with approval from The Hebrew University of Jerusalem Animal Ethics Committee. The Hebrew University is accredited by the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International (AAALAC).

An experimental social assay

Behavioral recording

A single focal fish was embedded head-fixed and tail-free in 2.5% low-melting agarose at the center of an optically transparent petri plate (Nunc 171099, 35 × 10 mm, thickness: 0.6 mm, refractive index ≈1.59). This plate was placed within a larger optically transparent petri plate (Corning Gosselin, 55 × 15 mm), held in place by custom-made 3D-printed clips (Vero, Stratasys), with the space between the plates filled with fish media. A freely swimming conspecific was introduced into the outer plate, occupying 230° of the focal fish’s visual field at a distance range of 17–28 mm from the focal fish, aligning with the reported attraction zone18,21,29. Behavioral recording of both fish was conducted from the bottom using a FLIR Grasshopper 3 camera at 166 Hz. A projector (Optoma ML750ST) illuminated the outer plate of the conspecific, and the plates were further illuminated using infrared LEDs (850 nm). The water temperature was set to 27 °C, maintained with a thermoplate, and monitored using a thermocouple (Tokai Hit). Following embedding, the focal fish was habituated in darkness for 30 min, and the conspecific was habituated in the outer plate for 5 min. The experiment began with a 5-min dark phase, after which a physical shutter on the projector was removed to allow the illumination of the plates. This experiment was conducted on 44 fish pairs across 31 independent experimental days. To increase the number of focal movements collected under this assay in the dark, we recorded an additional 6 fish pairs that were kept in darkness throughout the experiment. A detailed drawing of the behavioral setup, including all measurements, is available in our data repository67. Focal fish that elicited movements almost exclusively to one side (absolute median predicted turn angle >25°, n = 3) were excluded from the dataset. Non-social fish, with approach probability below 0.4 (n = 6/44), were excluded from the quantification of social behavior (Figs. 1 and 2), without affecting the significance of any reported effect.

Functional volumetric two-photon calcium imaging

Simultaneously with behavioral recording, volumetric two-photon imaging (Nikon objective, CFI × 16, 0.8 NA) captured neural activity across the telencephalon, diencephalon, midbrain, and hindbrain within a 705 × 352 × 70 μm volume (8 imaging planes) at 3 volumes/s (Fig. S1B and Supplementary Movie 4). This configuration provided broad brain coverage while maintaining sufficient temporal resolution. The top imaging plane was positioned, on average, 110 μm below the skin surface, enabling concurrent coverage of all four regions. For each focal fish, neural activity was imaged for 5 min in the dark, followed by 25 min in the light. Fish were excluded from the neural analysis if their recordings showed z-axis drift during functional imaging, exhibited low signal-to-noise ratio, or had synchronization issues between the microscope and the camera. This resulted in functional imaging data from 21 fish.

z-stack acquisition and image registration

Anatomical stacks were acquired at the end of the functional session and used for registration and anatomical segmentation. To acquire these stacks, we performed z-stack imaging of the entire brain (300–350 imaging planes, 1 μm apart) in several focal fish (n = 6) using the same microscope objective as in the functional imaging (1 s/plane). While a brain atlas for 6 dpf zebrafish is available, no published atlas currently exists for 15–17 dpf fish. Therefore, these fish were registered to a reference brain (selected from the recorded brains for function and structure), aligning all neurons within the same coordinate system (Figs. 4Cii and S4I). The registration process was done using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs68).

In a separate process, we registered our 16-dpf reference brain onto a 21-dpf brain atlas41 to identify the telencephalic structure containing neurons highly correlated with the approach regressor. This procedure revealed that these neurons were located in the pallium (Fig. S6A).

Preprocessing of neural data

The raw volumetric two-photon imaging data were processed using Suite2p69, including motion correction and cell detection. The ΔF/F traces for each cell were then calculated, with the baseline set to the 8th percentile of a moving window (167 s). An additional denoising step excluded cells whose ΔF/F distribution was not different from a Gaussian, a characteristic of non-responsive noisy neurons. Similar to a previous study31, the brain of each focal fish was manually divided into four major regions: telencephalon, diencephalon, midbrain, and hindbrain.

Behavioral tracking and feature extraction

Fish behavior was tracked using a custom-made algorithm originally developed for tracking natural behavior in freely swimming fish70. To remove stationary objects and maintain pixels that varied in their brightness, each raw frame was converted into a binary image by subtracting from each pixel its minimal intensity value recorded throughout the experiment. This step captured fish movements while eliminating stationary background objects. These binary images were then used as input for our algorithm, which tracked the behavior of each focal fish and conspecific. This step resulted in the extraction of key features, including the fish contour and the tail midline, extending from the swim bladder to the tail tip. To detect the timing of movements for both fish, we examined the velocity of the points along the tail and set a threshold for mean velocity across points to identify the onset and offset of each movement.

Predicting turn angle from tail dynamics

To develop a mechanism for predicting turn angles, we analyzed tail dynamics in freely swimming fish, where tail postures and the corresponding turn angles were accurately tracked and quantified. We used a natural behavior dataset in which individual fish (n = 15) were recorded for 15 min in a plate containing paramecia, using high spatiotemporal resolution imaging (500 Hz, 0.02 mm/pixel, Fig. S2A)70. During this period, fish engaged in both hunting behavior, detected by eye convergence, and exploratory behavior, characterized by diverged eyes. We tracked the tail midline from the swim bladder to the tail tip across all movements. Each tail posture was divided into 100 equidistant segments, and the angle of each segment relative to the horizon was computed (Fig. S2B). A high-dimensional representation of movement could thus be defined by a series of segment angles throughout the movement (Fig. S2C).

Previous studies have shown that tail postures are highly characteristic, with three principal components (PCs) explaining most of the variance in tail movements71,72. We reproduced this result in our dataset, showing that in both freely swimming fish and head-fixed, tail-free fish, the first three PCs accounted for approximately 85% of the variance (Fig. S2D). Importantly, the PCs extracted from head-fixed fish closely resembled those of freely swimming fish during hunting or social interactions (Fig. S2E), suggesting a strong similarity in tail postures across behavioral contexts. Each movement consisted of a sequence of tail postures, which could be represented as a time series of coefficients along the three PCs (Fig. S2F). Since fish first turn and then move forward or backward70, we focused on PC coefficients during the initial 30 ms of each movement, concatenating them into a single row vector. Tail dynamics were therefore represented in a matrix (A), where each row is a movement, and each column is a frame. For freely swimming fish, we measured changes in turn angle, constructing the result vector (b). We then used a linear regression model to find a transformation vector (x) that would optimally convert PC coefficient vectors into the corresponding turn angles (Fig. S2G).

Once the transformation vector was extracted, we evaluated its accuracy by comparing predicted and actual turn angles in freely swimming fish. We first trained the model on hunting movements of one fish and tested it on hunting movements of another fish. The model accurately predicted turn angles (Fig. S2H), suggesting that it could be generalized across individuals. We then used this transformation vector to predict the turn angles of focal fish in our assay based on their PC coefficients. To verify that these predictions did not depend on the behavioral context used for training, we compared the predicted turn angles obtained when the model was trained on hunting fish to those obtained when trained on either exploration or social interaction. The predictions derived from the hunting model were strongly correlated with those from both exploration and social models (Fig. S2I), indicating applicability across behavioral contexts. Notably, all PCs used for prediction were aligned in sign to ensure comparability across contexts and imaging conditions. Given that this transformation vector generalized across fish and behavioral contexts, and that PCs from head-fixed fish closely resembled those from freely swimming fish, we applied this method to predict the turn angles of focal fish in our assay.

Assessing movement synchrony

The synchrony level of each focal fish was measured using the Kullback-Leibler divergence (DKL) between its empirical time lag distribution and a chance level distribution, calculated using a conspecific from a different experiment. For each focal fish, the conspecific with the closest number of movements to the actual conspecific was selected. To account for the asymmetry of DKL, we averaged this measure over both directions of comparison. The observed synchrony in our dataset was also demonstrated using a Poisson process-based model with three parameters drawn from the empirical data: the movement rates of the focal fish and the conspecific, and the fraction of locked movements elicited by the focal fish (coupling fraction). The chance level distribution of the model was generated using two independent processes with no synchrony.

Classifying focal fish movements

The classification of locked and unlocked movements was based on the time lag distribution between the movements of each focal fish and those of its respective conspecific. A threshold was set for each fish based on the difference between the empirical time lag distribution and the chance level distribution, allowing us to identify the value below which the empirical distribution exceeded chance. Focal fish movements with time lags below or above this threshold were defined as locked or unlocked movements, respectively.

The classification of approach and non-approach movements was based on the predicted turn angle of the focal fish. Movements with a predicted angle greater than 5° to either side (right or left) were defined as approach movements if directed toward the half of the visual field where the conspecific was located (e.g., rightward movements when the conspecific was on the right). Movements directed to the opposite side of the conspecific were defined as non-approach. Analyses based on the probability or average of approach/locked movements included fish with more than eight movements of that type to allow robust statistics.

The division of approach and non-approach movements according to long and short conspecific sequences was based on the duration from the onset of the closest conspecific sequence to the movement onset of the focal fish. For each focal fish and movement group (approach and non-approach), the median duration from conspecific sequence onset was used as a threshold: movements elicited after a shorter/longer duration than the median were classified as movements following a short/long sequence, respectively.

Validating the tethered social assay using freely swimming fish

To validate our assay in light of the variability among individuals, we compared the social preference index (SPI) of freely swimming fish in an established social preference assay16 with the approach probability of the same individuals in our assay (Fig. S3G, n = 11 fish across 3 independent experimental days). Each fish was first acclimated in a U-shaped chamber for 15 min, after which two conspecifics were introduced into a pre-determined compartment (alternating between right and left), and behavior was recorded for an additional 15 min. Immediately afterward, the same fish was embedded in agarose and tested in the tethered assay for 20 min to measure its approach probability. The social preference index of freely swimming fish was calculated as the difference between the number of frames the focal fish occupied the region adjacent to the conspecifics (social zone) and the mirror-symmetric region on the opposite side, divided by the total number of frames in the session. One outlier fish with SPI lower than two standard deviations below the mean was excluded.

Identifying neurons correlated with regressors

To identify tectal neurons correlated with the angular position of the conspecific, we divided the frontal 100° of the focal fish’s visual field into four sections. A separate regressor was generated for each section, with regressor activity levels set to one when the conspecific moved within the corresponding section of the visual field. Each regressor was convolved with the temporal response kernel of GCaMP6s. Neurons with correlation values exceeding two standard deviations above the mean of the distribution of all neurons were considered highly correlated. Next, for each neuron within these four populations, we identified peaks in activity and averaged the conspecific angular position across all peaks, allowing us to quantify the preferred angular position of each neuron (Fig. 1I).

To identify neurons responsive to conspecific motion direction, we constructed two regressors (convolved with the GCaMP6s kernel) that were active at the onset of rightward or leftward conspecific sequences. Additionally, two regressors with the same number of sequences, but with randomly drawn timings, were constructed. Neurons were considered highly correlated if their correlation value with the actual regressor exceeded the 99th percentile of the corresponding random correlation value (Fig. S1E). This cutoff was applied to all regressors as detailed below.

To identify neurons active before or after focal fish movement, we constructed two box regressors with elevated activity either preceding or following each movement, both lasting 6.66 s (Fig. S4A). Two additional control regressors were generated with the same number of movements, but with randomly drawn timings. The 99th percentile of the correlation values with the control regressors was set as a threshold.

Similarly, we constructed a box approach regressor (Fig. 4Ci) with elevated activity before approach movements and reduced activity before non-approach movements. The corresponding control regressor exhibited elevated activity before a randomly selected group of focal fish movements and reduced activity before the other group. The 99th percentile of the correlation values with the control regressor was set as a threshold.

To identify neurons responsive to the swimming frequency of conspecifics, we calculated conspecific swimming frequency in 10-s windows throughout the experiment. We then constructed a box regressor with elevated activity in windows where the conspecific frequency fell within the characteristic range (1.2–1.8 Hz) and zero elsewhere. A control regressor with elevated activity in shuffled windows was generated to assess chance level correlations, with the 99th percentile of its correlation distribution used as the threshold. For each focal fish, the mean activity of the highly correlated neurons with the characteristic-frequency regressor was calculated separately for the 1.2–1.8 Hz range and for lower frequencies (Fig. S6E).

Decoding movement identity

To decode the upcoming movement identity (approach and non-approach) based on midbrain and hindbrain neural activity (Fig. 3G), we used a logistic regression decoder with leave-one-out cross-validation. For each fish and brain region, the 300 most contributing neurons were selected in each iteration using a training set, and their mean activity over the 5 s before movement onset was used to predict the left-out movement. Using the same procedure, we decoded movement identity of the two control groups, repeating it 50 times with different random realizations. To decode movement identity based on telencephalic neural activity (Fig. 4F), we used the same logistic regression model, selecting the 30 neurons most strongly correlated with the approach regressor. The different number of neurons used for decoding in the telencephalon compared to the midbrain and hindbrain reflected the population size that contributed to the neural distinction between approach and non-approach movements. While hundreds of neurons contributed to the cosine distance in the midbrain and hindbrain (Fig. S4I), only a small subset of telencephalic neurons (23 on average across fish) were highly correlated with the approach regressor (Fig. 4Cii). The number of movements in each group was balanced, and fish with more than 10 movements in each group were included.

Control experiment for social specificity

To demonstrate that the identified neural patterns are specific to social approach and do not occur during approaches to other objects, we designed a control experiment. This control experiment (Fig. 5A–C) consisted of two sessions, presented in a counterbalanced order, during which behavior and neural activity were recorded for 30 min. In one session, focal fish viewed a real conspecific, whereas in the other, the same individuals observed a synthetic moving dot occupying 4° of the visual field. The dot followed the trajectory of an example real conspecific, but moved continuously. All technical details were identical to those of the main experiment. Following the second session, a z-stack of the focal fish’s brain was acquired and subsequently registered to the reference brain from our dataset, allowing us to track the activity of the same neurons across the two sessions.

Assessing the effect of pallial ablation on social preference

To assess the role of the pallium in social behavior, we performed ablation experiments. In these experiments, 15 dpf fish (n = 9) were tested in the social preference assay (as described above) before and after targeted laser ablation of pallial neurons (Fig. 5D). Immediately following the first social preference session, each fish was embedded in agarose and positioned under the two-photon microscope. The pallial region of interest was visually identified by the experimenter. Ablations were performed across five planes spaced 10 μm apart, starting from the dorsal part of the pallium. Within each plane, 3–5 neurons per hemisphere were targeted and ablated sequentially, with an average of 46 pallial neurons across fish. Since ablation targeted neurons based on anatomical position rather than functional activity, the number of ablated neurons was larger than the identified pallial population active before approach movements (23 neurons on average), to increase the likelihood of targeting these neurons. Each neuron was stimulated for 1–1.5 ms with a 920 nm laser pulse at 350 mW. We confirmed ablation by the immediate appearance of non-fluorescent cores in the targeted somata, followed by the disappearance of these neurons 24 h later (Fig. 5Ei). A z-stack of the telencephalon was acquired before, immediately after, and 24 h after ablation for structural comparison.

Following ablation, each fish was released from agarose, paired with an age-matched wild-type conspecific, and allowed to recover overnight. On the following day, fish were assessed for successful prey capture to exclude gross visual or motor impairments. The majority of fish who successfully fed (n = 9) were included in subsequent experiments. This assessment was consistent with subsequent functional imaging analyses (n = 5 fish) showing preserved tectal selectivity to visual cues in pallium-ablated fish (Fig. S6C). To control for behavioral effects arising from the experimental procedure and from laser exposure independent of pallial neuron loss, we performed two control experiments. In both cases, fish underwent the same experimental protocol as the pallial ablation group, including social preference testing, agarose embedding and release, and overnight recovery. In one control condition (Fig. 5G, n = 10), fish underwent sham laser ablation in the olfactory bulb, a region not required for visually guided social preference in this assay. Ablations were performed using the same two-photon protocol and targeted a tissue volume comparable to the pallial ablation, with an average of 30 olfactory bulb neurons ablated per fish. In a second control condition (Fig. S6D, n = 7), fish were embedded and released but were not subjected to laser ablation.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using MATLAB and Python. Statistical details are provided in the figures and their captions. All tests were two-tailed. When comparing different conditions or movement types within the same fish, we used paired t-tests or repeated-measures ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.
