Fractional Orbital Angular Momentum Sorting Using Wind Spiral Transformation
Authors: Xiaobin Yang, Mengran Fan, Hao Zhang, Kun Liu, Juntao Hu, Yuping Tai, Yijie Shen, Xinzhong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOrbital angular momentum (OAM) mode sorting is a crucial component of the optical multiplexing systems based on the optical vortex beam carrying OAM. However, the number of sortable integer‐order OAM states remains limited. Herein, we propose a general spiral transformation, that is, wind spiral transformation, to conduct sortable fractional‐order OAM states to solve the existing drawback. By transforming a fractional‐order OAM mode into a superposition of integer‐order OAM states, the sorting capacity of OAM mode is significantly extended, where the proposed method is suitable for integer and fractional OAM mode sorting. Further, the proposed scheme is applied to image encoding and decoding, resulting in good image reconstruction and unlocking a scalable paradigm for high‐dimensional information encoding and decoding. This proposed sorting method shows great potential for implementing highly confidential data storage systems, which may encourage further applications in classical and quantum regimes.

Introduction

Optical vortex (OV), which possesses a spiral phase structure in the form of exp(ilθ), carries an additional degree of freedom, namely, the orbital angular momentum (OAM) [1, 2, 3]. In the spiral phase structure exp(ilθ), θ is the azimuthal angle around the beam axis, and the integer l is the topological charge (TC), where the magnitude of OAM is lℏ per photon [4, 5]. Recently, the OAM in OV beam has attracted significant attention in optical communication [6, 7] and quantum information due to its discrete mutually orthogonal modes in the spatial dimension [8, 9, 10].

There are many methods to generate an OV beam with OAM, such as using q‐plates, spiral phase plates, and integrated on‐chip components [11, 12, 13]. Besides generation, OAM mode sorting is a key issue for optical communication systems, which efficiently decomposes different OAM modes in terms of power loss and intermodal crosstalk [14, 15, 16]. Based on Mach–Zehnder interferometers, the OAM modes are sorted efficiently; however, complexity increases with the number of sorted OAM modes [17]. The same complexity also appeared in the mode sorter based on multi‐plane light conversion [18, 19]. The simplest way to circumvent this complexity was to use a specially designed Dammann grating [20] or cascaded tunable resonator [21], which diffracted different OAM‐states into different diffraction orders. However, the specially designed grating only works for a specific TC, where the efficiency was limited to 1/N, where N is the number of OAM‐states.

To sort the OAM states efficiently, Berkhout et al. proposed a method based on the log‐pol coordinate transformation [14]. However, mode overlap between adjacent OAM states occurred due to the limitations of the log–pol coordinates transformation in mathematics. Adding a fan‐out element can effectively reduce mode overlap at the expense of device efficiency and simplicity [15]. Yu et al. proposed a logarithmic spiral transformation to achieve low mode overlap without increasing the complexity of the device [22]. Cheng et al. achieved lower mode overlap by replacing the logarithmic with Archimedes’ spirals transformation, whereas the cost is a reduction in the OAM recognition range and the appearance of background noise [23]. Furthermore, coordinate transformation is not only for OAM sorting but also for designing a specific coordinate transformation to realize OAM states multiplication [24, 25, 26, 27]. In summary, it is an effective method to reduce mode crosstalk by optimizing the types of spiral transformation. The references above are focused on sorting integer OAM states. Predictably, fractional OAM states will provide more versatile modes for expanding information capacity.

To achieve fractional OAM state sorting with high resolution, we propose a general spiral model and apply it in coordinate transformation, that is, wind spiral transformation, which may be used to realize efficient and low‐crosstalk OAM mode sorting. The key idea is to increase the number of spiral turns within the same annular width. Compared with the existing spiral transformations, the proposed method exhibits more helical turns within the same annular width. Because the width of the characteristic peak is inversely proportional to the number of helical turns, the proposed wind spiral transformation helps to reduce mode crosstalk. Consequently, it offers a promising approach for efficiently sorting fractional‐order OAM states. In our case, the Perfect Laguerre–Gaussian (PLG) beam is chosen as the carrier of integer and fractional OAM states, owing to its minimal variation in radius with different fractional TCs. The experimental results also show that the proposed wind spiral transformation has optimistic performance in integer or fractional OAM states sorting. Further, the wind spiral transformation is applied in image encoding and decoding, which provides an approach to similar research areas such as information encryption, ultrahigh‐speed photography, and high‐dimensional information multiplexing with coordinate transformation [28, 29, 30, 31].

Principles, Methods, and Experimental Setup

As mentioned above, OAM mode crosstalk negatively correlates with the number of spiral turns. Increasing spiral turns is beneficial for reducing the mode crosstalk. Compared with the logarithmic spiral used in traditional spiral transformation, the wind spirals have more turns under the same OV size condition. To highlight the main idea, we compare the principal schemes between the logarithmic spiral transformation of Figure 1a and the proposed wind spiral transformation of Figure 1b. Because the logarithmic spiral contains an exponential function, the spiral radius r shows steep variation with the increase of θ. As illustrated in Figure 1a, for an input OAM state with topological charge l, the 2πl excursion of the phase is transformed into a stripe‐shaped output beam with the same phase distribution in the output plane. After passing through the lens, the input OAM states are sorted into different positions in the focal plane. As shown in Figure 1b, this proposed wind spiral transformation works by modulating the stripe‐shaped output beam to carry multiple copies of the 2πl phase excursion, thereby reducing mode crosstalk and achieving fractional OAM states sorting.

To verify the proposed OAM states sorting method, we set up an experimental device as shown in Figure 2b. The laser beam was transformed into an approximate parallel beam by passing through a pinhole filter and lens L1 (f 1 = 150 mm). Apertures A1 and P1 were used to intercept the central part of the expanded beam to obtain an approximate flat–top beam with more even intensity distribution and ensure horizontally polarized light, respectively. The expanded beam passed through the beam splitter BS and illuminated the spatial light modulator 1(SLM1, HOLOEYE, PLUTO‐VIS‐016, pixel size: 8 × 8 μm2), which was used to generate PLG beams carrying different OAM states. The modulated beam illuminated at SLM2 (SLM2, CAS MICROSTAR, FSLM‐2K39‐P, pixel size: 4.5 × 4.5 μm2) is loaded with a spiral converter, enabling it to transform gradually into a straight line‐shaped beam during propagation. Then, the straightened beam, modulated by the spiral converter, is incident on SLM3 (CAS MICROSTAR, FSLM‐2K39‐P, pixel size: 4.5 × 4.5 μm2) loaded with a phase correction mask, transforming into a tilted plane wave. Finally, a Fourier transformation was performed using lens L2 (f 2 = 500 mm), enabling the beams carrying different OAM states to be spatially separated and recorded by a complementary metal‐oxide semiconductor (CMOS, Basler acA1600‐60gc, pixel size of 4.5 × 4.5 μm2).

To study the variation of PLG beam diameter with different TCs, the PLG beam and its diameter VS TC of 0–50 were generated and determined, which is depicted in Figure 2c,d. As illustrated in Figure 2d, the PLG beam diameter exhibits a weakly decreasing trend with TC increasing. This property facilitates efficient sorting of OAM states. The comparison results indicate that the wind spiral transformation can generate more turns under the same conditions. To quantitatively evaluate and compare the sorting capabilities of different spiral transformations, we introduced a common figure of merit, Optical Fidelity, which characterizes the sorting capability of the sorter. Optical Fidelity is the ratio of the distance between adjacent OAM states over their average FWHM [34]. In our case, the Optical Fidelity achieved by the wind spiral transformation is improved by 45% and 25% compared with the Archimedean spiral and logarithmic spiral transformations, respectively, demonstrating its superior sorting performance. In addition, the mode purity (MP), which reflects the similarity between experimental and simulated results, is evaluated. Due to the presence of background noise, the measured MP is approximately 80%; however, this does not affect the sorting resolution of the sorter.

Surprisingly, the wind spiral transformation performs satisfactorily in fractional OAM state sorting, even though it does not uniquely identify the fractional value itself. This property manifested in that fractional OAM states sorting results are overlapping with adjacent integer OAM output. This is caused by the mismatch in fractional OAM states. To explore the potential application of the proposed OAM states sorting method, that is, wind spiral transformation, as a proof‐of‐concept demonstration, we perform OAM‐assisted image encoding/decoding to functionally validate the proposed wind‐spiral sorter for structured‐light information transfer. Hexadecimal representation provides a compact and efficient means of data encoding, which is particularly advantageous in optical information processing [38]. As illustrated in Figure 6a, optical vortex array representations of a pixel with grayscale value 128 are shown in decimal (3 PLGs), binary (8 PLGs), and hexadecimal (2 PLGs) formats, respectively. The hexadecimal format has more concise information presentation capabilities. Because complete encoding of all grayscale values can be achieved only using one 2‐bit hexadecimal representation, we adopt this 2‐bit hexadecimal encoding scheme for grayscale image processing. As shown in Figure 6b, the left Encoding part shows a PLG beam array for hexadecimal encoding, with each TC corresponding to a different code symbol. The right Decoding part shows the corresponding results of OAM sorting.

(a) Vortex array patterns corresponding to the grayscale value 128 represented in decimal, binary, and hexadecimal. (b) Encoding and decoding maps corresponding to the hexadecimal representation, respectively.

Because each pixel in a grayscale image has a grayscale value between 0 and 255, all pixel values can be encoded entirely using one 2‐bit hexadecimal representation. That is to say, grayscale values can be comprehensively represented using an optical vortex array consisting of two OAM states. Subsequently, to validate the theoretical framework described above, we performed grayscale image encoding and decoding experiments. We utilized a natural cloud vortex image of 372 × 407 pixels for the encoding demonstration, as shown in Figure 7a. First, a 5 × 2 pixel cell is randomly selected in the grayscale image, as shown in Figure 7a, and the corresponding grayscale values were extracted. After converting the extracted grayscale values into hexadecimal form, a 5 × 4 PLG beams array, as shown in Figure 7b, is generated based on the correspondence between hexadecimal characters and OAM states in Figure 6b. Then, the OAM states in this PLG beam array pass through the wind spiral transformation device in sequence, and decoding results were obtained, as shown in Figure 7c. The horizontal axis position of the characteristic peaks of the decoding results can be obtained by reading the one‐dimensional intensity profiles of the decoding results. Finally, these positions are mapped back to hexadecimal grayscale values to reconstruct the original image.

(a) Source image and grayscale values of the selected region. (b) Encoding the vortex array and the corresponding hexadecimal representation. (c) Each step of the encoding and decoding process was conducted from left column to right column.

At last, we conducted an experimental verification of the image encoding and decoding process using the natural cloud vortex image shown in Figure 7a as the source image. The detailed procedure is illustrated in Figure 8. First, the grayscale matrix of the source image is extracted, as shown in Figure 8b, and then converted into a hexadecimal grayscale matrix. Next, the hexadecimal grayscale matrix is encoded using PLG beams with different OAM states, generating a PLG beam array as shown in Figure 8c, where a pair of PLGs represented each byte. Subsequently, the PLG beam array was processed through the proposed wind spiral transformation for OAM sorting, and the sorting results are shown in Figure 8d. By reading the characteristic peaks' position, i.e., the bright fringes' horizontal position, from each decoded mode, we obtained the characteristic peak coordinate matrix, as illustrated in Figure 8e. Then, according to the previously established correspondence between coordinate positions and hexadecimal characters, the hexadecimal grayscale matrix was reconstructed, as shown in Figure 8f, and converted back into a decimal grayscale image. Finally, the source image was reconstructed from the grayscale matrix, as shown in Figure 8g, where pixels marked in red indicate decoding errors. According to the calculation, the bit error rate, which is defined by the ratio of the total number of encoded pixels (151,404 pixels) to the erroneous pixels (139 pixels), is as low as P e = 0.09%. In case of developing commercial products, there are many conditions to be systematically considered, including capacity scaling, costs, latency, security implications, and so on. Furthermore, multiplexed FOAM can significantly improve encoding and decoding speed but it also introduces problems such as mode overlap and a lower signal background ratio (More details see Supporting Information S1: Notes 9 and 10).

Conclusions and Outlook

In summary, we have proposed an efficient and low‐crosstalk OAM sorting method, termed wind spiral transformation, which leverages fractional‐order OAM states to overcome the limitations on the number of sortable modes. Starting from the theoretical predictions, we propose a general spiral mode, that is, wind spiral transformation, which converts OVs with fractional OAM states into bright fringes at distinct spatial positions. Effective sorting is achieved by identifying the characteristic peak positions of these fringes. Numerical simulation analysis and experimental demonstrations validate the feasibility of sorting integer and fractional OAM states. Furthermore, the proposed OAM mode sorting technique is successfully implemented in optical image encoding and decoding, exhibiting high fidelity, with a measured bit error rate as low as 0.09%. It can be further reduced by increasing the channel range. Although the wind spiral transformation performs disadvantageous in FOAM multiplexing, it paves the way for optical communication based on FOAM and data storage system. In the future, this disadvantage may be solved by combining deep learning [39, 40]. More attractively, the proposed scheme can further provide encryption for the communication process, opening up new applications in both classical and quantum regimes.
