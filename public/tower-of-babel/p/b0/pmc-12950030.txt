Maximum persistent Betti numbers of Čech complexes
Authors: Herbert Edelsbrunner, Matthew Kahle, Shu Kanazawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis note proves that only a linear number of holes in a Čech complex of n points in Rd can persist over an interval of constant length. Specifically, for any fixed dimension p<d and fixed ε>0, the number of p-dimensional holes in the Čech complex at radius 1 that persist to radius 1+ε is bounded above by a constant times n, where n is the number of points. The proof uses a packing argument supported by relating the Čech complexes with corresponding snap complexes over the cells in a partition of space. The argument is self-contained and elementary, relying on geometric and combinatorial constructions rather than on the existing theory of sparse approximations or interleavings. The bound also applies to Alpha complexes and Vietoris–Rips complexes. While our result can be inferred from prior work on sparse filtrations, to our knowledge, no explicit statement or direct proof of this bound appears in the literature.Keywords: Čech complexes, Betti numbers, Persistent homology, Snap complexes

Introduction

What is the maximum number of holes created by n possibly overlapping closed unit balls in a Euclidean space, and how big can they be? To move toward a more precise formulation of this question, let p<d be two positive integers, and consider the asymptotic behavior of

as n→∞. Here, B(x, 1) is the closed ball of radius 1 centered at x, and βp of the union of such balls is the p-th Betti number of this space. The Čech complex of A for radius r, denoted by Cˇr=Cˇr(A), is the simplicial complex whose vertices are the points in A, and whose simplices are the subsets of points such that the closed balls of radius r centered at these points have a non-empty common intersection. By the Nerve Theorem, the Čech complex for radius r=1 has the homotopy type of the union of the unit balls in (1). Therefore, Mp,d(n) is also the maximum p-th Betti number of Cˇ1, for any set of n points in Rd.

Recently, Edelsbrunner and Pach (Edelsbrunner and Pach 2024) proved that Mp,d(n)=Θ(nmin{p+1,⌈d/2⌉}). For example, M1,2(n) grows linearly in n, but M1,3(n) and M2,3(n) grow quadratically in n. The upper bound is easily derived from the Upper Bound Theorem for convex polytopes, see e.g. (Ziegler 1995), and their main contribution is the actual construction that proves that this upper bound is asymptotically tight. However, most holes in their construction appear to be small: they vanish when the balls are slightly enlarged. In the language of persistent homology, they have short lifetimes. Note that the scale need not be fixed at r=1: by rescaling the point configuration that attains the maximum, we may consider the persistent Betti number βp(Cˇr,Cˇ(1+ε)r) for any r≥0. When we fix the parameter ε>0, most of the holes in Edelsbrunner and Pach’s construction are no longer counted since they do not persist even to radius 1+ε, so it is not surprising that the asymptotic behavior of Mp,d,ε(n) is different from that of Mp,d(n), and we show that Mp,d,ε(n) grows only linearly in n. In words, the number of holes that cover a fixed interval of positive length in the Čech filtration is at most some constant, depending only on ε and the dimension d, times the number of points. It may also be of interest to consider the case in which the constant ε>0 depends on n. For further details, see Section 5.

The Čech complex has the same homotopy type as the Alpha complex for the same points and the same radius (Edelsbrunner and Harer 2010, Section III.4), , which is a subcomplex of the Delaunay mosaic of the points (Delaunay 1934). Hence, Mp,d(n) is also the maximum p-th Betti number of the Alpha complex of n points in Rd, and Mp,d,ε(n) is also the maximum p-th persistent Betti number of the Alpha complex for unit radius included in that of radius 1+ε. Less obviously, our linear upper bound for Mp,d,ε(n) extends to the inclusion of Vietoris–Rips complexes for unit radius in that of radius 1+ε. While we do not know of a direct connection, every step in our proof of the bound for Čech complexes extends to Vietoris–Rips complexes. The resulting linear upper bound for the persistent Betti numbers should be compared to the bounds of Goff (Goff 2011) on the (non-persistent) Betti numbers of Vietoris–Rips complexes. For p=1, he shows a linear upper bound in all dimensions, which is stronger than our linear upper bound for the persistent Betti numbers. However, for p>1, his bound is only o(np), which is much weaker than our linear upper bound for the persistent Betti numbers.

Related work on sparse approximations of filtrations

Although our initial motivation was to contrast the asymptotics of the maximum Betti number Mp,d(n) with those of the maximum persistent Betti number Mp,d,ε(n) in the context mentioned above, we recognize that our main result is closely related to the literature on sparse approximations of filtrations. In this line of work, a substantial body of literature shows that the Čech and Vietoris–Rips filtrations can be sparsified—significantly reducing their size—while still capturing similar topological information.

Persistent homology records how topological features (such as components, loops, and voids) appear and disappear as the scale parameter varies in a filtration; the birth-death intervals (bars) of these features are summarized in a barcode. Many sparsification results are stated in terms of (multiplicative) interleavings. Informally, and sufficient for our purposes, a (1+ε)-interleaving between filtrations means that there are natural comparison maps consistently relating the complex at scale α in one filtration to the complex at scale (1+ε)α in the other, and conversely. In particular, each topological feature whose lifetime strictly exceeds a factor of 1+ε; that is: a bar that strictly covers an interval [α,(1+ε)α) for some α≥0 in one filtration has a corresponding feature in the other.

A seminal result is Sheehy’s sparse Vietoris–Rips filtration (Sheehy 2013). For an n-point set in Euclidean space (more generally, for an n-point metric space of bounded doubling dimension), he constructs a linear-size (O(n)-size) filtration, based on a hierarchical net-tree, that is (1+ε)-interleaved with the exact Vietoris–Rips filtration. The hidden constant depends only on ε and the dimension of the point set. The same philosophy has been developed for Čech filtrations. Of particular relevance is Kerber–Sharathkumar’s sparse Čech filtration (Kerber and Sharathkumar 2013), which introduces the well-separated simplicial decomposition (WSSD), a higher-dimensional analogue of the well-separated pair decomposition. Using a WSSD, they construct a linear-size filtration that is (1+ε)-interleaved with the exact Čech filtration. Further refinements and alternative viewpoints include Botnan–Spreemann’s Čech approximation via sequences of good covers (Botnan and Spreemann 2015) and the geometric perspective of Cavanna–Jahanseir–Sheehy, who show that a sparse complex can be realized as a nerve in one dimension higher (Cavanna et al. 2015). A high-dimensional lattice-based approach by Choudhary–Kerber–Raghvendra yields polynomial-size approximations—independent of the dimensionality of the point set—together with a super-polynomial lower bound on the size required for a high-accuracy approximation, thereby demonstrating the inherent accuracy/size trade-off in high dimension (Choudhary et al. 2019). For developments in the two-parameter setting, we refer the reader to (Buchet et al. 2023; Lesnick and Mccabe 2024) and the references therein.

How sparse approximation implies our linear bound on Mp,d,ε(n)

We herein explain how earlier results on sparse approximation imply our linear bound on the maximum persistent Betti number Mp,d,ε(n). For a fixed ε>0, suppose that in the Čech filtration (Cˇr)r≥0 there exists a super-linear number of bars that strictly cover an interval [α,(1+ε)α) for some α≥0. Any proper sparsification scheme described above produces a linear-size filtration that is (multiplicatively) (1+ε)-interleaved with the exact Čech filtration and therefore still carries all such bars. However, a linear-size filtration can support only a linear number of bars, because each bar requires at least one simplex to witness its creation or destruction. This contradiction shows that, in the exact Čech filtration, the number of bars whose death-to-birth ratio is strictly greater than 1+ε grows only linearly. Note that this argument handles all such longer bars simultaneously, across all scales.

Our contributions

As mentioned already, our main result is a linear upper bound on the maximum persistent Betti numbers over any fixed interval of positive length. Precisely, for fixed 0≤s<t, the p-th persistent Betti number βp(Cˇs,Cˇt) is bounded above by some constant times the number of points in Rd, where the constant depends only on the ratio t/s and the dimension d. By rescaling the point configuration, we may therefore focus on βp(Cˇ1,Cˇ1+ε) with ε=t/s-1>0.

Our method proceeds as follows: we mesh the Euclidean space into hypercubes of diameter at most ε and identify the points lying in each cell. Applying this identification to the Čech complex Cˇ1 produces a smaller simplicial complex Q1, which we call a snap complex (see Definition 2). The key observation can be summarized as follows: there is a triangle of simplicial maps formed by Cˇ1→Q1→Cˇ1+ε and Cˇ1→Cˇ1+ε, which commutes on the homology level. Consequently, the rank of the induced map Hp(Cˇ1)→Hp(Cˇ1+ε)—that is, βp(Cˇ1,Cˇ1+ε)—is bounded above by dim(Hp(Q1)), which is in fact linear in the number of the input points by a packing argument (see the remarks in Section 3 for details).

Our snap complex is akin in spirit to earlier work, particularly the constructions of (Kerber and Sharathkumar 2013) and (Choudhary et al. 2019). Both also mesh Euclidean space into hypercubes or permutahedra, yet their foci differ from that of our snap complex argument.

In (Kerber and Sharathkumar 2013), the construction of a multiplicatively (1+ε)-interleaved filtration exploits the well-separated simplicial decomposition (WSSD). Intuitively, a WSSD decomposes the points into small clusters whose diameters are tiny compared with the distances separating them and groups these clusters into tuples so that every Čech simplex is captured by at least one such tuple. The construction is more sophisticated and delicate because the clusters must be small relative to their mutual distances to guarantee a multiplicative approximation across all scales. Our snap complex approach, based on a uniform mesh size, avoids this technical step and proceeds directly to the bound on the persistent Betti number, since we essentially focus on a single scale.

In (Choudhary et al. 2019), Euclidean space Rd is meshed into permutahedral cells. For a given finite point set, each cell containing at least one input point is taken as a vertex of a new simplicial complex, and simplices are defined as the nerve of these occupied cells. Taking an arbitrary base scale β>0 and varying the mesh size over the geometric progression βck with c=4(d+1)2, they obtain a discrete tower of such complexes, connected by natural simplicial maps, which approximates the Vietoris–Rips filtration within an O(d) multiplicative factor while achieving smaller size bounds than Sheehy’s sparse Vietoris–Rips filtration. In contrast, our snap complex construction takes into account not only the mesh size but also the scale r, which allows us to build a sparse filtration (Qr)r≥0 of snap complexes that approximates the classical filtrations with arbitrary accuracy—though only in an additive sense—by refining the mesh.

While our main result—a linear upper bound on persistent Betti numbers over a fixed interval of positive length—can be deduced from earlier results on linear-sized approximations of filtrations as discussed above, our objective is of a different nature. We present a direct and self-contained proof based on elementary geometric and combinatorial arguments. Our approach circumvents the technically more involved steps of filtration approximations that deal with all scales simultaneously in a multiplicative sense, and addresses directly the bound on the maximum persistent Betti number at a single scale, thereby offering a more accessible and clear exposition of this fundamental fact. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, no explicit statement of this linear bound on persistent Betti numbers exists, despite its noteworthy contrast with the tight polynomial upper bounds on (non-persistent) Betti numbers of Čech complexes by Edelsbrunner and Pach (Edelsbrunner and Pach 2024). We believe it is worthwhile to clearly articulate and prove this fact, both for its intrinsic interest and for potential applications in stochastic topology (see Section 5 for further discussion).

Outline

The outline of this paper is as follows. Section 2 proves technical lemmas about how cycles are maintained if we glue vertices in a simplicial complex. Section 3 introduces the snap complex of a Čech complex and relates its Betti numbers to the persistent Betti numbers of the Čech complex. Section 4 combines these preparations to prove the linear upper bound on the persistent Betti number of Čech complexes. Section 5 concludes the paper.

Gluing vertices

Let A⊆Rd be finite. By definition, the Čech complex of A for radius r≥0 consists of all subsets B⊆A such that the closed balls of radius r centered at the points in B have a non-empty common intersection. Writing r(B) for the radius of the smallest enclosing sphere of B, we have B∈Cˇr(A) iff r(B)≤r. Letting B′⊆Rd be another finite set, the Hausdorff distance between B and B′ is

We show that the difference between r(B) and r(B′) is at most the Hausdorff distance between the two sets.

Lemma 1

|r(B)-r(B′)|≤H(B,B′).

Proof

Let x be the center of the smallest enclosing sphere of B, whose radius is r(B). By definition of Hausdorff distance, B′ is contained in the union of balls of radius ε=H(B,B′) centered at the points in B. Hence, the sphere with center x and radius r(B)+ε encloses B′, so r(B′)≤r(B)+ε. Symmetrically, r(B)≤r(B′)+ε, so r(B′)-ε≤r(B)≤r(B′)+ε, which implies the claim. □

We employ Lemma 1 to reduce the size of a cycle. To explain how, we briefly review standard terminology from homology. For simplicity, we work over the coefficient field Z/2Z, though the results extend to any field (see the remark immediately after Corollary 5 for a more algebraic approach). A p-chain, γ, is a collection of p-simplices, and the vertices of γ are the vertices of its p-simplices. To add two p-chains means taking their symmetric difference: γ+γ′=γ⊕γ′. The boundary of a p-simplex are its (p-1)-dimensional faces, and the boundary of γ is the sum of the boundaries of its p-simplices. A p-cycle is a p-chain with empty boundary, denoted by ∂γ=0. Two p-cycles are homologous, denoted by γ∼γ′, if there is a (p+1)-chain, Γ, that satisfies ∂Γ=γ+γ′. In this case, we say Γ is a filling of γ+γ′. Finally, γ is trivial if γ∼0.

Definition 1

Let γ be a p-cycle and x≠y vertices of γ. To glue x and y, we substitute a new vertex, z, for x and y in all p-simplices, and write γ|x∼y for the resulting p-chain.

After the substitution, a p-simplex that contains both, x and y, is a (p-1)-simplex and thus implicitly removed from the p-cycle by the gluing operation.

As illustrated in Figure 1, it is possible that after substituting z for x and y, we have two p-simplices with the same p+1 vertices, which, by definition, are two copies of the same p-simplex. By the logic of modulo-2 arithmetic, the two copies cancel each other. Substituting a new vertex z for two distinct vertices x and y in a simplicial complex defines a simplicial map, as every simplex is mapped to a simplex in the new complex with x and y replaced by z. Since the p-chain γ|x∼y is nothing but the image of the p-cycle γ under this simplicial map, γ|x∼y is also a p-cycle. We state the fact without proof, as it is elementary.

Lemma 3

Let γ be a p-cycle, x≠y two vertices of γ, γ′=γ|x∼y, and Γ=γ|x∼y. Then ∂Γ=γ+γ′.

Proof

The p-simplices in γ that neither belong to Stγ(x) nor to Stγ(y) also belong to γ′. Indeed, they exhaust γ′\Stγ′(z), which implies γ+γ′=Stγ(x)∪Stγ(y)∪Stγ′(z). By construction of Γ=γ|x∼y, and the assumption that γ is a p-cycle, the right-hand side of this equation is the boundary of Γ. □

Snap complex

For values 0≤s≤t, we write Cˇs=Cˇs(A) for the Čech complex, βp(Cˇs) for the rank of its p-th (reduced) homology group, Hp(Cˇs), and βp(Cˇs,Cˇt) for the rank of the image of Hp(Cˇs) in Hp(Cˇt) induced by the inclusion Cˇs⊆Cˇt.

Definition 2

Let Ψ be a partition of Rd into cells, and call the supremum diameter of the sets in Ψ the mesh of the partition, denoted by mesh(Ψ).

The snap complex, Qs, of the Čech complex Cˇs along Ψ is defined as follows: the vertices of Qs are the cells that contain at least one point of A, and a set {ψ0,ψ1,…,ψp}⊆Ψ of distinct cells is a p-simplex in Qs iff there exist vertices xi∈ψi for 0≤i≤p such that {x0,x1,…,xp}∈Cˇs.

Note that mapping each p-simplex {x0,x1,…,xp}∈Cˇ1 with xi∈ψi∈Ψ (0≤i≤p) to a simplex {ψ0,ψ1,…,ψp}∈Q1 defines a surjective simplicial map q:Cˇ1→Q1. Letting γ be a p-cycle in Cˇ1, we call α=q(γ) its image, and γ a preimage p-cycle of α. Given two preimage p-cycles of α, they may or may not be homologous in Cˇ1. The next lemma, however, guarantees that they are homologous in Cˇ1+mesh(Ψ).

Lemma 4

Let ε=mesh(Ψ), α a p-cycle in Q1, and γ,γ3 two preimage p-cycles of α in Cˇ1. Then γ∼γ3 in Cˇ1+ε.

Proof

We construct a sequence of homologous p-cycles that interpolates between γ and γ3 in Cˇ1+ε. There is an initial sequence interpolating between γ and γ1, a middle sequence interpolating between γ1 and γ2, and a terminal sequence interpolating between γ2 and γ3.

The initial sequence reduces the number of vertices until the preimage p-cycle contains at most one vertex per cell in Ψ. Indeed, if γ has vertices x≠y in the same cell, then we can glue x and y, as described in Section 2, which produces a new p-chain, γ′=γ|x∼y. This operation introduces a new vertex, z. We are free to choose its location, and to facilitate the repetition of this argument, we choose it where y used to be. By Lemma 2, γ′ is a p-cycle with one fewer vertices than γ. Let Γ=γ|x∼y be the (p+1)-chain from Lemma 3. If all simplices in Γ belong to Cˇ1+ε, then γ and γ′ are homologous in Cˇ1+ε, so assume that at least one simplex υ∈Γ does not belong to Cˇ1+ε. Then r(υ)>1+ε. By construction of Γ, and the choice of z’s location in the cell that also contains x and y, there is a p-simplex τ in γ that has a vertex in every cell that contains a vertex of υ. Since the points in the same cell have distance at most ε from each other, Lemma 1 implies r(τ)>r(υ)-ε>1. But then τ is not in Cˇ1 and neither is γ, which contradicts the assumptions. This implies γ∼γ′ in Cˇ1+ε. By repeating the argument, all p-cycles in the initial sequence are homologous in Cˇ1+ε and, in particular, γ∼γ1 in Cˇ1+ε.

The terminal sequence of p-cycles reduces the number of vertices of γ3 if read from the end forward. Appealing again to Lemmas 1, 2, and 3, all p-cycles in the terminal sequence are homologous in Cˇ1+ε and, in particular, γ2∼γ3 in Cˇ1+ε.

Since γ and γ3 are preimage p-cycles of the same p-cycle, α, their vertices lie in the same cells of Ψ, and so do the vertices of γ1 and γ2. The middle sequence interpolates between the latter two p-cycles by changing one vertex at a time to a possibly different point in the same cell. Appealing to Lemmas 1, 2, and 3, the p-cycles in the middle sequence are again homologous in Cˇ1+ε and, in particular, γ1∼γ2 in Cˇ1+ε. But now we have γ∼γ1∼γ2∼γ3 in Cˇ1+ε and therefore γ∼γ3 in Cˇ1+ε, as claimed. □

Remark on Vietoris–Rips complexes. Lemma 4 generalizes to Vietoris–Rips complexes. Indeed, its proof generalizes provided we adapt Lemma 1, which in its current formulation is specific to Čech complexes. For the Vietoris–Rips complexes, we read the radius r(B) as half of the maximum distance between any two vertices in B. Then it is still true that the difference in radii is bounded from above by the Hausdorff distance between the two sets of vertices. In other words, Lemma 1 also applies to Vietoris–Rips complexes.

By Lemma 4, any two preimage cycles of a cycle in Q1 that already exist in Cˇ1 are homologous in Cˇ1+ε. We can therefore bound the persistent Betti numbers by the Betti number of the snap complex.

Corollary 5

With ε=mesh(Ψ), we have βp(Cˇ1,Cˇ1+ε)≤βp(Q1) for every p.

Proof

Recall that βp(Cˇ1,Cˇ1+ε) is the rank of the persistent homology group that captures all p-cycles in Cˇ1+ε that already exist in Cˇ1. It suffices to prove that the images of non-trivial p-cycles in Cˇ1+ε that already exist in Cˇ1 are non-trivial p-cycles in Q1. To derive a contradiction, let γ be a p-cycle in Cˇ1 that is non-trivial in Cˇ1+ε, and assume that α=q(γ) is trivial in Q1. Hence, there exists a (p+1)-chain, A, in Q1 with ∂A=α, and because q is surjective, there also exists a (p+1)-chain Γ in Cˇ1 with q(Γ)=A. Noting that γ and ∂Γ are both p-cycles in Cˇ1 whose images under q are equal to α, we obtain γ∼∂Γ∼0 in Cˇ1+ε by Lemma 4. This contradicts that γ is non-trivial in Cˇ1+ε and implies the claimed inequality. □

Remark on the proof of Corollary 5. Alternatively, Corollary 5 can be deduced using a more algebraic argument based on contiguous simplicial maps. Starting with a simplex in Cˇ1, one may either include it directly into Cˇ1+ε or first map it to Q1 and then further to Cˇ1+ε, which is possible by Lemma 1. These two simplicial maps are contiguous, meaning that for every simplex in Cˇ1, the union of its images under the two maps is also a simplex in Cˇ1+ε. It is a standard fact that contiguous simplicial maps induce the same homomorphism on homology groups. Consequently, the triangle of simplicial maps formed by Cˇ1→Q1→Cˇ1+ε and Cˇ1→Cˇ1+ε commutes on the homology level, which immediately yields Corollary 5. Although this proof is arguably cleaner, we have chosen to present the current proof to make the geometric ideas more transparent.

Remark on the right-hand side of the inequality. It is easy to see that the bound in Corollary 5 is not tight. Take for example three points equally spaced on a circle of radius strictly between 1 and 1+ε. Then Cˇ1 has a 1-cycle of three edges, while in Cˇ1+ε this cycle is filled by the triangle. Hence, β1(Cˇ1,Cˇ1+ε)=0, which is strictly smaller than β1(Q1)=1.

Note also that we cannot replace the upper bound βp(Q1) in Corollary 5 with βp(Q1+ε) in general. The reason is that the image of a homologically non-trivial cycle in Cˇ1+ε may be homologically trivial in Q1+ε, even if it already exists in Cˇ1; see Figure 2 for an example. Indeed, we have 1=β1(Cˇ1,Cˇ1+ε)>β1(Q1+ε)=0 in this example.

The upper bound

We use a packing argument together with Corollary 5 to prove that for every ε>0, the number of homology classes born before or at 1 and dying after 1+ε in the Čech filtration of n points in Rd is bounded from above by a constant times n. This constant depends on ε and d but not on n.

For every ε>0, there exists c=c(ε,d) such that βp(Cˇ1,Cˇ1+ε)≤c·n.

Discussion

The main result of this paper is a linear upper bound on the number of holes in the Čech complex of n points in Rd that persist from radius r=1 to r=1+ε, in which ε is a fixed constant strictly larger than 0. The upper bound generalizes to the Alpha complex and the Vietoris–Rips complex and thus holds for three of the classic types of complexes used in topological data analysis (Carlsson 2009; Edelsbrunner and Harer 2010). The work reported in this short note raises a number of questions, and we mention two.

The first natural question is how small we can make ε>0 in our linear upper bound on the persistent Betti numbers when we think of ε as a function of n that tends to 0, rather than a fixed positive constant. A detailed look at the analysis shows that for even d≥4, the persistence of the counted cycles is proportional to (and smaller for odd d≥3), in which we simplify by assuming that d is a constant.
