Beta Power Response After Levodopa Intake in Parkinson's Disease Patients With Chronic Sensing‐Enabled Deep Brain Stimulation
Authors: Martijn G.J. de Neeling, Bart J. Keulen, Mariëlle J. Stam, Deborah Hubers, Rob M.A. de Bie, Bernadette C.M. van Wijk, P. Rick Schuurman, Arthur W.G. Buijink, Martijn Beudel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIn Parkinson's disease (PD), the power of beta oscillations (± 13–30 hertz [Hz]) from subthalamic nucleus (STN) local field potentials (LFPs) is associated with motor symptoms. Beta power can be used for adaptive deep brain stimulation (aDBS) algorithms based upon symptom fluctuations. The time course of beta power modulations after levodopa intake at home remains to be described.MethodsTen‐minute averages of beta peak power were recorded in 33 patients with PD treated with STN‐DBS for a median duration of 134 days during the titration phase. Median beta power after scheduled medication intakes (n = 24,103) and the effects of peak frequency, stun effect, stimulation amplitude, levodopa dose, and preoperative levodopa response were analyzed using cluster‐based permutation testing.ResultsBeta power was significantly reduced between 30 and 140 minutes after intake for stimulation amplitudes between 0 and 2.1 mA (p < 0.01). This response was seen in low (12.7–20.5 Hz) but not high (21.5–30.3 Hz) beta frequencies. Significant clusters were found regardless of stun effect and preoperative levodopa response, during low stimulation amplitudes (< 1 mA), and for 100 and 150 mg but not 50 mg doses. A linear mixed‐effects model for the area under the curve (AUC) levodopa beta response (30–180 min) revealed a dose–response relationship, a stronger negative response in low‐beta compared with high‐beta frequencies, and no significant effect of stimulation amplitude.InterpretationSTN beta power responds to levodopa during low to moderate stimulation amperages, depending on the beta peak frequency and dose, which is informative for aDBS implementation. ANN NEUROL 2025;98:1352–1363

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is an established treatment for Parkinson's disease (PD) 1 , 2 , 3 with favorable effects on symptom severity, time spent in the OFF medication state, and quality of life (QOL) at a group level. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 It is well established that the power of beta (±13–30 hertz [Hz]) oscillations in local field potential (LFP) signals from the subthalamic nucleus (STN) decreases after the intake of dopaminergic medication 6 , 7 and DBS. 8 Beta power also significantly correlates with the severity of contralateral bradykinesia and rigidity. 9 , 10 Although patients typically reduce 50% of their dopaminergic medication after DBS implantation, 11 they still depend on levodopa besides their DBS treatment. Hence, symptom severity may still fluctuate according to levodopa intake, 12 , 13 and it frequently occurs that DBS with continuous amperage (cDBS) cannot be sufficiently titrated upward due to the risk of dyskinesias ON medication. This problem might be circumvented by adapting the amount of stimulation based on the actual (dopaminergic) state of the patient as indicated by beta power. This form of DBS is called adaptive DBS (aDBS) and has been shown to improve the side effects and efficacy of DBS. 7 , 14 , 15

Setting up aDBS algorithms currently requires manual adjustment of LFP sensing thresholds, such as in the ADAPT‐PD study, 16 and is dependent on the levodopa‐induced beta response. Understanding these responses in the chronically implanted state can inform aDBS protocols in sensing‐enabled DBS devices, especially those that can record chronically outside of the hospital. 16 , 17 , 18 Besides the clinical relevance for aDBS, levodopa beta responses can influence electrophysiology‐based diagnostics and non‐DBS treatments in PD for both clinical and scientific purposes. Examples include studying intraoperative targeting 19 and, after implantation, DBS programming 20 and investigation of noninvasive alternatives for DBS, such as vagus nerve stimulation 21 and neurofeedback. 22 Therefore, it is essential to understand the electrophysiological time dynamics after levodopa intake, its influence on STN LFP recordings, and their use as physiomarker (a neurophysiological biomarker) for research, diagnostics, and treatment.

In this study, we investigated the relation between short‐acting dopaminergic medication (levodopa) intake and beta power in a cohort of patients with PD treated with a sensing‐enabled DBS system. We hypothesized that beta power decreases ± 30 to 60 minutes after the ingestion of levodopa. This would be in accordance with the average pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles of levodopa (with a peripheral decarboxylase inhibitor), showing a maximum plasma concentration after ± 30 to 45 minutes in a laboratory setting, 23 as well as with findings from previous STN‐LFP studies in experimental, in‐clinic settings, showing a substantial beta peak decrease after 1 hour. 6 , 7 , 9 Additionally, we investigated the frequency specificity of the levodopa‐induced beta fluctuations and the influence of the stun effect, 8 , 24 levodopa dose, and preoperative improvement of clinical symptoms 9 after levodopa intake (“screening response”) on the time course and power of these fluctuations.

Materials and Methods

Participants

The Strobe criteria were used in the reporting of this study. 25 This study was approved by the local ethics committee (NL80384.018.22) and was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed written consent was obtained from all patients. Patients with PD and bilateral DBS surgery were screened for eligibility between November 2022 and May 2024. Inclusion criteria were DBS implantation in the STN with a Medtronic Percept (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) neurostimulator, the use of short‐acting levodopa with a minimum of 50 levodopa equivalent dose (LED) per intake during the total study period, availability of the medication regimen with the times and doses of the levodopa intakes, the presence of a beta peak in the recorded LFP power spectral density (PSD) on the first day after surgery regardless of the levodopa intake, defined by the highest microvolts power (μVp) value with lower μVp values in adjacent frequency bins between 12.7 and 30.3 Hz, and a minimum of 4 weeks of LFP recordings with available beta power data for 100 intakes 60 minutes before until 180 minutes after each intake. The median beta peak power with interquartile range (IQR) OFF medication and OFF stimulation can be found in Supplementary Table S1 – clinical characteristics. Exclusion criteria were the use of continuous intestinal or transdermal levodopa, serious adverse events after DBS surgery, and the presence of any cognitive and/or psychiatric problems interfering with adherence to the medication schedule. This includes documented mild cognitive impairment (MCI) on the preoperative neuropsychological examination or ongoing psychiatric symptoms. Leads were implanted in the STN (SenSight directional lead model 33005; Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) and connected to the Percept neurostimulator using previously described local techniques (for details see Ref 26) in the context of standard clinical care. For details regarding the acquisition and processing of the LFPs, please refer to the Supplementary Methods section.

Statistical Analysis

The frequency of interest (FOI) for tracking levodopa‐induced fluctuations in the LFPs was determined on the first day after surgery and adjusted if needed in the following clinical visits using various BrainSense features, which can be found in the Supplementary Methods. Additionally, at every clinical visit, a Unified Parkinson Disease Rating Scale part III (UPDRS) III score was determined. The time course of the BrainSense timeline (FOI ± 2.5 Hz) beta power surrounding levodopa intakes was analyzed by segmenting the data into a period of 60 minutes before until 180 minutes after intake, referring to this time period as the “beta response.” The 180‐minute interval was chosen because the majority of our cohort were taking levodopa with at least 3 hours between intakes (median = 3 hours, IQR = 3 to 4) after determining the average interval between intakes per patient and taking the overall median and IQR of all individual average levodopa intake intervals. To be able to compare beta responses across hemispheres, we normalized beta power values before and after each levodopa intake by dividing by the beta power value observed at the moment of intake. We then computed for each hemisphere the median normalized beta power across intakes for each 10‐minute time point of the beta response. Both hemispheres per patient were used for the analyses. Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests indicated a non‐normal distribution of the beta power response per hemisphere and for every time point across hemispheres.

At the group level, observed beta responses were statistically compared with beta responses around random time points from the same hemisphere using cluster‐based nonparametric permutation tests 27 as implemented in the permutest.m 28 MATLAB function (1,000 permutations, 2‐sided paired T‐test, p < 0.05). In this cluster‐based permutation test, we investigated whether the median beta power per 10‐minute interval around levodopa intake differs significantly from randomly selected beta power values. Further methodological details of the permutation test can be read in the Supplementary Methods. The beta response curves around random time points were created by selecting a random time point between 60 minutes before and 180 minutes after each levodopa intake event. Subsequently, the median beta power across intakes was computed for each surrounding time point, as before. This process was repeated 100 times, resulting in 100 median response curves that were averaged to yield one “random” beta response curve per hemisphere that entered the permutation test.

To test whether several clinical, physiological, and pharmacological factors had an effect on the time course and size of the beta response following levodopa intake, we conducted secondary analyses by splitting the data into different subsets for which the cluster‐based permutation tests was repeated. To investigate the choice of FOI, we separated beta responses measured at low (12.7–20.5 Hz) versus high (21.5–30.3 Hz) beta FOI. 29 Previous research shows that the stun effect (or “microlesion” effect) may influence beta power during the first month after surgery. 30 We therefore separately investigated the first month after surgery and the months thereafter in our cohort. To investigate a possible dose–response relationship, we separated intake with levodopa dosage of 50, 100, and 150 mg. Patients received continuous DBS treatment during most of the study period. To investigate possible disappearance of the levodopa intake beta response due to dominance of beta suppression with moderate stimulation amplitudes, we investigated beta responses below 1 mA versus those above 1.5 mA. 31 , 32 Finally, we created 2 subgroups with patients who showed < 50% and > 60% UPDRS III improvement at preoperative DBS screening (screening response). Intakes during stimulation at 0 mA were removed from the < 1 mA subset, as previous research suggests that the levodopa response during ON and OFF stimulation may differ in underlying neurophysiology. 31 , 32

We computed the area under the curve (AUC) of the beta response in relation to the point of normalization (100%) between 30 and 180 minutes after each intake to calculate a single value for the beta response. Because stimulation amperage and levodopa dose dynamically change over time due to the titration process, we analyzed the median AUC per hemisphere with stimulation amperages in 3 different stimulation ranges per dose by plotting the median and IQR of the AUCs per dose and stimulation range to get a sense of the relation between stimulation amperage and the beta response. Next, we tested which of the aforementioned factors contributed to the size of the overall beta response for all intakes during active stimulation. Each levodopa intake dose was log transformed, because this variable showed a nonlinear relationship to the beta response in our secondary analyses. We fitted a linear mixed effect model containing the variables showing a statistically significant effect on the beta response in the secondary analyses. A random intercept was used for each subject. Log transformed AUC served as outcome variable. The following fixed factors were used: hemisphere (left or right and categorical variable), frequency band (high or low and categorical variable), dose (continuous variable), and stimulation amplitude (continuous variable). Following, we compared different models in a backward stepwise approach with decreasing complexity by removing one variable at a time. After a variable was found to not significantly contribute to the model quality (Akaike information criterion [AIC] difference = < 2), this variable was removed from the model. After determining the variables that significantly impacted model quality, an interaction term between the type of hemisphere and type of FOI (high or low beta) was added to investigate a possible differential physiological role of low versus high beta in the right versus the left hemisphere due to cross‐hemispheric coupling. 33 The fitlme.m MATLAB function with the default maximum likelihood fit method (“ML”) was used. The fixed factors were pair‐wise tested for collinearity, which was not the case for any of the tested combinations. The residuals of the final model were tested for normality by visually inspecting their corresponding histogram and qq plots. The different models and their metrics can be found in Supplementary Table S2.

The combination of our primary and secondary group analyses with permutation testing allowed us to get a sense of the overall time dynamics of the beta response to levodopa and its confounders. Next, the mixed effects model was used with the subject as a random intercept to account for interindividual variability to show the contribution and statistical significance of these confounders across intakes. This allowed estimation of the overall association between the levodopa‐induced beta power change and potential confounders, while accounting for repeated measures within the subjects. Summary data are presented as median IQR unless otherwise described.

Results

Clinical Characteristics

Of the total cohort of 54 screened patients, 33 patients (64 hemispheres) matched our inclusion criteria (see Supplementary Table S1). The mean age of the participants was 65 ± 6 years (mean ± standard deviation [SD]), mean disease duration was 11 ± 6 years (mean ± SD) and 19 patients were men. The UPDRS III OFF‐med score during the preoperative screening was 43 (IQR = 37 to 53) and UPDRS III ON‐med score before surgery was 18 (IQR = 11 to 26), showing a median improvement after levodopa intake of 57% (IQR = 42% to 68%). The UPDRS III ON‐med ON‐stim score after the first programming (17 days, IQR = 13 to 21 after surgery) was 23 (IQR = 11 to 30), and 22 (IQR = 15 to 30) at the end of the period of analysis (132 days after surgery, IQR = 122 to 146). The levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD) during preoperative screening was 1,535 mg (IQR = 1,028 to 1865 mg), 1,200 mg (IQR = 765 to 1,611 mg) at first DBS programming, and 690 mg (IQR = 598 to 1,098 mg) at 132 days (fourth clinical visit post‐surgery), showing a reduction of 57% (IQR = 46% to 67%).

Per hemisphere, we computed the median beta power response to levodopa across the available intakes (n = 24.103; 377 ± 127 [mean ± SD] per hemisphere, 64 hemispheres). Compared with the response curves that were created around randomly selected time points, the observed beta response showed a significant cluster between 30 and 140 minutes after levodopa intake, with a maximum beta power decrease at 110 minutes (−4.7%, IQR = −10.3% to −0.1%, p < 0.01; Fig 1A).

Discussion

The relation between short‐acting dopaminergic medication (levodopa) intake and beta power was studied in a cohort of patients with PD treated with a sensing‐enabled DBS system. Using over 24,000 intakes from 33 patients, we found that beta power significantly decreases 30 minutes after levodopa intake. This is in line with the expected pharmacodynamics of levodopa due to the fact that levodopa concentration in the blood reaches its maximum approximately 30 minutes after intake. 23 Our secondary analyses showed that low beta power (12.7–20.5 Hz) is reduced 50 to 140 minutes after levodopa intake, while no significant fluctuations after levodopa intake were observed for the high beta frequency range (21.5–30.3 Hz). Beta responses during low stimulation amplitudes (< 1 mA) and high levodopa doses (100 and 150 mg) were both associated with significant clusters, whereas higher stimulation amplitudes (> 1.5 mA) and a lower levodopa dose (50 mg) were not. Group analyses per dose showed no clear reduction of the beta response with increasing stimulation amperage. Beta responses were similar during and after the stun period (ie, up to 30 days after surgery) and independent of the preoperative UPDRS III response to levodopa. Beta power was significantly reduced in the hour before medication intake for patients with a large levodopa response during preoperative screening, whereas this was not the case for patients with a medium to small preoperative levodopa response. This might indicate that beta power does not distinguish levodopa wearing off in patients with medium to small levodopa responses at preoperative screening (< 50% UPDRS III improvement after intake).

Comparisons of various linear mixed effects models using the AUC of the beta power 30 to 180 minutes after levodopa intake confirmed the findings from our secondary analyses. The linear mixed effects model accounted for individual subject variability, which resulted in linear associations between the levodopa beta response and the fixed factors. A frequency‐specific dose–response relationship and a significant interaction between hemisphere and frequency band was seen, showing a lower increase in AUC (ie, a smaller difference between low and high beta power responses) in the right hemispheres compared with the left hemispheres when comparing low to high beta power. OFF medication UPDRS scoring revealed no significant difference in symptom severity between the left and right body sides and all leads were implanted using standardized surgical approaches (see Supplementary Table S1). This may point toward hemisphere‐specific neural processing differences. Analyses on the median AUC per hemisphere per stimulation range and dose did not show a consistent effect of stimulation amperage on the beta response in our secondary analyses. This was confirmed in our linear mixed effects analysis, showing no significant impact of stimulation amperage on model quality. Furthermore, a significant beta response was found at higher doses and at lower stimulation amplitudes but not at higher stimulation amplitudes in our secondary group analyses. The lack of a significant result for the higher stimulation amplitudes may have been confounded by the levodopa dose as our linear mixed effects model analyses did not find a significant contribution of stimulation amplitude to model quality. Nevertheless, only 18% of intakes at stimulation amperages above 1.5 mA were 50 mg or lower in dose and only 9% of intakes below 1 mA were 50 mg or lower in dose. Therefore, this difference in the number of low dose intakes between the moderate and low stimulation amplitude groups may have contributed to the lack of a significant result for higher stimulation amplitudes. Our model shows that, in this dataset, beta power drops after levodopa intake according to the chosen FOI and levodopa dosage but regardless of stimulation amplitude. The type of hemisphere (left vs right) slightly influences the intensity of this response. Still, the time course of the beta response and size of these findings may differ from patient to patient as the results in this paper were performed on a population level.

The fact that beta power dropped, at group level, only a few percentages (< 10%) after intake may be because several factors influence beta power besides levodopa and DBS, such as movement, 31 , 35 circadian rhythms (including sleep), 35 , 36 (positive) stress, 37 and PD phenotype. 38 Several studies investigated beta power changes 30 minutes to 1 hour after intake, all in the clinic, and with none reporting the relative percentage of beta decrease and with some studies using externalized leads. 7 , 31 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 In the ambulatory setting, some studies indicate that beta power may not contribute to distinguishing the OFF‐ from the ON‐dopaminergic state or even show paradoxical increases in beta band power with elevating heart rates or physical activity. 35 Therefore, our results in the ambulatory setting provide the first real‐world evidence that beta power distinguishes the OFF‐ from the ON‐dopaminergic state on a group level in the ambulatory setting during the stimulation titration phase. Interestingly, although the beta power during the first weeks after surgery has been shown to be reduced due to post‐surgical edema and impedance changes around the electrode, 30 , 44 , 45 the beta power response was nevertheless found to be significant during and after this stun period. This may be explained by the relative higher dose of levodopa early after surgery combined with low or absent stimulation amplitudes, because increasing DBS amperage often reduces the need for levodopa. Second, the stun effect may impact the overall local neurophysiology around the electrode, but not necessarily its response to levodopa. Surprisingly, the percentage drop of beta power after levodopa intake did not change after only analyzing the low beta range. However, the IQR reduced substantially, which points toward a less noisy dataset that is likely explained by removing samples from the high beta range, which did not show a significant response. Beta power can also be influenced by underlying changes in aperiodic activity, which can lead to beta power increases or decreases that are not related to beta synchronization. One report did not find a significant effect of levodopa on the aperiodic component, 46 whereas other reports demonstrated larger exponents after levodopa intake. 47 , 48 This is only one component of the aperiodic activity, because the offset is not analyzed in these papers. Wiest et al also reported a significant effect of stimulation on the aperiodic component. 47 We were not able to correct for this factor, because the Percept system only saves average beta power per 10‐minute intervals for at‐home Timeline recordings without information about the full power spectrum or raw data. Further research has to determine how these and possibly other factors may impact the beta fluctuations in response to levodopa.

In the past years, aDBS has become a promising alternative to conventional, continuous DBS. Although its exact indication is not yet established, patients who experience side effects from stimulation, such as dysarthria, 49 gait difficulties, 50 or dyskinesia, 32 might benefit from aDBS. The aDBS algorithms have the ability to adjust stimulation based upon the medication state, therefore reducing the probability of overstimulation when dopamine levels are relatively high. Several algorithms have been developed to date, including the so‐called “single” – and “dual” LFP threshold algorithms. Whereas single‐threshold systems may respond quickly (< 1 second) to beta burst activity ad hoc, 38 dual threshold systems may use a slower (several minutes) algorithm that is able to adjust to medication states. 16 Manually determining the LFP thresholds and the performance of beta‐based aDBS algorithms may depend on the choice of frequency band and prescribed levodopa dose.

Two recent reports showed that beta may be a suboptimal physiomarker for guiding aDBS due to the fact that OFF/ON fluctuations might be better tracked using gamma activity ON stimulation in small groups of patients (n = 4 and n = 14). 32 , 51 Our study did not analyze gamma power, however, we were able to show that FOIs in the low beta range in a substantial group of patients may track OFF/ON fluctuations during DBS programming in the months following implantation whereas stimulation amplitudes were low to moderate with a median of 0.8 mA at DBS setup and 1.7 mA at the last clinical visit 4 months post‐surgery, see Supplementary Table S1 and Table 2. Furthermore, our linear mixed effects model did not show a significant contribution of stimulation amplitude to the model quality of the levodopa beta response (see Table 2). Although we do recognize that in some patient's beta power may be a suboptimal physiomarker, our findings indicate the ability of beta power to track levodopa OFF/ON fluctuations during active stimulation at least with low to moderate amperage. Second, current commercially approved devices do not allow for the inverse use of physiomarker power, preventing the use of gamma activity for aDBS in regular clinical care. When lower stimulation amperages for aDBS are used, beta power may be a feasible physiomarker in combination with LFP thresholds for distinguishing OFF‐ and ON‐dopaminergic states, 16 whereas at higher stimulation amperages this may not be the case. Future research has to determine whether beta power remains a feasible physiomarker at higher stimulation ranges. Multiple studies indicate that beta power responds to both levodopa 42 and DBS 8 and correlates with bradykinesia and rigidity severity. 10 This supports the use of beta power as a physiomarker for DBS programming 51 and aDBS algorithms 14 in the clinic 42 or at home. 52 In addition, our secondary analyses show that multiple factors may influence the levodopa beta response, which may aid in the selection of patients that could benefit from out‐of‐clinic beta‐based aDBS. We show that ON and OFF episodes can be distinguished using low beta power from the STN and that this separation may not be possible in patients that use relatively low doses of levodopa. Second, because beta power inversely follows the expected pharmacodynamics of levodopa, aDBS algorithms may be set up according to the levodopa beta response while using thresholds for determining the OFF‐ and ON‐medication state to improve symptoms during both OFF and ON episodes that may be caused by under‐ or overstimulation. This may create an optimal treatment “window” optimizing OFF‐ and ON‐related symptoms and stimulation‐related side effects while also balancing them with stimulation‐induced side effects. Our findings therefore support the use of algorithms that slowly track beta power over time. 16 Patients that may benefit from this treatment include individuals with low to moderate stimulation amplitudes and high levodopa doses due to risk of stimulation‐induced side effects. 53 Further research is needed to investigate these findings in relation to aDBS treatment on an individual level in the clinic.

Limitations

Whereas this study included a large number of patients and levodopa intakes, the use of medication schedules rather than real‐time (electronically) logged intakes may have caused false positive intakes or errors in the actual time points of intake. Second, our analyses of beta responses per the FOI frequency bin may have included selection bias due to the low number of hemispheres analyzed per frequency bin, especially in the high beta range (21.5–30.5 Hz; see Table 1, and Supplementary Fig S3). Nevertheless, when analyzing intakes as a group in the whole high beta range, we did have sufficient sample size to indicate a differential response of high beta in relation to levodopa intake compared with low beta FOIs. Whereas our analysis of high beta did not result in a significant levodopa beta response, previous studies do suggest that high beta may have a role in freezing of gait 54 and UPDRS III response to DBS. 29 A difference in sample sizes observed in our secondary analyses may have impacted the results of the cluster‐based permutation test by reducing statistical power, affecting the stability of the null distribution, and influencing the detection of significant clusters. This may limit the robustness and generalizability of the findings. However, the use of a mixed‐effects model accounts for variability across patients, mitigating the potential for selection bias in our secondary analyses. Because we were restricted by the contact point used by the clinician, we were unable to select the highest beta peak in all participants across all possible sensing configurations and may have selected a suboptimal beta peak. Second, we did not select the peak for the Timeline consistently in the OFF‐medication state at every patient visit using the BrainSense features, as can be read in our Supplementary Methods. Therefore, we are not able to analyze with certainty the relation between beta peak amplitude distribution selected for recording and the variability of the DBS response. Theoretically, this relationship might have influenced the levodopa response, which is commonly used as a screening criterion for DBS. The median and IQR of the beta peak amplitude during the OFF‐medication state at DBS activation can be found in the Supplementary Table S1. Furthermore, 26 of the 64 hemispheres had a change in contact point between the first postoperative day (which was selected based upon the highest beta frequency peak in the ON‐medication state) and the DBS activation visit. This may have changed the beta response after DBS activation. Nevertheless, the reduction of the stun effect on beta power after DBS activation could counteract upon the effect of the contact point change on beta power. The effect we found between the choice of hemisphere and the difference between low beta and high beta levodopa responses was not influenced by systematic difference in hemibody symptom burden. However, we cannot completely rule out systematic confounders regarding the location of implanted electrodes between the left and right hemispheres because we did not systematically analyze imaging postoperatively.

Conclusions

The current study shows that beta power follows the expected pharmacodynamics of levodopa in an at‐home setting during low to moderate stimulation amperages (median 1.1 mA, ranging from 0 to 2.1 mA) in the DBS titration phase. Levodopa dose, choice of high or low beta‐band FOI and its interaction with the type of hemisphere (left vs right) impacted the levodopa beta response, whereas stimulation amplitude, the preoperative UPDRS III response to levodopa, and the stun effect did not. Therefore, aDBS algorithms aimed at decreasing stimulation‐induced side effects while working in synergy with dopamine medication need to take into account these variables. In conclusion, these findings may inform studies investigating aDBS on the choice of physiomarker and clinical characteristics when investigating different patient populations that may benefit from aDBS.
