Impact of COVID‐19 Infection on Blood Pressure Variability and Cardiovascular Outcomes in Hypertensive Patients After Complete Liberalization of Epidemic Control in China
Authors: Quanbin Su, Mingming Wang, Jing Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis retrospective cohort study assessed the effect of COVID‐19 infection on blood pressure variability (BPV) and cardiovascular outcomes in hypertensive patients using 24‐h ambulatory blood pressure monitoring and structural equation modeling (SEM). Among 318 patients followed for 2 years, those with infection and poor prognosis showed the most abnormal hemodynamic patterns, including markedly elevated nocturnal SBP load (70.0% vs. 50.1%), higher ARVDBP (9.8% vs. 8.2%), wider pulse pressure (60.9 mmHg), and lower time in target range (30.9% vs. 74.7%, p < 0.001). Cox regression identified infection status, nocturnal BP load, and BP variability as major risk factors, while multivariate models confirmed 11 independent predictors. Neither diabetes nor antihypertensive medication class modified these associations. SEM demonstrated that infection influenced prognosis indirectly through elevated nighttime BP level, load, and variability (indirect effect β = 0.098, p < 0.001). Mechanistically, infection‐driven endothelial dysfunction, microthrombotic activation, and autonomic dysregulation, rather than prolonged inactivity, likely underlie the nocturnal amplification of BP instability. These findings support the need for individualized hypertension management during and after infection, focusing on renin–angiotensin system balance, continuation of ACEI/ARB therapy, nighttime dosing of long‐acting agents, and circadian BP monitoring to mitigate long‐term cardiovascular risk.Keywords: ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, blood pressure variability, cardiovascular outcomes, COVID‐19, hypertension

Introduction

Hypertension, affecting over 1.3 billion individuals globally, is one of the most prevalent chronic cardiovascular conditions [1]. Despite its widespread impact, nearly half of hypertensive patients are undiagnosed, and many diagnosed individuals fail to receive adequate treatment, increasing the risk of complications such as myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, and renal dysfunction [2]. The COVID‐19 pandemic has exacerbated the difficulties of managing blood pressure and cardiovascular risks. As of now, SARS‐CoV‐2 has caused over 770 million infections and more than 7 million deaths worldwide [3]. Hypertension is commonly reported as a comorbidity in COVID‐19 patients and is closely associated with increased disease severity and mortality [4, 5]. Moreover, many survivors continue to experience persistent hypertension and vascular dysfunction long after recovery from the acute phase of infection [6]. Blood pressure variability (BPV), an important marker of cardiovascular risk, may be influenced by infection‐induced physiological changes. However, the effects of COVID‐19 on BPV and long‐term cardiovascular outcomes in hypertensive patients remain poorly understood. This study aims to explore these associations using 24‐h ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM), with the objective of improving individualized hypertension management in the post‐pandemic era.

Methods

Study Design

This single‐center retrospective cohort study included hypertensive patients who underwent 24‐h ABPM at the Second Hospital of Lanzhou University between December 2022 and April 2023, with a 2‐year follow‐up. Patients were stratified into four groups based on SARS‐CoV‐2 nucleic acid test results and the occurrence of all‐cause death or major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) during follow‐up: uninfected/good prognosis, infected/good prognosis, uninfected/poor prognosis, and infected/poor prognosis. Group differences in blood pressure load, nocturnal dipping rate, coefficient of variation (CV), and average real variability (ARV) were compared to assess the impact of COVID‐19 infection on ambulatory blood pressure dynamics and guide individualized clinical strategies.

COVID‐19 Testing and Infection Status

Infection status at enrollment was determined solely from SARS‐CoV‐2 PCR/NAAT results documented in the electronic medical record on or before the enrollment date during the enrollment window (December 1, 2022–April 30, 2023). The follow‐up period ended on April 30, 2025. All participants were contacted at 6‐month intervals and provided with antigen self‐testing kits, with results recorded by investigators. Patients categorized as “infected” at enrollment were monitored for potential reinfection, while those categorized as “uninfected” were excluded from the uninfected analyses if they subsequently tested positive. Importantly, all ABPM recordings were obtained during clinically stable periods and not during the acute symptomatic phase of infection.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they: (1) met the diagnostic criteria for hypertension according to the “Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension (2024 Revised Edition)” [7]; (2) were aged ≥18 years; (3) underwent ABPM in the cardiology department of the Second Hospital of Lanzhou University during the study period; and (4) had complete medical records, ABPM data, and documented SARS‐CoV‐2 nucleic acid test results.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) acute cardiovascular or cerebrovascular events; (2) pregnancy or lactation; (3) endocrine or renal diseases, or undergoing dialysis; (4) current use of medications known to affect BPV (e.g., alpha‐blockers, monoamine oxidase inhibitors, tricyclic antidepressants, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, corticosteroids) [8, 9]; and (5) severe respiratory disease, malignancy, or psychiatric or cognitive disorders.

Data Collection

Demographic information, baseline diabetes status, medical history, and antihypertensive medication use were extracted from the electronic medical record using a standardized form. ABPM parameters included daytime, nighttime, morning, and 24‐h values for SBP, DBP, pulse pressure (PP), and heart rate. Data were cross‐checked by two independent investigators. All identifiers were removed before analysis. Given the retrospective design, informed consent was waived, and data were anonymized in accordance with institutional and national regulations.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 and R version 4.4.2. Normality of continuous variables was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk or Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non‐normally distributed variables were reported as median and interquartile range (P25–P75). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages (n, %). For group comparisons, independent‐sample t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U tests were used for two groups, and one‐way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests for multiple groups, with post hoc Tukey or Tamhane T2 tests applied as appropriate.

Time‐to‐event outcomes were analyzed using Cox proportional hazards regression to evaluate the associations of infection status, diabetes mellitus, blood pressure parameters, and medication use with adverse prognosis. Diabetes status was first included as a covariate; interaction between infection and diabetes was then formally tested, and when significant, a four‐group Cox model was constructed. Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding participants with reinfection and by adding reinfection as a baseline covariate.

To account for baseline antihypertensive therapy, the following steps were undertaken: (1) baseline distribution of drug classes (ACEI/ARB/ARNI, calcium channel blockers [CCB], β‐blockers, and diuretics) was compared across infection/prognosis groups using Chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests; (2) univariate Cox regression was conducted for each drug class; (3) multivariable Cox regression was performed including drug classes alongside infection status and blood pressure parameters; and (4) stratified Cox analyses by drug use were performed with formal tests for interaction. When sparse events resulted in non‐estimable models, Firth's penalized likelihood method was applied. Proportional hazards assumptions were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals.

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was further applied to explore indirect pathways by which infection status influenced prognosis through blood pressure–related factors. Eleven independent predictors identified from multivariable Cox regression were included in the SEM. Diabetes was not incorporated, as it did not demonstrate consistent independent associations in Cox models, and its inclusion reduced model stability. A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 318 hypertensive patients were included and divided into four groups according to COVID‐19 infection status and clinical outcomes (Table 1). The median age was 58 years, 60.7% were male, and the mean BMI was 25.5 ± 3.6 kg/m2. At baseline, 32 patients (10.1%) had diabetes mellitus, with a higher prevalence among infected versus uninfected patients (15.1% vs. 5.0%) and among those with poor versus good prognosis (17.8% vs. 8.8%). The distribution of age, sex, BMI, and antihypertensive medication use was broadly comparable across the four groups (all p > 0.05; Table S6).

Of the 318 patients enrolled between December 2022 and April 2023, 159 (50.0%) had at least one confirmed SARS‐CoV‐2 infection at or before enrollment (129 with good prognosis, 30 with poor prognosis). The remaining 159 patients (50.0%) were uninfected at enrollment (144 with good prognosis, 15 with poor prognosis). During the 2‐year follow‐up, no participant initially classified as uninfected developed a new positive test. Among those infected at baseline, 17 experienced reinfection and 3 had more than three positive episodes. These reinfections did not differ significantly in distribution across groups.

Ambulatory Blood Pressure Characteristics

Patients with poor prognosis exhibited higher nocturnal blood pressure load, greater 24‐h BP variability, and increased ARV compared with those with good prognosis (p < 0.05; Figure 1). The infected/poor prognosis group (Group IV) showed the highest values in nocturnal SBP load and BP variability indices (p < 0.05 vs. all other groups). Among systolic pressure parameters, daytime SBP differed significantly across groups (F = 3.779, p = 0.011), with Group II (infected/good prognosis) showing higher values than Group I (uninfected/good prognosis). Nighttime SBP showed a larger between‐group difference (F = 15.557, p < 0.001), with Group IV having the highest levels. A similar pattern was observed for 24‐h SBP (F = 14.487, p < 0.001). For diastolic pressure, both nighttime and 24‐h DBP showed significant intergroup differences (F = 20.127 and F = 18.042, respectively; both p < 0.001). Group IV had the highest DBP values in both periods. Nighttime and 24‐h pulse pressure also differed significantly (F = 10.450 and F = 9.289, respectively; both p < 0.001), with Group IV showing the highest PP values. No significant group differences were observed in heart rate (all p > 0.05; Table 2). Notably, these differences were predominantly nocturnal, while mean heart rate remained comparable across groups (all p > 0.05), suggesting that the observed BP variability reflects infection‐related endothelial and autonomic dysregulation rather than immobilization alone.

Blood Pressure Variability and Arterial Stiffness

Table 3 summarizes the BP variability indices and arterial stiffness measurements. Group IV demonstrated the highest nocturnal SBP load (H = 63.789, p < 0.001) and significantly higher 24‐h SBP load (p = 0.003). Both nighttime and 24‐h DBP load were also significantly increased in Group IV (H = 46.817 and H = 11.101, respectively; p ≤ 0.011). The nocturnal dipping rates of both SBP and DBP were significantly reduced in Group IV (SBP dipping rate: 0.91% ± 8.17%, p < 0.001), with greater intergroup variability observed for nighttime than daytime measures. The coefficients of variation for nighttime SBP and 24‐h DBP were higher in Group IV than in other groups (P ≤ 0.001). ARV values for SBP and DBP were also elevated in Group IV (p < 0.001). In addition, the arterial stiffness index (AI) was significantly increased in Group IV compared to the other groups (p < 0.001).

Cox Regression Analysis

Univariate Cox regression identified 19 variables significantly associated with adverse outcomes (Figure 2a). COVID‐19 infection (HR = 2.12, p = 0.018), nocturnal SBP load (HR = 1.16), and ARVDBP (HR = 1.17) were positively associated with risk, while BMI and TTR were inversely associated. Higher SBP and DBP dipping rates were associated with lower risk (approximately 10%–12% reduction; Table S1).

(a) Univariate Cox regression forest plot of prognostic factors for cardiovascular adverse outcomes. (b) Multivariate Cox regression forest plot of prognostic factors for cardiovascular adverse outcomes.

Multivariate Cox regression identified 11 independent predictors (Figure 2b). Nocturnal SBP was inversely associated with risk (HR = 0.795, p < 0.001). In contrast, higher nocturnal DBP, PP, and SBP load were associated with increased risk (HR = 1.250, 1.180, and 1.100; all p < 0.005). CV for nighttime SBP and 24‐h DBP remained significant (HR = 1.130 and 1.240, respectively; P ≤ 0.044). ARVSBP was positively associated with risk (HR = 1.260, p = 0.010), while ARVDBP was inversely associated (HR = 0.657, p = 0.014). Although infection status was significant in univariate analysis, it was not retained in the multivariate model. Meanwhile, multiple BP‐related variables remained significant (Table S2).

Diabetes and Interaction Analyses

When diabetes was added to the multivariable Cox model, neither infection status (HR = 0.63, 95% CI 0.27–1.47, p = 0.28) nor diabetes mellitus (HR = 0.65, 95% CI 0.26–1.66, p = 0.37) showed independent associations. A formal interaction between infection status and diabetes was statistically significant (p = 0.027; interaction HR = 0.12, 95% CI 0.02–0.70). A four‐group Cox model (uninfected/non‐diabetic, infected/non‐diabetic, uninfected/diabetes, infected/diabetes) was therefore estimated. Using uninfected/non‐diabetic as the reference, patients with coexisting diabetes but no infection showed the highest risk, whereas those with both infection and diabetes exhibited no significant difference compared with the reference group (Tables S3–S5).

Antihypertensive Medication Analyses

Baseline use of antihypertensive medications did not differ significantly across the four study groups (all p > 0.05; Table S6). In univariate Cox regression, none of the four major drug classes were significantly associated with adverse outcomes (HR range: 0.75–1.58; all p > 0.18; Table S7). After adjustment for infection status and BP‐related parameters in multivariable models, effect estimates shifted modestly but remained nonsignificant (e.g., ACEI/ARB/ARNI HR from 1.20 [95% CI 0.56–2.58] to 0.57 [95% CI 0.22–1.43]; CCB from 0.90 [95% CI 0.50–1.62] to 0.76 [95% CI 0.37–1.56]; β‐blockers from 1.58 [95% CI 0.80–3.12] to 1.45 [95% CI 0.66–3.21]; diuretics from 0.75 [95% CI 0.36–1.55] to 0.52 [95% CI 0.21–1.30]). None of the drug classes emerged as independent predictors of prognosis, while the main BP‐related predictors identified earlier remained robust.

These results are illustrated in Figure S2, which shows hazard ratios from both univariate and multivariable Cox regression models. All drug classes had wide confidence intervals overlapping the null, and no consistent associations with adverse outcomes were observed. Taken together, these findings indicate that baseline antihypertensive therapy did not materially influence the relationship between COVID‐19 infection status, BP variability, and prognosis.

Sensitivity Analyses

After excluding participants who experienced reinfection during follow‐up (n = 17), the multivariable Cox regression model produced results materially unchanged from the primary analysis: infection status remained nonsignificant (HR = 0.46, 95% CI 0.18–1.17, p = 0.10), while the main BP‐related predictors retained their statistical significance (Table S8). When reinfection was added as a baseline covariate alongside the same predictors, both infection status (HR = 0.70, 95% CI 0.30–1.63, p = 0.41) and reinfection (HR = 0.71, 95% CI 0.29–1.70, p = 0.44) remained nonsignificant. However, proportional hazards assumptions were violated for the reinfection term (global p = 0.0047); thus, this model is presented only as exploratory (Table S9).

In addition, within the infected cohort, we compared outcomes between patients with a single infection and those who experienced reinfection. Kaplan–Meier curves demonstrated lower event‐free survival in the reinfection group over 24 months (Figure S3), although these differences did not reach statistical significance after adjustment. This descriptive analysis supports that reinfection did not materially alter the prognostic associations observed in the main analyses.

Structural Equation Modeling

An SEM was developed using 11 variables identified in the multivariate analysis (Table 4). The model included three latent mediating constructs: blood pressure level, blood pressure load, and blood pressure variability. The model fit was acceptable (χ 2(3) = 16.72, p = 0.001; CFI = 0.966; TLI = 0.831; RMSEA = 0.120, 90% CI: 0.068–0.179; SRMR = 0.057). The SEM showed that infection status was indirectly associated with prognosis via increased blood pressure level (β = 0.454, p < 0.001), load (β = 0.529, p < 0.001), and variability (β = 0.223, p = 0.045), with a total indirect effect of 0.098 (p < 0.001). The direct path between infection status and prognosis was not statistically significant.

Discussion

Inflammatory and Arteriosclerotic Mechanisms: The Dual Pathogenic Role of Infection

The excessive inflammatory response, often referred to as a “cytokine storm,” is linked to increased vascular permeability, endothelial dysfunction, thrombogenesis, and multiorgan injury or failure in COVID‐19 patients [10]. Elevated levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐6 and TNF‐α, can compromise the blood‐brain barrier, allowing peripheral inflammatory mediators and neurotoxic substances to enter the brain. This neuroinflammatory cascade activates the sympathetic nervous system through central autonomic network (CAN) dysregulation [11]. In this study, COVID‐19 infection was associated with a marked increase in BP load and variability in hypertensive patients, with the most prominent alterations occurring during nighttime. These alterations can be interpreted within the broader pathophysiological framework of COVID‐19–related endothelial injury, vascular inflammation, and autonomic dysregulation. This pattern, characterized by higher nocturnal blood pressure load and variability, suggests a predominantly autonomic and vascular mechanism rather than a hemodynamic artifact. SARS‐CoV‐2 directly infects endothelial cells via ACE2, leading to endothelialitis, microthrombosis, and impaired nitric‐oxide–dependent vasodilation, which together promote increased arterial stiffness and higher nocturnal BP load [12, 13, 14]. Moreover, beyond endothelial inflammation, COVID‐19 elicits a prothrombotic milieu, characterized by microthrombi formation, platelet activation, and dysregulation of fibrinolysis [15, 16]. These microvascular thrombi increase resistance in the small vessels and compromise nocturnal vasodilatory reserve, thereby elevating nocturnal BP load and amplifying short‐term variability. This vascular obstruction, together with endothelial inflammation, creates a state of impaired microcirculatory adaptation that persists even beyond the acute phase, explaining the sustained nocturnal BP elevation seen in our cohort. Although prolonged bed rest may alter hemodynamics through venous stasis, reduced muscle pump activity, and fluid redistribution, such effects typically elevate daytime rather than nocturnal BP. In our cohort, the nocturnal‐dominant alterations and stable heart rate profile make it unlikely that inactivity alone accounts for the observed changes. Taken together, these observations indicate that infection‐driven endothelial dysfunction and microthrombotic processes are the most plausible mechanisms underlying nocturnal BP destabilization in hypertensive patients post‐COVID‐19 [17]. Neuroinflammatory responses further disrupt the central autonomic network, resulting in sympathetic overactivation and parasympathetic withdrawal, thereby reducing baroreflex sensitivity and increasing BP variability [18, 19]. The imbalance between the ACE–Ang II–AT1R and ACE2–Ang (1‐7)–MasR axes amplifies vasoconstriction, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction, further aggravating circadian BP instability [20]. Although behavioral factors such as reduced activity and prolonged bed rest may modestly affect hemodynamics, the nocturnal‐dominant alterations and stable heart rate profile observed in our cohort make it unlikely that inactivity alone accounts for these changes. Consistent with this interpretation, the initial report of the ESH ABPM COVID‐19 Study demonstrated that infected individuals exhibited significantly higher 24‐h SBP, DBP, and BP variability compared with pre‐pandemic controls [21, 22]. In the infected group with poor prognosis (Group IV), nocturnal SBP load reached 70.0%, an 88.3% increase compared to the uninfected group with good prognosis (Group I, 50.1%, p < 0.001). The ARV of DBP rose to 9.8%, representing a 19.5% increase over Group I (p = 0.002), exceeding the level observed in the poor‐prognosis‐only group. These findings suggest an independent amplifying effect of infection on BP variability, likely driven by inflammation‐induced sympathetic hyperactivity and circadian disruption.

The 24‐h pulse pressure in Group IV was 60.93 ± 9.00 mmHg, 20.0% higher than in Group I (50.84 ± 9.82 mmHg, p < 0.001). The arterial stiffness index (AI) was also significantly elevated compared to Group III (p = 0.004), indicating that infection exacerbates short‐term hemodynamic instability. This may accelerate arterial remodeling and reduced compliance via oxidative stress and nitric oxide (NO) suppression. These findings reinforce a vicious cycle linking inflammation, arteriosclerosis, and BP destabilization. The predominance of these effects during the nocturnal period likely reflects the vulnerability of autonomic and endothelial regulation when physiological sympathetic withdrawal is blunted by systemic inflammation.

Our findings also align with the follow‐up analysis of the ESH ABPM COVID‐19 Study, which confirmed these elevations and further reported an increased prevalence of sustained hypertension and loss of nocturnal dipping patterns during the pandemic [22]. Patients with severe or fatal COVID‐19 exhibited higher BPV than those with mild disease or survivors, and elevated BPV was strongly linked to worsened clinical outcomes [23, 24, 25]. Long COVID studies have further shown persistent fluctuations in SBP, DBP, and PP across the circadian cycle, even months after infection, highlighting ongoing autonomic and vascular dysfunction [26].

Amplification of Adverse Prognosis: Cascading Effects of Autonomic Dysregulation and Metabolic Disturbance

SARS‐CoV‐2 exerts its biological effects by binding its spike protein to the human ACE2 receptor, followed by activation through transmembrane protease serine 2 (TMPRSS2), thereby inhibiting the degradation of angiotensin II and contributing to blood pressure elevation [27]. The renin–angiotensin system (RAS) plays a central role in cardiovascular homeostasis and comprises both the classical ACE–Ang II–AT1R axis and the counter‐regulatory ACE2–Ang‐(1–7)–MasR axis [28, 29]. Disruption in the balance between these pathways, together with systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, blood–brain barrier impairment, and autonomic–immune imbalance, is considered a key mechanism leading to BPV and regulatory failure.

In our study, the infected group with adverse prognosis (Group IV) showed impaired circadian BP regulation and increased variability. The nocturnal DBP dipping rate was as low as 0.36% ± 9.19%, a 95.3% reduction compared to Group I (8.73% ± 7.94%, p < 0.001), indicating impaired baroreflex sensitivity (BRS) and sympathetic–parasympathetic dysregulation. The ARV of SBP in Group IV was 15.0%, significantly higher than in the infected‐only group (Group II, 12.1%, p = 0.003), suggesting cumulative autonomic dysfunction from both infection and poor prognosis. The 24‐h DBP in Group IV averaged 86.93 ± 11.33 mmHg, a 13.3 mmHg increase compared to Group I (p < 0.001), and exceeding the difference between Groups III and I (Δ = 14.7 mmHg, p < 0.001). This pattern indicates that adverse prognosis may trigger excessive renin release, while infection‐driven eNOS inhibition may promote sustained vasoconstriction and BP elevation. Additionally, the TTR (time in target range) in Group IV dropped to 30.9%, significantly lower than in Group I, suggesting poor BP control and elevated cardiovascular risk.

The LOCHINVAR study demonstrated that even among individuals without a prior hypertension history, SBP and DBP remained elevated 12 months post‐COVID‐19 infection (ΔSBP = +4.57 mmHg, p = 0.052; ΔDBP = +4.46 mmHg, p = 0.012), along with decreased flow‐mediated dilation (FMD), pointing toward persistent endothelial dysfunction and long‐term RAS imbalance [30]. Our findings corroborate this, as infection in patients with adverse prognosis was strongly associated with elevated BP load, ARV, and reduced dipping, collectively suggesting a convergence of arterial stiffness, autonomic overactivation, and circadian disruption that may drive long‐term cardiovascular deterioration.

This interaction appears to be more than additive, likely reflecting a pathological amplification effect, where infection and poor prognosis synergistically compromise BP stability and overall cardiovascular homeostasis. Consistent with this, arrhythmias have been reported in up to 10.4% of hospitalized patients with moderate to severe COVID‐19, including atrial fibrillation, non‐sustained ventricular tachycardia, and bradyarrhythmias [31, 32, 33].

Disrupted Blood Pressure Management amid the Pandemic: Synergistic Impact of Behavioral and Psychological Stressors

The COVID‐19 pandemic has not only influenced blood pressure dynamics through direct viral infection but also profoundly altered behavioral patterns and healthcare accessibility among patients with hypertension, presenting systemic challenges for chronic disease management. Chamberlain et al. [34] reported a decline in BP control rates from 60.5% in 2019 to 53.3% in 2020, while the BP improvement rate fell from 29.7% to 23.8%. Behaviorally, a Brazilian study involving 2140 adults noted a reduction in physical activity levels from 69% pre‐pandemic to 39% post‐pandemic onset [35]. Similarly, surveys conducted in the UK revealed a 30% decrease in overall physical activity during lockdowns [36].

Concurrently, lifestyle habits shifted in unfavorable directions. In the United States, household alcohol sales rose by 34.4% in Q2 2020 compared to the same period in 2019. Tobacco consumption increased by 13% from 2020 onward, while calls to smoking cessation hotlines dropped by 27% [37, 38, 39]. These behavioral regressions were paralleled by a substantial psychological burden. A global survey of 678 participants showed that over 50% experienced anxiety symptoms, nearly 60% reported high stress, and more than half exhibited depressive tendencies [40].

Conclusion

This single‐center retrospective cohort study employed 24‐h ABPM to evaluate the impact of COVID‐19 infection on hemodynamics and cardiovascular prognosis in hypertensive patients. To our knowledge, this represents the first real‐world study in China to apply 24‐h ambulatory blood pressure monitoring after full liberalization of epidemic control, providing unique insights into post‐COVID hemodynamic patterns. Our findings confirm that COVID‐19 infection significantly elevates nighttime and 24‐h systolic/diastolic blood pressure and load. When infection co‐occurs with adverse outcomes, patients showed the highest values in nighttime SBP load, non‐dipping patterns, and ARV of SBP/DBP, indicating a synergistic amplification of BP fluctuations.

Clinically, the “infection + adverse prognosis” group (Group IV) exhibited markedly abnormal BP dynamics, including an elevated 24‐h pulse pressure (60.93 mmHg, 20.0% higher than Group I, p < 0.001) and a TTR of 30.9% (vs. 74.7% in Group I, p < 0.001), highlighting poor control during nighttime and morning surges. In multivariate Cox regression, nighttime SBP load, nighttime pulse pressure, SBP/DBP dipping rates, BP variability, and ARV SBP were independent predictors, while higher TTR and BMI provided protective effects. SEM showed that infection exacerbates cardiovascular outcomes indirectly through increased nighttime BP levels, load, and variability. The indirect effect was significant, while the direct pathway from infection to prognosis was not, delineating a critical mediating chain: “infection → nighttime BPV → prognosis.”

Despite the strength of real‐world data, this study is limited by the small sample size of the adverse prognosis group (n = 45), which may introduce bias in multivariate modeling and reduce statistical power for subgroup analysis. Nevertheless, model diagnostics and internal validation indicated acceptable stability of coefficient estimates, suggesting that the multivariate findings are robust despite the limited event number. Future studies should adopt large‐scale multicenter cohorts incorporating dynamic BP parameters, biomarkers (e.g., hs‐CRP, IL‐6, endothelin‐1), and RAAS phenotypes to construct a more precise mechanistic network linking infection, BP dynamics, and prognosis. Therapeutic strategies should focus on time‐specific interventions targeting BP load and variability, such as adjusting medication timing to bedtime, using longer‐acting agents, or employing combination regimens to address nighttime instability and morning hypertension. In the context of personalized medicine, our findings underscore the need for infection‐specific optimization of antihypertensive management.

During and after COVID‐19 infection, maintaining equilibrium within the renin–angiotensin system (RAS) is crucial. Evidence suggests that continuing ACEI or ARB therapy does not worsen infection outcomes and may confer endothelial protection through ACE2 pathway modulation [20, 41]. Therefore, abrupt withdrawal of these agents should be avoided. For patients with persistent nocturnal BP elevation or increased variability post‐infection, bedtime dosing or the addition of long‐acting calcium channel blockers may improve circadian stability. Given the prothrombotic and inflammatory milieu of COVID‐19, individualized risk stratification should also consider low‐dose antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy in high‐risk hypertensive patients with endothelial dysfunction or elevated D‐dimer levels, consistent with contemporary cardiology consensus [14]. Adjusted BP targets may be warranted during acute infection to prevent excessive lowering and ensure adequate perfusion, particularly in older adults or those with multiorgan involvement. Collectively, these measures highlight a precision approach that integrates infection severity, vascular inflammation, and circadian hemodynamics into hypertension management during and after COVID‐19.

Given healthcare disruptions during the pandemic, strengthening home blood pressure monitoring (HBPM) and patient engagement is essential. The integration of telemedicine and AI‐assisted systems could enable real‐time BP data capture, personalized risk prediction, and automatic optimization of antihypertensive therapy.
