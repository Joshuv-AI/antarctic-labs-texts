Unveiling population heterogeneity in health risks posed by environmental hazards using regression-guided neural network
Authors: Jong Woo Nam, Eun Young Choi, Jennifer A. Ailshire, Yao-yi Chiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As environmental hazards become more frequent, it is critically important to understand their health impacts and identify individuals at disproportionately higher risk. Moderated Multiple Regression (MMR) provides a straightforward approach for investigating population heterogeneity by incorporating interaction terms between hazard exposure and population characteristics into a regression model. However, when vulnerabilities are embedded within complex, high-dimensional covariate spaces, MMR often fails to adequately model complex population heterogeneity. Here, we introduce a hybrid method, Regression-Guided Neural Networks (ReGNN), which integrates the flexibility of artificial neural networks (ANNs) within the structural form of a regression model. Briefly, ReGNN embeds an ANN inside a regression equation to generate a latent representation that nonlinearly combines potential sources of heterogeneity and moderates the effect of an environmental hazard. Because the outer layer maintains a regression structure, it delivers statistically robust inference while preserving traditional interpretability if augmented with Double Machine Learning (DML)-style residualization and cross-fitting. Through extensive simulation studies, we demonstrate ReGNN’s effectiveness in modeling complex heterogeneous effects. We further illustrate its utility by applying it to investigate population heterogeneity in the association of air pollution (PM2.5) with cognitive functioning scores. By comparing ReGNN’s results with those from traditional MMR models, we show that ReGNN can uncover patterns of heterogeneity that would otherwise remain hidden.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54345-y.

Introduction

In 2016, environmental hazards, including air pollution, extreme weather, and harmful chemical exposures, accounted for an estimated 24% of global deaths, or approximately 14 million annually. In the United States, ten climate-sensitive events in 2012 resulted in 10.0 billion dollars in health-related costs (2018 dollars)1,2. Although these threats are widespread, vulnerable groups, particularly those with limited capacity to anticipate, cope with, or recover from harm, face disproportionate risks3. Understanding population heterogeneity in environmental health risks is therefore essential for designing targeted and equitable policies and interventions.

With survey-based population studies becoming increasingly rich and multifaceted, researchers are now better equipped with data to capture a wide range of factors that may differentially influence individual health. Despite these advances, characterizing population heterogeneity remains challenging, as the relevant factors are often complex, and not easily reducible to a single or limited set of measurable variables.

While traditional regression-based approaches offer a straightforward and interpretable way to quantify heterogeneity, they are often inadequate in capturing the multidimensional nature of these relationships. Moderated multiple regression (MMR)4 extends linear regression, allowing researchers to use familiar tools, such as hypothesis testing, to evaluate the statistical significance and magnitude of moderation effects. However, MMR has several limitations: it is prone to structural multicollinearity due to correlations between interaction terms and their components; its statistical power decreases as additional interaction terms are added; and it requires strong prior hypotheses to determine the number and order of interaction terms to include5. Consequently, practitioners struggle to model and uncover complex heterogeneity when moderation effect is distributed across multiple variables.

Machine learning methods have emerged as flexible alternatives to traditional regression for modeling effect heterogeneity, enabling the discovery of complex patterns in high-dimensional data without requiring prespecified functional forms. However, existing approaches face a persistent trade-off between predictive flexibility and statistical interpretability. Generalized Additive Models (GAMs)6 and interaction forests7, for example, preserve interpretability by explicitly modeling low-order effects, but they become computationally burdensome in high-dimensional settings and often fail to capture smooth, higher-order dependencies. Tree-based causal methods such as BART8 and Causal Forests9 provide effective estimation of Conditional Average Treatment Effects (CATEs), supported by strong theoretical guarantees for inference. However, tree-based CATE methods may suffer from instability and limited smoothness in their estimates, as their partition-based structure can produce discontinuous treatment effect surfaces and sensitivity to tuning parameters. More recently, deep learning architectures—including TARNet10, Dragonnet11, and DRNet12—address high-dimensional heterogeneity through end-to-end representation learning. Although these models attempt to mitigate confounding via specialized loss functions or multi-head structures, they generally lack finite-sample guarantees and do not offer asymptotic properties, such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sqrt{n}$$\end{document}-consistency or valid confidence intervals, that are central to rigorous statistical inference. Thus, a significant gap remains for a method capable of simultaneously delivering the level of statistical interpretability required for population-based studies and the flexibility needed to accommodate increasingly high-dimensional data.

To address these limitations, we propose the Regression-Guided Neural Network (ReGNN), a semi-parametric framework that embeds a neural network within a regression model. Structurally, the neural network projects high-dimensional potential moderators into a low-dimensional composite score, which enters the outer regression as a single interaction term. This architecture forces the model to capture complex, nonlinear heterogeneity while retaining the parsimonious functional form of traditional Moderated Multiple Regression (MMR).

Crucially, ReGNN can operate within a Double Machine Learning (DML)-like framework13, utilizing cross-fitting and residualization. Assuming that ReGNN’s encoder achieves a convergence rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{O}_{p}\left({n}^{-1/4}\right)$$\end{document}, a rate theoretically attainable by suitably structured neural networks, the resulting estimators are asymptotically normal. This restores the validity of standard statistical tools, such as confidence intervals and hypothesis tests, that are typically inaccessible in deep learning. By automatically learning a composite heterogeneity score without requiring manual pre-specification, ReGNN synthesizes the expressive power of machine learning with the rigorous parameter-based inference of classical regression.

Taken together, ReGNN offers a framework that combines the flexibility of machine learning with the interpretability of regression, well-suited for exploratory studies where characterizing population heterogeneity is critical.

Interestingly, we find that this structural constraint guides the neural network to learn a meaningful reduced-dimensional representation that significantly moderates the focal predictor’s effect. While stochastic gradient descent may yield regression coefficients that differ from the closed-form estimates of traditional regression due to batch sampling noise, we observe that the overall magnitude of the coefficients (L2-norm) estimated in Step 1 stabilizes as training progresses. Correspondingly, the outcomes predicted in Steps 1 and 2 become highly correlated once the neural network converges. This indicates that the MSE objective, combined with the structural constraint imposed by the outer regression layer, effectively guides ReGNN to learn both a composite moderator and a set of regression coefficients that predict the outcome with reasonable accuracy. We therefore term this approach the Regression-Guided Neural Network, as its neural network component is trained via a regression loss that serves as a proxy supervisory signal, unlike conventional supervised algorithms, where minimizing MSE is both the means and the objective.

To illustrate, consider exposure to air pollution as the focal predictor. Suppose that for individuals characterized by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{p}}$$\end{document}, either (i) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{a}\boldsymbol{r}\left[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\boldsymbol{f}}\right|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{p}}]\ll\:\boldsymbol{g}({\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{p}}{)}^{2}$$\end{document}, or (ii) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{a}\boldsymbol{r}\left[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\boldsymbol{f}}\right|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{p}}]\gg\:\boldsymbol{g}({\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{p}}{)}^{2}$$\end{document}. In the first case, exposure levels are largely determined by structural factors (e.g., persistent residential segregation), implying limited within-profile variation. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{\lambda\:}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{p}}\right)\to\:0$$\end{document}, and ReGNN substantially attenuates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{\tau\:}}_{0}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{p}}\right)$$\end{document}. In the second case, exposure varies widely within profile, yielding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{\lambda\:}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{p}}\right)\approx\:1$$\end{document}, and ReGNN closely recovers the true heterogeneous effect. Consequently, omitting residualization may produce inaccurate estimates of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{c}}_{\boldsymbol{f}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{c}}^{\boldsymbol{i}\boldsymbol{n}\boldsymbol{t}}$$\end{document} in the presence of confounding.

However, non-residualized training does not render ReGNN uninformative. Instead, it yields a conservative representation of moderation patterns in the data. Because the attenuation operates toward zero, deviations of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{f}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{p}}\right)$$\end{document} from the population mean reflect comparatively robust associative signals. As a result, non-residualized ReGNN remains useful for exploratory analysis, as it prioritizes patterns supported by substantial within-profile variation.

Moreover, because our goal is to identify subgroups with relatively high or low effect heterogeneity, thresholding on the summary moderator \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{f}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{p}}\right)$$\end{document} primarily increases the risk of Type II error (false negatives), rather than generating spurious evidence of heterogeneity.

Simulated study

We assess ReGNN’s capacity to recover true moderation effects using synthetic data with complex, predefined heterogeneity. Specifically, we: (1) benchmark performance across varying signal-to-noise ratios, to provide heuristics for real-world applications lacking ground truth; and (2) investigate the relationship between recovery accuracy and the validity of post-training inferences, thereby establishing confidence in the proposed framework.

For our benchmarking effort, we compare ReGNN with and without residualization against Causal Forests. While alternative approaches such as elastic-net interaction models and generalized additive models (GAMs) are also relevant comparators, our primary goal is to provide an illustrative comparison. In this context, we focus on Causal Forests as a representative nonparametric baseline, given their ability to flexibly capture heterogeneous effects while retaining well-established inferential properties. Performance is quantified across three critical dimensions: structural fidelity, measured as the correlation between predicted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{z}$$\end{document} and ground truth moderator \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{M}$$\end{document}; estimation bias, assessed through the degree of shrinkage of the effect magnitude; and subgroup identification, defined as the model’s recall in correctly classifying the most vulnerable subpopulation (> 84th percentile of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{M}$$\end{document}). Hyperparameters remain fixed across all experiments to ensure comparability.

To this end, we generate a dataset of 8000 samples with 31 variables. All variables are initially continuous, drawn from a multivariate normal distribution. These results suggest that, in high-noise settings, features with substantially large influence relative to others can still be considered reliable, whereas smaller differences in SHAP values should be interpreted with caution.

An additional post-training analysis within the ReGNN framework involves clustering the vulnerable population identified by the model into subgroups with similar characteristics. Specifically, we fit a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) to the subset of units classified as vulnerable by ReGNN, using both the original input features and the ReGNN-derived index \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{z}$$\end{document} as clustering inputs.

In Table 1, we report recall, defined as the proportion of truly vulnerable units that are correctly identified, using the same percentile threshold (84th percentile of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{z}$$\end{document}). This quantifies the extent to which the ReGNN-identified vulnerable group overlaps with the ground-truth high-risk subpopulation.

Table 1 shows that recall declines as measurement noise increases, meaning that the ReGNN-identified vulnerable population captures a smaller share of the ground-truth vulnerable group. Since the goal is to identify and characterize vulnerable individuals, lower thresholds can be adopted to increase recall (Fig. 2c). However, achieving good clustering performance requires balancing recall with precision. If the identified vulnerable group contains more false positives than true positives (i.e., low precision), the resulting clusters may shift in distribution and deviate from the ground truth.

To examine how precision-recall trade-offs affect clustering performance, we evaluate the impact of applying different threshold levels (70th ~ 95th percentiles in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{z}$$\end{document}) to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{z}$$\end{document} on the GMM results. Procedurally, we first fit a GMM to the ground-truth vulnerable population (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{M}>\:$$\end{document}84th percentile), identifying four clusters. These clusters serve as the reference. We then fit a second GMM to the ReGNN-identified vulnerable population, defined by the chosen threshold, fixing the number of components at four to enable one-to-one alignment with the reference clusters. Cluster-to-cluster correspondence is established by minimizing the total Mahalanobis distance between matched pairs of clusters. Finally, we assess the sensitivity of the clustering results by examining how these Mahalanobis distances vary across threshold levels and SNR conditions.

In summary, our simulated study shows that ReGNN effectively learns a representation that interacts with the focal predictor, enabling the recovery of underlying moderation structures.

Cognition vs. air pollution: ReGNN uncovers moderation effect not discovered by traditional regression

We demonstrate ReGNN’s utility in real-world contexts by applying it to assess how short-term exposure to air pollution (30-day average) differentially affects cognitive functioning in older adults. Existing evidence on the short-term association of PM2.5 exposure with cognition remains mixed15,16.We therefore focus on a 30-day exposure window to test whether underlying population heterogeneity, captured by ReGNN, can help explain inconsistencies and uncover factors contributing to vulnerabilities that traditional approaches may overlook.

We implement ReGNN with cross-fitting but without residualization. Because we observe residualizing a low-variance predictor strips away essential signal, preventing model convergence and rendering moderation effects undetectable, we accept the attenuation induced by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{\lambda\:}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{p}}\right)$$\end{document} (Eq. (6)). While this approach provides valuable, conservative characterizations of heterogeneity for subgroups with sufficient variance, the resulting estimates are primarily associational rather than inferentially clean.

We compile four primary datasets for our analysis. First, we draw on individual-level data from the 2016 Health and Retirement Study (HRS), a nationally representative survey of U.S. adults aged 50 and older17. These data provide extensive sociodemographic and health information, including our outcome measure (cognitive functioning score). Second, we incorporate 2016 daily fine particulate matter (PM2.5) concentration estimates from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency’s Fused Air Quality Surface Using Downscaling (FAQSD) files, which employ a data fusion, or “downscaler,” model to generate local PM2.5 concentrations across the contiguous U.S18. To capture short-term exposure, we average PM2.5 concentrations over the 30 days preceding each respondent’s interview date. Third, we use tract-level socioeconomic indicators from the U.S. Census Bureau’s 2014–2018 American Community Survey (ACS) 5-year estimates19. Both the PM2.5 and ACS datasets are available through the HRS Contextual Data Resource and are linked to the 2016 h sample using respondents’ geocoded census tract and interview dates. Finally, we obtain tract-level land cover measures from the U.S. Geological Survey’s National Land Cover Database, accessed via the National Neighborhood Data Archive20, and link these to the 2016 h records. Further detail about each dataset is provided in the Methods section.

Compared with MMR model, ReGNN demonstrates greater capacity to identify population heterogeneity that is otherwise difficult to detect. Figure 5 illustrates this by comparing the regression coefficients of MMR and ReGNN’s second stage regression model. The MMR model includes all predictors as moderators, whereas the second stage regression model only has ReGNN-derived index \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{z}$$\end{document} as the sole moderator of PM2.5. Because residualization is not applied in this specification, we additionally conduct a bootstrap analysis to assess robustness. The bootstrap results confirm a comparable level of statistical significance (Fig. 3, Supplementary Materials). Although the regression coefficient of this index may look small (Fig. 5, top row, orange), its moderating effect is quite significant, as displayed in Fig. 6b. Here, we plot the means and confidence intervals of the predicted cognitive functioning scores using the fitted second stage regression model, for differing levels of PM2.5. Other independent variables are held at their means in getting the predicted cognitive scores. We observe that the deteriorating association of air pollution with cognition is significantly reduced for those with a lower summary index (blue, bottom 10th percentile) while it is inflated for those who score higher on the summary index (green, top 10th percentile). In other words, exposure to higher levels of air pollution is associated with a measurable decline in cognitive function among the vulnerable group, whereas the effect is minimal among the more resilient group. Because the error bars for each predicted point do not overlap across the three groups, we conclude that the summary index significantly moderates the association of PM2.5 with cognition.

We emphasize, however, that this finding is associational rather than causal, particularly given that the moderating effect is not robust to residualization.

With regression coefficients showing a significant moderation effect of the summary index, it remains now to understand how ReGNN produces them. To this end, we employ SHAP values and GMM-based clustering, to parse which predictors contributed to the network’s assignment of values for this index.

Figure 7a presents the feature-importance rankings of candidate moderators used as inputs to the trained ReGNN. Moderate physical activity emerges as the most influential feature, consistent with its prominent interaction coefficient in the MMR model (left panel, Fig. 5). Notably, however, the interaction coefficient for moderate physical activity in the MMR specification is negative, implying that individuals who engage in moderate physical activity exhibit greater cognitive decline with increasing PM2.5 exposure. A similar pattern is observed for years of education, where higher educational attainment appears to be associated with increased vulnerability to PM2.5 when modeled using MMR.

In contrast, ReGNN suggests the opposite pattern. The partial dependence plots in Fig. 7b and c indicate that the vulnerability captured by the summary index decreases with greater moderate physical activity and higher levels of education. This discrepancy underscores a key limitation of modeling interaction effects one variable at a time, as in MMR: such specifications aggregate moderation effects across the entire population and may obscure higher-order or joint patterns of heterogeneity that are captured by the representation-learning approach.

Overall, no moderate physical activity, BMI, and lower education emerge as key drivers of vulnerability. We conduct a review of relevant literature to assess how aligned the high-ranked features are with the past findings. We find that the directions of the influences for many features are aligned: physical activity is reported to mitigate negative association of PM2.5 with cognition21; people who received 8 years or less education are found to have higher probability of cognitive impairment with increased PM2.5 exposure22.

Limitations and future work

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the learned latent index is susceptible to credit assignment under multicollinearity: when predictors are highly correlated, the network may arbitrarily emphasize one feature over another, leading to instability in feature-level interpretation despite stable predictive performance. Second, although residualization enhances causal interpretability, the ReGNN-derived summary index should be interpreted as capturing population-level heterogeneity rather than as a precise estimate of individual-level treatment effects.

Future work could improve transparency through more structured encoders. Structural equation modeling (SEM)-inspired factorization architectures that group moderators into interpretable latent components can potentially resolve credit-assignment problem. Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks23 and soft decision trees can be adopted to serve as ReGNN’s encoder to enhance transparency. Finally, incorporating a variational autoencoder-like structure could further strengthen interpretability by providing uncertainty estimates for individual-level scores.

Discussion

The Regression-Guided Neural Network (ReGNN) introduces a principled framework that reconciles the predictive power of deep learning with the interpretability of regression. By compressing complex, high-order moderation patterns into a single, regression-compatible index, ReGNN enables the discovery of structured heterogeneity. Within a DML-style framework, ReGNN’s second-stage regression ensures that the resulting estimates retain the validity of familiar tools for hypothesis testing and inference. Using simulated data, we demonstrate that this representation-learning approach successfully recovers underlying moderation structures. Furthermore, in an empirical application examining the effect of PM2.5 on cognition, ReGNN reveals substantively meaningful population differences that remain obscured by conventional modeling strategies. While the deliberate omission of residualization limits these specific findings to associational inference, the approach provides an exploratory characterization of complex heterogeneity.

This capability is particularly timely for environmental health research. As climate change, urbanization, and environmental degradation intensify the scale and severity of environmental hazards, identifying vulnerable subpopulations is no longer optional; it is essential for designing equitable, targeted policy and intervention strategies. Traditional regression models, while interpretable, often lack the flexibility to capture the complex, interacting determinants of vulnerability in modern, high-dimensional datasets. ReGNN directly addresses this challenge by enabling data-driven discovery of vulnerability structure without sacrificing interpretability.

Beyond environmental health, the framework is broadly applicable to domains where data complexity is rapidly increasing and understanding population heterogeneity is central, such as precision medicine, social epidemiology, public policy, and behavioral science. By unifying representation learning with regression-based inference, ReGNN provides a scalable, interpretable, and methodologically rigorous foundation for studying heterogeneity in contemporary data environments. In doing so, it helps bridge a long-standing gap between machine learning flexibility and statistical accountability, offering a durable framework for future methodological and applied advances.

Regression guided neural network

ReGNN implementation details

We embed (an ensemble of) multi-layer perceptron within regression model setup in our study. We adopt either MSE or weighted-MSE as our training objective:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Weighted\:MSE\:=\:\frac{\sum\:_{i}{w}_{i}{({o}_{i}-{\widehat{o}}_{i})}^{2}}{\sum\:_{i}{w}_{i}}$$\end{document} where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{w}}_{\boldsymbol{i}}$$\end{document} is the survey weight of the ith sample, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{o}}_{\boldsymbol{i}}$$\end{document} is its measured outcome variable, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{\boldsymbol{o}}}_{\boldsymbol{i}}$$\end{document} is its predicted outcome.

To stabilize the outer-layer regression coefficients while retaining sufficient signal to effectively train the MLP encoder, we adopt several tricks. First, we batch-normalize the output of the MLP encoder and scale it by a single learnable parameter (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{c}}^{\boldsymbol{i}\boldsymbol{n}\boldsymbol{t}}$$\end{document}) before passing it to the regression layer. This separates the learning of the shape of heterogeneity (captured by the neural network) from the estimation of its magnitude, which is governed by the regression coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{c}}^{\boldsymbol{i}\boldsymbol{n}\boldsymbol{t}}$$\end{document}.

Second, we use gradient accumulation to update the regression coefficients (including \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{c}}^{\boldsymbol{i}\boldsymbol{n}\boldsymbol{t}}$$\end{document}) over a larger effective batch size. While the encoder is trained with mini-batches of 64, gradients for the outer regression layer are aggregated across 1024–4096 samples before updating, reducing variance and improving stability. We also adopt a larger initial learning rate for the regression coefficients (e.g. lr = 0.01) with exponential decay (0.9 per epoch), while training the encoder with a smaller constant learning rate (e.g. lr = 0.003).

All model training implementation is done using Pytorch24, while model fitting and evaluations of regression models are done using Stata25.

Hyperparameters used for simulated study

We use a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) of size (35, 40, 10, 1) embedded within a regression model. We train the network for 10 epochs with a batch size of 64. We update the regression coefficients every 32 iterations (2048 samples). Dropout rate of 20% is applied during training. We use distinct learning rates for the regression coefficients (lr = 0.01) and for the MLP parameters (lr = 0.003). We apply weight decay of 0.001 only to the MLP’s parameters. Post-hoc analyses

SHAP-based feature importance

We compute Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) using the Python shap library14. To approximate the background distribution, we generate 50 representative background samples via k-means clustering on the training set. For each cross-validation fold, we use the trained ReGNN model to compute SHAP values on the held-out fold, ensuring out-of-sample explanations. SHAP values are estimated using the KernelExplainer and aggregated across folds to obtain feature attributions for all samples in a cross-validated manner. We then compute mean absolute SHAP values to rank the features.

Gaussian mixture model

We fit Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) using the Python scikit-learn library26. We vary the number of components from 1 to 10 and select the model with the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). For the simulated study, we compute Mahalanobis distances for all cluster pairs to establish one-to-one correspondence between the reference clusters (from the GMM fit to the ground-truth vulnerable subgroup). Distances are calculated using a covariance matrix obtained by averaging the covariance matrices of the two clusters being compared. Cluster pairs are then matched by minimizing the total Mahalanobis distance.

Cognition vs. PM2.5 study

Dataset

Health outcome: cognitive functioning score

HRS respondents’ cognitive functioning is assessed using the modified Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICSm)27. The TICSm evaluates three cognitive domains: memory (test of 10 words immediate and delayed recall, range: 0–20 points), working memory (test of serial 7s subtraction, 0–5 points), and speed of mental processing (test of backward counting, 0–2 points). The composite summary score ranges from 0 to 27.

Focal predictor: air pollution (PM2.5)

For each HRS respondent, we calculate the mean concentration of residential PM2.5 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mu\:g}\cdot{m}^{-3}$$\end{document}) at the census tract level over the past 30 days preceding their interview dates.

Other input variables

Other predicting and moderating variables are selected a priori, based on their potential roles as effect modifiers in the association between air pollution and cognitive function28 as well as their roles as main risk (or protective) factors for the cognitive function.

Individual-level predictors include age, gender (men and women), race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and non-Hispanic Other), education years, household income (log-transformed), household non-housing financial wealth (transformed using an inverse hyperbolic sine), BMI index, five self-reported chronic conditions diagnosed by a physician (i.e., stroke, heart disease, lung disease, diabetes, hypertension), depressive mood, light, moderate, and vigorous physical activity levels (never, < 1 per month, 1–3 times per month, 1–2 times per week, and 3 + times per week).

Area-level predictors include urban/rural residence, and green space index. Green space within each census tract is quantified by calculating the percentage of land cover classified as various types of vegetation and wetlands (e.g., deciduous forest, evergreen forest, mixed forest, shrub/scrub, herbaceous, and hay/pasture), with the index ranging from 0 to 100, where higher values indicate greater coverage of green space.

Data Availability

The simulated dataset and accompanying code used in this study are available from the authors upon request. Data used to examine the relationship between cognition and air pollution are drawn from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), which is restricted but can be accessed through the HRS data request process: https://hrs.isr.umich.edu. Raw environmental and contextual data, including air pollution, sociodemographic, and land cover variables, are publicly available through the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, the American Community Survey, and the U.S. Geological Survey’s National Land Cover Database. Processed versions of these data can be accessed through https://gero.usc.edu/cbph/cdr/. Code for linking these external datasets to HRS is available from the authors upon request.
