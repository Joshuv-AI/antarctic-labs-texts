Head‐to‐head comparison of three chelates reveals DOTAGA promising for 225Ac labeling of anti‐FZD10 antibody OTSA101
Authors: Hitomi Sudo, Atsushi B Tsuji, Aya Sugyo, Yosuke Harada, Satoshi Nagayama, Toyomasa Katagiri, Yusuke Nakamura, Tatsuya Higashi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo select the most suitable chelate for 225Ac radiolabeling of the anti‐FZD10 antibody OTSA101, we directly compared three chelates: S‐2‐(4‐isothiocyanatobenzyl)‐1,4,7,10‐tetraazacyclododecane tetraacetic acid (p‐SCN‐Bn‐DOTA), 2,2′,2″‐(10‐(1‐carboxy‐4‐((4‐isothiocyanatobenzyl)amino)‐4‐oxobutyl)‐1,4,7,10‐tetraazacyclododecane‐1,4,7‐triyl) triacetic acid (p‐SCN‐Bn‐DOTAGA), and 1,4,7,10‐tetraazacyclododecane‐1,4,7,10‐tetraacetic acid mono‐N‐hydroxysuccinimide ester (DO3A‐NHS‐ester). We evaluated the binding affinity of the chelate‐conjugated OTSA101 antibodies, as well as the labeling efficiency and stability in murine serum of 225Ac‐labeled OTSA101 as in vitro properties. The biodistribution, intratumoral distribution, absorbed doses, and therapeutic effects of the chelate‐conjugated OTSA101 antibodies were assessed in the synovial sarcoma mouse model SYO‐1. Of the three conjugates, DOTAGA conjugation had the smallest impact on the binding affinity (p < 0.01). The labeling efficiencies of DOTAGA‐OTSA101 and DO3A‐OTSA101 were 1.8‐fold higher than that of DOTA‐OTSA101 (p < 0.01). The stabilities were similar between 225Ac‐labeled DOTA‐OTSA101, DOTAGA‐OTSA101, and DO3A‐OTSA101in serum at 37 and 4°C. The dosimetric analysis based on the biodistribution revealed significantly higher tumor‐absorbed doses by 225Ac‐labeled DOTA‐OTSA101 and DOTAGA‐OTSA101 compared with 225Ac‐DO3A‐OTSA101 (p < 0.05). 225Ac‐DOTAGA‐OTSA101 exhibited the highest tumor‐to‐bone marrow ratio, with bone marrow being the dose‐limiting tissue. The therapeutic and adverse effects were not significantly different between the three conjugates. Our findings indicate that among the three evaluated chelates, DOTAGA appears to be the most promising chelate to produce 225Ac‐labeled OTSA101 with high binding affinity and high radiochemical yields while providing high absorbed doses to tumors and limited absorbed doses to bone marrow.Keywords: alpha‐particle, chelate, FZD10, Radioimmunotherapy, therapeutic nuclear medicine

INTRODUCTION

In radioimmunotherapy, radionuclides are delivered specifically to tumors using high‐affinity antibodies against tumor‐associated antigens. 1 In clinical practice, radioimmunotherapy for hematologic malignancies is in many aspects an effective approach, especially anti‐CD20 antibodies conjugated with the β‐emitters 90Y and 131I, which provides high overall response rates (60%–80%) with a complete remission rate of 15%–40%. 1 , 2 Unfortunately, however, the efficacy is insufficient for solid tumors. 1

Radioimmunotherapy with α‐emitters is anticipated to overcome the low outcome for solid tumors. 3 Alpha‐particles have a greater linear energy transfer than beta‐particles and deposit more energy into cells, thereby inducing more cytotoxicity. 3 Actinium‐225 (225Ac) is the most promising α‐emitting radionuclide, generating a total of four α‐particles in the decay chain. 4 The half‐life of 225Ac is 10 days, which is appropriate for the pharmacokinetics of antibodies. 5 Many preclinical studies have demonstrated promising results. 3 , 6 , 7

For 225Ac labeling, 1,4,7,10‐tetraazacyclododecane‐1.4,7,10‐tetraacetic acid (DOTA) derivatives are used in most preclinical studies and clinical trials. 3 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 A phase 1 study with the 225Ac‐labeled antibody lintuzumab used SCN‐DOTA with four carboxyl groups as a chelate 9 although 225Ac‐labeled lintuzumab via 1,4,7,10‐tetraazacyclododecane‐1,4,7,10‐tetraacetic acid mono‐N‐hydroxysuccinimide ester (DO3A) with three arms exhibited a preferable biodistribution in mice. 13 Another derivative, 1,4,7,10‐tetraazacyclododececane,1‐(glutaric acid)‐4,7,10‐triacetic acid (DOTAGA), has been used in preclinical studies with 225Ac‐radioimmunotherapy 14 and in clinical studies for a non‐antibody‐based drug targeting the prostate‐specific membrane antigen (PSMA). 15 Although these three chelates are considered suitable for 225Ac‐radioimmunotherapy, no head‐to‐head comparisons have been performed to determine which derivative is the best.

Here, we conducted a comparison of three commercially available bifunctional chelates, S‐2‐(4‐isothiocyanatobenzyl)‐1,4,7,10‐tetraazacyclododecane tetraacetic acid (p‐SCN‐Bn‐DOTA), 2,2′,2″‐(10‐(1‐carboxy‐4‐((4‐isothiocyanatobenzyl)amino)‐4‐oxobutyl)‐1,4,7,10‐tetraazacyclododecane‐1,4,7‐triyl)triacetic acid (p‐SCN‐Bn‐DOTAGA), and 1,4,7,10‐tetraazacyclododecane‐1,4,7,10‐tetraacetic acid mono‐N‐hydroxysuccinimide ester (DO3A‐NHS‐ester) to evaluate the most suitable chelate of the three for 225Ac radiolabeling of antibodies. As an antibody, the anti‐Frizzled homolog 10 (FZD10) antibody OTSA101 was used. FZD10 is overexpressed in most synovial sarcomas, which is an intractable tumor. This antibody was used in a clinical trial of 111In/90Y‐labeled OTSA101 with a derivative of diethylenetriamine pentaacetic acid 16 and in a preclinical study with 225Ac‐labeled OTSA101 via p‐SCN‐Bn‐DOTA. 6 This evaluation would establish more effective alpha‐radioimmunotherapy using OTSA101 for synovial sarcomas. The present study evaluated in vitro properties such as the binding affinity, labeling efficiency, and stability in serum, as well as in vivo properties such as the biodistribution, intratumoral distribution, absorbed doses, and therapeutic effects in the synovial sarcoma mouse model SYO‐1.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell culture

A human synovial sarcoma cell line, SYO‐1, 17 was gifted by Dr. Akira Kawai (National Cancer Center, Tokyo, Japan). The cells were cultured in DMEM (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) containing 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in 5% CO2 at 37°C.

Antibody and chelate conjugation

The chimeric antibody OTSA101, which recognizes human FZD10 but not murine FZD10, was provided by OncoTherapy Science. Chelating agents p‐SCN‐Bn‐DOTA (DOTA) and DOTA‐NHS‐ester (DO3A) were purchased from Macrocyclics. Another chelating agent, p‐SCN‐Bn‐DOTAGA (DOTAGA), was obtained from CheMatech. OTSA101 (5 mg/mL) was reacted with 2.5, 3, and 25 equal molar amounts of DOTA, DOTAGA, and DO3A, respectively, in 50 mmol/L borate buffer (pH 8.5) for 16 h at 37°C. The conjugation ratios of antibody to chelate were determined by labeling with 111In (Nihon Medi‐Physics) by radio‐thin layer chromatography (TLC) as previously reported. 6 The ratios were approximately 2.1 for DOTA, 2.1 for DOTAGA, and 2.0 for DO3A. The chelate‐conjugated antibodies were purified by elution with 0.1 mol/L acetate buffer (pH 6.0) using a Sephadex G‐50 column (GE Healthcare BioSciences).

Binding affinity

The binding affinity of the chelate‐conjugated OTSA101 was evaluated by ELISA with recombinant human FZD10 peptide (Sino Biological). A 96‐well plate was coated with the peptide dissolved in PBS at 0.3 μg/mL for 60 min. After blocking with 1% BSA/PBS with Tween 20 for 60 min, various amounts of chelate‐conjugated antibodies were added and the mixtures were incubated overnight at 4°C. HRP‐labeled goat anti‐human IgG antibody (Bethyl Laboratories) and 3,3′,5,5′‐tetramethylbenzidine reagent (Nacalai Tesque) were used to detect bound antibodies. The reaction was terminated by 1 mol/L H2SO4 and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a microplate reader (SpectraMax i3x, Molecular Devices). The dissociation constant (K d) was determined using GraphPad Prism 9 software (ver. 9.3.0, GraphPad Software).

Antibody radiolabeling

Radiolabeling of the chelate‐conjugated OTSA101 with 225Ac was conducted as described previously. 7 225AcNO3 (Oak Ridge National Labs) dissolved in 200 mmol/L UltraPur HCl (Kanto Chemical) was added to 2 mol/L tetramethylammonium acetate (Tokyo Chemical Industry) and 150 g/L L‐ascorbic acids (Millipore Sigma). Chelate‐conjugated OTSA101s were added to 225Ac, and the mixtures were incubated for various durations at 37°C. The labeling efficiency was estimated by the ratio of total‐to‐labeled protein radioactivity based on the radio‐TLC. The radiolabeled antibodies were purified using an Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter (Merck) and the purified antibody was analyzed by radio‐TLC.

Stability of the radiolabeled antibody in murine serum

Purified 225Ac‐labeled OTSA101s were added to mouse serum (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical) and incubated at 37 or 4°C. At various time points, 3 μL of the reaction mixture was removed from each sample and mixed with 50 mmol/L DTPA (Millipore Sigma). Next, antibody‐bound 225Ac was analyzed by radio‐TLC using 10 mmol/L EDTA (Millipore Sigma) as the mobile phase.

Tumor model

The animal experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the National Institutes for Quantum Science and Technology (13–1022‐8, 18 Feb 2020), and all animal experiments were conducted following the Animal Care and Handling Guidelines. SYO‐1 cells (5 × 106) were subcutaneously inoculated into male nude mice (BALB/c‐nu/nu, 4 weeks old, CLEA Japan) under isoflurane anesthesia.

Biodistribution of the radiolabeled antibody

When tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm3, mice (n = 5/time point) were intravenously injected with the 225Ac‐labeled OTSA101s (3.7 kBq) in a total of 30 μg antibody, which was adjusted by adding the intact antibody. The mice were killed by isoflurane inhalation on days 1, 2, 4, or 7 after injection of the 225Ac‐labeled antibodies. Blood was obtained from the heart, and the tumor, lung, liver, spleen, pancreas, intestine, kidney, and muscle were dissected and weighed. Radioactivity was measured with a gamma counter (PerkinElmer). Uptake is represented as the percentage of the injected dose (radioactivity) per gram of tissue (% ID/g).

Autoradiography

Tumors (n = 3 per group) were dissected on day 4 after injection of the 225Ac‐labeled OTSA101s (3.7 kBq) in a total of 30 μg protein adjusted by the intact antibody. The tumors were frozen and sliced into 20‐μm thick sections. The sections were exposed to an imaging plate, which was then scanned with an FLA‐7000 image plate reader (Fuji Film). The sections were stained with H&E.

Dosimetry

As described previously, 6 , 18 the absorbed doses of the 225Ac‐labeled antibodies were estimated using the area under the curve based on the biodistribution data of 225Ac‐labeled antibodies and the mean energy emitted per transition of 225Ac and all the daughter nuclei with corrections for branching, 4.6262 × 10−12 Gy kg (Bq s)−1. 19 The bone marrow‐absorbed dose was based on the blood data, considering a red marrow‐to‐blood activity ratio of 0.4. 20 A radiation weighting factor of 5 was used as recommended by the Medical Internal Radiation Does Committee. 21 This biologically effective dose (BED) is expressed in Berendsen (Bd). 22

Radioimmunotherapy with the 225Ac‐labeled antibodies

The mice were intravenously injected with the 225Ac‐labeled OTSA101s (0.01 MBq, n = 5) in a total of 30 μg protein adjusted by the intact antibody. Tumor sizes and body weights were measured at least twice a week for 10 weeks after administration. Tumor size was measured using a digital caliper, and the tumor volume was calculated according to the following formula: tumor volume (mm3) = (length × width2)/2. When the tumor volume reached more than 800 mm3 and body weight loss was greater than 20% compared with that at day 0, the mouse was killed by isoflurane inhalation.

Histologic analysis

Tumors, liver, spleen, kidney, and femurs were resected from mice on day 4 or 7 after injection with the 225Ac‐labeled OTSA101s (0.01 MBq, n = 3/time point). The resected tumors and organs were fixed in 10% neutral‐buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into 1‐μm thick sections. The tumor and organ sections were deparaffinized and stained with H&E. Tumor cell proliferation was evaluated by Ki‐67 immunohistochemical staining with a rabbit anti‐Ki‐67 antibody (SP6, Abcam) and an Anti Rabbit HRP/DAB Detection Kit (Abcam). Apoptosis was detected using the DeadEnd Colorimetric TUNEL System (Promega).

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software. The uptake of the 225Ac‐labeled antibodies was analyzed by multiple comparisons with Tukey methods for two‐way ANOVA. Estimated absorbed dose data were analyzed by multiple comparisons with Tukey methods for one‐way ANOVA. Tumor volume and body weight data were analyzed by multiple comparisons with Tukey methods for two‐way ANOVA. A p‐value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all experiments.

RESULTS

Binding affinity by ELISA

The dissociation constants (K d) were estimated to be 0.15 nmol/L for intact OTSA101, 0.54 nmol/L for DOTA‐OTSA101, 0.28 nmol/L for DOTAGA‐OTSA101, and 0.42 nmol/L for DO3A‐OTSA101 (Figure 1). Significant differences in the K d values were detected between the three chelate‐conjugated OTSA101s (p < 0.01). Although significant differences were detected between intact and chelate‐conjugated OTSA101s (p < 0.01), the effect on binding affinity after chelate conjugation was limited.

Binding affinities of DOTA‐, DOTAGA‐, and DO3A‐conjugated OTSA101. Significant differences in the dissociating constants (K d) were detected between intact and each chelate‐conjugated OTSA101 (p < 0.01).

Radiolabeling efficiency and specific activity

The labeling efficiency of chelate‐conjugated OTSA101 increased with time and reached a steady state at 60 min (Figure 2). The maximum labeling efficiency of DOTA‐, DOTAGA‐, and DO3A‐conjugated OTSA101 was 30.5 ± 11.0%, 58.2 ± 14.5%, and 53.6 ± 7.3%, respectively. The maximum labeling efficiency of DOTA‐conjugated OTSA101 was significantly lower than that of the other conjugates (p < 0.01). A reaction time of 60 min was used for subsequent experiments. The specific activities at 60 min were 0.5 ± 0.3 kBq/μg for 225Ac‐labeled DOTA‐OTSA101, 0.7 ± 0.1 kBq/μg for 225Ac‐labeled DOTAGA‐OTSA101, and 0.7 ± 0.0 kBq/μg for the 225Ac‐labeled DO3A‐OTSA101. The radiochemical purities were 96.8 ± 4.2% for 225Ac‐labeled DOTA‐OTSA101, 98.8 ± 1.2% for 225Ac‐labeled DOTAGA‐OTSA101, and 97.8 ± 1.8% for 225Ac‐labeled DO3A‐OTSA101.

225Ac‐labeling efficiency of DOTA‐, DOTAGA‐, and DO3A‐conjugated OTSA101. **p < 0.01 versus DOTA‐OTSA101.

Stability of 225Ac‐labeled antibodies in murine serum

The in vitro stability of the 225Ac‐labeled DOTA‐, DOTAGA‐, and DO3A‐OTSA101 was indicated by the ratio of change in the labeled antibody at the start of incubation (Figure 3A). More than 96% of the 225Ac remained bound to all the three 225Ac‐labeled antibodies in murine serum at 37°C for 7 days (Figure 3A) and similar stability was observed at 4°C (Figure 3B).

Stability of 225Ac‐labeled DOTA‐, DOTAGA‐, and DO3A‐OTSA101 in mouse serum at 37°C (A) and 4°C (B).

Biodistribution and intratumoral distribution of the 225Ac‐labeled antibodies

Tumor uptake of all three 225Ac‐labeled chelate‐OTSA101s increased for up to 4 days after injection and decreased at day 7 (Table 1). The maximum tumor uptake was 32.8 ± 7.3% for 225Ac‐DOTA‐OTSA101, 30.4 ± 17.7% for 225Ac‐DOTAGA‐OTSA101, and 29.9 ± 16.5% for 225Ac‐DO3A‐OTSA101 (Table 1). Tumor uptake did not differ significantly between the three chelate‐OTSA101s. The blood level of 225Ac‐DO3A‐OTSA101 on day 4 was significantly lower than that of 225Ac‐DOTA‐OTSA101 and 225Ac‐DOTAGA‐OTSA101 (Table 1). Although the normal tissue uptake of the three 225Ac‐labeled chelate‐conjugated OTSA101s was almost similar, significant differences were observed in several organs, including the lung, kidney, and intestine (Table 1). Autoradiography and H&E‐staining of SYO‐1 tumors show that the radioactivity was colocalized with the tumor cell regions but not with the area of necrosis and fibrous tissue (Figure 4).

Dosimetry

The absorbed doses were estimated based on the biodistribution studies (Table 2). The absorbed doses of 225Ac‐DOTA‐OTSA101 in the lungs, kidneys, and intestine were significantly higher than those of 225Ac‐DOTAGA‐ and DO3A‐OTSA101 (Table 2). The absorbed doses in bone marrow were 137.9 ± 9.1 Gy/MBq for 225Ac‐DOTA‐OTSA101, 120.0 ± 5.7 Gy/MBq for 225Ac‐DOTAGA‐OTSA101, and 105.7 ± 4.5 Gy/MBq for 225Ac‐DO3A‐OTSA101 (Table 2). The absorbed dose of 225Ac‐DOTA‐OTSA101 in the bone marrow was significantly higher than that of the other conjugates (Table 2). The tumor‐absorbed doses were 546.4 ± 56.8 Gy/MBq for 225Ac‐DOTA‐OTSA101, 573.9 ± 54.2 Gy/MBq for 225Ac‐DOTAGA‐OTSA101, and 412.4 ± 72.1 Gy/MBq for 225Ac‐DO3A‐OTSA101. The tumor‐absorbed dose from 225Ac‐DO3A‐OTSA101 was significantly lower than that of the other conjugates (Table 2).

Treatment effects of radiolabeled antibodies

All three 225Ac‐labeled OTSA101s exhibited marked antitumor effects in tumor‐bearing mice (Figure 5A). In all three treatment groups, one of five mice exhibited such high tumor shrinkage that the tumor was unmeasurable (Figure 5A). The tumor size at the endpoint was smaller than 50 mm3 (Figure 5A,B). Tumor growth did not differ significantly between groups (Figure 5A).

In vivo evaluation of 225Ac‐labeled DOTA‐, DOTAGA‐, and DO3A‐OTSA101. (A) Tumor growth curves. Colored lines indicate the means. (B) Tumor images at day 70. (C) Body weight changes after treatment with 225Ac‐labeled DOTA‐, DOTAGA‐, and DO3A‐OTSA101. The colored lines indicate the means. When the tumor volume reached more than 800 mm3 and body weight loss was greater than 20% compared with that at day 0, the mouse was killed by isoflurane inhalation.

Adverse effects of treatment were evaluated by body weight change and histologic analysis of the bone marrow, liver, spleen, and kidney. Temporary body weight loss was observed in all groups after treatment with the 225Ac‐labeled antibodies but recovered thereafter (Figure 5C). In all three treatment groups, dilation of the blood vessels was transiently observed in the bone marrow on day 3 and did not differ from normal bone marrow on day 7 (Figure 6). No obvious damage from all any of the 225Ac‐labeled OTSA101 treatments was observed in the liver, spleen, and kidneys of the mice (Figure S1).

H&E‐stained sections of bone marrow at days 4 and 7 after injection with 225Ac‐labeled DOTA‐, DOTAGA‐, and DO3A‐OTSA101. Scale bar, 100 μm.

Histologic analysis of tumors

In all three treatment groups, differences were observed in the morphologic and apoptotic changes between days 4 and 7. In the tumors at 4 days after treatment, a mixture of necrotic and tumor cell areas was observed (Figure 7). At day 7, although several tumor cell areas remained, most areas were replaced by fibrous tissues, resulting in decreased tumor cellularity (Figure 7). The numbers of proliferating tumor cells were lower than those in untreated tumors at day 4 and further decreased at day 7 (Figure 8). Apoptotic cells were observed at day 4 in tumors treated with all 225Ac‐labeled OTSA101s but not at day 7 (Figure 9). The histologic findings did not differ significantly between tumors treated with the three 225Ac‐labeled OTSA101s.

DISCUSSION

Our findings based on a direct comparison of the in vitro and in vivo properties of three bifunctional chelates, DOTA, DOTAGA, and DO3A, revealed that DOTAGA was most suitable for 225Ac‐labeling of the anti‐FZD10 antibody OTSA101. Although the three chelate‐conjugated OTSA101s exhibited similar characteristics, they differed significantly in their binding affinity, labeling efficiency, tumor‐absorbed dose, and tumor‐to‐bone marrow ratio. DOTAGA conjugation had the smallest impact on the binding affinity of OTSA101 (p < 0.01). The labeling efficiencies of DOTAGA‐OTSA101 and DO3A‐OTSA101 were higher than those of DOTA‐OTSA101 (p < 0.01). The tumor‐absorbed doses from 225Ac‐labeled DOTA‐OTSA101 and DOTAGA‐OTSA101 were significantly higher than those of DO3A‐OTSA101 (p < 0.05). In addition, 225Ac‐DOTAGA‐OTSA101 showed the highest tumor‐to‐bone marrow ratio, with bone marrow being the dose‐limiting tissue. Therefore, DOTAGA is the most promising chelate to produce 225Ac‐labeled OTSA101 with high binding affinity and high radiochemical yields and to provide high absorbed doses to tumors and limited absorbed doses to bone marrow.

The in vitro assays showed that all three different chelate‐conjugated antibodies had similar properties, except for the binding affinity and labeling efficiency. The binding affinity of DOTAGA‐OTSA101 was significantly higher than that of DOTA‐OTSA101 and DO3A‐OTSA101 (p < 0.01). Chelate conjugation would affect the binding ability of antibodies depending on the chelate‐introduced sites and spatial conformation. 23 , 24 The immunoreactivity of a DOTAGA‐conjugate is reported to differ slightly from that of a DOTA‐conjugate by a difference in the spatial conformation due to the glutaric acid pendant arm in DOTAGA. 24 This could account for the minimal effects on the binding affinity of DOTAGA‐OTSA101.

Additionally, the labeling efficiencies of DOTAGA‐OTSA101 and DO3A‐OTSA101 were 1.8‐fold higher than that of DOTA‐OTSA101 (p < 0.01). These differences might be attributed to the increased kinetic fluctuation resulting from the substitution of glutaric acid in DOTAGA and carboxamide in DO3A for a carboxylic acid in DOTA. Moreover, all three 225Ac‐labeled antibodies showed similar stability in murine serum at 37°C for 7 days. Although concerns have been raised regarding the effect of the glutaric acid pendant arm of DOTAGA on the stability of antibody complexes, 24 this was not observed for OTSA101. Taken together with the immunoreactivity, we found that the in vitro properties of DOTAGA are most suitable for 225Ac labeling of OTSA101.

Several differences in the biodistribution of the 225Ac‐labeled OTSA101s were observed, likely due to differences in blood clearance. Faster clearance of 225Ac‐DO3A‐OTSA101 would lead to lower uptake in tumors and normal tissues compared with 225Ac‐labeled DOTA‐OTSA101 and DOTAGA‐OTSA101. Faster clearance could be caused by differences in the charge of the chelate conjugates, like those observed for 111In‐ and 64Cu‐labeled biomolecules. 13 , 24 , 25

Higher tumor‐absorbed doses suggest that DOTA and DOTAGA are appropriate for 225Ac‐labeled OTSA101. The dosimetric study showed the BED absorbed by tumors: 27.3 Bd for 225Ac‐DOTA‐OTSA101, 28.7 Bd for 225Ac‐DOTAGA‐OTSA101, and 20.6 Bd for 225Ac‐DO3A‐OTSA101, indicating higher antitumor effects of 225Ac‐DOTA‐OTSA101 and 225Ac‐DOTAGA‐OTSA101 than of 225Ac‐DO3A‐OTSA101. Unexpectedly, only slight differences were observed in the tumor growth curves and histologic findings between them. A previous study reported that 225Ac‐labeled OTSA101 achieved a complete response in 60% of the same tumor model mice when the BED was >20 Bd. 6 The current doses likely induced saturated effects in SYO‐1 tumors. Higher absorbed doses are expected to provide a better outcome in refractory tumor treatments; therefore, DOTA and DOTAGA would be more suitable than DO3A in clinical settings.

Considering absorbed doses to bone marrow, DOTAGA is the best chelate for conjugation with OTSA101. Bone marrow is generally the dose‐limiting tissue in radioimmunotherapy, 1 which is consistent with our dosimetric findings. Therefore, it is important to reduce the absorbed dose to bone marrow. The tumor‐to‐bone marrow ratio of 225Ac‐DOTAGA‐OTSA101 at 4.8 was higher than that of the other conjugates, 4.0 for 225Ac‐DOTA‐OTSA101 and 3.8 for 225Ac‐DO3A‐OTSA101. This suggests DOTAGA‐OTSA101 has the potential to deliver 225Ac to tumors, with fewer effects on bone marrow than DOTA‐OTSA101 and DO3A‐OTSA101.

Together, these findings indicate that DOTAGA is the most suitable chelate for 225Ac‐labeling of OTSA101. DOTAGA has been used in clinical studies (i.e., an anti‐GD2 antibody labeled with 64Cu 26 and PSMA‐targeting agents with 64Cu, 177Lu, and 225Ac). 15 , 27 , 28 , 29 These clinical studies suggest that DOTAGA‐conjugated OTSA101 has the potential for clinical use, and further translational studies to conduct clinical studies of 225Ac‐labeled OTSA101 with DOTAGA are encouraged.

Three limitations of the present study must be noted. First, there is a lack of a stability test of 225Ac‐labeled antibodies beyond day 7 in mice. The present biodistribution studies showed increased uptake in the liver on day 7 compared with day 4. It could be caused by free Ac‐225 from the radiolabeled antibodies. It is necessary to evaluate the longer‐term in vivo stability of 225Ac‐labeled OTSA101 considering the half‐life of 10 days for 225Ac before clinical studies. Second, the dosimetry studies estimating absorbed doses to bone marrow and the histological studies for bone marrow were conducted to predict hematological toxicity because it is mainly caused by radiation that damages bone marrow. 30 However, such analyses in bone marrow are not always accurate in predicting hematological toxicity. Further blood count tests should provide additional information to select the most suitable chelate for clinical studies. Third, only one radioactive dose of 225Ac‐labeled antibodies was used to evaluate the therapeutic effects. There are slight differences in the therapeutic effects, although the tumor‐absorbed doses were different. Studies with a lower dose are necessary to further assess dose dependence.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that DOTAGA is the most promising bifunctional chelator for 225Ac labeling of the anti‐FZD10 antibody OTSA101. DOTAGA is conjugated to the antibodies with minimal effects on antibody–antigen binding. The DOTAGA conjugates produce 225Ac‐DOTAGA‐OTSA101 with high radiochemical yields. The highest tumor‐to‐bone marrow ratio among the three evaluated conjugates means that 225Ac‐DOTAGA‐OTSA101 deposits high energy in tumors with limited damage to bone marrow. These findings encourage further translational studies toward clinical trials to evaluate the potential of 225Ac‐DOTAGA‐OTSA101 in patients with synovial sarcoma, an intractable tumor. FZD10 is expressed not only in synovial sarcoma but also in other tumors, including colorectal cancer. 31 Therefore, our alpha‐emitting agent could be applied to another refractory FZD10‐positive malignancy.
