Seismic loss assessment of direct-DBD platform-type cross-laminated timber shear wall systems using FEMA P-58 methodology
Authors: Hamed Dadkhah, Cristiano Loss
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn efficient design method should provide practitioners with a means for sizing timber buildings to meet specific performance levels against estimated earthquake intensities. Displacement and energy design considerations in force-based design (FBD) procedures are not as precise as intended in complex systems, such as mid- to high-rise timber buildings. The main aim of this study is to tailor the direct displacement-based design (D-DBD) classical framework to platform-type cross-laminated timber (CLT) shear wall structural systems and validate their performance for low-rise to high-rise timber mixed-use buildings. A comparison with results obtained via the FBD analyses is also provided. To this end, timber buildings with heights of 4, 8 and 12 stories are designed via the D-DBD and FBD methods. The seismic performance of platform-type CLT wall buildings is assessed in terms of the repair cost, repair time and casualty rate using FEMA P-58 methodology. The seismic response of CLT shear walls shows that the FBD method may lead to an expensive overdesign, especially in high-rise platform-type CLT walls. Conversely, the D-DBD method develops structural systems which can sustain a comparable level of damage from low- to high-rise platform-type CLT walls. Although the seismic loss assessment of buildings shows slightly better performance for the FBD method than the D-DBD method, it is worth noting that the D-DBD method does not lead to an unsafe building. Consequently, the D-DBD method sounds like a proper alternative approach for designing the CLT shear walls to achieve target performance levels without requiring a premium upfront cost.Keywords: Cross-laminated timber, Direct displacement-based design, FEMA P58, Force-based design, Fragility analysis, Seismic repair cost

Introduction

Engineered structural wood has received widespread attention in recent decades due to its unique features, such as performing as a carbon sink when used in wood-based construction. Consequently, the acceptance of timber-based projects has grown year after year (Natural Resources Canada 2021; WoodWorks 2022). On the other hand, the demand for high-rise buildings is inevitable due to population increase, urbanization and soil erosion. Design practice for high-rise timber buildings has not yet been harmonized. As a result, many building codes have generally limited timber structures to low-rise or mid-rise applications. In the latest NBCC (2020), for example, the height limit is set to 30 m for buildings with cross-laminated timber (CLT) shear walls. Furthermore, timber building typologies are limited by the nature of simplified seismic design frameworks, such as force-based design procedures. Hence, there is a need for seismic design procedures to be developed based on the new generation of performance-based seismic design methodologies to apply to a wide range of wood-based lateral resisting systems, from low-rise to high-rise buildings.

CLT is the most popular product for mass timber construction. It comprises face-glued cross-layer timber lamellas made of different wood species and strength grades lumber. Because of this orthogonal arrangement, CLT panels perform with enhanced load-carry capacity compared to conventional solid-sawn timber products. Assembly methods of CLT lateral resisting systems can be platform-type (Reynolds et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2021) or balloon-type (Porcu et al. 2018; Pan et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023; Xing et al. 2023). However, current design codes, such as NBCC (2020), lack design provisions and specifications for detailing balloon-type CLT shear walls. Platform-type CLT shear walls are shown to be one of the most effective wood-based lateral resisting systems for low-rise residential construction. Its seismic and lateral performance have been assessed thoroughly over the last decade. Ceccotti et al. (2013) conducted shaking table tests of a full-scale seven-story platform-type CLT building. Shahnewaz et al. (2019) proposed formulas to estimate the in-plane resistance of platform-type CLT shear walls. Sun et al. (2019) compared the seismic performances of conventional CLT shear walls and post-tensioned CLT shear walls. Shahnewaz et al. (2020) analysed the seismic fragility of a six-story platform-type CLT building. Khajehpour et al. (2022) studied the performance of multi-story platform-type CLT shear walls with openings. Masroor et al. (2020) evaluated the effect of the bi-axial behaviour of mechanical anchors on the lateral response of CLT shear walls. Polastri et al. (2019) assessed the seismic performance of multi-story timber buildings having CLT cores and perimeter shear walls as lateral resisting systems.

Many studies have focused on developing design methods for CLT shear walls. Shahnewaz et al. (2017) proposed a capacity-based design scheme for platform-type CLT walls such that the ductile connections dissipate the seismic energy while all other connections and CLT panels are designed with an over-strength factor to remain elastic. Casagrande et al. (2019) proposed an analytical expression for the capacity-based design of platform-type CLT walls. In coherence with the Force-Based Seismic Design (FBD) procedure (ASCE 7–10 2010; NBCC 2020), several studies have been conducted to develop the force modification factors tailored to mass timber buildings (Popovski et al. 2014; Pozza and Trutalli 2017). The reliability of FBD methods was questioned years ago by Priestley (1993) through a series of design examples of reinforced concrete buildings. Thenceforth, researchers’ and designers’ interests switched towards displacement- and energy-based seismic design methods. Specifically, researchers have focused on the development of alternative seismic procedures to accurately size buildings to meet specified performance levels under the estimated earthquake intensities. The performance-based seismic design (PBSD) framework has been generally tested against different structural systems and materials, except timber buildings, where only a few studies have been published, primarily focusing on platform-type CLT shear walls. Huang et al. (2021) developed a design method targeting the displacement of CLT walls by transforming the design problem into an optimization problem. Teweldebrhan and Tesfamariam (2022) developed a performance‐based seismic design workflow specifically for tall‐coupled CLT walls. Huang and Pei (2022) trained an artificial neural network to estimate the inter-story drift response of multi-story timber buildings for the performance‐based seismic design of CLT shear walls.

Displacement-based design (DBD) is one of the most well-known alternatives to the FBD method. DBD applies by attaining specified target deformation in structures under specified earthquake intensities. Despite being fully developed for concrete and steel construction, DBD of timber structures can only rely on a few studies, primarily focusing on platform-type CLT shear walls. As a further limitation, these studies focused only on low-rise timber buildings. Mergos and Beyer (2015) developed the N2-DBD method for symmetric single-story wood-frame buildings and proposed new behaviour factor–displacement ductility relationships. Fragiacomo et al. (2011) used the N2-DBD method for a four-story platform-type CLT wall. Hummel (2017) and Hummel and Seim (2019) proposed a modified version of the N2-DBD method for 2 to 4-story platform-type CLT shear walls. The N2-DBD method was first proposed by Fajfar and Gašperšič (1996) for assessing the seismic performance of existing or FBD-designed reinforced concrete buildings. In the purest sense of design procedure, methods like direct-DBD (D-DBD) and Modal-DBD can be considered explicit approaches by their step-by-step implemented procedures. A comprehensive review of these displacement-based methods is provided by Loss et al. (2018). Pang and Rosowsky (2009) first developed the Modal-DBD method for platform-type CLT walls. This design method needs access to the experimental dataset of CLT shear wall backbone curves. On the other hand, the D-DBD method is more well-known and robust than the Modal-DBD method, while this design method has not been previously developed for CLT shear walls. Therefore, this work studies the applicability and efficacy of the D-DBD method in designing CLT shear walls, targeting platform-type CLT buildings of different heights.

Despite the efficiency of D-DBD in attaining target deformation levels, the displacement is not best suited to gauging seismic damage, repair cost and causality rate. On the other hand, nonstructural elements comprise the majority of building costs (Taghavi and Miranda 2003) and their damage usually leads to costly repairs (Miranda et al. 2012). Nonstructural elements are generally velocity-sensitive and acceleration-sensitive components, while the D-DBD method only focuses on the drift response of buildings. Consequently, the effectiveness of D-DBD should be checked via the FEMA P-58 methodology (FEMA P-58,2012). FEMA P-58 is an important milestone in earthquake engineering to assess the structure performance based on the decision variables, such as repair cost, repair time and casualty rate. Current studies on the seismic loss assessment of CLT buildings are limited to the balloon-type CLT walls (Pei et al. 2019; Furley et al. 2021; Medel-Vera and Contreras 2021). For example, You et al. (2023) investigated economic losses, downtime, and resilience index of a 20-story balloon-type CLT wall according to the FEMA P-58 methodology. Wilson et al. (2021) evaluated the repair cost of a 5-story office building and a 12-story residential building. They concluded that the repair cost of low-rise CLT buildings is lower than that of high-rise CLT buildings at both the design and maximum considered earthquake intensities. To the best of these authors’ knowledge, no studies have been published focusing on the seismic loss assessment of conventional platform-type CLT via FEMA-P58.

The main objective of this study was to tailor the D-DBD classical framework to platform-type CLT wall lateral resisting systems and validate its performance in terms of seismic losses against low-rise to high-rise timber mixed-use buildings. Within the performance-based seismic design umbrella, a comparison with results obtained via the FBD analyses is also provided. Such comparison has been shown for other structural systems (Sullivan 2013; Sharma et al. 2020; Shakeri and Dadkhah 2022), whereas no studies have been conducted for CLT shear wall structures. Regardless of the design method above, this study specifically aimed to assess the repair costs, repair time and casualty rate of platform-type CLT wall buildings using FEMA P-58 methodology. The seismic evaluation focused on identifying the most vulnerable elements regarding the repair costs and the likelihood of casualties for platform-type CLT buildings prone to seismic hazards.

Archetype buildings

Building design overview and structural system

Three archetype mixed-use buildings with different heights are designed using the D-DBD and FBD methods. For their 4, 8 and 12-story lateral resisting system, platform-type CLT shear walls and their sub-assemblies are sized accordingly. The main floor plan of the buildings is shown in Fig. 1a. The gravity and lateral resisting systems work independently to resist the gravity and seismic loads, respectively. In other words, simply supported glulam beams on columns are used to withstand the gravity loads. Platform-type CLT shear walls and diaphragms then provide the lateral resistance. This work only reports details and specifications for the CLT shear walls. The number and location of CLT walls in Fig. 1a are for illustration purposes only and change with varying base shear demand. A 3D view of a 4-story timber building is shown in Fig. 1b. The inter-story height is set to 4 m, apart from the first story, where the height is set to 4.6 m. The calculated seismic mass, including dead loads of floors (1 kPa), the external cladding (1.2 kPa), the partition load (0.5 kPa), and 25% of the specified snow load (1.6 kPa) are 319 and 290 tons at the top and first floors, respectively, whereas 284 ton in the other stories.

Archetype buildings: a typical plan, b 3D view of 4-story building, c CLT shear wall, d angle bracket and e single-surface spline joint

Figure 1c shows a two-dimensional model of a 4-story platform-type CLT shear wall. CLT wall systems are conceived with standardized segments anchored at their base with hold-down connections and shear bracket connectors deemed to resist the seismic uplift and shear forces at each story, respectively. Aiming at providing high uplift load-carrying capacity, conventional hold-down connections (with screws or nails) are not considered in this study. From the list of available high-capacity hold-down technologies (Zhang et al. 2018; Shulman and Loss 2023), buckling-restrained brace-type (BRB) hold-down connections are used here to protect CLT walls against seismic uplift forces. BRB hold-downs are designed as dissipative connectors, while the seismic uplift forces under the design earthquake intensity are taken against their yield strength. CLT shear walls are connected to the floors using angle brackets shown in Fig. 1d. In this case study, BMF 90 × 116 × 48 × 3 mm with eleven 4 × 60 mm Anker annular ring nails is used per connection. Observed behaviour and actual capacity modelled with Pinching4 elements are available from Gavric et al. (2015). The number of angle brackets is determined according to its maximum shear capacity, set at 27.4 kN. Single-surface spline (SSS) joints are used to connect the CLT segments, as shown in Fig. 1e. Panel edges have been profiled to accommodate the laminated veneer lumber (LVL) strips. The width and thickness of the LVL strips are 180 and 28 mm, respectively. A double row of HBS 8 × 80 mm screws is used for fastening the strips on site. Its hysteresis behaviour based on the Pinching4 model is available from Gavric et al. (2015).

A 2.5% inter-story drift ratio is used as the design objective to check the life safety performance level of buildings under seismic hazard, which has a probability of occurrence of 2% in 50 years. Assuming archetype buildings located in Vancouver, Canada, with a site class D, Fig. 2 shows the elastic and design response spectrum, with design spectral accelerations matching the estimated fundamental period of the buildings. These spectral accelerations are derived using log–log interpolation, as recommended in the NBCC (2020). The fundamental periods have also been depicted in this figure for the archetype buildings, which will be designed via the D-DBD method in the next sections.

FBD of CLT shear walls

Seismic design provisions and system specification and connection detailing for FBD of CLT buildings are taken from the NBCC (2020) and (CSA 086–19 2019). Building typology is referred to as normal use. Therefore, an earthquake importance factor (IE) value 1 is set as the importance category. In accordance with NBCC (2020), a ductility-related force modification factor (Rd) of 2 and an over-strength-related force modification factor (Ro) of 1.5 are taken for moderately ductile CLT shear walls. In sizing the wall segments, CLT panels are designed according to capacity-based design principles such that the likelihood of brittle failure in wood is negligible. To achieve this end, the design section forces are determined using the modification factors of Rd × Ro = 1.3 (CSA 086–19 2019; FPInnovations 2019). In terms of hold-down connections and shear brackets, the design force is also determined by applying an over-strength factor of 1.2 to the uplift force and shear force. Platform-type CLT shear walls are designed such that their aspect ratio is between 2:1 and 4:1 per (CSA 086–19 2019). Specifically, CLT walls are built by assembling three subsegments of length equal to 2.04 m. The current limit on the height of the CLT buildings of the NBCC (2020) wall is 30 m. As a result, some design considerations will be added when analysing the results of the FBD method for the 12-story building. Upon finding the design base shear force in accordance with the force modification factors, each CLT panel and its connections are designed using the same formulas per design steps 11 to 15 reported in Sect. 2.3.

D-DBD of CLT shear walls

In this study, the D-DBD method explicitly considers inter-story drift as the design parameter and checks its target values under a code-specified seismic intensity level. Such a D-DBD framework is derived from Priestley (1993) approach and relies on the substitute structure method proposed by Shibata and Sozen (1976). The energy dissipation capacity of the system, including both elastic and hysteresis behaviours, is simulated by an equivalent viscous damping ratio. Compared to the D-DBD of reinforced concrete shear wall systems, the D-DBD framework design steps are tailored to platform-type CLT walls of timber structures from low-rise to high-rise buildings. Ground motions selection

Table 2 provides the list of the thirty-two earthquake records used in this study. These records were selected from the PEER Ground Motion Database to suit the seismic hazard in Vancouver, Canada. It can be found from the rupture distance of these records that a set of far-field and near-field earthquakes has been chosen in order to assess the seismic performance of the buildings under a panoply of earthquakes. These records are also related to a site class D in accordance with shear-wave velocity in the top 30 m of geomaterial, which ranges from 180 m/s to 360 m/s. Earthquake records are scaled according to the ASCE 7–10 (2010). This scale method assumes that the average response spectra of earthquake records do not fall below the design spectrum in a period interval from 0.2T to 1.5T where T is the fundamental period of building. However, ASCE 7–10 (2010) does not provide full instructions on the method to obtain the best suite of scale factors. Therefore, the scaling procedure by Reyes et al. (2021) is used to resize the earthquake record features. Accordingly, SF1 and SF2 scale factors are applied to each record. SF1 is individual for each record and is obtained by minimizing the difference between response and design spectra. SF2 is the same for the set of earthquake records to make the average response spectra above the design spectrum. For example, the scale factors of earthquake records are presented in Table 2 for nonlinear time-history analysis of the 12-story CLT wall building. The response spectra of these scaled records are shown in Fig. 7a. It is shown that the average response spectrum fits well with the elastic design spectrum, precisely in the range 0.2T = 0.61 s to 1.5T = 4.57 s.

Seismic fragility analysis of platform-type CLT buildings

The D-DBD method was developed to achieve a target drift under a specific level of seismic intensity. In order to be able to make a reasonable comparison between this design approach and the FBD method, the archetype buildings are designed to meet a value of 2.5% as the target inter-story drift ratio under a seismic hazard having a probability of occurrence of 2% in 50 years. Alarmingly, the designed buildings may experience different seismic intensity levels during its lifetime. Therefore, there is a need to assess their seismic performance based on a probabilistic approach, considering all earthquake intensities that could occur over a specified period. This section explains how the seismic fragility analysis of archetype buildings is performed. Fragility curves of the platform-type CLT buildings are developed via a Monte Carlo simulation. To this end, incremental dynamic analyses are performed, with Sa (T1) from 0.1 to 5 g used as the ground motion intensity measure (IM). Thirty-two earthquake records are scaled to these IMs, and the peak inter-story drift ratio is monitored as an engineering demand parameter (EDP). Nsim is the total number of simulations, whereas Nfail is the number of simulations where the demand exceeds the limit state (LS) capacity. In this work, the probabilistic performance assessment of CLT buildings is performed for two different limit states. LS-1 corresponds to the design objective of CLT walls, targeting a 2.5% inter-story drift ratio. LS-2 corresponds to the probability of collapse of CLT walls. It should be noted that there is no code-based evaluation criterion for the collapse of CLT walls and different limit states can be found in the literature (Jafari et al. 2022). Tesfamariam et al. (2021) have considered 2.5% as the collapse limit drift ratio. Kovacs and Wiebe (2019) defined the drift ratio of 10% as the collapse criterion. Based on CLT shear wall test results, van de Lindt et al. (2020) recommended a 4.5% and 5.5% inter-story drift ratio for the collapse evaluation of low aspect ratio panels and high aspect ratio panels, respectively. In this case study, the inter-story drift collapse ratio is set at 5.5%.

It is worth noting that the seismic fragility analysis is performed in line with the FEMA P-58 methodology, so the earthquake uncertainty has been taken into account and the uncertainties related to the structural and modelling parameters are applied indirectly in the process of seismic loss assessment with a dispersion factor of β depending on the modelling accuracy and construction quality. Based on FEMA P-58’s recommendations, a dispersion of 0.47 is taken for EDPs. If a research project is mainly focused on the seismic fragility analysis of CLT shear walls, it is important to take all possible uncertainties, including the structural and modelling parameters into account. For example, Aloisio et al. (2022) assessed the seismic fragility curve of a light-timber frame shear wall by considering the variabilities of pinching parameters as well as earthquake intensity.

Since the D-DBD and FBD of the CLT buildings provided systems with different members’ sizes and connection detailing, their fundamental periods are not comparable. As a result, with unmatched IM and vertical axes of fragility curves, it is unreasonable to compare the building’s seismic performance based on the fragility curves. This study compares their seismic performance based on risk-based assessment parameters, such as the annual frequency of exceedance, λc. The time-based assessment combines the results of several intensity-based assessments by weighting the outcome from each intensity based on its probability. More details about the assessment steps can be found in FEMA P-58, while only a brief summary is presented here. In the first step, a set of 11 earthquake records is scalded to the target spectral acceleration of Table 3. It is worth noting that the FEMA P-58 methodology recommends using 7 to 11 records of properly scaled motions in order to provide a reasonable estimate of median response. Because of the conservative view of FEMA P-58 in terms of earthquake variability, the dispersion of seismic responses must be determined based on the modelling accuracy and construction quality and not on the response of 11 earthquake records. The EDPs, such as inter-story drift ratio, acceleration, velocity and residual drift, are obtained by performing the nonlinear dynamic analysis. The average value of EDPs under 11 earthquake records is taken as the mean EDP, while a dispersion of 0.47 is taken for EDPs based on FEMA P-58’s recommendations. Nonlinear analysis is also performed under the earthquake records of Table 2 from No. 1 to 11, and the obtained EDPs are given as input data in PACT software (FEMA P-58,2012). Assuming that the obtained EDPs are multivariate lognormal (Yang et al. 2009), several EDPs are generated and the Monte Carlo simulation is performed to assess the seismic loss for each realization of EDPs. This study takes a value of 1000 as the number of realizations. For each realization, the building collapse is first checked based on the collapse fragility curve. If failure occurs, the repair costs and time will equal the replacement cost and time, respectively. The casualties are also determined based on the collapse modes of buildings that are given as input data in PACT software (FEMA P-58,2012).

If the integrity of the building is not compromised, it is possible to determine the repairability of the building in function of the residual drift. FEMA P-58 recommends a lognormal distribution fragility function with a 1% median residual drift value and 0.3 dispersion. If the building is deemed irreparable, the replacement values are taken as repair time and repair cost. For reparable realizations, the damage states of structural and nonstructural components are first determined based on the simulated EDPs. The consequence of damage states and the seismic loss of components, including repair costs and repair time, are then determined using the FEMA P-58 database. Many owners intend to replace buildings when the repair cost exceeds a specific level, defined as the total loss threshold. Different values can be found for the total loss threshold in the literature. For example, FEMA P-58 suggests that 50% of the replacement cost is a reasonable total loss threshold. In this study, 60% of the replacement cost is defined as the total loss threshold.

Causalities and population model

The casualties are determined based on a population model accounting for the number of people occupying the building at different times and days of the year. In the Monte Carlo simulation, these times and days are assumed randomly for each realization. If a realization leads to the building collapse, the casualties are determined in accordance with the number of people at this time and this day. With the increase in the seismic intensity, the repair cost of these components increases, as shown in Fig. 16b, but the peak repair cost is due to the components with fragility No. C3032.001a (suspended ceiling) and B1071.012 (CLT shear walls). Time-based loss assessment of CLT shear walls

A building may experience different seismic intensity levels during its lifetime, so its performance must be evaluated by considering all earthquake intensities that could occur over a specified period. In this section, the results of the time-based assessment are provided for the CLT shear walls, considering the seismic intensities of Table 3. The results of the time-based assessment are presented in Fig. 17. It is shown that the seismic performance is almost the same for the 4-story building, regardless of the design method. Figure 17d and e indicate that the seismic performance of buildings designed using the FBD method is slightly better than that using the D-DBD method. For example, the annual exceedance probability of $1 million repair cost is 0.00138 for the 12-story building designed with the D-DBD method (see Fig. 17g). In contrast, it is 0.000995 when designed via the FBD procedure. This means a 28% reduction when the 12-story building is designed with the FBD method instead of the D-DBD method. The same results can be found in terms of repair time and fatality. For instance, the annual exceedance probability of 200 deaths is 0.00021 for the 8-story building designed with the D-DBD method (see Fig. 17f), whereas 0.00012 when designed via the FBD method. However, it should be noted that the cost of construction of buildings is also different between the two design methods, and the FBD method has been shown to lead to a premium-cost design in terms of wall and connection detailing. As a future investigation, it is proposed to make a comparison between the DDBD and FBD methods in such a way that the total costs, including the construction cost and the repair cost, are incorporated into account. It is worth noting also that the D-DBD method does not lead to an unsafe building. For instance, the exceedance probability in 50 years corresponding to the annual exceedance probability of 0.00021 is equal to 1.04%, and its value is insignificant. Consequently, the D-DBD method sounds like a proper alternative approach for designing the CLT shear walls to achieve target performance levels without requiring a premium upfront construction cost.

Conclusions

The main objective of this study was to tailor the D-DBD classical framework to platform-type CLT wall lateral resisting systems and validate its performance in terms of seismic losses using the FEMA P-58 methodology. 4, 8 and 12 stories archetypes were designed with the D-DBD assuming life safety performance level as the design objective under a seismic hazard with a probability of occurrence of 2% in 50 years. After validating the effectiveness of the D-DBD method in achieving the design objective under thirty-two earthquake records, fragility analyses of CLT shear walls were performed. Per FEMA P-58 methodology, the seismic performance of CLT shear walls was assessed in terms of repair cost, repair time and casualty rate. Additionally, a comparison with results obtained via the FBD analyses is also provided. The following conclusions can be drawn:

The FBD approach can be regarded as an effective design method for low-rise CLT wall building systems, as demonstrated by the 4-story CLT shear wall system comparing the attained and design objective under the design level of seismic intensity. However, there is a significant discrepancy between the peak drift ratio and the target drift as the number of stories increases. Consequently, the FBD method may result in an expensive overdesign for high-rise platform-type CLT wall structures
