AgNW emulsion inks for direct printing of high-resolution and stretchable electrodes with semi-embedded network structure
Authors: Xiaoqian Mi, Lixue Liu, Shujia Yang, Peiqi Wu, Weiqing Zhan, Xinyi Ji, Jiajie Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Achieving directly printed patterns that are conductive, stretchable, and high-resolution is critical for the scalable manufacturing of wearable electronics, but the competing demands of these properties make it an unresolved challenge. Here, we present a one-step fabrication strategy that overcomes these conflicts using a rationally designed silver nanowire (AgNW)/emulsion ink. The thixotropic AgNW-based ink comprises trace hyperbranched molecules as stabilizers and an aqueous polyacrylic emulsion, enabling direct screen-printing of patterns with a fine resolution of 30 µm via a critical stepwise solvent-drying process. This process first allows AgNWs to settle into a percolating conductive network, then triggers the polyacrylic emulsion particles to coalesce and fuse into an elastomeric matrix that semi-embeds the nanowire junctions, thereby establishing an effective mechanism for dissipating strain. The resulting composite electrodes achieve an initial conductivity of up to 1.6×104 S cm−1, remarkable stretchability exceeding 500% strain, and stable electromechanical performance under stretching (R/R0 = 2.3 at 100% strain and 6.6 at 200% strain) and over 10000 stretching cycles under 40% strain. Utilizing relatively environmentally friendly solvents, our work provides a scalable, post-treatment-free platform for fabricating high-performance stretchable electrode materials for wearable electronics.

Introduction

Wearable electronics have emerged as a transformative platform for seamless human-machine integration and personalized health monitoring, profoundly shaping the future of digital life1–5. The advancement of wearable electronics critically depends on the development of stretchable electrode materials, which directly govern the electromechanical robustness and functional integrity of wearable devices under dynamic deformation6–10. Printing technologies offer a promising, scalable and cost-effective fabrication pathway for wearable electronics due to their compatibility with flexible or stretchable substrates11–15. Consequently, extensive research has focused on developing printable electrode materials that simultaneously achieve high conductivity and mechanical compliance16–20. However, integrating these often-conflicting properties into a single material system for wearable electronics remains a formidable challenge.

While liquid metals and their composites have been widely explored as printable and stretchable conductors, their propensity for leakage significantly limits practical application21–23. In contrast, silver nanowires (AgNWs) offer a promising alternative due to their high intrinsic conductivity, solution processability, and ability to form compliant conductive networks24–28. Nevertheless, concurrently optimizing conductivity, stretchability, and printability in AgNW electrodes presents a fundamental design conflict. This is because three key requirements are often mutually exclusive: (1) forming a densely interconnected nanowire network for high conductivity inherently creates a brittle structure with limited strain tolerance29,30; (2) incorporating AgNWs in an elastomer matrix to impart stretchability often compromises electrical connectivity by physically separating the nanowires30,31; and (3) stabilizing AgNW inks with polymeric additives for printing inevitably impedes inter-nanowire contacts, while their subsequent removal process could damage the stretchable substrates or electronic components32–35. Thus, developing high-performance, printable, and stretchable AgNW-based materials remains a significant challenge.

To address these trade-offs in fabricating stretchable or printable AgNW electrodes, various strategies have been explored36,37. Our prior work involved a multi-step process of printing dispersant-containing AgNW ink into high-resolution patterns, washing the dispersant to recover conductivity, and embedding AgNW patterns in elastomer matrix to achieve stretchability30. This sequential methodology is, however, inherently complex and poorly suited for practical scale-up. We subsequently developed AgNW inks stabilized by trace hyperbranched molecules (HBMs) in our recent work25, enabling the direct printing of high-resolution, conductive patterns without post-treatment to remove additives25. However, such AgNW electrodes remained flexible but not intrinsically stretchable. Meanwhile, solution-based phase-separation methods have been developed to obtain high conductivity and stretchability in AgNW/elastomer composites but suffer from poor pattern resolution and reliance on toxic solvents38–40. To date, the direct printing of AgNW inks into high-resolution patterns that are simultaneously highly conductive, intrinsically stretchable, and free of complex post-treatments remains an unmet goal.

Herein, we present a one-step fabrication strategy to overcome these design conflicts. We formulated a stable AgNW emulsion ink comprising trace HBMs as stabilizers, isopropanol/water as the solvent, and vital polyacrylic emulsions as the elastomeric matrix. This thixotropic gel-like ink can be directly screen-printed into stretchable, high-conductivity patterns, which are solidified via a controlled stepwise solvent-drying process without requiring complex and harsh post-treatments. After printing, slow evaporation at room temperature (RT) first allows AgNWs to settle and form a percolating conductive network, ensuring high initial conductivity. Subsequent heating accelerates evaporation, triggering the emulsion to demulsify and the polymer particles coalesce around the junctions of AgNW network. Ultimately, driven by capillary forces, the polymer particles fuse into a stretchable matrix that semi-embeds and encapsulates these AgNW junctions, providing an effective mechanism for strain dissipation. The resulting printed electrodes achieve a fine feature size of 30 μm, an initial conductivity of up to 1.6 × 104 S cm−1, and remarkable stretchability exceeding 500% strain. The stretchable electrodes demonstrate stable electrical performance under large deformation (R/R0 = 2.3 at 100% strain and 6.6 at 200% strain) and can withstand over 10,000 stretching cycles at 40% strain. Furthermore, this fabrication strategy utilizes water- and alcohol-based solvents, aligning with relatively environmentally friendly manufacturing principles for next-generation skin-interfaced and implantable bioelectronics.

Results

Design rationale of AnEm ink

We first formulated the AgNW ink for stretchable electrode employing amino-terminated hyperbranched polyamide (NHBM) as the key dispersants and stabilizer for AgNWs (Supplementary Fig. 1). The AgNWs, synthesized and purified as previously reported25, have an average length and diameter of 40 µm and 40 nm, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2). The NHBM features a three-dimensional branched architecture with 16 peripheral amino groups, allowing it to anchor onto AgNW surfaces through strong amino-silver coordination while sterically stabilizing the nanowires in isopropanol (IPA) (Supplementary Fig. 3)25. The resulting AgNW dispersion, prepared with an ultralow NHBM loading (AgNW: NHBM = 1:0.001 by weight), was then blended with an aqueous polyacrylic emulsion (particle size about 100 nm) to obtain the final printable AgNW emulsion ink, denoted as AnEm ink, where n and m represent the weight percentage (wt%) of AgNWs and polyacrylic emulsion particles, respectively. The polyacrylic emulsion was chosen for its intrinsic elasticity and strong interfacial adhesion to AgNWs, enabling it to serve as a stretchable matrix for the printed composite electrode.

To achieve both high conductivity and stretchability in the printed AnEm electrodes, we devised a stepwise solvent-drying process (Fig. 1a). Freshly printed AnEm patterns are first left under RT conditions for 30 min, allowing the water/IPA solvents to evaporate slowly. During this stage, AgNWs gradually settle and interdigitate under gravity, forming a percolating conductive network while the polyacrylic particles remain uniformly dispersed in the residual solvent (Fig. 1b). The patterns were then heated at 120 °C for 10 min to rapidly remove the remaining solvent. This heating step triggers the breakdown of the emulsion, causing the polyacrylic particles to coalesce and pack around the pre-formed AgNW network. Upon complete solvent removal, capillary forces and surface tension drive the polyacrylic particles to rupture and fuse into a continuous elastomeric network that tightly wraps and embeds junctions of the AgNW network. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) confirms that the printed composite exhibits a semi-embedded structure in which a portion of the AgNW junctions embedded within the polymer network, while the remaining nanowire segments remain exposed (Fig. 1c). The elemental mapping (C, N, and O) of the printed A9.5E0.25 electrode dried via a stepwise process of RT drying 30 min followed by 120 °C heating 10 min under 100% strain clearly showed that C, N, and O signals (characteristic of the polyacrylic) are highly concentrated around the AgNW junctions (Supplementary Fig. 12). This architecture enables the composite electrode to accommodate large mechanical deformations while retain high conductivity: the polymer-embedded junctions dissipate local stress and provide stretchability, while the unembedded AgNW segments adapt to structural changes due to the substantial mechanical mismatch, preserving interconnected conductive pathways (Fig. 1d)38,41. As a result, the printed AnEm electrodes maintain stable conductivity even under tensile strains of up to 500% (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 13).

Printability of AnEm ink

The dispersibility and stability of the AnEm inks, prerequisites for reliable application, were first examined by systematically varying the concentrations of AgNWs and polyacrylic emulsion. A stable formulation window was identified, with AgNW contents ranging from 0.7 to 10.5 wt% (5–90 mg mL−1) and polyacrylic emulsion from 0.15 to 0.75 wt% (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 4). These inks remained homogeneous without sedimentation for over 3 months. The rheological properties of the AnEm ink, crucial for printability, were characterized using a cone-plate rheometer. Four AnEm inks with AgNW concentrations of 2.5, 5, 9.5, and 10.5 wt% (at a fixed emulsion content of 0.25 wt%) all exhibited pronounced shear-thinning thixotropy, indicative of a dynamic nanowire network, allowing the AnEm inks to smoothly flow into the narrow apertures and fully fill the corners (Fig. 2b)42. The viscosity at a shear rate of 0.1 s−1 increased from 9 to 136 Pa s as the AgNW loading increased from 2.5 to 10.5 wt%. Notably, varying the emulsion content at a fixed AgNW concentration had little effect on viscosity (Supplementary Fig. 5), suggesting weak interactions between AgNWs and emulsion particles. This weak coupling is critical, as it allows AgNWs to settle and interconnect during the initial slow RT-drying step without interference from the emulsion, thereby facilitating the formation of a continuous conductive network. Furthermore, the rheological behavior of the AnEm inks during screen printing was further studied from a peak hold step (PHS) test consisting of holding the sample at different shear rates in three intervals, as shown in Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 1. Among these four inks, the A9.5E0.25 ink exhibited the most appropriate viscosity and rheological behavior for screen printing. The A9.5E0.25 ink had a viscosity of 94.5 Pa s at 0.1 s−1 at the time of 30 s, and 40.6, 83.1, 97.3, 99.1, and 99.6% of initial values were recovered in 1 s (at the time of 61 s), 5 s (at the time of 65 s), 10 s (at the time of 70 s), 30 s (at the time of 90 s) and 60 s (at the time of 120 s) after the shear rate reduced from 100 to 0.1 s−1. The hysteresis loop area is 26.8 Pa s−1, and the recovery time constant is 1.79 s (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table 1). This thixotropy allow for leveling of the A9.5E0.25 ink and uniform line formation. The viscoelastic response was further probed through oscillation strain sweeps. The storage modulus (G”) and loss modulus (G”) of AnEm inks with different AgNW loadings displayed two distinct regimes (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 5g–i). Figure 2d plots the loss tangent (tan δ = G”/G’) as a function of oscillation strain. The linear viscoelastic (LVE) region and is defined as the maximum deformation that can be applied to the ink without destroying its structure. In region I, both G’ and G” decreased with increasing oscillation strain, while G’ remained higher than G″, reflecting the progressive breakdown of the AgNW network while the ink retained a predominantly elastic character44,45. For the A9.5E0.25 ink, the LVE region extends to oscillation strains below 0.32%, which before the yield point, and the ratio of liquid‑like to solid‑like behavior (G”/G’) within this region is calculated to be approximately 0.26. The crossover point was reached for oscillation strain approximately 46.6%. (Figure 2d and Supplementary Table 1). Region II began at the crossover point where G’ = G” (tan δ = 1) and extended into the region where G” > G’, marking a transition from solid-like to liquid-like behavior, which is essential for ink flow during printing42,44,45.

The favorable rheological and viscoelastic properties, coupled a high viscosity, enables the AnEm ink to be readily patterned via printing techniques. Here, screen printing was selected to demonstrate its printability, owing to the method’s low cost, design versatility, operational simplicity, relatively high resolution, and minimal material waste46–48. As shown in Fig. 2e–h and Supplementary Fig. 7, the A9.5E0.25 ink can be directly screen-printed onto stretchable substrates, such as polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) and thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) to form various stretchable, complex circuits and patterns. Optical microscopy and SEM images (Fig. 2i–m and Supplementary Fig. 8) confirm that the printed features achieve a high resolution—including line width and spacing—of 30 μm, which approaches the practical limit of screen-printing technology49,50. Based on the measured resistances and the line geometries, the conductivities were calculated to be 4.47 ± 0.40 × 104, 3.83 ± 0.42 × 104, 3.26 ± 0.12 × 104, 2.86 ± 0.22 × 104, 2.48 ± 0.11 × 104, and 2.29 ± 0.07 × 104 S cm−1 for the line widths of 30, 50, 100, 150, 200, and 250 μm, respectively. It can be seen that the electrical conductivity decreases as the printed line width increases, which was attributed to the presence of voids in the AgNW patterns with larger line widths42. The printed lines also exhibit high uniformity (width ~30.9 ± 0.7 µm), smooth edges (line edge roughness ~0.62 µm) and high repeatability (the combination of optical integrity and electrical continuity yield statistics ~96%) (Fig. 2n and Supplementary Fig. 9), further attesting to the printability of the A9.5E0.25 ink.

Electromechanical performance of AnEm electrode

To optimize the conductivity and stretchability, we systematically compared the electromechanical performance of screen-printed AnEm electrodes under different drying protocols, varying the AgNW and emulsion concentrations. First, using a fixed-formulation ink (A9.5E0.25), we examined five solvent-removal conditions post-printing: (1) RT drying only, (2) direct heating only, (3) a stepwise process of RT drying 10 min followed by 120 °C heating 10 min, (4) a stepwise process of RT drying 20 min followed by 120 °C heating 10 min, and (5) a stepwise process of RT drying 30 min followed by 120 °C heating 10 min. As shown in Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 15, all five drying methods yielded A9.5E0.25 electrodes with initial conductivities exceeding 10,000 S cm−1 (Supplementary Table 3). However, electrodes dried solely at RT or solely with heat showed a rapid increase in resistance under strain and failed below 200% elongation (Fig. 3b). In contrast, the stepwise-dried (RT 30 min, heat 10 min) electrode exhibited an initial conductivity of 15,700 S cm−1 and maintained stable electrical performance under large deformation, with R/R0 values of 1.6, 2.3, 6.6, and 18.2 at 50%, 100%, 200%, and 400% strain, respectively (Fig. 3b). A comparison across the three drying conditions (RT drying only, direct heating only, and a stepwise process of RT drying 30 min followed by 120 °C heating 10 min) showed that both RT drying only and direct heat only resulted in partial peeling at the grid edges, corresponding to a 3B rating, whereas the stepwise drying condition yielded completely smooth cut edges with no peeling at all, achieving the highest adhesion strength for 5B rating (Supplementary Fig. 14). These results establish the stepwise drying protocol as essential for achieving concurrent high conductivity and stretchability. We attribute this to the distinct structural outcomes of each method: RT-only drying prevents the emulsion particles from fully fusing together and embedding the AgNW junctions, whereas rapid heating alone causes premature coagulation of the emulsion particles with AgNWs, leaving insufficient time for the polymer particles to coalesce and properly encapsulate the junctions of nanowire network, as evidenced by SEM images and discussed below (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Figs. 10–12).Fig. 3Electromechanical property of the AnEm electrode.a Electrical conductivities as a function of uniaxial tensile strain and b resistance changes as a function of uniaxial tensile strain for the printed A9.5E0.25 electrodes with three solvent-removal conditions: RT drying only (gray), a stepwise process of RT drying 30 min followed by heating 10 min (red), and direct heating only (blue). c Electrical conductivities as a function of uniaxial tensile strain and d resistance changes as a function of uniaxial tensile strain for the printed A9.5E0.15 (gray), A9.5E0.25 (red), and A9.5E0.5 electrodes (blue) with a stepwise process of RT drying 30 min followed by heating 10 min. e Electrical conductivities as a function of uniaxial tensile strain and f resistance changes as a function of uniaxial tensile strain for the printed A5E0.25 (gray), A9.5E0.25 (red), and A10.5E0.25 electrodes (blue) with a stepwise process of RT drying 30 min followed by heating 10 min. Using the optimal stepwise drying protocol of RT drying 30 min followed by 120 °C heating 10 min, we then sought the best-balanced composition of the AnEm ink by varying the emulsion and AgNW contents. Electrodes printed from AnEm inks with a fixed AgNW concentration (9.5 wt%) but different emulsion loadings (0.15, 0.25, and 0.5 wt%) were first evaluated (Fig. 3c, d). As anticipated, the A9.5E0.15 electrode, with the lowest polymer content, delivered the highest initial conductivity (33,000 S cm−1) but showed the most rapid degradation under strain and failed below 150% elongation. In contrast, both the A9.5E0.25 and A9.5E0.5 electrodes exhibited similarly stable, gradual resistance changes during stretching. However, the A9.5E0.25 electrode achieved a significantly higher initial conductivity (15,700 S cm−1) compared to the A9.5E0.5 electrode (6600 S cm−1), as the higher insulating polymer content in the latter introduces more barriers within the conductive AgNW network. Furthermore, we examined electrodes printed from inks with a fixed emulsion content (0.25 wt%) but different AgNW concentrations (5, 9.5, and 10.5 wt%). As expected, lower AgNW loading (A5E0.25) reduced the initial conductivity, whereas higher loading (A10.5E0.25) compromised stretchability (Fig. 3e, f, and Supplementary Table 3). The intermediate formulation A9.5E0.25 thus strikes the optimal balance, delivering both high conductivity and robust stretchability, and was therefore selected for all subsequent investigations.

The long-term cycling stability of the stepwise-dried A9.5E0.25 electrode was further assessed under repeated tensile loading. The electrode exhibited high durability, with minimal resistance change after extended cycling: R/R0 remained at 2.6, 2.1, 2.4, and 2.9 following 10,000, 5000, 3500, and 1000 cycles at 40%, 50%, 60%, and 100% strains, respectively (Fig. 3g, h, and Supplementary Fig. 16). For the first time, our A9.5E0.25 ink allows direct printing of high-resolution patterns that exhibit a specific combination of high conductivity, substantial stretchability, and robust cyclic endurance—eliminating the need for harsh post-treatment (Supplementary Table 4)40,41,51–63.

Enhancement mechanism

To understand the mechanism by which polyacrylic emulsion enhances electromechanical performance, we analyzed A9.5E0.25 electrodes stretched to 100% strain with five different drying treatments: (1) RT drying only, (2) direct heating only, (3) a stepwise process of RT drying 10 min followed by 120 °C heating 10 min, (4) a stepwise process of RT drying 20 min followed by 120 °C heating 10 min, and (5) a stepwise process of RT drying 30 min followed by 120 °C heating 10 min. As the RT drying time increased (from 10 to 30 min) before heating, the A9.5E0.25 electrode microstructure underwent two critical transitions. First, polymer-embedded junctions gradually replaced the polymer-attached AgNW structure (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 10). Second, the distribution of the polyacrylic extended from the top region throughout the entire thickness of the conductive layer until reaching the elastic substrate, thereby forming a rough, adhesive interface (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Specifically, SEM images show that only the AgNW network in the stepwise-dried (RT 10 min, heat 30 min) electrode remains largely continuous without obvious cracking at 100% strain (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 10). Higher-magnification SEM image reveals that the stepwise protocol of RT drying 30 min followed by 120 °C heating 10 min drives the emulsion particles to fuse into a continuous polymer matrix that wraps and embeds most AgNW junctions (Fig. 4b). For the A9.5E0.25 electrodes prepared by the stepwise drying process, the junction density at 100% strain increases from 24 to 32 µm−2 as the RT drying time increases from 10 to 30 min, resulting in more stable electromechanical performance. In cross-sectional SEM images, when the RT drying time was increased from 10 to 20 min, the main distribution zone of the polyacrylic in the cross-section shifted from the upper 1/2 to the upper 2/3 of the conductive layer, and the interface between the conductive layer and the substrate remained relatively smooth but free of large gap (Supplementary Fig. 11c, d). The A9.5E0.25 electrode prepared under a stepwise process of RT drying 30 min followed by 120 °C heating 10 min exhibited a uniform, through‑thickness polymer-embedded junctions within the conductive layer, with the polyacrylic in intimate contact with the substrate and forming a highly adhesive interface (Supplementary Fig. 11e). During stretching, these polymer-embedded junctions dissipate local stress, allowing the majority of nanowires to remain interconnected and maintain electrical percolation.

In contrast, A9.5E0.25 electrodes dried solely at RT or by heating alone develop extensive microcracks under identical strain (Fig. 4c, e). Higher-magnification SEM images reveal that in these two electrodes, the polyacrylic merely adheres to the AgNW surface without forming an embedded hybrid structure, and the junction densities of are 12 and 16 µm−2, respectively (Fig. 4d, f, and Supplementary Table 2). And a distinct gap is visible at the interface between the conductive layer and the elastic substrate, indicating poor interfacial adhesion (Supplementary Fig. 11a, b). Moreover, the polyacrylic is predominantly concentrated in the top one-third of the electrode in A9.5E0.25 electrodes dried by heating alone, indicating premature coagulation (Supplementary Fig. 11b). This polymer-attached structures leave the nanowire network mechanically vulnerable, unable to effectively dissipate stress, and consequently results in poor stretchability. Consequently, upon stretching, the AgNW junctions disconnect first, rapidly breaking conductive pathways and degrading conductivity. These results demonstrate that the stepwise drying process is critical for forming polymer-embedded nanowire junctions, which simultaneously enhance both the conductivity and stretchability of the printed A9.5E0.25 electrodes.

Application demonstration

Printed electrodes and circuits are essential components for stretchable or wearable electronics. Reliable interconnects that maintain stable conductance are essential to prevent device performance degradation resulting from circuit deformation, ensuring the stable operation of wearable electronics under diverse mechanical strain64–66. As a proof of concept, we fabricated stretchable light-emitting diode (LED) arrays (5 × 5 LEDs in Fig. 5a and 10 × 10 LEDs in Fig. 5b) on PDMS substrates by using the stretchable printed circuits to connect the LEDs. The circuits were produced by directly screen-printing A9.5E0.25 ink, followed by stepwise drying. We also developed a wearable electronic glove (E-glove) capable of real-time monitoring of environmental temperature and pressure. The E-glove integrates a tellurium nanowire-based pyroresistive temperature sensor and a PDMS/carbon black-based piezoresistive pressure sensor, connected via screen-printed A9.5E0.25 electrodes (Fig. 5c). A volunteer wearing the glove performed finger bending motions at varying angles, which stretched the printed electrodes. During these movements, the temperature and pressure sensors were subjected to simultaneous cyclic thermal (20–25 °C) and mechanical (0–0.5 N) stimuli, respectively. As shown in Fig. 5d, the relative resistance responses of both sensors remained highly stable, with negligible signal interference or performance drift, even as the A9.5E0.25 electrodes were stretched under different bending-induced strains. These findings confirm the robustness and practical applicability of the stretchable A9.5E0.25 electrodes for diverse wearable and printed electronics.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a one-step, post-treatment-free strategy for directly printing high-performance stretchable electrodes. This is achieved through a rationally designed AgNW emulsion ink and a stepwise solvent evaporation process. The ink’s formulation, comprising trace hyperbranched stabilizers and an aqueous polyacrylic emulsion, decouples the often-conflicting requirements for printability, conductivity, and stretchability. The stepwise drying process—slow evaporation followed by heating—precisely orchestrates the assembly of a percolating AgNW network and the subsequent formation of an elastomeric matrix that semi-embeds the nanowire junctions. This specific semi-embedded architecture enables the printed electrodes to concurrently achieve high resolution (30 μm), high conductivity (up to 1.6 × 104 S cm−1), stretchability (>500% strain), and cyclic durability. Our work fundamentally addresses the long-standing materials and manufacturing conflict in stretchable electronics by providing a scalable, relatively environmentally friendly solvent-based printing platform. The direct-printability, combined with the superior electromechanical properties, positions this technology as a key enabler for the scalable fabrication of next-generation wearable devices, from high-density epidermal sensors to complex bio-integrated circuits.

Methods

Materials

Amino-terminated hyperbranched polyamide (NHBM, Mw = 2260 g mol−1) was provided by Wuhan Hyperbranched Polymers Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China. Silver nitrate (AgNO3, ≥99.8%), PVP (K88-96), sodium chloride (NaCl, ≥99.5%), ethanol (EtOH, ≥99.5%), isopropanol (IPA, ≥99.7%), DMF (≥99.5%) and n-hexane (≥98%) were purchased from Aladdin Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China. Ethylene glycol (EG, >99%) was supplied by Concord Co., Ltd., China. Thermoplastic polyurethane elastomer (TPU, 1185-B) was obtained from Yantai Wanhua Polyurethane Co., Ltd., China. The water-based polyacrylic emulsion (Joncryl® 7169, Mw > 200,000 g mol−1) was purchased from BASF SE, Germany. Carbon black (P80) was purchased from Tianjin Huayuan Chemical Technology Co., Ltd., China. The PDMS mixture (SYLGARD 184) was purchased from Dow Corning Co., Ltd., China. All reagents were of analytical grade and used as received.

Characterization

SEM analysis was conducted on a JSM-7800F field-emission scanning electron microscope (JEOL, Japan). The junction density was statistically analyzed using ImageJ. XPS was performed on a Thermo Scientific K_α X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (ThermoFisher, USA). Optical microscopy images were acquired using an upright metallurgical microscope (Leica DM750 M, Germany). Digital photos of the samples were obtained using a Canon 5D Mark III camera. The rheological properties of the nanowire inks were measured on a DHR-2 rheometer (TA Instruments, USA) at room temperature (25.00 ± 0.3 °C). The steady-state flow step (SSFS) test was used to measure the shear viscosity of the inks at shear rates in the range of 0.1–1000 s−1. Stress sweep step (SSS) test was performed with an oscillation strain range of 0.1–1000% at a frequency of 1 Hz. All measurements were carried out at room temperature.

Thixotropic behavior measurements

The peak hold step (PHS) test was performed with constant shear rates in three intervals (0.1 s−1 shear rate for 30 s, 100 s−1 for 30 s, and 0.1 s−1 for 60 s). Sheet resistance measurements

The sheet resistance was measured with a four-point probe (M-3 Mini type four-probe tester, Suzhou Jingge).

Resistance change measurements

Stretching tests and cyclic strain tests of the screen-printed AnEm electrodes were performed using a motorized linear stage with a built-in controller (Zaber Technologies) by varying the distance between the two stepper motors. Changes in the resistance of the samples were recorded using a digital multisource meter (Keithley DMM6500, Tektronix). The screen-printed AnEm electrodes have dimensions of 30 mm × 3 mm (length × width). The thickness of the printed conductivity layer after drying was approximately 0.5–1.2 µm. The TPU substrate thickness is 70 µm, the gauge length is 6 mm, and the stretching rate during testing is 0.5 mm s−1.

Adhesion test

Use a cross-cut tester to scribe 100 grids, each with an area of 1 mm2, on a film with a screen-printed conductive layer. Firmly apply 3 M tape over the grids, then peel off the tape at a constant speed perpendicular to the plane. Refer to the standard ASTM D-3359 to evaluate the ink adhesion rating.

AgNW synthesis

AgNWs with an average length of 40 μm and diameter of 40 nm (aspect ratio of approximately 1000) successfully synthesized using the polyol method reported in ref. 68. PVP and ethylene glycol (160 mL, 12.5 mg mL−1) were added to a brown conical flask, heated to 150 °C, and stirred until PVP was completely dissolved. Cool the solution to room temperature, then sequentially add sodium chloride in ethylene glycol (16 mL, 0.42 mg mL−1) and silver nitrate in ethylene glycol (40 mL, 50 mg mL−1). After vigorous magnetic stirring for 5 min, transfer the brown conical flask to an oven preheated to 110 °C and maintain for 12 h. Dilute the as-prepared AgNWs mixture with ethanol to a concentration of 0.5 mg mL−1. Pour 6 L of the diluted mixture into a filtration membrane-based cylindrical chamber with a pore size of 8 µm and mechanically stir at 200 rpm. Ethanol slowly flowed continuously into the filtration setup, ceasing the ethanol purification process after 80 min. While stirring and washing, concentrate the solution to 1 mg mL−1, then collect the purified AgNWs solution.

AnEm inks formulation

AgNW dispersed in ethanol (1 mg mL−1) were coordinated with the NHBM dispersant at an optimal weight ratio of 1:0.001 and then centrifuged. The precipitates were dispersed in IPA, which containing different polyacrylic emulsion concentrations to prepare AnEm inks. Solvent evaporation dynamics are closely related to stencil release behavior. The AnEm inks contain a trace of water introduced by the water-based polyacrylic emulsion component, forming a moderately volatile solvent system. This system ensures the ink’s flowability during the printing process and, through continuous evaporation, transforms the ink into a semi-solid state, thereby suppressing edge spreading.

Fabrication of AnEm electrodes

The AnEm patterns were prepared by a screen printer (TC-4060k, Dongguan Ta Chen Screen Printing Machine & Materials Co., Ltd.). Stencil design determines the initial printing boundaries, capillary flow, and stencil release behavior. The 80-μm-thick stainless-steel SMT laser-cut stencil was used, which is suitable for fine-pitch components (˂0.4 mm-pitch). The aperture geometry are standard rectangular apertures suitable for most components, and their straight-walled apertures provide clear lateral confinement. Laser cutting combined with electropolishing makes the aperture walls smoother to improve ink release, while a nano‑coating reduces ink adhesion to the aperture walls. The reduced friction coefficient allows ink to release smoothly even when the area ratio (aperture area/wall area) is below the conventional standard of 0.66. The stencil with an appropriate pattern was installed in the screen printer before printing. Following installation, the gel-like AnEm inks was applied onto the screen-printing plate and printed onto the TPU substrate by sliding the squeegee over the stencil. Substrate surface energy determines the spreading behavior of the ink. To improve wettability, the TPU substrate was treated with plasma for 15 min, balancing spreading and confinement. A squeegee with an ergonomic holder formed an angle of ≈45° with the screen mesh. The printing parameters included a pressure of 32.2 N, a snap‑off gap of 0.1 mm, a speed of 50 mm s−1, and one printing stroke. We examined five solvent removal conditions post-printing: (1) RT drying 30 min, (2) a stepwise process of RT drying 10 min followed by 120 °C heating 10 min, (3) a stepwise process of RT drying 20 min followed by 120 °C heating 10 min, (4) a stepwise process of RT drying 30 min followed by 120 °C heating 10 min, and (5) 120 °C heating 10 min. The conductive films were dried under a series of temperature conditions without any additional harsh posttreatment process to optimize their conductivity. To ensure stable equipment operation and printing quality, the entire fabricating process was carried out under controlled environmental conditions of 23 °C ± 2 °C and 55 ± 5% RH, following the standards of a high‑precision electronic manufacturing facility.

Fabrication of a stretchable LED array

A 10 × 10 pixels stretchable LED array was successfully fabricated by using screen printing to apply the A9.5E0.25 ink (with an AgNW solid content of 9.5 wt% and a polyacrylic emulsion content of 0.25 wt%) onto a TPU substrate (12 × 5 cm2). The width and spacing of the A9.5E0.25 electrodes were 1.5 and 2 mm, respectively. Then, the LEDs were welded to the electrodes to complete the construction of the stretchable LED array.

Fabrication of a wearable electronic glove (E-glove)

A TeNW-based temperature sensor was fabricated according to our previous reports25. The TeNW-based temperature sensor was approximately 1 cm in length and 0.5 cm in width. Among them, the widths and spacings of the two electrodes are 1 and 3 mm, respectively.

The pressure sensor was prepared according to the following steps: (1) disperse polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) prepolymer, carbon black (CB) powder, and NaCl particles in a mass ratio of 1:0.2:0.1:2 in n-hexane. Magnetically stir for 10 min, then shake for 2 h. Subsequently, add the curing agent (PDMS prepolymer to curing agent mass ratio of 10:1) and continue shaking for 10 min. Pour the resulting mixture into a mold and cure at 90 °C for 6 h. Immerse the cured sample in water for 24 h to obtain the final porous PDMS/CB conductive active layer. (2) A 0.5 × 0.5 cm2 A9.5E0.25 electrode was screen-printed onto an O3 plasma-treated glass substrate. PDMS was then cast onto the surface, cured at 90 °C for 3 h, and the cured film was peeled off to obtain the electrode layer. (3) The active layer and electrode layers were assembled and encapsulated with PDMS to form the pressure sensor.

For a E-glove, A9.5E0.25 ink is printed on the TPU to fabricate stretchable electrodes. Excess solvents were removed by drying at 25 °C for 30 min and heating at 120 °C for 10 min. Temperature sensor, pressure sensor, and electrodes were fixed on a latex glove and ensured good contact.
