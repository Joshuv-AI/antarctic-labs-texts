Impact of acute blue light irradiation on the molecular clock and markers associated with photoaging in skin cell models
Authors: Agnia Vibriani, Xinyue Chen, Susumu Kajiwara, Kanami Orihara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractThe increased reliance on digital devices in our daily lives has exposed humans to intense levels of high-energy blue light. However, the harmful effects of blue light irradiation on human skin are less understood than UV irradiation. Notably, we demonstrated that blue light induces skin photoaging through a novel mechanism involving disruption of the circadian rhythm. An in vitro study using human keratinocyte cells and dermal fibroblasts was conducted. The results showed that blue light irradiation triggers cellular senescence and downregulates key aging-associated markers, including extracellular matrix components, epithelial barrier protein, NAD+-dependent metabolic stress sensor (SIRT1), and aging-sensitive NAD + producer (NAMPT). Remarkably, we found that blue light irradiation disrupted the circadian rhythm at the cellular level. This is evidenced by phase-shifted PER3 mRNA levels, loss of BMAL1 protein rhythmicity, and altered temporal expression of aging-associated markers. Our results demonstrate that blue light triggers skin photoaging through circadian disruption, establishing a novel mechanistic pathway that transforms our understanding of photoaging complexity. This study initiates the framework for developing chronotherapy-based interventions to address the growing epidemic of digital device-associated skin damage and, therefore, could be used as a scientific foundation for developing novel active compounds in cosmeceutical products.Key messages We identified novel mechanisms in blue light-induced skin photoaging.Acute blue light exposure induced cellular senescence in skin cells.Acute blue light exposure downregulated key aging-associated markers in skin cells.Acute blue light exposure disrupted the circadian rhythm of skin cells.Targeting circadian rhythm offers a promising therapeutic route for skin photoaging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00109-026-02664-y.Keywords: Blue light, Circadian rhythm, Chronotherapy, Peripheral clock, Skin photoaging

Introduction

Skin aging is the most noticeable sign of aging in the body, involving a complex biological process of chronological aging and extrinsic aging. The aging process is further complicated by environmental factors that can accelerate its progression, especially in exposed skin areas (face, neck, arms). It has been known that the primary cause of extrinsic aging in the skin is ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the sun [1, 2]. In fact, the spectral composition of sunlight is about 3–7% UV, 44% visible, and 53% infrared [3]. While UV radiation reaches the skin up to the epidermis layer, visible light and infrared radiation can penetrate deeper into the dermis layer [4], potentially stimulating more biological responses. Recent studies have shown that high-energy visible (HEV) light, like blue-violet light (380–495 nm), can cause stress to the skin [3, 5–9]. Moreover, heavy use of electronic devices in the current digital era increases blue light exposure to human skin. Therefore, concerns have been raised on the potential of blue light in skin health, specifically in inducing skin photoaging.

Circadian rhythms are daily (~ 24 h) changes in an organism’s physiological and behavioral functions regulated by the circadian clock. In mammals, the circadian clocks consist of transcription factors BMAL1/CLOCK and their repressors PER/CRY, which work in a transcriptional-translational feedback loop (TTFL) mechanisms [10]. At the genomic level, the binding of transcription factors BMAL1/CLOCK to the E-boxes on the target genes produces transcriptional rhythms. Indeed, 43% of proteins showed a circadian rhythm in an organ-specific manner [11]. These circadian regulations help the body to anticipate and adapt to environmental changes in the surroundings, hence increasing the fitness of organisms. In the skin, the circadian clock molecules are involved in hair growth, stem cells proliferation/differentiation, skin hydration, skin immune, and UVB-induced DNA repair [12]. The involvement of circadian rhythm in many important physiological processes in the skin contributes to the progression of skin diseases and skin photoaging.

Dysregulation of circadian rhythms manifests in aging-related phenotypes. At the molecular level, dermal fibroblasts from old mice (12–24 months) exhibited low amplitude of PER1 [13]. Regarding skin photoaging, the contribution of circadian rhythm has been linked to the hormone melatonin, one of the rhythm drivers for most non-brain organs. This connection is supported by findings that silencing melanin receptor (MT-1) in human dermal fibroblasts produced higher H2O2 production and DNA damage after UV exposure [14]. However, the connections between skin photoaging and circadian rhythm have never been clearly investigated.

This research aims to (1) deepen understanding of harmful effects from blue light irradiation, and (2) provide comprehensive insight into how circadian rhythm disruption contributes to skin photoaging pathology. The results from this study will establish a new approach to intervene in the progression of skin photoaging due to modern light exposure by strengthening the circadian rhythm of skin.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human keratinocyte cells (HaCaT) were obtained from CLS Cell Lines Service (Eppelheim, Germany). Normal human dermal fibroblasts (NB1RGB, RCB0222) were obtained from RIKEN, BRC Cell Bank (Ibaraki, Japan). All cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium-high glucose (DMEM) (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemicals, Osaka, Japan) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Cytiva, USA) and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin (PS) 10,000 U/mL (Gibco by Life Technologies, Tokyo, Japan). Cells were maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2 incubator. In this study, HaCaT cells from passages 11–12 were used, while dermal fibroblasts from passages 6–7 were used.

Blue light irradiation

The blue light LED lamp with a wavelength of 460–465 nm was purchased from Kaito Denshi (Saitama, Japan). Twenty-four LEDs were arranged on an acrylic plate (10 cm × 20 cm) in a 4 × 6 grid. The illumination intensity was measured using a lux meter from H HoldPeak (Guangdong, China) and was controlled to 10,000 lx to achieve an acute exposure [15]. Cells were irradiated inside an incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2, with the distance between LEDs and cells being 2 cm. HaCaT cells and dermal fibroblasts seeded on 12-well plates or 35-mm culture dishes were irradiated for 1 h. For gene and protein expression analysis, blue light irradiation was done at Circadian Time (CT) 2 – CT3. All cells were cultured in phenol red-free DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% PS during blue light irradiation.

Cellular senescence

Cellular senescence was done by measuring the activity of β-galactosidase. Dermal fibroblasts were cultured in 35-mm culture dishes at a confluency of less than 50%. After 1 h of blue light irradiation, dermal fibroblasts were incubated for an additional hour under normal culture conditions. Then, cells were subjected to SA-β-Gal staining as described previously [16]. The stained dermal fibroblasts were examined and captured using a digital imaging system (APX100, Olympus, Japan). Total cells were counted by three individuals independently and blindly with ImageJ Version 1.54 g, while positive senescent cells were manually and blindly counted. The SA-β-Gal activity was expressed as the ratio of positive senescent cells to the number of total cells.

Circadian entrainment

Cells were cultured in 24-well plates (Violamo, Osaka, Japan) or 35-mm culture dishes until reaching 80% confluency. To induce synchronization of the circadian clock, cells were cultured in serum-free DMEM for 24 h, followed by treatment with 100 nM dexamethasone (Dex) (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) for 2 h. Then, cells were cultured again in complete media (DMEM + 10% FBS + 1% PS). Circadian time 0 (CT0) was defined after the cells finished dexamethasone treatment. The timeline of cell entrainment is illustrated in Fig. 1. Cells were harvested at designated time points for subsequent analysis.

Experimental timeline of the in vitro study. Cells were serum-starved for 24 h prior to cellular clock reset with 100 nM dexamethasone. Following 2 h of Dex treatment, the cellular circadian clock was designated as CT0. Blue light irradiation was done for 1 h at CT2-CT3. Cells were harvested at the indicated time points for further molecular analysis

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Total RNA from collected cells was isolated using Qiazol lysis reagent (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and reverse transcribed into cDNA using the ReverTra Ace kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). The qRT-PCR reaction was performed using Thunderbird SYBR qPCR mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) on the Roche LightCycler 96 thermal cycler. Primer sets used for qPCR are listed in Table S1. The copy number of each sample was calculated using a standard curve generated by plotting the quantification cycle (Cq) value against known copy numbers of a linear DNA standard. The target gene copy numbers were normalized to reference genes: 18S rRNA for HaCaT cell samples and GUSB for dermal fibroblast samples.

Western blot analysis

Total protein from collected cells was isolated using RIPA buffer (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemicals, Osaka, Japan) supplemented with proteinase inhibitor cocktail set I (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemicals, Osaka, Japan). A total of 40 µg of protein was loaded to a 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide (SDS-PAGE) gel for electrophoresis. Then, the protein was transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane in a semi-dry system. After blocking with 5% skim milk powder in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBS-T, pH 7.4) for 1.5 h, the membrane was incubated with a primary antibody against BMAL1 (1:1000, Cell Signaling, 14,020; overnight at 4 °C) or GAPDH (1:1000, Cell Signaling, 2118; 1 h at RT). Subsequently, 1:10,000 horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Jackson Immuno Laboratory, 115-035-144) was added and incubated for 1 h at RT. The membrane was developed by a chemiluminescence reagent (Chemi-Lumi One Super, Nacalai Tesque, Japan) and visualized using a CCD camera (Amersham Imager 600, GE healthcare). The protein bands were quantified using ImageJ and normalized against GAPDH.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in Graphpad Prism software (version 10.5.0 for Mac OS, GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA) or R Studio version 4.3.1. Data was reported as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). The statistical comparison was conducted using an unpaired t-test for comparing two groups. The statistical significance was defined as p-value < 0.05. A Pearson correlation analysis was done in R using “Hmisc” package. The circadian rhythm analysis was done using cosinor analysis in GraphPad Prism, with the equation from the previous study [17].

Results

Effects of blue light irradiation on cellular senescence

To investigate the potential of blue light in inducing skin photoaging, we measured the cellular senescence activity in dermal fibroblasts. As shown in Fig. 2, the percentage of senescent cells was significantly higher in blue light-irradiated cells. Additionally, senescent cells displayed morphological changes such as enlarged nuclei and cytoplasm. These results suggest that acute blue light irradiation caused cellular senescence in dermal fibroblasts, which prolonged irradiation may lead to skin photoaging.

Effect of blue light irradiation on the circadian rhythm of dermal fibroblasts

To investigate the circadian rhythm associated with blue light-induced senescent dermal fibroblasts, the mRNA expression levels of clock genes PER3 (Fig. 3A-–D) and BMAL1 (Fig. 3E-–H) were measured every 4 h for 24 h. Blue light irradiation significantly delayed the trough time of PER3 gene expression by 2 h (Fig. 3A, D). Consistent with PER3, the peak time of BMAL1 protein was also delayed, although the cosinor fitting of the blue light group did not meet the criteria for circadian rhythm (Fig. 3I-–K). The BMAL1 protein results also indicated a dampened rhythmicity in blue light-irradiated cells, suggesting that blue light disturbed the circadian machinery of dermal fibroblasts.

Effect of blue light irradiation on the clock-related genes, extracellular matrix genes, and NAD+ metabolism genes in dermal fibroblasts

To further investigate the effect of blue light irradiation in dermal fibroblasts, extracellular matrix (ECM) genes and NAD+ metabolism genes were measured. In this study, the ECM genes measured did not display a circadian rhythm. Meanwhile, blue light irradiation induced a circadian rhythmicity in NAD+ metabolism genes, NAMPT and SIRT1.

In addition, this study also observed the time-dependent response to blue light irradiation in the skin cells. The levels of gene expression in the control and blue light groups were compared at each time point. Time-dependent analysis revealed that blue light-irradiated cells may undergo stress responses characterized by ECM reorganization and unbalanced NAD+ metabolism. Specifically, an increase in the expression levels of MMP2 and MMP1 was observed at CT4 and CT12, respectively (Fig. 4C, D). This upregulation was followed by a downregulation of COL1 and COL3 at CT12 (Fig. 4A, B). Meanwhile, SIRT1 expression levels remained consistently low during the latter phases (CT16-24) (Fig. 4F). Although the expression of SIRT1 and NAMPT was induced during the initial phases (Fig. 4E, F). A depletion of SIRT1 in dermal fibroblasts supported the cellular senescence results occurring in blue light-irradiated cells.

To analyze the relationship of clock-related genes to ECM genes and NAD+ metabolism genes in dermal fibroblasts, Pearson correlations were performed. This study focused on BMAL1 interaction, considering its important role as a transcription factor for clock-controlled genes. Despite lacking a robust circadian rhythm, BMAL1 showed strong relationships with ECM and NAD+ metabolism genes in the control group. As expected, blue light irradiation disrupted the interaction between BMAL1 and ECM genes. For example, BMAL1 and COL1 or COL3 or MMP2 have a positive correlation coefficient in the control group (Fig. 4H). However, their correlation coefficient became negative in blue light-irradiated cells, suggesting that blue light disturbed BMAL1 regulation on ECM genes (Fig. 4I).

Effect of blue light irradiation on the circadian rhythm of HaCaT cells

We also studied the epidermal responses against blue light irradiation using HaCaT cells. In HaCaT cells, a significant alteration of circadian rhythm can be observed in BMAL1 protein (Fig. 5I). Blue light irradiation diminished the circadian rhythmicity of BMAL1 protein and reduced the average protein level (Fig. 5I-–K). Meanwhile, the circadian parameters of BMAL1 and PER3 gene expression were unaffected, suggesting that post-transcriptional mechanisms may contribute to the BMAL1 protein disruption in HaCaT cells (Fig. 5A-–H).

Effect of blue light irradiation on the clock-related genes, tight junction genes, and NAD+ metabolism genes in HaCaT cells

Tight junction genes were examined to investigate the impact of blue light irradiation on HaCaT cells, as the primary function of keratinocyte cells is to act as a protective barrier. The circadian rhythm of TJP1 was weakened in blue light-irradiated cells, whereas the circadian rhythm of OCLN was entirely disrupted (Fig. 6A–E). These circadian disruptions were further corroborated by lower amplitude of TJP1 in the blue light group.

Consistent with tight junction genes, blue light irradiation attenuated the circadian rhythm of NAMPT and SIRT1 gene expression (Fig. 6F, G). Although the circadian rhythm of SIRT1 in the control group showed a low R2 value. These results were in contrast with dermal fibroblasts, where blue light irradiation strengthened the circadian rhythmicity of NAMPT and SIRT1. These findings suggested cell-specific responses to blue light irradiation, especially for the NAD+ metabolism genes whose regulation depends on the cell energy consumption.

Time-dependent analysis revealed that HaCaT cells exhibited susceptibility to blue light irradiation at the initial phase; however, they may recover at the latter phase. Blue light irradiation caused early downregulation of OCLN expression level at CT4, followed by a reduction of SIRT1 expression level at CT8. Subsequently, an increase in BMAL1, TJP1, and NAMPT expression levels was detected at CT16 and CT24, respectively.

In line with dermal fibroblasts, blue light irradiation broke down the interactions between BMAL1 and clock-controlled genes in HaCaT cells. Control cells showed a positive correlation between BMAL1 and TJP1 or OCLN or NAMPT or SIRT1. However, blue light irradiation dampened these correlations, becoming less positive compared to control cells (Fig. 6I, J).

Discussion

This study provides evidence that blue light induces skin photoaging by disrupting circadian rhythms. We demonstrated that an acute dose of blue light irradiation significantly increased the percentage of senescent cells in dermal fibroblasts (Fig. 2), indicating cellular aging progression. The senescent cells are the result of an accumulation of early responses to blue light irradiation, including ROS generation and DNA damage [18]. Consistent with previous studies, blue light irradiation slows the cell cycle rate by inducing arrest in the G1 and G2 phases [19, 20]. The cellular senescence level was not determined in HaCaT cells because of mutations in both p53 alleles (key molecule in cellular senescence) as a result of spontaneous transformation during the immortalization process [21–24]. Although the percentage of senescent cells remains lower compared to UV light exposure [25, 26], the increase is still significant relative to the control group of this study. Nevertheless, this result suggests that even short-term exposure to an acute dose of blue light was sufficient to trigger cellular senescence in dermal fibroblasts. Further investigation on chronic dose effects of blue light with prolonged or repeated exposure is needed.

Changes in the dermal layer are key factors in the visible features of skin aging, such as loss of skin elasticity and wrinkles. We observed a significant decline in collagens (COL1 and COL3) and an increase in matrix metalloproteinases (MMP1 and MMP2) mRNA levels upon blue light irradiation (Fig. 4). Blue light-induced skin photoaging involves three distinct signaling cascades: TGF-β pathway, JNK/AP-1 (c-Jun N-terminal kinase/AP1-) signaling, and EGFR activation. These pathways result in promoting extracellular matrix degradation through enhanced collagen breakdown and MMPs expression, leading to dermal deterioration [9]. Furthermore, the dermal stiffening in aged skin can also be induced by upregulation of Ptx3 (pentraxin 3), an inflammation-sensitive gene. Through this pathway, a prolonged calcium influx by PIEZO1 (a mechanosensitive ion channel) was activated and resulted in interfollicular epidermal stem cells (IFSCs) dysregulation, leading to skin aging progression [27]. Moreover, blue light irradiation independently triggers calcium influx in skin cells [28]. Therefore, it is speculated that blue light-induced skin photoaging fundamentally alters the dermal-epidermal microenvironment, creating a pathological milieu that perpetuates aging through mechanotransduction and calcium-dependent signaling pathways.

Circulating metabolic factors like NAD+ contribute to shaping the aging microenvironment. The NAD+ plays multiple essential roles: maintaining cellular redox balance and energy metabolism, facilitating DNA repair, and regulating circadian rhythmicity [29, 30]. To assess NAD+ metabolic alterations, we measured the mRNA levels of NAMPT (NAD+ producer) and SIRT1 (NAD+ consumer) in both skin cells. Overall, blue light irradiation decreased SIRT1 mRNA levels and increased NAMPT mRNA levels in dermal fibroblasts and HaCaT cells (Figs. 4G and 6H). A low level of SIRT1 often correlates with low NAD+ availability in the cells, thereby promoting cellular senescence [31]. Notably, in dermal fibroblasts, an upregulation of NAMPT at CT12 failed to restore SIRT1 levels at subsequent timepoints, indicating persistent NAD+ metabolic dysfunction. This dysregulated NAD+ homeostasis provides a mechanistic explanation for the increase of SA-β-Gal activity observed in blue light irradiated-cells (Fig. 2), confirming accelerated senescence induction.

In the epidermal layer, tight junction barriers can be damaged by skin inflammation [32], skin infection [33], skin aging [34], and UVB-induced skin photoaging [35]. Similarly, we observed an inhibition of OCLN at CT4 upon blue light irradiation (Fig. 6H), suggesting that blue light exposure impaired epidermal barrier function and potentially increases infection susceptibility. This aligns with clinical observations demonstrating correlation between sunburn frequency and skin infection rates in immunocompromised individuals [36], indicating photodamage undermines cutaneous immune defense regardless of the wavelength involved. Taken together, at the molecular level, we demonstrated that acute blue light induced skin photoaging characterized by (1) structural dermal degradation, shown in downregulation of mRNA levels of collagens and upregulation of MMPs; (2) metabolic dysfunction, demonstrated in reduced SIRT1 expression indicating NAD+ pathway disruption; (3) barrier arrangement, revealed in decreased OCLN expression. This molecular response recapitulates the key hallmarks of chronological skin aging, thus establishing blue light as a significant photoaging stimulus.

A novel finding of the present study was the disruption of circadian rhythmicity in dermal fibroblasts and HaCaT cells following blue light irradiation. Various stimuli can alter cutaneous circadian rhythm, modifying circadian properties (amplitude, peak/trough time, MESOR, and rhythmicity stability) [37, 38]. However, no previous studies showed the circadian properties in skin photoaging. We provide the first evidence that blue light irradiation disrupts cutaneous circadian rhythm through multiple molecular endpoints: (1) phase delay in PER3 trough timing in dermal fibroblasts (Fig. 3D), (2) diminished rhythmicity of BMAL1 protein in both cell types (Fig. 3I and 5I), and (3) reduced BMAL1 protein level in HaCaT cells (Fig. 5J). Notably, this in vitro finding translates to the animal model, where blue light exposure induced a phase shift in mouse skin impedance (see Supplementary Fig. S1 online). Given that mice received blue light stimuli through both ocular and cutaneous routes, we speculated that this peak adjustment resulted from circadian regulation by both the central (suprachiasmatic nucleus) and peripheral clock networks. As our study focused on peripheral clock regulation, we limited our in vivo evaluation to this preliminary verification. Collectively, these results establish that skin peripheral clocks exhibit direct photosensitivity to blue light irradiation, suggesting a novel chronobiological pathway underlying skin homeostasis disruption and photoaging initiation. This circadian-photoaging axis represents a paradigm shift in understanding how environmental light exposure accelerates skin aging beyond traditional UV-mediated mechanisms.

The interaction among circadian machinery, the heat-shock response pathway, and p53 molecules may explain how the skin peripheral clocks respond to light stress [39]. Previously, it was reported that UVB irradiation induced PER2 phase adjustment in NIH3T3 fibroblasts [39]. Specifically, shortly after UVB irradiation, the interaction of BMAL1 and HSF1 (a transcription factor of the heat-shock response pathway) was promoted. While BMAL1 binds to E-box elements, HSF1 binds to the heat-shock response elements (HSE2) site within the PER2 promoter, synergistically enhancing PER2 expression. Then in parallel, BMAL1 and HSF1 simultaneously trigger p53 activity as part of the stress-protection system. Unlike HSF1, p53 stimulates PER2 downregulation [39]. Therefore, the regulation of PER2 transcription via the BMAL1-HSF-p53 axis was proposed to contribute to phase shifts in the peripheral clock upon light stress [39]. Furthermore, previous studies have reported the sensitivity of PERs to light signals in different peripheral organs [40, 41]. These previous findings align with our results, where we observed PER3 phase adjustment in dermal fibroblasts after blue light irradiation, suggesting that PER3 can serve as an emerging biomarker for assessing circadian rhythm disruption associated with skin photoaging. Since HaCaT cells carry mutations in both alleles of the p53 gene, a phase adjustment for the PER expression is not anticipated.

To further explore the relationship between circadian rhythm and skin photoaging, Pearson’s correlation analysis was done between clock genes and aging-associated markers. This study demonstrated that blue light irradiation diminished strong correlations between clock genes and aging-associated genes, and replaced some relationships from negative to positive and vice versa (Figs. 4I and 6J). In addition, our in vitro analysis showed loss of BMAL1 protein rhythmicity in the blue light group in both skin cells (Figs. 3K and 5K), with a significant reduction of BMAL1 protein levels in the blue light group of HaCaT cells. Considering BMAL1’s role as a key transcription factor for clock-controlled genes [10, 42], we observed significant temporal changes in aging-associated markers in the blue light group. Specifically, blue light irradiation reduced the amplitude of TJP1 and lower fit to the circadian rhythm for OCLN, NAMPT, and SIRT1 in HaCaT cells. Although direct functional interaction between BMAL1 and aging-associated markers was not shown, our results strongly suggested that circadian rhythm disruption occurred in blue light-induced skin photoaging. Hence, targeting BMAL1 could represent a new chronotherapy pathway for strengthening skin circadian rhythm in skin photoaging conditions. Currently, nobiletin, a small molecule derived from Japanese citrus flavonoid, has been identified as a potent enhancer and stabilizer of circadian clock gene oscillations [43], and shows a protective role against UV light-induced skin photoaging [44, 45]. Based on these previous studies, we hypothesized that the protective roles of nobiletin extend beyond oxidative stress prevention and include the restoration of circadian rhythm dysregulation, offering a dual-action approach to treat skin photoaging.

In conclusion, this investigation establishes the dermatological consequences of blue light irradiation through a circadian-mediated photoaging paradigm. We demonstrated that blue light induced skin photoaging at the cellular level through the interplay between circadian clock genes and aging markers. Although the blue light emitted from electronic devices is less intense than solar light, the cumulative exposure from daily device usage warrants serious consideration. The chronic low-level blue light exposure may constitute an underappreciated environmental stressor contributing to accelerated cutaneous aging. These findings underscore the need for protective strategies against this emerging photodamage mechanism and highlight the potential of circadian-targeted interventions in dermatological health.
