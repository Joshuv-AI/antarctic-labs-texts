Knowledge, attitudes, and practices toward premature rupture of membranes among pregnant women and their associated factors: A study at Beijing friendship hospital, Beijing, China
Authors: E Dan, Haifeng Wang, Nan Jia, Na Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess pregnant women's knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding premature rupture of membranes (PROM) and associated factors.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted at Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University (Beijing, China) from January 10th, 2025 to February 28th, 2025. Using convenience sampling, pregnant women with gestational age ≥ 28 weeks who provided informed consent were recruited, while those with abnormal pregnancy/adverse outcomes, psychological/mental illness, or current PROM/PPROM at enrollment were excluded. Data were collected via an online questionnaire, and statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.2) and Stata (SEM).ResultsA total of 422 participants were included. Mean (SD) knowledge, attitude, and practice scores were 36.32 (7.91), 20.76 (3.61), and 6.91 (1.50), respectively. Using the 60% cutoff, 81.04% had good knowledge, 45.02% had positive attitude, and 84.36% had proactive practice. Proactive practice was associated with higher knowledge (OR = 1.09, 95% CI 1.04, 1.13) and attitude scores (OR = 1.20, 95% CI 1.08, 1.34) and higher monthly income. SEM showed knowledge directly affected attitude (β = 0.18) and practice (β = 0.46).ConclusionsPregnant women showed relatively good knowledge and proactive practices, while attitudes were less favorable. Knowledge played a key role in shaping reported practices.Keywords: Pregnant women, Knowledge, Attitude, Practice, Premature rupture of membranes

Introduction

Premature rupture of membranes (PROM) is defined as spontaneous rupture of the fetal membranes before the onset of labor(Prelabor Rupture of Membranes: ACOG Practice Bulletin, Number 217, 2020). These membranes provide mechanical protection for the fetus against microbial invasion in the uterus(Menon and Richardson, 2017). In China; the prevalence of PROM ranges from 2.7% to 17%(Zhou et al., 2014). Particularly concerning is preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM); which occurs before 37 weeks of gestation and is associated with the greatest risks; with an incidence of approximately 1–4% of all pregnancies(Jena et al., 2022). PPROM is responsible for 30% to 40% of preterm births(Linehan et al., 2016); and preterm births account for 75% of perinatal deaths(Gezer et al., 2013). Additionally; PROM at term is associated with an increased risk of fever before; during; and after labor; as well as intrauterine and neonatal infections(Diguisto, 2020). Several factors elevate the risk of PROM; including a previous history of preterm delivery; genital tract infection; lower socioeconomic status; cigarette smoking; uterine over-distension (e.g.; polyhydramnios or multiple pregnancy); cervical procedures (e.g.; cerclage) or amniocentesis; cervical insufficiency; extremes of maternal age; multiparity; antepartum hemorrhage; and history of abortion(Addisu et al., 2020; Jena et al., 2022).

The Knowledge, Attitude, and Practices (KAP) survey serves as a valuable diagnostic research tool, revealing a group's understanding, beliefs, and behaviors concerning a particular topic. In this study, KAP is used to describe pregnant women's knowledge of PROM, their attitudes toward the condition, and their intended or reported response practices, and to examine factors associated with these domains. For pregnant women, identifying gaps in their knowledge and attitudes is crucial due to the significant risks associated with PROM, including infection and preterm birth(Lin et al., 2024; Skrypchenko and Lozova, 2024). Pregnant women play a central role in recognizing early symptoms and seeking prompt care. Focusing on this specific group ensures that interventions are tailored to those most impacted, ultimately leading to improved maternal and neonatal health outcomes(Ferro et al., 2018).

KAP studies among pregnant women in China have mainly focused on other pregnancy conditions rather than PROM. To our knowledge, PROM-specific KAP data among pregnant women in mainland China remain scarce, supporting the need for the present survey(Li et al., 2025; Lumbanraja et al., 2024). Despite the importance of this issue, there is currently a lack of KAP research targeting PROM specifically. Therefore, this study aims to investigate pregnant women's knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding PROM. Because PROM is a common obstetric complication and its recommended responses (e.g., timely assessment and infection prevention) are broadly consistent across clinical guidelines, understanding how pregnant women perceive and respond to PROM in different health-care contexts can inform patient education strategies internationally. Findings from a large tertiary hospital setting in China may help identify common, potentially modifiable knowledge gaps and behavioral patterns that are relevant to other settings, while also highlighting context-specific needs for tailoring communication and support. Although KAP levels may vary across health-care settings and populations, establishing baseline KAP profiles and their associated factors is necessary to inform context-appropriate counseling and resource allocation. Moreover, using a standardized KAP framework enables comparisons across regions and supports the generalizability of findings by identifying consistent patterns and modifiable gaps.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study was conducted from January 10th, 2025 to February 28th, 2025 at Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University, among pregnant women. The study obtained ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the institution's guidelines for the protection of human subjects, concerning participant safety and privacy, and the corresponding author confirms that the IRB approval documentation is on file at the authors' institution.

Inclusion criteria: 1) Gestational age ≥ 28 weeks; 2) Undelivered; 3) No history of mental illness; 4) Voluntary participation in this study.

Exclusion criteria: 1) Abnormal pregnancy or adverse pregnancy outcomes, including embryonic arrest, fetal death, stillbirth, miscarriage, dystocia, fetal malformation, or fetal growth/developmental abnormalities; 2) Presence of psychological/mental illness; 3) Refusal to participate in the study or failure to provide written informed consent;4) No current PROM/PPROM at the time of enrollment.

Sampling method: A non-probability convenience sampling method was used. The questionnaire link was generated on Wenjuanxing and distributed via offline QR codes in the obstetrics outpatient clinic, prenatal education classes, and maternity wards of Beijing Friendship Hospital during the study period. Pregnant women who met the inclusion criteria and provided informed consent participated voluntarily by accessing and completing the online questionnaire through the QR code. No probability-based sampling (e.g., random sampling) was applied.

According to the formula for calculating the sample size in cross-sectional surveys n=Z1−α/2δ2×p×1−p In the formula, “n” represents the sample size for each group, “α” represents the type I error, which is typically set at 0.05，Z1−α/2=1.96，δ represents the allowable error, typically set at 0.05, and “p” is set at 0.5 (as setting it at 0.5 maximizes the value and ensures a sufficiently large sample size). The calculated sample size “n” is 384. Considering an estimated questionnaire response rate of 80%, the final plan is to collect 480 valid questionnaires.

Feasibility: Beijing Friendship Hospital has an annual delivery volume of approximately 4000 cases, providing a sufficient pool of pregnant women meeting the eligibility criteria (gestational age ≥ 28 weeks). Therefore, recruiting the planned number of questionnaires within the 7-week study period at this single center was feasible.

Measures

After the initial draft of the questionnaire was designed and pilot-tested on a small scale (n = 32), achieving a reliability of 0.821. This reliability coefficient (Cronbach's α) refers to the overall questionnaire in the pilot test (all scored items across the Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice sections combined).

The final questionnaire, written in Chinese, consisted of four dimensions (Supplementary material). The basic information section included age, educational level, and occupation. The knowledge dimension contained 10 scored items and 1 additional trap question (the 11th item), the attitude dimension included 7 items, and the practice dimension comprised 9 items. The knowledge dimension utilized a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 5 (“very knowledgeable”) to 1 (“very unaware”), with overall scores for the 10 items ranging from 10 to 50. The 11th item was an attention-check question used to identify careless responses and was not included in the total knowledge score. The attitude dimension relied on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 5 (“very positive”) to 1 (“very negative”), with total scores ranging from 7 to 35. In the practice dimension, responses were scored as 1 (“yes”) and 0 (“no” or “uncertain”), resulting in scores ranging from 0 to 9.

The questionnaire was designed on Wenjuanxing, a professional online questionnaire software platform provided by Changsha Ranxing Information Technology (China) and a link to the questionnaire was generated. Questionnaires were distributed by offline QR code distribution in locations such as the hospital's obstetrics outpatient clinic, prenatal education classes, and maternity wards. Questionnaire administration: The survey was self-administered online via Wenjuanxing. Participants accessed the questionnaire by scanning the QR code and completed it independently.

Statistical analysis

Construct validity of the final questionnaire was further examined using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations (SD) or median, 25th percentile, and 75th percentile. Categorical data were expressed as frequency (percentage). For normally distributed continuous variables, comparisons between groups were conducted using the t-test, and comparisons for skewed distribution data were performed using the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test. For three or more groups with normally distributed continuous variables and uniform variances, ANOVA was employed, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for skewed distribution data. Correlations between scores in different dimensions were assessed using Pearson's correlation coefficient for normally distributed data and Spearman's correlation coefficient when normality assumptions were not met. Univariate and multivariate logistic regressions were conducted with dichotomized KAP categories (good vs. poor knowledge, positive vs. negative attitude, proactive vs. non-proactive practice) as dependent variables to analyze relationships between demographic characteristics and KAP levels. For logistic regression analyses, KAP scores were dichotomized into binary outcomes (good vs. poor knowledge, positive vs. negative attitude, proactive vs. non-proactive practice) based on the 60% cutoff. The cutoff value for scores was set at 60% of the possible highest score(Bloom, 1968). In multivariate regression analysis, variables with a significance level of P < 0.1 in univariate regression were included. Using the KAP theoretical framework, a structural equation model (SEM) was employed to verify whether attitude mediated the relationship between knowledge and behavior. Direct and indirect effects were calculated and compared, with thresholds for goodness-of-fit indices set as follows: root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) < 0.08, standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) < 0.08, Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) > 0.8, and comparative fit index (CFI) > 0.8. All non-SEM analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2, and structural equation modeling was conducted in Stata using the built-in sem command.

Results

A total of 505 participants were initially recruited. Of these, 59 answered the trap question incorrectly in the knowledge section, and 24 completed the questionnaire in less than 90 s (indicating insufficient engagement). After excluding these cases, 422 participants were included in the final analysis. CFA showed RMSEA = 0.06, TLI = 0.91, SRMR = 0.10, and CFI = 0.92, demonstrating an acceptable model fit and supporting the construct validity of the questionnaire.

Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. The knowledge, attitude, and practice scores were 36.32 (7.91), 20.76 (3.61), and 6.91 (1.50), respectively. Using the 60% cutoff, 342 (81.04%) had good knowledge (cutoff = 30), 190 (45.02%) had positive attitude (score > 21; cutoff = 21), and 356 (84.36%) had proactive practice (cutoff = 6). Practice scores differed by monthly income (P = 0.038) and gestational weeks (P = 0.02) (Table 1).

The distribution of knowledge dimensions showed that 39.57% were very familiar with immediate medical attention is required when a significant amount of fluid is discharged vaginally (K8), 52.61% were familiar with it is important to maintain genital hygiene and avoid taking baths in a tub after premature rupture of membranes (K10), and 26.07% were moderately familiar with premature rupture of membranes refers to the rupture of the fetal membranes before the onset of labor (K1). In addition, 23.22% were unfamiliar with the fact that for full-term pregnant women with premature rupture of membranes, there may be a waiting period of 2–12 h after membrane rupture if waiting for spontaneous labor (K6) (Supplementary Table 1).

Responses to the attitudinal dimension showed that 45.02% of the participants had full confidence that the doctor would try to help them get through it if they had a preterm rupture of membranes (A5), and 57.11% agreed that they would try to adjust their state of mind when they had a preterm rupture of membranes (A4). When it comes to the question of whether they were worried about preterm rupture of membranes (A6), 35.55% were neutral, however, 30.57% were concerned (Supplementary Table 2).

Regarding practices related to premature rupture of membranes, more than 78.00% of the participants would perform most of the practice items except for P1 and P3. Specifically, regarding whether they would be able to distinguish whether it was amniotic fluid or urine when they felt fluid flowing from their lower body in the late stages of pregnancy (P1), 55.45% said they could, while 34.36% were unsure. With regard to whether they would refuse antibiotics in the event of premature rupture of membranes, 38.86% said they would not, while 48.82% were unsure (Supplementary Table 3).

In the correlation analysis, significant positive correlations were found between knowledge and attitude (r = 0.267, P = 0.002), knowledge and practice (r = 0.343, P < 0.01), and attitude and practice (r = 0.256, P < 0.01), respectively (Table 2).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional survey, pregnant women showed relatively good knowledge and proactive practices regarding premature rupture of membranes (PROM), whereas attitudes appeared less favorable. Structural equation modeling (SEM) indicated that knowledge had a direct effect on both attitude and practice, whereas the direct effect of attitude on practice was not statistically significant. PROM-specific KAP evidence among pregnant women remains limited internationally, and direct cross-country comparisons are therefore challenging. Existing KAP research related to PROM has more frequently focused on healthcare providers; for example, a cross-sectional study among midwives in Indonesia reported substantial proportions with poor knowledge and poor attitudes toward PROM, suggesting that gaps in PROM-related perceptions and preparedness may also occur outside China(Lumbanraja et al., 2024). More broadly; KAP studies in pregnant populations addressing other maternal health topics have reported that favorable attitudes are not universal even when awareness or intended behaviors are present; implying that emotional responses; perceived risk; and contextual factors may weaken attitudes relative to knowledge or practices(Temtanakitpaisan et al., 2020). Our pattern—higher knowledge and proactive practice but less favorable attitudes—may reflect a similar phenomenon across settings.

Although knowledge and practice levels were generally high, attitudes may still appear less favorable because PROM is perceived as a high-risk and uncertain obstetric event, which can elicit worry even among well-informed women. Item-level results showed that a notable proportion of participants were neutral or concerned about preterm rupture of membranes (A6), suggesting that anxiety and risk perception may coexist with adequate knowledge. SEM further showed a modest positive effect of knowledge on attitude (β = 0.18) but no statistically significant effect of attitude on practice, indicating that proactive practices may be driven more by perceived severity and the expectation to seek prompt medical care than by positive feelings or confidence. Therefore, improving knowledge alone may not fully translate into more positive attitudes, and prenatal counseling may need to include reassurance and psychosocial support to address PROM-related worry.

Importantly, the interpretation of “less favorable attitudes” partly depends on the dichotomization using the 60% cutoff (21 points). In our sample, the mean attitude score (20.76) was very close to this threshold, suggesting that many participants may cluster around the cutoff and that small score differences could change classification (positive vs. negative). Such proximity reduces the discriminative ability of binary categorization and may introduce misclassification, potentially overstating the contrast implied by labeling attitudes as “negative.” Accordingly, this finding is better interpreted as indicating a borderline or mixed attitude profile rather than a definitively negative one. Our analyses using continuous scores (correlation analysis and SEM) provide complementary evidence, and SEM results emphasize the central role of knowledge in shaping practice.

We observed differences in practice scores across demographic categories, and multivariate logistic regression identified monthly income as an independent predictor of proactive practice and of attitude. This aligns with evidence that socioeconomic disparities influence access to health information and healthcare utilization during pregnancy(Chen et al., 2018; Ito et al., 2019). Higher-income women may have greater access to prenatal education and healthcare resources, which may facilitate more proactive responses to PROM, whereas lower-income women may face barriers that hinder timely care-seeking and adherence to recommended management. Practice scores also differed across gestational weeks, which may reflect increasing healthcare engagement and preparedness as pregnancy progresses, consistent with concepts of evolving maternal role attainment(Wu et al., 2023).

Notably, no demographic factors were associated with good knowledge in univariate analysis, underscoring the multifactorial nature of knowledge acquisition among pregnant women. Beyond socioeconomic factors, cultural beliefs, information channels, and the quality and consistency of prenatal counseling may shape understanding of pregnancy-related complications(Clifton et al., 2022; Mudra et al., 2022). Together, the correlations among knowledge, attitude, and practice and the SEM findings support a framework in which knowledge plays a key role in shaping both perceptions and behaviors, while the pathway from attitude to practice may be weaker in the context of PROM, where perceived severity may prompt action irrespective of affective responses.

Several limitations should be considered. First, dichotomizing KAP scores using a 60% cutoff may have resulted in information loss, and this threshold is not a clinically validated standard; the close proximity of the attitude mean to the cutoff further increases classification uncertainty. Second, the study relied on self-reported questionnaire data, which may be affected by recall and social desirability bias; reported practices may be overestimated, and attitudes may reflect situational worry that could be amplified in a hospital setting. Third, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Finally, this was a single-center convenience sample recruited via hospital-based QR codes, which may introduce selection bias and under-represent women with limited access to hospital services or lower digital literacy; generalizability to other regions and care settings should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Despite these limitations, the findings have practical implications. Brief, standardized antenatal counseling that emphasizes symptom recognition and clear action steps when fluid leakage occurs, combined with concise explanations of common management options (including antibiotics when indicated), may help reduce uncertainty and support appropriate care-seeking. Future multicenter and longitudinal studies are needed to validate KAP classification approaches, examine changes over time, and test whether targeted counseling can improve attitudes and reduce PROM-related worry while maintaining proactive practices.

Conclusion

Pregnant women demonstrated good knowledge and proactive practice toward PROM, whereas attitudes appeared less favorable. SEM revealed that knowledge directly affected attitude and practice. Clinicians, together with antenatal educators and public health services, should prioritize educational interventions aimed at improving pregnant women's knowledge about PROM, which could enhance their attitudes and practices toward this condition and potentially reduce associated risks and complications. Future studies should evaluate how clinicians' counseling practices and professional experience influence pregnant women's decision-making and attitudes toward PROM.
