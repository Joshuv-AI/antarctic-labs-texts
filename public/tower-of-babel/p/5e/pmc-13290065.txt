Endoscopic spine surgery
Authors: Jordan Lebovic, Charla Fischer, Yong Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndoscopic spine surgery (ESS) has emerged as a transformative approach in the treatment of spine pathology, offering advantages over traditional open surgical techniques. This review examines the historical development of ESS, highlighting key figures and milestones that have shaped its evolution. It explores different endoscopic techniques, including transforaminal, interlaminar, lumbar fusion, cervical, and thoracic approaches. The review also provides an overview of the ongoing debate between uniportal and biportal ESS, discussing the relative invasiveness, capabilities, costs, and learning curves. In addition, it examines the complication profiles of ESS compared with open surgery as well as the economic implications of these techniques. Finally, the review outlines future directions for ESS and the impact of enabling technology.

History of endoscopic spine surgery

The earliest known contribution to the field of endoscopic spine surgery (ESS) was made by Dr. Michael Burman, a surgeon at the Hospital for Joint Diseases in New York City, who is credited with being the first to publish on the visualization of the spine using a scope. In October 1931, during a traveling fellowship under the mentorship of Dr. Schmorl in Germany, Dr. Burman published his article titled “Myeloscopy or the Direct Visualization of the Spinal Canal and Its Contents” in The Journal of Bone & Joint Surgery.1 This pioneering study laid the groundwork for future explorations into endoscopic techniques in spinal surgery, demonstrating the potential for direct visualization of the spinal canal and its contents.

Several key figures have played pivotal roles in the evolution of ESS. Dr. Parviz Kambin is credited with developing the percutaneous posterolateral approach for accessing the disc space, laying the foundation for endoscopic transforaminal lumbar discectomy.2 In 1987, Kambin and Brager3 described the anatomy and mechanism of percutaneous posterolateral discectomy, highlighting a new concept in treating radiculopathy associated with disc herniation. In 1975, Japanese orthopedic surgeon Dr. Hijikata4 introduced and refined the concept of percutaneous discectomy, further advancing the field (Fig. 1). They used what would be termed the “inside-out” technique, involving the creation of the working space within the disc itself.

Anatomic diagram from Dr. Hijikata4's original article describing the inside-out percutaneous nucleotomy technique.

The modern era of ESS, focusing on lumbar discectomy, began in the late 20th century. As technology progressed, the introduction of flexible endoscopes and improved optics allowed for enhanced visualization and manipulation of instruments within the spinal canal. Following the advancement of percutaneous discectomy, Dr. Schreiber and Dr. Suezawa developed a series of cannulas that were telescoped one over the other and placed in the center of the intervertebral disc through the Kambin triangle, consisting of the space between the superior articular process, the superior endplate of the vertebral body, and the exiting nerve root.5 This led to the development of various endoscopic techniques, including the interlaminar approach, expanding the indications for ESS beyond simple discectomy to more complex spinal pathologies such as spinal stenosis and foraminal stenosis.6

Dr. Anthony Yeung, after training with Dr. Kambin, expanded the application of endoscopic techniques through the development of the Yeung7 Endoscopic Spine System (Fig. 2). This was the first commercially successful endoscopic spine system. Soon thereafter, Dr. Thomas Hoogland introduced foraminoplasty methods to enlarge the working space outside the disc, allowing for a new “outside-in” technique (Fig. 3).8 This improved the efficacy of endoscopic approaches, providing better access to extract migrated discs and address pathology outside the disc space.9 The establishment of the International Society for Minimal Intervention in Spinal Surgery in 1989 promoted the standardization and dissemination of endoscopic techniques, providing guidelines for training and practice.

Dr. Yeung7's Endoscopic Spine System that ushered in the commercial age of endoscopic spine surgery.

Dr. Thomas Hoogland's original images describing bone removal to establish a working space for the outside-in technique.8

In 2001, Dr. Sang-Ho Lee and Dr. Gun Choi continued to refine endoscopic techniques. They developed advanced uses of the burr to decompress more complex spine pathology.10 Thereafter, new biportal endoscopic techniques emerged initially with Dr. Gaffer, which have gained recognition for their effectiveness and versatility in addressing various spinal conditions.11 Along with Dr. Soliman, their contributions have been instrumental in popularizing the biportal approach, allowing for improved access and visualization during procedures (Fig. 4).10,12

Dr. Soliman12's original imaging for his developing biportal endoscopic technique.

In recent years, the advent of biportal endoscopic techniques has revolutionized the field, allowing for greater maneuverability and improved outcomes.12–14 Biportal ESS uses 2 portals for visualization and instrumentation, enhancing the surgeon's ability to perform complex decompressions and fusions with minimal tissue disruption. The integration of new enabling technologies including three-dimensional systems, navigation, robotics, and improvements in digital visualization, have provided surgeons with stereoscopic views of the surgical field, thereby improving safety and precision.

Endoscopic techniques

Endoscopic spine surgery encompasses various techniques tailored to address specific spinal conditions. This section will assess the different approaches and pathologies that endoscopic spine surgery can help treat, as well as the associated literature.

Transforaminal endoscopic surgery

Transforaminal endoscopic surgery was the first approach used in endoscopic surgery, popularized by Dr. Kambin. This approach involves accessing the intervertebral foramen to often remove herniated disc material while preserving surrounding structures (Fig. 5).15 The approach often only removes minimal bone from the caudal superior articular process. The technique is characterized by its minimally invasive nature, resulting in reduced postoperative pain and quicker recovery times compared with traditional open discectomy.9

Image demonstrating the endoscopic interlaminar and transforaminal approaches to the lumbar spine.16

Studies have demonstrated that transforaminal endoscopic surgery is effective in treating various lumbar pathologies, including foraminal stenosis and radiculopathy.17 The technique allows for direct visualization of the nerve root, facilitating precise decompression while minimizing the risk of nerve injury. Ahn et al.18 analyzed the effectiveness of percutaneous endoscopic lumbar foraminotomy as an advanced surgical technique, reporting favorable clinical outcomes. In their study, they included 50 patients and found symptomatic improvement in 93.9% of patients by 1 year postoperatively. Gibson et al.17 conducted a randomized controlled trial comparing transforaminal endoscopic discectomy with microdiscectomy, finding that transforaminal endoscopic surgery provided comparable results with less postoperative leg pain and shorter recovery times. They did, however, find a slightly higher revision rate in the endoscopic group. Their study involved 143 patients and concluded that transforaminal endoscopic surgery was a reliable alternative to open microdiscectomy.17

Osman et al.19 performed a comparative study of transforaminal and posterior decompressions of the lumbar spine, demonstrating the stability and intervertebral foramen area achieved through transforaminal endoscopic surgery. Their findings indicated that transforaminal endoscopic decompression resulted in a larger foramen expansion postoperatively, crucial for nerve root decompression as well as less instability.19 Hoogland et al.20 evaluated the outcomes of endoscopic transforaminal discectomy for recurrent lumbar disc herniation in a cohort of 262 consecutive cases, further supporting the efficacy of this technique. They reported a success rate of 85% and a recurrence rate of 4%, with a mean follow-up of 24 months.20 Yagi et al.21 discussed the advantages of revision transforaminal full-ESS in patients with previous posterior spine surgery, emphasizing its role in managing recurrent and adjacent segment pathology. They noted that the approach used in transforaminal endoscopic surgery allows surgeons to avoid re-entering scar tissue from previous surgeries, thereby reducing the risk of complications associated with scar tissue manipulation.21 This point is further supported by a study conducted by Telfeian et al.22 in 2020, which demonstrated that endoscopic techniques can achieve favorable outcomes in revision surgeries and even be completed under local anesthesia because of their ability to navigate around existing scar tissue within a single corridor.

Hudak and Perry reported on outpatient minimally invasive spine surgery using endoscopy for the treatment of lumbar spinal stenosis among obese patients, highlighting the adaptability of interlaminar endoscopic decompressions in patient populations that are more difficult to treat.23 They found that a significant reduction in the level of pain and disability among obese patients, with minimal blood loss and no complications.23 Bansal et al.24 further examined ESS as a potentially preferred treatment modality for obese patients. They conducted a meta-analysis of 8 studies with a subgroup analysis that analyzed the differences in outcomes among obese patients between open and endoscopic surgery. They found that while clinical outcomes were equivalent, operative times were significantly shorter for obese patients undergoing endoscopic surgery.24 This can potentially be attributed to the significantly shorter time for exposure and closure required in endoscopic surgery.

Interlaminar endoscopic surgery

The interlaminar approach provides access to the spinal canal through the interlaminar space, the opening between the lamina of adjacent vertebrae (Fig. 5).25 The ability to perform interlaminar endoscopic surgery with minimal tissue disruption contributes to reduced postoperative pain and shorter recovery times. This technique is particularly useful for central disc herniations and pathology in general that causes central or bilateral stenosis.25 The interlaminar approach offers a wider field of view compared with the transforaminal approach, especially in the lower lumbar spine, but it often requires more bone removal. This bone removal is similar to traditional open and tubular interlaminar laminotomies. It can be difficult to access the lower lumbar spine through the transforaminal approach as the crest can obstruct the corridor and the foramen in the lumbar spine becomes smaller as one moves lower in the lumbar spine. Conversely, the interlaminar window actually becomes larger as one moves lower in the lumbar spine. Therefore, the interlaminar approach became an attractive alternative at these levels. This approach through the interlaminar space allows for direct visualization and decompression of the thecal sac and nerve roots.26

Interlaminar endoscopic surgery has been shown to be effective in treating conditions such as lumbar spinal stenosis and foraminal stenosis, with studies indicating comparable outcomes to traditional open decompression techniques.25,26 McGrath et al. compared clinical outcomes after minimally invasive or lumbar endoscopic unilateral laminotomy for bilateral decompression, demonstrating the efficacy of interlaminar endoscopic surgery in achieving satisfactory results.27 Their study included 95 patients and reported that the endoscopic patients experienced a significantly greater improvement in their back and leg pain at 1-year follow-up. They also found that endoscopic patients had lower estimated blood loss and shorter hospital stays; however, their surgeries took significantly longer. Ruetten et al.25 conducted a prospective, randomized, controlled study comparing surgical treatment for lumbar lateral recess stenosis using the full-endoscopic interlaminar approach vs. conventional microsurgical techniques, further validating the effectiveness of interlaminar endoscopic surgery. They compared 161 patients and found that those undergoing interlaminar endoscopic surgery had fewer overall complications and equivalent leg pain at 2-year follow-up. They also measured back pain immediately postoperatively and found that open patients had significantly greater back pain during the initial postoperative period relative to endoscopic patients.25

Lee et al.26 performed a meta-analysis on the efficacy and safety of full-endoscopic decompression via the interlaminar approach for central or lateral recess spinal stenosis, analyzing 156 patients and concluding that interlaminar endoscopic surgery is a safe and effective option for these conditions. Their analysis, which included data from multiple studies, confirmed that interlaminar endoscopic surgery resulted in significant improvements in back and leg pain, as well as the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI).26

Lumbar fusion

Endoscopic lumbar fusion techniques have gained popularity as a minimally invasive alternative to traditional fusion methods. This technique combines the principles of endoscopic surgery with interbody fusion, allowing for direct access to the disc space while minimizing soft-tissue damage.28 An interesting benefit of endoscopic fusion surgery is that the surgeon is now able to directly visualize and evaluate endplate preparation. Endplate preparation represents one of the most critical steps of a fusion procedure to ensure bony union. Before endoscopy, decortication of the endplates was performed blindly or under fluoroscopic guidance. Now, the endoscope can assess the fusion bed through direct visualization.28

Wu et al.29 conducted a proof-of-concept study for uniportal endoscopic transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) in 27 patients followed for 1 year. They endoscopically removed the facet to access the disc space. Patients demonstrated significant improvements in postoperative pain, disc height, and segmental alignment.29 Kang et al. completed a retrospective study of 79 patients, comparing tubular TLIF with biportal endoscopic TLIF.30 In both groups, all clinical parameters improved significantly over baseline by 1 month after surgery, which lasted and were equivalent in both groups at the final follow-up examination at 1 year. Notably, back pain and 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) significantly improved in the endoscopic group faster than in the tubular group, with lower back pain scores and greater improvement in SF-36 scores at 1 month postoperatively.30 They also measured postoperative creatinine phosphokinase (CPK) and C-reactive protein (CRP) as a proxy for both tissue damage and inflammatory state related to pain. The endoscopic group had significantly lower postoperative CPK and CRP, suggesting less of an immediate postoperative inflammatory state in the endoscopic group. Both groups achieved equivalent satisfactory fusion rates at 1 year.30 The development of advanced instrumentation, such as endoscopic retractors and drills, has facilitated the safe and effective placement of interbody devices, further enhancing the technique's efficacy.28

Cervical endoscopic surgery

Cervical endoscopic surgery has emerged as a viable option for treating cervical disc herniations and foraminal stenosis.31 Posterior cervical spine exposure can involve significant soft-tissue damage and subsequent complications. An attempt to minimizing these exposure-related complications led to the development of endoscopic techniques in the cervical spine. The endoscopic approach allows for direct access to the posterior cervical spine, enabling decompression while minimizing tissue trauma (Fig. 6).31

Image demonstrating the endoscopic foraminotomy approach to the cervical spine.32

Ruetten et al.33 conducted a prospective, randomized, controlled study on full-endoscopic cervical posterior foraminotomy for lateral disc herniations, demonstrating the safety and efficacy of this technique. One-hundred seventy-five patients were randomized to either anterior cervical discectomy and fusion or posterior endoscopic foraminotomy with 2-year follow-up. The authors reported equivalent functional outcomes in both groups, with no significant differences in the reoperation rate or number of overall complications. Their study reported a success rate of 87.9% in relieving symptoms, with minimal complications.33 Owing to the maneuverability of the endoscope and the ability to manipulate optical field of view, endoscopic foraminotomy can potentially allow for a more thorough decompression of the cervical nerve root beyond the pedicle with minimal bony resection.33

Minamide et al.34 compared endoscopic decompression with conventional laminoplasty for cervical spondylotic myelopathy, which can require significant bilateral decompression. They followed 61 patients for 5 years. The endoscopic patients had significantly less blood loss, shorter operative times, and lower postoperative CK levels.34 The endoscopic group also achieved equivalent improvement in myelopathy scores with superior pain scores. In addition, while the laminoplasty group experienced a slight increase in cervical kyphosis over the 5-year observation period, the endoscopic group experienced a slight improvement in the cervical lordosis over that time.34 Kim et al.35 described the biportal technique for endoscopic unilateral laminotomy for bilateral decompression to treat cervical spondylotic myelopathy and demonstrated significant decompression with maintenance of stability. This technique guide further supports the effectiveness of these endoscopic approaches in managing cervical myelopathy.35

While studies have reported excellent clinical outcomes with posterior cervical endoscopic surgery, including significant pain relief and improved functional status, some surgeons are experimenting with additional endoscopic techniques in the cervical spine. Kotheeranurak et al.36 described their technique for successful placement of an endoscopic anterior odontoid screw for odontoid fractures. In addition, Ratre et al. and Chen et al. described their techniques for anterior cervical discectomy with disc preservation.37,38 The ability to perform these procedures in a less invasive fashion has made cervical endoscopic surgery an attractive option for patients seeking relief from cervical radiculopathy or even myelopathy.

Thoracic endoscopic surgery

Surgery for thoracic disc herniations and stenosis can be very challenging and fraught with potential complications.39,40 Depending on the location of the pathology, various approaches can be used. The more posterior approaches often require significant bony removal that can risk stability and may ultimately require fusion; however, anterior surgery can lead to complications related to opening the thoracic cavity as well as mobilization of ribs or large vessels.39,40 Thoracic endoscopic surgery is a less common but increasingly used technique for addressing thoracic disc herniations and spinal stenosis.41 The thoracic spine presents unique challenges due to its anatomical complexity, but advancements in endoscopic techniques have made it possible to perform thoracic decompressions in a less invasive manner.41,42

Ruetten et al.42 explored full-endoscopic uniportal decompression in disc herniations and stenosis of the thoracic spine, demonstrating the effectiveness of this approach. They conducted a study of 55 thoracic endoscopic patients, reviewing the 3 endoscopic approaches to the thoracic spine. They described the difference in access between the 3 approaches. The interlaminar approach can provide bilateral posterior access unlike the transforaminal approach. However, the transforaminal approach does allow for variations of anterior and posterior decompression.42 The transthoracic approach represents the most anterior access, which allows the surgeon to address bilateral anterior pathology. They found that sufficient decompression was achieved and myelopathy scores improved in all approaches with a relatively safe complication profile (Fig. 7).42

Image demonstrating the endoscopic approaches to the thoracic spine.42

Kang et al.43 provided insights into biportal endoscopic thoracic decompression for ossification of the ligamentum flavum, highlighting the feasibility and safety of this technique. Their findings indicated that patients experienced minimal complications and achieved satisfactory functional recovery.43 Within their described technique, they discuss the importance of ensuring proper bony removal of the medial portion of the caudal superior articular process to ensure lateral enough access to avoid the cord.43

Thoracic endoscopic surgery can achieve favorable clinical outcomes, including reduced postoperative pain and shorter recovery times, similar to those observed in lumbar and cervical procedures.41,42 The true benefit of this technique is in its potential to help avoid the complications related to thoracic spine exposures.40,41 The technique's efficacy is enhanced by the use of specialized instruments designed for thoracic applications, allowing for safe and effective decompression of thoracic pathologies.41–43

Uniportal vs. biportal

The choice between uniportal and biportal endoscopic surgery is a critical consideration in the planning and execution of endoscopic spine procedures. Each approach has its advantages and limitations, influencing the surgeon's decision based on the specific clinical scenario. This section will discuss the differences between uniportal and biportal techniques in terms of cost, learning curve, ability to address bilateral pathology, degree of invasiveness, and angle of vision (Fig. 8).

Table overview of comparative parameters between conventional microscopic, biportal endoscopic, and uniportal endoscopic surgery.

Both uniportal and biportal endoscopic surgery are considered techniques that minimize soft-tissue disruption; however, there are differences in the degree of invasiveness associated with each approach. Uniportal surgery only involves 1 incision and corridor to the pathology, which can lead to reduced postoperative pain and a quicker recovery. Choi et al.44 conducted a study to assess paraspinal muscle injury and pain postoperatively for patients undergoing lumbar microdiscectomy among open, biportal, and uniportal cases. C-reactive protein and CPK were found to have the most significant elevation postoperatively in open surgery, followed by biportal, and finally uniportal.44 Furthermore, they also compared preoperative and postoperative MRI to measure the cross-sectional area of high-intensity exposure-related lesions in the paraspinal musculature. Similar to the inflammatory markers, postoperative high-intensity lesions were the largest in the open group and smallest in the uniportal group, suggesting that uniportal surgery causes the least soft-tissue damage.44

Uniportal ESS also has an added potential benefit of being able to be completed under local anesthesia. Since this technique only involves a single corridor, often referred to as a keyhole approach, a simple injection of anesthetic along this direct path can potentially eliminate most procedural pain. This aspect makes uniportal endoscopy a potentially attractive option for patients seeking a less invasive surgical experience. Han et al.45 completed a meta-analysis of 549 patients, comparing uniportal endoscopic surgeries under local vs. general anesthesia. They found equivalent clinical outcomes with decreased costs and shorter hospital stays in the local anesthetic group.45

While uniportal surgery can provide potential benefits in invasiveness, biportal surgery can more effectively address bilateral pathology. Hua et al.46 compared clinical outcomes of 72 endoscopic patients, biportal vs. uniportal, who required bilateral decompression for lumbar spinal stenosis. They found equivalent leg pain, back pain, ODI, and Macnab scores. In their study, the biportal cases had a faster operative time.46 They attributed this to biportal surgery being better designed to treat bilateral pathology. The use of 2 portals allows surgeons to access both sides of the spinal canal, facilitating comprehensive decompression of neural structures. This capability is particularly advantageous in cases of bilateral foraminal stenosis or central canal stenosis, where thorough decompression is essential for optimal patient outcomes.

Biportal endoscopy enhances visualization and access to the pathology as it provides a wider working space through an independent portal, and the added ability to tilt the endoscope ensures sufficient exposure of the contralateral side, enabling effective decompression of the contralateral nerve root.46 The angle of vision provided by each technique is another critical factor influencing surgical outcomes. Uniportal endoscopy typically offers a limited angle of vision, which can hinder the surgeon's ability to visualize the surgical field adequately, especially in complex cases. Conversely, biportal endoscopy provides a wider angle of vision, allowing for enhanced visualization of the surgical field.47

Heo et al.47 completed a study of 97 patients, split into open, biportal, and uniportal surgery groups, to decompress lumbar stenosis. While most clinical outcomes were equivalent, the endoscopic cases achieved significantly lower back pain scores relative to the open cases.47 In addition, while dural expansion measured by comparing preoperative and postoperative MRI improved for all 3 groups, it improved more significantly for the biportal and open groups than it did for the uniportal group.47

The learning curve associated with each technique is a crucial factor influencing the adoption of ESS. Uniportal endoscopic surgery is often perceived as having a steeper learning curve because of the limited working space and the need for precise instrument manipulation within a single portal.48 Surgeons may find it challenging to achieve optimal outcomes without extensive training and experience.49 The learning curve associated with uniportal transforaminal endoscopic surgery can be steep, necessitating extensive training and experience to achieve optimal outcomes. While it can be difficult to learn, uniportal ESS can provide the least invasive soft tissue–sparing technique.49

Biportal ESS is generally considered to have a more manageable learning curve. The use of 2 portals allows for greater flexibility in instrument handling and improved visualization, which can facilitate the learning process for novice surgeons.49 This technique also represents a clearer direct evolution from open techniques. Furthermore, the biportal endoscopic instruments are already familiar to orthopedic surgeons as these are often similar to those used in knee and shoulder arthroscopy. Heo et al.47 highlighted that the biportal approach provides a more forgiving environment for instrument manipulation, making it easier for surgeons to perform complex maneuvers. This aspect can be particularly beneficial for those new to endoscopic techniques, as it allows for a smoother transition from traditional open surgery to minimally invasive approaches.

Cost is a significant factor when evaluating surgical techniques. Uniportal instrumentation is often more expensive, requiring a capital investment in new equipment due to the specialized instruments required for these procedures. By contrast, biportal endoscopic surgery often uses instrumentation that is not only cheaper but also more accessible.48 Many orthopedic practices already have access to arthroscopic instruments, which can be effectively used in biportal spine surgery. This accessibility can reduce the overall costs associated with biportal surgery, making it a more economically viable option.48 Conversely, uniportal surgery has the capability of being accomplished under local anesthesia, which can provide significant cost savings. Therefore, while instrumentation for biportal surgery can be more accessible and affordable, uniportal surgery in certain cases can become more cost-efficient by reducing the cost of anesthesia.45

The choice between uniportal and biportal endoscopic surgery involves a careful consideration of various factors, including cost, learning curve, ability to address bilateral pathology, degree of invasiveness, and angle of vision. While uniportal surgery may offer advantages regarding reduced invasiveness, biportal surgery provides enhanced visualization and flexibility, making it a valuable option for more complex cases. The adoption of uniportal and biportal ESS varies depending on surgeon preference, training, and the specific clinical scenario. Some surgeons prefer the minimal bony resection and opportunity to complete procedures under local anesthesia offered by uniportal surgery, while others find the instrument maneuverability, learning curve, and improved access to instrumentation of biportal surgery more appealing. Cost constraints can further complicate this debate as biportal surgery often represents a more accessible and affordable option for a surgeon beginning to use endoscopy. Ongoing research and clinical experience will help to further define the optimal applications for each technique. The development of new instruments and technologies may also influence the evolution and adoption of these approaches. A trend toward more personalized approaches, considering individual patient anatomy and pathology, will likely guide the selection of uniportal or biportal techniques in the future. More long-term comparative studies are needed to definitively establish the relative advantages and disadvantages of each technique in terms of clinical outcomes, complications, and cost-effectiveness.

Complications

Despite the advantages of ESS, complications can arise, necessitating careful consideration and management. However, the complication profile of ESS is generally considered more favorable compared with conventional open surgery. Evidence suggests fewer infections and a lower incidence of specific complications, such as dural tears requiring repair in ESS.

One of the significant advantages of ESS over traditional open surgery is the reduced risk of surgical site infections. A retrospective cohort study by Mahan et al.50 compared over 1,200 noninstrumented endoscopic spine surgeries with matched nonendoscopic National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) surgeries. In the matched data, the infection rates for nonendoscopic and endoscopic patients were 1.1% and 0.001%, respectively, which demonstrated a 16-time reduction.50 The minimally invasive nature of endoscopic techniques allows for smaller incisions, less soft-tissue disruption, and continuous irrigation, which in turn contribute to a lower incidence of infections.

While the continuous irrigation can lower the infection rate, there are concerns of water pressure around the spine. Studies have cited concerns for headache and even some citing risk of retroperitoneal fluid extravasation in transforaminal surgery.51 Kang et al.51 conducted an experiment in which they inserted cervical epidural catheters to measure intraoperative pressure during 27 biportal endoscopic lumbar microdiscectomies. After completion of each case, they obstructed the outflow and thereafter released the obstruction. The measured baseline pressure and the pressures during the case were not significantly different. In the deliberate obstruction phase, the pressure rose steadily with time until stopped at a proximately 60 mm Hg and then in the final phase after removal of the obstruction the pressure returned to baseline.51 No patient had any neurological complications, neck pain, or headache. They noted that maintaining inflow pressures of 30 mmHg while ensuring proper outflow could be considered a safe technique.51 Notably, if there is a very large dural tear at the start of the case, through which significant inflow would pass, the recommendation is to seal or repair this defect before proceeding.52

The incidence of incidental durotomy, a common complication in spine surgery, is reported to be lower in endoscopic procedures.52 While dural tears can occur during ESS, they often do not require repair as frequently as in open surgeries. Heo et al.53 noted that incidental durotomy during biportal endoscopic surgery can be managed effectively, and in many cases, the use of sutureless nonpenetrating clips can mitigate the need for extensive open repair. This contrasts with traditional open surgeries, where dural tears may necessitate more invasive repair techniques, potentially leading to increased morbidity. This was further examined by Lewandroski et al. who polled 93 endoscopic surgeons about their incidence of dural tears and how they manage them intraoperatively. The dural tear rate was approximately 1%, which included not just microdiscectomy but also decompressions for central stenosis.52 Notably, the risk of dural tear for just open microdiscectomy in the SPORT trial was 4%.54 Dural tears in endoscopic surgery have been found to often be less concerning complications than dural tears in open surgery. In the study by Dr. Lewandroski, over half of respondents noted their dural tears often do not require any closure.55 If they do need coverage often, they only need sealants to place over the defect. The maintenance of the soft-tissue envelope is thought to provide significant pressure to prevent cerebrospinal fluid leak.55 Patients with small to medium-sized dural tears often do well without any significant symptoms.

Compagnone et al.56 assessed the common complications in endoscopic surgery. They completed a systematic review and analyzed differences in complications among different types of endoscopic surgery. One hundred seventeen articles were assessed, including over 20,000 patients.56 They found increased rates of radicular pain or weakness in uniportal transforaminal surgery and an increased rate of dural tear in biportal interlaminar surgery.56 Uniportal transforaminal endoscopy often requires maneuvering around the dorsal root ganglion, which would have an increased risk of radicular pain and weakness.57 Furthermore, biportal interlaminar endoscopy often involves more dural retraction and addresses bilateral pathology, which would be more prone to dural tears.

While ESS is associated with a favorable complication profile, it is essential to acknowledge that complications can still occur. Common complications include incidental durotomy, nerve root injury, and postoperative hematoma. The enhanced visualization provided by endoscopic techniques can aid in the identification and avoidance of these complications. Continued advancements in endoscopic techniques, along with effective management strategies, contribute to the overall safety and efficacy of endoscopy in treating various spinal disorders.56

Learning curve

The learning curve associated with ESS is a critical factor influencing the successful implementation of these techniques in clinical practice. Understanding the challenges and strategies for overcoming the learning curve is essential for both novice and experienced surgeons. Endoscopic spine surgery presents unique challenges because of its technical complexity and the need for specialized skills in handling endoscopic instruments. The steep learning curve can result in variations in surgical outcomes, particularly among less experienced surgeons. Factors contributing to the learning curve include the intricacies of instrument manipulation, the need for precise visualization, and the ability to navigate the anatomic complexities of the spine.

Uniportal endoscopic surgery often takes longer than biportal surgery to master. Morgenstern et al.58 examined the learning curve in uniportal lumbar transforaminal endoscopic discectomy, identifying that proficiency—defined as achieving a 90% Macnab success rate—was reached after 72 cases. Once the learning curve was reached, each case took approximately 1 hour to complete.58 Lee et al.59 investigated the surgical learning curve in uniportal endoscopic decompression for lumbar stenosis. They found that it took approximately one hundred cases to achieve consistent outcomes without sacrificing significant operative time.59 They completed a subgroup analysis comparing outcomes before and after completion of the learning curve. They found significantly fewer complications and increased dural expansion after completion of the learning curve.59

Biportal endoscopic surgical techniques are more similar to traditional spine surgery compared with uniportal surgery. Biportal surgery often involves instrumentation familiar to orthopedic surgeons as the equipment is similar to that of knee and shoulder arthroscopy. Given the lack of extensive biportal training in neurosurgical residency, orthopedic surgeons may be more facile with this equipment when starting out. In addition, the dual-portal approach provides a means to navigate the spine that is more similar to open surgery. Peng et al.60 provided insights into learning curve dynamics in biportal endoscopic surgery. They conducted a systematic review of all learning curve studies for biportal cases. The average number of cases to complete the learning curve was 32.60 In addition, once the learning curve was reached, the average operative time for posterior cervical foraminotomy was approximately 1 hour, for lumbar decompression was approximately 1 hour and a half, and for lumbar fusion was slightly over 2 hours.60 Their findings indicated that the biportal approach allowed for a more forgiving learning curve, with surgeons achieving satisfactory outcomes more quickly compared with the uniportal approach.

To mitigate the challenges associated with the learning curve, several strategies can be used. As ESS becomes more popular, trainees will gain more significant exposure during residency and fellowship. This will allow the learning curve to begin before a surgeon practices on their own. In addition, workshops and hands-on training sessions can also facilitate knowledge sharing and skill acquisition among surgeons. Emphasizing the importance of continuous education and practice can further enhance the overall competency of surgeons performing ESS.

Cost-effectiveness

With costs rising and reimbursement declining, it is important to consider the cost-effectiveness of these new procedures. Proving cost-effectiveness continues to become more difficult because of the law of diminishing returns. This debate often attempts to differentiate between minimally invasive tubular decompression and endoscopic decompression. The margins of improvement have been shrunk with each subsequent advancement in technology. Evaluating the economic implications of ESS involves analyzing direct costs such as surgical costs, hospital stays, and medication cost, as well as indirect costs such as lost wages, decreased productivity, and staff replacement.

Direct costs associated with ESS often include the expenses related to instrumentation, operative time, and hospitalization. While endoscopic surgery may incur higher costs due to specialized instruments and initially longer operative times, studies have indicated that these costs can be offset by reduced hospitalization and postoperative care expenses.61 Gadjradj et al.61 conducted a cost-effectiveness analysis comparing microdiscectomy with endoscopic discectomy for lumbar disc herniation. Their findings revealed that while endoscopic procedures may have higher direct costs initially, the overall expenses related to hospitalization and postoperative care were lower, leading to equivocal direct costs between open and ESS.

Choi et al.62 completed a retrospective study of over 500 patients with 1-year follow-up, comparing uniportal, biportal, and open microdiscectomy in terms of cost-effectiveness. While all surgeries were cost-effective, the endoscopic cases were found to have a significantly improved cost-effectiveness relative to open spine surgery.62 Two of the most notable cost drivers in cost reduction were decreased direct hospital charges due to shorter hospital stays and decreased costs for anesthesia in transforaminal uniportal endoscopic surgery, as these procedures could be completed under local anesthesia alone.62

Golan et al.63 completed a systematic review of costs in ESS. While many studies comparing direct costs of endoscopic and open spine surgery vary slightly, overall the hospital stay often costs less for endoscopic patients, which is offset by the surgery costing more in this cohort. This led to an equivocal result in direct costs between open and endoscopic surgery.63 Indirect costs associated with ESS also play a significant role in its overall cost-effectiveness. Unlike their direct cost analysis, the indirect cost analysis was consistent in the few studies that assessed societal costs. Patients undergoing endoscopic procedures often return to work faster than those undergoing traditional open surgeries.61 Endoscopic surgery provides indirect financial benefit by allowing patients to return to work faster and be more productive once they return to work.63 The earlier return to work not only benefits patients but also reduces the economic burden on society by minimizing lost productivity.

The cost-effectiveness of ESS is influenced by both direct and indirect costs. The initial direct costs may be equivocal or even higher for endoscopic procedures; however, the overall economic implications, including reduced hospitalization and faster return to work, contribute to its favorable cost-effectiveness profile.

Enabling technology

Advancements in technology have played a pivotal role in the evolution of ESS, enhancing the capabilities and outcomes of these procedures.64 While endoscopic surgery has significant potential benefits such as less soft-tissue trauma, lower blood loss, reduced hospital stays, and earlier functional recovery, all of these are irrelevant if surgeons cannot withstand the steep learning curve. Fortunately, there are new enabling technologies that have helped improve the early stages of the learning curve. Emerging technologies continue to shape the landscape of endoscopy, offering new opportunities for improved patient care.65

Navigation systems are emerging technologies that hold promise for improving the accuracy and safety of endoscopic spine procedures.66,67 These systems have the potential to enhance the precision of instrument placement by providing real-time guidance during surgery.66 Studies have also assessed how navigation affects radiation and operative time. Gong et al.68 conducted a retrospective study assessing 64 patients undergoing endoscopic TLIF with and without navigation. The 2 groups had equivalent clinical outcomes, with an approximately 85% reduction in surgical team radiation exposure in the navigation group.68 Their study demonstrated that the use of navigation systems not only improved surgical accuracy but also significantly decreased radiation exposure to the surgical team, thereby enhancing safety during procedures. Furthermore, despite the navigation group requiring increased time for navigation setup, the remainder of the surgery was more efficient for the navigation group and, therefore, the navigation group ultimately had a significantly faster overall surgical time than the non-navigation group.68

Navigation can provide significant help during endoscopic surgery, especially during the early stages of the learning curve.69 It can help throughout the entire procedure from guiding the ideal incision and trajectory and confirming localization, ensuring proper decompression by validating removal of bony landmarks.66,69 Ao et al.69 conducted a prospective cohort study assessing endoscopic lumbar discectomy with and without navigation from a learning curve perspective. While the 2 groups ultimately demonstrated equivalent clinical outcomes, the endoscopic surgeons completed their learning curve significantly faster and demonstrated shorter operative times.69

Robotic guidance has also been assessed as an enabling technology in ESS. Wang et al.70 conducted a retrospective study on 55 patients undergoing endoscopic lumbar discectomy with and without robotic assistance. The ideal trajectory was mapped onto the robot for lumbar discectomies. The robotic arm guided the scope to the corridor, which was designed to perfectly access the disc herniation. The 2 groups had equivalent outcomes and operative times despite the robot setup time.70 In addition, the robotic group demonstrated a significant reduction in surgical team fluoroscopic radiation exposure.70

Technological advances are also being made in visualization. The transition from tubular surgery to endoscopic surgery proved to be a significant leap in terms of being able to visualize the anatomic structure. The higher resolution now provided by the transition 4K cameras allows for clearer and more detailed images of the surgical field, facilitating better identification of anatomical structures and enhancing the intraoperative precision (Fig. 9).66 Heo et al.71 experimented with 3D technology to improve depth perception during ESS. The camera projects a 3D image into glasses worn by the surgeon. They conducted a survey-based study asking surgeons whether they would prefer 2D or 3D for each individual stage of the procedure. After a preference for 2D for the early exposure stages, 90% of respondents preferred 3D visualization for the decompression stages.71 The view offered by 3D endoscopy allows for better depth perception and spatial awareness, facilitating more precise surgical maneuvers.71

Conclusion

Endoscopic spine surgery represents a significant advancement in the treatment of spinal disorders, offering numerous benefits over traditional open techniques. As the field continues to evolve, ongoing research, technological innovation, and enhanced training will be crucial for optimizing patient outcomes and expanding the applications of ESS. The future of ESS is promising, with ongoing research and technological advancements poised to further enhance the capabilities and applications of these techniques.

As these developments evolve, the indications for ESS are expected to expand.72 Research is ongoing to explore the efficacy of endoscopy in treating a broader range of conditions, including complex degenerative diseases and spinal tumors.73 The development of new techniques and technologies will likely facilitate the safe and effective treatment of these challenging cases.

The establishment of formalized training programs and educational initiatives will be crucial for ensuring the widespread adoption of ESS.74 Collaborative efforts among surgical societies, academic institutions, and industry partners can facilitate the development of standardized training curricula, mentorship programs, and simulation-based training. Enhancing the educational framework for ESS will contribute to improved surgeon competency and patient outcomes.

Ongoing technological innovation will play a pivotal role in shaping the future of ESS. The integration of artificial intelligence, machine learning, and advanced imaging techniques will likely enhance surgical planning, intraoperative decision making, and postoperative care. Continued investment in research and development will drive the evolution of endoscopic techniques, ultimately improving patient safety and outcomes.
