Internalizing symptoms are indirectly associated with simultaneous alcohol and cannabis use through specific motives for simultaneous use: A longitudinal study of young adults
Authors: Jeffrey D Wardell, Kyra N Farrelly, Annabelle Moore, Nicolle Fox, Sophie G Coelho, John A Cunningham, Roisin M O'Connor, Christian S Hendershot
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study examined motivational pathways between internalizing symptoms (i.e., depression, anxiety, stress) and simultaneous alcohol and cannabis use among young adults.MethodsParticipants (N = 151; 64% female, Mean age = 22.07) completed baseline questionnaires assessing internalizing symptoms and simultaneous use motives, and then reported their alcohol and cannabis use each day for 21 days. Participants repeated these procedures again 6 months and 12 months postbaseline. Daily survey responses were used to calculate the number of simultaneous use days involving heavy drinking (4 or more drinks for females; 5 or more drinks for males) and light drinking at each wave for each participant. The total number of negative consequences reported across all simultaneous use days was also calculated for each participant at each wave.ResultsMultilevel mediation analyses revealed that within‐person increases in internalizing symptoms (a latent factor consisting of depression, anxiety, and stress indicators) at a given wave were indirectly associated with (a) a greater number of heavy drinking simultaneous use days (controlling for number of cannabis‐only days and heavy drinking alcohol‐only days) and (b) greater negative consequences on simultaneous use days (controlling for negative consequences on cannabis‐only and alcohol‐only days). These within‐person associations were mediated by increases in positive (i.e., reward/enhancement) motives for simultaneous use. At the between‐person level, greater average internalizing symptoms (aggregated across waves) were indirectly associated with more light drinking simultaneous use days via coping motives, and with fewer heavy drinking simultaneous use days via conformity motives (controlling for frequency of single substance use).ConclusionsYoung adults may combine cannabis with heavy episodic drinking more frequently during periods when they experience elevations in internalizing symptoms, mediated by a desire to achieve the positive/enhancing effects of simultaneous use. Findings may inform alcohol and cannabis harm reduction interventions tailored for young adults with internalizing symptoms.Keywords: alcohol, anxiety, co‐use, depression, marijuana

INTRODUCTION

Simultaneous use of alcohol and cannabis (i.e., using alcohol and cannabis at the same time and/or with overlapping effects) is common among young adults and is associated with elevations in both acute and chronic harms relative to using alcohol or cannabis on their own (for recent reviews, see Farrelly et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2022). The high prevalence of simultaneous use among young adults has motivated research on risk factors with the goal of informing prevention and intervention efforts. Among these, internalizing symptoms (e.g., anxiety, depression) and simultaneous use motives (e.g., coping, enhancement) have received research attention. Yet, no studies to our knowledge have provided a comprehensive examination of motivational pathways from internalizing symptoms to simultaneous use outcomes, and so the motives that might explain the link between internalizing symptoms and simultaneous use remain unclear. This study aimed to address this gap by examining simultaneous use motives as mediators of within‐person associations between internalizing symptoms and simultaneous use behavior and consequences over a period of 1 year.

Internalizing symptoms and simultaneous use

There is a rich literature linking internalizing symptoms, such as depression and anxiety, with elevations in problematic alcohol and cannabis use behavior. For example, alcohol use disorder (AUD) and cannabis use disorder (CUD) are highly comorbid with major depressive disorder, most anxiety disorders, and PTSD (Ait Bouighoulidne et al., 2024; Brière et al., 2014; Hasin & Walsh, 2020; Kedzior & Laeber, 2014; Lai et al., 2015; McHugh & Weiss, 2019; Smith & Cottler, 2018). Further, many studies of nonclinical samples of adolescents and young adults have observed links between internalizing symptoms and use of alcohol and cannabis along with associated negative consequences (for reviews, see Hussong et al., 2017; Schry & White, 2013; Single et al., 2022). However, findings are also mixed, with some studies finding null or even negative associations between certain internalizing symptoms (e.g., social anxiety) and frequency or quantity of alcohol and cannabis use among youth and young adults (Hussong et al., 2017; Schry & White, 2013; Single et al., 2022). Further, many studies have failed to find an event‐level association between negative affect and alcohol or cannabis use in the daily lives of young adults (Dora et al., 2023; Sznitman et al., 2022). Thus, the links between internalizing symptoms and alcohol and cannabis use in this population are complex and require further clarification, particularly given the tendency for young adults to combine alcohol and cannabis.

Indeed, few studies have examined how internalizing symptoms relate specifically to the simultaneous use of alcohol and cannabis. There is some (albeit mixed) evidence from laboratory studies that the coadministration of alcohol and cannabis results in greater sedation and mood enhancement relative to administering either substance on its own (Bedillion et al., 2024; Fares et al., 2022). Thus, perhaps individuals with elevated depression or anxiety symptoms may gravitate toward simultaneous use because it offers greater relief from anxious arousal, negative affect, or low mood than single substance use, reinforcing simultaneous use for these individuals. Indeed, a handful of studies have found that depression and anxiety are associated with an increased likelihood or frequency of engaging in simultaneous use relative to using cannabis or alcohol on their own (e.g., Fleming et al., 2021; Lipperman‐Kreda et al., 2018; Romm et al., 2024; Vieira et al., 2024), suggesting that internalizing symptoms may be an important risk factor for simultaneous use specifically. However, motivational processes in the association between internalizing symptoms and simultaneous use remain to be examined.

Simultaneous use motives

Motivational models of substance use describe key motivational constructs that underlie substance use behavior and posit that distinct motives serve to mediate the influence of distal risk factors (e.g., personality, mental health diagnoses) on substance use outcomes (Cox & Klinger, 1988; Votaw & Witkiewitz, 2021). Research has identified motives that are common to both alcohol and cannabis, including social motives (i.e., using to facilitate social interaction), enhancement motives (i.e., using for enjoyment and to increase positive emotions), conformity motives (i.e., using to fit in with others), and coping motives (i.e., using to cope with distress; Cooper et al., 1995; Simons et al., 1998). Some research has also described motives unique to cannabis use, such as expansion motives (e.g., using to heighten awareness/creativity; Simons et al., 1998), and motives related to alleviation of boredom and physical or mental health symptoms (e.g., Haug et al., 2017; Lankenau et al., 2018). Motivational models posit that internally focused motives (i.e., coping and enhancement) play an important role in substance use among people who are especially motivated to use substances to regulate internal affective states (Cooper et al., 1995). Consistent with this model, many studies show that coping motives for alcohol or cannabis use mediate associations of internalizing symptoms with alcohol or cannabis use behavior, respectively (e.g., Bravo et al., 2018, 2019; Buckner et al., 2012; Matei‐Mitacu et al., 2024; Scarfe et al., 2022; Wardell et al., 2020). Further, coping motives for alcohol and cannabis show strong associations with negative consequences of substance use (such as risky behaviors, dependence symptoms, and social and academic consequences; e.g., Bravo et al., 2019; Buckner et al., 2012; Colder et al., 2019; Cooper et al., 2016), and thus coping motives may be a mechanism linking elevated internalizing symptoms with alcohol and cannabis problems. This is consistent with the self‐medication hypothesis (Khantzian, 1997), which posits that use of substances to cope with mental health symptoms is a risk factor for substance‐related problems.

However, several studies suggest that coping motives are not the only motives that play a role in this association. For example, in a cross‐sectional study of college students, Bravo et al. (2020) found that internalizing symptoms, measured with the Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scales (DASS; Henry & Crawford, 2005), were associated with coping, conformity, and enhancement motives for both alcohol and cannabis (assessed separately). Further, both coping and enhancement drinking motives, and both coping and expansion cannabis motives, mediated associations of internalizing symptoms with alcohol or cannabis use, respectively. Other studies have also found that enhancement motives mediate the association between internalizing symptoms and either alcohol or cannabis use (e.g., Buckner et al., 2006; Scarfe et al., 2022). The importance of enhancement and expansion motives in this link may reflect the use of substances not only to cope with distress, but also to boost mood and experience pleasure in the presence of low mood, which would align with the self‐medication hypothesis. Accordingly, because enhancement motives are linked with heavier use of alcohol and cannabis, they are also predictive of negative consequences associated with the use of these substances (e.g., Cooper et al., 2016; Merrill & Read, 2010; Simons et al., 2005).

Although there is an extensive literature on alcohol and cannabis motives, only recently have researchers examined motives specific to simultaneous alcohol and cannabis use. Much of this research has used ecological momentary assessment (EMA) to examine motives that immediately precede simultaneous use, with social and enhancement motives showing consistent associations with the likelihood of engaging in simultaneous use over single substance use at the event level (Arterberry et al., 2021; Jackson et al., 2021; Patrick et al., 2019). Coping motives also have shown associations with simultaneous use behavior and negative consequences on simultaneous use days in some of these studies (Patrick et al., 2019, 2020). Further, motives specific to simultaneous use (e.g., to enhance subjective effects by combining alcohol and cannabis) have also been shown to predict simultaneous use in EMA studies (Patrick et al., 2020). However, EMA studies typically assess motives using single items, which may have limited reliability and construct validity. Patrick et al. (2018) developed the Simultaneous Alcohol and Marijuana (SAM) Motives Questionnaire, which provides a reliable and comprehensive assessment of simultaneous use motives. The SAM motives questionnaire has a factor structure similar to existing measures of alcohol and cannabis motives, with social, coping, conformity, and positive (primarily reflecting mood or subjective effect enhancement or intensification) motives subscales. Importantly, individual differences in most of these SAM motives have been shown to predict unique variance in simultaneous use behavior and negative consequences over and above separate alcohol and cannabis motives (Conway et al., 2020; Patrick et al., 2018).

However, no studies to our knowledge have examined SAM motives as mediators of the link between internalizing symptoms and simultaneous use outcomes. This is an important research gap, as it may not be possible to extrapolate findings from studies of separate alcohol and cannabis motives to understand motivational processes that are specific to simultaneous use. Research on motivational pathways linking internalizing symptoms with simultaneous use is important for informing interventions that are tailored for young adults who engage in simultaneous use, a population at greater risk for harms from substance use relative to their peers who use only one of these substances.

Current study

This study examined indirect pathways from internalizing symptoms to simultaneous use behavior and consequences via specific simultaneous use motives. Data were from a larger longitudinal study of young adults who engage in simultaneous use in Ontario, Canada (Wardell et al., 2024), where both alcohol and cannabis are legal for adults ages 19 and older. Participants in the study completed measures of internalizing symptoms and SAM motives at 3 time points spaced 6 months apart. An intensive longitudinal burst design was used to capture patterns of simultaneous use and consequences at each time point using daily surveys administered for a 3‐week period immediately following the assessment of internalizing symptoms and motives. This allowed us to examine whether within‐person changes in internalizing symptoms during a 1‐year period predicted corresponding changes in simultaneous use patterns, and whether changes in SAM motives mediated these associations.

We forwarded the following hypotheses based on theory and previous research: (1) Within‐person changes in internalizing symptoms would be positively associated with corresponding changes in coping and positive SAM motives; (2) Within‐person increases in positive, coping, and social SAM motives assessed prior to each EMA burst would be associated with increased frequency of simultaneous use and greater negative consequences across simultaneous use days during the ensuing EMA burst (controlling for frequency of, and consequences on, alcohol‐only and cannabis‐only days, respectively); and (3) Coping and positive SAM motives would mediate the within‐person associations of internalizing symptoms with simultaneous use frequency and consequences (controlling for alcohol‐only and cannabis‐only frequency and consequences, respectively). We limited our hypotheses to within‐person associations given our focus on longitudinal pathways, but we planned to examine between‐person associations in exploratory analyses.

Further, prior research has shown that the amount of alcohol consumed on simultaneous use days is important for understanding risk for negative consequences (e.g., Boyle et al., 2024; Wardell et al., 2024). For example, simultaneous use days involving heavy drinking (i.e., 4 or more drinks for females, 5 or more drinks for males) tend to be associated with more negative consequences than simultaneous use days involving light drinking (Mallett et al., 2019; Waddell et al., 2023), but findings for differences in negative consequences across heavy drinking simultaneous use days and heavy drinking alcohol‐only days have been mixed (Boyle et al., 2024; Mallett et al., 2019; Waddell et al., 2023). Given that several prior studies have isolated heavy and light drinking simultaneous use days, we planned to examine heavy drinking and light drinking simultaneous use days as separate outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants

Young adults (N = 151) were recruited from across Ontario, Canada using advertisements on social media; participants were also recruited locally in the Toronto area through fliers posted on campuses and in public spaces. All participants were above the legal age for purchasing both alcohol and cannabis (age 19 in Ontario) at the time of data collection, with a mean age of 22.07 (SD = 2.10). Sixty‐four percent (n = 96) were assigned female at birth; at baseline, 60% (n = 90) identified as a woman, 38% (n = 57) identified as a man, 3% (n = 5) identified as nonbinary, and 1% (n = 2) identified as transgender. A majority (62%; n = 94) identified as White, followed by 16% (n = 24) identifying as Asian and 9% (n = 13) identifying as Black/African/African descent; other racial/ethnic labels were identified by fewer than 5% of the sample. The median annual household income at baseline was in the $40,000 to $49,999 range, and 70% (n = 107) were enrolled as either full‐time or part‐time students. See Wardell et al. (2024) for additional participant characteristics.

Interested individuals completed an online eligibility screening survey. Eligibility criteria included: being 19–25 years of age; reporting at least weekly use of both cannabis and alcohol in the past month; using both cannabis and alcohol within the same 2‐h period at least twice during the past month; and owning an Android or iOS phone. Participants were excluded if they reported using drugs other than cannabis, alcohol, or nicotine more than once per month; were currently seeking treatment for or attempting to reduce cannabis or alcohol use; reported a diagnosis of psychosis, mania, or a neurodevelopmental disorder; or reported using cannabis for medicinal purposes only. Although 176 participants were enrolled in the study, n = 17 were withdrawn during the baseline assessment due to ineligibility, and n = 9 were withdrawn from the longitudinal follow‐ups for completing <10% of the prompted EMA surveys, leaving a final sample of N = 151.

Procedures

The study received ethics approval from York University and the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health, Toronto, Canada. Eligible participants who provided informed consent were enrolled in an ecological momentary assessment study with a longitudinal burst design that included three 21‐day EMA bursts scheduled 6 months apart. Prior to each EMA burst, participants attended an assessment session (occurring via videoconferencing in most cases), during which they completed questionnaires (administered via an online survey; see Measures) assessing demographics, internalizing symptoms, simultaneous use motives, and other constructs that are not the focus of the current analyses. Participants also were instructed on how to install the EMA app (MetricWire, Inc., Waterloo, ON) onto their mobile devices and completed an interactive tutorial introducing the EMA protocol. During the tutorial, participants were trained to report alcohol consumption in standard drink units and quantities of cannabis flower in grams using infographics and personalized examples.

The day after each assessment session, participants began the 21‐day EMA period, which involved completing a survey each morning assessing the previous day's alcohol and cannabis use and associated consequences (see Measures). This daily morning survey was available in the app between 7 am and 2 pm each day, and reminder notifications were sent to participants at several times leading up to survey expiry. The EMA protocol also involved event‐contingent surveys during substance use episodes and twice daily randomly timed surveys that served to remind participants to complete event‐contingent surveys. However, because it is not possible to determine compliance with event‐contingent surveys, we focused on the daily morning surveys (which had compliance rates of 88% to 92% across waves) to estimate the frequency of cannabis use, alcohol use, and simultaneous use (which was reported retrospectively for the previous day on each morning survey) across the burst period.

Participants were provided with cash or gift cards worth $40, $50, and $60 for the baseline, 6‐month, and 12‐month assessment sessions, respectively, and up to $90, $105, and $120 for participating in the baseline, 6‐month, and 12‐month EMA assessments, respectively, with amounts varying based on compliance with the EMA surveys.

Measures

Demographics

Participants completed an online survey prior to each EMA burst that included items assessing age, sex, gender, race, ethnicity, income, student status, and other demographic variables.

Internalizing symptoms

Prior to each EMA burst, participants completed the 21‐item version of the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS‐21; Henry & Crawford, 2005), which assessed internalizing symptoms over the past week. The DASS‐21 consists of three 7‐item subscales: depression (e.g., “I couldn't seem to experience any positive feeling at all”), anxiety (e.g., “I felt scared without any good reason”), and stress (e.g., “I found it difficult to relax”), which can be combined to form a higher order factor representing general distress (Henry & Crawford, 2005). Participants responded to each item using a 4‐point scale (0 = Did not apply to me at all, to 3 = Applied to me very much, or most of the time). Each subscale was scored by summing the relevant items. The DASS‐21 subscales exhibited good internal consistency across waves (see Table 1 for coefficient alphas).

Simultaneous alcohol and cannabis use motives

Prior to each EMA burst, participants completed the Simultaneous Alcohol and Marijuana Use (SAM) Motives Questionnaire (Patrick et al., 2018). Participant were asked to rate the extent to which they used alcohol and cannabis simultaneously for each of 26 reasons on a 5‐point scale (1 = Almost never/never to 5 = Almost always/always). The SAM Motives Questionnaire includes four subscales: conformity (e.g., “Because others are doing it”), positive effects (e.g., “To get a better high”), calm/coping (e.g., “To calm me down”), and social (e.g., “To be sociable”). Each subscale was scored by calculating the mean of the relevant items. The SAM motives scales exhibited good internal consistency across waves (see Table 1, for coefficient alphas).

Cannabis, alcohol, and simultaneous use frequency

During each 21‐day EMA burst, daily morning surveys asked participants to report if they had used alcohol, cannabis, or any other drug on the previous day (i.e., between the time they woke up and the time they went to bed). When prior day alcohol use was reported, participants were asked to record the number of standard drinks they consumed the previous day, and a standard drink conversion chart was linked to this item on the survey. On days when cannabis was used, participants were asked to report the type (i.e., flower, concentrates, edibles/beverages) and quantities (grams for flower, number of hits for concentrates, number of edible/beverage servings). Simultaneous use was assessed with a yes/no item reading, “Did you use both alcohol and cannabis in the same 2‐h period yesterday?”

For analyses, simultaneous use frequency at each wave was represented by the number of daily surveys on which the participant reported using alcohol and cannabis in the same 2‐h period, calculated separately for each EMA burst. This was further divided into the number of heavy drinking (4+/5+ standard drinks for females/males) and the number of light drinking (<4/5 standard drinks for females/males) simultaneous use days. We also calculated the number of heavy drinking and light drinking alcohol‐only days (on which no cannabis was used) as well as the number of cannabis‐only days (on which no alcohol was used). Given that the majority of cannabis use days (74%) involved cannabis flower use, we did not subdivide these days based on the type of cannabis used; we also did not subdivide cannabis days based on quantities consumed given challenges with equating quantities across forms of cannabis and a lack of data on the potency of each form of cannabis used.

Negative consequences

When participants reported using either alcohol or cannabis the previous day on the daily survey, they were also asked to respond to items assessing negative consequences adapted from the Marijuana Consequences Questionnaire (MACQ; Simons et al., 2012) and the Young Adult Alcohol Consequences Questionnaire (YAACQ; Read et al., 2006; see Wardell et al., 2024 for more detailed description). Participants indicated which of the consequences they experienced since they began using alcohol or cannabis the previous day. Most items were not substance‐specific, and participants were asked whether the consequence occurred following “alcohol and/or cannabis use.” However, 3 items were specific to alcohol and cannabis (e.g., I drove a vehicle after using [alcohol/cannabis]), and participants responded to the items separately for each substance. On simultaneous use days, we collapsed these substance‐specific items so that they were counted only once (e.g., the impaired driving consequence was considered present if it was endorsed with respect to either alcohol or cannabis).

We derived an index of negative consequences on simultaneous use days for each burst by summing the number of consequences reported across all simultaneous use days captured during the burst. Similar indices of consequences on alcohol‐only and cannabis‐only days were calculated by summing the number of consequences reported across all alcohol‐only and cannabis‐only days, respectively.

Data analysis

To examine whether simultaneous use motives explained the association between internalizing symptoms and simultaneous use frequency across time, we specified multilevel mediation models in Mplus v.8.8, with burst‐level variables (Level 1) nested within participants (Level 2). These models allowed us to isolate within‐person associations of changes in internalizing symptoms with SAM motives across waves and examine how they predicted simultaneous use frequency observed during the corresponding EMA bursts. This also allowed us to model between‐person associations among individual differences in average levels of these variables across time, disaggregating these from within‐person longitudinal associations. The Bayes estimator was used, which produces 95% credibility intervals (CI) that can be interpreted like confidence intervals when noninformative priors are used (Muthén, 2010). The Bayes estimator can accommodate missing data (using all available observations rather than listwise deletion) and is appropriate when normality cannot be assumed (as CIs are asymmetric). All models were run with 50,000 iterations and thinned by a factor of 10. A Potential Scale Reduction (PSR) value of 1.000 to 1.001 was observed consistently across nearly 10,000 iterations in all models, supporting model convergence.

In one model, the frequency of heavy drinking and light drinking simultaneous use days (aggregated at the burst level) was specified as parallel outcomes, and in a separate model, negative consequences on simultaneous use days (aggregated at the burst‐level) was specified as the outcome. All outcomes were specified as count variables and were modeled with a negative binomial distribution given statistically significant dispersion factors were observed for each (95% CIs did not contain zero). Both models included the 4 SAM motive scales as parallel mediators at both levels of the models, on which the outcome variables were regressed. Because the three subscales of the DASS‐21 were highly intercorrelated (rs ranged from 0.66 to 0.69 at T1, 0.74 to 0.80 at T2, and 0.78 to 0.80 at T3), these subscales were specified as indicators of a latent Internalizing Symptoms factor at both level 1 and level 2 of the models, which served as the independent variable on which the outcome and mediator variables were regressed. This approach is consistent with psychometric work indicating that a general internalizing symptom factor underlies all items on the DASS‐21 (Henry & Crawford, 2005).

To determine whether associations were specific to simultaneous use frequency, the number of heavy drinking and light drinking alcohol‐only days was specified as covariates predicting the number of heavy drinking and light drinking simultaneous use days, respectively. The number of cannabis‐only days was also specified as a covariate predicting the number of heavy and light drinking simultaneous use days. Similarly, in the model with negative consequences across simultaneous use days as the outcome, negative consequences across alcohol‐only days and negative consequences across cannabis‐only days were specified as covariates. Further, the negative consequence model was rerun, including heavy and light drinking simultaneous use frequency as additional covariates to examine whether any observed associations with negative consequences on simultaneous use days were accounted for by the frequency of simultaneous use. Time since the baseline assessment was included as a level 1 covariate in all models, coded as days since the baseline assessment divided by 365 days (to put it into units of years). Age and sex were initially included as level 2 covariates, but they did not show significant associations with any of the mediator or outcome variables, so they were removed from models for parsimony.

To disaggregate within‐person and between‐person variance in the motives variables and internalizing symptoms indicators, we person‐mean‐centered these variables on Level 1 and included person‐level means of these variables (which were grand‐mean‐centered) on Level 2. Models were specified according to guidance provided by Preacher et al. (2010), which included constraining the means of the intercepts for the Level 1 mediators and latent factor indicators to zero (as these were held constant through centering). Indirect paths were specified as the product of the coefficients for the paths from internalizing symptoms to motives (a paths) and from motives to each outcome variable (b paths), using the MODEL CONTRAINT command in Mplus. In both models, residual variances for the motives variables were allowed to freely covary at Level 1, and random intercept variances for the motives variables were allowed to freely covary at Level 2. In the model with simultaneous use frequency as the outcome, the random intercept variances for the heavy and light drinking simultaneous use frequency variables also freely covaried with one another. Residual covariances were not specified for the indicators of the internalizing symptoms factor to aid in model identification.

RESULTS

The study retention rate was 72% (n = 109) at the 6‐month follow‐up and 66% (n = 99) at the 12‐month follow‐up. One participant was missing data on the DASS scales at Wave 1, and two participants were missing data on the DASS scales and SAM motives questionnaire at Wave 2, but listwise deletion was avoided with the use of the Bayes estimator. Participants who were missing one or more follow‐ups (n = 60) had significantly higher baseline scores on positive SAM motives than participants who completed both follow‐ups (n = 91), t(149) = 2.30, p = 0.023, but these groups did not differ on any other SAM motives, nor on any other baseline DASS scales or substance use frequency and consequence variables used in the models (all p > 0.05). A total of 6749 daily surveys were completed across all participants and all EMA bursts, with completion rates ranging from 88% to 92% across the three 21‐day bursts.

Table 1 shows descriptive statistics for the burst‐level substance use frequency variables as well as SAM motives and DASS scales at each time point. Across the three EMA bursts, the average number of heavy drinking SAM days ranged from 0.71 to 0.96 and the average number of light drinking SAM days ranged from 1.18 to 1.54 (Table 1). Although means suggest low frequency of simultaneous use, there was a wide range observed (0–12 and 0–17 for baseline heavy and light drinking simultaneous use days, respectively, out of a possible range of 0–21). The average number of light drinking and heavy drinking alcohol‐only days was slightly lower than the corresponding number of heavy and light drinking simultaneous use days, but the average number of cannabis‐only days was much higher across all time points (ranging from 3.40 to 4.72). Across all participants and time points, participants endorsed using both alcohol and cannabis within the same 2‐h period (i.e., simultaneous use) on 802 days. Given the small number of days on which both alcohol and cannabis were used but not within the same 2‐h period (n = 87 across all participants and time points), these days were not incorporated into our models. Further, 33% of cannabis use days were simultaneous use days (67% were cannabis‐only days), whereas 64% of alcohol use days were simultaneous use days (64% were alcohol‐only days). Average total negative consequences summed across all simultaneous use days ranged from 1.77 to 2.62 across time points (see Table 1).

The intraclass correlation coefficients for the mediator and outcome variables in the model ranged from 0.58 for conformity motives to 0.82 for light drinking simultaneous use frequency, suggesting that a majority of the variance in these variables was between‐person variance, although there was still a meaningful amount of within‐person variance.

Model predicting frequency of heavy and light drinking simultaneous use days

Figure 1 shows the path model of the indirect associations between internalizing symptoms and simultaneous use frequency outcomes mediated by SAM motives, and full results of the model are found in Table 2. The three subscales of the DASS loaded highly on the latent Internalizing Symptoms factor at both within‐person and between‐person levels of the model. As shown in Figure 1, within‐person increases in internalizing symptoms were positively associated with corresponding increases in all four SAM motives scales, such that participants reported higher SAM motives at time points when they also reported higher levels of internalizing symptoms (relative to their own average levels). In turn, within‐person increases in positive SAM motives at a given timepoint predicted increased frequency of heavy drinking simultaneous use days during the immediately following EMA burst (controlling for number of heavy drinking alcohol‐only days and number of cannabis‐only days). Further, the within‐person indirect association between internalizing symptoms and increased frequency of heavy drinking simultaneous use mediated via positive SAM motives was significant (estimate = 0.015; 95% CI [>0.000, 0.043]). Contrary to hypotheses, social and coping motives did not show significant associations with simultaneous use frequency at the within‐person level, nor were conformity motives associated with frequency outcomes, and none of the within‐person indirect associations through the other motives scales were significant at the within‐person level. Further, internalizing symptoms were not directly associated with either simultaneous use frequency variable at the within‐person level after accounting for SAM motives.

Results at the between‐person level of the model showed that participants who reported higher internalizing symptoms on average across time points had higher scores on all four SAM motives scales averaged across time (Figure 1). In turn, participants who reported greater coping SAM motives on average across time engaged in more light drinking (but not heavy drinking) simultaneous use days overall (controlling for number of cannabis‐only and light drinking alcohol‐only days), and there was a significant between‐person indirect association between greater average internalizing symptoms and a greater number of light drinking simultaneous use days that was mediated by greater average coping motives (aggregated across waves; estimate = 0.053, 95% CI [0.012, 0.108]). None of the other SAM motives were significantly associated with the number of light drinking simultaneous use days at the between‐person level. Interestingly, conformity motives were negatively associated with the frequency of heavy drinking simultaneous use days at the between‐person level (controlling for number of cannabis‐only and heavy drinking alcohol‐only days), and greater internalizing symptoms were indirectly associated with fewer heavy drinking SAM days via greater overall conformity motives for SAM at the between‐person level (estimate = −0.032; 95% CI [−0.090, −0.001]). Consistent with findings at the within‐person level, there were no significant direct associations between internalizing symptoms and simultaneous use frequency outcomes.

Finally, when the model was re‐run to include frequency of heavy and light drinking simultaneous use days as additional covariates, results revealed that the within‐person association between positive SAM motives and negative consequences on simultaneous use days was no longer significant (estimate = 0.433, 95% CI [−0.001, 0.871]), and instead frequency of both heavy (estimate = 0.533, 95% CI [0.315, 0.786]) and light (estimate = 0.287, 95% CI [0.131, 0.466]) drinking simultaneous use days was significantly associated with negative consequences on simultaneous use days at the within‐person level. DISCUSSION

This study examined the mediating role of motives for simultaneous alcohol and cannabis use in the links between internalizing symptoms and simultaneous use behavior among young adults. Using three waves of data, we found that positive SAM motives mediated the within‐person associations of internalizing symptoms with both frequency of heavy drinking simultaneous use days and greater negative consequences on simultaneous use days, although the latter association was no longer significant when accounting for simultaneous use frequency. Further, we observed between‐person indirect associations of internalizing symptoms (aggregated across time points) with greater frequency of light drinking simultaneous use days via elevated coping SAM motives, and with lower frequency of heavy drinking simultaneous use days via elevated conformity SAM motives. Since we controlled for single substance counterparts when predicting simultaneous use outcomes, significant associations are specific to simultaneous use outcomes. The use of a longitudinal burst design allowed us to examine how changes in internalizing symptoms and SAM motives over 1 year were associated with corresponding changes in simultaneous use behavior, complementing prior EMA studies that have examined internalizing symptoms or SAM motives as proximal predictors of simultaneous use. Findings provide new insight into motivational processes that link depression, anxiety, and stress with simultaneous use over time among young adults.

Our results indicated that young adults with higher levels of internalizing symptoms on average across time tended to have higher scores on SAM motives overall (between‐person level), and that within‐person increases in SAM motives occurred during time points when a given participant's internalizing symptoms also increased (relative to their typical level of symptoms). That we observed within‐person and between‐person associations of internalizing symptoms with both coping and positive SAM motives is consistent with hypotheses, as well as past evidence of links between internalizing symptoms and separate alcohol and cannabis motives related to coping, enhancement, and expansion (Bravo et al., 2020; Kenney et al., 2013; Scarfe et al., 2022). These findings provide support for motivational processes that are consistent with a self‐medication perspective, suggesting that young adults experiencing internalizing symptoms report greater coping and positive/mood enhancing reasons for simultaneous use.

Although not hypothesized from a self‐medication perspective, the observed associations between internalizing symptoms and both social and conformity SAM motives make sense conceptually. Perhaps among young adults whose internalizing symptoms (e.g., social anxiety, depressed mood) interfere with their social life, simultaneous use may serve to facilitate social interactions, contributing to social SAM motives. Further, those whose internalizing symptoms make them sensitive to criticism from peers may be motivated to engage in simultaneous use for conformity reasons. This notion aligns with previous evidence of links between social anxiety and both social and conformity motives when examining alcohol and cannabis separately (e.g., Buckner et al., 2012; Ham et al., 2009; Stewart et al., 2006; Villarosa‐Hurlocker et al., 2019), albeit findings have been mixed, with null associations between social anxiety and social motives also sometimes observed (e.g., Buckner et al., 2006, 2007; Norberg et al., 2010).

Although within‐person increases in internalizing symptoms were associated with increases in all SAM motives, only within‐person increases in positive SAM motives were, in turn, associated with increased simultaneous use frequency captured during the ensuing EMA burst. This within‐person association between positive SAM motives and simultaneous use is consistent with hypotheses and aligns with previous EMA studies finding that enhancement‐related motives are proximally associated with the likelihood of engaging in simultaneous use (Arterberry et al., 2021; Jackson et al., 2021; Patrick et al., 2019). We also found that this association was specific to the frequency of heavy drinking simultaneous use days. As this is the first study to our knowledge to examine how SAM motives relate differentially to heavy and light drinking simultaneous use days, we can only speculate on the reasons for these findings. Perhaps young adults who are motivated by the desire to obtain more intense subjective effects (referenced in positive SAM motives) more frequently combine cannabis with heavy drinking because they believe these subjective effects are more likely to occur when consuming larger amounts of alcohol. This notion is supported by research suggesting that young adults expect more intense arousing effects of alcohol when engaging in heavy versus light drinking episodes (e.g., Read et al., 2009), although we are unaware of research that has examined dose‐dependent alcohol expectancies in the context of simultaneous use. Future research on dose‐dependent expectancies for simultaneous use may be informative. We also observed a significant, within‐person indirect association between changes in internalizing symptoms at a given time point and frequency of heavy drinking simultaneous use days during that time point that was mediated by positive SAM motives. This finding suggests that experiencing an increase in internalizing symptoms may predict more frequent engagement in cannabis use combined with heavy episodic drinking, perhaps as a result of an associated desire for more intense or positive intoxicating effects (i.e., positive SAM motives). That there was also a significant within‐person indirect association between internalizing symptoms and greater negative consequences across SAM days that was mediated by positive SAM motives suggests that this pathway is associated with greater risk for substance‐related harms.

However, we did not observe support for the hypothesis that coping SAM motives would mediate the within‐person associations between internalizing symptoms and SAM frequency and consequences over time. This finding contributes to an already mixed literature, as some EMA studies have observed within‐person associations between coping motives and simultaneous use (Patrick et al., 2019) while others have not (Jackson et al., 2021). Yet, we did observe a significant between‐person association, such that individuals with higher coping SAM motives on average across time points also reported more frequently engaging in light drinking simultaneous use across EMA bursts. There also was a significant between‐person indirect association between internalizing symptoms and light drinking simultaneous use frequency through coping SAM motives. This finding converges with the few previous between‐person studies that have used the SAM motives measure, which have found some (albeit mixed) evidence that coping SAM motives are associated with simultaneous use behavior (Conway et al., 2020; Patrick et al., 2018). That between‐person differences in SAM coping motives were associated only with frequency of light drinking (and not heavy drinking) simultaneous use days in the current study is a novel finding. Perhaps young adults who are more highly motivated to engage in simultaneous use to decrease arousal and induce relaxation (referenced in coping SAM motives) tend to do so more often at home alone to unwind or before bed to help induce sleep, contexts in which heavy episodic drinking may be less common compared to social contexts (Stanesby et al., 2019). As several studies suggest that social context and location influence simultaneous use behavior (e.g., Jackson et al., 2021; Lipperman‐Kreda et al., 2018; see Farrelly et al., 2025 review), future research should incorporate contextual factors into motivational pathways from internalizing symptoms to simultaneous use to examine this possibility.

We also observed an unexpected indirect association at the between‐person level between greater internalizing symptoms and less frequent heavy drinking simultaneous use through higher conformity motives. This finding is consistent with past research showing a negative between‐person association between SAM conformity motives and likelihood and frequency of engaging in simultaneous use (Conway et al., 2020; Patrick et al., 2018). Thus, while individuals with higher average levels of internalizing symptoms might be motivated to engage in simultaneous use to conform with others, it appears that they engage in heavy drinking simultaneous use less often, perhaps because they avoid social events, where heavy drinking tends to be more common (see Stanesby et al., 2019). This notion aligns with research on drinking motives that suggests inverse associations between conformity motives and frequency and quantity of alcohol consumption (see Bresin & Mekawi, 2021).

Limitations

Despite the strengths of the longitudinal burst design, this study had several limitations. First, internalizing symptoms and SAM motives were examined contemporaneously in the model, precluding inferences about the temporal ordering of these variables. Similarly, daily surveys assessed alcohol and cannabis use for the previous day, so retrospective bias is not entirely eliminated in this approach. Second, given current limitations in the ability of statistical software to model count variables as mediators in a multilevel framework using the Bayes estimator (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2021), we were not able to include the number of heavy and light drinking SAM days as mediators in the pathways to negative consequences. Moreover, our operational definition of simultaneous use (using both cannabis and alcohol within the same 2‐h period) is limited because we cannot establish that the psychoactive effects of the two substances overlapped, which is a more common way to operationalize simultaneous use (see Lee et al., 2022). Further, we were not able to examine nonsimultaneous couse days in our analyses given that they were rare in our dataset. We also did not model heterogeneity in the forms and quantities of cannabis used on simultaneous and cannabis‐only use days; future studies that capture more nonflower cannabis use observations will be necessary to determine how internalizing symptoms relate to specific combinations of alcohol and cannabis products.

Additionally, the average frequency of simultaneous use and heavy episodic drinking was relatively low across the EMA bursts, so findings have limited generalizability to young adults who engage in high frequency simultaneous use or heavy episodic drinking. Similarly, our sample was not selected to represent young adults with elevated depression, anxiety, or stress, and the study lacked any diagnostic assessments; future studies with clinical samples would be informative to elucidate the role of SAM motives in simultaneous use among young adults diagnosed with mood and anxiety disorders. Further, although we excluded participants who reported frequent illicit drug use, we did not assess the use of prescription medications such as sedatives, antidepressants, and other psychoactive medications that could affect motivations to use alcohol and cannabis.

The study was conducted in Canada where both alcohol and cannabis were legal for all young adults in the sample, so findings may differ in other jurisdictions with different cannabis and alcohol policies. For example, social and conformity SAM motives might behave differently in contexts where use of both cannabis and alcohol is legal for young adults than they do in contexts where use of cannabis is not legal. Further, even among jurisdictions with legal adult cannabis use, regulations vary with respect to types and potencies of cannabis products available, which could impact cannabis use, motives, and consequences. Additionally, participant attrition from the follow‐up assessments further limits generalizability.

CONCLUSIONS

Findings suggest that young adults with higher average levels of internalizing symptoms engage in light drinking simultaneous use more frequently as a function of greater coping‐related SAM motives, but they also engage in heavy drinking simultaneous use less frequently as a function of greater conformity motives for simultaneous use. On the other hand, during time periods when young adults experience increases in internalizing symptoms (relative to their typical level of symptoms), they tend to increase their frequency of heavy drinking simultaneous use, mediated by concurrent increases in their motives to experience the positive effects of simultaneous use. These findings may have implications for interventions, suggesting that screening for SAM motives may be beneficial for identifying intervention targets given that changes in SAM motives predict changes in simultaneous use outcomes. Indeed, positive SAM motives may be a particularly important target for interventions during times when young adults are experiencing the onset or intensification of depression, anxiety, or stress. Further, coping motives may be important to address among young adults with a pattern of more chronic depression and anxiety, as these motives may play a role in more stable patterns of frequent light drinking simultaneous use. Future research should examine the utility of targeting specific SAM motives in alcohol and cannabis harm reduction interventions tailored toward young adults experiencing internalizing symptoms.
