Pulmonary cryptococcus (case series, Regional VIC, Australia)
Authors: Aung Khine Lin
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and objectivesCryptococcus is a fungal pathogen, epidemiologically dominant in tropical and subtropical areas of Australia. With this clinical case report, we aim to acknowledge the incidence of pathogen in Victoria and solidify clinicians to in considering as a differential diagnosis with its different nature of presentations.Materials and methodsWe identified five cases of pulmonary cryptococcus over a period of 10 months at Monash Medical Centre, Clayton, Victoria. Cases were retrospectively reviewed from onset of presentations through diagnosis and management. Identities and personal information of patients remained anonymous. Consents were obtained via electronic medical documentation.ResultsIn this case study, only one patient was immunosuppressed while others were immunocompetent. Number of cryptococcus gatti and neoformans was equally distributed in immunocompetent patients, while immunosuppressed patient had C.neoformans. Four out of five patients had respiratory symptoms and remaining patient (immunocompetent) had neurological complication despite absence of respiratory symptoms. As of radiological features, cavitating lesion was observed in immunosuppressed patient, while in immunocompetent ones, two had nodular feature, one had cavitating and other had endobronchial soft tissue density. Three patients achieved diagnosis confirmation by bronchial washings + BAL while two achieved by biopsy.

Background

Cryptococcus gattii is a fungal pathogen that is endemic in the tropics and subtropics and temperate climatic regions of Australia. An environmental link with two species of Australian Eucalypts (or red gum), Eucalyptus camaldulensis and Eucalyptus tereticornis, was first reported in the 1990s. The concentration of E. camaldulensis along water courses and the association of rural-dwelling Aborigines with these trees are thought to explain the high prevalence of infection in this population, although host genetic factors in Aborigines and the non-Aboriginal population have not been studied.

As of disease manifestation, cryptococcus neoformans usually transverse through pulmonary lesions to cross blood brain barrier without definite incubation period. With immunosuppression state, this facilitates the macrophage migration, promoting the risks of neurological complications. Cryptococcus gatti, in contrast, has less risk of neurological manifestation by unclear pathophysiology, though pulmonary involvement remains primary portal of entry with the risk depending upon host's immune status.

Radiological features of pulmonary cryptococcus vary from FDG +ve/-ve nodules to calcifications, tree in bud appearance, lymphadenopathy and pleural thickening (Setianingrum et al., 2019). Changes can be aggressive in immunocompromised patients as cavitations, pneumonitis and consolidations. Antigen testing in BAL show near 100% sensitivity with 91% specificity in HIV patients however sensitivity reduced in cryptococcus gatti and immunocompetent patients of cryptococcus neoformans.

Typical histopathological findings would show encapsulated yeast forms but atypically could only show granuloma formations. It is confirmed by culture which can be false -ve due to granuloma formation rendering cryptococcus growth in sample. Finally, multiplex PCR became widely used in pulmonary samples demonstrating around 90% sensitivity and 100% specificity for both cryptococcus species.

Materials and Methods

Over a period of 10 months between May 2022 and February 2023, five cases of pulmonary cryptococcal cases were identified by bronchoscopy washings or endobronchial ultrasound biopsy at Monash Medical centre, Clayton VIC. Cases were retrospectively reviewed after consents were taken via electronic medical documentation and data were collected without involving identities of patients. Nature of symptoms, immune status, course of disease and extrapulmonary manifestations were obtained from documented electronic notes by medical practitioners involved in patients’ care. Relevant radiological features and reports were collected from Monash medical imaging. Bronchoscopy washing and biopsy results were obtained from institutional electronic medical records.

Case 1

34-year-old gentleman presented initially with symptoms of meningitis such as headache, weight loss, nausea and vomiting. He was active smoker with significant smoking history but had no underlying medical condition. His family history was significant for adenocarcinoma of unknown origin in his sister at age of 31. He was a welder in city train tracks.

Further investigations showed right lower lobe soft tissue nodule on CT chest/abd/pelvis. Lumbar and CSF examination confirmed cryptococcal meningitis and he was treated with induction therapy (amphotericin+flucytocine) followed by fluconazole.

Over next 9 months, pulmonary nodule on surveillance CT chests showed no improvement in size despite appropriate antifungal therapy. This was avid +ve on PET scan. Case was further discussed in radiology MDTs with concern for malignancy, therefore VATS + wedge resection was performed by cardiothoracic team. Histology changes were in keeping with cryptococcus organisms with no evidence of malignancy.

Case 2

62-year-old male presented to hospital with 6 weeks of progressive shortness of breath, fatigue, and 2 months of intermittent non-productive cough. His medical background was significant for immunosuppression with tacrolimus, mycophenolate and prednisolone following renal transplant in 2020 and treated latent TB in 2019. He was non-smoker and his last overseas trip was to Europe 6 years ago.

Clinical examination revealed audible coarse crackles on left upper lobe without oxygen requirement. CT chest demonstrated multiple cavitating lesions with nodular opacities in left lower lobe and right upper lobe. He received bronchoscopy with washings and subsequently started on empirical anticryptococcal therapy. Bronchoscopy washings later confirmed growth of cryptococcal neoformans + and pseudomonas aeruginosa ++. Following this result, antipseudomonal cover was added on existing PO fluconazole.

Case 3

56 year female who arrived to Australia 4 months ago presented with 24 hours of acute onset of frequent, low volume haemoptysis. She had no other constitutional symptoms or underlying medical condition. She had close contact with her son who was treated for active TB 2 years ago in Vietnam.

CTPA demonstrated left lower lobe 5cm cavitating lesion while sputum AFBs and MCS were negative. Further investigation with bronchoscopy and washings grew cryptococcus neoformans. Her haemoptysis settled with inhaled tranexamic acids. She was discharged and immediately followed up by infectious disease team for initiation of anticryptococcal therapy.

Case 4

74-year female was found to have isolated 1.6cm size pulmonary nodule in left lower lobe 1 year ago with complaint of intermittent low volume haemoptysis for 12 months. This nodule was minimally FDG+ve on PET scan. Her past medical history included well controlled asthma with normal lung function, hypertension and osteoarthritis. She was non-smoker and retired from childminding.

She underwent CT guided lung biopsy of this nodule which histology showed only benign lung parenchyma with focal fibrosis and chronic inflammation without evidence of malignancy. The procedure was complicated by bleeding and limited biopsy samples. She was then regularly followed up however this nodule was waxing and waning in surveillance CT chests with FDG-ve on PET scans. At a serial CT scan at 9 months after biopsy, this showed size progression to 4.7 cm with spiculations and ground glass changes. As a result, she received radial EBUS and bronchial brushings, which histology demonstrated features consistent with cryptococcus species. Soon after this, she was admitted due to intermittent headache with CSF culture +ve for cryptococcal gatti and received treatment accordingly.

Case 5

53-year female presented with acute onset of low volume haemoptysis with no other constitutional or systemic symptoms. She was non-smoker and there was no other past medical history. Her family history was significant for lung cancer in her 1st and 2nd degree relatives (father and uncle). Clinical examination was unremarkable without oxygen requirement.

CT chest revealed Right middle and lower lobe endobronchial densities. Bronchoscopy and BAL grew Cryptococcus neoformans. However, patient had nil further haemoptysis after initial 48hrs of presentation without any active treatment required. She is currently being followed up in infectious disease outpatient clinic.

Discussion

From epidemiology case reports in Australia, two significant case reports from Northern Territory (Sim et al., 2022) and Hunter regions of New South Wale supported the high incidence of cryptococcal cases in rural areas with tropical climate and aboriginal populations. Between 1993 and 2000, 18 cases were reported by Royal Darwin Hospital (Jenney et al, 2004), tropical area, in which 12 cases were by C.gatti complex and 13 cases involving pulmonary manifestations varying from mass/nodules (8 cases) to infiltration/consolidation (5). Of these 13 cases, 6 patients had concurrent neurological disease. Respiratory symptoms were non-specific including cough and dyspnoea. An epidemiological retrospective study 107 cryptococcal antigen +ve cases between 2003 and 2016 was reported from Hunter region of NSW (Hughes et al, 2020), a subtropical area. This report demonstrated around 50% of cases were immunocompromised in which C.neoformans comprised 81% of this. A global literature review of PubMed database showed C.gatti is the most common species of cryptococcus in Oceania, including Australia. All these case reviews point out the equal dominance of C.gatti and C.neoformans in Australia, although historically less incidence in cold weather areas like Victoria.

As similar as in this case series report, literature background also indicates non-specific symptoms and signs of pulmonary cryptococcus which might well be asymptomatic and found incidentally. A case report published in SAGE journal (Kanjanapradit et al, 2017) mentioned 7 cases with mass like lung parenchymal lesions of varying sizes in immunocompetent patients without symptoms. Although it was known to anticipate more aggressive radiological manifestation in immunocompromised patients according to existing case studies analysis, we can appreciate the presence of cavitating and significant mass lesions in immunocompetent patients in this case report. A review article published by IDSA, Infectious Disease Society of America reported the tendency of spontaneous resolutions of pulmonary lesions in immunocompetent patients without treatment over 1–2 years, however it is crucial to monitor these patients due to unpredicted nature of symptoms and radiological progression (Howard-Jones et al, 2022).

In addition, it appears that no strong relation exist between host immune status and type of cryptococcus species; whereas a case report of 17 years collection in Thailand indicates more frequency in immunosuppressed patients and another case report of 76 patients by Shanghai Pulmonary hospital (Zhang et al, 2012) showed only 2% culture positive for C.neoformans. Evidently in this case report, we can witness the equal distribution of cryptococcus gatti and cryptococcus neoformans in both immune status of patients.

Conclusions

In summary, it is important to consider pulmonary cryptococcus as differential diagnosis as it can demonstrate a variety of radiological features and course of disease manifestation. Although higher prevalence was known in tropical and subtropical areas epidemiologically, it would be interesting to look out for more case reports in other parts of Australia. Finally, immune status of patients does not strongly predict nature of disease manifestations. If biopsy is achievable and safe for patients, this option should be explored before patients acquire extrapulmonary manifestations.
