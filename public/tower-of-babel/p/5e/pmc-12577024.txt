Gender differences in institutional trust and covid-19 vaccines uptake in Ghana
Authors: Irenius Konkor, Florence Dery, Ebenezer Dassah, Elijah Bisung, Vincent Kuuire
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOne of the most studied social phenomena since the emergence of the covid-19 pandemic is trust. This renewed interest demonstrates the crucial role of trust in crises management. Unfortunately, most of the studies on trust have been conducted without considerations to saliant social categorisations like gender that might matter on how trust is built and radiated. This is despite differing socialisations of gender values in social contexts and how that might translate into how trust is built and demonstrated. The goal of this study, therefore, was to examine prevailing gendered patterns of institutional trust and how that might translate into the uptake of covid-19 vaccines in Ghana. We conducted logistic regression analyses on a sample of 1692 individual responses from a cross-sectional survey that was collected between October and November 2022 across four cities in Ghana. Results show that women were more trusting and were consequently more likely to take the vaccine in comparison to men. Interaction analysis further revealed that even when both men and women trusted a lot in relevant institutions like health, women were about 64% more likely to take the vaccine in comparison to men. Only men were significantly less likely to take the vaccine if they had trust concerns with political institutions. But both men and women were less likely to take the vaccine if they had trust concerns with health institutions. This study underscores the need for gender-specific policy programmes on public health issues.Keywords: Institutional trust, Gender, Social categorisation, Vaccines uptake, Covid-19, Ghana

Introduction

Trust in public health agencies amidst the covid-19 pandemic was identified as a crucial tool for containing – achieving herd immunity through vaccination and recovering from the pandemic [1]. During the initial stages of the pandemic, early public health communications emphasised behaviours like staying at home and physically distancing from others and later extended to include precautionary measures such as masking and vaccination (i.e. when vaccines became available), revealing some level of uncertainties surrounding the pandemic. As the pandemic evolved, misinformation (the dissemination of false information without intending to cause harm) and disinformation (the dissemination of false information with the intent to cause harm) were linked to the uncertainties surrounding the covid-19 pandemic, fuelling public mistrust [2]. Consequently, one of the most studied social phenomena since the emergence of the covid-19 pandemic is trust [3]. However, most studies on trust in the context of the pandemic have been conducted without considerations to saliant social categorisations like gender that might matter on how trust is built and radiated. This is particularly unfortunate given differing socialisations of gender values in diverse social contexts and how that might translate into actions which flow from trust (such as healthcare behaviours including vaccine uptake). Indeed, gender as a social categorisation has been implicated in how trust is built and demonstrated in healthcare systems and political institutions [4, 5]. The main goal of this study, consequently, was to examine prevailing gendered patterns of trust in social, political and health institutions and how that might translate into the uptake of covid-19 vaccines in Ghana. Contextual gendered dynamics of institutional trust and how that might impact health decisions is particularly crucial in a patriarchal setting like Ghana for the management of future pandemics. Thus, future pandemic management efforts in Ghana and similar settings could benefit by adopting relevant context specific gender strategies in rallying support from the public during public health crisis.

Contextualising the concept of trust

Robbins [6] has defined trust as “belief about another person’s trustworthiness with respect to a particular matter (e.g., covid-19 pandemic) that emerges under conditions of unknown outcomes” (p.976). Given the complexity of trust, the concept has been conceptualised in various ways in the literature and some scholars have broadly categorised these conceptualisations into three analytically distinct dimensions [6]. The first dimension is how to trust. Here, trust is understood either as the individual’s assessment of another person rooted in social cognition and/or innate values that reflect how the individual would perceive others and ought to behave towards them [7, 8]. In relation to the individual’s assessment of others, trust is often conceptualised as beliefs or expectations informed by personal experiences with and contingent on the trustworthiness of others [6, 9]. In terms of innate values, however, trust is usually inherited through transmission and socialisation of values and beliefs that are influenced by the individual psychological predisposition [6, 10].

The second dimension of trust – whom to trust, is conceived either as particular trust or general trust [6]. Particular trust encompasses the idea that trust can only be placed on specific people that we know or at least bear some information on [11, 12]. In line with this, Hardin [7] noted that people tend to trust others when they have a fair amount of information about whether their motivations align with their own interests. Particular trust, therefore, might be limited to individuals within our social circles. On the other hand, general trust applies to people we have little information on or may not know at all. In this context, trust is assumed to be indiscriminately applied to everyone and the institutions they may represent regardless of how well we know them or who they are. The issue of public trust in the institutions and individuals managing the covid-19 pandemic fits well within this dimension. The final dimension of trust – what one trusts others to do, is concerned with the specific matter upon which trust is placed (e.g., covid-19 control measures). Thus, there may be specific issue/s that the individual may trust others to do or not do and in the context of this study, the public may or may not trust the government on covid-19 control measures including vaccine mandates due to previous experiences, socialisation, mis/disinformation and or corruption perceptions.

As noted above, socialisation plays a crucial role in how people build and demonstrate trust. Indeed, Grimmelikhuijsen and colleagues [13] posit that, the ways people view public institutions and how much trust they place in such institutions may vary depending on their cultural upbrings. For example, women continue to shoulder core component of the caregiving load, especially in more traditional societies where the taken for granted notion of women as natural caregivers is still pervasive and deeply ingrained. This is partly so because women are socialised to have strong feeling of obligations to their family, and their major responsibilities are seen to be focused on the home. This increases the likelihood that women will frequently devote more time to taking care of other family members and exhibiting socialised feminine virtues like modesty, support, and tolerance. These virtues also breed cooperation and call upon women to be more trusting and by extension, increased trust in relevant institutions. Put differently, Willis et al. [14] noted that the socialisation of men and women on differing gendered roles might lead women to value communal and caring behaviours in comparison to men. Indeed, McDermott and Jones [15] posit that gender traits like femininity affects trust in government institutions with higher level of feminine personality traits being associated with more politically trusting. They argue that individuals who exhibit higher feminine traits are more communal and therefore more likely to have faith in government. Yet, studies on the intersection of gender and trust in developing country settings are often conducted in the context of violence and conflict situations [16].

Our study expands the discourse on trust through an important but less highlighted lens – a gendered lens to examine the association between trust and covid-19 vaccines uptake in Ghana. In modern history, infectious diseases such as the severe acute respiratory syndrome, H1N1, Ebola virus disease and now covid-19 illustrate the recurrent topicality of pandemics. Because human behaviour greatly influences the transmission of these diseases, public compliance with health recommendations is crucial for a successful control of highly contagious diseases [17]. Thus, trust building forms a fundamental basis for compliance. At a time women in developing country contexts are starting to move beyond being just victims of disasters to being active agents of change and playing crucial roles in disaster managements [18, 19], understanding the gendered dynamics of trust is vital in identifying how to effectively engage them in identifying sound solutions and promoting positive behaviours during health crises. Even though trust is not a new theme within public health emergencies, the renewed interest in trust amidst the covid-19 pandemic is a clear manifestation of the salience of building trust during uncertain times to chart a path for recovery. We believe that understanding the gendered dynamics of trust in our quest for the new path will offer a better direction especially given women’s urgency in disaster management in recent years in developing country settings [18, 19]. Consequently, the objectives of this study are two-fold. First, the paper tried to understand the determinants of trust, and secondly, the paper examined the extent to which gender and trust concurrently impact the health behaviour of covid-19 vaccines uptake.

In their gendered analysis of the conceptualisation of trust/mistrust, Bunting et al. [4] noted that trust tends to be interpreted as positive or optimistic and distrust as pessimism or lack of faith. Whereas trust is interpreted across gender as positive or optimistic, they suggest women are inclined to conceiving trust as requiring caution, and men as a naïve judgement. According to their study, there are differences between trust and mistrust and that mistrust presents the obvious split between genders. We believe that the social context of socialisation is crucial in influencing the gendered nuances of trust and mistrust. In a patriarchal setting like Ghana, we expect gender differences in trust with feminine traits likely to be accepting and trusting than masculine traits.

Study context

This study was conducted in four cities – Accra, Kumasi, Tamale and Wa in Ghana. Accra is the administrative and most populous city and located along the coast of Ghana. At the time of the pandemic, Accra was the only city that received international flights which explained why it recorded the first case of the virus. Kumasi is the second most populous city in Ghana and located in the middle belt. In Ghana, the first case of the coronavirus was recorded on 12th March 2020. Following this period and informed by increasing cases, the then president announced a 14-day partial lockdown starting on 30th March 2020 [20]. At the time of the announcement on 28th March, the Greater Accra region (Accra), Ashanti region (Kumasi), and Upper West region (Wa) had all reported cases of the virus [20]. As part of the lockdown measures, citizens could only go out for essential services including grocery, health needs, water, pay utility bills, or access financial services. The initial lockdown was later extended in April 2020 with strict enforcement in cities like Kumasi and Accra which were recording a higher proportion of positive cases. Some religious leaders in the cities broke restriction orders and others discouraged their members from taking the vaccine [21, 22]. Accra and Kumasi also have the best as well as the most health facilities than any other city in the country. While cities like Wa barely had isolation centres, Kumasi and Accra had such facilities. Indeed, much of the resources and support services intended for containing the virus were concentrated in locations like Accra and Kumasi. Even though Wa was the first region in Northern Ghana to report a positive case imported from Accra, Tamale later reported more cases. Further, social services like free meals were only provided in the larger cities like Accra. These dynamics are important for understanding trust in relevant institutions amid the pandemic and how that could influence citizens uptake of the covid-19 vaccines in Ghana, and the above cities were selected for that purpose.

Data and methods

Study design, sampling and data collection

To achieve the research objectives, which were to understand the determinants of institutional trust, and then examine how that might translate into the uptake of covid-19 vaccines, a cross-sectional study was designed and implemented in four Ghanaian cities (Accra, Kumasi, Tamale, and Wa) using a structured survey. The survey was designed in Qualtrics software, installed on mobile devices and administered by trained research assistants in a face-to-face interview with the respondents. We tested the questionnaire on 1% of the targeted sample for cultural sensitivity and appropriate wording and the necessary corrections were made in the final questionnaire. The data was then collected from individuals at the household level between October and November 2022, using multi-stage sampling technique. In each city, the random number generator was used to select a specific neighbourhood and starting household and subsequent households were systematically selected thereafter. Four experienced research assistants proficient in the English language and at least two of the local languages (i.e., Twi, Ga, Waali, and Dagbani) administered the survey. For respondents to have been included in the survey, they had to be at least 18 years old at the time of the fieldwork and resided in the study city. We successfully administered the survey to 2059 individuals from households in selected neighbourhoods across the four study cities. The survey collected information on institutional trust, covid-19 vaccine status, gender, and demographic and socio-economic characteristics of the respondents. The University of Toronto Social Sciences Humanities and Education Research Ethics Board; the General Research Ethics Boards of Queen’s University; and the Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology granted ethics approval for the study. Participants were required to grant verbal consent to participate in the study.

Variable measures

The are two categories of dependent variables in this analysis. The first category, institutional trust, comprises of seven variables: trust in the presidency, trust in parliament, trust in the ruling party, trust in traditional leaders, trust in religious leaders, trust in health administrators, and trust in health workers. Trust in these institutions were adapted from the Afrobarometer survey [24]. In the survey, respondents were asked to indicate the extent of their trust in each institution with predefined Likert scale type responses including not at all, just a little, somewhat, a lot and don’t know. Informed by the study objectives, we dropped respondents who responded don’t know to the question on institutional trust as well as missing responses, limiting the analytical sample to about 1692 individuals. Due to small cases in some response categories (i.e., not at all, just a little, somewhat, and a lot) we binarily coded institutional trust into 0 = No for those who indicated not at all, and 1 = Yes for those who said either just a little, somewhat, or a lot. This recoding was only used in analysing the determinants of institutional trust in a logistic regression analysis. The second dependent variable is covid-19 vaccine uptake coded as 0 = No and 1 = Yes. This variable was created from a question that asked respondents if they had taken at least one dose of any covid-19 vaccines prior the interview.

Similarly, there were two key independent variables in the analysis. The first is gender as a binary variable (0 = men and 1 = women). Gender was binarily coded as man or woman to avoid any public uproar about ongoing debates on anti-gay laws in the country. We conceptualise woman as an individual who self-identify and exhibit feminine traits and a man as an individual who self-identify and exhibit masculine traits. The second independent variable measured institutional trust in three ways: (1) trust in political institutions, (2) trust in social institutions, and (3) trust in health institution. Trust in political institutions consists of trust in the presidency, trust in parliament, and trust in the ruling party with response categories including not at all, just a little, somewhat, or a lot (after dropping the “don’t know” category). These institutions combined favourably with Cronbach alpha value of 0.91 and were combined for that reason using the egen command in Stata and recoded as 0 = a lot, 1 = somewhat, 2 = not at all. Similarly, trust in social institutions with a Cronbach alpha 0.73 consisted of trust in religious leaders and traditional leaders coded as 0 = a lot, 1 = somewhat, 2 = not at all. The third and final institutional trust variable, trust in health institution, is made up of trust in health workers and trust in health administrators. They combined favourably with a Cronbach alpha 0.88 and was combined for that reason and recoded as the previous two institutional variables.

We measured other theoretically relevant variables broadly categorised as socio-demographic and economic characteristics of the respondents. Socio-demographic variables included city of residence, age of respondent, marital status, ethnicity, and religion. Previous studies have shown that younger people are less inclined to taking vaccines than older people [25], hence age was categorised for that purpose. The socio-economic variables also included level of education, employment status and household wealth. The household wealth variable was created as an index using several household items that are indicators of wealth status in Ghana [26] and then categorised as quintiles.

Analytic strategies

Three complementary analyses were employed to address the research objectives. The first is a descriptive analysis to understand the distributions of the dependent and independent variables. To address the first objective of the study (explore the determinants of trust), we conducted logistic regression analysis of institutional trust for each of the seven institutions. We also conducted logistic bivariate and multivariate regression analyses to observe how these determinants might influence vaccine behaviours among the study population with a gendered lens. The bivariate analysis helped us observe the relationship between each independent variable and the uptake of covid-19 vaccines while the multivariate logistic regression analysis measured the cumulative impact of all the variables on vaccines uptake. Thus, the multivariate logistic regression analysis comprised of two nested models. Informed by the second objective (gender differences in trust and covid-19 vaccine uptake), we included gender and the three institutional trust variables (i.e., political institutions, social institutions, and the health institution) in model 1, and model 2 further accounted for the socio-demographic and economic characteristics of the respondents. The final set of analysis is an interaction analysis between gender and institutional trust and how such an interaction may influence the uptake of covid-19 vaccines. All analyses were conducted using Stata software and the results reported as odds ratios (ORs) for the bivariate and adjusted odds ratios (aORs) for the multivariate logistic regression analyses together with their respective confidence intervals (CI).

Results

Descriptive results

Results of the descriptive analysis as presented in Table 1 show that about 46% of the respondents had taken at least one dose of the covid-19 vaccines and more than half (58.8%) of the respondents identified as women. At the individual institutional level, nearly half (48.2%) of the sample indicated they did not trust the presidency, and more than half (53.2%) did not trust the ruling party. About 45% and 30% also indicated they did not trust parliament and the traditional leaders respectively. Trust in health administrators (89.7%) and health workers (91.5%) recorded the highest rating followed by trust in religious leaders (81.9%). The sample was nearly evenly distributed across the study cities, and a higher proportion (48.2%) were 30 years old or younger. About 64% of the sample were married, 34% identified as Akan and 55% identified with the Christian faith. More than 81% of the respondents said they attained some formal education, and 44% indicated they were privately employed. Household wealth quintile was relatively evenly distributed.

Determinants of institutional trust

Results of the determinants of institutional trust are presented in Table 2. We observed that the odds of trusting in the presidency (aOR = 1.33, CI = 1.08,1.63), trust in parliament (aOR = 1.30, CI = 1.06,1.60), and in the ruling party (aOR = 1.39, CI = 1.13,1.72) were significantly higher for women compared to their male counterparts. Compared to respondents aged 30 years and younger, those aged between 31 and 40 years had significantly lower odds of trusting in parliament (aOR = 0.73, CI = 0.55,0.97) and the ruling party (aOR = 0.75, CI = 0.56,0.99). Marital status was found to be a significant determinant of trust in social and health institutions. For instance, married respondents about about 1.3 times higher odds (aOR = 1.31, CI = 1.02,1.69) of trusting in traditional leaders, and about 1.5 times higher (aOR = 1.48, CI = 1.11,1.98) trusting in religious leaders compared to those not married. Similarly, married respondents had higher odds of trusting in both health workers (aOR = 1.52, CI = 1.02,2.27) and health administrators (aOR = 1.49, CI = 1.02,2.16) when compared to those not married. Level of educational attainment showed that educated respondents had significantly higher odds of trusting in traditional leaders (aOR = 0.61, CI = 0.44,0.86), religious leaders (aOR = 0.61, CI = 0.40,0.91) and health administrators (aOR = 0.47, CI = 0.28,0.79) in comparison to the uneducated respondents. In comparison to respondents who were employed by the government, farmers (aOR = 0.45, CI = 0.23,0.88) and the unemployed (aOR = 0.52, CI = 0.34,0.81) reported higher odds of trusting in the presidency. Similarly, both farmers (aOR = 0.41, CI = 0.21,0.79) and the unemployed (aOR = 0.46, CI = 0.29,0.72) were found to have lower odds of trusting in parliament. Only the unemployed, however, reported significantly lower odds of trusting in the ruling party (aOR = 0.57, CI = 0.37,0.90), and in health administrators (aOR = 0.44, CI = 0.21,0.92). On the contrary, were found to have significantly higher odds (aOR = 1.79, CI = 1.07,2.99) of trusting in traditional leadership compared with government employees. The relationship between ethnicity and institutional trust showed that Ewe’s, Dagaaba/Waala’s and Ga’s had lower odds of trusting in political institutions including the presidency, parliament and the ruling party when compared with the Akan ethnic group. Similarly, Ga’s reported lower odds of trusting in health workers (aOR = 0.35, CI = 0.21,0.60) and health administrators (aOR = 0.34, CI = 0.21,0.57) compared with the Akan ethic group but Dagomba’s reported significantly higher odds of trusting in traditional leaders (aOR = 2.71, CI = 1.72,4.29) and religious leaders (aOR = 4.22, CI = 2.24,7.97) in comparison to the Akan ethnic group. Wealth status also appeared to be a significant determinant of institutional trust. For instance, the richest category of respondents reported significantly higher odds of trusting in parliament, the ruling party, religious leaders and health workers compared with the poorest. In terms of trust in health administrators, for example, the poorer (aOR = 1.98, CI = 1.19,3.27), middle (aOR = 2.02, CI = 1.23,3.33), richer (aOR = 1.78, CI = 1.09,2.92) and the richest (aOR = 2.27, CI = 1.32,3.93) category of respondents had higher odds of reporting greater trust compared with the poorest category of respondents.

Bivariate results

We observed that women had about 1.4 times higher odds [CI = 1.17,1.71] of taking at least one dose of the covid-19 vaccine compared with their male counterparts. Institutional trust was also found to be a significant determinant of covid-19 vaccines uptake. Discussion

This study had two main objectives: first, to understand the determinants of institutional trust, and secondly, to examine the extent to which gender status and trust concurrently impact the health behaviours of vaccine uptake. In relation to the first research objective, we found that women were more institutionally trusting than men, which is consistent with previous studies [15]. Consistent with our study, Thomas and colleagues [27] suggest that men have lower generalized institutional trust. Even though the relationship between employment status and trust in political institutions (i.e., the presidency, parliament, and the ruling party) was not uniform, non-government employees and the unemployed were generally less trusting in political institutions (see Table 2), which might reflect years of leadership failure and a disappointment in the political class. In contrast, however, and with the exception of students, employment status did not significantly matter on how trust is reflected in traditional leaders, religious leaders and health workers probably because these institutions do not play meaningful role in the labour market. Ethnic affiliation was also found to be an important determinant of trust. Compared with the Akan ethnic group, for example, the other ethnic groups were significantly less trusting in political institutions while Dagombas were more trusting in religious and traditional leaders. On the other hand, educated people did not trust traditional and religious leaders. In relation to wealth, however, respondents from wealthy households were generally more trusting in institutions. These findings reflect the complexity of institutional trust in the study setting and call for nuanced understanding and approaches to building trust.

The results also revealed gendered nuances in institutional trust with women particularly being more trusting in political institutions, which are male dominated in the study milieu. This finding supports previous studies elsewhere [28]. Concomitantly, and in relation to the second objective, we found that women had higher odds of taking at least one dose of the covid-19 vaccines in comparison to men. Interaction analysis between gender and institutional trust further revealed insightful gendered nuances. For instance, even though both men and women had higher odds of taking the vaccine if they trusted in the relevant institutions, women had higher odds of doing so in comparison to men. On the other hand, both men and women reported lower odds of doing so if they had trust concerns in the institutions even though women may have been relatively worse off. For example, the odds of not taking the vaccine were relatively higher for women than men (38% verses 20%) when both men and women had no trust at all in the health institution. This finding highlights the crucial role of the intersection of gender and trust in social, political, and health institutions and how that might affect health decision making and health outcomes. In the context of public health, the observed gendered differences suggest that trust in public health authorities and systems may be filtered through gender norms, which influence both how health messages are received and how people respond to health mandates like vaccine uptake.

Gender is a social construct, and its framing and concomitant social expectations are usually a reflection of the cultural orientation of the society in question. In the case of Ghana, for instance, feminine expectations and values generally entail tolerance, modesty and solidarity and these values usually invoke and radiate trust. As Kaasa and Andriani [29] noted, good relations are valued in feminine societies, and by extension, more cooperation can be expected, which might engender increased trust in institutions through civic associations. Given the trust laden nature of the covid-19 pandemic, prevailing gendered differences in institutional trust could reflect on how women and men respond to the uptake of the covid-19 vaccines. In explaining gender differences in trust, Maddux and Brewer [30] opine that women are relationally interdependent while men are collectively interdependent. They explained collective interdependence of trust as a situation where people are inclined to trust individuals who share in-group membership. Thus, men are inclined to trust members of a particular group (e.g., a political party) if they themselves are members of that group. In the context of the dimensions of trust previously explained, this will imply men are more of particular trust than general trust. On the other hand, Maddux and Brewer [30] described relational interdependence as a situation where people (especially women) tend to trust individuals who are likely to share a direct or indirect network of relationships. According to Rogers and Ballantyne [31], women’s increased interaction with health providers also calls upon them to be more trusting. In relation to the dimensions of trust above, their increased exposure could create a network of social relations with health workers which could qualify as how to trust. Thus, women would have assessed these health workers during their regular visits to health facilities that might meet their expectations and create positive experiences. Such a phenomenon could partly explain the strong significant relationship between women’s trust in the health institution and consequently the higher odds of taking the covid-19 vaccines relative to men.

Moreover, masculine norms could partly explain the observed gendered differences in vaccine uptake in the study context. While research studies in recent years recognize the relationship between masculinity constructs and health outcomes is evolving, more nuanced and may depend on determining factors including how masculinity is operationalized, the specific dimensions of masculinities, types of outcomes, or the populations being studied, traditional gender conforming norms that encompass dominance, risk-taking, emotional control and self-reliance among others [32] serve as barriers to men’s uptake of preventive health services including vaccines uptake. Wong and colleagues [32], for example, in a meta-analysis of conformity to masculine norms and mental health outcomes found that conformity to masculine norms such as self-reliance, and dominance over women was negatively associated with mental health outcomes and seeking psychological help. Earlier studies elsewhere on masculinity and the adoption of covid-19 preventive measures found that subscribing to masculine ideologies was negatively associated the likelihood of men adopting protective behaviours like social distancing and good hand hygiene [33]. In patriarchal societies like the Ghanaian setting, men often subscribe to and internalize masculine ideologies including the ability to suppress emotions, acting strong even during adversities and abstaining from vaccine mandates. The pervasiveness of such masculine tendencies in the study setting could therefore explain the observed gendered differences in vaccine uptake in this study, with lower uptake of vaccines among men.

Less than half (46%) the study sample (49% women and 41% men) took at least a single dose of the vaccines at the time of the fieldwork. Importantly, both men and women who had no trust in the relevant institutions reported lower odds of taking the vaccine, which emphasizes the importance of trust during public health crisis. This finding supports results of previous studies on the relationship between institutional trust and vaccines uptake [1, 34–36]. Khodyakov [37] noted that trust in institutions reflects perceived legitimacy of the institutions and by extension compliance with both formal and informal norms. Consequently, public health information and policy programs emerging from such institutions will receive positive or negative reactions, adhered to or not depending on the public’s perceived legitimacy in the institutions in question. Those who reject the vaccines, for instance, might question the legitimacy of the people and institutions delivering the vaccines or vaccine messaging, their motives and even the effectiveness of the vaccines. Consequently, gender is important in trust in public health because it informs who is listened to, who is believed, and who benefits from health interventions. Trust-building strategies that ignore gender risk reinforcing inequities and weakening the effectiveness of public health efforts.

Finally, while institutional trust was generally found to be an important determinant of vaccines uptake, mistrust in political institutions appeared to be the more detrimental factor as those who indicated they did not trust at all in political institutions had about 46% lower odds of taking vaccinated relative to 24% for trust in health institutions (see Table 4). In contrast, health workers were accorded the greatest trust and by extension legitimacy as respondents who trusted in the health institutions had relatively higher odds of taking the vaccine. Even though this observation may be a reflection of government’s failure to live up to expectations over the years, the emergence of the coronavirus in itself was initially underscored by politicization, with leading political figures even questioning the seriousness of the threat posed by the virus, public health mitigation measures and even the credentials of experts in the field [38].

Study limitations and strengths

While this study contributes to an important but less highlighted component of trust and health in the context of Ghana, there may be some limitations associated with the study worth highlighting. First, the study by design is cross-sectional and our findings only demonstrate associations between gender, trust, and covid-19 vaccines uptake. The findings consequently do not imply causation and should therefore be interpreted with the design of the study in mind. Secondly, the data was self-reported and as is the case with all self-reported data, reporting bias may have been inevitable. For example, respondents’ political affiliations may have biased how they responded to questions of trust in political institutions like the presidency and the ruling party. Finally, quantitative analyses often miss opportunities to engage and understand the lived experiences of respondents and in the case of this study, the underlying drivers of mistrust in institutions. Future research studies should employ mixed qualitative-quantitative methods to comprehensively unpack the underlying drivers of mistrust, gendered dynamics of trust and how they influence health behaviours including vaccines uptake.

Despite the above potential limitations, our study is insightful and offers some policy directions to guide future pandemics and policy programs on trust building. The most important contribution of this study is the observed gendered differences in trust and associated variations in the uptake of the covid-19 vaccines. In spite of the numerous studies on trust and the covid-19 pandemic, the role of gender on trust and how that might influence how individuals respond to pandemic control measures has been timidly explored. Our study highlights the importance of embracing a gendered lens in trust building and leveraging during public health crises. Secondly, the study differentiates the various institutions i.e., social, political and health institutions and how each uniquely and collectively informed trust and the vaccines uptake. At the bivariate level for instance, trust in social institutions was significantly associated with vaccines uptake but completely attenuated in the multivariate models when we accounted for gender and other institutional trust variables.

Conclusion

This study examined the gendered dynamics of institutional trust and how trust may impact covid-19 vaccines uptake in Ghana through a gendered lens. The results show that women were more trusting and respondents who did not have trust in public institutions were generally at lower odds of taking the covid-19 vaccine. Moreover, political institutions especially the presidency was the least trusted institution, which re-emphasise the important role political leaders need to play and how to re-position themselves to effectively fight future pandemics. This is particularly important because building trust in relevant institutions is crucial for the public’s response to policy programmes and mandates that may emerge from those institutions in times of future public health crisis. While it is recommended that public officials take the necessary steps to build confidence and legitimacy in the institutions they occupy, building apolitical alliance across health, political and social institutions, and the scientific community might inure trust and compliance with public health protocols in times of crises. These institutions should maintain high ethical standards and even take further steps to leverage and improve the existing trust the public already has in them. Finally, given the relatively higher trust status among women and higher odds of the vaccine uptake, women groups could be engaged to play active roles in future pandemic management. The Peace Hut Network led by women in post-war Liberia [19] is a typical example of how women can lead the way in building trust during crisis and managing disasters when supported with the necessary resources and tools.
