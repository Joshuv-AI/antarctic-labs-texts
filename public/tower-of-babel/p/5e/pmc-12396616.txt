Cutaneous Chronic Graft-Versus-Host Disease: Clinical Manifestations, Diagnosis, Management, and Supportive Care
Authors: Connie R Shi, Alana L Ferreira, Manjit Kaur, David Xiang, Jean Caputo, Hannah K Choe, Nada Hamad, Edward W Cowen, Benjamin H Kaffenberger, Emily Baumrin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCutaneous chronic graft-versus-host disease (cGVHD) is associated with morbidity, mortality, and impaired quality of life after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. The clinical features of cutaneous cGVHD are heterogeneous but can be broadly classified into nonsclerotic or sclerotic presentations. This review provides an overview of clinical presentation, diagnosis and differential diagnosis, grading, and treatment of cutaneous cGVHD. Particular attention is given to cutaneous cGVHD in skin of color, which can have unique features and is generally underrepresented in the literature leading to delays in diagnosis. Finally, an overview of long-term skin care for patients with cutaneous cGVHD is provided in order to support patients from a dermatologic perspective as they recover from cGVHD. Multidisciplinary care with frequent communication between transplant specialists and dermatologists is critical to effectively managing cutaneous cGVHD.Keywords: Cutaneous graft versus, host disease

INTRODUCTION

Cutaneous chronic graft-versus-host disease (cGVHD) is seen in up to 80% of patients with cGVHD [1–4] and presents early in the disease course of cGVHD [5], making it critical to establishing a cGVHD diagnosis. Furthermore, cutaneous cGVHD is independently associated with impaired quality of life and symptom burden [6,7] and an increased risk of nonrelapse mortality [5,7,8]. Despite its importance to both cGVHD diagnosis and prognosis, the cutaneous manifestations of cGVHD are heterogeneous and challenging to navigate [9]. This review aims to assist transplant clinicians and dermatologists in the diagnosis and treatment of cutaneous cGVHD within the larger context of post-transplant care.

Risk Factors

Risk factors for cutaneous cGVHD generally align with those that increase the risk for cGVHD more generally. However, the use of peripheral blood stem cells and conditioning with total body irradiation are risk factors for the development of cutaneous sclerosis, in particular [10–12]. Onset or flare of cutaneous cGVHD is commonly seen immediately following taper of GVHD prophylactic therapy, after intense ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure (i.e., sunburns) [13], and following tissue injury [14], including after cutaneous viral infections [15–17].

CLASSIFICATION OF CUTANEOUS GVHD

Classification of cutaneous GVHD follows the classification scheme of acute and cGVHD as outlined by the 2014 National Institutes of Health (NIH) Consensus Criteria and is defined by clinical features [18]. Cutaneous manifestations of acute skin GVHD include a morbilliform eruption (Figure 1A), which can progress to desquamation and bullae formation in severe (e.g., grade IV) cases (Figure 1B) [19]. Acute GVHD frequently involves the head, neck, and acral areas and may initially appear folliculocentric (rash around hair follicles) (Figure 1C); however, differentiation from a drug eruption and viral exanthem is difficult clinically and histologically [20–22].

Acute GVHD. (A) Dusky red papules symmetrically distributed on acral skin. (B) Diffuse areas of dusky erythema have coalesced with bullae formation and epidermal sloughing resembling toxic epidermal necrolysis in severe disease. (C) Folliculocentric appearance of macules and papules on the trunk.

While cutaneous cGVHD tends to appear later than acute skin GVHD, onset can occur well before the 100-day mark. It can be further defined as progressive (arising from acute GVHD), de novo (arising without prior acute GVHD), or quiescent (arising after resolved GVHD) [23]. Cutaneous cGVHD is classified based on cutaneous features distinct from acute skin GVHD, including lichen planus-like, poikiloderma-like, morphea-like, and deep sclerotic morphologies, among others (Table 1) [18].

In some cases, a rash resembling acute GVHD can occur in a patient with an established diagnosis of cutaneous chronic or extra-cutaneous cGVHD [18,23]. These patients are classified as overlap cGVHD, which is associated with a poor prognosis [24–27].

CLINICAL PRESENTATION

Cutaneous cGVHD presents heterogeneously and resembles many autoimmune and autoinflammatory skin conditions. Whether clinical heterogeneity reflects aberrations in multiple biologic pathways or dysregulation of a single pathway with protean manifestations remains unknown. Clinical features can broadly be classified into nonsclerotic (refers to all clinical manifestations of cutaneous cGVHD in the absence of sclerosis) and sclerotic (refers to all superficial and deep sclerotic manifestations) as defined by the NIH consensus criteria [18]. Nonsclerotic disease tends to present earlier after transplant compared to sclerosis (median 9 months versus 19 months in one study) and is more responsive to treatment when measured by the body surface area involved [5]. While sclerotic cutaneous cGVHD is generally considered a highly morbid form of cutaneous cGVHD given the progressive and irreversible nature [28], transplant patients with “skin of color” self-reported a greater quality of life detriment from nonsclerotic disease and its associated dyspigmentation than with sclerosis [29].

Nonsclerotic Manifestations

The authors and the dermatologic community prefer the term nonsclerotic to the previously used term “lichenoid GVHD” because of the clinical heterogeneity of nonsclerotic disease. Lichen planus-like cGVHD, however, is one of the more common presentations of nonsclerotic disease. Lesions are pink to purple, well defined, and with adherent scale, resembling idiopathic lichen planus (Figure 2A), but often more extensive and involving atypical areas such as the face (Figure 2B–D), neck, palms, and soles. Erythema can often present more subtly in darker skin tones due to baseline melanin content. For example, in patients with darker skin tones this may appear more violaceous (purple) rather than brightly erythematous (Figure 2C,D). Other common nonsclerotic presentations include poikiloderma (Figure 3), psoriasis-like lesions, eczema-like lesions, and keratosis pilaris-like lesions. Early manifestations of nonsclerotic cutaneous GVHD can include dry, scaly, and pruritic skin. Patients who experience the onset of such signs or symptoms should report them to their transplant care team, particularly if they do not respond as expected to usual dry skin care and emollients.

Dyspigmentation (hypo- and hyperpigmentation) and ichthyosis (dry, thickened, or scaly changes) commonly follow the resolution of the aforementioned clinical features and are not considered active diseases when grading disease severity (Figure 4A,B) [30]. However, these clinical features of damage can be challenging to treat and significantly impact quality of life [29]. Pruritus can be a good clue to the activity of nonsclerotic disease and has been associated with mortality [31].

Hair involvement is common in chronic skin GVHD and presents as nonscarring (Figure 5A,B) and scarring alopecia [32]. Pruritus, scaling, and rash on the scalp can be a clue to the latter and should be treated aggressively to prevent permanent hair loss. Nail involvement can range from mild (longitudinal ridging and splitting) [33] to severe (anonychia, pterygium) and can cause significant functional impairment with activities of daily living (Figure 6). Involvement of the adnexal structures (e.g., hair, nails) has also been associated with more severe oral and ocular involvement [34].

Alopecia. (A) Diffuse loss of scalp density and (B) focal areas of complete loss consistent with alopecia areata in a pediatric patient after allogeneic stem cell transplantation.

Nail changes. Nail abnormalities range from prominent longitudinal ridging (onychorrhexis, 3rd digit) thin nail plate with nail splitting (2nd digit) to complete nail loss with scarring (anonychia, 4th and 5th digits).

Cutaneous cGVHD can manifest similarly to clinical presentations of autoimmune skin conditions such as vitiligo and alopecia areata (Figure 5A,B)—which has been reported in 5% to 20% of cutaneous cGVHD patients [35–37]—cutaneous lupus erythematosus [38], and dermatomyositis [39]. It remains unclear if the pathobiology of these manifestations resembles cGVHD or distinct autoimmune conditions. Autoantibodies, however, are not independent risk factors for developing cGVHD [11,40].

Sclerotic Manifestations

Sclerosis can present superficially in the dermis or deep in the subcutaneous tissue and fascia and can occur with or without prior nonsclerotic disease [5]. Superficial sclerotic manifestations are often clearly defined by visual inspection and the change in dermal thickness can be confirmed by gently pinching the skin. Clinical manifestations include lichen sclerosus-like (more superficial, involving the papillary dermis) (Figure 7) and morphea-like lesions (deeper, involving the reticular dermis and subcutis) (Figure 8), which tend to involve the trunk, proximal extremities, and waistband area. In lichen sclerosus-like cGVHD, patients may initially note dryness and wrinkling of the skin (often referred to as cigarette paper-like texture) that appears white and shiny. In morphea-like cGVHD, patients may initially develop round or oval plaques that progressively become indurated and firmer and may have a shiny appearance. When sclerotic cGVHD crosses over joints, patients may perceive tightness or pulling sensation with movement or activity. Deep sclerosis and fascial disease can be heralded by an edematous phase, which can often be difficult to distinguish from other causes of volume overload or third-spacing of fluid. In contrast to other sclerosing disorders (e.g., scleroderma), cGVHD tends to spare the face and digits (Figure 9A,B) [41,42]. Clinical clues to sclerosis include shiny skin with loss of hair follicles, a “pipestem” appearance of the lower extremities (Figure 10A,B), a cellulite-like appearance in fat-bearing areas (Figure 11), and decreased ability to pinch the skin. Sequelae of sclerosis include pulmonary dysfunction from chest wall restriction and impaired diaphragmatic excursion, limited range of motion, chronic ulcers, GVHD-associated angiomatosis, calcinosis, and the development of advanced squamous cell carcinomas (SCCs).

While vulvovaginal and penile cGVHD are beyond the scope of this section, genital cGVHD is likely underdiagnosed and undertreated in routine post-transplant care [4,7,43,44]. An external genital examination is important in the context of symptoms or cutaneous and or oral GVHD, with abnormalities triggering referral to gynecology or urology to evaluate for vaginal or urethral involvement.

PRESENTATION IN SKIN OF COLOR

There is limited literature and availability of clinical images of cutaneous cGVHD in patients with skin of color of various pigmentation, which is a barrier to recognition and can cause delays in diagnosis for both patients and clinicians. Signs of active cutaneous inflammation may be more difficult to visually appreciate in skin of color which can lead to missed or later diagnosis. Assessments of cutaneous cGVHD activity in darker skin tones require close attention to subjective symptoms of itch and irritation and close physical examination for signs of cutaneous inflammation. Nonsclerotic disease lesions (e.g., lichen planus-like, psoriasis-like) in darker skin tones can appear brown to dark purple depending on baseline pigmentation and resemble postinflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) (Figure 2C,D). A slate-gray appearance in sun-exposed areas can be a clue to active disease instead of resolved pigmentary changes. Sclerotic cutaneous cGVHD may present with a brown or gray hue in more pigmented skin (Figure 10B). Postinflammatory pigmentation changes result from irregular deposition of pigmentation in the skin following skin inflammation and are more commonly seen in more heavily pigmented skin after resolution of active cutaneous cGVHD (Figure 4). These pigmentary changes can persist for many months to years in patients with skin of color with associated quality of life implications [29].

DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIA AND PROCESS

Diagnosis can be guided by the 2014 NIH diagnostic criteria which is informed by clinical manifestations and pathology findings [18]. Clinical features can be divided into (1) diagnostic: sufficient for diagnosis and not requiring additional testing; (2) distinctive: insufficient for diagnosis and requiring additional testing; (3) other: acknowledged as a cGVHD feature if diagnosis confirmed elsewhere; (4) common: seen in both acute and chronic skin GVHD (Table 1). A survey of 72 transplant centers in 3 countries supported the NIH recommendations that skin biopsy is indicated in suspected cutaneous cGVHD lacking diagnostic features [22]. Early diagnosis is critical to initiating appropriate treatments for cutaneous cGVHD and limiting the quality of life interruptions and morbidity that can be associated with cutaneous cGVHD activity. Nonsclerotic disease often presents early in the course of cGVHD and lichenoid morphology of cutaneous cGVHD has been associated with poor outcomes and mortality previously [45]. Sclerotic disease can be very difficult to treat and responds slowly to therapy, and prompt recognition and treatment can prevent irreversible sclerotic change. Patients in whom cutaneous cGVHD is diagnosed should be referred to transplant centers for multidisciplinary management with transplant specialists and dermatologists.

Physical Examination and Biopsy

A thorough physical examination with adequate exposure of skin areas is critical to diagnosing cutaneous cGVHD. Skin examination not only includes skin inspection but also palpation for abnormalities in skin thickness. Abduction and external rotation of the arms about the shoulder joints and the thighs about the hip joints can reveal grooving (Figure 12) and cellulite-like changes in the deep subcutaneous tissues suggestive of fascial disease. The GVHD Photographic Range of Motion assessment can also assist in identifying contractures and range of motion limitations consistent with fascial GVHD [18]. Presence of diagnostic features of cutaneous cGVHD is sufficient to establish the clinical diagnosis (Table 1).

Fascial cGVHD. Prominent linear grooving may be seen along the path of superficial vessels or demarcating muscle/fascial groups, as in the popliteal fossae in this patient with subcutaneous sclerotic disease.

A skin biopsy can provide valuable insight when clinical features are not diagnostic [46]. In the authors’ experience, a 4 mm punch biopsy is sufficient for nonsclerotic disease, and a 5 to 6 mm punch biopsy to the depth of the subcutaneous fat is adequate to evaluate sclerotic chronic skin GVHD, though fascia is rarely visualized. Due to the potential for delayed wound healing, particularly from skin biopsies taken of sclerotic involvement of the lower extremities, caution should be exercised prior to biopsy of these areas. In addition, it may not be necessary to obtain a skin biopsy if the skin findings are clinically diagnostic of sclerotic cutaneous cGVHD.

The histology of cutaneous cGVHD can evolve, overlap with features of acute GVHD, and be difficult to distinguish from other skin conditions, infections, or drug reactions [47–49]. Histologic criteria for nonsclerotic GVHD include apoptosis in the basilar layer [50,51], vacuolar changes [52], lichenoid inflammation, and lymphocyte satellitosis [53–55], but biopsy may not be diagnostic [47]. Sclerosis can present with thickened collagen bundles at any level of the dermis or subcutis with or without overlying vacuolar changes (Figure 13) [54,56].

Review of Systems

Considering the multisystem nature of cGVHD, a comprehensive review of systems is critical to assess other organ involvement. Identifying abnormalities in extra-cutaneous organs can help confirm the diagnosis of cutaneous cGVHD when diagnostic features are evident in noncutaneous organs such as lichenoid changes in the mouth. No diagnostic biomarker has been validated for skin GVHD or cGVHD in general.

Special Tests

Sclerotic and fascial cGVHD can often be difficult to diagnose, given its indolent nature and clinical overlap with other lower extremity disorders (Figure 14). MRI can be an additional tool to demonstrate active disease [57], detecting pathological changes in the deep soft tissues or heightened signal intensity in the superficial and deep fasciae [57–59]. Furthermore, use of contrast may allow for estimating the degree of inflammation [60]. Other techniques, such as high-frequency ultrasound, digital heat mapping, myotonometry, and durometry, have been explored in sclerotic GVHD. However, they have not been validated for routine use in clinical practice [60].

Differential Diagnosis

Cutaneous cGVHD is clinically heterogeneous, with a distinct differential diagnosis depending on the clinical manifestation (Figure 14). Lichen-planus-like cGVHD (Figure 15) can present similarly to a lichenoid drug eruption, tinea corporis, psoriasis, subacute cutaneous lupus erythematosus, secondary syphilis, mycosis fungoides, and, of course, idiopathic lichen planus. Lichenoid drug eruptions can be caused by common medications (e.g., ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers, thiazide diuretics, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and allopurinol) and may not develop until several months after drug initiation.

Lichen planus-like cutaneous GVHD on the dorsal hands.

Deep sclerosis of the lower legs is challenging to diagnose and differentiate from chronic venous stasis, lipodermatosclerosis, and, less commonly, scleroderma, eosinophilic fasciitis, or nephrogenic systemic fibrosis. A biopsy may be nondiagnostic, and the presence or absence of associated features (e.g., varicose veins, brawny hyperpigmentation in the setting of chronic venous stasis) is critical.

Grading of Cutaneous cGVHD

The 2005 NIH Consensus criteria, updated and revised in 2014, is the most widely used tool for clinical scoring of cGVHD and overall severity assessment. It is a 4-point scoring system (0–3) based on the extent of involvement across organ systems and is scored for each site as 0 representing no involvement, 1 as mild, 2 as moderate, and 3 reflecting severe impairment [18,46]. Cutaneous cGVHD features incorporated into the scoring system include body surface area involvement by rash, involvement of sclerotic features, fascial involvement, and genital, ocular, and oral involvement, among others [18]. By combining organ and site-specific scores, assessment of the need for therapy can be conducted consistently across clinicians and investigators. The global severity assessment is graded as mild, moderate, or severe depending on the extent and severity of involvement of the affected organ systems [18,60].

Other grading systems that have been utilized in the cGVHD population include the Lee symptom scale, a rating system validated and designed exclusively for cGVHD patients [61], and the Short Form 36 [62] among others [63–65]. Further attempts to establish more robust–skin-specific staging methods have not performed better at predicting disease mortality [7,66]. Additional scoring systems for cutaneous cGVHD that have been studied include body surface area assessments [5,67], modified Rodnan scoring assessments [68], various physician global assessments [60], and skin punch biopsy to detect the change in fibrosis depth from a target location [69] versus mechanical or imaging approaches [60,65]. Automated algorithms using artificial intelligence have been developed to identify cGVHD and measure the affected skin in photographs [70,71]. Other novel techniques include the use of serial imaging [71,72] and image segmentation [73] to more precisely delineate active from quiescent cGVHD. However, while an erythema score [5] has been shown to predict survival outcomes in cGVHD, these techniques still need further validation.

Treatments

Treatments for cutaneous cGVHD can be categorized into skin-directed and systemic treatment approaches. The type of treatment often depends on the presence of other organ involvement and severity score. Skin-directed therapy (e.g., topical steroids) is often appropriate to initiate immediately. Systemic therapy should consider other post-transplant complications such as risk of infection, symptomatology and impact of GVHD on the patient’s quality of life, and risk of irreversible tissue damage. If possible, patients requiring systemic therapies should be referred to transplant centers for multidisciplinary consultation with transplant specialists and dermatologists.

Skin-Directed Therapies

Topical corticosteroids and calcineurin inhibitors

Topical corticosteroids are the primary choice of topical treatment for limited, mild forms of cutaneous cGVHD and are beneficial in papulosquamous, eczematous, lichen planus-like, lichen sclerosis-like, and other superficially sclerotic forms. They can also be helpful in combination with systemic therapy for moderate to severe forms of cutaneous cGVHD. Topical steroids come in a variety of strengths and formulations and choice of the appropriate agent varies depending on the body site affected and severity of rash (Table 2). Cutaneous complications of long-term topical steroid use include dermal atrophy, striae, and telangiectasias, and therefore it is typically recommended that patients take a break from topical steroid use for 1 to 2 weeks per month of use and consider discontinuing or switching to a lower potency topical steroid if adverse effects are observed. Topical calcineurin inhibitors, such as topical tacrolimus ointment or topical pimecrolimus cream, can be useful options in areas in which topical steroid side effects would optimally be avoided, such as on the face or lips, or cycled as an alternative to topical steroid use, as they do not carry a risk of atrophy, striae, or telangiectasias. The benefits of topical tacrolimus were demonstrated in several small studies [74,75], and in one study the use of topical tacrolimus in addition to systemic therapies benefitted 13/18 cGVHD patients with a reduction in scaling, pain, or tightness [74]. However, caution is advised for the use of tacrolimus ointment on large surface areas as systemic absorption resulting in toxic levels has been reported in cGVHD patients [76]. Neither topical steroids nor topical tacrolimus offer much benefit in deeper sclerotic forms of the disease.

Alternative topical agents

A recently published article detailed the use of topical 5-fluorouracil cream specifically for cGVHD that present in a photo-distributed pattern [77]. The hypothesized benefit results from a decrease in mutational burden in the skin and reduced abnormal epitopes to be targeted by the grafted immune system, however further research is needed. Interim results of a prospective, randomized, vehicle and comparator-controlled phase 2 ongoing trial of topical ruxolitinib 1.5% cream in adults with nonsclerotic and superficially sclerotic cGVHD showed a reduction of involved body surface area and improvement in lesion severity on the treatment side versus vehicle side [78].

Phototherapy

Phototherapy inactivates antigen-presenting cells and activated T-cells resulting in local immunomodulation. Over time this leads to antigen tolerance while avoiding systemic immunosuppression. Subtypes of phototherapy include PUVA (psoralen + UVA with 320–400 nm spectrum), UVA1 (340–400 nm), and narrow-band UV B (311–313 nm), which differ in the wavelength of light utilized and the depth of cutaneous penetration. Phototherapy is a nonimmunosuppressive treatment and therefore may be a useful adjunctive agent in patients for whom additional immunosuppression may be less preferred. Depending on the availability and geographic proximity of phototherapy centers, patients can often receive phototherapy in the community setting. Data on efficacy, however, is limited by small sample sizes and lack of controlled trials evaluating its effectiveness.

Narrow-band UVB (nbUVB)

The available evidence suggests that nbUVB can be a beneficial treatment for certain forms of cutaneous cGVHD and is typically more widely available than PUVA and UVA1 phototherapy, though studies on its efficacy in cGVHD are limited in size. Due to the superficial cutaneous depth of penetration of nbUVB wavelengths, it is likely more beneficial for nonsclerotic involvement than sclerotic involvement. In one report, 4/5 patients with lichenoid disease achieved complete remission [79]. In another report, 1 patient with lichenoid disease had complete remission while 2 other patients with sclerotic disease showed significant symptomatic improvement [80]. However, PUVA and UVA1 are considered better alternatives for deeper sclerotic forms of the disease.

PUVA

PUVA has been successfully used for cutaneous cGVHD with a reduction in systemic immunosuppression therapy [81,82]. For PUVA, patients may ingest 8-methoxypsoralen, a light-sensitizing agent, at least an hour before UVA phototherapy. In a variant called bath PUVA, the patient soaks in topical diluted light-sensitizing agent prior to phototherapy and has been used in cases of cutaneous cGVHD with some effect [83] Additionally, when compared to oral PUVA therapy, bath PUVA has better patient compliance as it bypasses the systemic adverse effects of oral therapy and lower UVA doses are needed [84]. However, skin ulceration has been reported as a complication with the use of bath PUVA therapy in patients with sclerotic cGVHD in the early treatment period [85]. PUVA is associated with heightened risk of cutaneous malignancies and routine skin cancer screening is recommended.

UVA1

UVA1 is primarily used in sclerotic diseases such as morphea because of its ability to penetrate the dermis where it acts by upregulating collagenases, thus softening the skin [86]. However, there is a shortage of facilities offering UVA1 because of costly equipment and the need for long treatment sessions. Several case reports have suggested its efficacy in highly pretreated patients [87,88]. In a case series, among six patients diagnosed with sclerodermatous cGVHD, UVA1 therapy led to an improvement in cutaneous condition as well as joint mobility [89]. UVA1 may be more suited for the sclerotic type cGVHD as suggested by results of one study wherein 3/5 patients with sclerotic disease achieved complete remission and 2/5 had partial improvement in an average of 21 sessions while patients with lichenoid type experienced a relapse within a month of discontinuing treatment sessions and required prolonged maintenance therapy [90].

Systemic therapies

Systemic corticosteroids are the first line of systemic therapy for newly diagnosed moderate to severe cGVHD. Systemic steroids are typically initiated at a dose of 0.5 to 1 mg/kg prednisone equivalent daily and gradually tapered if improvement is seen [91]. However, if patients do not sufficiently improve with systemic steroids, an agent for steroid-refractory GVHD should be considered. Currently, ibrutinib, ruxolitinib, and belumosudil are Food and Drug Administration-approved for use in steroid-refractory cGVHD. The overall response rate (ORR) in cGVHD was 67% in the open-label phase II trial of ibrutinib, and cutaneous responses occurred in 88% of subjects, noting however that subjects were required to have erythematous-type skin disease at trial entry [92]. In the randomized REACH3 trial of ruxolitinib, in which 70% of enrolled patients had cutaneous involvement, skin response in the ruxolitinib-treated group (41.2%) was higher compared to the control group (15.2%) [93]. A meta-analysis of the use of ruxolitinib in steroid-resistant cGVHD demonstrated best ORRs for oral disease, followed by skin and GI, suggesting it may be a preferred choice for patients with cutaneous involvement [94]. Similarly, in the pivotal trial of belumosudil, 83% patients had cutaneous disease and the ORR in the skin was 37%. Out of 41 patients with a reported skin response, 15 had a decrease in BSA involvement, 11 had a decrease in fibrosis and 13 had both a decrease in fibrosis and involved BSA [95,96]. If these options are ineffective, consideration can be given to additional agents such as calcineurin inhibitors, extracorporeal photopheresis, rituximab, or emerging candidate treatments and clinical trials.

Extracorporeal photophoresis (ECP)

The exact mechanism of action of ECP in cGVHD is unknown but its role is similar to cutaneous photochemotherapy, albeit ex vivo prior to the immune cells being reinfused into the patient. Mononuclear cells are collected from the patient and 8-methoxypsoralen is added. Subsequently, the product is exposed to UVA and then reinfused back into the patient [97]. Evidence suggests that ECP is a well-tolerated and beneficial form of treatment for cGVHD with reports ranging from partial response to complete remission in patients [98–102]. ECP led to best responses in cutaneous, liver, oral, and ocular disease, with improvements seen in 59% of patients with cutaneous GVHD, both with sclerotic and nonsclerotic involvement [100]. In a prospective study, a decrease in standard immunosuppression among patients was observed and significant improvement specifically in cutaneous cGVHD was noted in the nonblinded analysis [98]. Limitations of ECP include the lengthy time needed for treatments, often lasting for several hours one to two times weekly for months, as well as the need for reliable venous access with potential risk for infection in patients requiring central catheter placement for access. Furthermore, centers that offer ECP may not be available across at smaller medical centers and would require extensive travel for some patients, limiting potential access to this treatment.

Other systemic agents

Many other systemic agents have been variably studied in steroid-refractory GVHD, including axatilimab, rituximab, and imatinib, among other agents. Axatilimab is a humanized IgG4 monoclonal antibody that acts by inhibiting the colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF-1R) signaling pathway and the development of profibrotic macrophages. A recent phase I/phase II study in refractory cGVHD showed an 82% response rate in 40 patients aged ≥6 years and responses were noted in all organ systems irrespective of previous therapy [103]. Rituximab, a monoclonal CD20 chimeric antibody, was shown in a meta-analysis to yield variable cutaneous response rates between 0% and 83%, with significant limitations of small study sample sizes and variable criteria among studies for reporting outcomes [104]. Patients with mild to moderate skin and oral disease might have greater benefits as compared to those with advanced disease [105]. In one case report, rituximab was used to treat a patient with a mucous membrane pemphigoid-like presentation of oral cGVHD with oral blisters and erosions, leading to clinical improvement [106]. Imatinib, a multiple tyrosine kinase inhibitors, works by modulating transforming growth factor-β and platelet-derived growth factor, among other pathways. Its prescribed dose is 400 mg daily in adults. Initial studies of imatinib use in the sclerotic type of cGVHD showed response rates of 50% [107], but in larger case studies, roughly 30% of patients responded [108,109]. In a prospective cross-over study of imatinib versus rituximab for sclerotic cutaneous GVHD, the significant clinical response rates at 6 months were equivalent, at 26% and 27%, respectively [110].

Supportive care with chronic cutaneous GVHD

Cutaneous cGVHD can significantly impact patients’ quality of life for years even after being treated for GVHD. Recognition and attention to these important aspects of recovery are essential to post-transplant care. Diligent surveillance for skin malignancies in the post-transplant population and prompt management of wounds that can predispose to infection are important to prevent secondary complications arising from cGVHD and its treatment. Finally, long-term cutaneous consequences such as permanent pigmentary changes in the skin can significantly impact patients’ quality of life after having recovered from GVHD.

Skin cancer screening and prevention

Hematopoietic stem-cell transplant is a significant risk factor for developing skin cancers, including basal cell carcinoma (BCC), SCC, and melanoma (Figure 16) [111]. Having cGVHD and receiving immunosuppressive treatment for cGVHD are both associated with an increased risk of developing SCC and BCC [112]. For example, ruxolitinib has been shown to increase the risk of SCC and BCC (HR 2.69, 95% CI 1.03–7.02) with a particularly higher risk of SCC specifically (HR, 3.24; 95% CI, 1.45–7.22), though this study examined its use in myelofibrosis treatment rather than in cGVHD management [113]. Age <18 years at transplantation has been associated with the development of nonmelanoma skin cancer and myeloablative total body irradiation conditioning has been associated with the development of both nonmelanoma skin cancers and melanoma [114]. Additional risk factors for the development of skin cancers include prior radiation therapy and chronic inflammation of the skin, such as from longstanding, nonhealing wounds that can occur with sclerotic cutaneous GVHD. Due to chronic immunosuppression, skin cancers can also present more aggressively in patients who have undergone HSCT.

(A) Melanoma in situ presenting as an asymmetrically pigmented patch on the shoulder, (B) Squamous cell carcinoma presenting as a crusted, nonhealing hyperkeratotic papule on the forearm, both in patients who had undergone HSCT.

Patients should be referred to dermatology for annual full-body skin exams depending on risk factors for skin cancer [114]. In addition to transplant-related risk factors described above, general risk factors for the development of skin cancers include extent of UV radiation exposure [115], history of tanning bed use [116], having skin that sunburns easily or does not tan [115], history of many blistering sunburns [117], and for melanoma specifically, a personal or family history of having melanoma or having many atypical moles [118,119]. Patients should be counseled to alert their care team for any rapidly growing, changing, bleeding, or chronically nonhealing skin lesions that may be suggestive of skin malignancy. Additionally, sun protection with application of sunscreen of at least sun protective factor 30, sun-protective clothing, and avoidance of tanning, blistering sunburns, and excessive sun exposure can significantly reduce UV light-related skin damage that predisposes to skin cancer development and should be emphasized in all cutaneous cGVHD patients [120]. In addition to increasing the risk of skin cancer, UV radiation has also been demonstrated to induce or exacerbate cutaneous GVHD [13], and therefore rigorous sun protection is an essential component of preventive cGVHD care. Medications that may increase cutaneous sensitivity to sun exposure (photosensitivity), such as trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, or induce both photosensitivity and skin cancer (e.g., voriconazole) [121], should be monitored carefully. Should skin cancer develop during treatment with voriconazole, an alternative antifungal agent should be prescribed [122].

Wound and infectious management

Areas of sclerotic cutaneous GVHD have particularly poor wound healing, particularly when ulcers develop on the lower extremities (Figure 17) [11]. Peripheral edema and concomitant venous stasis can further complicate healing and should be addressed with compression (if tolerated and not contraindicated by peripheral arterial disease). Because of this, it is important to aggressively manage new and developing wounds to prevent further deterioration. While supportive measures such as emollients, skin cleansers, wound dressings, and antimicrobial agents may be used, if severe, patients may require skin grafts [123,124]. For severe ulcerations, interdisciplinary consultation with dermatology, plastic surgery, and wound care teams is advised. Chronically nonhealing wounds may also necessitate skin biopsy to evaluate for underlying skin cancer in the area.

(A) Ulceration on the medial malleolus of a patient with sclerotic and myofascial GVHD and concomitant venous stasis. (B) Traumatic injury on the lower leg resulting in a difficult-to-heal ulceration in a patient with sclerotic GVHD.

To reduce the risk of infection of erosions and ulcerations in areas of sclerotic cutaneous GVHD, dilute acetic acid, dilute sodium hypochlorite and hypochlorous acid solutions may be used for cleansing the wound daily [125]. Dressings with antimicrobial properties include those that contain silver, gentian violet, methylene blue, or iodine, among others. Patients should be counseled to moisturize the skin frequently with emollients and avoid trauma to the skin which can predispose to wounds developing.

GVHD-associated angiomatosis

Patients with chronic cutaneous GVHD can develop benign reactive vascular proliferations within areas of sclerosis, termed GVHD-associated angiomatosis [126]. Developing a median of 44 months after transplant, this eruption is characterized by asymptomatic vascular papules and nodules, most frequently arising on the lower extremities and trunk (Figure 18). The clinical differential for such lesions can include angiomas, pyogenic granulomas, and angiokeratomas. The exact etiology is unclear and varying treatments have been attempted with some cases of success, including external-beam radiation [127], shave biopsy with electrocautery [126], and oral propranolol and sirolimus [126]. Thalidomide was found to have no benefit [127,128].

Pigmentary alterations and considerations in skin of color

Both hyperpigmentation and hypopigmentation can be seen after cutaneous GVHD as postinflammatory manifestations rather than signals of active disease (Figure 4). As noted previously, however, active cutaneous involvement can present more subtly in skin of color and therefore care should be taken to distinguish between active cutaneous GVHD and postinflammatory pigmentary changes and it is important to not automatically attribute darkening of the skin to inactive postinflammatory change only. In a study of skin of color patients with cutaneous cGVHD, patients with dyspigmentation self-rated these changes as more bothersome than patients with active sclerotic involvement rated their disease [29]. Such dyspigmentation can persist despite interventions, and though current cGVHD quality of life and symptom severity measures do not specifically ask about pigmentation [29], dermatologists should actively discuss these potential consequences especially, with patients with skin of color, and be proactive in management of pigmentary changes should they occur given the risks of quality of life implications especially in this population [29,125,129].

PIH can be challenging to treat [130]. First, any active ongoing cutaneous GVHD should be addressed with GVHD-directed treatment. Patients should be counseled that pigmentary changes generally gradually resolve over time, though it can take months to years to see improvement. Patients with more heavily pigmented skin and more intense cutaneous inflammation will experience slower improvement in PIH, and expectations surrounding the rate of improvement should be discussed. Patients should be counseled to practice diligent sun protection, as UV exposure will exacerbate PIH and inhibit improvement.

If patients desire to treat PIH, topical treatments such as hydroquinone combined with topical retinoids (such as tretinoin, adapalene, or tazarotene) and topical steroids can be considered. An example of such a formulation includes hydroquinone 4%, tretinoin 0.05%, and fluocinolone 0.01%. Patients should be counseled about the risk of paradoxical ochronosis, in which brown-black discoloration results from chronic topical hydroquinone usage, and use of hydroquinone-containing agents should be limited to a few months’ duration with careful monitoring of side effects. Formulations that contain a topical steroid ingredient should also be used with caution on the face given risks of skin atrophy, striae, and telangiectasias with long-term topical steroid use on areas of thinner skin. Topical retinoids may cause dryness and irritation but are likely safe to use. Additional agents that may help with PIH include topical azelaic acid, kojic acid, vitamin C (ascorbic acid), and niacinamide, among others [130], though studies on efficacy are limited overall and none have specifically been studied in the cutaneous GVHD population.

Cosmetic interventions

Patients with cutaneous cGVHD may occasionally inquire about cosmetic procedural interventions such as laser or chemical peels both for reasons related and unrelated to skin changes from cGVHD such as to address pigmentation, uneven skin texture, or aging. This area is not well-studied; controlled, large-scale studies of efficacy and safety are not available in the cGVHD population. A few cases have described applications of laser, such as CO2 laser, for the treatment of oral GVHD [131] and sclerodermatous GVHD [132]; however, laser treatment is not well-studied for cosmetic improvement of skin appearance due to pigmentary or other skin changes resulting from cGVHD. Laser treatment causes tissue injury, which is associated with exacerbating cutaneous GVHD. Any treatment pursued primarily for the purposes of cosmetic benefit must be carefully weighed against patients’ level of cutaneous cGVHD activity and immunosuppression. In the authors’ experience, patients should be off immunosuppressive therapy prior to commencing any cosmetic procedural interventions and their cGVHD should be quiescent and well-controlled.

CONCLUSION

Early detection and management of cGVHD are crucial in improving patients’ quality of life and survival [133]. As the skin is often the first organ impacted, timely communication of cutaneous cGVHD diagnosis with the multi-disciplinary team is paramount. Knowledge of skin involvement in cGVHD will dictate the screening trajectory for other organs, including pulmonary function tests, ocular and gynecological examinations, and laboratory evaluation [114]. Given the complexity of cutaneous cGVHD, dermatologists play a crucial role in its diagnosis and should serve as a resource to transplant clinicians to identify new manifestations of cGVHD skin involvement, monitor response to therapy, and diagnose skin complications, including infections and malignancy. A variety of skin-directed therapies and an increasing armamentarium of systemic treatment options are available to treat patients with cutaneous cGVHD depending on the severity and level of cutaneous involvement. Finally, cutaneous cGVHD has substantial, lasting impacts on patients’ quality of life especially in patients with skin of color, and necessitates diligent long-term dermatologic follow-up for secondary cutaneous complications associated with cGVHD and its treatments.
