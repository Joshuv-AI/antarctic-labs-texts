Emerging significance of E3 ubiquitin ligases and Deubiquitinases in pulmonary hypertension
Authors: Chao-Wei Ding, Jia-Yong Qiu, Hui Shen, Yi Yan, Ze-Xin Liu, Shen-Shen Huang, Yue-Jiao Ma, Zhi-Cheng Jing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPulmonary hypertension (PH) is a progressive, life-threatening cardiovascular disorder. It features irreversible pulmonary vascular remodeling and causes right ventricular failure and mortality. The underlying pathogenesis of PH is incompletely elucidated. Ubiquitination is a reversible post-translational modification. It regulates protein degradation and membrane trafficking and contributes critically to PH development and progression. The ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), especially E3 ubiquitin ligases and Deubiquitinases, modulates the function of pulmonary artery endothelial cells and pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells in PH. These molecules exert distinct roles and regulatory mechanisms in PH-related signaling pathways. The pathways include bone morphogenetic protein, nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), hypoxia inducible factor-1α, P53, Hippo, and mitochondrial quality control. UPS-targeted small-molecule agents, proteasome inhibitors, and proteolysis-targeting chimeras have therapeutic potential for PH. They also face notable translational challenges. Ubiquitination provides new mechanistic insights into PH pathogenesis and identifies innovative avenues for targeted therapy.Keywords: Pulmonary hypertension, Ubiquitin ligase, Ubiquitination, Deubiquitination, Therapeutic targets

Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a cardiovascular disorder associated with high morbidity and mortality, primarily resulting from irreversible pulmonary vascular remodeling and dysfunction of pulmonary arterial endothelial cells (PAECs) and pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) (Humbert et al. 2019). According to the 6th World Symposium on Pulmonary Hypertension (WSPH) classification, PH is categorized into five distinct groups based on etiology, pathological features, and therapeutic strategies (Simonneau et al. 2019). Among these subgroups, pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), a typical pre-capillary PH, is a rare and severe subtype diagnosed by right heart catheterization with the following hemodynamic criteria: a mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) ≥ 20 mmHg at rest, a pulmonary arterial wedge pressure (PAWP) ≤ 15 mmHg, and a pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) ≥ 3 Wood units. Clinically and mechanistically, PAH is distinguished by selective pathological remodeling of small distal pulmonary arteries, strong genetic susceptibility (e.g., bone morphogenetic protein type II receptor [BMPR2] gene mutations), and progressive pulmonary vascular dysfunction independent of left heart or parenchymal lung disease, making it the most extensively studied PH subtype in the context of molecular regulatory mechanisms (Ruopp and Cockrill 2022).

Epidemiological data indicate that PH affects approximately 1% of the global population, with prevalence rates reaching up to 10% among individuals above the age of 65 years (Hoeper et al. 2016). Current pharmacological treatments for PH predominantly focus on regulating vasodilatory pathways and include agents such as inhaled nitric oxide, phosphodiesterase inhibitors, calcium channel blockers, prostaglandins, and endothelin receptor antagonists. However, existing therapies may not effectively halt disease progression in all patients and struggle to suppress or reverse vascular remodeling. Consequently, there is a critical need to further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying PH and to develop novel therapeutic strategies aimed at slowing disease progression and improving clinical outcomes (Hoeper et al. 2016; Humbert et al. 2022).

In recent years, researchers have discovered that the pathophysiological mechanisms of PH bear numerous similarities to those observed in cancer. These similarities include excessive cell proliferation, migration capabilities, resistance to apoptosis, evasion of growth suppression, and endothelial–mesenchymal transition (Cool et al. 2020). Ubiquitination represents one of the most prevalent post-translational modifications affecting proteins. The ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) serves as a key therapeutic target in oncology and supports the development of several novel anticancer agents. In line with these shared pathological features, the UPS also a critical role of in regulating PH pathogenesis, with multiple E3 ligases and Deubiquitinases (DUBs) identified as promising therapeutic targets. Individual studies have explored specific UPS components in PH. However, no comprehensive review has systematically integrated these findings across all core pathogenic pathways or addressed their clinical translational potential. In this review, we summarize the latest progress in this field, providing a clear framework for future research and therapeutic development.

Ubiquitin, E3 ubiquitin ligases, and Deubiquitinases

Ubiquitin is a small protein consisting of 76 amino acid residues and is widely distributed across eukaryotic cells and tissues. The ubiquitination process mediated by the UPS, one of the most essential pathways in cellular protein metabolism, involves three types of enzymes (Li et al. 2022). Initially, the substrate protein is activated by a ubiquitin-activating enzyme (E1) in an ATP-dependent manner, resulting in its conjugation with a ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme (E2). Subsequently, the ubiquitin ligase enzyme (E3) transfers the activated ubiquitin to the lysine residues of the target protein, marking it for recognition and degradation by the proteasome or inducing functional alterations (Fig. 1) (Li et al. 2022).

The ubiquitination process mediated by the ubiquitin–proteasome system

The process of modifying the substrate protein at a single lysine residue is called mono-ubiquitylation, and modifying multiple lysine residues on the same substrate is termed multi-mono-ubiquitylation. By contrast, polyubiquitylation involves the sequential addition of ubiquitin molecules. In this process, new ubiquitin moieties are covalently attached to the carboxyl terminus of previously conjugated ubiquitin proteins, forming a polyubiquitin chain (Yin et al. 2020). Polyubiquitin chains can be classified into eight distinct types (K6, K11, K27, K33, K48, K63, M1), with K48-linked ubiquitin chains, (requiring a minimum of four ubiquitin proteins) being recognized by the 26 S proteasome, leading to the degradation of the substrate protein. Alternatively, K63-linked ubiquitin chains are primarily involved in proteolytic processes mediated by the autophagy-lysosomal pathway and are implicated in cellular processes such as mitophagy, protein transport, and innate immunity (Renz et al. 2024). DUBs have the opposite function of E3, capable of selectively cleaving ubiquitin chains on target proteins, preventing them from being recognized and degraded by the proteasome or reversing their corresponding functions.

Classification of E3 ubiquitin ligases

E3 ubiquitin ligases determine the specificity of ubiquitin attachment to substrate proteins and are considered critical components of the UPS. To date, researchers have identified over 600 distinct E3 ligases. Based on their structural domains and mechanisms for recognizing target protein sequences, E3 ligases are broadly classified into three groups: RING-finger, homologous to E6AP C-terminus (HECT), and RING-in-between-RING (RBR) E3 ligases (Li et al. 2022). RING-finger E3 ligases represent the most abundant subclass. They mediate ubiquitination by transferring ubiquitin directly from E2 enzymes to the substrate protein (Cai et al. 2022). HECT E3 ligases, comprising 28 known members, are subdivided into three categories: NEDD4 family members, HERC family members, and other HECT-type E3 ligases. These HECT E3s utilize a conserved cysteine residue to accept ubiquitin from E2, forming a transient covalent thioester intermediate, which is subsequently transferred to the substrate protein (Sun et al. 2023). RBR E3 ligases contain two RING domains (Ring1 and Ring2) and employ a two-step mechanism for ubiquitin transfer, similar to both RING-finger E3 and HECT E3 processes. Initially, Ring1 retrieves ubiquitin directly from E2 to form the E3-ubiquitin conjugate (Cotton and Lechtenberg 2020). Subsequently, the Ring2 domain forms a covalent thioester intermediate with ubiquitin before being transferred to the substrate protein (Cotton and Lechtenberg 2020).

Classification of deubiquitinases

The ubiquitination process is inherently dynamic and reversible, paralleling most post-translational modifications. Substrate proteins modified by ubiquitin can evade proteasomal degradation or reverse functional alterations by specifically interacting with DUBs, which cleave ubiquitin chains (Mevissen and Komander 2017). The dynamic equilibrium among E3 ligases, DUBs, and substrate proteins is essential for maintaining protein homeostasis and regulating their functional activities. To date, approximately 100 DUBs have been identified in humans. These enzymes are classified into seven families based on their structural and gene sequences: ubiquitin-specific proteases (USPs), ovarian tumor-related proteases (OTUs), ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolases (UCHs), Machado-Joseph disease protein domain proteases (MJDs), Jab1/MPN domain-associated metallopeptidases (JAMMs), monocyte chemotactic protein-induced proteins (MCPIPs), and MINDY proteases (motif interacting with ubiquitin-containing novel DUB family) (Zou et al. 2021).

Among these, USPs represent the largest subclass, comprising > 50 members in human cells. They primarily function by cleaving ubiquitin chains from monoubiquitylated or polyubiquitylated substrate proteins, thereby stabilizing these substrates proteins and regulating a broad spectrum of essential cellular processes, including cell cycle regulation, signal transduction, DNA damage repair, autophagy, and apoptosis (Faesen et al. 2011). The structural architecture of USPs is characterized by three core domains: “fingers”, “palm”, and “thumb”. USPs possess two highly conserved sequence motifs—the Cys Box (~ 19 amino acids) and the His Box (~ 60–90 amino acids)—which contribute to their structural homology and similar mechanisms of action among most USPs (Faesen et al. 2011).

The OTU family constitutes the second-largest group of deubiquitinating enzymes and can be divided into four subgroups: the Otubain family, OTUD family, OTULIN family proteins, and A20 (Du et al. 2020). OTUs serve as critical regulators of intracellular signaling transduction and are critically involved in modulating inflammatory responses and immune regulation. Among these, A20 (also known as tumor necrosis factor alpha [TNF-α]-induced protein 3) plays a pivotal role in regulating the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway, where it attenuates inflammation by inhibiting TNF-α-dependent activation of NF-κB signaling and immune cell-induced activation of NF-κB (Chen et al. 2020). The UCH family comprises only four members: UCH-L1, UCH-L3, UCH-L5, and BAP1. UCH-L1, the first enzyme discovered, catalyzes the cleavage of the C-terminal peptide bond of ubiquitin molecules in ubiquitin chains, thereby releasing free ubiquitin monomers (Fang and Shen 2017).

Among the seven DUB families, six function as cysteine proteases, whereas JAMMs represent a unique class of zinc-dependent metalloproteases. These enzymes activate water molecules by coordinating with histidine, aspartate, and serine residues in the presence of Zn2+, enabling the selective cleavage of ubiquitin chains (Xian et al. 2024). To date, 12 distinct JAMM proteins have been identified in the human genome, with only seven (AMSH, AMSH-LP, BRCC36, eIF3h, Rpn11, MYSM1, and CSN5) exhibiting enzymatic activity toward polyubiquitin chains (Pan et al. 2022).

Role of UPS in PH

PH is marked by aberrant proliferation of vascular wall cells and an imbalance in apoptosis. This pathological process is typically initiated by biomechanical stress or hypoxia stimuli, which induce injury and apoptosis in PAECs, ultimately compromising endothelial integrity. In response, the damaged PAECs subsequently release paracrine signals that activate neighboring PAECs, PASMCs, and fibroblasts. This intercellular communication initiates pro-inflammatory and proliferative signaling pathways, promoting phenotypic switching of PASMCs and contributing to disorganized vascular remodeling (Humbert et al. 2019). Crucially, UPS components orchestrate this pathological cascade by regulating vascular inflammation, redox homeostasis, and cell proliferation in a coordinated manner.

Roles of UPS in BMP signaling

In the classical BMP signaling pathway, BMP receptors are classified into type-I and type-II receptors. Type-I receptors include activin receptor-like kinase (ALK-2), BMPR-ⅠA (ALK-3) and BMPR-IB (ALK-6), while type-II receptors consist of BMPR2, activin type-II receptor and activin type-IIB receptor. Their signaling functions are complementary. Upon the binding of BMP ligands to type-II receptors on the cell membrane surface, the constitutive activity of their serine/threonine kinase domains is activated, which in turn mediates the phosphorylation of the intracellular GS domain of type-I receptors to activate type-I receptor kinases. The activated type-I receptor kinases further phosphorylate downstream receptor-regulated Smads (R-Smads), including Smad1/5/9. Phosphorylated R-Smads bind to the co-mediator Smad4 to form a complex, which translocates into the nucleus and initiates the transcription of target genes that are mainly involved in inhibiting cell proliferation, promoting apoptosis and maintaining vascular homeostasis (Humbert et al. 2019; Akiyama et al. 2024; Li and Quigley 2024).

In PH, the dysregulation of the BMP signaling pathway is a core mechanism driving pulmonary vascular remodeling, with the overall inhibition of the pathway as the main feature of its functional disorder, and it is closely associated with the pathogenesis of idiopathic PAH (IPAH) and familial PAH. Among them, BMPR2 gene variation is the most important genetic inducement, which has been detected in more than 70% of familial PAH cases and approximately 15%-20% of sporadic IPAH cases, directly leading to BMPR2 protein synthesis disorder or functional deficiency and impairing the initial link of BMP signaling (Atkinson et al. 2002; Hautefort et al. 2019; Southgate et al. 2020). Even without BMPR2 gene variation, the expression of BMPR2 in pulmonary arterial tissues is significantly downregulated in patients with PH and animal models of PH induced by monocrotaline or chronic hypoxia (Crosby et al. 2015; Hurst et al. 2017). Meanwhile, the abnormal upregulation of endogenous BMP antagonists such as gremlin and noggin can directly bind to BMP ligands to block receptor binding and inhibit the activation of the downstream Smad pathway (Atkinson et al. 2002; Hurst et al. 2017). In addition, inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α can specifically downregulate the expression of BMPR2 in pulmonary vascular cells, further exacerbating the impairment of BMP signal transduction (Hurst et al. 2017).

The above multiple factors jointly lead to the loss of the physiological regulatory function of the BMP signaling pathway, making it unable to effectively inhibit the abnormal proliferation of PASMCs and maintain the normal function of PAECs. Ultimately, it promotes the formation of proliferative, migratory and anti-apoptotic phenotypes of PASMCs, and simultaneously induces PAECs injury and endothelial-mesenchymal transition, thus driving the pathological process of pulmonary vascular remodeling and PH (Long et al. 2015; Southgate et al. 2020).

Smad ubiquitination regulatory factor 1 (SMURF1) is an E3 ubiquitin ligase(Cao and Zhang 2013). Studies have demonstrated upregulated SMURF1 expression in lung tissues from both patients with IPAH and monocrotaline-induced PH (MCT-PH) rat models, showing positive correlation with disease severity. SMURF1 interacts with Smad1 and Smad5 to promote their ubiquitination and degradation, thereby inhibiting BMP signaling pathway activation, thus contributing to pulmonary vascular remodeling and the development of PAH (Murakami et al. 2010; Barnes et al. 2016).

In addition to regulating Smad1 and Smad5, SMURF1 also contributes to pulmonary vascular remodeling in PH by regulating BMPR2. Beyond BMPR2 gene mutations, another pathogenic mechanism involving abnormal BMPR metabolism through endocytosis-lysosomal degradation has been identified in recent years. Under normal conditions, SMURF1 mediates the basal endocytosis of the BMPR protein. However, mutations in BMPR cysteine residues further activate SMURF1-dependent endocytosis, leading to enhanced BMPR protein degradation. Mutant BMPR significantly impairs BMP signaling, promoting a proliferative phenotype in PASMCs. This effect can be reversed by inhibiting SMURF1 expression (Murakami and Etlinger 2019).

SMURF1 expression is elevated in PASMCs of patients with PAH and correlates with disease severity (Rothman et al. 2025). High-throughput screening of 1.1 million compounds identified multiple potent and selective SMURF1 inhibitors, including compound 38 (a triazole-core derivative) and compound 6 (a pyrazolone-class inhibitor) (Shaw et al. 2023; Rothman et al. 2025). These agents act allosterically by binding a cryptic cavity in the SMURF1 HECT domain’s N-lobe, extending the αH10 helix over the conserved glycine hinge (G634) and restricting catalytic motion between the N- and C-lobes to suppress SMURF1 activity.

In MCT-PH and SuHx-PH rat models, oral administration of compound 38 and 6 at 3 ~ 10 mg/kg significantly reduced right ventricular systolic pressure and attenuated pulmonary vascular muscularization (Shaw et al. 2023; Rothman et al. 2025). Both inhibitors show high selectivity for SMURF1 over other HECT ligases (e.g., SMURF2, E6AP, WWP1), with compound 38 exhibiting > 100-fold selectivity against SMURF2. Target engagement is confirmed by increased cellular SMURF1 substrate levels and enhanced BMP signaling (elevated p-SMAD and ID1).

In hypoxia-exposed human PASMCs, SMURF1 inhibitors restored BMPR2 expression, enhanced BMP signaling, inhibited cell proliferation and migration, and reduced apoptosis. Notably, these inhibitors ameliorated established PAH phenotypes in preclinical models, with 10 mg/kg compound 38 showing superior efficacy and a clearer dose-response than benchmark inhibitor compound 29 in the SuHx model. Rat toxicological assessments revealed no significant biochemical or histopathological abnormalities, and the inhibitors had a clean safety profile against standard ion channels and G Protein-Coupled Receptors, demonstrating favorable tolerability and low toxicity (Shaw et al. 2023; Rothman et al. 2025).

Beyond the aforementioned regulation in PH, SMURF1 is subject to extensive multi-level modulation in vascular cells: at the transcriptional level, upstream stimulatory factor 2 directly binds to its promoter region to repress transcriptional activity, while androgens/androgen receptor, retinoic acid and the inflammatory cytokine TNF-α positively regulate its transcriptional expression via distinct signaling cascades (Kaneki et al. 2006; Sato and Heuckeroth 2008; Gang et al. 2015; Tan et al. 2019); at the epigenetic level, multiple miRNAs including miR-125a, miR-194-5p and miR-4319 target its 3’UTR to inhibit translation, and the long non-coding RNA SNHG3 indirectly upregulates SMURF1 expression by sponging miR-577 (Xu et al. 2019; Bian 2020; Li et al. 2020); at the post-translational level via protein interaction, CKIP-1 activates its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity, whereas SMURF2, FBXL15 and PSME3 promote its degradation through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway or a ubiquitin-independent manner, and TRIB3 induces its autoubiquitination to accelerate cytoplasmic dissociation and degradation (Fukunaga et al. 2008; Nie et al. 2010; Cui et al. 2011). This intricate multi-dimensional regulatory network precisely modulates SMURF1 expression and functional activity.

Moreover, accumulating evidence identifies miRNAs as key post-transcriptional regulators of SMURF1, with miR-140-5p and miR-424/322 being the most well-characterized (Rothman et al. 2016; Baptista et al. 2018). These miRNAs directly target the 3’ untranslated region (3’UTR) of SMURF1 mRNA to suppress its translation, thereby reducing SMURF1 protein levels in target cells. Clinically, their circulating expression profiles in PAH patients show distinct alterations closely linked to disease severity and long-term prognosis: miR-140-5p is consistently downregulated in whole blood, while miR-424/322 is significantly upregulated compared to healthy controls (Baptista et al. 2018). Notably, despite correlating with more severe hemodynamic abnormalities (e.g., elevated mPAP and PVR), higher circulating miR-424/322 confers a better prognostic outcome—particularly in patients with congenital heart disease-associated PAH (CHD-PAH). Mechanistically, the majority of circulating miR-424/322 is secreted by hypoxic PAECs, primarily encapsulated in exosomes to maintain stability and enable intercellular transfer. These exosome-carried miRNAs are taken up by cardiomyocytes, where they downregulate SMURF1 to enhance BMPR2/SMAD5 signaling activity, ultimately alleviating right ventricular hypertrophy and improving contractile function (Baptista et al. 2018). This regulatory cascade highlights miR-424/322 as a critical paracrine messenger linking pulmonary vascular pathology to right ventricular adaptation in PAH. For miR-140-5p, its downregulation in PH patients further amplifies SMURF1-mediated suppression of BMP signaling, exacerbating pulmonary vascular remodeling, which underscores the coordinated role of these miRNAs in fine-tuning SMURF1 activity and disease progression (Baptista et al. 2018).

Ubiquitin-specific peptidase 9X (USP9X) is a key deubiquitylating enzyme with a highly conserved catalytic domain. It cleaves ubiquitin chains to stabilize specific substrate proteins and is involved in regulating cell apoptosis, cell cycle, and cell migration. It also participates in key signaling pathways such as TGF-β and Wnt/β-catenin (Meng et al. 2023). Recent evidence indicates that USP9X is a downstream effector of ID1/3. When BMPR2 signaling is impaired, the expression of ID1 and ID3 decreases, leading to downregulated USP9X expression. This downregulation impairs cardiomyocyte differentiation and function, disrupts normal heart development, and contributes to PAH. This suggests that restoring USP9X expression may enhance cardiomyocyte function and offers a novel therapeutic strategy for PAH (Du et al. 2022).

Roles of UPS in the NF-κB signaling pathway

NF-κB, a key transcription factor family (p65/RelA, RelB, cRel, p50, and p52), functions through two pathways: canonical and non-canonical. In the canonical pathway, p50/p65 heterodimers remain cytoplasmic until IκB is degraded, allowing their translocation into the nucleus to trigger pro-inflammatory gene transcription. The non-canonical pathway, activated by TNF receptor superfamily members, involves NIK- and IKKα-mediated processing of p100 into p52, which pairs with RelB to form an active complex (Guo et al. 2024a, b). NF-κB plays a key role in cell proliferation, differentiation, survival, immunity, and inflammation. Inhibition of TNF-α expression and suppression of NF-κB pathway activation in MCT-PH model rats alleviated excessive pulmonary vascular remodeling (Wang et al. 2013a, b; Jang et al. 2021). Furthermore, the activation of PPAR-γ alongside the inhibition of NF-κB signaling was found to attenuate PH in sickle cell disease mouse models (Wang et al. 2013a, b; Jang et al. 2021). Consequently, targeting the overactivation of the NF-κB signaling pathway presents a promising therapeutic strategy for PH.

Zinc finger protein A20, a member of the OTU family, is a negative feedback regulatory protein of the NF-κB signaling pathway, which affects NF-κB activation by regulating key upstream proteins of IKKs (e.g., TRAF2 and RIP1) (Chen et al. 2020). In the early stages of hypoxia, PAECs secrete increased levels of A20, which subsequently declined below normal levels in hypoxia-induced PH (HPH) rat models, suggesting that the NF-κB signaling pathway is essential for the self-protection of PAECs (Li et al. 2016). Chen et al.(Chen et al. 2020) revealed that adenovirus-mediated overexpression of A20 significantly attenuated the abnormally elevated right ventricular systolic pressure and pulmonary vessel muscularization in HPH mice. This suggests that A20 can effectively block the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby alleviating HPH. Recent studies also indicated that zinc upregulates A20 expression in PASMCs, enhancing the suppression of the NF-κB pathway and reducing inflammation. For example, zinc treatment in MCT-induced PH rats increased A20 levels and reduced PH progression (Chen et al. 2024a, b).

In addition to A20, CYLD—a member of the USP family of DUBs—functions as a negative regulator of the NF-κB signaling pathway (Zhou et al. 2021). Zhou et al. demonstrated that CYLD expression is significantly upregulated in lung tissues from patients with CHD-PAH and in a PAH rat model induced by MCT combined with aortic-venous (AV) shunt. Consistently, CYLD levels are also elevated in human PASMCs exposed to hypoxic conditions. Mechanistically, inhibition of CYLD notably enhances the nuclear translocation of p65 in PASMCs, thereby promoting NF-κB pathway activation (Zhou et al. 2021). However, despite this NF-κB activation, CYLD silencing fails to exert the anticipated pro-proliferative effect on PASMCs. Instead, the authors confirmed that CYLD drives abnormal hyperproliferation of PASMCs primarily by modulating the activation of the p38 and ERK mitogen-activated protein kinase pathways. Consequently, inhibition of CYLD effectively reverses pulmonary vascular remodeling in experimental PAH, and this protective effect is independent of the NF-κB signaling pathway (Zhou et al. 2021).

In the Cullin family of E3, Cul5 binds to TNFR-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) and promotes the ubiquitination of TRAF6 at Lys63 upon lipopolysaccharide stimulation, activating the NF-κB signaling pathway (Zhu et al. 2016). Cul5 expression has been shown to be significantly upregulated in lung tissues of patients with HPH and HPH mouse models. Fluorescence localization techniques revealed that this upregulation primarily occurred in PAECs (Wang et al. 2023b). The interaction of Cul5 with TRAF6 promotes the activation of NF-κB, which further activates HIF-1α and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), thereby increasing VEGF levels and exacerbating HPH development (Wang et al. 2023a). Silencing of Cul5 by adeno-associated virus improved pulmonary vascular muscularization and right ventricular remodeling in HPH mouse models. In in vitro experiments, the addition of Cul5 (10 ng/mL) enhanced angiogenesis and adhesion of PAECs (Wang et al. 2023a); however, silencing of Cul5 under hypoxic conditions attenuated the over-proliferation of PAECs by inhibiting the NF-κB/HIF-1α/VEGF signaling pathway by modulating TRAF6 ubiquitination (Wang et al. 2023a).

Roles of UPS in HIF-1α signaling pathway

HIFs are central regulators of systemic oxygen homeostasis, critically governing cellular energy metabolism, metal ion transport (e.g., calcium/iron), and proliferation–apoptosis dynamics. The HIF family comprises three isoforms: HIF-1, HIF-2, and HIF-3. Their dysregulation drives pathological vascular remodeling in PH (Pullamsetti et al. 2020). Under hypoxic conditions, nuclear-translocated HIF-1α heterodimerizes with HIF-1β to transcriptionally activate downstream effectors—including endothelin-1, glucose transporter 1, and VEGF—that collectively mediate pulmonary vascular metabolic reprogramming, hyperproliferation, vasoconstriction, and angiogenesis, all hallmark features of PH pathogenesis (Albanese et al. 2020; Evans 2022).

The post-translational regulation of HIF-1α involves several modifications, including ubiquitination, hydroxylation, phosphorylation, and SUMOylation, with ubiquitination and hydroxylation being the predominant mechanisms. Ubiquitination typically depends on prior hydroxylation. The prolyl hydroxylase domain (PHD)-containing enzyme family comprises PHD1, PHD2, and PHD3 (Gaete et al. 2021). These enzymes catalyze the hydroxylation of two proline residues (Pro402 and Pro564) within the oxygen-dependent degradation domain of HIF-1α, thereby facilitating its ubiquitination and ultimately impacting its stability (Albanese et al. 2020).

Once hydroxylated, HIF-1α is recognized by the von Hippel-Lindau protein (pVHL), which targets it for proteasomal degradation via the pVHL pathway. pVHL functions as a classic E3 ubiquitin ligase with tumor suppressor properties. It forms an elongin B/C-cullin 2-VHL E3 complex with elongins B, C, and cullin 2 to facilitate substrate protein recognition and degradation. Under normoxic conditions, pVHL rapidly identifies and binds to hydroxylated HIF-1α, maintaining its levels at a lower threshold (Yang et al. 2020). Furthermore, HIF-1α can be negatively regulated by factor-inhibiting HIF, which specifically hydroxylates asparagine residues—thereby inhibiting its transcriptional activity in the nucleus (Wu et al. 2021). Under hypoxic conditions, both PHDs and the pVHL pathway are suppressed in PAECs and PASMCs, leading to diminished hydroxylation of HIF-1α, evasion of pVHL detection, and a significant increase in its half-life, thereby promoting cell proliferation and metabolic activity (Zhang et al. 2019). Notably, P4HA1 modulates this process by regulating the α-ketoglutarate/succinate axis: its upregulation under hypoxia inhibits HIF-1α hydroxylation, further preventing pVHL-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of HIF-1α, while P4HA1 knockdown enhances HIF-1α hydroxylation and subsequent recognition by pVHL, accelerating HIF-1α proteasomal degradation; this reciprocal regulation forms a P4HA1/HIF1α positive feedback loop that exacerbates endothelial dysfunction and pulmonary vascular remodeling in PH (Chen et al. 2025). Elevated HIF-1α expression has been observed in lung tissues of patients with PH associated with chronic high-altitude exposure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or pulmonary interstitial fibrosis. However, studies investigating HIF-1α levels in IPAH have yielded inconsistent results, underscoring the need for further investigation to elucidate the specific dynamics governing HIF-1α expression within this context (Barnes et al. 2017). Seven in absentia homologue (Siah) proteins are highly conserved RING-domain-family E3 ligases that comprise Siah1, Siah2, and Siah3 in humans (Ye et al. 2021). These proteins modulate the stability of PHDs within cells. Under hypoxic conditions, Siah activity is upregulated, leading to increased degradation of PHDs via the ubiquitin pathway and a subsequent reduction in HIF-1α hydroxylation, allowing HIF-1α to evade recognition by pVHL (Xu and Li 2021). Recent studies have indicated Siah1/2 upregulation in the pulmonary arterial walls of HPH models, accompanied by reduced PHD protein levels. This suggests that Siah may inhibit the HIF-1α hydroxylation by destabilizing PHDs; conversely, inhibiting Siah expression could indirectly regulate HIF-1α ubiquitination and mitigate the effects of HPH (Fu et al. 2015).

Otud6b is a member of the OTU family, which enhances the stability of various downstream proteins by deubiquitination and is involved in key cellular functions. High Otud6b expression levels have been associated with enhanced proliferation in several cancer types (Yang et al. 2024). Otud6b expression was found to be significantly elevated in HPH mice models and hypoxia-cultured human PAECs and PASMCs, thereby increasing Calpain-1 and HIF-1α levels, exacerbating inflammation and apoptosis in PAECs, and promoting inflammation and proliferation in PASMCs (Liu et al. 2024). Additionally, Otud6b has been identified as a direct transcriptional target of HIF-1α, establishing a positive feedback loop with Calpain-1/HIF-1α under hypoxic conditions that further promotes PASMCs proliferation. Unfortunately, this study did not clarify the specific downstream direct targets of Otud6b and merely presented trends regarding the expression of related genes such as HIF-1α (Liu et al. 2024). This aspect necessitates further investigation for confirmation.

Roles of UPS in the P53 signaling pathway

P53, a 53 kDa tumor suppressor protein, orchestrates metabolic reprogramming and reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis under various stress conditions, including DNA damage and oncogenic signaling (Oren and Prives 2024). Through transcriptional activation of target genes governing cell cycle arrest, DNA repair, senescence, and apoptosis, P53 coordinates adaptive responses that mobilize autophagy and ferroptosis mechanisms and promotes the elimination of compromised cells while suppressing carcinogenesis (Duffy et al. 2022).

In normal cells, P53 exists at low levels due to ubiquitin-mediated degradation orchestrated by an E3, ligase mouse double minute 2 (MDM2). MDM2 is a member of the RING-finger-domain family of E3 ligases and exerts complex regulatory mechanisms on P53 (Fåhraeus and Olivares-Illana 2014). High MDM2 levels catalyze P53 polyubiquitination—leading to its degradation via the proteasome pathway—whereas low MDM2 levels induce multi-ubiquitylation, facilitating the nuclear export of P53 and consequently reducing its activity as a transcription factor. Notably, elevated P53 levels can also stimulate MDM2 transcription, resulting in increased ubiquitin degradation of P53 and establishing a negative feedback mechanism that maintains the physiological functions of P53 within the cell (Coutts et al. 2009).

Recent studies have demonstrated dysregulation of P53 expression during the development of PH. In the MCT-PH model, P53 levels were elevated in PAECs but decreased in PASMCs. A similar phenomenon was also observed in in-vitro hypoxia experiments. Such abnormal P53 expression leads to increased apoptosis in PAECs and excessive proliferation in PASMCs (Wang et al. 2019). Mouraret et al.(Mouraret et al. 2013) demonstrated that MDM2 expression is elevated in the lung tissues of chronic HPH model mice and SuHx-PH mice, whereas P53 protein levels were decreased. Treatment with the MDM2 inhibitor Nutlin-3a significantly increased P53 protein levels in the lung tissues of these mouse models, reversing vascular remodeling in the pulmonary arteries (Mouraret et al. 2013). Beyond its interactions with P53, MDM2 also serves as an E3 ligase for ACE2. MDM2 expression is increased in the lung tissue of mice induced by hypoxia, and it can ubiquitinate the K788 site of the ACE2 protein, thereby promoting ubiquitin-mediated degradation of ACE2, leading to the dysregulation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, and further aggravating PH. The MDM2 inhibitor JNJ-165 can effectively reverse this pathological process (Shen et al. 2020). USP7—a member of the USP family—enhances the intracellular stability of MDM2 through deubiquitination, indirectly regulating the growth, proliferation, and apoptosis of various tumor cells (Harakandi et al. 2021). Zhu et al.(Zhu et al. 2021) found that the siRNA-mediated suppression of USP7 expression in PDGF-induced proliferative PASMCs effectively inhibited the upregulation of MDM2, thereby limiting excessive cell proliferation by increasing the expression of downstream proteins such as P53 and FoxO4.

In addition to MDM2, Cul7—a member of the Cullin family—plays a significant role in regulating P53 in PH. Liu et al.(Liu et al. 2019) discovered that hypoxia upregulates Cul7 expression in PASMCs and that siRNA-mediated knockdown of Cul7 significantly reduces P53 protein levels, alleviating excessive proliferation and migration of PASMCs induced by hypoxia. However, the regulatory mechanism through which Cul7 affects P53 remains controversial. Some studies have indicated that knocking down Cul7 in mouse embryonic fibroblasts does not increase P53 levels. Other studies suggested that Cul7 may directly bind to P53 in the cytoplasm, potentially inhibiting some transcriptional activities of P53 within the nucleus (Jung et al. 2007). In lung and breast cancer cells, Cul7 knockdown has been associated with increased P53 protein levels. However, direct evidence supporting the notion that Cul7 promotes P53 degradation via ubiquitination is lacking (Guo et al. 2014). Therefore, the specific mechanism underlying the interaction between Cul7 and P53 has yet to be clearly defined.

Notably, P53 is also regulated by the BMP signaling pathway, dependent on ALK2 activation. BRCC3—a homolog of BRCC36—was found to enhance ALK2 stability and activity by removing K63 ubiquitin chains from ALK2 (particularly at Lys-472 and Lys-475), activate receptor-regulated Smad1/5/9, resulting in the transcriptional activation of BMP-regulated PPARγ, P53, and Id1. In vitro experiments indicated that BRCC3 overexpression or the de-ubiquitin-mimetic ALK2-K472/475R attenuated PASMCs proliferation and migration while enhancing PASMCs apoptosis (Shen et al. 2024). Furthermore, BRCC3 overexpression in the lungs of SM22α-BRCC3-Tg mice partially restored phosphorylated ALK2, phosphorylated Smad1/5, PPARγ, P53, and Id1 levels and ameliorated PH. Notably, BRCC3 is significantly reduced in PASMCs of patients with IPAH, as well as in PH animal models (Shen et al. 2024). Consequently, targeting BRCC3 represents a promising direction for further therapeutic exploration (Shaw et al. 2023; Shen et al. 2024).

Roles of UPS in the hippo signaling pathway

The evolutionarily conserved Hippo signaling pathway, originally discovered in Drosophila genetic studies two decades ago, orchestrates tissue homeostasis and organ size control through the precise regulation of balance between proliferation and apoptosis (Fu et al. 2022). The core of the mammalian Hippo pathway comprises a kinase cascade reaction chain, including mammalian Ste20-like kinase 1 and 2 (MST1/2) and large tumor suppressor 1 and 2 (LATS1/2), Salvador 1 (SAV1), and MP one binder 1 (MOB1). MST1/2 kinases complexed with SAV1 scaffold phosphorylate and activate LATS1/2-MOB1 effectors. This activated kinase module phosphorylates YAP/TAZ transcriptional coactivators, triggering their cytoplasmic retention via 14-3-3-mediated cytoplasmic sequestration and subsequent proteasomal degradation (Ma et al. 2019).

Siah2, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, promotes YAP dephosphorylation by targeting Lats2 in the Hippo pathway, leading to its destabilization. Wang et al.(Wang et al. 2020) reported that Siah2 expression was significantly increased in lung tissues of the MCT-PH rat model, promoting proteasome-dependent degradation of Lats1/2 proteins. The reduction in Lats1/2 decreases YAP phosphorylation, thereby increasing the levels of its activated forms. Activated YAP translocates to the nucleus and acts as a transcriptional co-activator to promote cell proliferation and inhibit the expression of apoptosis-related genes, such as CCND1, FOXM1, CYR61, and CTGF. Both the Siah2 inhibitor vitamin K3 and the proteasome inhibitor MG-132 reduced the degradation of Lats1/2 in PASMCs, inhibited YAP activation, and mitigated the hyperproliferation of PASMCs in MCT-PAH rats.

As an E3 ligase for YAP, β-transduction repeat-containing protein (β-TrCP) primarily mediates its ubiquitin-mediated degradation. Recent findings indicate that β-TrCP expression is inhibited by S1P/STAT3/miR-135b signaling in PASMCs. This inhibition supressed the ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of YAP, subsequently leading to the overactivation of the Hippo signaling pathway alongside increased expression levels of downstream protein Notch3. This ultimately promoted the proliferation among Sprague Dawley rat-derived PASMCs cultured in vitro (Wang et al. 2021a, b).

In addition to E3 ligases, YAP is also regulated by other DUBs. USP15 expression is significantly increased in PASMCs of patients with PH (Wu et al. 2023). Unlike β-TrCP, USP15 counteracts its effects by cleaving the K48-linked ubiquitin chains on YAP, maintaining YAP stability, enhancing its nuclear transcriptional activity, and promoting the proliferation and migration of PASMCs, which further facilitates the remodeling of pulmonary arteries (Wu et al. 2023). Additionally, in another study (Ma et al. 2025), upregulated USP15 transcriptionally activated by RUNX1 deubiquitinates and stabilizes CBX5, thereby triggering abnormal proliferation, migration and anti-apoptosis of PASMCs and exacerbating pulmonary vascular remodeling in hypoxic PH.

TRIM59 is a member of the tripartite motif protein (TRIM) family and serves as a downstream target of YAP. Recent studies have demonstrated that elevated TRIM59 expression is closely associated with the activation of the Hippo signaling pathway in lung tissues of patients with PH. YAP interacts with the transcription factor TEAD4, synergistically enhancing the transcriptional activity of the TRIM59 gene. The overexpressed TRIM59 functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, exerting its regulatory effects on ubiquitination and subsequently influencing downstream target proteins such as ACSL4, ANKRD13A, CAVIN1, and MCL-1. This cascade ultimately stimulates cell proliferation in PASMCs (Liu et al. 2023a, b). Conversely, inhibiting TRIM59 expression can effectively mitigate excessive PASMCs proliferation.

Roles of UPS in mitochondrial quality control

Mitochondrial quality control is maintained through three key mechanisms: dynamic fusion and fission processes that adjust the mitochondrial shape and size, biogenesis to replenish mitochondrial numbers, and autophagy to degrade damaged mitochondria via lysosomes. These processes are crucial for maintaining mitochondrial health and homeostasis (Pickles et al. 2018). Mitochondrial autophagy, a conserved cellular mechanism, eliminates dysfunctional mitochondria and maintains energy metabolism equilibrium. Key pathways involved in this process include those mediated by PINK1, Parkin, FUNDC1, Bcl-rambo, BNIP3, and NIX (Onishi et al. 2021). Aberrant mitochondrial autophagy contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction during PH progression, with the ubiquitin system playing a regulatory pivotal role.

Parkin, an E3 ligase originally implicated in Parkinson’s disease, is composed of 465 amino acids and features a ubiquitin-like structural domain at the N-terminus, two RING structures at the C-terminus, and an in-between-ring structure located between them (Shimura et al. 2000). Research has demonstrated that the PINK1/Parkin autophagy pathway is aberrantly activated in PH, where PINK1 recruits Parkin to damaged mitochondria, initiating ubiquitination of outer membrane proteins and mitochondrial degradation via lysosomal activity (Li et al. 2023). In a HPH mouse model, increased expression of both PINK1 and Parkin proteins promoted mitochondrial autophagy, promoting PASMCs proliferation while reducing apoptosis. Conversely, knockdown of either PINK1 or Parkin expression reversed hypoxia-induced proliferation and apoptosis inhibition in PASMCs (Linqing et al. 2021). Recent studies have found that the PINK1/Parkin pathway is activated by HIF-1α, a discovery that further elucidates the regulatory mechanisms of the PINK1/Parkin pathway under hypoxic conditions (Chen et al. 2024a, b).

In addition, another study found that female mice had higher Parkin expression at baseline as well as increased mitochondrial respiratory capacity when exposed to oxidative stress in lung endothelial cells. Parkin inhibition increased metabolic activity but reduced cell proliferation to different degrees depending on sex. However, these intervention experiments have been conducted exclusively in vitro and in animal models to date. Consequently, evidence regarding differences in Parkin expression and its regulatory role within the pulmonary vasculature among patients with PH across different sexes remains insufficient (Bazan et al. 2022). Therapeutic targeting of Parkin to modulate mitophagy may yield novel treatment strategies for PH. Furthermore, a systematic investigation into sex hormone interplay with Parkin signaling could elucidate the sexual dimorphism underlying PH pathogenesis.

FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy is distinctively characterized in PH. First, FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy was found to be upregulated in hypoxic PAECs and pulmonary arteries in hypoxic PH, promoting its interaction with LC3B, triggering mitochondrial damage and intracellular ROS generation (Liu et al. 2022). Furthermore, this study also demonstrated that upregulated expression of FUNDC1 reduced ubiquitin-mediated degradation of HIF-1α, thereby promoting cell proliferation. Conversely, FUNDC1 inhibition suppressed hypoxia-induced ROS generation, HIF-1α activity, and excessive proliferation of PAECs, ultimately reversing pulmonary vascular remodeling (Liu et al. 2022). Although this study highlighted the role of FUNDC1 in regulating HIF-1α ubiquitin degradation, the precise mechanism remains to be elucidated. Another study found that FUNDC1 expression is downregulated in hypoxic PAECs, in contrast to PAECs. This study did not explore the interaction of FUNDC1 with LC3B but instead proposed that low FUNDC1 expression stabilizes and increases Drp1 protein levels by enhancing USP15-Drp1 interaction, which subsequently promotes mitochondrial fission and autophagy, increases ROS production, and leads to abnormal cell proliferation (Quiles and Gustafsson Å 2022), (Xu et al. 2024). In summary, the FUNDC1 expression level and its different mechanisms in PH pathogenesis may be related to different cell types.

UBA52 encodes a fusion protein that contains ubiquitin at the N-terminus and ribosomal protein L40 at the C-terminus, functioning as an E3-like enzyme to promote the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of downstream target proteins (Ma et al. 2022). UBA52 knockdown did not alter the total cellular ubiquitin levels but significantly reduced intracellular protein synthesis and induced cell cycle arrest. Recent studies have indicated that UBA52 expression is elevated in PASMCs under hypoxic conditions, exacerbating mitochondrial dysfunction by targeting the degradation of apoptosis-inducing factor (AIF) while simultaneously increasing cellular glycolysis and ROS release. UBA52 inhibition or AIF overexpression can ameliorate hypoxia-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and inhibit excessive cell proliferation (Ma et al. 2022).

Other functions of E3 or DUBs

A variety of E3 ubiquitin ligases and DUBs play critical roles in regulating pulmonary vascular and right ventricular remodeling in PH. For instance, UCH-L1—significantly elevated in the plasma of patients with PAH—enhances PI3K/AKT pathway activity by stabilizing AKT1 through deubiquitination. Inhibition of UCH-L1 expression attenuates pulmonary arterial remodeling (Tang et al. 2024). TRIM32 exerts a regulatory role in the development of PAH by inhibiting the proliferation, migration, and dedifferentiation of hypoxia-induced PASMCs and promoting their apoptosis through the suppression of PI3K/Akt signaling pathway activation (Hu et al. 2021). USP3 is suppressed by miR-146-5p in hypoxic PAECs, and its reduced expression leads to abnormal endothelial cell proliferation, suggesting its importance in maintaining vascular homeostasis (Zhang et al. 2021). USP8 enhances JAK-STAT3 signaling by deubiquitinating the leptin receptor ObR-b, and its specific inhibitor DUBs-IN-2 effectively alleviates the progression of experimental PH (Jutant et al. 2024). OTUB1 inhibits ferroptosis in PASMCs by stabilizing SLC7A11, and its knockdown decreases the GSH/GSSG ratio and reduces cell viability, suggesting its involvement in hypoxia adaptation mechanisms (Hu et al. 2022). CHIP suppresses the proliferation of hypoxic PASMCs by regulating calcium signaling through store-operated calcium entry, and its upregulation is closely associated with distal pulmonary vascular remodeling (Dong and Zhang 2019). Peli1 promotes the ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation of Foxp1, thereby alleviating its suppression of IL-6 transcription. This process facilitates the activation of macrophages and exacerbates the development of PH (Lin et al. 2025). Macrophage derived inflammation (including IL-33) promotes pulmonary vascular remodeling in SuHx induced PAH mice (Liu et al. 2023a, b). NEDD4, acting as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, interacts with Irisin to promote the ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of ENO1. This process inhibits the proliferation and cell cycle progression of PASMCs, thereby alleviating the development of PAH (Sun et al. 2025). MuRF1/2 double knockdown exacerbates cardiac dysfunction in PH models, whereas low expression of atrogin-1 reduces the degradation of myofibrillar proteins. These E3s have dual roles in the metabolic homeostasis of skeletal muscles and the cardiorespiratory system (Nguyen et al. 2020). UBR5 interacts with PPARγ to regulate ATM signaling, and its aberrant function in PAECs results in defective DNA repair. Furthermore, PPARγ agonists may improve mitochondrial function through this mechanism (Li et al. 2019). SOX9 promotes the proliferation and migration of PASMCs by stabilizing DPP4, contributing to elevated pulmonary vascular resistance and PH development (Guo et al. 2024a, b). NEDD4L promotes PAH by ubiquitinating and degrading ACE2 (Wang et al. 2024a, b). MFN2-dependent mitochondrial dysfunction acts on Relm-β-induced PAH through the USP18/Twist1/miR-214 pathway (Wang et al. 2024a, b). USP10 stabilizes the BUB3 protein in PASMCs, thereby activating the Akt-mTORC1 signaling pathway and promoting cell proliferation and survival in Relm-β-induced PAH (Kudryashova et al. 2022). Protein levels of USP28 were upregulated in PASMCs under hypoxic conditions, promoting cell proliferation (Hao et al. 2018). FBXW7, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, forms a complex with ASH2L to promote KLF5 ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, thereby inhibiting NOTCH3 transcription and attenuating vascular smooth muscle cell abnormal proliferation and pulmonary vascular remodeling in PH; its vascular protective function is impaired when ASH2L is downregulated (Zhang et al. 2025). Stub1, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, is acetylated at K287 by CBP/p300 under hypoxia, translocates to the nucleus, and regulates HIF-1α/HIF-2α switch to attenuate pulmonary vascular remodeling in PH via non-E3 activity, with reduced K287 acetylation exacerbating disease (Czerwinski et al. 2025).

Additionally, CUL2 gene is significantly upregulated in CHD-PAH, CTD-PAH, and IPAH and is closely associated with the cell cycle. In hypoxic PAECs, CUL2 protein expression is increased, and silencing CUL2 can mitigate hypoxia-induced endothelial injury (Wang et al. 2021a, b). The RNF213 p. Arg4810Lys mutation is strongly associated with IPAH, and patients carrying this mutation exhibit a worse prognosis than those with the BMPR2 mutation. This variant of the E3 ubiquitin ligase may promote the proliferation of PASMCs and fibroblasts by activating the CXCL12/CXCR4 signaling pathway, leading to pulmonary vascular remodeling and the development of PAH (Hiraide et al. 2020, 2024; Luo et al. 2022). Collectively, these findings reveal a complex regulatory network of protein ubiquitination modifications in PH and provide potential new therapeutic targets for PH.

Notably, while extensive studies have focused on the regulatory roles of E3 ubiquitin ligases and DUBs in PH-related pulmonary vascular and right ventricular remodeling—as summarized above—research investigating proteasome performance differences remains scarce. Emerging evidence indicates that proteasome-mediated degradation itself is aberrantly regulated in PH: in HPH-rat models, the proteasome catalytic subunit PSMB6 is significantly upregulated in distal pulmonary arteries and PASMCs, accompanied by enhanced proteasome activity. This directly promotes hypoxia-induced PASMCs proliferation, a core driver of pulmonary vascular remodeling (Wang et al. 2013a, b). However, proteasome overactivation appears context-dependent (prominent in hypoxia-induced PH but not consistently in other subtypes), and critical gaps remain: limited data on proteasome capacity/UPS flux in human PH tissues, dynamic changes across disease stages, and potential heterogeneity across PH etiologies.

Common limitations of existing studies

Although studies on UPS regulation in PH have progressed, and one clinical study has been conducted. However, existing studies share common limitations. These limitations restrict the clinical translational value of the research and make it difficult to fully reflect the pathological essence of PAH. First, severely insufficient research translatability: the vast majority of relevant studies remain at the stage of in vitro cell experiments or in vivo animal experiments, with only one clinical study carried out, and a mature clinical research evidence system has not yet been formed. There are significant differences between the regulatory mechanisms at the cellular and animal levels and the human physiological and pathological environment, making it difficult to directly extrapolate to clinical applications. Second, lack of a unified dose qualification standard: the doses used for drug intervention in existing studies are all suitable for experimental animals such as rats and mice. No conversion system between animal doses and human doses has been established. This makes it difficult to translate in vitro cell drug concentrations and in vivo animal doses into clinically feasible regimens. Third, single and poorly representative animal models: most studies only use 1–2 types of PH animal models (SuHx-PH, HPH, MCT-PH, MCT-AV-PH, etc.) for verification. For example, the HPH model only reflects the pathological characteristics of hypoxia-induced PH, and cannot simulate the core disease characteristics of PAH such as the genetic background of IPAH (e.g., BMPR2 mutation) and the hemodynamic abnormalities of CHD-PAH, making it difficult to fully reveal the regulatory rules of UPS in PAH. Fourth, methodological flaws and questionable data reliability: when verifying the direct interaction between E3 ubiquitin ligases/ DUBs and target proteins, some studies did not use gold standard experiments such as co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) for validation, but only deduced the regulatory relationship between them by citing conclusions from studies on other diseases. This may lead to the omission of intermediate links in upstream and downstream regulation, resulting in deviations in the deduction of regulatory mechanisms and insufficient reliability and rigor of research data.

Prospects

Clinical translation progress of UPS-targeted therapies

In recent years, research on antitumor drugs and therapeutic targets has expanded rapidly, with significant advancements in studies focusing on small molecular compounds targeting the UPS. Additionally, proteasome inhibitors suppress the excessive proliferation of PAECs and PASMCs. This effect reverses pulmonary vascular remodeling and right ventricular hypertrophy in PH animal models using agents such as MG-132, bortezomib, carfilzomib, and marizomib (Wang et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2022). Among these, bortezomib is primarily used for the treatment of multiple myeloma in clinical settings. It can also inhibit the proliferation of PASMCs, promote apoptosis, and ameliorate PAECs dysfunction by modulating caveolin-1 and mitofusin-2 (Chen et al. 2022), thereby alleviating PH. However, due to its toxic side effects on the heart, bortezomib is unlikely to be approved for PAH treatment (Avitabile et al. 2020). Previously, carfilzomib—a second-generation proteasome inhibitor—was considered a promising PH candidate due to its reduced cytotoxicity relative to bortezomib; in PH animal models, it promotes vascular smooth muscle cell apoptosis and significantly reverses pulmonary vascular remodeling when combined with pulmonary vasodilators (Wang et al. 2016). Notably, recent clinical evidence has challenged this notion: carfilzomib can induce PH as a rare but definite adverse effect, thus eliminating it from potential PH treatment options (Grynblat et al. 2024).

The translation of UPS-targeted therapies into clinical practice for PAH remains extremely limited, and the process has been fraught with setbacks. To date, only Novartis’ LTP001 has entered clinical trials (NCT05764265), an open-label extension study involving 31 PAH participants. Unfortunately, the study was terminated by the sponsor due to unspecified reasons, with limited public information regarding the specific contributing factors. No other UPS-targeted agents have advanced to clinical development for PAH, and traditional proteasome inhibitors (bortezomib, carfilzomib) have been ruled out due to safety concerns, resulting in a stagnant clinical translation landscape.

Core limitations of UPS-targeted therapies

UPS-targeted therapies face multiple insurmountable limitations in PAH treatment, covering safety, clinical translation, and technical application aspects: (1) Safety risks: The complex UPS signaling network leads to a high risk of off-target toxicity. E3 ligases and DUBs regulate numerous substrates, and non-specific targeting easily triggers unintended biological effects (e.g., carfilzomib-induced PH (Grynblat et al. 2024); bortezomib-induced cardiac toxicity (Avitabile et al. 2020). Additionally, clinical candidates such as LTP001 have poor tolerability, and the fragile cardiopulmonary function of PAH patients further amplifies safety risks. (2) Druggability and chronic dosing toxicity risks: Most UPS-targeted candidates suffer from common druggability issues such as low solubility, poor membrane permeability and insufficient metabolic stability (Wu et al. 2024). Moreover, PAH is a chronic non-malignant disease requiring long-term medication, and prolonged administration of such drugs may cause cumulative toxicity to the vulnerable cardiopulmonary system of PAH patients—proteasome inhibitors (a major class of UPS-targeted agents) have been reported to induce cardiovascular adverse events including heart failure, hypertension and arrhythmias (Fricker 2020), posing severe challenges to the safety and tolerability of the drugs. (3) Obstacles to clinical translation: Effective clinical candidates are scarce—traditional inhibitors are eliminated due to toxicity, and LTP001’s trial termination has left a lack of viable options. PAH is highly heterogeneous, with no reliable biomarkers or stratification criteria to identify responsive subgroups, leading to imprecise clinical application (Fessel et al. 2011). Preclinical PH models fail to simulate the complex pathological microenvironment of human PAH, resulting in unforeseen clinical adverse effects (Cook and Bies 2016).

As a novel UPS-targeted technology, proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) fuse target protein ligands with E3 ligase-recruiting ligands via linker chains, enabling efficient and specific degradation of target proteins by the cellular proteasome (Bricelj et al. 2021). Compared with traditional small-molecule inhibitors, PROTACs offer unique advantages: enhanced specificity via dual recognition of target proteins and E3 ligases, which reduces off-target toxicity (Tsai et al. 2024); the ability to degrade mutant target proteins and overcome drug resistance; and a flexible target spectrum that covers “undruggable” proteins (Hanafi et al. 2021). However, PROTACs still require optimization for PAH application, such as improving pulmonary tissue targeting to reduce systemic toxicity and verifying pulmonary vascular safety in preclinical models (Kelm et al. 2023).

As research into the pathogenesis of PH deepens, the specific mechanisms of various intracellular signaling pathways are becoming increasingly well defined, further elucidating the relationship between the UPS and PH. Building upon the clinical use of bortezomib and the lessons learned from LTP001’s trial termination and carfilzomib’s PH-inducing effect, future efforts should prioritize advancing PROTACs technology and optimizing the safety profile of UPS-targeted agents. It is anticipated that PROTACs-based therapies may potentially address some of the current limitations of UPS-targeted treatments, and could play a valuable role in PH therapy pending further preclinical and clinical validation. Addressing core limitations and leveraging the potential of PROTACs will be essential for advancing UPS-targeted therapies from preclinical promise to clinical utility in PAH.

Conclusion

In summary, the UPS influences the proliferation and migration of PAECs and PASMCs through the regulation of vascular inflammation and cellular hypoxia responses by multiple signaling pathways. Given the vast number of E3 ligases and DUBs and their complex regulatory mechanisms, whether other UPS components are involved in PH pathogenesis remains unclear. Therefore, further in-depth investigations are warranted to gain a comprehensive understanding of the role of the UPS in PH and its potential therapeutic implications.
