Recent antibiotic exposure and response to treatment of juvenile idiopathic arthritis: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Daniel B. Horton, Charu Verma, Sanika Rege, Stephen Crystal, Tobias Gerhard, Alicia Iizuka, Matthew Iozzio, Dawn Koffman, Lauren E. Parlett, Julianne Varga, Carlos D. Rose, Brian L. Strom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundJuvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) has been tied to microbiome disruption and antibiotic exposure. Gut microbiota may affect how adults with rheumatoid arthritis respond to methotrexate. We tested if exposure to antibiotics was associated with response to methotrexate for JIA.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using national US public and private insurance claims data (2001–2023). We included children ages 1–17 continuously enrolled for ≥ 10 months and diagnosed with JIA who initiated methotrexate monotherapy or, for comparison, tumor necrosis factor inhibitor (TNFi) monotherapy without prior disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) exposure. Antibiotic exposure during the 10-month baseline period was characterized by number of courses, timing, and type. The primary outcome was initiation of a second DMARD after ≥ 30 days (proxy of treatment ineffectiveness). Associations between antibiotic exposure and treatment change were estimated using Cox regression, adjusting for database and baseline demographic, disease, treatment, and health utilization covariates, and represented by adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). In additional analyses, we examined differences based on JIA category (juvenile spondyloarthritis vs. others), outcome specification, and follow-up starting up to 90 days after initial DMARD exposure.ResultsWe identified 6,135 new methotrexate users (54.4% antibiotic-exposed) and 1,554 new TNFi users (49.5% antibiotic-exposed), among whom approximately 20% experienced the primary outcome over 10 months of follow-up. No relationships were observed between pre-DMARD antibiotic exposure and DMARD changes in methotrexate users (aHR 1.01, 95% CI 0.90, 1.13) or TNFi users (aHR 1.10, 95% CI 0.86, 1.40). Number of antibiotic courses, antibiotic timing, and type of antibiotic exposure, as well as nonbacterial antimicrobial drug exposure, were also not associated with treatment changes in methotrexate or TNFi users. Findings based on JIA category, alternative outcome definitions, and delayed start of follow-up were consistent.ConclusionsRecent antibiotic exposure is not associated with changes in DMARD treatment in children with JIA. This finding is reassuring for a population with higher risks for serious infections than other children and with high rates of antibiotic exposure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13075-026-03842-6.

Background

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is a family of chronic conditions that collectively represent the most common pediatric rheumatic diagnosis. [1] Inadequate treatment or refractory disease can lead to various complications, including growth abnormalities, deformities, chronic pain, challenges with physical function and activities, vision loss, and chronic disability. [1, 2] Considerable advances have been made in the treatment of JIA, with the emergence of multiple effective disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), including conventional synthetic DMARDs (e.g., methotrexate) as well as biologic DMARDs, including tumor necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi, e.g., etanercept and adalimumab). [3] Nonetheless, many people with JIA have suboptimal response to treatment and need to use multiple agents serially [4].

JIA has been tied to microbiome disruption (dysbiosis). [5] Across multiple studies, children with JIA have been shown to have distinct gut microbiota from unaffected controls. [6–11] Exposure to antibiotics, well-known disruptors of gut microbiota, has also been associated with the subsequent diagnosis of JIA in multiple populations. [11–14] However, little is known about the effects of antibiotics and the microbiome in children who already have JIA. In adults with arthritis and other autoimmune diseases, states of the microbiome have been associated with response to treatment. [15] For example, adults with rheumatoid arthritis who respond to methotrexate have been shown to have differences in gut microbiota compared with non-responders. [16, 17] Furthermore, microbiota-mediated metabolism of methotrexate has been implicated as the mechanism underlying differential responses. [16] However, it is unclear to what extent the state of the microbiome, or microbiome disruptors such as antibiotics, affect response to DMARDs such as methotrexate in children with JIA.

We examined whether antibiotic exposure in children with JIA was associated with response to methotrexate and, for comparison, TNFi. We hypothesized finding shorter times to treatment change after antibiotic exposure (a proxy for treatment ineffectiveness) and larger effects with more frequent, recent, or disruptive antibiotic exposures.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using two large United States (US) administrative claims databases: Medicaid claims data from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (45 states, 2001–2013; 50 states, 2014–2019); and a commercial claims database, the Healthcare Integrated Research Database® (2006–2023). Both data sources contain longitudinal medical and pharmacy insurance claims data from health plan members across the US, including enrollment data, medical care, outpatient prescription drug dispensing, and other healthcare utilization (e.g., outpatient, emergency, and hospital visits). Both data sources have been used previously to study children with JIA and their treatments. [18–21].

Study population

The study population consisted of children ages 1–17 years diagnosed with JIA who initiated methotrexate monotherapy or TNFi monotherapy based on pharmacy dispensing or documented administration. Children were eligible for inclusion if continuously enrolled at least 10 months prior to DMARD initiation, no prior documented DMARD, and no other DMARD initiated within 30 days after the first DMARD. Diagnoses of JIA were based on validated International Classification of Diseases, Ninth or Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9/10) diagnostic codes (ICD-9-CM: 696.0, 714 other than 714.9 [714.0, 714.1, 714.4, or 714.8 at ages 16–17], or 720; ICD-10-CM: L40.5, M08, M45; M05 or M06 at ages 16–17). The combination of these ICD-9/10 codes with a dispensed DMARD had a positive predictive value (PPV) of > 90% for JIA. [22].

Exposure

Based on the premise that antibiotic exposure would most likely disrupt response to DMARDs in the months after exposure, we examined antibiotic exposure during the 10-month period prior to DMARD initiation (Supplementary Fig. 1). In addition to evaluating any antibiotic exposure, we characterized antibiotic exposure by the number of courses (to assess for dose response), defined as the count of distinct systemic antibiotic dispensing dates. For the same antibiotic, consecutive prescriptions were considered part of the same course unless a gap of ≥ 7 days occurred between the end of the prior days’ supply and subsequent dispensing date. Prescriptions for different antibiotics were considered separate courses regardless of timing or overlap. We also characterized antibiotic exposure by the timing of exposure within the 10-month baseline period and type of antibiotic. Type of antibiotic exposure was further classified as macrolides or clindamycin vs. penicillins, anaerobic vs. non-anaerobic, and degree of significant inhibition (false discovery rate < 0.01) based on a sample of 40 commensal and other common human gut microbes in vitro (high, ≥ 90% strains inhibited; medium, 76–89% strains inhibited; low, ≤ 75% strains inhibited). [23] Nonbacterial antimicrobial drugs were evaluated as a negative control exposure since they were not hypothesized to be associated with the outcome.

Outcome

The primary outcome was initiation of a second DMARD in addition to or instead of the index DMARD, at least 1 month after index DMARD initiation. The 1-month lag period between DMARD initiation and outcome ascertainment represented a hypothetical induction time between antibiotic-induced dysbiosis and altered DMARD response (e.g., through impaired effectiveness or altered disease activity). The lag period also accounted for infection-associated flares, which typically occur acutely in the setting of infection, whereas the effects of antibiotics and dysbiosis hypothetically last longer. Finally, the lag period would exclude delayed initiation of a second DMARD (e.g., biologic) intended for combination use (e.g., due to delays in approval by insurance companies). Longer lag periods up to 3 months were evaluated in sensitivity analyses (see Statistical Analysis). This outcome was used as proxy for treatment ineffectiveness and adapted from a previously validated outcome of treatment change, which represented insufficient disease response with PPV ≥ 94% [24]. Given that biologics are often added to methotrexate in the case of insufficient clinical response, a secondary outcome definition only included addition of a second DMARD for methotrexate monotherapy users. Censoring occurred with the earliest of the following: a 60-day gap without additional DMARD (index DMARD or another DMARD type) following the end of the index DMARD course based on days’ supply (similar to the gap used to study biologics in a recent claims-based population with JIA) [25]; completion of 10 months of follow-up; loss of enrollment; or death.

Covariates

Numerous potential confounders were assessed during the 10-month baseline period: demographic factors, including, sex, race and ethnicity (to account for inequities in health and health care among different racial and ethnic groups), and region of the country; temporal factors, namely, calendar year and quarter of index DMARD initiation; JIA category (juvenile spondyloarthritis [enthesitis-related arthritis or psoriatic arthritis] vs. other categories); selected comorbidities: asthma or allergic conditions, uveitis, autoimmune conditions (besides uveitis), Down or Turner syndrome; Pediatric Medical Complexity Algorithm, a publicly available algorithm that identifies children with complex chronic disease [26]; receipt of other JIA treatments (nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, glucocorticoids, or folic acid); documented infection within one month prior to index DMARD initiation; and baseline healthcare utilization (healthcare plan type, number of outpatient visits, number of emergency visits, recent hospitalization). Covariates in methotrexate models also included methotrexate dose (low vs. high, categorized based on median weekly dosage by age) and route (oral vs. subcutaneous).

Statistical analysis

Characteristics of the study population were summarized using descriptive statistics, either frequencies and percentages for categorical variables or means with standard deviation for continuous variables. Associations between antibiotic exposure and treatment changes were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression, adjusting for database, demographic, clinical, and baseline healthcare utilization covariates, and represented by adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). aHRs greater than 1 signified shorter times to initiation of a second DMARD after antibiotic exposure. Evaluation of scaled Schoenfeld residuals, log-log plots, and global statistical tests did not identify any violations of the proportional hazards assumption. While analyses were initially planned to be stratified by database and combined via fixed-effects meta-analysis, the small number of outcomes in one database and potential for model overfitting led to analyses of the combined cohort with adjustment for database. In secondary analyses, we evaluated associations within subgroups by JIA category (juvenile spondyloarthritis or other categories of JIA) and also considered the alternative outcome of an additional DMARD for methotrexate users. We performed multiple sensitivity analyses to test the impact of model assumptions: (1) models incorporating longer lag periods after DMARD initiation (60–90 days); (2) evaluation of a 4-month antibiotic exposure ascertainment period and a 6-month covariate ascertainment period preceding antibiotic exposure to evaluate potential bias from simultaneous variable ascertainment; (3) considering hospitalizations for infection as equivalent to antibiotic exposure to capture missing inpatient antibiotic data in the setting of more severe infections; and (4) censoring after a 90-day gap without additional DMARD (index DMARD or another DMARD type). Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

We identified 7,689 eligible patients with JIA (90% publicly insured): 6,135 new users of methotrexate and 1,554 new users of TNFi. In general, exposed patients tended to be younger, White non-Hispanic, from the South, have more comorbidities, more concomitant medication use, and more healthcare utilization (Table 1). Compared to the commercially insured population, publicly insured children tended to be younger, more likely to be non-Hispanic Black or Hispanic/Latino and from the South, use more concomitant medications, and have more healthcare utilization (Supplementary Table 1). A majority of the study population had antibiotic exposure in the 10 months before DMARD initiation (methotrexate users, 54.4%, median 1 course (interquartile range, 0–2); TNFi users, 49.5%, median 0 course (interquartile range, 0–2)) (Supplementary Table 2).

About one in five patients added or switched to a second biologic within 10 months after first DMARD initiation (methotrexate users: 21.7% exposed, 20.9% unexposed; TNFi users: 21.0% exposed, 17.6% unexposed). About half of the cohort was censored for a 60-day gap in the index DMARD (methotrexate users: 54.4% exposed, 45.6% unexposed; TNFi users: 47.3% exposed, 52.7% unexposed). After adjustment for database, demographic, clinical, and baseline healthcare utilization covariates, antibiotics were not associated with treatment change in methotrexate users (aHR 1.01, 95% CI 0.90, 1.13) (Fig. 1) or TNFi users (aHR 1.10, 95% CI 0.86, 1.40) (Fig. 2). We did not find appreciable relationships between either the number of antibiotic courses dispensed or the timing of antibiotic exposure pre-DMARD initiation and subsequent receipt of a second DMARD among initiators of methotrexate (Fig. 1) or TNFi monotherapy (Fig. 2). Furthermore, associations were null and not meaningfully different based on anti-communal activity in vitro or spectrum of antibiotic activity (clindamycin or macrolides vs. penicillin; anti-anaerobic versus non-anti-anaerobic) (Figs. 1 and 2). We also found no significant associations between exposure to non-bacterial antimicrobial drugs and receipt of a second DMARD (Figs. 1 and 2).

Discussion

In a large population of publicly and privately insured children diagnosed with JIA from across the US starting either methotrexate or TNFi monotherapy, we found no meaningful differences in the rates of the subsequent receipt of a second DMARD based on patterns of antibiotic exposure in the 10 months leading up to DMARD initiation. These results were consistent across multiple dimensions of antibiotic exposure, including the number of courses, the timing of exposure, and the degree of anticipated microbiome disruption. Results were also consistent within different subgroups of JIA category and across several alternate outcome definitions. While these findings do not support our hypotheses of antibiotic-mediated effects on methotrexate response, they are, nonetheless, reassuring for a population that is at higher risk for serious infections and with high rates of antibiotic exposure.

Gut microbiota can influence treatment response directly through drug metabolism and indirectly by other means. [27, 28] For instance, certain commensal gut bacteria produce carboxypeptidase enzymes that metabolize methotrexate into inactive metabolites. [29] In one study of adults with rheumatoid arthritis, those who did not respond to methotrexate were more likely to have fecal microbiota that metabolized and depleted methotrexate ex vivo, [16] implicating this as a mechanism underlying the observed clinical responses. Gut microbiota may also indirectly affect treatment response by changing disease activity, as exemplified by the effectiveness of fecal microbiota transplantation in children and adults with inflammatory bowel disease. [30, 31] Fecal microbiota transplant has also shown promise as a treatment for psoriatic arthritis in adults, particularly those with Bacteroides-predominant microbiota. [32] Early evidence suggests that certain dietary interventions in JIA may also have clinical benefits due to hypothetical effects on the gut microbiome. [33] Medications may also exert therapeutic responses by modifying microbiota. For example, methotrexate kills certain commensal bacteria in vitro, particularly those from phylum Bacteroidetes. [23, 29] Interestingly, several studies have shown increased abundance of Bacteroidetes bacteria in children with JIA, [7, 8, 34, 35] raising this as another hypothetical mechanism of response to treatment with methotrexate. Nonetheless, our results did not support the hypothesis that antibiotic-mediated dysbiosis influenced the response to methotrexate in children with JIA, either from the effects of microbiota on methotrexate or from the effects of methotrexate on microbiota. TNFi treatment with adalimumab has also been shown to correspond to normalization of gut microbiota in adults with spondylarthritis. [36] However, as with methotrexate use, we also found no meaningful link between antibiotic exposure and response to TNFi in children with JIA, both with adjustment for JIA category and within subgroups with or without juvenile spondyloarthritis.

Children with JIA are at higher risk for serious infections than unaffected children, irrespective of DMARD exposure. [18] Indeed, children with JIA face risks of infections of various types and severity, with risks relating to factors such as age, glucocorticoid exposure, and disease activity. [18, 37–39] In our study population, a large proportion of children with JIA initiating a DMARD—approximately half—had received antibiotics at some point in the prior 10 months, increasing proportionally in the months leading up to initial treatment. While we did not focus specifically on children with newly diagnosed JIA, a JIA population initiating a first-line DMARD would be expected to be enriched with newly diagnosed children. Prior studies have suggested higher rates of antibiotic exposure within one year before JIA diagnosis, though it is debated whether antibiotics represent triggers of disease or are early markers of immune dysfunction. [12, 13] For those previously diagnosed with JIA, another possibility is that pediatricians and others may see patients with JIA more frequently and thereby detect more infections, or they may have a lower threshold for prescribing antibiotics for infections in patients with JIA than for other children. Regardless of the reasons, this large burden of infection and antibiotic exposure attests to importance of understanding the influence of antibiotics on response to DMARDs for JIA.

Our study had multiple strengths, addressing a novel research question in a large, diverse population of children with JIA initiating treatment with either a conventional or biologic DMARD using two US databases. While diagnoses of JIA were not directly confirmed for each individual, our definition for cohort identification was supported by prior validation work and likely highly accurate given our focus on DMARD users. We examined antibiotic exposure in multiple ways related to our overlying hypothesis of microbiome-mediated treatment disruption, providing deeper understanding of the influence of antibiotics and a more thorough demonstration of null effects. We adjusted for multiple potential sources of confounding between exposed and unexposed children, including sociodemographic factors, clinical factors, and healthcare utilization, which serve as proxies for unmeasured confounding. [40] In addition, our findings were highly consistent across multiple secondary and sensitivity analysis, including when stratified by category of JIA.

Our study also had certain limitations. As with any observational study, residual or unmeasured confounding may have influenced our findings. While medication dispensing data in administrative claims is closer to consumption than prescribing data alone, [41] we could not ascertain or control for what antibiotic doses children did or did not actually take. Additionally, we could not directly assess disease severity, activity, or reasons for new DMARD therapy, nor did we consider use of other treatments for flares (e.g., glucocorticoids), which may have contributed to outcome misclassification. Nonetheless, our outcome was derived from a previously validated algorithm representing inadequate treatment response, [24] and our findings were consistent across multiple alternative outcome definitions. As our study focused on users of either methotrexate or TNFi, our findings may not apply to populations with systemic JIA, for whom risks of serious infection are even higher. [38] While our analysis were well-powered overall, we were unable, given the small size of one of the databases, to explore potential heterogeneity across distinct populations with different insurance plans and socioeconomic backgrounds. The precision of certain analyses (e.g., for TNFi users with juvenile spondyloarthritis) was also limited.

Conclusions

In conclusion, in a large US population with JIA initiating either methotrexate or a TNFi, antibiotic exposure was not associated with substantive changes in DMARD treatment. These findings suggest that antibiotics do not substantially alter response to either methotrexate or TNFi for JIA. These negative findings should be reassuring to clinicians, patients, and families alike, given the elevated risks for serious infections in children with JIA and the frequent need for antibiotic treatment in this population.
