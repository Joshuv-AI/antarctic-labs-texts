Photodynamic/Photothermal Gold Nanoclusters Chitosan Sponge for Hemostasis and MRSA Infected Wound Healing
Authors: Xiaoyi Shi, Jingqian Su, Wenting Li, Keke Li, Xiangjun Chen, Wei Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMultidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria, particularly methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), impede wound infection treatment. Novel strategies shall be designed to effectively eliminate bacteria and facilitate wound recovery.MethodsThis study fabricated a new chitosan-based sponge, CS-AuNCs/ICG, to combat MDR bacteria and treat skin wounds. In the CS sponge system, ICG can rapidly generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) upon light irradiation, thereby exerting a rapid bactericidal effect; in contrast, the gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) slowly released from the system can achieve a long-lasting bactericidal effect.ResultsIn vitro, CS-AuNCs/ICG exhibited excellent antibacterial activity against both planktonic MRSA and biofilm. Moreover, this sponge featured not only an efficient hemostatic property but also a high-porosity porous structure ideal for wound exudate absorption. Local remedy of MRSA-infected mice using the CS-AuNCs/ICG sponge combined with light irradiation resulted in prompt bacterial elimination and prolonged bacteriostasis in the skin wounds, which accelerated wound healing without any observed side effects.DiscussionIn summary, the CS-AuNCs/ICG sponge possesses both high-efficiency antibacterial activity and sustained bacterial inhibition ability, demonstrating significant application potential in treating wounds infected with drug-resistant bacteria.Keywords: wound, chitosan sponge, gold nanoclusters, photodynamic/photothermal antibacterial therapy, MRSA

Introduction

Wound healing is dynamic and intricate, involving granulation tissue formation, matrix re-modelling, re-epithelialization.1 Wound dressings help maintain an optimally moist environment favorable for healing.2–4 However, wounds such as trauma and burns are susceptible to bacterial contamination and the consequent infection-related syndromes, including sepsis and acute renal failure. Though antibiotics often successfully address bacterial infections, the rise of antibiotic-resistant strains, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) for example, often diminish or even completely negate their bactericidal and bacteriostatic activities. With it comes excessive persistent inflammation, delayed healing and chronic wounds.5–7 Therefore, developing antibacterial wound dressing is crucial for fostering tissue regeneration and enhancing the wound healing process.

Chitosan (CS)-based wound dressings hold great promise due to their minimal toxicity, natural biodegradability, and strong hemostatic performance. Among the CS-based wound dressing products ratified by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), CS sponges have the greatest potential for wound treatment due to their high porosity structure, favorable mechanical properties and optimal moisture vapor transmission rate, which help maintain a favorable environment for wounds.8–16 Nevertheless, most CS sponge products are limited in antibacterial efficacy, restricting their ability to prevent infections.17 It is therefore imperative to develop a novel therapeutic CS-based sponge to deal with bacterial infection and improve wound healing.

One strategy is to incorporate newly developed antibacterial materials (eg. cationic polymers, metal and metal oxide nanoparticles) into CS-based materials.4,18–20 For example, Chen and his coworkers designed sponge-like silver nanoparticles (AgNPs)/chitosan which exhibited remarkable antibacterial ability. Though promising, the slow release of AgNPs does not provide an immediate bactericidal effect, resulting in a long healing time.21 Better wound healing requires both prompt bacterial killing and prolonged bacteriostasis. To achieve rapid bacterial eradication, light-mediated therapeutic strategies, such as photothermal therapy (PTT) and photodynamic therapy (PDT), have offered valuable insights.22,23 PTT nanoagents, such as carbon-based, gold-based and diverse organic nanomaterials, can transform light into heat, leading to prompt bacterial elimination through physical thermal effects.24–26 Photosensitizers (PSs) employed in PDT produce hydroxyl radicals, superoxides, or singlet oxygen (1O2), effectively disrupting biofilms and the internal structure of bacterial cells.27–29 However, these two approaches still have limitations. Conventional PTT tends to generate heat indiscriminately, potentially damaging surrounding healthy organs and tissues. Although PDT reduces this risk, its nonselective sterilization may cause microbiota dysbiosis. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop a highly selective PDT strategy able to treat infections with minimal disruption to the microbiota balance and normal tissues and cells. The integration of PSs into wound dressing materials, such as foams, nanoagents, and hydrogels, has demonstrated great improvement in the antibacterial efficiency. Miao et al fabricated a chitosan-based antibacterial sponge (CS‑ZnO/Ce6) integrating zinc oxide and chlorin e6 Upon 660 nm irradiation, it achieved rapid photodynamic sterilization and long‑term antibacterial effects against MDR strains, significantly promoting MRSA‑infected wound healing with favorable biocompatibility.30

Gold nanoclusters (AuNCs), nanomaterials composed of gold atoms, may provide prolonged bacteriostasis. Gold, known for its inert nature, exhibits excellent biocompatibility. Additionally, AuNCs possess other unique properties such as ultra-small size, photoluminescence and remarkable catalytic activity.31 Though gold has traditionally been perceived as having limited antibacterial capabilities, recent evidence reveals remarkably high, broad-spectrum intrinsic antimicrobial activity in ultrasmall-sized AuNCs, which is not observed in larger AuNCs.32–35 Studies suggest that these ultrasmall-sized AuNCs eradicate pathogenic bacteria via a combined physicochemical mechanism that could destroy the cell membrane, damage DNA and generate intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS).36,37

Currently, chitosan-based materials loaded with gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) or single indocyanine green (ICG) have been investigated to a certain extent in the biomedical field. However, such single-component loading systems often suffer from limitations including monotonous functionality, restricted photothermal conversion efficiency, and insufficient in vivo stability. In this study, benefiting from the three-dimensional porous structure and favorable biocompatibility of chitosan, we innovatively developed a chitosan sponge incorporated with PSs and AuNCs, which enables rapid bacterial eradication and sustained bacterial inhibition against MDR bacterial wound infections. The FDA-approved fluorescent imaging agent, Indocyanine green (ICG), was loaded to the sponge via electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions and functioned as a PS. With its outstanding photochemical properties, ICG generate substantial heat and ROS under light exposure, thereby immediately inactivating bacteria. Meanwhile, AuNCs provide sustained bacterial inhibition, preventing secondary infection and facilitating wound healing (Scheme 1). This approach not only overcomes the core challenge of single-modal therapy, namely the failure to simultaneously meet the requirements of immediate and long-term efficacy, but also provides a safe, efficient and low-resistance novel therapeutic strategy for chronic wounds. Furthermore, its synergistic mechanism offers a new paradigm for the design of antibacterial materials.

Methods

Preparation of CS-AuNCs/ICG Composite Sponge

AuNCs with the 6-mercaptohexanoic acid (MHA) ligands were prepared following the previously reported methods.38 HAuCl4 (20 mM, 250 μL) and MHA (5 mM, 2 mL) were mixed with 2.4 mL of water to form an Au-MHA complex, followed by dissolution upon the addition of NaOH solution (1 mM, 250 μL). A freshly prepared NaBH4 solution (20 mM, 100 μL) was then dropwise introduced to the complex with stirring at 25rpm, and left to react for 3 h at room temperature, accompanied by the solution turning to yellowish-brown. Finally, the resulting AuNCs were collected and purified using Ultrafiltration in Stirred Cell with a semipermeable membrane of having a molecular weight cutoff of 3 kDa. The AuNCs’ size and zeta potential were characterized using a JEOL JEM1400 transmission electron microscope (Japan) and a NanoBrook 90PlusPALS analyzer (USA).

The chitosan sponges were fabricated using the method of freeze-drying.30 A chitosan aqueous solution (20 mg/mL, 1% acetic acid) was poured into molds and frozen at −20 °C for 12 h. Following a 24-hour freeze-drying process, the chitosan sponges were formed. They were then immersed in a 3% NaOH solution for 2 h and thoroughly washed in deionized water till a neutral pH was achieved. To be loaded with AuNCs, the prepared chitosan sponges were ultrasonically soaked for 2 h in weakly alkaline AuNCs aqueous suspension at different concentrations (50, 100, 150 and 200 μg/mL). The resultant sponges after lyophilization were named CS-50AuNCs, CS-100AuNCs, CS-150AuNCs and CS-200AuNCs, respectively. Afterwards, these chitosan sponges with AuNCs were ultrasonically soaked with 150 μL of ICG solution at different concentrations (0.5, 0.75, 1 and 1.25 mg/mL) at room temperature. Consequently, the CS-AuNCs/ICG composite sponges were obtained upon freeze-drying. The photothermal properties of these CS-AuNCs/ICG sponge samples (diameter: 12 mm, thickness: 2 mm) with different ICG loading contents were examined via an IR thermal imaging system to determine ideal ICG content for subsequent experiments.

The Zeta potential and morphology of the sponges (CS, CS-ICG, CS-150AuNCs and CS-150AuNCs/ICG) were measured using a NanoBrook 90PlusPALS analyzer (USA) and a Zeiss EVO LS15 scanning electron microscopy (Germany). The ICG content in the sponge was assessed by evaluating the absorbance at 740 nm using a PERSEE TU-1901 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (China).

Whole-Blood Clotting Assay

To evaluate the blood coagulation performance, the time for blood clotting upon the chitosan sponge treatment was measured. 1 mL citrated whole blood was applied to either cotton balls or the sponge samples (diameter: 5 mm, thickness: 1.5 mm) and cultured for 5 min at 37 °C. After 50 μL of CaCl2 (20 mM) was introduced, the mixtures were inverted every 15s till the blood-sponge/cotton aggregate no longer flew. The clotting time was recorded.

The blood-clotting index (BCI) of the sponges was also evaluated. A 50 μL of recalcified whole blood was applied to the surface of cotton balls or the sponge disks (diameter: 5 mm, thickness: 1.5 mm), and cultured for 150 s at 37 °C. Then, 10 mL of deionized water was used to wash away the unattached blood cells, leaving the clot intact. The supernatant absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader to obtain the value of Is. The absorbance value of the negative control group, Ir, was determined by mixing with 10 mL of deionized water with 50 mL of whole blood.

Blood Cell Adhesion Assay

Whole blood was applied to the sponge samples (diameter: 5 mm, thickness: 1.5 mm). After 5-mintues incubation at 37 °C, physically attached blood cells were gently removed from the samples before they were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 2 hours. Then, the samples were dehydrated using ethanol solutions at incremental concentrations of 30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90% and 100%. At last, images of the adherent blood cells were captured via field emission SEM.

Biocompatibility Test

The cytotoxicity of the chitosan sponges was evaluated against L929 and RAW264.7 cells using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) reduction assay. L929 and RAW 264.7 cells were seeded at a density of 1×104 cells per well into 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Then, the pre-sterilized chitosan sponge samples (diameter: 5 mm, thickness: 1.5 mm, and pre-sterilized in 75% alcohol), including CS, CS-50AuNCs/ICG, CS-100AuNCs/ICG, CS-150AuNCs/ICG and CS-200AuNCs/ICG, equilibrated in complete growth medium, were placed into the wells and co-cultured with the cells for 6 h. 20 μL of MTT solutions (5 mg/mL) was applied to the wells and cultured for 4 h before the removal of medium. Finally, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was introduced to dissolve the formazan. A microplate reader measured the absorbance at 490 nm to determine the cell viability. The same tests were carried out in a blank control group without sponge treatment. All experiments were carried out in triplicate.

The hemolytic activity of the chitosan sponges was examined by utilizing red blood cells of mouse. Whole blood was obtained from mice following the standard procedure. The red blood cells were separated and purified through centrifugation (4000 rpm, 15 min), followed by dilution to a 2% concentration using normal saline. The diluted red blood cells were subsequently incubated with chitosan sponge samples (diameter: 5 mm, thickness: 1.5 mm). Deionized water (DW) and normal saline served as the positive and the negative controls, respectively. After incubation at 37 °C for 2 h, the samples were centrifuged (4000 rpm, 15 min) again at room temperature. The supernatant absorbance was measured at 545 nm to evaluate the hemoglobin release.

For the scratch assay, L929 cells were seeded at a density of 1×105 cells per well in 24-well plates and cultured until reaching 90% confluence. Following 6-hour treatment with saline or chitosan sponge samples (diameter: 5 mm, thickness: 1.5 mm, and pre-sterilized in 75% alcohol), respectively, a uniform scratch was made in each well using a p10 pipette tip. The scratch areas were monitored every 12 h via an inverted optical microscope.

In vitro Antibacterial Assay

Firstly, the microbial penetration test was conducted to assess the sponge’s capability to block the spread of the environmental microorganisms to the wound surface. Sponge samples of equal volume, including CS, CS-AuNCs, CS-ICG, and CS-AuNCs/ICG, were placed onto 10 mL open vials, each containing 5-mL nutrient broth (test area: 0.8 cm2). Sealed vial with a screw cap was used in the negative control, and unsealed vials was used in the positive control. The vials were placed in the same open environment for 1 week. Any turbidity of the nutrient broth in the vials indicated microbial contamination.

To evaluate the photodynamic bactericidal ability of the sponges against MRSA, the method of standard plate counting was employed. Bacteria were first cultured in LB at 37 °C overnight with shaking. The sponge samples (diameter: 5 mm, thickness: 1.5 mm), were applied to the above LB broth with bacteria (107 CFU/mL) and cultured for 5 min under either dark conditions or NIR illumination (808 nm, 1 W/cm2). The tested agents included saline, CS, CS-AuNCs, CS-ICG, and CS-AuNCs/ICG. Then, 100 μL of each bacterial suspension was harvested and plated onto an LB agar plate for CFU counting.

The antibacterial activity of the sponges was also assessed by the live/dead assay with a BacLight Bacterial Viability Kit. CS, CS-AuNCs, CS-ICG, and CS-AuNCs/ICG were incubated with MRSA suspension overnight. Irradiation began 1 h after the start of incubation. Upon the incubation finished, the samples were stained by a dye mixture composed of propidium iodide and SYTO 9 in a 1:1 ratio. After the 30-minute staining at 25 °C, fluorescence images were acquired via an Olympus BX53F2 Fluorescent Microscope (Japan).

SEM examination was used to further observe the effects of the sponges on the bacterial suspensions. Samples were prepared as outlined in the above procedure, and then gradually dehydrated and coated with gold before they were assessed by a Zeiss EVO LS15 SEM (Germany).

Anti-Biofilm Assay

The anti-biofilm activity of different formulations (CS, CS-AuNCs, CS-ICG, and CS-AuNCs/ICG) against MRSA biofilms were evaluated utilizing crystal violet staining. After incubated for 1 h, the samples were exposed to irradiation for 5 min. Subsequently, 0.5% crystal violet solution was used to stain the residual biofilms for 10 min. The mass was then examined using a Japanese Olympus BX43F2 Fluorescent Microscope.

CLSM was employed to further assess the biofilm elimination capacity of CS-AuNCs/ICG. Mature MRSA biofilms were treated with CS, CS-AuNCs, CS-ICG, and CS-AuNCs/ICG and incubated overnight. Irradiation commenced 1 h after the start of the incubation. Finally, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, Zeiss LSM 880) was employed to capture Z-stack images at 1 μm intervals via c to acquire the fluorescence images.

In addition, the effects of the sponges on the biofilm were observed through SEM examination. Biofilm samples prepared following the aforementioned procedure were gradually dehydrated, gold-coated, and then analyzed using a Zeiss EVO LS15 SEM (Germany).

ROS Generation in Biofilms and Planktonic Bacteria

To reveal the underlying mechanisms, generation of ROS pre- and post-treatment was tested using 2,7-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). Bacterial suspension or biofilms were cultured with DCFH-DA (10 μM) for 10 min, followed by several washes to remove excess dye. PBS, CS, CS-AuNCs, CS-ICG and CS-AuNCs/ICG were then added. Irradiation commenced after 1-hour incubation. Finally, fluorescence intensities and images were measured using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer (USA) and an Olympus BX53F2 Fluorescence Microscope (FM, Japan).

In vivo Antibacterial and Wound Healing Assay

BALB/c mice were under anesthesia with 2.5% avertin solution via intraperitoneal injection (200 μL). The dorsal surface of the BALB/c mice was shaved and disinfected with alcohol. A circular full-thickness defect wound (diameter: 5 mm) was prepared by disinfected surgical scissors, followed by the application of MRSA (107 CFU/mL) to the wound surface. 24 h post-infection, these mice were randomly assigned to 5 groups, each receiving treatment with saline, CS, CS-AuNCs, CS-ICG, and CS-AuNCs/ICG, respectively. The dressings (sponge size: diameter: 12 mm, thickness: 2 mm) applied to cover the wounds were replaced every other day. The wounds were subject to an 808 nm laser (0.5 W/cm2) for 5 min every time the dressings were replaced. Thermal images of each mouse were captured using an infrared thermal camera at every 1-minute interval following irradiation. The wounds were photographed every other day to observe healing progress. The wound area was quantified and the mice weight was recorded accordingly. On Days 2 and 14 after the surgery, bacterial exudate from the wound was collected and incubated in LB broth for 4 h at 37 °C. Bacterial load was determined using the standard plate counting method. On Day 14 post-treatment, the mice were sacrificed by carbon dioxide (CO2) inhalation and the infected tissues were collected for H&E to conduct the histological analysis.

Statistical Analyses

Data are displayed as mean ± standard deviation (S.D). Student’s t-test was employed to analyze the comparisons between two groups. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, and ns means not significant.

Results and Discussion

Fabrication of CS-AuNCs/ICG Sponge

The NaBH4 reduction method was utilized to synthesize AuNCs. According to the TEM images, the AuNCs were spherical and well-monodispersed (Figure 1A), with a core size less than 2 nm. The photothermal responsiveness of the CS-AuNCs/ICG was evaluated by exposing the sponges to the 808 nm laser at a density of 0.5 W/cm2 for 5 min. CS-AuNCs/ICG sponges with varying ICG loading contents all exhibited significant temperature rises (Figure 1B and C). This thermal change was further confirmed by NIR thermal imaging. The sponges containing 0.75 mg/mL ICG were chosen and tested under different laser powers of 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1.0 W/cm2. As presented in Figure 1D and E, CS-AuNCs/ICG demonstrated a favorable temperature increase (≈ 55 °C) under the laser intensity of 0.5 W/cm2. Consequently, the CS-AuNCs/ICG sponge with 0.75 mg/mL ICG and the laser intensity of 0.5 W/cm2 were identified as the ideal preparation for subsequent antimicrobial investigations.

(D) Temperature change and (E) photographs of CS-AuNCs/ICG under various irradiation intensities as a function of irradiation time with an 808 nm laser. (F) Cell viability and (G) hemolysis rates of CS and CS-AuNCs/ICG prepared at various AuNCs concentrations. (H) Scratch closure of L929 monolayer cells after treatment with different agents (saline, CS, CS-50AuNCs/ICG, CS-100AuNCs/ICG and CS-150AuNCs/ICG). Scale bar=200 μm.

Direct contact pattern was adopted to assess the biocompatibility of the CS sponges with different compositions. Upon direct interaction with cells, the CS sponges were found to mildly enhance the L929 cell growth, suggesting their favorable biocompatibility. L929 cells cultured with the CS-50AuNCs/ICG, CS-100AuNCs/ICG, and CS-150AuNCs/ICG sponges exhibited viabilities exceeding 80% (Figure 1F). In contrast, a remarkable viability reduction to 75% was observed for the cells treated with the CS-200AuNCs/ICG sponge. The cytotoxicity test of the sponges to RAW264.7 cells yielded similar results (Figure S1). Hemolysis studies demonstrated that all the sponges induced negligible hemolysis activity in blood cells, as shown in Figure 1G. The scratch assay (Figure 1H) exhibited little difference in the scratch closure rates between the saline-treated and the sponge-treated cells, indicating that the sponges exerted no adverse effect on fibroblast growth. Therefore, CS-50AuNCs/ICG, CS-100AuNCs/ICG and CS-150AuNCs/ICG were selected for the subsequent experiments.

Physical and Mechanical Properties of CS-AuNCs/ICG Sponge

Porosity tests (Figure 2A) demonstrated high porosities in all sponges, ranging between 80–100% even after the incorporation of AuNCs and ICG. This property could enhance the wound exudate absorption and prevent infection. Moreover, this porous architecture facilitates the transport of oxygen and nutrients to the adhered cells. The water absorption capacity of all the sponges exceeded 2000% (Figure 2B). As shown in Figure 2C, the moisture retention capacity remained unchanged after the incorporation of AuNCs and ICG, indicating that CS-AuNCs/ICG sponges with various AuNCs loading content have similar water retention capacity as CS sponges.

(J) SEM elemental mapping of Au in the CS-AuNCs/ICG sponge. Scale bar=250 μm.

The CS sponges demonstrated a tensile strength of 0.25 MPa and an elongation at break of 21.67%, making them suitable for wound dressing. Interactions between AuNCs and chitosan resulted in a marginal rise in tensile strength after the incorporation of AuNCs (Figure 2D). The elongation at break, indicating material flexibility, plays an important role in enabling the sponge to conform effectively to wound surfaces. Notably, the CS-150AuNCs/ICG sponge demonstrated an elongation at break of 30.41%, surpassing that of the CS sponges (21.6%), suggesting improved flexibility with increasing AuNCs loading (Figure 2E). Based on the results, the CS-150AuNCs/ICG sponge was chosen for the subsequent experiments.

According to the photographs displayed in Figure 2F, the CS and CS-AuNCs sponges appeared whitish, while the CS-ICG and CS-150AuNCs/ICG sponges showed a greenish tint because of the incorporation of ICG. SEM images (Figure 2G) indicated that all the lyophilized sponges possessed a loose and porous form with a structure of uniform microporous network. The introduction of ICG and AuNCs did not brought significant changes. The average zeta-potential of solutions CS, AuNCs, ICG, and CS-AuNCs/ICG were measured respectively as 58.21 mV, −33.37 mV, −12.16 mV, and 49.32 mV, which indicating that the electronic interaction between positively charged chitosan and negatively charged ICG and AuNCs could facilitate the drug encapsulation (Figure 2H). UV-vis spectra (Figure 2I) exhibited a characteristic band at around 780 nm in the spectrum of CS-150AuNCs/ICG, corresponding to the ICG chromophore and suggesting the successful loading of ICG. SEM elemental mapping displays the presence of Au in the CS-150AuNCs/ICG, indicating effective incorporation of AuNCs (Figure 2J). The loading efficiency of AuNCs was assessed by ICP, with results calculated on the standard concentration curve of Au. The loading efficiency of AuNCs in the sponges exceeded 65% for all formulations. ICG’s loading efficiency was nearly 100% via absorption quantification.

In vitro Blood-Clotting Assay

The blood coagulation performance of the sponges was evaluated by measuring blood clotting time. As shown in Figure 3A, the average blood clotting time for cotton balls was 181 s, remarkably longer than those of the CS-based sponges (CS sponge: 121 s, CS-AuNCs: 104 s, CS-ICG: 103 s, CS-AuNCs/ICG: 105 s). These findings demonstrated the capability of the chitosan-based sponges to effectively facilitate blood coagulation.

Data are the mean ± SD, n=3, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ****p < 0.0001 compared with cotton ball, ##p < 0.01 compared with CS-AuNCs/ICG, and n.s. represents no significant difference. (D) SEM images of blood cell adhesion of CS, CS-AuNCs, CS-ICG, and CS-AuNCs/ICG.

The hemostasis mechanism of the sponges was assessed by measuring the blooding-clot index (BCI). Red cell suspensions were dropped onto cotton balls, CS, CS-AuNCs, CS-ICG and CS-AuNCs/ICG sponges. The samples were then cultured for 150 s at 37 °C. The cotton ball group showed an average BCI of 65.17% (Figure 3B), 2–3 times higher than the CS and CS-AuNCs/ICG sponges, suggesting more successful blood coagulation of CS-based sponges.

To further elucidate the hemostatic mechanism, the adhesion geometry and morphology of blood cells on different dressing surfaces were examined by SEM. As shown in Figure S2, minimal cell adhesion was observed to cotton, while a greater number of blood cells with altered morphology were found on the CS sponge, possibly caused by the positively charged CS (Figure 3C). The CS-AuNCs/ICG sponge demonstrated extensive and robust blood cell attachment (Figure 3D). The results confirm that the CS-AuNCs/ICG sponge possessed excellent blood-clotting capability to effectively prevent bleeding, demonstrating its potential as an ideal hemostasis material.

In vitro Antibacterial Assays and Bacterial Inhibition Mechanism

Microbial penetration tests (Figure 4A) have shown that under identical conditions, only the culture liquid in the unsealed vial became turbid, this indicated that the sponges effectively blocked bacteria, demonstrating their ability to physically shield the wound from the external environment and significantly reduce the infection risk.

The bactericidal ability of the CS-AuNCs/ICG sponge was evaluated against planktonic MRSA via the method of plate counting, as illustrated in Figure 4B. The CS-AuNCs/ICG sponge showed excellent antibacterial efficiency, approaching nearly 100% under light irradiation. Similar results were observed through CLSM and SEM assays. As shown in Figure 4C, the number of dead bacteria (red fluorescence) was significantly reduced in samples treated with CS-AuNCs/ICG under irradiation compared with other tested agents. Moreover, after treatment with the CS sponge combined with irradiation, MRSA cells maintained a typical spherical morphology with smooth surface and intact cell walls, suggesting limited antibacterial efficiency. In contrast, MRSA treated with CS-AuNCs/ICG under light irradiation exhibited severe structural disruption, characterized by multiple fractures and pores visible in the SEM images, hereby confirming the exceptional antibacterial activity of CS-AuNCs/ICG in the presence of light.

Its antibiofilm performance was further assessed using crystal violet staining plate counting assay (Figure 4D). Upon light irradiation, the CS-treated biofilms exhibited a dark purple, while those treated with CS-AuNCs/ICG showed a significantly lighter hue. The quantitative results (Figure 4E) revealed that the survival rate of MRSA biofilm was reduced to 12.1% after treatment with CS-AuNCs/ICG under irradiation, compared to approximately 87.2%, 71.9% and 54.9% for those treated with CS, CS-AuNCs and CS-ICG sponges, respectively. These findings were in agreement with the crystal violet staining results.

The anti-biofilm effects of sponges with different formulations were observed using SEM (Figure 4F). After the co-cultivation of CS-AuNCs/ICG+light with MRSA, significant changes were observed in the biofilm and intracellular structures, indicating the destruction of biofilms and pronounced changes in cell morphology. The CS sponge-treated group exhibited different results, with intact biofilm structure and nearly unaffected cell morphology. Both the CS-AuNCs sponge and CS-ICG sponge demonstrated certain biofilm elimination capabilities, but their effect was inferior to that of the CS-AuNCs/ICG+light treatment.

The viability of bacteria within the biofilms was further evaluated using live/dead (green/red) fluorescence staining (Figure 4G). In the CS sponge-treated group, abundant green fluorescence signals (live bacteria) were observed, displaying dense, structurally intact biofilms with multi-layered live bacteria. Treatment with CS-ICG resulted in strong red fluorescence, signifying widespread bacterial death, but the biofilm structure was found compact. In contrast, for the MRSA biofilm treated with CS-AuNCs/ICG+light, green fluorescence was scarcely detected, while red signals predominated, demonstrating the exceptional efficacy of CS-AuNCs/ICG in eradicating bacterial cells and ablating the biofilm under irradiation.

Fluorescence intensity and corresponding flow cytometry analysis (Figure 4H) indicated continuous ROS generation in planktonic MRSA upon treatment with CS-AuNCs/ICG under irradiation. The ROS produced could induce the oxidation of the lipid membrane in bacterial cell wall, acting as a key mechanism for antibacterial actions. A similar result was observed in MRSA biofilms, where CS-AuNCs/ICG generated the highest level of ROS (Figure 4I).

In vivo Antibacterial and Wound Healing Assay

To further assess the translational potential of CS-AuNCs/ICG sponges, their antibacterial efficacy and wound healing performance were evaluated using an MRSA-infected skin abscess model. The experimental procedure is depicted in Figure 5A and B displays thermal images of the sponges under 808 nm NIR irradiation, while Figure 5C illustrates the temperature change over time, corresponding to the stages depicted. This data aligns with the conclusion drawn from in vitro experiments. The CS-AuNCs/ICG sponge temperature gradually rose to ~55 °C within 5 min under NIR irradiation.

To quantitatively evaluate the treatment efficacy, wound exudate was collected on Day 2 post-treatment, and the bacterial load of MRSA was quantified via colony-forming unit (CFU) counts. As demonstrated in Figure 5D and E, the CS-AuNCs/ICG+light group exhibited a substantially lower bacteria burden than the other groups, indicating the enhanced antibacterial effect of CS-AuNCs/ICG under light irradiation. Moreover, the progress of wound healing was photographed and documented (Figure 5F). Mice treated with CS-AuNCs/ICG+light exhibited significantly faster wound closure compared with those in the other groups. A noticeable difference in wound size between the CS-AuNCs/ICG+light group and the others emerged from Day 2 post-treatment, suggesting exceptional antibacterial properties of CS-AuNCs/ICG when combined with light irradiation. By Day 14, wounds treated with CS-AuNCs/ICG+light had achieved almost 100% recovery, with the wound surface smoothing out and new epidermal tissue forming. In contrast, 58% of the wounds in the saline group remained unhealed, presenting irregular scar formation (Figure 5G). These results suggest that the CS-AuNCs/ICG+light sponge promotes both wound healing and skin regeneration. The systemic impact of infection and wound burden greatly affected the physical condition of the mice, which was reflected in mouse body weight changes. Collectively, all the findings confirm that the treatment with CS-AuNCs/ICG+light yielded the most effective wound healing outcomes among all the experimental groups (Figure 5H).

Finally, pathological histological analysis was conducted, as illustrated in Figure 5I. H&E staining revealed the presence of numerous inflammatory cells and epidermal damage in all groups except the CS-AuNCs/ICG+light group. The exceptional physicochemical properties and strong antibacterial capability of the sponge effectively protected the wound against further damage and secondary bacterial infections which often impede the healing process. The ability of CS-AuNCs/ICG to instantly inactivate bacteria upon light irradiation, combined with the sustained antibacterial effects conferred by AuNCs, enables continuous protection against bacterial infection. Consequently, due to reduced bacterial infection, the CS-AuNCs/ICG+light group exhibited a much milder inflammatory response than the other groups. Moreover, the alleviated inflammatory response indicated that the effects exerted by the sponge may facilitate extracellular matrix reorganization and promote tissue regeneration, thereby accelerating wound healing.

Conclusions

In this study, we successfully developed a photodynamic/photothermal gold nanocluster chitosan sponge (CS-AuNCs/ICG) for hemostasis and healing of MRSA-infected wounds. The CS-AuNCs/ICG sponge features a porous surface structure with high porosity (over 80%), which facilitates wound exudate absorption. Besides, the sponge ensures a moist microenvironment for wounds conducive to healing, due to its favorable swelling capacity and moisture retention properties. In vitro antibacterial tests demonstrated that the CS-AuNCs/ICG sponge under light irradiation exhibits excellent bactericidal activity against both planktonic bacteria and biofilm and and sustains a prolonged inhibitory effect on bacterial growth. The CS-AuNCs/ICG sponge also exhibited satisfactory in vivo antibacterial activity, accelerating wound closure in infected mice. This efficacy is likely attributed to the synergistic therapeutic effects of gold clusters’ sustained bacterial inhibition effects and ROS’s rapid antibacterial action. The continuous increase in the body weight of the mice suggested that CS-AuNCs/ICG was well tolerated. In short, CS-AuNCs/ICG exhibited efficient antibacterial activity against MRSA both in vitro and in vivo, providing a promising alternative for next-generation wound dressings. Future work will investigate the efficacy against other common wound pathogens and assess the safety and efficacy in a large animal model of infected wounds. In view of the limited light penetration depth of ICG, the high preparation cost of AuNCs, and the difficulty in achieving industrial-scale preparation of CS sponges, these factors collectively constitute the core bottlenecks for their clinical translation. Relevant optimization research will be carried out as a key research direction in subsequent experiments.
