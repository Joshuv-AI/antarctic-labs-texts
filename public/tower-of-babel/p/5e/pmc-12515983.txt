Longitudinal trajectories of major depressive disorder provide further clinical perspectives for precision psychiatry
Authors: Murat İlhan Atagün
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is a common and debilitating disorder. MDD was considered to be a relatively benign disorder compared to other major psychiatric disorders. However, longitudinal research revealed heterogeneous and frequently chronic course. Episodic or chronic progress reframes MDD as a disorder with a variable and enduring course. The recognition of MDD's heterogeneity has prompted a methodological evolution in psychiatric research, moving away from group‐level averages toward more sophisticated, person‐centered analytical techniques. In addition to inherent heterogeneity, biological, psychosocial, and environmental factors are also influential on the clinical manifestations of MDD. The course of major depressive disorder is not only heterogeneous among individuals but also varies systematically across different populations. Multiple models have consistently identified several distinct trajectory classes across diverse populations and study designs, providing a more nuanced map of how MDD unfolds over a lifetime. The concept of MDD subtyping within precision psychiatry directly addresses its inherent heterogeneity. If different subtypes of MDD possess distinct neurobiological underpinnings, then identifying these specific subtypes through biomarkers may lead to highly targeted and significantly more effective treatments. Longitudinal perspectives are indispensable for an accurate understanding of prognosis, the development of preventive strategies, and the implementation of effective long‐term management plans that can alter the illness's trajectory. It was aimed to review the MDD trajectories, treatments, and symptom clusters from population and life span‐based perspectives in this paper.

INTRODUCTION

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a common and profoundly debilitating mental health condition, recognized globally as a leading cause of disability. 1 It is clinically defined by a constellation of symptoms that represent a significant change from an individual's previous functioning, including a persistent depressed mood or a marked loss of interest or pleasure (anhedonia). 2 While the clinical presentation validates a diagnosis based on a set of criteria, cross‐sectional diagnosis provides only a snapshot of a dynamic and often lifelong illness. Historically, MDD was viewed as a self‐limiting, episodic condition with a relatively favorable course characterized by full recovery between episodes. 3 , 4 However, this perspective has been fundamentally challenged by decades of longitudinal research, which reveals a far more heterogeneous and frequently chronic reality. The contemporary understanding of MDD is that of a highly recurrent disorder. 4 Seminal long‐term studies have shown that a significant proportion of the individuals who experience a first major depressive episode (MDE) will go on to have episodes (approximately 70%), 5 or even chronic MDD (15% unremitting, 35% recurrent). 6 This high rate of recurrence fundamentally reframes MDD not as a series of isolated events, but also as a condition with enduring, variable courses. 5 A longitudinal perspective is therefore indispensable for accurate prognosis, the development of preventive strategies, and the implementation of effective long‐term management plans that potentially alter the illness's trajectory. 4

Heterogeneity of MDD is a major challenge in terms of treatment planning and response monitoring. Longitudinal research shed light over the heterogeneous progress of depressive disorders. A large Danish cohort consisting of 11,640 individuals determined distinct trajectory classes in 10‐year trajectories for moderate‐to‐severe MDD. 7 Accordingly, most of the patients were classified in the “brief contact” group (64%–77%), which have a low probability of further psychiatric contact after 2 years. In total, 12%–13% proportion was classified as the “prolonged initial contact” group, which had high but decreasing contact over the 5 years. In total, 7%–42% have a moderate probability of “later reentry” in later years. In total, 3%–18% maintain high or moderate contact throughout the decade, which was named as “persistent contact.” Severity, course, and within‐person variability differ notably in MDD. In addition, women gender, greater initial severity, hospitalization, early age of onset, older adults, comorbid conditions (history of emotional abuse, trauma, and anxiety), and parental psychiatric history are the apparent risk factors linked to more severe or persistent trajectories. To define the terms about distinct prognostic indicators, the following terms, established through extensive research, define the key phases and transitions of the illness over time. 8 −Remission: This phase is the marker of the initial resolution of an episode. It is defined as a period of at least three weeks where the individual experiences an absence of both sad mood and anhedonia, with less than three remaining symptoms. 8 Individuals achieve symptomatic remission but continue to experience significant functional impairment and a burden of residual symptoms. 9 −Recovery: This term denotes a more sustained period of wellness. It is defined as the state of sustained remission, typically lasting from two to four months or longer, during which the MDD episode is considered to have ended. 8 −Relapse: Refers to the return of a full MDE after a period of remission but before full recovery has been achieved. The risk of relapse is highest in the immediate aftermath of an episode, particularly within the first 16 weeks of symptom improvement.−Recurrence: This term signifies the onset of a new, distinct MDE that occurs after a period of full recovery. The probability of recurrence increases with each episode.−Chronicity: This term describes a course characterized by the persistence of depressive symptoms (DS) over long periods. It can manifest in several ways, including a continuous MDE that lasts for 2 years or longer (termed chronic MDD) or a pattern of persistent, subthreshold DS, which is the hallmark of persistent depressive disorder.

The distinction between remission and complete recovery reveals a critical challenge in MDD treatment. The persistence of residual symptoms even after a patient is considered “in remission” is not a trivial matter; it is one of the most powerful predictors of subsequent relapse and the development of a chronic course. 9 This observation suggests that clinical definitions of remission, often based on simple symptom counts or rating scale cutoffs, may not capture a state of genuine wellness or resilience. A treatment approach focused solely on achieving this minimal definition of remission may inadvertently leave patients in a state of high vulnerability. Therefore, to truly alter a patient's long‐term trajectory, clinical goals must extend beyond mere symptom reduction to encompass the elimination of all residual symptoms and the restoration of full psychosocial and occupational functioning. A significant discrepancy often exists between symptomatic remission and functional recovery. Functional recovery, which encompasses an individual's ability to resume daily activities, engage in social roles, and maintain occupational functioning, frequently lags behind the resolution of medical symptoms, particularly during the initial episode. 10 Patients often report considerable struggles with the abrupt transition from hospital to home, difficulties in reengaging with daily routines, and profound experiences of loneliness and social isolation after discharge. Barriers to full functional engagement include a perceived lack of support, reduced self‐efficacy, diminished self‐worth, and ineffective coping responses. The observation that functional recovery often lags behind symptomatic remission represents a critical challenge in the treatment of MDD. Therefore, an individual may appear “well” on standardized symptom scales, yet may still struggle significantly with the practicalities of daily life, social participation, and occupational roles. This highlights a potential limitation in current measures of “recovery,” which may focus too narrowly on symptom reduction rather than a more holistic restoration of an individual's quality of life and functional capacity. This situation underscores the urgent need for broader outcome measures in both research and clinical practice that encompass quality of life, social engagement, and occupational functioning to ensure truly comprehensive and meaningful recovery for patients (Figure 1).

MAPPING THE PATHWAYS: IDENTIFIED SYMPTOM TRAJECTORIES

The recognition of MDD's heterogeneity has prompted a methodological evolution in psychiatric research, moving away from group‐level averages toward more sophisticated, person‐centered analytical techniques. Methods such as group‐based trajectory modeling (GBTM) and latent class analysis (LCA) allow researchers to analyze longitudinal data to identify distinct subgroups of individuals who share similar developmental patterns or illness courses over extended periods. 11 , 12 These data‐driven approaches have been instrumental in revealing that the course of MDD is not a simple binary of “episodic” versus “chronic” but rather a spectrum of distinct, empirically derived pathways. 12

The statistical models have consistently identified several distinct trajectory classes across diverse populations and study designs, providing a more nuanced map of how MDD unfolds over a lifetime. Population‐based trajectories in women: A landmark prospective cohort study, the Korean Longitudinal Survey of Women and Families, followed 7691 women over a 6‐year period and identified five distinct trajectories of DS. 12 The largest group, “persistent low symptoms” (68.1%), represented a resilient majority who maintained low symptom levels. Three other groups showed dynamic patterns: a “decreasing symptoms” group (12.1%) whose symptoms improved over time; a “transient high symptoms” group (5.5%) with an episodic course; and a highly concerning “increasing symptoms” group (10.7%) whose symptoms worsened over the study period. A final, small group exhibited “persistent high symptoms” (3.6%), representing a chronically ill population strongly associated with low socioeconomic status, physical comorbidity, and being divorced or widowed.Developmental trajectories in adolescence: Longitudinal research in adolescents reveals similarly heterogeneous patterns that capture the unique challenges of this developmental period. A 7‐year study following 243 adolescents from age 12 to 18 identified four primary trajectory groups. 13 , 14 These included a “stable low” group, a group with “high initial symptoms and a steep decrease” (representing acute but resolving MDD), a group with “moderate initial symptoms that decreased,” and a critical group characterized by “initially low symptoms that increased over time.” This last group exemplifies the emergence of MDD during the adolescent transition and represents a key target for early intervention.Treatment‐response trajectories: The heterogeneity of MDD is also evident in how individuals respond to treatment. Studies analyzing symptom changes during interventions have identified multiple response pathways. One study of patients undergoing antidepressant treatment found six distinct trajectories for both MDD and anxiety, with three classes showing varying degrees of improvement and three classes showing nonresponse. Similarly, research on patients receiving high‐intensity psychological therapy identified four classes: a “fast improvement” group, a “gradual improvement” group, a group with “mild symptoms and minimal improvement,” and a nonresponsive “moderate‐severe plateau” group. These findings demonstrate that even within a treated population, the course of recovery is highly variable.Long‐term prognostic trajectories: Very long‐term follow‐up studies of individuals with severe MDD paint a sobering picture of the most chronic pathways. A 25‐year longitudinal study found that only a small fraction of patients (12%) recovered and remained continuously well. 12 The vast majority (84%) experienced recurrent episodes, with an average of three episodes over the 25‐year period. 15 A small but significant portion had an unremitting course (2%) or died by suicide (2%). These findings underscore the profound and lasting burden associated with the most severe depressive trajectories.

The identification of an “increasing symptoms” trajectory in both adult and adolescent populations is particularly significant. This pattern does not represent a classic MDE but rather a gradual deterioration in mental health over time. From a clinical and public health perspective, this trajectory represents a critical window of opportunity. It reframes the focus from reactively treating established episodes to proactively preventing the full manifestation of the illness. Identifying the clinical, behavioral, or biological markers that signal an individual is on this downward path, before they meet the full criteria for a MDE, could enable preemptive interventions designed to alter their course and avert years of potential disability. This shifts the clinical paradigm from treatment to true prevention.

Furthermore, the discovery of multiple non‐improving trajectories in treatment studies—such as the “persistent high” class and the “moderate‐severe plateau” 16 —challenges the simple notion of “treatment resistance (TR)” as a single entity. It suggests that the reasons for treatment failure are themselves heterogeneous. One patient on a “persistent high” trajectory may have a biological resistance to a specific class of medication, while another on a “plateau” trajectory might be contending with insurmountable psychosocial stressors that blunt the effect of any intervention. This implies that a more nuanced approach to TR MDD is needed, one that seeks to characterize the type of nonresponse trajectory to better guide subsequent treatment decisions, such as whether to switch pharmacotherapy, augment with a different agent, or add intensive psychosocial support. I. Clinical predictors of course and chronicity The remarkable heterogeneity in the course of MDD is not random. A vast body of research has identified a range of powerful predictors that help explain why some individuals recover and remain well while others follow a path of chronicity and recurrence. These determinants span clinical, psychosocial, and biological domains, and their interplay shapes an individual's unique illness trajectory. The characteristics of the MDD itself, particularly during its initial presentation, are among the most robust predictors of its long‐term course. Severity of illness, age at onset, history of prior episodes, comorbidities, and residual symptoms are the established clinical risk factors for recurrence and poor outcomes. 4 , 9 , 17 The clinical predictors—early onset, high severity, and multiple prior episodes—are not merely independent risk factors. They can be understood through a unifying neurobiological framework known as the “kindling” or “stress sensitization” hypothesis. 18 This model proposes that initial MDEs, often triggered by severe life stress, induce lasting neurobiological changes. These changes may progressively lower the threshold required to trigger subsequent episodes. As a result, later recurrences may become more autonomous, developing in response to progressively milder stressors or even spontaneously. 9 This theory provides a compelling explanation for why these clinical features are so predictive: they are markers of an underlying progressive sensitization process. An early, severe episode inflicts a greater neurobiological insult, and each subsequent recurrence further entrenches this vulnerability. This framework highlights the profound importance of preventing early episodes and achieving full, sustained recovery from the very first episode to halt this potentially progressive disease process.II. Psychosocial and environmental predictors The environment in which an individual lives and the psychological resources they possess are powerful determinants of their depressive trajectory. These factors can act as triggers, maintainers, or moderators of the illness course. Childhood trauma, life stress, social support, interpersonal relationships, socioeconomic status, personality, and cognitive style are the established risk factors. 4 It is crucial to highlight that these psychosocial factors are not merely triggers for a predetermined biological illness. They are active participants in a dynamic, bidirectional process. The traditional diathesis‐stress model, which posits that stress activates a latent genetic vulnerability, is an important but incomplete picture. Evidence now clearly shows that psychosocial factors can mediate and moderate biological risk, and can even induce biological changes themselves. For instance, severe early life stress does not just “flip a switch” on an MDD gene; it can actively alter the developmental trajectory of the brain, leading to lasting structural changes and dysregulation of the HPA axis and inflammatory systems. This process can create a biological vulnerability where one may not have existed before. Conversely, protective psychosocial factors like strong social support can buffer the impact of high biological risk, moderating the course of illness. 19 This integrated perspective reframes psychosocial factors from simple triggers to active sculptors of the neurobiological landscape upon which MDD develops and unfolds.III.Biological and genetic architecture of MDD trajectoriesThe course of MDD is profoundly influenced by an individual's biological and genetic makeup. While no single gene or biomarker can fully explain the disorder, research has identified a complex web of genetic predispositions, neurobiological abnormalities, and physiological dysregulations that contribute to vulnerability and shape the illness trajectory. Heritability estimates, which quantify the proportion of risk attributable to genetic factors, consistently demonstrated increased risk of developing MDD among individuals. 20 The genetic risk for MDD is polygenic, meaning it arises from the combined, cumulative effect of many different genes, each contributing a small amount to the overall vulnerability. 21 This complexity explains why there is no single “depression gene” and why inheriting a predisposition is not deterministic. While genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) continue to identify new risk loci, several key genes and pathways have been consistently implicated in the pathophysiology of MDD. Gene environment interaction is the latest up‐to‐date model that explains heterogeneity. The literature is focused on serotonergic and other neurotransmitter systems, neuroplasticity, neurotrophic and neuroendocrine factors, and other regulatory systems, many of which converge on the synapse. 22

Biological and genetic architecture of MDD trajectories

The course of MDD is profoundly influenced by an individual's biological and genetic makeup. While no single gene or biomarker can fully explain the disorder, research has identified a complex web of genetic predispositions, neurobiological abnormalities, and physiological dysregulations that contribute to vulnerability and shape the illness trajectory. Heritability estimates, which quantify the proportion of risk attributable to genetic factors, consistently demonstrated increased risk of developing MDD among individuals. 20 The genetic risk for MDD is polygenic, meaning it arises from the combined, cumulative effect of many different genes, each contributing a small amount to the overall vulnerability. 21 This complexity explains why there is no single “depression gene” and why inheriting a predisposition is not deterministic. While genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) continue to identify new risk loci, several key genes and pathways have been consistently implicated in the pathophysiology of MDD. Gene environment interaction is the latest up‐to‐date model that explains heterogeneity. The literature is focused on serotonergic and other neurotransmitter systems, neuroplasticity, neurotrophic and neuroendocrine factors, and other regulatory systems, many of which converge on the synapse. 22

While neuroimaging literature is still in its early stages, 23 cross‐sectional studies have identified consistent abnormalities in MDD. These include increased white matter hyperintensities in subcortical regions and reduced metabolic activity in the left anterior brain. Advanced data‐driven models are beginning to leverage neuroimaging data to map the trajectories of these brain changes over time. For example, the SuStaIn model can infer distinct pathways of brain functional abnormality progression, particularly in circuits like the Default Mode Network (DMN) and cortico‐subcortical loops, providing a potential biological signature for different illness courses. 24 The link between MDD and inflammation is increasingly clear. Elevated levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines are associated with MDD and can predict a poorer response to conventional antidepressant treatments. 19 Research has specifically linked the regulation of inflammatory mediators through transient receptor potential channels to the volatility of antidepressant response, suggesting that inflammatory pathways may drive the fluctuating, unstable treatment trajectories observed in some patients. 19 Genetic factors are not only involved in the initial risk for MDD but are also strongly implicated in the vulnerability to a recurrent course. 17 This suggests that there may be specific genetic profiles associated with more chronic or relapsing trajectories. This interaction powerfully predicted the risk of future recurrence, indicating that genetic factors can moderate the long‐term impact of clinical events. 17

The search for the genetic underpinnings of MDD is evolving. The moderate heritability of MDD as a broad category, combined with its profound clinical heterogeneity, 12 suggests that the genetic architecture may differ according to the specific trajectory of the illness. 25 Evidence is accumulating that heritability may be higher for more severe and recurrent forms of MDD. Furthermore, specific genetic pathways are now being linked not just to the disorder itself, but to particular treatment‐response trajectories, such as neurotrophin signaling being associated with the efficiency of response and inflammatory pathways with the volatility of response. 19 This finding points toward a new research paradigm. Instead of conducting a single GWAS for “MDD,” the future lies in genetically stratifying patient populations by their empirically derived longitudinal trajectory. The “persistent high” trajectory may be driven by a different set of polygenic risks than the “increasing” or “transient” trajectories. This approach could help explain the modest results of many past genetic studies, which may have been diluted by averaging across biologically distinct subtypes. The clinical implications of this are immense, as genetic screening could one day be used not just to predict a general risk for MDD, but to forecast the most likely course of the illness, thereby enabling more precise and proactive interventions. HETEROGENEITY IN MDD TRAJECTORIES: SUBPOPULATIONS

The course of MDD is not only heterogeneous among individuals but also varies systematically across different populations. Developmental stage and gender are two of the most critical factors that shape the presentation, etiology, and long‐term trajectory of the illness. I.Developmental perspective: adolescents and adults

I.Developmental perspective: adolescents and adults

Developmental perspective: adolescents and adults

While the same core diagnostic criteria for MDD are applied across the lifespan, a growing body of evidence demonstrates that adolescent‐onset MDD is a clinically and biologically distinct entity from adult‐onset MDD (Table 3). This distinction has profound implications for assessment, treatment, and prevention. The divergence in symptom profiles between adolescents (vegetative) and adults (cognitive/anhedonic) is more than a superficial difference; it strongly suggests distinct underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. The prominence of vegetative symptoms—disturbances in sleep, appetite, and energy—in adolescents may point to a primary dysregulation in fundamental homeostatic systems, such as the HPA axis and inflammatory pathways, that are being disrupted during a critical window of development. 21 In contrast, the predominance of anhedonia and cognitive deficits in adults may reflect a more entrenched dysregulation in higher order cortical circuits, such as prefrontal–striatal reward pathways, that have been progressively altered by a longer history of recurrent episodes or chronic stress. This hypothesis helps to explain not only the different symptom presentations but also the observed differences in TR, challenging the view that adolescent MDD is simply an earlier version of the adult disorder.

Furthermore, the identification of childhood irritability as a key mediator between early neurodevelopmental problems and later MDD offers a powerful insight for prevention. 30 Rather than waiting for a full MDE to emerge in a child with ADHD or communication difficulties, clinical efforts could be directed toward targeting the underlying affective dysregulation and irritability early on. This would represent a shift from a reactive treatment model to a proactive, developmentally informed prevention strategy aimed at altering a high‐risk trajectory before it culminates in MDD. II.Gender differences

II.Gender differences

Gender differences

One of the most robust and consistent findings in psychiatric epidemiology is the marked gender disparity in MDD. Beginning in adolescence, women are diagnosed with MDD at approximately twice the rate of men, a difference that persists across the lifespan and is independent of race or ethnicity. 35 This disparity is not limited to prevalence; longitudinal studies reveal that women also tend to experience a more severe and chronic illness course, characterized by a higher incidence of new episodes, a greater number of lifetime episodes, and a tendency for episodes to be of longer duration. 36 This profound difference is believed to stem from a complex interaction between biological vulnerabilities, particularly hormonal fluctuations, and a range of sociocultural and psychosocial factors. Hormonal influences across the female lifespan: A central biological hypothesis for the gender disparity focuses on the powerful influence of female sex hormones, such as estrogen and progesterone. These hormones are not merely reproductive; they are potent neuromodulators that interact with key neurotransmitter systems implicated in mood (serotonin, dopamine, gamma aminobutyric acid) and play a critical role in brain plasticity. 35 The female lifespan is punctuated by periods of significant hormonal transition, each of which represents a window of heightened vulnerability to MDD. oPuberty: The dramatic increase in MDD rates among girls, and the emergence of the 2:1 gender ratio, coincides with the onset of puberty and its associated hormonal surges. 35 , 36 , 37 oMenstrual cycle: For a subset of women, the cyclical fluctuations of hormones across the menstrual cycle are directly linked to severe mood symptoms, culminating in the diagnosis of premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD). Furthermore, a majority of women with preexisting mood disorders (up to 60%) report a significant premenstrual exacerbation of their DS. 35 oPregnancy and the postpartum period: The perinatal period, with its massive hormonal shifts during pregnancy and precipitous drop after childbirth, is another well‐established period of high risk. Factors such as the stress of caring for a newborn and lack of social support interact with this biological vulnerability to trigger postpartum depression. 37 oPerimenopause and menopause: The transition to menopause (perimenopause), characterized by erratic hormonal fluctuations, and the postmenopausal period, defined by significantly reduced estrogen levels, constitute a final window of increased MDD risk for women. 9 , 38 Sociocultural and psychosocial factors: Sociocultural norms, gender roles, and differential exposure to life stressors are critical contributors to the higher rates and more chronic course of MDD in women. oGender roles and socialization: Traditional gender role socialization may encourage women to adopt a more emotion‐focused, ruminative coping style (mulling over problems), which is known to maintain MDD, while men may be socialized toward a more problem‐focused, distracting style. 39 Women often face the pressure of “work overload” or the “second shift,” balancing careers with primary responsibility for home and family care, which is a source of chronic stress. 40 Societal devaluation of traditional female roles or, conversely, discrimination against women who may also negatively impact mental health. 39 oDifferential exposure to stress and trauma: Women are disproportionately exposed to certain types of severe trauma, such as childhood sexual abuse and interpersonal violence, which are among the most potent risk factors for developing chronic and severe depression. 36 oStress response: Beyond exposure, there may be sex differences in stress reactivity. Some evidence suggests that females have a heightened subjective experience of stress and may exhibit greater sensitivity of the HPA axis, the body's central stress response system, which is modulated by sex hormones. 35 Differences in symptom presentation and biology: The experience of MDD may manifest differently in women. Women are more likely to present with “atypical” depressive features, such as hypersomnia (oversleeping), hyperphagia (increased appetite), and weight gain. 12 Women with MDD also tend to report higher rates of comorbid anxiety disorders and somatic symptoms. 41 There is also evidence of greater sexual dysfunction in depressed women compared to men. 42 These clinical differences are likely mirrored by underlying biological distinctions, with neuroimaging studies beginning to uncover sex‐specific patterns of brain function abnormalities in MDD. 41

The pronounced gender disparity in MDD is best understood not as a result of either biology or society alone, but as a product of their continuous and dynamic interaction. A plausible integrated model suggests that the cyclical and transitional fluctuations in female hormones may sensitize the female brain's stress‐response systems, particularly the HPA axis. 35 This creates a state of heightened biological vulnerability. This heightened sensitivity then interacts with a higher average exposure to specific and potent psychosocial stressors that are more common in women's lives, such as interpersonal trauma, role strain, and economic inequality. 13 It is this combination of a biologically sensitized system and a higher burden of specific environmental risks that likely drives the higher prevalence, greater severity, and more chronic course of MDD observed in women. III.The impact of interventions on long‐term course

III.The impact of interventions on long‐term course

The impact of interventions on long‐term course

Understanding the natural history of MDD is critical, but the illness trajectory is rarely “natural.” It is almost always shaped by interventions, primarily pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy. The long‐term impact of these treatments is a subject of intense research and considerable debate, with evidence supporting both profound benefits and potential iatrogenic harms that can fundamentally alter the course of the disorder. −Pharmacotherapy

−Pharmacotherapy

Pharmacotherapy

Antidepressant medications are a cornerstone of modern MDD treatment, and their ability to alter the illness trajectory, particularly in the prevention of relapse, is well‐documented. However, a growing body of evidence and clinical observation raises significant questions about their long‐term consequences, suggesting a more complex and sometimes paradoxical role. Efficacy in relapse and recurrence prevention: The most significant and well‐supported benefit of long‐term antidepressant use is the prevention of relapse and recurrence. Continuing an effective antidepressant medication after recovery from an acute episode dramatically reduces the risk of relapse. 43 , 44 This protective effect appears to be most pronounced in patients with a single prior episode but remains significant, albeit slightly less robust, for those with a history of recurrent illness. 45 For many individuals, particularly those with severe and recurrent MDD, sustained pharmacotherapy is a critical tool for maintaining wellness and preventing the devastating impact of repeated episodes. 45 The oppositional model of tolerance and iatrogenic harm: Juxtaposed with the evidence of benefit is a compelling theoretical framework and a body of clinical evidence suggesting that long‐term antidepressant use can, in a subset of vulnerable individuals, paradoxically worsen the course of MDD. This concept is formalized in the “oppositional model of tolerance.” 46 oThe mechanism: The model proposes that the central nervous system strives to maintain homeostatic equilibrium. When an antidepressant chronically perturbs a neurotransmitter system (e.g., an SSRI persistently increasing synaptic serotonin), the brain initiates compensatory, or “oppositional,” processes to counteract this effect. These can include downregulating postsynaptic receptors, reducing the synthesis of the neurotransmitter, or other adaptive changes. 46 oClinical consequences: Over time, these oppositional processes can manifest in several ways. They may lead to a gradual loss of the medication's effectiveness, a phenomenon known as tachyphylaxis or “poop out,” where a previously effective treatment stops working. 47 More critically, if the antidepressant is discontinued, these now‐entrenched oppositional processes are left unopposed, leading to a state of neurotransmitter deficit that can be more severe than the original condition. The decision to initiate or continue long‐term antidepressant therapy is therefore not straightforward. It involves navigating a fundamental tension between robust evidence for short‐to‐medium‐term relapse prevention and a growing body of evidence pointing to the potential for significant long‐term iatrogenic harm. This reframes the clinical decision from a simple question of efficacy to a complex, individualized risk‐benefit analysis over the patient's entire life course. According to the symptom profile, adjunctive medications including antipsychotis, mood stabilizers, ketamine and modafinil are available in cases of TR. 51 A clinician can prescribe a medication that is highly likely to prevent a relapse in the next 1–2 years, a clear and important benefit. However, in doing so, they may be setting a subset of patients on a path toward a more chronic, treatment‐resistant course, or creating a state of physiological dependence that makes future discontinuation exceedingly difficult. The concept of “enduring effects” provides a powerful framework for understanding the unique value of psychotherapy in the long‐term management of MDD. The sustained benefits of therapies like CBT and psychodynamic therapy, which persist after treatment termination, suggest that these interventions do more than simply suppress DS; they appear to alter the underlying psychological mechanisms of the disorder. By explicitly targeting and modifying dysfunctional thought patterns, maladaptive behaviors, and problematic relational styles, psychotherapy equips the patient with a new set of skills and deeper self‐understanding. 53 , 62 This process can be conceptualized as building internal resilience and “rewiring” the psychological processes that generate and maintain MDD. Pharmacotherapy, in contrast, can be viewed as an external regulator of neurochemistry; its benefits are powerful but often dependent on its continued presence. This distinction provides a strong rationale for prioritizing psychotherapy for patients seeking long‐term, self‐sustaining recovery and for whom preventing future episodes is a primary goal. The finding that initial combination treatment might undermine this enduring effect is a critical area for future research, perhaps suggesting that patients may attribute their improvement to the medication rather than their own therapeutic work, thus failing to fully internalize and consolidate the skills learned in therapy.

CONCLUSION

Despite robust clinical heterogeneity, observations of the longitudinal course defined the clinical progress in MDD. Since heterogeneity of MDD confuses medical decisions as well as research, longitudinal clinical progress may add value to the further determination of MDD. Improving outcomes in MDD treatments could only be available after drawing objective conclusions from longitudinal research. The field of “precision psychiatry” offers a promising paradigm shift, aiming to move beyond the current “one‐size‐fits‐all, trial‐and‐error approach” to MDD treatment. This approach seeks to identify and leverage individual differences in biology, lifestyle, environment, and social determinants of health to optimize prevention, diagnosis, and treatment. A core objective of precision psychiatry is to develop robust, generalizable, and clinically valuable predictive models for MDD, with a particular focus on severe and treatment‐resistant forms of the disorder. Key areas of investigation include MDD subtyping, which involves classifying MDD into distinct, biologically informed subtypes to gain a deeper understanding of their underlying mechanisms. This subtyping can then inform the optimization of treatments for specific patient subgroups, with the ultimate goal of delivering “the right treatment to the right patient at the right time.” The concept of “MDD subtyping” within precision psychiatry directly addresses the inherent heterogeneity of MDD. If different subtypes of MDD possess distinct neurobiological underpinnings—for example, an inflammatory subtype versus an anhedonic subtype—then identifying these specific subtypes through the use of biomarkers can lead to highly targeted and significantly more effective treatments. This approach has the potential to dramatically reduce the current reliance on a trial‐and‐error method, representing a fundamental paradigm shift from treating a broad, undifferentiated disorder to treating specific biological profiles, thereby enhancing treatment efficacy and patient outcomes. Determination of biomarkers should be evaluated and improved in the longitudinal course of MDD.
