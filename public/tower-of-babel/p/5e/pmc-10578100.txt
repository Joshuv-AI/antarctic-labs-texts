A pronatalist turn in population policies in Iran and its likely adverse impacts on reproductive rights, health and inequality: a critical narrative review
Authors: Khadijeh Asadisarvestani, Tomáš Sobotka
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIran has witnessed three major reversals of population policies since their inception in the 1960s. In response to a rapid decline in fertility to very low levels, the latest policy shift has led to the development of legislation that aims to encourage marriage and fertility, particularly the “Youthful Population and Protection of the Family” law approved in 2021. This study reviews the changes in population policy and their interrelations with fertility trends, focusing mainly on the shift towards pronatalist policies since 2005, and accompanying restriction of reproductive health and family planning services. Combining international and national sources, we position the new pronatalist drive in the country within the broader trend of government attempts to reverse fertility decline and promote conservative family values. Our study has three main aims. (1) We provide an overview of fertility trends, policy discourses and policy shifts in the context of the changes in the societal and political structures of Iran during the last half a century. (2) We highlight and discuss the most problematic features of the new Family Law, especially the legislation pertaining to maternal and reproductive health, access to abortion and contraception, and incentives supporting earlier marriage and higher fertility. (3) We discuss the likely consequences of the new legislation for maternal and child health and sexual and reproductive rights, for women in general, and the country’s socio-economic disparities. As well as violating reproductive rights, the new policy is unlikely to achieve its aim of initiating a sustained rise in fertility in Iran.Keywords: population policy, family policy, fertility, family planning, health, Iran, contraception, pronatalism, reproductive rights, reproductive health

Introduction

Fertility rates in many middle-income countries including Bangladesh, Brazil, China, Iran, Thailand, Turkey and Uruguay have recently dropped to low or very low levels, often at a pace that was unforeseen in past population projections. Today, two thirds of the global population live in countries and areas with total fertility rates at or below 2.1, which is taken as a rough indicator of the long-term population replacement level in the absence of migration.1 Such rapid declines in fertility have amplified concerns about future population ageing and, eventually, population decline and have led to the reorientation of population policy debates and priorities.2

Governments and policy makers in these countries are increasingly adjusting their family planning (FP) and fertility policies so as to incorporate a pronatalist* agenda that aims to encourage more births and higher fertility rates, and they have frequently set numerical targets for the desired future fertility level and population size.2,3 Such policies often provide stronger support for parents and families in the form of child allowances and other financial benefits, childcare and parental leave provisions, and other family-friendly policies that are commonly provided by many higher-income countries.4,5 However, fears of an impending “population crisis” often result in policies that limit access to contraception and abortion, stigmatise or marginalise specific population groups, including unmarried and childless people, and promote a conservative family model that is detrimental to women’s autonomy and gender equality.2,6 Such coercive pronatalism approaches act to limit the physical autonomy of women and place their bodies and wombs in the “service” of government population policy targets.7 Such policies violate the principles of reproductive rights and freedoms as enshrined in the 1994 International Conference on Population and Development (ICPD) Programme of Action8 and the more recent Agenda for Sustainable Development.2

The new low-fertility landscape, fears of future population declines, and sudden policy changes that threaten previous progress on reinforcing reproductive rights, are intertwined in the Islamic Republic of Iran, a country that has recently experienced one of the world’s most rapid fertility declines.9,10 By the start of the twenty-first century, the total fertility rate in Iran had fallen below the replacement level,11,12 later dropping to a low level of around 1.7 births per woman in 2021.11 Low fertility levels over the last two decades have reinvigorated the population policy discourse in the country and, ultimately, motivated the recent shift towards pronatalist policies. Although this process officially began in 2012, discussions on policy reorientation commenced as early as in 2005 when the new government - at the time headed by populist and religiously conservative president Mahmoud Ahmadinejad - came to power. This policy reorientation resulted in the formulation of “General Population Policies” that were announced by the Supreme Leader, Ayatollah Khamenei, in 2014 and later in the “Youthful Population and Protection of the Family”† law as approved in 2021 (hereafter also referred to as the “2021 Family Law” or “Family Law”), which signalled a definitive break with previous family planning support policies which were broadly in line with ICPD principles. The new family legislation was criticised for its potential adverse consequences for reproductive rights and health, as well as the restriction on the autonomy of women and gender equality.13–15 The new law was also condemned by the UN human rights body for “violating women’s rights under international law”.16 Despite the international condemnation, however, the problematic features and likely consequences of this legislation have not, to date, been discussed in detail by researchers and policy experts outside Iran. (A 2023 study by Aramesh,17 which critically assesses the new law from the perspective of bioethics, is one of the few exceptions.)

Our study highlights the various shifts in Iranian fertility and family policies that have taken place over the last few decades and analyses the development of new pronatalist policies and the key related discourses in the context of the complex political and policy-making systems in the country. Combining international and national sources and documents, the study has three main objectives. First, it aims to provide the reader with a broad overview of the fertility trends, policy discourses and policy shifts in Iran over the last half a century. By interpreting these shifts in the broader context of the rapidly changing society and politics of Iran, we outline the developments that led to the introduction of the restrictive 2021 legislation. Second, we aim to highlight and discuss the most problematic features of the new Family Law, focusing especially on the legislation pertaining to maternal and reproductive health, access to abortion and contraception, and incentives designed to support earlier marriage and higher fertility. Third, we aim to discuss the likely negative consequences of the new legislation, highlighting its impact on maternal and child health, access to contraception and sexual and reproductive rights, as well as on women and socio-economic disparities. Following the Methods section, we consider the background to the fertility changes and family policy reversals witnessed in Iran. We then take a closer look at the changes in family policy over time, mapping the origins and eventual implementation of pronatalist policies that aim to increase fertility rates. Subsequently, we discuss the 2021 Family Law and its likely negative consequences in detail. The final section highlights the long-term implications of the new policies which, though unlikely to reverse fertility trends, could lead to unsafe abortions, unplanned pregnancies and births, and serious violations of reproductive rights.

Methods

The study draws on previously published national and local (in Persian) and international (in English) studies, reports and documents, supplemented with selected national media articles. We provide an overview of previous fertility and family policy changes in the form of a critical narrative review that aims to identify key policy turnarounds and summarise the discussions, political changes and other relevant factors that preceded them. The review is complemented by a brief analysis of the annual fertility trends in the country, based on the data collected and estimated by the United Nations Population Division.

We identified a range of studies and documents that discuss the demographic changes and population policies in Iran via a search of established international (Google Scholar, PubMed, Science Direct and Web of Science) and national databases (Magiran, Medlib and SID). These databases were searched by entering the terms “population changes”, “demographic changes”, “population policies”, “family planning” and “reproductive health” accompanied by the country specification (Iran). In addition, we searched the reference lists of selected studies aimed at identifying additional resources and consulted two prominent population experts who studied Iran’s family-related policies in the past.

The discussion of two comprehensive policy documents, the 2014 “General Population Policies” and the 2021 “The Youthful Population and Protection of the Family” law is based on the English translation of the former,18 and on the full Persian version of the latter,19 the parts of which that relate to marriage, fertility, contraception, abortion and family planning services were selected, translated and summarised by the first author of this study (see Appendix 1).

Iran has a complex system of theocratic and elected governance, the key positions in which are held by non-elected religious leaders and institutions. We provide a brief summary of the country’s political and policy-making system in Appendix 2 with the aim of helping readers to better understand the approach to the reshaping of family and population policies adopted since the 1979 Islamic Revolution.

We tried to avoid possible bias, including cultural and confirmation bias, in our research. The first researcher is a female scholar who lived in Iran until recently and who has an in-depth knowledge of Iranian policies and institutional settings. Her research interests span both quantitative and qualitative data and methods, as well as the study of disadvantaged communities and regions in Iran. In addition, the paper benefited from the comments of two highly experienced demographers who have an in-depth knowledge of demographic changes and population policies in Iran (see the Acknowledgements). We tried to avoid confirmation bias by compiling a careful assessment of the impacts of previously introduced policies on fertility in Iran, which included ample evidence on the strong impacts of other factors in driving past fertility declines. Our discussion on the likely adverse impact of the recent pronatalist policies in the country attempts to provide a broad picture that emphasises the uncertainties surrounding future developments in fertility and reproduction. However, in line with the policy of the journal, we applied a primarily rights-based approach to the research, which potentially downplays certain economic and normative arguments that might have supported a more positive view of the recently introduced pronatalist policies.

Results

Fertility changes and reversals in population policies in Iran

Iran has experienced rapid demographic, political and socio-economic changes in recent decades. The 1979 Islamic Revolution, which ended the regime of the Pahlavi Royal Dynasty (1925–1979) and led to the establishment of the Islamic Republic, constituted a major political upheaval that profoundly changed Iranian society. The country was also strongly affected by the eight-year war with Iraq in 1980–1988. These events also shaped Iran’s population policies and fertility trends.

Total fertility rates (TFR) in Iran have dropped from a high of around seven births per woman in the 1960s to around a quarter of this level today. However, this decline has been uneven over time and was interrupted by two brief reversals (Figure 1). Fertility rates began to decline gradually following the launch of the first national family planning programme in 1967 and reached around 6.0 in 1976.20 Following the 1979 revolution, the war with Iraq and the suspension of the family planning programme, fertility witnessed a short-lived increase before resuming a declining trajectory again from the mid-1980s. The TFR dropped from approx. 6.5 to 5.0 between 1980 and 198812; moreover, the decline accelerated in the 1990s, at which time the two-child pattern became increasingly common.10 By the turn of the century, the TFR had fallen to below the replacement level, reaching 2.0 in 2000, and after stabilising and briefly reversing in the early 2010s, it dropped once more to a very low level of around 1.7 in 2021.11

Since the family planning programme was introduced in 1967, Iran witnessed three major turnarounds in its population policies, of which two, in 1979, and more recently, in 2012, pursued pronatalist objectives. As shown in Figure 1, policies aimed at promoting family planning were launched at times when fertility rates were already declining (1967, 1989), whereas policies aimed at boosting fertility (1989, 2012) failed to lead to sustained fertility increases. It is evident that the fertility decline in Iran was due largely to rising levels of education, improved healthcare services and enhanced social and economic development, and shifts in family planning policies either helped couples to better achieve their fertility goals or rendered this aim more difficult (in the case of pronatalist policies). The next sections of the study consider these population policies and fertility shifts in more detail.

Population policies in Iran from the 1960s to 2005

Before analysing Iran’s shift towards pronatalism that has been under way since 2005, we provide an overview of the population policy changes and selected political, social and economic factors that affected fertility and families between the 1960s and 2005, a period that included the Islamic Revolution of 1979 and the Iraq-Iran war in the 1980s.

During the rule of the Pahlavi dynasty (from 1925 to 1979) Iran saw acceleration in its development and the introduction of efforts towards modernisation, as first pursued by Reza Shah Pahlavi in the late 1920s. The various structural changes in the economy were accompanied by social reforms, the establishment of compulsory education and the introduction of new family legislation that was aimed at upgrading the legal status of women, including an increase in the minimum marriage age for girls to 14 years.12,21 The social and economic changes, accompanied by improvements in health and sanitation, including vaccination and expanded access to clean water, led to a decline in mortality rates. The 1966 census reported a total population of 26 million with an annual growth rate of 3.1%, a figure that was incompatible with the aims of the development project pursued by the Pahlavi government. In 1967, Iran signed the “Declaration on Population: The World Leaders Statement”,22 which included a commitment to implement family planning programmes aimed at bringing high rates of population growth under control. The country’s first national FP programme, which included a range of explicit demographic and health objectives, was introduced in the same year.9,23–27 The new programme included the distribution of contraceptives via public clinics and the legalisation of abortion and sterilisation in 1973.26,28 It was accompanied by a huge pro-FP mass media campaign, including banners that proclaimed, “two children is enough”.28,29

However, during the 1960s and 1970s, despite strong financial support, aggressive publicity, and targeted social marketing strategies, FP programmes enjoyed only limited success in terms of reducing population growth.27,30 The growth rate remained practically unchanged, while the TFR dropped from 7.1 to 6.0 between 1966 and 1975 (see Figure 1).31 The absence of a more rapid decline in fertility was explained by slow socio-economic development accompanied by the lack of a social security system, high mortality rates, illiteracy and the continuing prevalence of early marriage in the country.27,30

FP policies were suspended following the 1979 Islamic revolution.29,32 The new political establishment perceived these policies as being closely related to those of the deposed royal family,29 as a strategy encouraged by Western countries to reduce the population of Muslim countries,33,34 and as part of the plans of “imperialists” to harness the dynamism of less developed countries for their own benefits.27 Therefore, the new government advocated population growth.35,36 During the 1980s, having a large population was considered to be a “gift from God” and it was believed that “Iran can manage a population of 200 million people and should have that population”34 [cited by Ref. 37].

The new government indirectly promoted fertility by establishing a universal rationing system, with food and consumer goods distributed on a per-capita basis, thus favouring large families.12,24,29,37 Although no explicit family policies were launched in the 1980s, the government and religious leaders in effect promoted pronatalist social and economic policies by encouraging early marriage, discouraging birth control and re-criminalising abortion.24,27,38,39 Pronatalism persisted during the war with Iraq (1980–1988), when having a large population was considered an advantage in terms of increasing the number of soldiers available to defend the country.29,30,33 Together, these factors contributed to an increase in Iran’s population from 34 million to around 50 million between 1976 and 1986, characterised by an annual average growth rate of 3.9%.30,33,37 Despite discouraging birth control,39 the new government did not prohibit the use of contraceptives.27

However, the end of the war with Iraq (1988) was quickly followed by yet another sharp policy reversal, which marked a resurgence of antinatalist‡ policies in Iran. The eight-year war, the depletion of Iran’s oil revenues39 and rapid population growth presented the government with a range of socio-economic challenges, which ultimately led to the re-introduction of the FP programme in 1989. The government began to encourage the two-child family model, aimed at slowing down population growth and assisting the reconstruction of the damaged post-war economy.26,38,40 The government restored the widespread and free-of-charge availability of FP including sterilisation. These efforts were further supported by the issuance of a fatwa (religious edict concerning daily life) by Ayatollah Khomeini in 1980,28,30,33 which declared that Islam permits the use of contraceptives.41

The Iranian parliament abolished previously introduced pronatalist incentives and initiated a media campaign aimed at encouraging women to limit the number of children they had, space out their pregnancies by 3–4 years, and avoid pregnancy under the age of 18 and over the age of 35.31,33 The target of the population policy was to reduce the TFR from 6.4 in the mid-1980s to 4.0 in 2011.27,30 However, Iran achieved these objectives in less than a decade, and the TFR eventually dropped much further (Figure 1).30,32,42,43 The contraceptive prevalence rate among married women, which stood at 37% in 197633 had jumped to 81.5% in 2011.44

While FP played a significant role in the reduction in fertility,24,30,41,45 fertility in Iran had already begun to decline in 1985, four years prior to the introduction of the new FP policies.27,46 This trend was driven mainly by falling marital fertility and was rooted in the strong desire of families to limit the number of children they had, mainly via the use of traditional contraceptive methods (specifically, withdrawal) and by delaying marriage.30,42,47 Abbasi-Shavazi et al. (chapter 5)12 determined that around 84% of the TFR decline in Iran in the decade from 1986 was due to declining marital fertility, while the remaining 16% was due to fewer and later marriages.

The decline in fertility and the wider adoption of contraception were driven by a broad set of socio-economic factors. Paradoxically, the pro-natalist period after the 1979 revolution laid the foundations for subsequent declines in fertility. Although the economic situation worsened during the war with Iraq, the education of women and the healthcare system continued to improve, while infant mortality declined and rural areas saw development of infrastructure and basic services. Urbanisation accelerated during the 1980s, with cities absorbing large numbers of uneducated labourers.48 The establishment of a comprehensive healthcare system, especially in rural areas, in the early 1980s subsequently allowed for the rapid expansion of FP across the whole country.10 Female literacy, one of the main factors behind the decline in fertility, improved dramatically. From 1966 to 1996, the literacy rate of women aged 15–19 increased from 57% to 97% in urban areas and from 5% to 86% in rural areas.49 The combination of rising education levels, urbanisation, improved healthcare and overall socio-economic development provided a favourable background for the widespread adoption of FP in Iran during the 1990s, especially in rural areas.30,46,50 However, these trends also contributed to the rise in contraceptive use and the limitation of family size as early as in the 1980s, i.e. prior to the introduction of the new FP programme.27 The new policy thus merely accelerated the shift towards a decline in fertility that was already under way.27,40,51

Despite the dramatic decline in fertility, population policies remained unchanged during the presidency of Khatami (1997–2005), who followed the Reformist Movement approach.37 Khatami emphasised the role of law, democracy, broader political participation, and the importance of a civil society, social and individual freedoms and respect for human dignity.52 [cited by Ref. 37].

Shifts in population policies since 2005

The major reorientation of population policies in Iran commenced in 2005 during the presidency of Mahmoud Ahmadinejad. Over time, the official policy discourse adopted an increasingly pronatalist tone, and specific policies restricted reproductive rights and limited the provision of FP. We discuss the changes in population policies in two parts. First, we conduct a review of the new pronatalist discourse along with the motivation for, and contexts that led to, changes in population policies. We go on to discuss in detail the cornerstone of the new policy, “The Youthful Population and Protection of the Family” law, which was approved by the Islamic Consultative Assembly on 16 March 2021.

Emerging pronatalism (1): population policy discourses in 2005–2011

A dramatic decline in the total fertility rate to 1.8 was reported by the 2006 census, which coincided with the beginning of the presidency of Ahmadinejad, who adhered to the Principlist movement, and which marked the beginning of a turnaround in the population policy of Iran. The ideological foundations of the Principlist movement, including its stand on population policies, stemmed from the principles of the Islamic Revolution. The movement’s core tenet was the strong allegiance to the Supreme Leader (rahbar), implicitly including allegiance to the broader political and institutional system (nizam).53 Ahmadinejad’s government devoted particular attention to population growth and increasing the fertility rate, which were emphasised by Islamic Idealism in the first half of the 1980s (Islamic idealism stressed loyalty to the ideals and aims of Islam regardless of their feasibility and costs54, p. 130 [cited by Ref. 37]). Ahmadinejad stated that Iran could support a population of up to 150 million people55,56 [cited by Ref. 29]. On 13 April 2010, he expressed his opposition to the two-child policy, arguing that it was a bad policy introduced by Western states37,55,56 [cited by Ref. 29]. He frequently mentioned the importance of population and fertility growth.37

In December 2010, a major economic reform – the replacement of indirect subsidies with a programme involving the direct transfer of cash to all Iranian households – was introduced by the government. Such transfers were paid monthly to the head of the family. Since the amount of the cash transfer was based on the number of family members, this plan was considered an incentive for increasing fertility.26 However, families in the three highest-income deciles were gradually excluded from receiving this subsidy. In March 2011 (the Iranian New Year), Ahmadinejad announced that the government would provide one million toman (equivalent to $US 1,343 in 2011 – own computations from historical data obtained from exchangerates.org.uk; accessed on 16 June 2023) and a monthly payment of 100,000 toman for children born in that year. This proposal was widely criticised by experts and the media and, in fact, was never introduced57 [cited by Ref. 58].

Emerging pronatalism (2): early efforts to reorient population policies (2012–2020)

The support of the supreme leader, Ayatollah Khamenei, played a critical role in population policy changes. He urged the authorities to revise the country’s population policies and called on the clerical community to employ all means possible to clarify the importance of this issue for public opinion purposes27,29 Ayatollah Khamenei also repeatedly expressed his concerns about the current fertility rate. For instance, in September 2012, he stated that population control policies should have been stopped already in the mid-1990s.29,58

Ayatollah Khamenei officially announced a shift to pronatalist population policies in May 2014, when he presented 14 new “General Population Policies”27 that aimed to “achieve national power”, “improve the dynamism, development and youthful nature of the population” and “achieve the economic, social and cultural growth of the country”.18 These policies were expected to lead to an increase in fertility to above the replacement level. They covered a wide range of topics including improving the “quality of the population”, addressing population distribution, removing barriers to marriage, managing immigration and reducing the rate of emigration. They also included normative and ideological aims such as strengthening national identity, promoting an “Islamic-Iranian lifestyle” and “confronting the negative aspects of the western lifestyle”.

Most of these new policies were never implemented; indeed, they did not even reach the legislative stage until 2021. Although these ideas paved the way for the introduction of new pronatalist legislation in 2021, their main impact concerned the provision of FP. Following the official announcement of the changes to population policies by Ayatollah Khamenei in 2014, the government abolished the national FP budget and terminated the provision of free contraceptives and FP education programmes for the general population. The government also banned the use of permanent sterilisation methods except in cases where the pregnancy poses a risk to the woman’s physical or mental health.26,29 Nevertheless, up to 2020 (see below), the new reproductive health programme continued to provide publicly funded FP (with the exception of vasectomy for men) and education programmes to women who lived in regions with fertility rates at or above the replacement level,§ as well as to poor women and those in high risk groups (e.g. women with certain diseases, women under the age of 18 and over 40, women with a child below the age of 3 and women with a history of four caesarean sections) so as to prevent unintended pregnancies. However, married women who lived in low-fertility regions were no longer entitled to free contraceptive services, although they were still offered training and information on the spacing of births59 [cited by Refs. 27,57,58]. The FP programme was further restricted in the summer of 2020. The free provision of contraceptives to high-risk groups, which had been exempted from previous cuts in the provision of FP, was discontinued. Contraceptive pills and condoms continued to be available via drugstores.

During the period 2012–2021, the government also curbed the dissemination of information with regard to FP and reproductive health as a part of its push to increase fertility. For example, the Supreme Council of the Cultural Revolution eliminated the only FP course, “Population and Family Planning”, from the university curriculum. In 2013 it was replaced with a new course, “Knowledge of Family and Population”, which focuses on marriage, families, marital relationships and childbearing and is based mainly on Islamic teachings rather than providing information on sexual and reproductive health60 [cited by Ref. 58].

“The Youthful Population and Protection of the Family” law of 2021

On 16 March 2021, the Islamic Consultative Assembly (the parliament, see Appendix 2) approved “The Youthful Population and Protection of the Family” law. This major policy shift coincided with the beginning of a new term of office for the current president of Iran, Ayatollah Raisi (a Principlist), and can be considered the second stage of the recent shift towards pronatalist policies in Iran. The president declared the seven-year pilot implementation of the new law on 16 October 2021. The Act contains 73 articles and covers a wide range of issues related to the family and childbearing (see Appendix 1 for a summary of selected articles).

The new Family Law aims to support marriage and families and to stimulate an increase in birth rates. It introduces new incentives for marriage and childbearing in the form of marriage loans, loans to buy or build housing, child and marriage benefits, tax incentives, and priority in employment (Appendix 1, parts 1-9). The law also aims to support mothers and improve their financial and employment situation via an extension of paid maternity leave, the expansion of flexible working arrangements options, and the selective reduction of the retirement age (Appendix 1, parts 10-17). The law primarily targets regions with low and moderate fertility rates, and some of its provisions and privileges are limited to counties with a TFR of 2.5 or lower. In addition, most of the incentives apply to married couples with two or more children (Appendix 1, parts 1, 7, 8, 14, 16, 17).

The new law is not limited to “positive” incentives and family support strategies, but also includes restrictive policies that limit reproductive rights, with the assumption that they will contribute to increasing the birth rate. Specifically, the new law imposes restrictions on access to FP and reproductive health services and includes strict rules on fetal screening and abortion.13 The act also forbids the free distribution or financing of contraception, the implantation of contraceptive devices and encouragement to use such devices. Furthermore, the provision of contraceptives in pharmacies and medical centres and the placement of contraceptive devices must be prescribed by a medical doctor. Permanent sterilisation has been prohibited, although female sterilisation is permitted in specific cases where the health or life of the woman is threatened (Appendix 1, parts 20–22). In addition, organisations involved in implementing the new family legislation, particularly health centre staff, are required to highlight the risks associated with the use of contraceptives (Appendix 1, part 20). The Family Law prohibits doctors and health centre staff from recommending fetal screening tests to pregnant women and sets specific conditions concerning screening (Appendix 1, part 25). It also severely restricts access to abortion (Appendix 1, part 23-24).

Likely negative consequences of the new family law

The introduction of the “Youthful Population and Protection of the Family” law in Iran is the result of both long-term concerns and discourses surrounding low fertility, its drivers and consequences, including the prospects of population ageing, population decline and a shrinking labour force,10,12,29,58,61 and an overall shift in Iranian politics towards a more socially conservative and religious ideology. The new population policies are explicitly pronatalist in their support of earlier marriages and larger family sizes. These policies are, however, controversial from both the ethical and health perspectives: they severely limit the reproductive rights and freedoms of Iranian women and couples and potentially exert negative impacts on reproductive health for both the mother and the child.26,62

The next part of the study provides a summary of the main concerns surrounding the new population policies and provides a critical discussion on their likely detrimental impact on reproductive rights and the health and status of women. We also evaluate their potential impacts on fertility and socio-economic inequality.

Concerns surrounding reproductive rights and health and ethical issues

The population policies established since 2014, especially the 2021 Family Law, have raised serious concerns about maternal, fetal and reproductive health. The restriction of fetal screening, the limiting of access to contraceptives and the tightening of the already strict rules on abortion are likely to lead to an increase in the number of newborns with physical and mental disabilities.62–64 Concerns have also been voiced about the threat of severe punishment under the criminal code for performing illegal abortions.13

Broad access to contraceptives and freedom to decide on the number, timing and spacing of children are fundamental reproductive rights that are closely linked to the quality of life and health of women and of their children.65,66 Iran’s new policy is in conflict with the standards of international human rights agreements and treaties, including those concerning sexual and reproductive health.61 The new policy, including its emphasis on highlighting the “risks” of the use of contraceptives, violates Iran’s commitments to the 1994 ICPD, which was ratified by the country. ICPD signatories are bound to ensure universal access to FP and to help prevent unintended pregnancies by providing the required education and contraceptives.26

Prior to the new legislation, many couples, particularly in rural regions, relied on the free provision of contraception by health centres. For those who live at a distance from the nearest health centre, doctor or pharmacy, access to contraception and the related important information has been seriously restricted. Furthermore, many people are unable to afford a visit to the doctor or to buy contraceptives.

Limiting access to FP services increases the probability of unintended pregnancies, unsafe abortions, high-risk sexual behaviour and the incidence of sexually transmitted diseases,26,29,61,64 especially for lower socio-economic groups and those living in rural and remote areas. Consequently, the new restrictions will widen both reproductive health disparities and the socio-economic gap between privileged and disadvantaged social groups.26,29,58,64

The new legislation has also negated past efforts aimed at promoting sexual and reproductive health, including the significant resources spent over many years on promoting safe sex and reducing the incidence of sexually transmitted infections.29 Although since 1979 strict rules have been in place aimed at preventing abortions, unofficial estimates show that many women in fact undergo illegal abortions, often without any legal consequences.67,68 For instance, Rastegari et al.69 estimated that between 129,000 and 219,000 “non-medical” (i.e. illegal) abortions are performed in Iran annually. The new limitations on access to FP are likely to further increase the already high number of illegal and unsafe abortions. Unsafe abortion rates have been shown to be higher in countries with stricter abortion laws,70 including many countries in Latin America.71

Impact of the new policies on fertility and socio-economic inequality

One of the main objectives of the new population policies in Iran was to increase the fertility rate. However, although Iran started to limit access to FP services in 2012, the long-term decline in fertility was reversed only briefly due to the shortening of birth intervals.27

The decline in fertility in Iran was driven by economic, cultural and institutional factors in combination with previously successful FP policies. The drivers of the decline included falls in infant and child mortality rates, delayed marriage, increased female education levels, the empowerment of women, a change in the balance of the “costs and benefits” of having children, rapid urbanisation, and changes in values and preferences.12,24,27,72,73 The adoption of the two-child family pattern played a key role in fertility decline in Iran in the 1990s and 2000s, when the progression to a third birth declined rapidly.74,75 Abbasi-Shavazi et al.51 suggested that the previous reduction in the ideal family size for Iranian women renders it unlikely that many of them will increase their fertility even with the introduction of government economic incentives. Today, most Iranians wish to have a relatively small family of up to two children.51

Many of the benefits of the new law are only available for married couples with two or more children – a group that is much less relevant for future fertility trends in Iran than childless and unmarried young adults.10 Moreover, many people are unable to take advantage of the new financial instruments, e.g. marriage loans, mortgages and loans for newborns, that are set out in the new law, due to the lengthy application process and the requirement for multiple guarantees.

Population policies should avoid the perpetuation of structural discrimination.76 While all the country’s provinces have been deprived of free FP services under the new policy, high-fertility districts have been deprived of numerous privileges and facilities aimed at supporting families. The new law is likely to deepen structural inequality and deprivation and widen the regional and urban-rural disparities in the country.

Impact on women and gender inequality within the family

Although the new policy purports to support women in childbearing, it negatively impacts them in two important ways – first, by limiting their access to FP services and threatening their reproductive rights, as discussed above. Second, without the appropriate oversight, the obligations placed on employers by the new legislation regarding pregnant women and those with young children may, paradoxically, further reduce the already dismally low formal employment rate of women by leading some employers to prefer men when recruiting, and push more women into informal employment. At present, only 19% of Iranian women of productive age are employed.77 The employment and maternity regulations target only this minority of women who are formally employed. They focus primarily on the child’s pre-school years,78 while some of the envisioned longer-term benefits, such as a reduction in the retirement age by one year for mothers with one or two children and by an additional 1.5 years for each subsequent child, represent uncertain privileges that may or may not materialise in the very distant future. In Iran, where such regulations change frequently, this factor is unlikely to impact the motivation of women to have children.

Male engagement in childcare and parenting has expanded in recent years in Iran due to a raft of social and cultural changes. However, over the last four decades, Iranian policies have highlighted solely women’s family roles, with an emphasis on the “breadwinner man, caregiver woman” pattern.78 Population policies that ignore men and fathers fail to reflect the shift to a more egalitarian division of childcare and household labour. In low-fertility countries, the shift to more gender egalitarian values and policies paves the way to a possible future recovery in fertility.79,80 In contrast, the persistence of conservative and highly unequal gender norms in Iran – broadly supported by the new Family Law – may further accelerate fertility declines to very low levels. Research on East Asian countries with patriarchal cultures and “ultra-low” fertility suggests that more educated women frequently postpone or avoid marriage, an event that often leads to their temporary exit from the labour market and marks the start of their new demanding roles as housewives, mothers and even carers for their parents-in-law.81

Discussion: the long-term implications of restrictive pronatalism

The new pronatalist family policies and discourse in Iran fit the pattern observed in many authoritarian countries with falling fertility rates. From a demographic perspective, the observed shift to low fertility since the early 2000s and the prospects of population ageing justify a reconsideration of the country’s population and family planning policies and, ideally, would lead to a turn towards family-friendly policies providing a comprehensive support to families and children. Such policies would also help women and couples to realise their reproductive goals. However, the policies actually implemented in the country have been formulated largely by male policymakers focusing primarily on the goal of reversing declining fertility and supporting population growth. These policies have many harmful consequences for women, families, children and society. They promote a patriarchal vision of family and gender roles, limit access to prenatal screening, contraception and abortion, and stigmatise those who do not conform to the narrowly-defined view of the “traditional” family. They are also likely to fail to achieve their main aim – a strong and lasting reversal in the fertility rate. Ideological, political and economic objectives cannot be achieved by adopting policies that negatively affect gender equity and limit sexual and reproductive health provision and rights.6,37

As argued by Mahmoodi et al.37 the various population policy shifts in Iran have been driven by political aims. The new pronatalist policies mainly reflect political and ideological objectives aimed at boosting Iran's geopolitical role in the region27,82 and the desire to perpetuate the patriarchal view of the family and family relations. Population growth is seen as a key factor in terms of the balance of power between states63 and limiting the future burden of an ageing population.26,82 Compared to the earlier policies that aimed to reduce fertility, which were more closely aligned to individual preferences, government intervention to increase fertility is more controversial and costly, and often exerts no appreciable impacts.37,58 Pronatalist policies potentially lead to undesired and unforeseen outcomes beyond their impact on reproductive, maternal and child health. For instance, the pronatalist policies that followed the 1979 revolution led to a baby boom which contributed to rising unemployment and housing shortages two decades later.29,61,83 The new policies target mainly married couples with at least two children; however, they do not adequately address the costs of family formation and childrearing, especially for younger women and men. One of the key causes of the delay in marriages and first births in Iran concerns the lack of housing and formal employment.

The imposition of more restrictions on access to FP since 2021 points to the limited understanding of fertility and its determinants by policymakers. Fertility decision-making is complex and multidimensional, involving individual and family circumstances, societal and institutional conditions and future expectations. It is difficult to identify a simple cause–effect link between policies and fertility change. Pronatalist policies often fail to increase fertility84 or lead merely to its modest recovery.85 When they do stimulate an increase in fertility, their impact is typically temporary, driven largely by changes in the timing and spacing of births rather than a genuine increase in family size.3

As highlighted by Pritchett,86 fertility differences between countries are not closely linked to the availability of contraceptives or to contrasts between government-instigated FP policies; rather, they are due largely to differences in couples’ perceived ideal family size. Even when access to contraceptives is limited, the motivation to delay, space or limit childbearing plays the decisive role in determining fertility. Accordingly, Iran’s rapid fertility decline over the past few decades can be seen as a response to the wider global transition towards lower family size preferences and low and highly controlled fertility rates.87 It seems that Iran is repeating the mistakes of the draconian pronatalist policy enacted in Romania in 1966, which imposed a ban on abortion (with certain exceptions) and severely curtailed access to contraception. This policy led to a sharp rise in unintended pregnancies, unsafe abortions and maternal morbidity.62,88–90

The new pronatalist policies will not lead to a change in broader Iranian population trends: even if they result in a higher number of births, population ageing will continue to progress due to declining mortality and increases in longevity. Therefore, the government needs to design a new comprehensive plan for adapting healthcare and social services to an ageing population.29,91 Policymakers should take into account that Iran is a vast country composed of different economic, social and cultural groups that, despite many similarities,50 differ in their fertility desires, and behaviours and attitudes to population policies.92 Rather than achieving the intended results, the implementation of a uniform pronatalist policy that fails to take these differences into account will have wide-reaching negative consequences for Iranian society.

Political system and policy decision-making in Iran

Iran’s post-revolutionary political setup is a complex mix of theocratic and democratic institutions, sometimes referred to as a “republican theocracy”.93 This implies a complicated structure of decision-making pertaining to population and family policies. The theocratic structure rests upon the authority of the Supreme Leader of the Islamic Republic of Iran and the Guardian Council. The Supreme Leader holds a lifetime position and “controls the regular Army, the Revolutionary Guards (a paramilitary force) and the Basij (militia) organisations charged with safeguarding the principles of the revolution; he is also the head of the judiciary and the state-monopolised media”.93 Moreover, he supervises the legislature, the judiciary and the executive branches of the constitution.94 Based on Article 110 of the constitution, two of the main responsibilities of the leadership are to delineate “the general policies of the Islamic Republic of Iran after consultation with the Expediency Discernment Council” and to supervise “the proper execution of the general policies of the system”. The 12-member Guardian Council influences legislation in two ways. First, it holds veto power over legislation passed by the parliament. Second, it vets candidates running for elected offices, including candidates for the presidency and parliamentary positions. By blocking certain candidates, it indirectly steers the policy agenda. In addition, the Supreme Leader also appoints the Expediency Discernment Council set up in 1988 “to resolve differences or conflicts between the Majlis (parliament) and the Guardian Council”,95 which also serves as his advisory body.

The secular/elected governing structure consists of the president and the one-chamber parliament (The Islamic Consultative Assembly (Majlis)). The president selects and heads the Council of Ministers, with the approval of parliament. The president and his ministers are also responsible for the implementation of laws enacted by the legislature. Government bodies are entitled to obtain advice from external experts and non-governmental organisations such as Islamist women’s organisations, which influence policy discussions and legislation pertaining to family and gender issues.93

This complex system that involves an unelected group of officials, who control and oversee the elected government and legislative bodies, often results in a fragile decision-making balance between more conservative/religious and more centrist or reformist forces.93 Important decisions on policy orientation, such as decisions related to family planning and birth control, need to be supported by the religious leaders before they can be debated and approved by the elected bodies. For instance, the decision to pursue an “antinatalist” population policy in 1988–1989 was first considered behind closed doors by religious leaders, elected representatives (the president and the speaker of parliament) and policy experts. Subsequently, the specific policies (the Family Planning Law) and appropriate budget were ratified by the parliament, and the Ministry of Health and Medical Education was given the mandate and resources to operate the new programme12,41 (Chapter 2).
