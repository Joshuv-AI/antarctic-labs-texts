Protocol for the neonatal intracerebroventricular delivery of adeno-associated viral vectors for brain restoration of MECP2 for Rett syndrome
Authors: Kan Yang, Tianshu Li, Yixiao Geng, Ru Zhang, Zhankui Xu, Jun Wu, Yiting Yuan, Yuefang Zhang, Zilong Qiu, Fei Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol for neonatal intracerebroventricular (ICV) delivery of adeno-associated viral vectors (AAVs), achieving gene therapy for a Rett syndrome mouse model. We describe steps for preparing mouse lines, replacing foster mothers, sex typing, and genotyping. We then detail procedures for ICV delivery and validation through immunofluorescent and immunoblot techniques. This protocol is also applicable to preclinical gene therapy research that targets the neonatal mouse brain for other neurodevelopmental disorders.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Yang et al.1Subject areas: clinical protocol, model organisms, neuroscience

Before you begin

The following protocol, adapted from Yang et al. 2022, describes the injection of adeno-associated viral vectors serotype 9 (AAV9) into the lateral ventricle of neonatal pups at postnatal day2–3 (P2-P3). The protocol below presents an example for Mecp2 knockout C57BL/6 pups with 4 bp deletion in exon3 for Rett syndrome. However, this protocol can also in theory be applied using other viral vectors (e.g., lenti- or retro viral vectors) in different mouse models (e.g., Shank3 knockout pups), or with dyes.

Note: The CRITICAL steps must be followed with no modification.

Injection at P0-P1 is not recommended without prior knowledge of the genotype and the specific viral vectors required. Familiarizing the ICR mother and pups with each other’s odors during P0-P1 contributes to post-surgical survival. The age of P2-P3 is therefore suitable for injection. Performing the injection at P2 is the optimal choice.

Step-by-step method details

Anesthesia of the pup

Timing: 10–20 s

This step describes the whole process of pup from the awake state to anesthesia before surgery for ICV delivery of AAV.

CRITICAL: Hypothermia induced anesthesia is recommended, as isoflurane and injectable anesthesia have challenging dosage control, which can result in the death of the pup. Ensure adherence to the ethical requirements of local regional policies.

1.Place the pup on crushed ice.a.Wait 15–25 s for the pup.b.Apply a gentle pressure to the paw, and closely observe for the absence of motor response or respiratory activity to verify anesthesia (Figure 2D).

Place the pup on crushed ice.a.Wait 15–25 s for the pup.b.Apply a gentle pressure to the paw, and closely observe for the absence of motor response or respiratory activity to verify anesthesia (Figure 2D).

a.Wait 15–25 s for the pup.

Wait 15–25 s for the pup.

b.Apply a gentle pressure to the paw, and closely observe for the absence of motor response or respiratory activity to verify anesthesia (Figure 2D).

Apply a gentle pressure to the paw, and closely observe for the absence of motor response or respiratory activity to verify anesthesia (Figure 2D).

Note: A duration of 15–25 s is recommended, confirmed by the absence of movement and struggling.

2.Label the injection sites on the skin above the crania with a non-toxic marker (Figure 2E).

Label the injection sites on the skin above the crania with a non-toxic marker (Figure 2E).

Note: According to Figure 2E, point a is designated as the forefront of the visible blood vessels in the mouse brain, and points b and c are the turning points of the left and right brain blood vessels from the bottom to the top, that is, the blood vessel position closest to the neck. Connect a, b and a, c, d, e respectively as the midpoint of these two line segments. Connecting the two points d and e, the two quartiles of this line segment are the marking points.

CRITICAL: The example of two injection sites for bilateral ventricular injection is shown in the Figure 2E. Injecting based solely on predetermined stereotaxic x and y coordinates is discouraged due to variations in skull size among different batches of pup at P2. Coordinates for the injection site, determined by relative positioning, are more adaptable and precise.

CRITICAL: Injecting only one ventricle is acceptable for the sake of saving the surgery time. The cerebrospinal fluid circulation transports the viral vectors injected on one side to both hemispheres, which may cover parts of brain regions. However, bilateral injection is still highly recommended for more comprehensive coverage of the brain compared with unilateral injection.

3.Transfer the anesthetized pup to a prone position on the injection stage, covered with paper towels. Gently cover the body of the pup with tape and immobilize the pup on the stage (Figure 2F).

Transfer the anesthetized pup to a prone position on the injection stage, covered with paper towels. Gently cover the body of the pup with tape and immobilize the pup on the stage (Figure 2F).

Note: Take care not to press the tape too tightly to avoid obstructing blood circulation and causing crush injury to internal organs.

4.Secure the mouse’s head using the designed stabilizing device.

Secure the mouse’s head using the designed stabilizing device.

Note: The stabilizing device is constructed using a knife and sterile EP tube. For the shape refer to Figure 2F.

5.Put a small amount of crushed ice around the body of each mouse during the injection. For the quantity of ice refer to Figure 2G.

Put a small amount of crushed ice around the body of each mouse during the injection. For the quantity of ice refer to Figure 2G.

CRITICAL: Maintain hypothermia induced anesthesia throughout the entire injection process. Hypothermia induced anesthesia can relieve pain of pup and reduce pup movement to lower the risk of additional damage and injection site deviation. We suggest that the maximum anesthesia time should be controlled within 10 min, though we find the hypothermia survival rate is not hampered (Figures S5B and S5C). The pups should be given some measures to keep warm after the experiment, such as electric heating pad or cotton. Excessive crushed ice should not be put around the body of the pup (Figure 2G). Regional hypothermia caused by a large ice cube can result in the pup sticking to it and suffering from frostbite. The use of ice packs is prohibited because their heavy weight can cause severe crushing injuries to the pup.

ICV injection of AAV

Timing: <10 min

This step describes the injection of neonatal ICV with AAV at P2 (Figure 2G).

CRITICAL: The injection for each pup completes the injection process within 10 min.

Note: The flow rate of the injection must not exceed 15 nL/s to prevent increased intracranial pressure.

6.Adjust the glass capillary needle tip at the marked injection site.a.Lower the tip of the needle until it touches the injection mark on the skin above the crania of the pup and zero the z-axis.b.Gently lower the tip to −1.5 mm on the z-axis.

Adjust the glass capillary needle tip at the marked injection site.a.Lower the tip of the needle until it touches the injection mark on the skin above the crania of the pup and zero the z-axis.b.Gently lower the tip to −1.5 mm on the z-axis.

a.Lower the tip of the needle until it touches the injection mark on the skin above the crania of the pup and zero the z-axis.

Lower the tip of the needle until it touches the injection mark on the skin above the crania of the pup and zero the z-axis.

b.Gently lower the tip to −1.5 mm on the z-axis.

Gently lower the tip to −1.5 mm on the z-axis.

7.Inject up to max 1 μL at a constant speed of 8.3 nL/s, administered over a period of 2 min. Do not exceed an injection flow rate beyond 15 nL/s.

Inject up to max 1 μL at a constant speed of 8.3 nL/s, administered over a period of 2 min. Do not exceed an injection flow rate beyond 15 nL/s.

Note: If the fluid enters the subcutaneous part, the fluid takes on a markedly irregular shape with greater fluidity and greater brightness. If the fluid enters the brain parenchyma, no obvious color is seen on the outside. It is only after the fluid enters the ventricles that it takes on a distinct color of the crescent shape (Figure S5D).

CRITICAL: Viral titers for injection range from 4×1012 vg/mL to 6×1012 vg/mL. A titer of 5×1012 vg/mL is recommended. Strictly control the injection dose to 1 μL /site (1 μL for each left and right ventricle). Each pup receives an average of 1×1010 vg of viral vectors. Excessive injection (1.5 μL or 2 μL/site) increases intracranial pressure and significantly decreases survival rate at 1-week post-surgery.

8.Keep the needle in situ for 1 min after injection. Adjust z-value height of the stereotaxic instrument to slowly withdraw the needle.

Keep the needle in situ for 1 min after injection. Adjust z-value height of the stereotaxic instrument to slowly withdraw the needle.

CRITICAL: Keeping the needle in situ allows the viral vectors to mix with the cerebrospinal fluid. Withdraw the needle slowly to prevent leakage of the viral vectors.

9.Once the injection at the first site is completed, immediately begin injecting the other side by repeating steps 6–8.

Once the injection at the first site is completed, immediately begin injecting the other side by repeating steps 6–8.

Note: The maximum time recommended to finish the injection per ventricle is 3 min.

Note: If a leak occurs at the injection site, continue the injection if the unilateral loss is less than 500 nL. However, if both sites leak and severe blood return occurs, potentially due to issues with the injection site, discontinue the procedure on this mouse. Such an alive pup cannot be kept aside as a control sample.

10.Gently remove any liquid off the body of the pup after injection, and place the pup in cages lined with absorbent cotton, along with a designed post-surgery card for specialized post-operative management and care5 (Figure 2H).

Gently remove any liquid off the body of the pup after injection, and place the pup in cages lined with absorbent cotton, along with a designed post-surgery card for specialized post-operative management and care5 (Figure 2H).

Note: To maximum survival rates, use generous amounts of sterile absorbent cotton in cages to facilitate the pups’ return to normal body temperature.

Note: Consistently use a shading cloth and prevent cages from jostling during post-surgical transportation to minimize anxiety levels in the foster mothers.

Optional: Provide an appropriate amount of sunflower seeds into the cage to support lactation in foster mothers, which also facilitates to reduce infanticide.

Note: Cadavers of pups that die during injection should be immediately frozen (−20°C) and will then serve as control samples for subsequent immunoblot and immunofluorescence at P0.

Expected outcomes

ICV delivery of AAV to restore the target gene expression is a crucial technique in gene therapy strategies for neurodevelopmental disorders such as Rett syndrome. This protocol offers a method for administering AAV into the cerebral ventricles of pup, ensuring high coverage, low hepatotoxicity, and efficient and sustained expression.

To compare the efficacy of intraventricular injection with that of other commonly used viral injection approaches, we compared the viral expression in the brain and hepatotoxicity produced by the different modes of injection 30 days after viral injection (Figure 3A). With the facial intravenous (FIV) injection contrast, ICV delivery of virus is more accurate in hippocampus and cortex, whose functions are critical for autism research. Immunofluorescent staining reveals effective virus expression of ICV in brain regions such as the cortex and hippocampus at 30 days post-injection, which is consistent with the results of previous study6 (Figures 3B and 4A). In addition, ICV delivery shows milder hepatotoxicity compared with tail intravenous (TIV) injection, which is a common intravenous delivery approach of virus injection.7 We determine the concentrations of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) in serum via FIV, TIV and ICV delivery and find the ICV delivery is less toxic (Figures 3C and 3D). At the same time, intravenous injection results in more virus consumption (Figure 3B). In contract with FIV and TIV, ICV injection causes less hepatotoxicity, consumes less virus, and intervenes earlier in pups (Table 3).

Advantages of ICV compared with FIV and TIV

The efficacy and persistence of AAV expression have historically been major concerns in the field of gene therapy. By immunoblotting, the MECP2 expression level of the brain of Mecp2-/y mice restores to 79.1% of that in Mecp2+/y mice 30 days after AAV delivery, and reaches 82.8% at 60 days; and still remains at 64.6% even at 120 days, demonstrating high efficacy and persistence with this protocol (Figures 4B and 4C). To separate the cortex, hippocampus and hypothalamus, the MECP2 expression level also remains at a high level (Figures 4D–4I). Therefore, this protocol can be extended to preclinical gene therapy research targeting the pup brain for various neurodevelopmental disorders.8

Limitations

The protocol offers a simple and efficient technique for neonatal ICV delivery of AAV. The complete entry of AAV into the cerebrospinal fluid within the ventricles determines brain coverage. Intraventricular injection solves the problem of intracranial pressure and toxicity caused by direct injection into the brain parenchyma. However, the injection technique in this protocol primarily relies on the experimenter’s surgical experience without the benefit of the assistance of ultrasound visualization techniques.9 Therefore, it is crucial to continually assess the depth of the glass capillary needle and make adjustments as needed during the experiment (refer to troubleshooting).

Indeed, balancing viral expression strength with viral toxicity is a critical issue that must be fully considered in all gene therapy projects. For genes (such as Mecp2) that are widely expressed in various types of cells throughout the brain, including neurons, astrocytes, microglia, etc., the key to achieving efficient and sustained expression in the brain is to realize widespread and mild overexpression in different cell types. The cytomegalovirus immediate-early enhancer/chicken β-actin (CAG) promoter used in this protocol drives widespread and mild overexpression of the target gene in different cell types. However, further screening and validation are needed to determine whether other broad-spectrum promoters may have better effects. For genes that are limited to express in synaptic structures (such as Shank3), the human Synapsin (hSyn) promoter has more advantages in specificity for cell.

How to ensure the sustained expression of the viral vectors is also worth noting. The technique in this protocol involves only a single injection, which is able to remain sustained expression until adulthood (Figure 4). However, the exogenously injected viral vectors will inevitably be cleared over time after slow metabolism within the brain. Therefore, a single injection strategy may not be sufficient to cover the entire lifespan.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Infanticide by mothers of certain autistic mouse models (refer to step 1 of preparing mouse lines and foster mothers).

Potential solution

Observations have noted severe infanticidal behavior among mothers of several autistic mouse models with genetic mutations (such as Mecp2+/− female mice, Mecp2 duplication female mice, Shank3+/− female mice, and Senp1+/− female mice). This behavior is exacerbated for the post-surgical neonatal pup. It is strongly advised to use WT ICR female mice, known for their superior maternal instincts, for breeding purposes. The ICR mothers can be prepared by the experimenter or purchased from a vendor. It is important to note that ICR female mice need to give birth before the experimental mice to allow for replacement at P0. If ICR female mice are not available in the short term, WT C57BL/6 female mice can also be used for breeding (Figure S1B).

Before replacement with ICR foster mothers, a risk of infanticide in mothers of autism model will still happen shortly after delivery. For autism mouse models with mutations not located on the X chromosome (e.g., Shank3+/− female mice), adopt the following mating scheme to reduce this risk before replacement with ICR foster mothers: Male Shank3+/− or Shank3−/− crosses with female WT.

Problem 2

Variations in head circumference sizes among different batches of pup at the same time affect the targeting of ICV injection (refer to step 2 of anesthesia of the pup).

Potential solution

This technique strongly recommends utilizing a consistent targeting strategy. Using the superficial temporal artery as a reference point for lateral ventricle targeting offers a straightforward and adaptable method.

Utilizing a thick-tipped non-toxic marker for marking will be faster and clearer. When marking, simply place the thick tip of the marker vertically against the injection site. We do not recommend tilting the marker to make circular marks on the scalp (as this can lead to displacement and inaccuracies). Adjust the z-axis value according to the age of the pup. For pup at P2, z: −1.5 mm; for pup at P3, z: −1.6 mm. Based on practical experience, minor adjustments may be necessary for both the injection coordinates and the z-axis value.

Problem 3

During ICV injection in pup, head movement can cause viral leakage and may even result in the breakage of the glass capillary needle (refer to step 4 of anesthesia of the pup).

Potential solution

It is strongly recommended to utilize a designed head stabilizing device, modified from sterile EP tubes. This device effectively immobilizes the neonatal mouse’s head without causing any harm, facilitating the stable insertion of the glass capillary needle into the lateral ventricle. By utilizing the head stabilizing device, times of leak decreases, while survival rate of 1-week post-surgery for pup increases (Figure S4).

If pups continue to exhibit slight head movement during surgery even after the application of the stabilizing device, the application of crushed ice should be increased appropriately to enhance the effect of hypothermia induced anesthesia. The positioning of the sterile tape and the body of the pup can also be adjusted to ensure moderate tightness while strengthening the fixation of the head.

Problem 4

Risk of hypothermia and mortality in pup after ice anesthesia (refer to step 5 of anesthesia of the pup).

Potential solution

Prepare a small electric heating pad and pre-set the temperature to 37°C. Immediately wipe the liquid on the surface of mouse body and gently place it on the electric heating pad after the injection. Wait until the mouse body regains a pink color, it can adjust posture autonomously and crawl, which indicates that the temperature of the mouse body has returned to normal. Then, gently return it to the cage with the ICR foster mother. If the temperature of the mouse recovers too slowly, reduce the amount of crushed ice utilized in subsequent surgeries. If the mouse ceases breathing and cardiac function after the surgery, the experimenter should immediately perform cardiopulmonary resuscitation by placing the mouse flat on the electric heating pad and rhythmically pressing its chest with an index finger.

Problem 5

The liquid level fails to decrease as a result of obstruction within the glass capillary needle (refer to step 7 of ICV injection of AAV).

Potential solution

If the glass capillary needle becomes obstructed, the volume of the viral solution may not decrease. If the neonatal mouse bleeds significantly, stop the experiment immediately to help stop the bleeding, and replace the mouse with a new one. If no significant bleeding is observed, the experiment can continue. First, remove the crushed ice from the experimental mouse, wipe it clean, and let it emergence from hypothermia induced anesthesia. Utilizing scissors to trim the plugged glass capillary needle appropriately until the viral liquid is pushed out by the injection pump. Start the experiment again from the ice anesthesia step. The number of times the hypothermia induced anesthesia can be done on a single pup should be capped according to the requirements of the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee.
