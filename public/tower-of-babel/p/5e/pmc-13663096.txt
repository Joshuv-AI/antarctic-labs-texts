Artificial intelligence for carbon emissions management: advances, challenges, and future directions across monitoring, prediction, and reduction
Authors: Xiyue Cao, Xujiang Qin, Yanqiu Zuo, Junjie Fang, Hongqiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rising anthropogenic carbon emissions are a major driver of climate change and pose a critical challenge to global sustainable development. As a rapidly advancing technology, artificial intelligence (AI) has shown strong potential to enhance carbon emissions management. This review provides a critical and comprehensive synthesis of recent advances in AI-enabled approaches for carbon emissions monitoring, prediction, and reduction. For monitoring, it explores the integration of satellite remote sensing, sensor networks, and machine learning (ML) algorithms, which can improve multi-scale, high-resolution, and near-real-time monitoring capabilities. For prediction, it categorizes prediction models into three groups, namely deep learning (DL), ensemble learning, and statistical learning, to facilitate the selection of appropriate technical approaches based on varying data characteristics and prediction requirements. For reduction, it examines the practical effectiveness of AI in industrial process optimization, energy structure transformation, transportation scheduling and management, construction energy efficiency improvement, and carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS). We further reveal core challenges and potential solutions across the data layer, model layer, and application layer in AI deployment, including data availability and quality, model generalization and interpretability, and engineering and governance barriers that hinder the translation of AI methods into real-world applications. Furthermore, future research directions are discussed to promote the development of more reliable and scalable AI methods that can better support decision-making and practical governance in carbon emissions management. Overall, distinct from previous reviews that mainly focus on single tasks, specific model types, or sectoral applications, this review represents, to our knowledge, one of the first review-level attempts to develop a policy-relevant and interdisciplinary AI framework for carbon emissions management across the full process of monitoring, prediction, and reduction. By integrating unified evaluation metrics, evidence matrices, deployment-constraint analysis, and a technology readiness level (TRL)-based assessment, this framework links methodological performance, application readiness, and governance needs. It provides an integrated methodological foundation for fine-grained emissions sensing, predictive analysis, and emissions reduction decision support, while supporting quantifiable, verifiable, and actionable carbon balance and management.

Introduction

The continuing rise in global carbon emissions poses serious ecological challenges [1]. Reducing carbon emissions is a key path to achieving sustainable development. IPCC assessments demonstrate that global warming-induced sea level rise profoundly affects ecosystems, human livelihoods, infrastructure, and food security [2, 3]. Under the Paris Agreement, the international community has set the goal of limiting warming to well below 2 °C by the end of this century, while pursuing 1.5 °C [4, 5]. Yet current and planned measures remain insufficient, and more ambitious reduction actions are urgently needed.

In carbon emissions modeling, traditional methods such as material balance and emission factor approaches are predominantly used for emissions accounting [6–8]; whereas STIRPAT (stochastic impacts by regression on population, affluence, and technology) models, multiple regression, grey models, and economic analysis models are primarily employed for prediction. These methods provide foundational support for policy formulation. However, due to the highly nonlinear and spatiotemporal coupling characteristics of carbon emissions processes, traditional models often exhibit limitations in timeliness and adaptability when handling large-scale, dynamically changing data [9], thereby constraining the achievement of high-precision monitoring and prediction.

In recent years, leveraging its strengths in data processing, pattern recognition, and optimization, AI has advanced carbon emissions monitoring, prediction, and reduction. For instance, combining remote sensing with ML has improved monitoring accuracy [10]. ML prediction models also have captured complex nonlinear emission trends more effectively [11]. In addition, AI-enabled smart power systems and low-carbon manufacturing have supported optimized reduction pathways [12].

Figure 1 shows that AI-based carbon research has grown rapidly, outpacing traditional modeling approaches, which indicates rising interest in and strong potential for data-driven methods. From the perspective of carbon balance and management, the value of AI methods lies not only in improving the predictive accuracy of individual models, but also in linking key processes such as carbon-source identification, emissions estimation, emissions trend prediction, reduction optimization, and governance feedback. Embedding AI across the full chain of carbon monitoring, prediction, and reduction can promote interdisciplinary integration among carbon-cycle research, monitoring-reporting-verification (MRV) systems, and low-carbon governance, thereby enhancing the policy relevance and practical usability of research outputs. Against this background, this review develops a practice-oriented full-chain framework for AI-enabled carbon emissions management and systematically explains how AI methods can be embedded across monitoring, prediction, and reduction. It aims to provide a reference for researchers in carbon neutrality, machine learning, and related interdisciplinary fields, while supporting the identification of key intervention points and windows of opportunity in carbon-climate-human systems. Therefore, this review is well aligned with the scope of carbon balance and management research and contributes a framework-oriented perspective to this field.

Artificial intelligence in carbon emissions research

Carbon emissions monitoring

Carbon emissions monitoring underpins climate governance by providing consistent, timely, and spatially resolved evidence on greenhouse gas concentrations, emissions, and sinks to support policy design and implementation. CO₂ remains the dominant contributor to warming [20], and thus is the primary focus of monitoring efforts.

Carbon emissions monitoring covers multiple subtasks, including point-source detection, event identification, anomaly detection, plume or hotspot segmentation, and emission quantification and inversion. To improve cross-study comparability, we standardize evaluation metrics by task type: detection and recognition tasks use precision, recall, F1-score, and accuracy; segmentation tasks use IoU (intersection over union), Dice, and BCE (binary cross-entropy); and continuous estimation or inversion tasks use Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and Bias.

By embedding ML throughout data acquisition, inversion, fusion, and quality control, AI has significantly enhanced coverage breadth, spatial resolution, and temporal timeliness [21]. Technical pathways and AI intervention points in carbon emissions monitoring

Satellite remote sensing technology

Since the 1990s, multiple countries have successively launched greenhouse gas monitoring satellites, continuously strengthening global observation capabilities. Satellite observations also provide an important basis for mapping forest carbon stocks and aboveground biomass, thereby supporting large-scale carbon sink monitoring and uncertainty reduction [28]. However, variations in spectral bands, spatial resolutions, overpass times, and aerosol interferences across different platforms present persistent challenges. Traditional physical inversion methods are highly sensitive to prior assumptions and parameter settings, making them vulnerable to errors under complex surface and atmospheric conditions. AI enhances physical models through data-driven approaches, improving both quality and efficiency across the entire workflow of identification, detection, inversion, and data fusion.

In point source detection and segmentation, Fu et al. developed a global-scale road carbon emissions identification system by integrating convolutional neural network (CNN)- with graph neural network (GNN)-based methods [10]. In terms of automatic feature extraction, Zhou et al. developed a regional-scale carbon emissions estimation approach for agricultural areas and combustion hotspots by integrating Sentinel-2 and Landsat-8 data with CNN algorithm. By introducing gamma correction and a multi-model ensemble strategy, they further enhanced the robustness of feature extraction [24]. Another study employed a U-Net CNN to overcome the limitations of traditional methods in low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and complex plume morphology segmentation, achieving precise, generalizable, and quantifiable plume segmentation. This approach provided high-quality input data for subsequent emission inversion, thereby improving the accuracy of local CO₂ emission estimates [29].

In the area of regional flux estimation and multi-source data fusion, to address the heterogeneity among statistical data, remote sensing indices, and socioeconomic datasets, Cao et al. integrated energy statistics with nighttime-light remote sensing and built a multi-scale estimation model using Bayesian-optimized XGBoost, which outperformed conventional linear regression [22]. This reveals how AI enhances the ability to identify and quantify hotspots and key industries.

The core advantage of integrating satellite remote sensing with AI lies in its global coverage capability. However, it relies heavily on high-quality imagery and large volumes of training data, making it most suitable for macro-level trend analysis and preliminary hotspot identification, though ground-based data are still needed for calibration and accuracy validation.

Sensor networks

Sensor networks, featuring low-cost and densely distributed nodes, enable high-frequency, near-surface, and spatially continuous observations. However, they are often affected by sensor drift, inter-device inconsistencies, and data loss, which can compromise data reliability. To address these limitations, AI offers scalable solutions for data quality control, model fusion, and dynamic inversion within carbon monitoring networks.

(1) Data Quality Control and Calibration. The DBSCAN (density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise) algorithm, as a representative unsupervised method, enables anomaly detection and event clustering, thereby enhancing the robustness of subsequent modeling [30]. In addition, studies have employed ensemble linear models (e.g., Linear forest regressor (LFR)) [31] and artificial neural network (ANN) [32] to optimize the modeling of low-cost sensors, improving their measurement stability and accuracy under conditions of cross-scenario transfer and cross-sensitivity, respectively. AI-based methods empower sensor networks to automatically identify anomalies, dynamically calibrate errors, and adapt to heterogeneous environments from raw data streams, thus supporting stable city-scale carbon monitoring.

(2) Multi-source fusion and dynamic tracking. In urban and campus applications, sensor networks are often jointly modeled with meteorological, activity, and remote sensing proxy data. City-scale monitoring networks such as BEACO₂N, when combined with AI-driven data fusion algorithms, are capable of generating high spatiotemporal resolution emission maps. Moreover, dynamic tracking algorithms allow for the identification and temporal analysis of high-emission hotspots [33–36]. The MoreAir system, which integrates random forest (RF), support vector regression (SVR), and multiple linear regression (MLR) models, achieves effective calibration and data fusion even under low-cost operational constraints [27].

(3) Atmospheric inversion optimization. AI can be employed to optimize parameterization and background field estimation in atmospheric inversion models, thereby improving the efficiency and stability of city-scale emission inference [37]. In addition, AI enables the integration of satellite and ground-based sensor data, achieving format harmonization, error correction, and ultimately air-space-ground integrated monitoring [38].

(4) Platform and deployment practices. In smart-city settings, integrated monitoring platforms support automated collection, real-time visualization, and operational management via unified workflows, providing standardized interfaces for AI modules [39]. Several countries and cities, including India, London, and Dubai-have already incorporated IoT sensor networks for carbon monitoring into their air quality infrastructure [38], underscoring the substantial potential of AI-IoT synergy in expanding monitoring coverage, improving responsiveness, and strengthening policy support capabilities.

The integration of AI and sensor networks offers a distinctive advantage by providing near-surface, continuous monitoring data with high spatial and temporal resolution, particularly effective for urban and industrial zone-scale dynamic source tracking. However, this pathway involves higher deployment and maintenance costs, and data quality remains susceptible to hardware performance and network stability. Consequently, it is better suited as a complementary approach for fine-grained monitoring of critical regions, operating in tandem with satellite-based macro-scale monitoring.

It is also important to note that the suitability of monitoring methods depends not only on technical accuracy but also on data availability, spatiotemporal scale mismatches, and governance and regulatory needs. Policy-oriented studies show that when data are incomplete or regional heterogeneity is substantial, relying on a single data source or a single model often fails to ensure robustness and verifiability; in contrast, information integration or hybrid monitoring frameworks that fuse multiple data types and modeling layers are more likely to improve consistency and interpretability across scales [40]. For policy-facing carbon governance, monitoring systems typically need to provide stable and traceable data foundations for subsequent evaluation and decision-making; therefore, monitoring strategies that emphasize structural transparency and multi-source fusion are more practical.

In summary, AI is driving carbon emissions monitoring from “algorithm validation” toward “system deployment”, and research attention is shifting toward robustness and verifiability across scenarios. Future work should advance multi-source fusion and cooperative inversion under unified evaluation conventions and evidence-synthesis protocols. This can enable a reliable monitoring-evaluation loop across scales and provide comparable, auditable data for regulation and policy assessment.

DL model

DL models can capture nonlinearities and spatiotemporal dependencies in emission systems. This section reviews three classical architectures, namely CNN, long short-term memory network (LSTM), and artificial neural network (ANN). It also discusses recent advances in GNN and Transformer-based models for carbon emissions prediction. The aim is to analyze their core application paradigms, performance characteristics, and inherent limitations.

CNN is effective at extracting local spatial and structural patterns and is therefore well suited to prediction tasks where input data contain explicit or implicit spatial features. For example, Yan et al. employeda CNN-based approach to model residential unit layout images and achieved high-accuracy prediction of carbon emissions at the design stage, which showed the capability of CNN to process spatially structured inputs [47]. To capture spatiotemporal dependencies in a coordinated manner, many studies adopt hybrid architectures that embed CNN within more complex pipelines. Typical examples include combining CNN with LSTM or generative adversarial network (GAN) to construct composite models, such CNN-based hybrid models often outperform single-model baselines in complex trend prediction tasks [48, 49]. These applications indicate that CNN serves as a powerful algorithm for carbon emissions prediction problems with spatial structure. However, CNN typically requires structured spatial inputs and sufficient training data, and can be costly to train; practical deployment must balance accuracy and computation. The core advantage of LSTM lies in addressing long-term temporal dependencies, making it suitable for medium-to-long-term carbon emissions trend prediction. In port traffic emission prediction, an integrated framework that combined LSTM with STIRPAT and ARIMAX achieved higher predictive accuracy and stability than single time-series models [50]. In real-time ship emissions prediction, LSTM is capable of modeling the coupled relationships among multiple factors such as vessel speed and meteorological conditions [51]. Overall, LSTM demonstrates strong adaptability to non-stationary sequences and performs particularly well in contexts affected by exogenous shocks such as policy changes or energy structure adjustments. However, LSTM is sensitive to data scale and hyperparameters, and its training cost rises quickly for long sequences. LSTM is also prone to overfitting when data are limited or noisy, leading to noticeable performance degradation in small-sample scenarios.

ANN can flexibly model complex nonlinear interactions among high-dimensional features, demonstrating strong adaptability and learning capacity [52]. For example, in a study on carbon emissions prediction in Turkey, Kayakus et al. showed that ANN outperforms support vector machines and decision trees, highlighting its ability to capture intricate patterns in multi-source datasets [53]. ANN has also been successfully applied in cross-country carbon emissions prediction [54] and in real-time tailpipe emission prediction under heterogeneous traffic conditions [55]. These applications demonstrate that ANN has advantages in handling large datasets and improving prediction performance. ANN is often more efficient than LSTM at similar scale and is relatively lightweight for tabular features compared with CNN. However, ANN is more susceptible to overfitting in small-sample, high-dimensional settings, which necessitates the use of regularization, early stopping, and structural compression techniques.

In addition to the classical architectures discussed above, GNN and Transformer models provide new algorithmic perspectives for capturing more complex cross-scale dependencies in carbon emissions systems. Because emissions exhibit networked interregional linkages, GNN can model node-edge dependencies (e.g., spatial adjacency and socio-economic connections), which helps capture cross-regional propagation mechanisms [56, 57]. Nevertheless, the performance of GNN is highly dependent on the appropriateness of the constructed graph structure and the completeness of node features. When relational information is ambiguous or data are substantially noisy, its generalization ability and stability can deteriorate significantly.

Transformer builds on a self-attention mechanism that enables the effective capture of global dependencies in very long sequences, addressing limitations inherent to LSTM-based algorithms [58]. For example, recent studies integrated graph convolutional networks with Transformer, using the former to encode spatial relationships and the latter to model temporal dependencies. This design simultaneously accounted for geographic proximity and socio-economic dynamics, achieving superior performance to LSTM and CNN in multi-step and multi-region carbon emissions prediction tasks [59]. The attention weights can, to some extent, be used to assess the relative importance of different time periods and variables, offering auxiliary insights into which intervals and drivers predominantly shape emission variations. However, the advantages of Transformer depend heavily on large-scale, high-frequency time-series data and substantial computational resources. In scenarios characterized by data scarcity, short temporal spans, or high noise levels, Transformer often struggles to consistently outperform simpler models, and its training, tuning, and deployment requirements remain comparatively demanding.

Overall, DL methods are capable of modeling the complex spatiotemporal dependencies inherent in carbon emissions systems, but their applicability is constrained by the need for large-scale datasets, substantial computational resources, and their limited interpretability. In data-scarce environments or decision-making contexts that demand high transparency, these limitations become particularly pronounced. In practice, it is advisable to carefully assess the data and computational foundations required for such models and to consider statistical learning approaches or hybrid frameworks that incorporate causal inference as more robust alternatives.

Ensemble learning model

Compared with DL, ensemble learning generalizes well, trains efficiently, and performs robustly on structured data. We focus on RF and XGBoost and discuss their use patterns and suitability in carbon emissions studies.

RF has been widely applied to identify drivers of carbon emissions and to support predictive modeling. Across scales (buildings, commercial sectors, and cities), RF is widely used to learn nonlinear mappings between emissions and structured drivers (e.g., design, operational, and industrial indicators). RF feature-importance analysis helps identify key drivers, supporting green-building optimization, sectoral reduction prioritization, and differentiated urban reduction policy design [60–62]. These studies have showed that RF can satisfy both predictive accuracy and factor-identification needs in contexts where sample sizes are limited and the number of potential drivers is large, making it a suitable foundational tool for empirical carbon emissions research. RF is also resistant to overfitting and can efficiently handle high-dimensional datasets while requiring relatively modest computational resources. However, it is less effective at capturing fine-grained continuous temporal patterns or highly complex feature interactions. Although its predictive accuracy may not match that of some more sophisticated models, the high degree of parallelism in its training process gives it considerable practical potential across a wide range of applications.

XGBoost is particularly effective at extracting statistical regularities from structured data, and has been widely used for carbon emissions prediction due to its efficiency and robustness. It generally achieves higher prediction accuracy than RF on structured tabular data. The algorithm can effectively control overfitting and models heterogeneous features and nonlinear relations effectively, making it well suited to multi-source feature fusion in emissions prediction [17]. Related studies on building carbon emissions prediction and on urban and industrial emission trend modeling suggested that XGBoost can characterize complex driving structures and improve prediction accuracy, and that optimization algorithms can further enhance hyperparameter selection [63–66]. Overall, XGBoost tends to deliver high prediction accuracy and good robustness when applied to medium-sized samples with multiple driving factors. However, its performance depends on careful hyperparameter tuning, and the algorithm is essentially non-temporal, so it requires feature engineering to introduce information on the time dimension.

Ensemble learning models serve as efficient and robust practical tools in carbon emissions prediction. Compared to DL models, their primary advantages lie in computational and deployment efficiency, inherent interpretability, and strong adaptability to small-to-medium-sized datasets with varying quality. However, when the core objective of a prediction task is to uncover deep spatiotemporal patterns from raw unstructured data, their expressive power is generally inferior to that of DL model.

Statistical learning model

Support vector machine (SVM) has been widely used to model the relationships between carbon emissions and multiple influencing factors, due to its nonlinear fitting ability and structural risk minimization principle [18]. For example, studies employing least squares support vector machine (LSSVM) to model CO₂ emissions from sectors such as industry and residential consumption have achieved prediction accuracy superior to that of traditional approaches [67]. Because SVM is sensitive to hyperparameters, many studies combine it with heuristic optimizers such as genetic algorithms (GA) and particle swarm optimization (PSO). These combinations, represented by model such as GA-SVM, have demonstrated stable and effective performance in carbon emissions prediction tasks across transportation [68], construction [69] and urban households [70, 71].

SVM exhibits strong theoretical generalization capability in small-sample and high-dimensional settings. With clear decision boundaries, SVM can be more stable and efficient than DL when data are limited and feature dimensionality is moderate. However, its computational complexity increases sharply with sample size, which limits its applicability in large-scale scenarios. Its performance is also highly dependent on the choice of kernel functions and parameter settings, and it is sensitive to data noise, which places high demands on data preprocessing.

Comparative performance of common AI-based carbon emissions prediction models

In summary, different ML methods for carbon emissions prediction are not simple substitutes in terms of superiority or inferiority, but rather complement each other along dimensions such as data scale, feature structure, and computational constraints. For high-dimensional spatiotemporal and heterogeneous data, when computational resources are sufficient and the focus is on mining complex patterns, DL models can be prioritized. For medium-scale and structured emission accounting data, ensemble learning models usually provide a good balance between prediction accuracy and computational efficiency. In situations where samples are limited and highly transparent evidence is required to support policy making, statistical learning models still hold clear advantages.

Model selection should jointly consider predictive performance, interpretability, and resource consumption, and should be evaluated under realistic data conditions and governance requirements for policy implementation. In data-scarce or long-term prediction tasks, single ML models are often constrained by training sample size and interpretability, whereas ensemble or hybrid prediction frameworks that emphasize structural constraints can achieve a better trade-off between robustness and policy relevance. Particularly in cross-regional and long-timescale prediction tasks, studies emphasized the need to ensure consistent metric conventions and clearly defined scenarios to support cross-region comparability and policy-evaluation usability [72]. Future research should further prioritize the match between “data-scale-governance context” and transferability, and focus on robustness and interpretability alongside accuracy, with modular ensemble or hybrid strategies preferred to improve decision usefulness.

Intelligent emissions reduction in key areas

AI technology is reshaping the carbon emissions reduction mechanism in key areas such as industry, energy, transportation, and buildings by promoting green technology innovation, optimizing resource allocation, and strengthening energy management and process control.

In the industrial field, the primary mechanisms through which AI promotes emissions reduction include green technological innovation, intelligent system upgrades, optimized resource allocation, and sustained improvements in energy efficiency. Related research has shown a significant negative association between digital innovation and regional carbon intensity, with industrial agglomeration serving as an important transmission mechanism [74]. For instance, a study indicated that AI indirectly reduced reliance on high-carbon industries by fostering green technological innovation and industrial upgrading, thereby generating synergistic emission reduction effects [75]. A panel study of 30 Chinese provinces (2013–2021) found that AI development significantly reduced industrial carbon intensity, and the result was robust [76]. Further spatial analysis showed that industrial intelligence not only reduced carbon intensity locally but also produced positive regional spillover effects, enabling surrounding areas to achieve joint emission reductions [77], which provides empirical support for designing cross-regional coordinated low-carbon policies. In practical applications, industrial robots, as an important embodiment of AI, enhance energy efficiency by transforming production processes [78]. From an enterprise perspective, AI and big-data systems can improve energy efficiency and reduce emissions by cutting redundant output and enabling process-level feedback control [79, 80].

In the energy field, AI contributes to emission reduction primarily through three pathways: improving energy efficiency, optimizing structural configurations, and enabling firm-level energy conservation. In terms of energy efficiency enhancement, AI technologies have been widely applied in load prediction, energy system dispatch optimization, and demand-side response modeling. For instance, a study of Nigeria’s power system for 2050 showed that ML (e.g., MLP, XGBoost, SVR) combined with Bayesian optimization (BO) could substantially improve system configuration and scheduling, offering a feasible pathway toward the country’s target of a 50% emissions reduction by 2050 [81]. Such scenario simulations are useful, but results depend strongly on assumptions and parameters; applicability should be assessed in light of local institutions and development stages.

Second, in the area of energy structure adjustment, AI supports the low-carbon transition of energy systems by enabling the efficient integration of clean energy and helping manage its inherent variability. Multiple empirical studies have indicated that the level of AI development is negatively correlated with regional carbon emissions, primarily by improving energy structures and accelerating the diffusion of green technologies [82, 83]. Together, these studies suggest that AI supports both local optimization and longer-term structural shifts in energy systems.

In addition, when it comes to promoting energy conservation and emission reduction at the enterprise level, AI is increasingly embedded in energy management systems and intelligent manufacturing platforms. Through intelligent scheduling, precise sensing, and anomaly detection, AI can substantially enhance both energy efficiency and carbon performance [84–86]. Existing studies in this area often rely on enterprise-level or project-level econometric and optimization models, which are effective in capturing the micro-level linkages between energy use and carbon emissions. Even so, the evaluation of long-term and systemic reduction effects still needs to be better integrated with insights from macro-level panel analyses.

In the transportation field, AI promotes emission reduction primarily through data-driven optimization models and decision-support systems. By collecting and analyzing data on energy consumption, traffic flows, and economic activity, AI provides refined support for formulating emission reduction policies in transport systems [87]. Typical applications include using Bayesian regression models to optimize electric vehicle routes for reduced energy consumption [88]. Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has also been applied to logistics scheduling and vessel trajectory prediction [89]. Recently, Shang et al. proposed a traffic signal control framework based on DRL that integrates cooperative vehicle-infrastructure systems with day-to-day dynamic traffic assignment models. Through road network simulation cases, the study achieved system-level collaborative control, realizing approximately 21%-27% CO₂ emission reductions at the system level [90]. These technologies improve operational efficiency and can scale toward system-level transport reduction across operations, equipment, and behavior. Looking ahead, the integration of AI with smart grid technologies, telematics, and related digital infrastructures is expected to further unlock the reduction potential of the transport sector.

In the construction field, AI demonstrates substantial potential in supporting energy management and carbon emissions control across the entire life cycle of buildings. Its emission reduction effects are mainly reflected in two areas. The first is energy consumption prediction and intelligent control during the operational phase. Accurate ML-based energy modeling supports operations, improves efficiency, and contributes to building reduction. For example, Zhan et al. developed a model predictive control-based energy management framework for buildings, identified the regulation potential of hybrid cooling systems in tropical office buildings, and validated the feasibility of AI algorithms for energy-saving and emission-reduction decision-making within this context [91]. Second, front-end design and regional climate adaptation optimization. Models such as ANN are employed to optimize building orientation and envelope configuration under varying climatic conditions. The Turkey case confirms that AI-based parametric optimization can significantly reduce operational-phase carbon emissions [92]. The AI-driven framework for reducing construction-related carbon emissions is evolving from an emphasis on operational management toward broader integration with front-end design optimization and regional climate adaptation.

From a cross-sectoral perspective, AI-enabled emission reduction applications exhibit significant differences in deployment readiness across key areas. These differences stem from structural heterogeneity in technological foundations, data availability, and institutional environments across sectors. When studying the application of AI in carbon reduction, in addition to focusing on theoretical reduction potential, attention should also be paid to empirically validated actual reduction effects. In particular, it should be noted that actual net reductions are often constrained by multiple demand-side transformation factors. From a system-level decarbonization perspective, rebound effects and governance barriers may weaken or even offset the efficiency gains delivered by AI. For example, recent system-level empirical evidence showed that end-use electrification is often accompanied by rising electricity demand (e.g., broader appliance ownership and smart-home adoption), which can offset efficiency gains, meaning that “efficiency improvement” does not necessarily translate into “net emissions reduction” [93]. Meanwhile, governance and planning studies on net-zero pathways indicated substantial heterogeneity in peaking trajectories and reduction responsibilities across regions and between urban and rural housing; without enabling measures such as standards, financing mechanisms, and a verifiable implementation framework, scaling demand-side reduction in the construction sector and performance auditing will be constrained [94]. Therefore, beyond improving models and optimization performance, future AI-enabled reduction studies should clearly define system-level evaluation boundaries and key assumptions, and incorporate rebound effects and governance barriers into the assessment framework to enhance the verifiability and feasibility of net emissions reduction outcomes.

CCUS technical innovation

CCUS is a key decarbonization option for the energy and industrial sectors. AI-driven carbon capture and management applies AI to optimize carbon capture, storage, and utilization, enabling more accurate modeling and more efficient operation of CCUS systems. These advances support progress toward global carbon neutrality.

In carbon capture, AI is mainly used to improve the performance of key processes such as absorption, adsorption, membrane separation, and chemical cycling [95]. Deep neural networks can predict CO₂ uptake under complex conditions, accelerating adsorbent screening and development. Meanwhile, reinforcement learning algorithms are used to automatically search for optimal operating parameters, improving dynamic response and energy-consumption control of the capture system. In membrane separation and chemical absorption, AI also supports modeling and control-strategy optimization for complex reactions, improving system robustness and reducing operating costs.

The integration of AI enhances carbon utilization efficiency. For instance, employing the multivariate adaptive regression splines (MARS) algorithm to construct high-precision prediction models resolves parameter uncertainties in CO₂ sequestration and enhanced oil recovery within residual oil zones (ROZs). This optimizes operational strategies for CCUS projects and improves field evaluation efficiency, indirectly supporting carbon reduction targets [96]. In conventional CO₂-EOR (enhanced oil recovery) projects, compositional numerical simulation software like CMG-GEM requires extensive computational time per simulation when calculating carbon utilization metrics, failing to meet the computational efficiency demands of multivariable optimization. The application of ML techniques has addressed the high computational costs and low multivariable optimization efficiency of traditional numerical simulations by developing two surrogate models [97].

For carbon transportation and geological storage, AI can enable dynamic behavior prediction and risk warning in the CO₂ transportation process. Specifically, ML models can establish CO₂ migration patterns in underground reservoirs based on parameters such as geological formations, pressure fields, and temperature gradients, and can be used to identify potential leakage channels or seepage risk points [95]. In addition, integrating real-time sensor data with AI-assisted diagnostic techniques can improve long-term monitoring capability, helping ensure environmental safety and reservoir stability [98]. In this process, AI models improve response efficiency and enhance monitoring reliability by reducing human error.

In addition to process optimization, AI also shows great promise in supporting the exploration of new mechanisms and system innovation in CCUS. Based on theoretical simulation and empirical data, an AI-driven carbon interfacial behavior prediction model has been constructed, which can be used to simulate the interfacial tension, diffusion rate, and reaction rate of CO2 in porous media, and then provide accurate parameter support for novel material development and reactor design [99]. From a system perspective, AI optimization of CCUS development is reflected in two dimensions: improved operational performance and enhanced cost-effectiveness. By integrating AI into the planning and evaluation of carbon capture projects, multi-dimensional assessments of project risks, economics, and carbon reduction potential can be achieved, providing data support for investment decisions. Studies have shown that AI-enabled CCUS projects outperform traditional solutions in both system performance and economics, especially when integrated with bioenergy, wind, and nuclear energy systems, which can effectively reduce marginal abatement costs [100]. AI is deeply embedded in key links of the CCUS technology chain. Despite challenges in algorithm reliability, model interpretability, and cross-scenario generalization, AI-enabled CCUS provides solid technical support for closed-loop management from “source carbon reduction” to “process optimization” and “terminal monitoring”, thereby contributing to global carbon neutrality. It also injects new momentum into CCUS deployment and scale-up.

The use of model compression and lightweight deployment techniques provides practical feasibility for implementing AI-driven emission reduction solutions in diverse application scenarios. Due to the computational power, memory, and energy constraints of edge devices, high-complexity models cannot be deployed directly. Techniques such as pruning, quantization, and knowledge distillation enable model compression, and when combined with lightweight deployment strategies, they enable low-latency and energy-efficient operation at the device level. For example, in the power sector, a lightweight edge computing framework based on dynamic network pruning can achieve dynamic compression and real-time prediction on resource-constrained devices. This approach satisfies computational limitations while maintaining prediction accuracy, and it shows potential for extension to edge-based dynamic data processing scenarios in transportation and other sectors [104]. Knowledge distillation using randomized temperature settings can also reduce model complexity substantially while meeting the requirements of mobile and edge devices for low energy consumption and small carbon footprints [105]. In the field of construction, a study employing a lightweight deployment technique centered on the RED WoLF algorithm highlights that rule-based computational simplification can be adapted to edge devices in building energy management systems. This approach achieves approximately 10 to 25% reductions in greenhouse gas emissions [106] and provides a transferable technical paradigm for applications requiring edge deployment, including industrial energy regulation and storage scheduling in transportation systems.

Taken together, AI is emerging as a core technological engine for achieving targeted decarbonization and substantial gains in energy efficiency. By driving emission reduction in key domains such as industry, energy, transport, construction, and CCUS, and by enabling practical implementation through lightweight deployment, AI not only improves reduction performance at individual stages but also facilitates the formation of an intelligent carbon governance system characterized by cross-sectoral coordination and full-chain optimization.

Embedding governance for a practice-oriented AI framework

This study reviews AI research in carbon governance by three task categories, monitoring, prediction, and reduction, and accordingly proposes a practice-oriented AI full-chain analytical framework for decision-making. To enhance the practical applicability of this framework, this section draws on recent application studies in national inventories, MRV, city CAPs, and sectoral transition roadmaps, summarizes key deployment constraints, and proposes output requirements of “audit-ready, decision-ready, and execution-ready” to narrow the gap between methodological innovation and real-world deployment.

In IPCC inventories and MRV, constraints center on auditability, traceability, and consistent accounting. For example, Mosley et al. emphasized that prediction models should be consistent with official greenhouse gas inventory accounting conventions to improve the comparability of carbon sink assessments and the policy usability of MRV systems [107]. Traceable data hardening and access control support third-party review of MRV data and accounting results [108]. While traceable and verifiable activity-data inputs are regarded as prerequisites for improving inventory consistency and verifiability [109]. Accordingly, future MRV implementation studies should target audit-ready delivery by providing quality control and metadata, audit logs, and uncertainty alongside results, and by promoting standardized data interfaces and verification linkage.

The core constraint for city CAPs is decision usability: models should support cross-sectoral, comparable pathway assessments and action sequencing. For example, urban digital twin dashboards can translate building operational emission assessments into intervention priorities and action guidelines [110]. Accordingly, future CAPs research should deliver decision-ready scenario assessments and prioritized action lists, and report the basis for trade-offs under cost, timeline, and equity constraints.

In the context of sectoral transition roadmaps, deployment constraints are more systems-engineering oriented: strategies must be executable and verifiable under safety, real-time, and service-level constraints. Low-carbon scheduling in rail transit incorporates carbon performance into constrained optimization frameworks, minimizing system carbon emissions while satisfying feasibility constraints such as operational and grid safety, demonstrating the importance of system feasibility and trade-off transparency [111]. Studies on charging-infrastructure planning further indicate that multidimensional constraints, such as equity, spatial conditions, and grid hosting capacity, shape plan feasibility and implementation [13]. Therefore, research aimed at sectoral transition roadmap implementation should aim for “execution-ready” deliverables: executable control, scheduling, and planning schemes with quantified carbon performance changes, and establish an operational feedback loop.

The above content supplements the delivery requirements of the framework from an implementation perspective. Section “Challenges and opportunities” further discusses related challenges and opportunities.

Challenges and opportunities

AI technology holds vast application potential in carbon emissions monitoring, prediction, and reduction. However, its practical deployment still faces multifaceted challenges. This review subsequently analyzes the technical bottlenecks and potential solutions for AI in carbon emissions management across three dimensions: the data layer, model layer, and application layer. Data layer

First, data accessibility is poor and data quality is low. For instance, in carbon emissions accounting for the construction industry, data are susceptible to tampering to circumvent regulatory quotas, while third-party data verification lacks independence and transparency. Currently, most systems rely on centralized databases and manual records, where data managed by a single entity can conceal critical information. Furthermore, a significant portion of raw data in the construction sector exists in paper, image, or low-structured sensor formats, making them difficult to usedirectly in AI model training and analysis [112]. This not only affects data preprocessing efficiency but also weakens model training effectiveness and generalization capabilities [113]. Similar data gaps also exist in the transportation sector. For instance, real-time trajectory data for private vehicles are difficult to obtain due to privacy protection, which leads urban transport emission accounting to rely heavily on taxi GPS data. Because taxi data cannot represent private-vehicle-dominated travel, samples are biased in behavior and spatial coverage, which directly limits prediction accuracy [114]. This clearly illustrates the problem of core feature omission caused by data scarcity, which prevents AI models from developing stable and transferable spatiotemporal representations of emissions and hinders their broader application at the urban scale.

Second, data fragmentation hinders the integration and sharing of carbon emissions data. In practice (e.g., supply chains, transport, industry), emissions data are fragmented across systems and organizations, with incompatible interfaces and inconsistent formats [115]. For instance, the construction industry involves multiple stakeholders including material supply, transportation, and construction, resulting in dispersed data sources that are prone to gaps or inconsistencies [112]. Data silos between different departments (e.g., energy, transportation, environmental protection) further complicate integration, creating “data islands” [88]. Additionally, issues like inconsistent data formats between devices, data gaps, and noise weaken the effectiveness of AI algorithms to varying degrees [116]. In industrial carbon emissions research, the lack of data transparency and the need to integrate emissions data across multiple industries and stages make it exceptionally challenging to construct continuous, comprehensive, and auditable time series. This indirectly impacts the deployment of sequence models (such as LSTM) that rely on such data [117].

AI’s reliance on large-scale, multi-source sensitive data also gives rise to new risks related to privacy and fairness. Without proper anonymization, operational and household-related data may expose trade secrets or privacy, reducing sharing willingness and worsening scarcity and sample bias [118].

To address these challenges, blockchain technology offers a potential pathway for building trustworthy and transparent data infrastructure. For example, an AI and blockchain enabled carbon emission ledger system (AB-CELS) uses hash chains and consensus mechanisms to lower the risk of data tampering and to realize decentralized and transparent management across multiple actors. This provides the construction sector with a verifiable and traceable foundation of high-quality carbon data [112]. In carbon monitoring scenarios that involve multiple sensors and multi- stage data collection, consortium blockchain can serve as both a data security layer and a coordination network. It improves the efficiency of data transmission and filtering while safeguarding the quality of inputs for platforms such as digital twins, thereby enhancing the reliability of real-time carbon emissions estimates [119].

Overall, poor data accessibility, fragmentation, and privacy constraints have already been shown in sectors such as construction, transport, and industry to be core obstacles to the implementation of AI driven carbon management. Addressing this weakness at the data layer is a prerequisite for moving from isolated pilot projects to large scale deployment of these technologies.

Model layer

AI models face several challenges at the model layer when processing high-dimensional, multi-source, nonlinear, and spatiotemporally heterogeneous carbon emissions data. First, their high sensitivity to data quality can lead to poor stability and limited generalization in real-world applications [17]. Although mainstream ML methods (e.g., LSTM, CNN) show strong performance in specific experimental settings, their effectiveness heavily depends on sample size, label quality, and feature consistency. Data bias, outliers, and noise can distort predictions or trigger overfitting, and the optimization process may also become trapped in local optima [113, 120].

Second, model parameter determination remains a major optimization challenge. Many algorithms require careful hyperparameter tuning; for example, random forest models typically need predefined settings such as the number of trees (ntree), the minimum node size (nodesize), and the number of features considered at each split (mtry). Improper choices (e.g., overly deep trees) can cause overfitting or underfitting. Too few trees may reduce prediction stability, whereas too many increase computational cost, requiring a trade-off between accuracy and efficiency [60].

Moreover, DL models commonly exhibit “black box” characteristics, making their internal mechanisms difficult to intuitively explain [121, 122]. For instance, ANN can fit complex relationships between carbon emission drivers and emissions levels through multi-layer structures, but their weight distribution and hidden layer interaction mechanisms remain opaque, hindering the identification of specific features (such as energy intensity) that causally influence prediction outcomes [123]. In the transportation sector, the “black box” nature of many AI models makes their decision-making logic difficult to interpret, thereby weakening the trust of traffic managers and policymakers in related technologies. Some performance-driven research has demonstrated significant emission reduction potential at the simulation level, including system-level CO₂ reductions achieved through DRL technology [90]. However, practical deployment remains challenging in the absence of interpretable decision-making mechanisms to justify the rationality of adjustments such as signal timing. A similar problem arises in carbon market prediction. Existing models tend to focus on improving accuracy while neglecting interpretability and hyperparameter optimization. The lack of interpretability prevents stakeholders from understanding the drivers behind prediction outcomes and from adjusting their actions in response to key underlying factors, such as price characteristics or policy impacts. This can reduce acceptance of the models and hinder their practical deployment in real carbon market operations [124].

To address the aforementioned issues, researchers have progressively introduced heuristic optimization algorithms to enhance model robustness and generalization. The preceding discussion has repeatedly cited examples where optimization algorithms were employed to improve model accuracy [61, 68–71]. Such algorithms optimize model performance in complex nonlinear data environments through global search and adaptive parameter tuning. Additional optimization examples include Chiroma et al. employed CS-PSO to optimize ANN construction, enabling adaptive adjustment of neuron counts [125]. Sun et al. utilized PSO to optimize input weights and biases, constructing a PSO-ELM model that outperforms traditional ELM and BP neural networks in prediction accuracy [126]. These methods significantly enhance prediction accuracy, generalization capability, and computational efficiency by optimizing model parameters such as weights and thresholds.

To address poor stability and limited generalization, hybrid modeling is viewed as a more structurally constrained solution. By combining the nonlinear fitting capability of AI with the mechanistic constraints, conservation laws, and dynamic structures of process-based models, hybrid modeling mitigates overfitting and physical inconsistency in data-limited or distribution-shift scenarios, thereby enhancing extrapolation credibility and prediction robustness. For example, knowledge-guided ML frameworks can incorporate process mechanisms into model structure design and training constraints, improving prediction robustness when training samples are limited and reducing physical inconsistencies, thereby enhancing extrapolation credibility [127]. AI-process model hybrid modeling uses the mechanism-consistent temporal dynamics provided by process models as extended training signals, with ML characterizing spatial heterogeneity. This improves prediction accuracy while enhancing the mechanism consistency of results and the credibility and robustness of cross-scenario extrapolation [128]. A recent study on an AI-integrated framework for life cycle assessment (LCA) pointed out that combining AI with Python-based process simulation models and digital twins to bridge process and value chain modeling and interface with environmental impact assessment helps improve assessment efficiency and enhance adaptability to rapidly changing data contexts, as well as the feasibility of practical deployment [129]. Therefore, hybrid modeling that combines AI with process-based models or integrated assessment models can improve model stability and cross-scenario generalization while preserving mechanism consistency, and can also balance assessment efficiency with practical deployment.

In recent years, to further enhance model interpretability and policy transferability, researchers have progressively integrated causal reasoning into predictive frameworks. The DDCFTN model effectively improves the interpretability and transferability of urban carbon emissions prediction by combining decision trees, feature interactions, and causal reasoning [130]. Another study employs a holistic framework encompassing causal screening (Lasso), causal modeling (CNN-BiLSTM), causal optimization, and causal interpretation (SHAP) (see Fig. 8), achieving a closed-loop system that bridges “data to decision-making and prediction to explanation” [131]. By embedding XAI techniques, this system quantifies and visualizes causal mechanisms, overcoming the “black-box dilemma” of traditional DL models. It enables prediction systems not only to “predict” but also to “explain why they predict”, providing a reusable end-to-end paradigm for high-reliability decision-making in carbon trading markets.

A schematic diagram of the explainable prediction framework, integrating descriptive, predictive, optimization and explanatory steps [131]

To enhance interpretability, researchers have introduced explainable AI (XAI) to reveal how models generate predictions, thereby turning black-box outputs into an evidence trail that can be incorporated into policy and governance processes. Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) and local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME) are common representative methods. Based on this, Chen et al. proposed the REED hybrid encoder-decoder model and embedded SHAP into the prediction framework to achieve interpretable multi-step prediction of building carbon emission time series. The explanation results revealed key influencing factors and their contribution differences across time periods, providing traceable bases for time-segmented operation control and emissions reduction strategy formulation [132]. In addition, scholars have combined SHAP values with model visualization to enhance interpretation transparency across three dimensions: feature importance, nonlinear relationships, and interaction effects. In particular, by revealing key thresholds and interaction mechanisms, policy recommendations are no longer limited to “which factors are important”, but can also answer “under which condition combinations are they effective”, thereby enhancing the defensibility of policy arguments and the verifiability after implementation [133].

From a policy application perspective, XAI outputs not only improve model transparency but also help explain the key drivers of emissions changes, assess sensitivity to policy-parameter adjustments, and provide an auditable evidence trail for implementation, such as data and model versions and assumption boundaries, thereby reducing uncertainty and risk in policy design and execution. This increased transparency helps policymakers understand the model’s decision process, strengthens trust among the public and decision makers, and supports third-party verification and accountability, ultimately enhancing policy credibility and accountability.

Application layer

Insufficient technological maturity and engineering implementation challenges

Current AI models still exhibit shortcomings in adaptability to complex operating conditions and cross-scenario transferability. Taking post-combustion carbon capture (PCC) as an example, ANN models must operate stably under wide-range conditions such as CO₂ capture rates of 55%-95% and flue gas flow rates of 500–600 kg/s. However, constrained by insufficient coverage of out-of-distribution conditions in initial training data, these models exhibit weak robustness and generalization capabilities, severely limiting their engineering applicability and predictive accuracy [101]. The maturity of AI technologies still requires continuous improvement to facilitate the transition from laboratory research to real-world deployment.

Engineering challenges associated with AI model deployment span technical, operational, and organizational dimensions. Issues such as infrastructure and data-management incompatibility, difficulties in automating and generalizing deployment pipelines, the lack of standardized toolchains, inefficient cross-team collaboration, and shortages of interdisciplinary talent collectively raise the barrier for transforming “laboratory models” into “production-grade systems” [134]. Reinforcement learning (RL) provides a typical example: while experimental settings allow high-risk exploration through isolated simulation platforms, real energy and industrial systems rely exclusively on production-site data. To avoid safety hazards, agents are essentially prohibited from active exploration, resulting in extremely low data variance and coverage of only narrow state spaces. Consequently, RL agents struggle to learn robust policies for edge or abnormal operating conditions [135].

In advancing the transition toward practical deployment, several safeguards have been implemented. Platforms such as AWS SageMaker and TensorFlow TFX support comprehensive workflow automation by integrating data storage, model hosting, system monitoring and related functions, thereby alleviating operation and maintenance burdens. Weak-supervision frameworks, exemplified by Snorkel, help mitigate the challenge of missing labels in real-world settings. Moreover, adopting a data-oriented construction (DOA) in place of microservice-based designs enhances data traceability and enables more effective workflow optimization. The ML Technology Readiness Level (MLTRL) framework further provides structured quality criteria for each development stage, ensuring alignment with the unique demands of deployment [136]. Sustainable and scalable AI adoption becomes possible only when technological adaptation, engineering deployment, and compliance verification operate as an integrated and self-reinforcing process [137].

Disparities in regional and sectoral adaptability

The emission-reduction effects of AI show clear heterogeneity across regions and industries. Existing studies suggest that AI suppresses carbon-emission intensity more strongly in high-GDP and coastal cities, whereas the effect is relatively limited in low-GDP and inland cities [138]. Cross-national research by Cao et al. also indicates that countries geographically close to AI technology leaders, with lower income levels and higher dependence on fossil fuels, exhibit stronger AI-driven emissions reduction effects. At the sectoral level, AI generally achieves superior emission reduction performance in energy management compared to industrial manufacturing and dispatch [83]. These disparities underscore the difficulty of adapting AI solutions to different development stages, infrastructure conditions, and industrial structures.

Han et al. proposed a solution strategy of “tailored approaches based on local conditions”, with the core logic being that “adapting to regional endowments and avoiding a one-size-fits-all approach” reduces disparity issues [76]. Scenario-based assessments under emission-standard constraints can also provide quantitative support for differentiated regional emission-reduction strategies [139]. For instance, at the sectoral level, a machine-learning-based scenario prediction study of railway carbon emissions showed that considering regional operating conditions and energy structures could support the design of differentiated reduction pathways [140]. Additionally, spatial similarity matrices and geographic adjacency matrices can enhance a model’s transferability across heterogeneous regions [57]. Future work should adopt tiered governance: on one hand, strengthening computational and data infrastructure in resource-constrained areas while promoting standardized data governance and research on small-sample, weakly supervised algorithms; on the other hand, establishing scenario-based application catalogs and quantifiable emission reduction benchmark systems in key industries to improve system implementation effectiveness.

Economic cost constraints and the rebound effect of energy consumption

The high initial investment and operational costs are key factors hindering the widespread adoption of AI technology. Deploying AI monitoring systems (such as high-density sensor networks and dedicated satellite data processing platforms) or implementing intelligent emission reduction solutions (such as process optimization and building energy management systems) often requires substantial expenditures on hardware procurement, software development, and system integration. For small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) and developing countries, such investments may be prohibitively expensive. Surveys indicate that many companies cite “excessively high initial investment” as the primary barrier to adopting AI technologies [141, 142]. To address this, governments can implement policies such as equipment subsidies and tax incentives to alleviate the initial economic burden of green transformation for businesses. Meanwhile, targeted transition finance, flexible carbon allowance allocation, and incentives linked to verified abatement outcomes can further ease short-term financial pressures and support the low-carbon transformation of high-carbon enterprises [143].

With the rapid iteration of AI models, reflected in exploding parameter scales and exponentially increasing computational demand, the energy consumption and carbon emissions generated during computation can no longer be treated as negligible marginal costs. One study quantified the carbon emissions of 79 well-known AI systems released between 2020 and 2024, and found that for the top 20 systems the estimated annual total carbon footprint can reach 102.6 million tons of CO₂ equivalent [144]. In particular, innovative AI systems tend to exhibit a carbon-increasing effect in their early stages, as algorithm training and data storage require large amounts of energy. Once their development surpasses a certain threshold, however, long-term large-scale effects can lead to net emission reductions, giving rise to an overall inverted U-shaped relationship [145]. At the global scale, the direct environmental cost of AI still accounts for a relatively small share of total emissions at present, but it shows a clear upward trend [146].

Therefore, the trade-off between the application of AI technology and the resulting increase in energy consumption is, in essence, a problem of finding a new equilibrium between “emission reduction benefits” and “emission costs”, rather than a simple binary opposition. Existing studies indicate that the environmental cost of AI is no longer negligible. AI’s benefits can be retained while controlling its footprint through efficient hardware, optimized architectures, cleaner energy, lightweight deployment, and emissions caps, aligning with “net-zero” goals [144, 145].

Social acceptance and privacy security concerns

The promotion of AI in carbon management is constrained by both public perception and data privacy concerns. AI systems rely on large-scale data collection and sharing, involving corporate operational data, energy consumption information, and even individual behavioral data, which has raised widespread worries about data privacy and security [118, 120]. Ensuring security and compliance throughout data collection, storage, analysis, and sharing, while preventing the leakage and misuse of sensitive information, is crucial for building user trust and driving technological adoption.

Privacy techniques such as federated learning are providing solutions for this challenge. This mechanism replaces “data sharing” with “parameter sharing”, enabling “zero-disclosure” collaborative training that effectively balances data security and value extraction.

In industrial emission prediction, for example, the VF-LSTMSA (vertical federated LSTM with a self-attention mechanism) framework, which integrates vertical federated learning with homomorphic encryption, has indicated the feasibility of this paradigm. In power and steel industry scenarios, its prediction accuracy differs only marginally from that of centralized models (with MAPE discrepancies generally below 1%), while substantially reducing data transmission and governance costs. This validates that “privacy protection” and “model performance” are not inherently conflicting objectives [147]. The framework is illustrated in Fig. 9. Furthermore, by incorporating SARIMA-based clustering into federated training with LSTM models, the framework leverages similarities in temporal structures across enterprises to accelerate convergence and improve training efficiency. Overall, its performance surpasses that of conventional MLP and vanilla LSTM model, indicating that collaborative training under privacy constraints can still be significantly enhanced through structured modeling strategies [148]. In “power-carbon” relational modeling, federated LSTM architectures likewise exhibit stable temporal representation capabilities, confirming that traditional ML models and federated mechanisms can be mutually complementary, achieving high predictive accuracy while preserving data privacy [149].

Their core value lies not merely in improving the accuracy of individual models, but in establishing a sustainable institutional foundation for data collaboration across enterprises and sectors.

In summary, with the continuous advancement of next-generation AI model architectures, explainability algorithms, and privacy protection mechanisms, overcoming the aforementioned bottlenecks has become technically feasible. Against the backdrop of coordinated evolution in data, models, and application policies, AI holds promise to address complex carbon emissions challenges while driving systemic restructuring of low-carbon governance models. Emerging trends in future research

Interdisciplinary integration: an intelligent path from data silos to collaborative governance

Interdisciplinary integration is becoming a key pathway for advancing carbon emissions management. With its robust data processing and modeling capabilities, AI is increasingly integrated into environmental science, economics, and sociology, enabling intelligent solutions for emissions monitoring, prediction, and reduction.

In the integration of environmental science and AI, integrating AI with atmospheric chemistry and remote sensing improves carbon-source analysis. For example, panel models using nighttime-light data can estimate urban emissions, showing that multi-source integration improves spatial resolution [150]. Practical experience from China’s carbon emissions monitoring platforms further indicates that building AI-driven, real-time monitoring systems based on multi-source heterogeneous data can help break down “data silos” across departments and regions, thereby strengthening governmental capacity to perceive and respond to dynamic changes in carbon emissions [39]. Meanwhile, ML methods such as XGBoost have been used to uncover the nonlinear relationships between natural factors and carbon sinks, providing an operational technical pathway for quantifying ecological carbon sequestration potential [151]. These integrations make observations quantifiable and provide verifiable data to identify hotspots and evaluate natural and engineered sinks. The integration of AI and environmental science modeling also provides a transferable data foundation and verification methods for optimizing the operation of high-emission systems. Taking aviation operation optimization as an example, research has suggested that integrating real-time trajectory adjustments with ML-based optimal descent profile predictions could adapt to dynamic traffic conditions and further reduce fuel consumption and carbon emissions [152]. Overall, interdisciplinary integration supports a closed loop of monitoring, assessment, optimization, and verification for low-carbon governance.

In the integration of economics and AI, research attention has shifted toward disentangling the complex socioeconomic drivers of carbon emissions and simulating and optimizing market-based policies. Studies suggest that after the digital economy passes a maturity threshold, net environmental benefits accelerate and digital technologies contribute more strongly to carbon reduction [153]. AI provides an important simulation-based experimental environment for the design and evaluation of economic policy instruments such as carbon markets. For example, Zhang et al. pointed out that AI-based data trading platforms can improve the allocation efficiency of carbon emissions allowances and data assets, thereby overcoming the limitations of traditional governance models [154]. Taken together, integrating AI with economics promotes dynamic simulation and market-mechanism optimization for more adaptive climate governance frameworks.

In the integration of sociology and AI, real-time social data has been proven to effectively characterize carbon footprint features at fine spatiotemporal scales. For instance, research based on public transportation smart card data conducted spatiotemporal analysis of public transportation system carbon footprints by characterizing urban travel behavior, thereby validating the application value of social behavior data in carbon emissions research [155]. Building upon this foundation, such behavior-driven data can further serve as input variables for training carbon footprint prediction models at individual or group levels, thereby supporting precision-based policy formulation. For example, integrated approaches combining GIS with ML algorithms (e.g., CART) can identify spatial distribution patterns of high-carbon individuals, providing support for community-level carbon reduction policies [156]. In addition, AI technology is driving the digital transformation of the service industry, optimizing resource allocation within the tertiary sector and indirectly promoting the low-carbon evolution of industrial structures [157].

It is evident that the AI-driven interdisciplinary integration is reshaping carbon emissions management. This convergence effectively bridges data gaps in traditional monitoring while enhancing modeling accuracy and predictive capabilities. By leveraging data as the connecting element and intelligent algorithms as the driving force, interdisciplinary innovation delivers systematic solutions that are both scientifically sound and practically applicable for the global low-carbon transition. It also establishes a methodological foundation for subsequent exploration of more complex carbon emissions governance scenarios.

Intelligent decision support systems: from data-driven to human-AI collaboration

At the governmental level, the generation and scaling of multi-agent systems (MAS), LLMs, and generative AI are accelerating the development of AI-assisted decision support systems.

At the level of carbon-market simulation and policy optimization, Guo et al. and Zhou et al. used multi-agent DRL to evaluate system performance under different trading rules and price signals [158, 159]. Their results demonstrate the effectiveness of MAS in integrated power-carbon-green-certificate settings and multi-microgrid joint energy-carbon trading scenarios. MAS enable the simulation of strategic learning and systematic policy comparison, offering policymakers novel tools for rule design and incentive structuring. However, most existing studies remain confined to the simulation stage. Therefore, within a foreseeable time horizon, further efforts should focus on actively exploring MAS as auxiliary tools at the governmental level to facilitate their transition from experimental modeling to practical implementation.

At the level of policy interpretation, LLMs can parse complex texts to extract core provisions, policy instruments, and constraints, and map them to standardized policy-taxonomy frameworks [160–162]. This approach reduces the costs associated with policy review and impact assessment while enhancing cross-regional or cross-sectoral comparability and timeliness. By relying on such models, policymakers can undertake more targeted policy adjustments and optimization designs based on a more holistic understanding of existing policy portfolios and their historical implementation outcomes.

At the level of emissions reduction scenario simulation, generative AI provides new technical means for constructing and expanding reduction pathways. Reviews on the application of generative AI in climate research indicate that LLMs and other generative tools can accelerate knowledge synthesis and scenario formulation, thereby indirectly improving the real-world relevance of reduction scenarios [163]. Recent studies further show that scenario databases derived from integrated assessment models can be used to train generative DL models, enabling the efficient generation of large-scale synthetic sets of reduction pathways. These generated pathways preserve key structural relationships, facilitating in-depth analysis of the drivers of reduction trajectories and providing an important complement to traditional integrated assessment models [164].

At the enterprise level, intelligent decision systems are evolving from tools focused primarily on prediction and recommendation toward instruments for operational optimization and risk management that explicitly account for carbon constraints. Existing studies have already validated the feasibility of applying constrained reinforcement learning and multi-agent reinforcement learning to enterprise-level carbon-constrained operational decision-making within simulated supply chain and energy system settings. For example, the DOPPOL framework proposed by Wang et al. explicitly models carbon allowance constraints as a constrained Markov decision process and jointly optimizes ordering and carbon trading strategies in a virtual supply chain environment. By balancing business costs and environmental costs, this approach significantly enhances profit levels while complying with emissions constraints [165].

Overall, in the foreseeable future, a more realistic trajectory lies in gradually embedding MAS, LLMs, and generative AI into governmental and corporate carbon governance processes under institutional constraints and rigorous validation frameworks, thereby forming intelligent decision-making systems characterized by human-machine collaboration.

Carbon emission trading market: Intelligent trading and regulation

Since the European Union pioneered the establishment of the EU Emissions Trading System (EU-ETS) in 2005, the global carbon market has rapidly expanded, becoming a vital pillar for advancing the green transition. AI technologies are gradually permeating all key segments of carbon markets, with their enabling applications expanding from price prediction to broader domains such as risk management and market regulation. This evolution is driving carbon markets toward a hybrid model characterized by intelligent prediction, positioning of decision support, and refined regulatory oversight.

Regarding carbon price prediction, in response to the nonlinear and non-stationary characteristics of carbon prices, relevant models have evolved from the early ARIMA-LSSVM ensemble model [166] and MLP neural networks [167], to multi-scale ensemble models integrating empirical mode decomposition, LSSVM, and LSTM [168–170], as well as ensemble learning systems incorporating 48 model combinations [171], and an adaptive hybrid prediction framework based on periodic reconstruction and RF algorithms [172]. More recently, CEEMDAN (complete ensemble empirical mode decomposition with adaptive noise)-Attention-RNN ensemble deep-learning models have also been applied to regional carbon market price prediction [173]. These studies indicate that carbon price prediction models are continuously evolving in terms of structural design, feature selection, and optimization strategies. Overall, existing studies have substantially improved the accuracy and stability of carbon price prediction. However, considerable room for improvement remains in accommodating unstructured factors such as sudden policy shocks and sentiment fluctuations, as well as in systematically evaluating the applicability and transferability of different models across multi-regional and multi-stage carbon markets.

In the domains of risk management and market regulation, AI has also demonstrated significant value. Based on dynamic spillover network analysis, ML models can provide timely and effective prediction and early warning of climate risk indices and cross-market linkages, offering quantitative tools for regulators to monitor carbon price volatility and identify cross-market contagion and systemic risks. This, in turn, supported more forward-looking price interventions and macroprudential regulation [174]. In carbon data anomaly detection, some studies employed ML to generate ensemble anomaly scores and allocated verification resources in a tiered manner, enabling efficient identification of data misreporting and manipulation. Robustness tests showed high detection accuracy for tampered data and high-risk anomalies, improving emissions trading systems (ETS) data verification efficiency [175]. Looking ahead, it is necessary to embed these algorithms into real regulatory processes for pilot evaluation, gradually establishing intelligent regulatory systems that are auditable and accountable.

Conclusion

This review adopts a practice-oriented full-chain “monitoring-prediction-reduction” framework and establishes unified evaluation metrics for each stage to comprehensively synthesize and assess research progress and practical applications of AI in carbon emissions management. Building on this framework, it further integrates evidence aggregation matrices, deployment-constraint analysis, and TRL-based assessment to connect methodological performance, application readiness, and governance needs. The findings indicate that the role of AI is shifting from improving the performance of individual models toward forming an integrated methodological system that connects carbon data acquisition, emissions prediction, reduction assessment, and governance applications. By linking AI-driven methods with MRV requirements, policy relevance, and practical intervention pathways, this review provides methodological support for quantifiable, verifiable, and actionable carbon balance and management, while further supporting low-carbon transition and carbon governance practices under carbon neutrality goals.

In monitoring applications, AI integrates satellite remote sensing and sensor network data to establish intelligent monitoring networks that span multiple scales and heterogeneous environments, significantly enhancing the accuracy and update frequency of carbon emission data. In prediction modeling, ML algorithms have overcome the limitations of traditional linear models in modeling non-stationary sequences and multi-factor coupled systems. These methods better capture long-term trends, seasonal cycles, and abrupt changes driven by time-varying conditions and complex emission determinants, leading to higher prediction accuracy. Meanwhile, AI has enabled the exploration of intelligent emission reduction pathways in critical domains such as industrial process optimization, energy system allocation, transportation scheduling, construction energy efficiency control, and CCUS.

Despite its promise, large-scale deployment remains constrained by data (quality, fragmentation, sensitivity), models (generalization, hyperparameter sensitivity, limited interpretability), and applications (maturity, cost, and acceptance). The development of technologies such as blockchain, heuristic optimization algorithms, hybrid modeling, causal inference frameworks, XAI, federated learning, MAS, LLMs, generative AI, and lightweight deployment strategies provides viable pathways for addressing these challenges. To further advance AI-driven carbon emissions research, future efforts should focus on interdisciplinary collaborations, intelligent decision-making, and the intelligence of carbon trading markets.

Although this review synthesizes existing applications and advances of AI in carbon emissions management, it has several limitations. First, discussion remains insufficient on cross-sector collaboration and system-level integrated applications, particularly on how to achieve seamless integration and synergistic use of AI across departments and sectors. Second, research on the adaptability and flexibility of AI applications under different policy contexts is limited, which poses challenges for diffusion and adoption given regional heterogeneity, sector-specific needs, and policy frameworks. Third, the linkage between AI methods and practical governance needs is underexplored, especially regarding policy design, incentive mechanisms, and implementation.

In response to the above limitations, future work will focus on the following areas: (1) constructing and optimizing cross-sectoral coordination mechanisms, exploring how to achieve AI technology integration and application under different policy environments and industry needs, and developing standardized collaboration frameworks and interfaces; (2) deploying AI for policy consistency, developing AI models that support multi-scenario policy alignment, with particular emphasis on strengthening policy target alignment and evaluation systems; (3) studying the coupling and embedding of AI with policy instruments, promoting the deep integration of AI technology with policy implementation in policy scenarios such as carbon markets, city CAPs, and sectoral emission reduction roadmaps, and ensuring the closed-loop pathway of monitoring-prediction-decision-execution-verification is fully connected.
