Development of gemcitabine-modified multimodal miR-129 mimic as a novel therapeutic in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Erick M. Intriago, Amartya Pal, Anushka Ojha, Mingrui Cui, Ilan Y. Pesselev, Nicolas Coant, Isabel Guo, John Haley, Jingfang Ju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Resistance to chemotherapy and tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI)-based therapy is the main reason for treatment failure in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop novel strategies to combat resistance. In this study, we designed and developed a novel, multi-targeted microRNA (miRNA) approach to overcome resistance to EGFR inhibitors. Specifically, the guide strand of the tumor suppressor miR-129 was modified by replacing cytidine with the nucleoside analog gemcitabine (Gem). The therapeutic potential of Gem-miR-129 was investigated in both in vitro and in vivo NSCLC model systems. Our results demonstrate that Gem-miR-129 is a potent inhibitor, with an IC50 in the low nanomolar range in NSCLC cell lines. Gem-miR-129 retains same nanomolar activity in both erlotinib- and osimertinib-resistant NSCLC cell lines. This potency is achieved through the dual mechanisms of the multi-targeted tumor suppressor miR-129, which suppresses HMGB1, YAP1, and PBX3, in conjunction with the expected therapeutic contribution of Gem following degradation of Gem-miR-129. A unique feature of Gem-miR-129 is its vehicle-free uptake in cultured NSCLC cells. Gem-miR-129 effectively inhibits NSCLC growth in a mouse model and significantly prolongs survival without overt toxicity under the tested conditions. Overall, Gem-miR-129 provides proof-of-concept for a novel tumor-suppressor miRNA-based therapeutic strategy for NSCLC and for overcoming TKI resistance.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths in the United States.1 Lung cancer is generally classified into two main types, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer, with NSCLC accounting for ∼85% of diagnosed cases.1 Several treatment strategies have been developed to treat patients with NSCLC, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy. Recent advancements in targeted therapies have improved survival for a subset of patients with NSCLC harboring specific oncogenic driver mutations. In particular, NSCLC patients with specific mutations in the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) are sensitive to EGFR blockade with specific tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) such as erlotinib and gefitinib.2,3 However, despite the initial good response to TKIs, approximately half of these cancers acquire resistance, most commonly through the emergence of secondary mutations in EGFR (such as T790M) or through another resistance mechanism such as mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor (MET) receptor tyrosine kinase amplification.4 Osimertinib was subsequently developed to address resistance mediated by the EGFR T790M mutation. Unfortunately, patients treated with osimertinib eventually developed new resistance mechanisms. Despite the advancement of therapeutics to treat patients diagnosed with NSCLC, the 5-year survival rate after metastasis remains low (26%).4 Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop new therapeutics that can overcome resistance to cancer therapies in NSCLC. Tremendous efforts have been dedicated to investigating the mechanisms of resistance and to developing novel strategies capable of overcoming chemotherapy and TKI resistance.

Non-coding RNAs, including microRNAs (miRNAs) are frequently dysregulated in cancer and contribute to cancer progression and therapeutic resistance by modulating the expression of multiple target genes and key signaling pathways.5 The multimodal polypharmacology afforded by targeting miRNA function offers significant advantages over traditional single-target therapeutic approaches, including the ability to modulate complex and compensatory signaling networks and to impact targets that are not druggable by conventional means.5,6,7 Among these, the tumor suppressor miR-129 has emerged as a critical regulator of NSCLC pathogenesis and therapeutic response. miR-129 expression is frequently downregulated in NSCLC tumors and is associated with enhanced tumor aggressiveness, poor prognosis, and resistance to chemotherapy and targeted therapies. Restoration of miR-129 expression suppresses tumor growth, inhibits migration and invasion, and promotes apoptosis by coordinately targeting multiple oncogenic and survival-related factors, including SOX4, DLK1, BCL2, HMGB1, and PBX3.8,9,10,11,12,13 Notably, several of these targets are involved in pathways implicated in drug resistance, autophagy, and evasion of apoptosis. YAP1 has also emerged as an important resistance-associated effector, with reported roles in EGFR inhibitor tolerance and drug-persister phenotypes in lung cancer, as well as prior evidence supporting its regulation by miR-129 in ovarian cancer.14,15,16 Together, this suggests that miR-129 restoration can reprogram malignant signaling networks rather than merely inhibiting a single oncogenic node. This ability to simultaneously suppress diverse resistance-associated oncogenic drivers and pathways highlights miR-129 as a particularly attractive therapeutic candidate for overcoming treatment resistance and restoring drug sensitivity in NSCLC.

In this study, we designed a gemcitabine (Gem)-modified miR-129 mimic (Gem-miR-129) by replacing all the cytidine (C) residues in the guide strand of miR-129 with the nucleoside analog, Gem. The passenger strand was left unmodified to avoid potential off-target effects. Using this approach, we combine the therapeutic power of Gem, which is a key chemotherapy drug used in NSCLC, with the tumor suppressor miR-129 into a single entity, enabling coordinated suppression of multiple oncogenes, including HMGB1, YAP1, and PBX3. Following intracellular uptake, Gem-miR-129 is expected to undergo intracellular degradation and potentially releasing Gem within the target cells, potentially providing an additional therapeutic contribution. Another unique feature of Gem-miR-129 is that it can be internalized by NSCLC cells without the need for an exogenous delivery vehicle. This is primarily due to the enhanced lipophilicity conferred by two 2′-fluorine groups on the ribose ring of Gem. We expect that this unique design of multimodal tumor suppressor miR-129 will enhance its therapeutic efficacy, stability, and ease of delivery.

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of Gem-miR-129, we conducted in vitro and in vivo experiments across a panel of NSCLC models, including KRAS-driven parental lines A549 and Calu-1, EGFR-mutant parental lines PC9 and HCC827, and their erlotinib- and osimertinib-resistant (OR) derivatives, with in vivo efficacy assessed in a luciferase-labeled A549 tail-vein xenograft model. Our results show that Gem-miR-129 significantly inhibits proliferation and induces apoptosis in NSCLC cell lines A549, PC-9, and Calu-1. We also discovered that Gem-miR-129 retains its function as a miR-129 mimic by reducing the expression of HMGB1, PBX3, and YAP1. Furthermore, Gem-miR-129 was able to overcome resistance to TKIs such as erlotinib-resistant (ER) and OR cells. More importantly, we observed that the inhibitory effect of Gem-miR-129 can also be achieved in NSCLC cells under vehicle-free conditions, a significant advancement in nucleic acid-based cancer therapy. Finally, Gem-miR-129 significantly inhibited NSCLC tumor growth and extended survival in vivo without any noticeable side effects. Altogether, our results demonstrate the potential of Gem-miR-129 as a novel therapeutic agent for NSCLC, exhibiting distinctly superior anti-tumor efficacy compared to conventional chemotherapy and TKIs by efficiently inducing apoptosis and cell-cycle arrest, thereby resensitizing drug-resistant NSCLC cells. These data show that Gem-miR-129 is a promising therapeutic candidate for treating NSCLC that can overcome various resistance mechanisms and improve patients’ quality of life.

Results

Gem-miR-129 exhibits low-nanomolar potency across NSCLC cell lines and retains activity in erlotinib and OR models

The anti-proliferative effect of Gem-miR-129 was evaluated across various NSCLC cell lines utilizing the water-soluble tetrazolium salt 1 (WST-1) cell viability assay. Treatment with Gem-miR-129 under vehicle-free conditions resulted in a significant decrease in the number of viable cells across all examined NSCLC models (Figure 1). Gem-miR-129 induces apoptosis in NSCLC and TKIs resistant NSCLC cells

Apoptosis induced by Gem-miR-129 treatment was quantified using Annexin V/PI flow cytometry 72 h after treatment (Figure 2). A preliminary time course experiment in Calu-1 cells showed that apoptosis induction by Gem-miR-129 was most pronounced at 72 h post-treatment, consistent with the time required for miRNA-mediated target suppression to translate into a functional phenotype (Figure S1). At 50 nM, Gem-miR-129 treatment caused significant increase in apoptotic cells across all NSCLC lines compared to Gem or unmodified miR-129 treatment. Representative Annexin V/propidium iodide flow cytometry plots show that Gem-miR-129 (50 nM) induces a greater apoptotic population than unmodified miR-129 or Gem alone in Calu-1, A549, and HCC827 erlotinib and OR cells (Figure 2A). In A549 cells, Gem-miR-129 treatment caused around 15-fold increase in apoptotic cells compared to the negative control treated group (p < 0.001) (Figure 2B). Calu-1 cells, which lack functional p53 and are usually resistant to chemotherapy, also had significant increase in apoptosis following Gem-miR-129 treatment (about 3-fold, p < 0.05) (Figure 2C). More importantly, in the ER HCC827ER cells, Gem-miR-129 treatment was able to induce about 4.5-fold increased apoptosis despite erlotinib resistance (Figure 2D) as compared to the control cells (p < 0.05). A similar impact of Gem-miR-129 on apoptosis was observed in OR HCC827OR cell line, where Gem-miR-129 elicited ∼3-fold increase in apoptosis as compared to control cells (p < 0.05), (Figure 2E). These results together show that Gem-miR-129 effectively induces apoptosis in both parental and TKI-resistant NSCLC cell lines. Gem-miR-129 triggers S-phase cell-cycle arrest

To further elucidate the effect of Gem-miR-129 treatment on cell proliferation, the cell-cycle distribution of NSCLC cell lines was analyzed 72 h post treatment by propidium iodide staining followed by flow cytometry. Overall, Gem-miR-129 significantly disrupted cell-cycle regulation, as shown by reduction in the G2/S ratio suggesting accumulation of cells in S phase (Figure 3). This effect was substantially greater than that observed with either unmodified miR-129 or Gem alone at equimolar concentrations. For example, in A549 cells, Gem-miR-129 treatment reduced the G2/S ratio from 1.0 in control cells to ∼0.03, representing an approximately 30-fold decrease (p < 0.0001), whereas Gem alone reduced the ratio to ∼0.3 (approximately 3-fold reduction) (Figure 3A). A similar pattern of cell-cycle disruption was consistently observed in Calu-1 cells also, where Gem-miR-129 treatment resulted in a >5.5-fold reduction in the G2/S ratio (p < 0.01) (Figure 3B). In ER HCC827ER cells, Gem-miR-129 induced an approximately 30-fold reduction in the G2/S ratio (p < 0.0001) (Figure 3C), while in OR HCC827OR cells, treatment resulted in an approximately 2-fold reduction in the G2/S ratio (p < 0.05) (Figure 3D). Collectively, these findings showed that Gem-miR-129 reduced G2/S ratio to cause replication stress-associated cell death in both parental and TKI-resistant NSCLC cell lines. Gem-miR-129 inhibits protein expression of oncogenic target genes HMGB1, PBX3, and YAP1 in NSCLC cells

To assess whether Gem-miR-129 retains activity against key miR-129-associated oncogenic targets, the protein levels of miR-129 target genes (HMGB1, YAP1, and PBX3) were quantified by western blotting (Figure 4). Gem-miR-129 significantly decreased HMGB1 protein levels in both Calu-1 and A549 cells compared to the negative control (scramble mimic), with densitometry analysis showing around 60%–70% knockdown. This was consistent with the reduction by the native miR-129. However, Gem treatment alone did not significantly alter HMGB1 protein expression (Figure 4A). We noted similar tendencies for the additional oncogenic proteins examined. PBX3 (Pre-B cell leukemia transcription factor 3), an oncogenic transcription factor associated with NSCLC proliferation and invasion, was significantly reduced in Gem-miR-129 treated Calu-1 and A549 cells (about 50%), analogous to the effect observed with miR-129 mimic alone (Figure 4B). Gem-miR-129 also lowered YAP1 (Yes-associated protein 1), which is a downstream effector of the Hippo pathway that is known to enhance cell proliferation and drug resistance when it is overexpressed. Densitometric analysis across three experiments confirmed significant knockdown of YAP1 in Gem-miR-129-treated A549 and Calu-1 cells compared with control cells (p < 0.01; Figure 4C). At the matched 50 nM dose, unmodified miR-129 showed a weaker effect than Gem-miR-129 in Figure 4C. However, dose-response analysis confirmed that unmodified miR-129 can significantly suppress YAP1 at higher concentrations, with Gem-miR-129 producing the strongest suppression at 50 nM (Figure S3). RNA-seq analysis supports miR-129- and Gem-like global activity of Gem-miR-129

To assess whether Gem-miR-129 produced unexpected transcriptomic changes beyond its parent miR-129 and Gem components, we performed exploratory RNA sequence (RNA-seq) analysis in A549 cells treated with negative control, parent miR-129, Gem, or Gem-miR-129. Gene-level modeling showed that the observed Gem-miR-129 transcriptional response was strongly predicted by the combined parent miR-129 plus Gem response, with a model R2 of 0.887 and root mean square error (RMSE) of 0.271 (Figure S4A). Most genes clustered closely along the predicted-observed diagonal, indicating that Gem-miR-129 largely retained the expected activity of its miR-129 and Gem components.

Residual analysis further showed that the difference between observed and predicted Gem-miR-129 responses was centered near zero across most genes, with no broad systematic deviation (Figure S4B). This suggests that Gem-miR-129 did not induce a widespread unrelated off-target transcriptional program.

At the pathway level, Hallmark pathway scoring showed strong agreement between the observed Gem-miR-129 response and the predicted parent miR-129 plus Gem response (Figure S4C). Pathways such as E2F targets and G2/M checkpoint showed reduced activity, consistent with suppression of proliferative and cell-cycle programs. In contrast, modest positive residuals were observed for selected stress- and immune-associated pathways, including p53 pathway, interferon responses, IL6/JAK/STAT3 signaling, allograft rejection, complement, and xenobiotic metabolism. Additional residual changes were observed in tumor-adaptive pathways, including Wnt/β-catenin, TGF-β, hypoxia, angiogenesis, and mTORC1 signaling (Figure S4D).

Together, these results indicate that Gem-miR-129 produces a largely component-driven transcriptomic response that closely resembles the combined effects of parent miR-129 and Gem, while showing only limited and biologically interpretable residual pathway changes. This supports the conclusion that Gem-miR-129 retains miR-129-like global activity without inducing a broad off-target expression signature.

Suppression of NSCLC tumor growth in vivo by Gem-miR-129 using mouse model

A proof-of-concept in vivo experiment was conducted using a lung tumor xenografts model using luciferase expressing A549 NSCLC cells. Following tumor establishment via intravenous (i.v.) tail vein injection, mice were treated on alternating days for 2 weeks with 4 mg/kg Gem-miR-129, delivered either i.v. or intraperitoneally (i.p.). Bioluminescence imaging (IVIS imaging) was used to quantify tumor growth over time. Compared to the vehicle control group, mice treated with Gem-miR-129 significantly inhibited tumor growth by both i.v. and i.p. delivery (Figure 5). After 4 weeks, the average tumor burden quantified by the IVIS imaging in the Gem-miR-129 groups was only around 20% of that in control mice (p < 0.001). Mice treated with vehicle control exhibited rapid tumor development. Strikingly, tumors treated with Gem-miR-129 showed minimal growth and, in some cases, regression throughout the treatment period (Figure 5A). At the end of 8 weeks of monitoring of tumor volume, all control mice exhibited large palpable tumors, but Gem-miR-129 treated mice presented with significantly smaller tumors. Discussion

Resistance to chemotherapy and EGFR TKIs in NSCLC is highly complex and is driven by diverse genetic, epigenetic, and adaptive signaling mechanisms.17 These include oncogenic alterations in EGFR, anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), receptor tyrosine kinase (ROS1) proto-oncogene (ROS1), serine/threonine-protein kinase b-raf (BRAF), and other less-established oncoproteins. Third generation EGFR inhibitors, such as osimertinib, have improved outcomes in patients with T790M mutation. Unfortunately, the patient eventually develops resistance to osimertinib treatment.4

Given the diversity of oncogenic driver mutations and gene amplifications in NSCLC; single-target strategies provide limited benefits. With this in mind, instead of a single-target approach, to which lung tumors will eventually develop resistance quickly, we took advantage of multi-targeted tumor suppressor miRNA, with the potential to impact multiple targets to overcome drug resistance. Our previous studies have identified miR-129 as a key tumor suppressor that suppresses key oncogenic targets such as E2F3, BCL2, and thymidylate synthase (TS). Previously, we have established 5-Fluorouracil (5-FU) modified miR-129 as a promising therapeutic strategy with enhanced efficacy in both colorectal cancer and NSCLC.8,18 Because 5-FU is no longer used in NSCLC, we instead incorporated Gem—a frontline NSCLC chemotherapy. We therefore designed Gem-miR-129 by replacing all cytidines in the miR-129 guide strand with Gem, yielding substantially greater potency than native miR-129, Gem alone, or 5-FU-miR-129 in inducing apoptosis and overcoming TKI resistance in NSCLC (Figure 2).

A primary impetus for the development of Gem-miR-129 was to address treatment resistance in NSCLC. Gem-miR-129 strongly inhibited NSCLC cells resistant to both standard chemotherapy and targeted EGFR inhibitors. In KRAS-mutant lines (A549 and Calu-1), resistance to chemotherapies and the absence of targeted treatment options often result in unfavorable outcomes. Gem-miR-129 demonstrated significant therapeutic efficacy in these cell lines, suggesting its ability to overcome resistance mechanisms. In contrast, Gem alone has been shown to have micromolar potency in NSCLC cells.19,20 In EGFR-mutant HCC827 cells exhibiting acquired resistance to TKIs, Gem-miR-129 elicited substantial apoptosis and growth suppression, thereby retaining activity in erlotinib- and OR models (erlotinib, osimertinib) (Figures 1D, 2D, and 2E). Notably, Gem-miR-129 demonstrated over 2,500-fold increase in potency compared with osimertinib HCC827OR cells (Table 1). Importantly, Gem-miR-129 was highly effective irrespective of the p53 status as shown by its activity in both p53-deficient models (such as Calu-1) and p53-wild-type A549 cells (Figures 1B, 2B, and 2C). The ability of Gem-miR-129 to retain activity regardless of EGFR, KRAS, or p53 status is a potentially important feature for future NSCLC therapy. The less pronounced cell-cycle response in HCC827OR cells may reflect model-specific adaptations acquired during prolonged osimertinib selection, including pathway rewiring, altered apoptosis, and reduced dependence on the cell-cycle programs most strongly affected by Gem-miR-129.

The cell-cycle results are mechanistically consistent with Gem action. Gem causes S-phase arrest by depleting nucleotide pools and triggering replication fork collapse. Following cellular uptake, Gem is either deaminated to an inactive form, 2′, 2′-difluorodeoxyuridine (dFdU) by cytidine deaminase (CDA), or phosphorylated by deoxycytidine kinase (dCK) to dFdC-5′-monophosphate (dFdCMP) and active metabolites diphosphate and triphosphate. dFdC-5′-diphosphate inhibits ribonucleotide reductase (RR), an enzyme essential for the production of deoxyribonucleotides before DNA synthesis in the S phase of dividing cells. dFdC-5′-triphosphate gets incorporated into DNA, resulting in premature chain termination and inhibition of DNA synthesis.21 Consistent with this, our results showed that Gem-miR-129 caused an S-phase accumulation and reduction of G2 cell-cycle arrest (Figure 3), indicative of replication stress and impaired DNA synthesis. The impact from miR-129 component likely amplified this effect by suppressing pro-survival genes like HMGB1, YAP1, and PBX3, weakening DNA damage tolerance and shifting cells toward apoptosis. Thus, Gem-miR-129 induces replication stress-associated cell death by combining S-phase disruption with suppression of survival pathways. Future studies measuring intracellular dFdCTP, testing dCK dependence, and comparing Gem-miR-129 with Gem-free stabilized miR-129 will help define the contribution of the Gem component.

The superior therapeutic efficacy of Gem-miR-129 is driven by its impact on multiple targets and pathways, thereby inhibiting NSCLC proliferation and inducing cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis (Figures 2 and 3). HMGB1, a previously confirmed direct target of miR-129, has been implicated in the resistance to several targeted therapies.22 It plays critical roles in the regulation of transcription and maintenance of chromosome structure and stability. Elevated HMGB1 levels have been associated with treatment resistance and aggressive disease in lung cancer patients.23 HMGB1 is also elevated in resistant NSCLC and confers treatment resistance by activating autophagy. Suppression of HMGB1 by Gem-miR-129 is consistent with the activity we observed in TKI-resistant cells. YAP1, a central effector of the Hippo-signaling pathway, has been shown to contribute to various resistance mechanisms in lung cancer.24 YAP1 activation is a major driver of EGFR-TKI tolerance and the drug-persister phenotype in lung adenocarcinoma.25 Prior evidence supports regulation of YAP1 by miR-129 in other cancer contexts. Consistent with this, our results show that Gem-miR-129 suppresses YAP1 expression (Figure 4C). However, whether this occurs through direct canonical 3′-UTR targeting, indirect regulation through HMGB1 or cell-cycle stress, or both remains to be fully determined. The Hippo pathway has also been shown to modulate the immune checkpoint molecule PD-L1.26 Therefore, suppression of YAP1 expression by Gem-miR-129 will likely influence the immune response of T helper and Treg cells. We also validated that Gem-miR-129 also suppressed PBX3, a transcription factor associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and stemness in cancers and has been shown to enhance the proliferation and spread of NSCLC cells by transcriptionally activating oncogenic programs. The expression of PBX3 was highly elevated in resistant cancer cells by regulating several stemness genes.27 The ability of Gem-miR-129 to suppress multiple major oncogenic and resistance-associated pathways simultaneously is a notable example of how miRNA-based therapy may provide significant therapeutic benefits to NSCLC patients in the future clinical management of the disease. The present study also addressed whether chemical modification with Gem could alter target specificity or introduce modification-associated off-target effects. Because the two fluorine substitutions of Gem are located on the ribose ring, they are not predicted to disrupt Watson-Crick base-pairing between the miR-129 guide sequence and target mRNAs. Consistent with this, Gem-miR-129 retained regulation of validated miR-129 targets and showed a global transcriptomic profile largely explained by the combined effects of parent miR-129 and Gem. In exploratory RNA-seq analysis, the observed Gem-miR-129-induced gene-expression response was strongly predicted by the parent miR-129 plus Gem model, supporting preservation of miR-129-like regulatory activity together with Gem-associated transcriptional effects. Importantly, residual pathway analysis did not reveal a broad, unrelated off-target transcriptional program. Instead, modest residual signature was mainly associated with biologically plausible stress and drug-response pathways, including xenobiotic metabolism, interferon/stress signaling, p53-associated response, and reduced tumor-adaptive signaling programs such as Wnt/β-catenin, TGF-β, hypoxia, angiogenesis, and mTORC1 signaling. These findings support that Gem-miR-129 retains miR-129-directed regulatory activity while conferring Gem-like transcriptional effects, rather than broadly redirecting the molecule toward unrelated transcriptomic targets.

Low-dose Gem has been reported to reduce tumor growth in immunocompetent models by depleting tumor-infiltrating regulatory T cells (TI-Tregs), which are associated with poor prognosis and resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors.28,29 Upon intracellular degradation, Gem-miR-129 may release low-dose Gem, potentially providing additional antitumor activity. However, the current study used non-obese diabetic (NOD)/severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) mice, immune modulation and tumor-infiltrating regulatory T cell depletion could not be evaluated directly. This potential immunomodulatory mechanism should therefore be investigated in future syngeneic or humanized immune-competent NSCLC mouse models.

The therapeutic potential of Gem-miR-129 was validated in vivo as a proof-of-concept using an NSCLC metastatic model (Figure 5). Mice treated with Gem-miR-129 at 4 mg/kg by i.p. or i.v. injection showed significant tumor growth inhibition without substantial systemic toxicity. This favorable safety profile is important because some TKI inhibitors are associated with adverse effects, including cardiotoxicity. Although Gem-miR-129 demonstrated vehicle-free activity in vitro, the in vivo proof-of-concept study used low-dose PEI-MAX formulation to improve systemic exposure, serum stability, and delivery efficiency. Therefore, the current study demonstrates vehicle-free uptake in cultured NSCLC cells but does not yet establish fully vehicle-free systemic delivery in vivo. The vehicle control was important because linear polyethylenimine (PEI-MAX) itself can contribute to toxicity, particularly at higher amounts, and this allowed us to evaluate whether any observed toxicity was related to the formulation rather than Gem-miR-129. Using low-dose PEI-MAX may also help avoid potential toxicities associated with higher vehicle doses. A similar approach has been successfully demonstrated in a phase 1 clinical trial of our 5-FU-miR-15a in patients with relapsed/refractory acute myeloid leukemia (AML).5,30 Vehicle-free uptakes may reduce formulation-associated toxicity, but normal-tissue exposure remains an important consideration. Gem-miR-129 may have a therapeutic window because its miR-129 component targets oncogenic and resistance-associated pathways such as HMGB1, YAP1, and PBX3, which are more active or functionally important in cancer cells than in normal cells. In addition, the Gem component is expected to preferentially affect rapidly dividing cells and is incorporated at a relatively low dose within Gem-miR-129. Nevertheless, this proposed therapeutic window requires further validation through biodistribution, pharmacokinetic, histopathologic, and serum-chemistry studies.

The in vivo therapeutic efficacy of Gem-miR-129 was especially promising for treating difficult-to-treat NSCLC with different driver gene mutations. A549, a KRAS-mutant lung cancer cell line that typically responds poorly to Gem in vivo, showed significantly reduced tumor growth after Gem-miR-129 treatment. This suggests Gem-miR-129 could offer advantages for patients with KRAS-driven lung adenocarcinomas, which currently derive limited benefit from targeted therapies. Although an in vivo Gem-alone control was not included, the Gem-equivalent exposure from 4 mg/kg Gem-miR-129 is much lower than systemic Gem doses commonly used to inhibit A549 xenograft growth.31,32 Therefore, an equimolar Gem-only control would have limited activity, whereas high-dose Gem would represent a different pharmacologic exposure rather than direct molar control. Future studies will include free Gem comparisons to better define the relative contribution of the Gem component versus miR-129-mediated activity.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the in vivo A549-Luc tail-vein model does not represent an acquired EGFR-TKI-resistant model. Although Gem-miR-129 showed activity in TKI-resistant cells in vitro, validation of in vivo activity in dedicated EGFR-TKI–resistant xenografts will be required. Second, because NOD/SCID mice lack functional immune compartments, the immune-related findings (TI-Treg depletion, PD-L1 modulation) discussed previously will require validation in syngeneic or humanized immune-competent models. Third, although our in vitro dose-response data showed much lower activity of unmodified miR-129, even at 200 nM, compared with Gem-miR-129 at 50 nM (Figure S2), an in vivo unmodified-miR-129 control was not included. Future studies should include non-targeting oligo and unmodified miR-129 control arms using the same PEI-MAX formulation to better distinguish sequence-, modification-, and formulation-related effects. Fourth, intracellular Gem release upon Gem-miR-129 degradation is currently inferred from the molecular design rather than directly demonstrated; definitive evidence will require gemcitabine triphosphate (dFdCTP) measurement or dCK-inhibition rescue experiments. Fifth, dedicated Gem-resistant NSCLC models were not formally evaluated. Although A549 and Calu-1 provide a partially Gem-tolerant background, established Gem-resistant models may help dissect the relative contributions of the miR-129 and Gem components in future work. Finally, biodistribution and serum-stability properties of vehicle-free Gem-miR-129 in vivo, including potential impact on non-tumor tissues, remain to be fully characterized.

In conclusion, we have developed a new multimodal Gem modification strategy for tumor suppressor miRNA mimics, aiming for future NSCLC treatment. We provided proof that the design of Gem-miR-129 created by replacing C with Gem-enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of native miRNA by increasing effectiveness, stability, and ease of delivery. The multi-targeted Gem-miR-129 induces several phenotypic effects in NSCLC, including inhibition of proliferation, S-phase cell-cycle arrest, and apoptosis, likely through suppression of multiple oncogenic and resistance-associated pathways involving HMGB1, YAP1, and PBX3, with additional targets such as TS, E2F3, and BAK supported by prior miR-129 studies. Potential Gem-associated effects, including low-dose immune-modulatory activity such as TI-Treg depletion, remain plausible but require validation in immune-competent models (Figure 6). The ability of Gem-miR-129 to overcome TKI resistance, regardless of the status of EGFR, p53, or other oncogenic driver mutations in NSCLC underscores its potential therapeutic significance. Gem-miR-129 also has the potential to modulate immune tumor microenvironment by suppressing TI-Tregs. Thus, our study provides a strong foundation for future clinical research and development into Gem-miR-129-based therapy for NSCLC.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of Gem-miR-129 mimics

Gem-miR-129 was designed by replacing all the cytidine residues in the guide strand of hsa-miR-129 with cytosine analog Gem (Gem). The passenger strand of hsa-miR-129 sequence was left unmodified to avoid potential off-target effects and to preserve miRNA function. The two strands were ordered from Dharmacon (Horizon Discovery) then annealed in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Additionally, a non-specific scramble miRNA, Pre-miR Negative Control #2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to represent the negative control. Native hsa-miR-129-5p (miR-129) was used as a mimic identical to one purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific.

Cell lines and culture

We utilized the following NSCLC cell lines: A549 (adenocarcinoma, KRASG12S, p53 wild-type), Calu-1 (lung epidermoid carcinoma, KRASG12C, p53-null), PC9 (wild type KRAS, EGFR-mutant, wild type p53), HCC827ER (EGFR-mutant, acquired ER derivative of HCC827), PC9ER (wild type KRAS, EGFR-mutant, wild type p53, acquired ER derivative of PC9), and HCC827OR (EGFR-mutant, acquired OR). All parental cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). The HCC827ER, PC9ER, and HCC827OR cell lines were established by continuously exposing parental HCC827 and PC9 cells to escalating doses of erlotinib or osimertinib, respectively, until resistant populations emerged. Calu-1 were grown in McCoy’s 5A (Modified) Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (MilliporeSigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), A549 were grown in Ham’s F-12K (Kaighn’s) Medium with 10% FBS, HCC827ER, and HCC827OR were grown in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. For routine maintenance, cells were passaged at ∼80% confluence. Prior to addition, FBS was sterilized via Steriflip-GP, 0.22 μm (MilliporeSigma, St. Louis, MO, USA). Resistant lines were maintained in drug-free medium for at least two passages before use in experiments. Cell line identities were confirmed by short tandem repeat profiling, and mycoplasma testing was negative.

Transfection and treatment

Transfections were performed using Oligofectamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or under vehicle-free conditions. For vehicle-mediated transfections, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and transfected the next day at 50 nM final concentration of mimic (Gem-miR-129, unmodified miR-129, and negative control) along with Oligofectamine, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The negative control was a non-specific scramble miRNA (Pre-miR Negative Control #2, Thermo Fisher Scientific, AM17111) and for single agent comparison, cells were treated with 250 nM Gem, corresponding to the equimolar Gem content of 50 nM Gem-miR-129, 24 h post transfection. 5-h post-transfection, media was changed to fresh media supplemented with 10% dialyzed fetal bovine serum (DFBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Vehicle-free transfections were conducted to assess the cell viability, where cells were seeded at 1,000 cells/well onto a 96-well plate. For IC50 determination, 24-h post-plating, cells were transfected or treated with a range of Gem-miR-129 concentrations (0.1–100 nM) or with TKIs (erlotinib or osimertinib) (MedChem Express) over a broad concentration range.

Cell viability assay using WST-1

Cell proliferation and viability were determined using the WST-1 colorimetric assay (MilliporeSigma), which quantifies the metabolic activity of viable cells. Cells were seeded onto a 96-well plate and transfected under vehicle-free condition as described earlier. Six days post-transfection, cell viability was assessed by incubating the cells with 10 μL of WST-1 reagent per 100 μL of culture medium for an hour at 37°C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a reference wavelength of 650 nm using a SpectraMax iD3 plate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA) to quantify viable cell numbers. Viability was normalized to the negative control (no drug treatment), which was set to 100%. Nonlinear regression analysis (variable-slope model) was performed in GraphPad Prism 9 to calculate the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50), using the following equation:(Equation 1)Y=Bottom+(Top−Bottom)/(1+(IC50X)HillSlope).

Each condition was tested in at least three independent experiments (n = 3).

Western blot analysis

Protein expression of key targets of miR-129 was analyzed by western blotting. A549 and Calu-1 NSCLC cells were transfected along with Oligofectamine, as described earlier. After 72 h of transfection, cells were lysed with a radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer containing protease inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein (20–30 μg) were then resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. Blots were then probed with primary antibodies against HMGB1, PBX3, YAP1, and β-Actin (loading control). All primary antibodies were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology and Santa Cruz Biotechnology and used at the following dilutions: HMGB1 (1:1,000), PBX3 (1:1,000), YAP1 (1:1,000), and β-actin (1:10,000). After probing with primary antibodies, proteins were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, either goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse at a 1:5,000 dilution (Bio-Rad), depending on the corresponding primary antibody. After incubation with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, signals were detected by chemiluminescence (ECL Plus kit, GE Healthcare) and imaged using LI-COR Biosciences Odyssey FC imaging system. Band intensities were quantified through densitometry with ImageJ software. For each target protein, expression levels were normalized to β-actin and then expressed relative to the negative control sample (set as 100).

Cell cycle analysis

NSCLC cells were transfected along with Oligofectamine, as described earlier and harvested after 72 h. Cells were trypsinized and washed with PBS. The cells were then resuspended in Krishan modified buffer, supplemented with RNase H (0.02 mg/mL) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and stained with propidium iodide (0.05 mg/mL) (MilliporeSigma) for 15 min in the dark. Cells were analyzed by flow cytometry using the CytoFLEX Flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) and at least 10,000 events were recorded per sample (n = 3). Cell cycle distribution was examined by propidium iodide (PI) DNA staining. DNA content histograms were analyzed using Modfit LT software (BD Biosciences) to determine the percentage of cells in G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases.

Apoptosis assay

The NSCLC cells were plated and transfected with their respective treatment conditions via vehicle-mediated transfection as described earlier. Following 72 h of treatment, both floating and attached cells were collected, washed with cold PBS, and stained with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated Annexin V (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and PI according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Flow cytometric acquisition was performed within 15 min of staining, on a CytoFLEX Flow Cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). Quadrant gating was applied to distinguish live cells (Annexin V–/PI–), early apoptotic (Annexin V+/PI–), late apoptotic/necrotic (Annexin V+/PI+), and dead cells (Annexin V–/PI+). The sum of early and late apoptotic populations was taken as the total apoptosis percentage. To compare across treatments, we calculated fold changes in apoptosis relative to the negative control.

RNA-seq transcriptomic modeling and residual pathway analysis

A549 cells were transfected as described earlier, and total RNA was isolated 72 h post-transfection using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Poly(A)-enriched mRNA sequencing and raw sequencing data processing were performed by Novogene. Subsequent analyses were performed using R (v.4.2) and Python (v.3.9).

Exploratory log2 fold changes were calculated relative to the negative control condition for each treatment:(Equation 2)P129log2FC=P129logCPM−NClogCPM.(Equation 3)G129log2FC=G129logCPM−NClogCPM.(Equation 4)Gemlog2FC=GemlogCPM−NClogCPM.

To evaluate whether the Gem-miR-129 transcriptomic response could be explained by the expected contribution of parent miR-129 and Gem, a gene-level linear model was fitted:(Equation 5)ObservedG129log2FC=β0+β1(P129log2FC)+β2(Gemlog2FC).

The predicted G129 value represents the expected gene-expression change if the G129 response is explained by the combined P129 and Gem transcriptional effects. Model fit was assessed by comparing observed G129 log2FC values with predicted G129 log2FC values. The coefficient of determination (R2) was used to quantify how much of the observed G129 response was explained by the P129 + Gem model.

A residual was then calculated for each gene:(Equation 6)Gene−levelresidual=observedG129log2FC−predictedG129log2FC).

A residual close to zero indicates that the gene behaved as expected based on the P129 + Gem model. A positive residual indicates that the gene was higher in G129 than predicted, whereas a negative residual indicates that the gene was lower in G129 than predicted. For pathway-level analysis, Hallmark gene-set scores were calculated for each treatment condition and observed versus predicted Gem-miR-129 pathway activity was compared using the same residual framework.

In vivo efficacy study using mouse NSCLC tumor metastatic model

All in vivo experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and conducted in accordance with NIH guidelines. Six-week-old female and male NOD/SCID mice (The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) were injected with 1 × 106 A549 cells stably expressing firefly luciferase (A549-Luc) suspended in 0.2 mL sterile PBS via tail vein injection to establish a metastatic lung cancer model. Once bioluminescence IVIS imaging confirmed tumor engraftment (approximately 106 photon flux at ∼7 days post-injection), mice were randomized into three groups (n = 5 per group): (1) Control (vehicle injections), (2) Gem-miR-129 intravenous (i.v.) treatment, and (3) Gem-miR-129 intraperitoneal (i.p.) treatment. Gem-miR-129 (formulated in PEI) was administered at 4 mg/kg per dose. Mice were treated with either 80 μg of PEI-MAX (vehicle control, Polysciences, Warrington, PA, USA) in 5% D-glucose (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) or 80 μg of PEI-MAX complexed with 80 μg of Gem-miR-129 in 5% D-glucose, corresponding to an miRNA dose of ∼4 mg/kg based on an average mouse body weight of ∼20 g. Tumor growth was monitored by BLI using the IVIS Spectrum imaging system (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Mice were imaged once weekly after IP injection of D-luciferin, RediJect (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) (150 mg/kg) to quantify tumor luminescence (photons/sec). Tumor growth was calculated as a function of (total flux at time of measurement [p/s])/(total flux at initial measurement [p/s]). Mice were monitored daily and euthanized according to IACUC guidelines (>15% loss in body weight over 7 days). Euthanasia time points from mice used for in vivo study were recorded and analyzed for survival studies.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Each experiment was repeated at least three times unless otherwise noted. Group comparisons were performed with Student’s t test (for two groups). For the in vivo studies, two-way ANOVA with mixed effects was performed to evaluate the impact of treatment over time on the tumor progression rate in mice. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and significance levels are indicated as p < 0.05 (∗), p < 0.01 (∗∗), p < 0.001 (∗∗∗), and p < 0.0001 (∗∗∗∗). Kaplan-Meier survival curves were compared using the log rank test. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).
