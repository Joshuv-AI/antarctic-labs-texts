Modified versus conventional intubation and early versus delayed oral intake in thyroid surgery: a 2 × 2 factorial randomized controlled trial
Authors: Juelun Wu, Yuelun Zhang, Le Shen
Published: 2025

Background

Thyroid cancer is the fourth most common cancer among women in China, with its incidence continuing to rise globally (Zheng et al. 2022; Pizzato et al. 2022). Thyroid surgery has become increasingly common worldwide, with a growing emphasis on ambulatory and short-stay procedures (Sun et al. 2013; Loyo et al. 2013). As a result, there is a rising need for optimized perioperative protocols that can ensure both patient safety and efficient recovery. Early oral intake (EOI) has been widely adopted in enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways in other surgical fields, but its implementation after thyroid surgery remains inconsistent due to concerns about swallowing dysfunction, aspiration, and patient discomfort (Herbert et al., 2018; Jochum et al. 2020; Bevilacqua et al. 2019; Ishikawa et al. 2010; Kaaki et al. 2022; Charoenkwan and Matovinovic 2014). However, despite recommendations for EOI, its safety and effectiveness in thyroid surgery remain uncertain primarily due to a relative lack of relevant studies (Dort et al. 2017). A recent nationwide survey in China revealed that clinical practices regarding early postoperative oral intake remain highly variable and are strongly influenced by practitioners’ understanding of ERAS protocols (Huang et al. 2021).

Another key component of perioperative care in thyroid surgery is intraoperative neural monitoring (IONM) using electromyography (EMG) endotracheal tubes (Bai and Chen 2018; Bonati et al. 2022). The International Intraoperative Monitoring Study Group has established standardized protocols for implementing IONM during thyroid procedures (Randolph et al. 2011). This traditional EMG tube placement often requires repeated adjustments and additional laryngoscopy after patient positioning, potentially increasing intubation time, patient discomfort, and procedural complexity. To optimise intraoperative neural monitoring, a modified EMG intubation protocol combining video laryngoscopy and real-time EMG feedback has been developed and shown to reduce adjustment rates and streamline the intubation process (Jin et al. 2022). This approach aims to minimize intubation-related complications and postoperative throat discomfort, but its impact on perioperative workflow and patient-centered outcomes has not been adequately evaluated.

Considering that both early oral intake (EOI) and the quality of intraoperative airway management may influence postoperative comfort and recovery, this study was designed to investigate not only the individual effects of EOI and a modified EMG intubation protocol, But also their potential interaction. A 2× 2 factorial randomised controlled trial design was adopted to allow simultaneous evaluation of the main and interaction effects of these two interventions on postoperative recovery outcomes in thyroid surgery. It was hypothesised that these interventions, individually or combined, would improve procedural efficiency and reduce patient discomfort without compromising safety or overall recovery quality, as assessed by the Quality of Recovery-15 (QoR-15) questionnaire (Stark et al. 2013; Chazapis et al. 2016; Bu et al. 2016).

Methods

Ethics

Ethical approval for this study (Ethical Committee No. K2501) was provided by the Ethical Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital, Beijing, China (Chairperson Prof Zhaohui Zhu) on 2 November 2022.

Study design and registration

This was a single-centre, randomised, 2 × 2 factorial controlled trial conducted at Peking Union Medical College Hospital to evaluate the effects of a modified endotracheal intubation protocol and early oral intake (EOI) on postoperative recovery following thyroid surgery. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05624463). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrolment. The study protocol was prospectively published (Wu et al. 2024) on January 18th, 2024, and the trial adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and followed the CONSORT 2010 reporting guideline (Moher et al. 2012).

Participants

Patients scheduled for elective thyroid surgery with intraoperative neural monitoring (IONM) were screened for eligibility. A research team member provided trial information and obtained written informed consent from each participant. Patients had the right to withdraw at any point, and investigators could discontinue participation for safety reasons.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) ASA physical status I–II; (3) BMI between 18.5–29.9 kg/m2; and (4) first surgical case of the day. Exclusion criteria included: inability to understand the study; preoperative respiratory or swallowing symptoms; high aspiration risk; clinical judgement against early oral intake; contraindication to visual laryngoscopy; or inability to complete QoR-15 due to language barriers, psychiatric conditions, or serious comorbidities. Patients undergoing emergency surgery or with life-threatening conditions were also excluded.

Withdrawal criteria included intolerable adverse effects, voluntary withdrawal, unforeseen clinical ineligibility, or failure to adhere to follow-up.

Randomisation and blinding

On the day of surgery, patients were monitored using standard protocols, including pulse oximetry, ECG, non-invasive blood pressure, and bispectral index (BIS). Intraoperative neural monitoring was conducted using the NIM system (Medtronic, USA). Participants underwent two sequential randomisations:

First (pre-induction): modified vs. conventional EMG intubation protocol

First (pre-induction): modified vs. conventional EMG intubation protocol

Second (postoperatively, if eligible): early oral intake (EOI) vs. delayed oral intake

Second (postoperatively, if eligible): early oral intake (EOI) vs. delayed oral intake

Randomisation was based on block random sequences (block size 4 or 8) generated by an independent statistician. Allocation was concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque envelopes. Due to the nature of the interventions, anaesthesiologists and surgeons were not blinded, but they remained unaware of the other intervention. Patients were blinded to their intubation group, and data collectors and outcome assessors were blinded to all group allocations.

Interventions

General anaesthesia was induced with midazolam (0.03–0.05 mg/kg), lidocaine (1 mg/kg), fentanyl (2 μg/kg), rocuronium (0.6–1.2 mg/kg), and propofol (1.5–2.5 mg/kg). Patients received either:

Modified intubation (Group M): EMG tube placed under video laryngoscopy with real-time EMG signal monitoring; tube secured before positioning (Jin et al. 2022).

Modified intubation (Group M): EMG tube placed under video laryngoscopy with real-time EMG signal monitoring; tube secured before positioning (Jin et al. 2022).

Conventional intubation (Group C): EMG tube inserted by depth estimation, adjusted after positioning with re-laryngoscopy as needed (Randolph et al. 2011).

Conventional intubation (Group C): EMG tube inserted by depth estimation, adjusted after positioning with re-laryngoscopy as needed (Randolph et al. 2011).

Anaesthesia was maintained with intravenous remifentanil and propofol to maintain BIS between 40–60.

After surgery, patients were extubated and transferred to the PACU. Based on second randomisation:

Group E (EOI): received 30–50 mL of room-temperature water in PACU once Steward score ≥ 4, under supervision.

Group E (EOI): received 30–50 mL of room-temperature water in PACU once Steward score ≥ 4, under supervision.

Group D (delayed): resumed oral intake 6 h postoperatively in the ward, with intravenous glucose-saline given beforehand.

Group D (delayed): resumed oral intake 6 h postoperatively in the ward, with intravenous glucose-saline given beforehand.

Patients who tolerated initial water intake resumed oral fluids and diet gradually, guided by the clinical team.

Outcomes

Patients were followed during their hospital stay. QoR-15 and symptom scores were recorded at baseline (T0), postoperative day one (T1), and at discharge (T2). The QoR-15 includes 15 items across five dimensions: physical comfort, emotional state, physical independence, psychological support, and pain (Stark et al. 2013). Scores range from 0 (poor recovery) to 150 (full recovery).

The primary outcome was the QoR-15 score at T1. Secondary outcomes included QoR-15 at T2, intubation time, ETT readjustment, sore throat, thirst, hunger, nausea, vomiting, incisional pain, patient satisfaction, gastrointestinal recovery time, drainage volume, length of hospital stay, and adverse events. Symptom scores were measured using visual analogue or numeric rating scales.

Sample size calculation

The study aimed to detect a 10-point difference in QoR-15 scores between groups. For oral intake strategies, previous literature (Wang et al. 2021) suggested a 10-point difference with an SD of 20. Assuming a two-sided alpha of 0.05 and 20% dropout, 200 patients would yield 88% power. Due to limited prior data on intubation protocol effects, the same assumptions were applied to both hypotheses.

Statistical analysis

All analyses followed the intention-to-treat principle. ANOVA was used for continuous outcomes. Repeated measures ANOVA was used to analyse QoR-15 over time, with Greenhouse–Geisser correction. Two-way ANOVA tested for interaction effects. Kruskal–Wallis and chi-square tests were used for non-parametric and categorical variables, respectively. Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons.

For missing primary outcome data, QoR-15 at T1 was imputed using baseline (T0) scores. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using complete case datasets. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Between April 9th, 2023, and February 4th, 2024, a total of 211 patients were screened for eligibility. Among them, 200 patients met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled. All patients completed the study and follow-up, with the last visit on February 9th, 2024. No changes were made to the study protocol after trial initiation. Secondary outcomes

In the comparison between intubation methods, patients in the modified intubation group had shorter intubation times (median difference: –13.0 s; 95% CI: –24.0 to –3.0) and reported less sore throat and incisional pain before drinking water (median differences: –0.9 and –0.8, respectively). The number of patients requiring endotracheal tube adjustment or experiencing coughing after first oral intake was lower in Group M than in Group C (2 vs. 5, and 2 vs. 7 respectively), although these differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 3).

For oral intake strategies, patients in the early intake group reported less thirst and hunger before their first drink compared with those in the delayed intake group (both median difference: –1.0). However, the early group also showed a slightly higher sore throat score before drinking (median difference: 0.6; 95% CI: 0.0 to 1.1). No difference was found in coughing after drinking (1 vs. 6), or in the rate of postoperative nausea and vomiting (11.2% vs. 10.0%; Fig. 3). Results of multiple comparisons are shown in Table 5.

There were no differences in hospital stay length or patient satisfaction scores between groups. No adverse events or serious complications occurred in any group.

Discussion

This randomised controlled trial demonstrated that both the modified EMG intubation protocol and early oral intake were safe and well tolerated in patients undergoing thyroid surgery. Previous studies have shown that patients with a total QoR-15 score of ≥ 118 points could be considered in a healthy or good condition (Myles et al. 2016). While the QoR-15 did not show statistically significant differences in all groups of this study, all patients achieved scores indicative of good postoperative recovery. This finding contrasts with results from other surgical populations, including upper gastrointestinal and colorectal surgeries, where EOI has been shown to enhance recovery (Jochum et al. 2020; Willcutts et al. 2016). This discrepancy may be attributed to the unique characteristics of thyroid surgery. Unlike gastrointestinal procedures, thyroidectomy is associated with minimal surgical trauma and does not directly affect the digestive tract.

And this result may be partly explained by the relatively low physiological and psychological stress associated with thyroid surgery, which is typically characterized by minimal tissue trauma and low complication rates. Consequently, baseline recovery scores tend to be high, limiting the capacity of patient-reported outcome measures like the QoR-15 to detect additional improvements—a phenomenon often referred to as the "ceiling effect." In our cohort, the majority of patients already reported QoR-15 scores above the established threshold for good recovery on postoperative day one, suggesting that both groups had limited room for measurable enhancement.

This ceiling effect does not undermine the clinical value of the interventions, but rather highlights the need for complementary outcome measures—such as symptom-specific discomfort scales or workflow-related metrics—when evaluating perioperative improvements in low-risk surgeries. Although these interventions did not significantly improve global recovery scores, they offered measurable benefits in terms of procedural efficiency and patient comfort.

Among the secondary outcome measures, we observed the potential advantages of the modified intubation protocol in reducing intubation time and postoperative throat discomfort, as well as the early oral intake (EOI) in alleviating postoperative hunger and thirst discomfort. No differences in safety were observed between the different intubation protocols and oral intake strategies. Postoperative discomfort in thyroid surgery is often related to both surgical trauma and endotracheal intubation. Previous studies have reported sore throat rates of 50–65% after thyroidectomy (Moon et al. 2023; Ryu et al. 2013; Koo et al. 2019). Complications such as sore throat and coughing following ETT intubation are common (Koo et al. 2019). In our study, patients in the modified intubation group experienced less sore throat prior to oral intake compared to those in the conventional group. This may be due to shorter intubation times, resulting in reduced vocal cord irritation and edema. Importantly, among patients who received EOI, those intubated using the modified protocol reported lower levels of both sore throat and incisional pain than those who received conventional intubation. These findings suggest a potential synergistic effect between the two interventions in reducing early postoperative discomfort and facilitating a more tolerable EOI experience.

Besides, the EMG endotracheal tube (ETT) remains the most widely used method for intraoperative neural monitoring (IONM). One concern with EMG ETT use is positional displacement during patient repositioning. Yap et al. reported that ETT positioning may shift significantly when moving from a neutral to an extended neck position (Yap et al. 1994). To address this, guidelines from the International Intraoperative Monitoring Study Group recommend verifying tube placement after positioning using EMG impedance checks and video confirmation (Randolph et al. 2011).

In our study, we observed that the modified intubation protocol—combining video laryngoscopy and real-time EMG feedback—reduced intubation time and allowed for accurate ETT placement with minimal need for intraoperative repositioning. Our findings suggest that with careful initial placement, the risk of signal degradation due to tube displacement may be overestimated.

This study has several strengths, including its randomised design, standardised intervention protocols, and high participant retention. However, limitations should be noted. First, the single-centre design may limit the generalisability of findings. Second, the lack of blinding and reliance on patient-reported outcomes introduce a potential for bias. Third, high-risk or complex surgical cases were not included, limiting the applicability of results to broader thyroidectomy populations. These aspects warrant further investigation in multicentre trials. Overall, these results support the incorporation of both modified intubation and early oral intake into enhanced recovery protocols for thyroid surgery. The improvements in patient comfort and clinical workflow, without added risk, highlight their practical value in routine care.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that both a modified EMG intubation protocol and early oral intake can be safely applied in patients undergoing thyroid surgery. While neither intervention significantly improved global recovery scores, each offered practical benefits: the modified protocol shortened intubation time and reduced throat discomfort, and early oral intake alleviated thirst and hunger without compromising safety. These findings support the integration of both strategies into enhanced recovery protocols to improve perioperative efficiency and patient comfort. Further multicentre research is warranted to confirm these benefits across diverse patient populations and surgical settings.
