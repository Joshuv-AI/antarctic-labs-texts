Social Isolation and Mortality in Adults With Chronic Kidney Disease
Authors: Fan Zhang, Yifei Zhong
Published: 2025

To the Editor:

Social isolation, defined as an objective state of limited or no social contact, has been associated with adverse health outcomes and increased mortality in older adults.1 chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a prevalent condition among aging populations, often accompanied by physical and psychological burdens that may exacerbate social isolation.2 However, the relationship between social isolation and mortality in adults with CKD has not been thoroughly investigated. This study examines the association between social isolation and all-cause mortality in a nationally representative cohort of Chinese adults with CKD.

This study used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study from 2011 to 2020, a nationally representative survey of adults aged 45 years or older.3 The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent. This study adhered to the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines.

Social isolation was assessed using a previously validated scale, which included factors such as marital status (unmarried), living alone, lack of social contact with children, family, or friends, and no community participation (1 point each; range, 0-6 points). A score of ≥ 3 was classified as social isolation.4 The inclusion population was self-reported CKD or estimated glomerular filtration rate based on cystatin C calculations < 60 mL/min/1.73m2,5 and responded to at least 3 social isolation questions, and the primary outcome was all-cause mortality, determined using China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study date-of-death measures.

Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the association between social isolation and all-cause mortality, adjusting for covariates including age, gender, education, body mass index, marital status, smoking, drinking, depression, cognitive function, estimated glomerular filtration rate at baseline, and comorbid conditions (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, liver disease, and heart disease). Missing covariate data was handled using multiple imputations.6 To assess the potential effect of unmeasured confounding, we calculated E-values for the observed hazard ratio (HR) and the lower bound of their 95% confidence interval (95% Cl).7 Analyses were conducted using Stata, version 17.0 (StataCorp LLC) and R software (version R-4.4.2).

A total of 2,198 participants (age, 67.6 ± 10.2 years; 1,061 [48.3%] female) were included (see Fig S1 for a flowchart of the participant screening process), and 796 (36.2%) were classified as During a median follow-up of 109.6 months (203,634.66 person-years), 671 participants (30.5%) died were documented. Participants with social isolation had a significantly higher risk of death compared with those without social isolation (unadjusted HR, 1.62 [95% CI, 1.39-1.89], P < 0.001; adjusted HR, 1.20 [95% CI, 1.00-1.44], P = 0.04). Median survival time was shorter in the socially isolated group (7.8 [95% CI, 6.9-8.7] years) compared with the nonisolated group (9.6 [95% CI, 8.9-10.3] years) (Fig 1). The E-value was 1.69, and the lower bound of the 95% CI was 1.11. In this cohort study, social isolation was independently associated with increased mortality in adults with CKD. The adjusted HR of 1.20 suggests that social isolation contributes to a modest but significant increase in mortality risk, even after accounting for demographic, behavioral, and clinical factors. These findings align with prior research demonstrating the detrimental effects of social isolation on health outcomes in other chronic conditions, such as heart failure and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.8,9 The E-value indicated that unmeasured confounders would need to have a risk ratio of at least 1.69 with both social isolation and mortality to fully explain the observed results, suggesting the robustness of our findings.

The mechanisms underlying this association may include limited access to social and medical support, delayed dialysis initiation, poor adherence to dietary and medication regimens, accelerated kidney function decline, and exacerbation of psychological stressors such as depression and cognitive decline.10 Given the high prevalence of social isolation in CKD, these findings underscore the importance of integrating social interventions into CKD management strategies. Potential interventions include group-based kidney rehabilitation programs, peer support groups, and community-based initiatives to enhance social engagement and self-efficacy.

Study limitations include the lack of proteinuria data, which may have led to an underestimation of the CKD population, and the observational nature of the study, which precludes causal inference. Additionally, there may be residual confounding (including diet, nutritional status, and physical activity) due to unmeasured covariates. Furthermore, the study was conducted exclusively in a Chinese population, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Future research should explore targeted interventions to mitigate social isolation and evaluate their effect on health outcomes in CKD populations.
