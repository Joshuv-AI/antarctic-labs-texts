Massive Upper Gastrointestinal Bleeding From Ruptured Gastric Varices: A Rare Presentation of Type 1 Autoimmune Pancreatitis
Authors: Thitichai Wongsiriamnuey, Piyapoom Pakvisal, Anapat Sanpavat, Aniwat Saleepol, Thanatarn Sathidatekoonchorn, Rungsun Rerknimitr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 59-year-old Thai woman presented with jaundice and massive hematemesis complicated by cardiac arrest. Emergency endoscopy revealed isolated gastric varices type 1, successfully treated with cyanoacrylate injection. Axial imaging demonstrated diffuse pancreatic enlargement with splenic vein compression and distal common bile duct obstruction. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography with biliary stenting was performed. Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) showed diffuse mixed-echogenic pancreatic infiltration, and EUS-guided biopsy revealed dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltration with 20 immunoglobulin G4-positive plasma cells per high-power field. Elevated serum immunoglobulin G4 (405 mg/dL) also confirmed type 1 autoimmune pancreatitis. Prednisolone therapy resulted in complete clinical and radiologic resolution at 6-week follow-up.

INTRODUCTION

Autoimmune pancreatitis type 1 (AIP-1)1 is a distinct fibroinflammatory disorder characterized by diffuse or focal pancreatic enlargement and irregular narrowing of the main pancreatic duct. It is associated with elevated serum immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4) levels and histopathologic findings of dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltration rich in IgG4-positive plasma cell.1,2 Nearly all patients (95%–100%) respond dramatically to glucocorticoid therapy.2–6

AIP-1 typically presents with progressive jaundice and weight loss.2,4 Although vascular involvement has been reported, clinically significant vascular complications are uncommon.7,8 Peripancreatic inflammation may extend to the splenic vein, leading to thrombosis or compression, left-sided portal hypertension, and collateral formation, including isolated gastric varices.7,8

We report a rare case of AIP-1 presenting with jaundice and massive upper gastrointestinal bleeding due to ruptured gastric varices.

CASE REPORT

A 59-year-old Thai woman presented with a 1-day history of melena and dizziness. She reported anorexia, an unintentional 7-kg weight loss over 2 weeks, and progressive jaundice for 1 week. She denied a history of chronic liver disease or alcohol use and had no personal or family history of autoimmune disease.

Shortly after presentation, she developed massive hematemesis complicated by cardiac arrest. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation was initiated, and return of spontaneous circulation was achieved after one cycle. Physical examination revealed marked scleral icterus and pale conjunctivae, without stigmata of chronic liver disease.

Laboratory evaluation showed a hemoglobin level of 8.2 g/dL (baseline, 12.2 g/dL) and a platelet count of 240,000/µL. Liver tests demonstrated a total bilirubin level of 12.8 mg/dL, direct bilirubin of 9.6 mg/dL, aspartate aminotransferase of 243 U/L, alanine aminotransferase of 363 U/L, and alkaline phosphatase of 361 U/L.

Emergency esophagogastroduodenoscopy revealed isolated gastric varices type 1 with stigmata of recent hemorrhage. Hemostasis was achieved using 1 mL of n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (Figure 1). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography demonstrated diffuse edematous pancreatic enlargement with extrinsic splenic vein compression (Figure 2) and segmental distal common bile duct (CBD) wall thickening with upstream dilation.

Computed tomography scan demonstrating inflammatory changes in the body and tail of the pancreas, leading to obliteration of the splenic vein. The arrow indicates pancreatic enlargement causing compression of the splenic vein.

Subsequent endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography revealed a 3.0-cm distal CBD stricture (Figure 3), which was managed with fully covered metallic biliary stenting. Endoscopic ultrasound demonstrated a diffuse infiltrative lesion with mixed echogenicity involving the entire pancreas (Figure 4). Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided fine-needle biopsy was performed to exclude lymphoma and revealed dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltration (Figure 5). Immunohistochemistry showed 20 IgG4-positive plasma cells per high-power field and an IgG4/IgG ratio of 50%. Serum IgG4 was elevated at 405 mg/dL, confirming type 1 autoimmune pancreatitis according to the International Consensus Diagnostic Criteria.1

Endoscopic ultrasound showed infiltrative lesion of mixed hyper/hypoechoic lesions in entire pancreas, no main pancreatic duct dilation. BOP, body of pancreas.

By the International Consensus Diagnostic Criteria, the diagnosis fulfilled the threshold for definitive type 1 AIP: Level 1 parenchymal imaging (diffuse pancreatic enlargement on contrast-enhanced computed tomography), Level 1 serology (serum IgG4 of 405 mg/dL, greater than twice the upper limit of normal), and supportive pancreatic histology (dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltration with 20 IgG4-positive plasma cells per high-power field and an IgG4/IgG ratio of 50%), together with a favorable response to corticosteroid therapy.1

Oral prednisolone was initiated at 40 mg/d (0.6 mg/kg/d) with gradual tapering. At 6-week follow-up, repeat computed tomography demonstrated reduced pancreatic edema and resolution of distal CBD obstruction, with spontaneous migration of the biliary stent (Figure 6). No recurrent bleeding occurred.

DISCUSSION

AIP-1 is a systemic IgG4-related fibroinflammatory disease that can closely mimic pancreatic malignancy.9,10 While pancreatic enlargement and biliary strictures are characteristic features, vascular involvement is increasingly recognized.7,8 A multicenter study reported peripancreatic vascular involvement in 51.3% of patients at diagnosis.7

Vascular complications in AIP-1 are primarily inflammatory rather than thrombotic. Perivascular infiltration and external compression most commonly affect the splenic (48.3%), superior mesenteric (23.9%), and portal (9.6%) veins.7 Severe splenic vein stenosis can result in left-sided portal hypertension and collateral formation in approximately 40% of patients.7

Despite the relatively high prevalence of vascular involvement, clinically significant bleeding is rare. Gastric varices occur in approximately 10.9% of patients with vascular complications; however, overt upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage has been reported in only 3 cases.7,11 Massive hematemesis, as observed in our patient, is therefore an exceptional but life-threatening presentation.

The underlying mechanism involves splenic vein compression, leading to increased pressure within the short gastric and submucosal veins, resulting in isolated gastric varices type 1 formation and potential rupture. Recognition of splenic vein stenosis or collateral vessels on cross-sectional imaging is critical and should prompt early consideration of steroid therapy.

The endoscopic ultrasound findings in this case were atypical. Classic AIP is characterized by diffuse, homogeneous hypoechoic enlargement (“sausage-like” appearance), often accompanied by hyperechoic strands reflecting fibrosis.12–14 By contrast, our patient demonstrated diffuse heterogeneous infiltrative changes, highlighting the variability of EUS manifestations in AIP.

Glucocorticoids remain the cornerstone of therapy. Because vascular involvement is predominantly inflammatory, it responds favorably to treatment. Steroid therapy has been associated with resolution rates of 95.6% for splenic vein stenosis, 93.8% for portal vein stenosis, and 93.1% for superior mesenteric vein stenosis, with regression of collateral vessels in 80.9% of patients.7,8 Although pancreatic parenchymal improvement is often rapid, vascular resolution may require prolonged therapy.

In conclusion, we report a rare case of massive upper gastrointestinal bleeding due to ruptured isolated gastric varices as the initial presentation of AIP-1. After successful endoscopic hemostasis, the diagnosis of AIP-1 was established because of the unusual concomitant gastric variceal bleeding and obstructive jaundice, prompting initiation of prednisolone therapy. Subsequent follow-up demonstrated marked clinical and radiologic improvement.
