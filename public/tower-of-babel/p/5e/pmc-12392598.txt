Effect of radical resection of lung cancer combined with breathing training on lung cancer patients in thoracic surgery: a meta-analysis
Authors: Biao Lu, Xiaobing Li, Hao Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIts objective was to use meta-analysis (MA) to methodically assess the impact of perioperative breathing exercises on the postoperative rehabilitation of patients with lung cancer (LC) having radical resection. MethodsPubMed, and other databases were searched for randomized controlled trials on radical resection of LC combined with perioperative respiratory training from January 1995 to January 2024. After quality assessment, MA was performed using Review Manager 5.3 software. ResultsA total of 14 studies involving 880 patients were included, of which 434 patients received perioperative breathing training (Intervention group, IG). The differences between the two groups in terms of MIP (MD = -13.31, 95% CI: [-24.43, -2.19]; Z = 2.35, P = 0.02), FVC (MD = -0.23, 95% CI = [-0.36, -0.11]; Z = 3.69, P = 0.0002), 6-minute walk test (6MWT) values (MD = 36.42, 95% CI: [4.37, 68.48]; Z = 2.23, P = 0.03), incidence of pneumonia (OR = 0.38, 95% CI = 0.20–0.72; Z = 2.95, P = 0.003), and complication rates (OR = 0.66, 95% CI = 0.46–0.94; Z = 2.29, P = 0.02) were statistically significant. However, there were no significant differences between the two groups in MEP (MD = -6.10, 95% CI: [-12.10, -0.11]; Z = 2.00, P = 0.05), FEV1 (MD = -0.25, 95% CI = [-0.58, 0.08]; Z = 1.47, P = 0.14), FEV1/FVC (MD = -3.78, 95% CI = [-7.65, 0.09]; Z = 1.91, P = 0.06), PEF (MD = -15.02, 95% CI = [-45.88, 15.83]; Z = 0.95, P = 0.34), atelectasis (OR = 0.52, 95% CI = 0.24–1.12; Z = 1.68, P = 0.09), pneumothorax (OR = 1.20, 95% CI = 0.63–2.29; Z = 0.57, P = 0.57), and mechanical ventilation incidence (OR = 0.99, 95% CI = 0.34–2.91; Z = 0.01, P = 0.99).ConclusionThis MA demonstrates that perioperative respiratory training significantly improves MIP, FVC, and 6MWT in patients undergoing radical LC surgery, while effectively reducing the incidence of postoperative pneumonia and overall complications. However, its impact on MEP, certain pulmonary function parameters (FEV1, FEV1/FVC, PEF), and specific complications (atelectasis, pneumothorax, mechanical ventilation requirement) did not reach statistical significance. The findings support the inclusion of respiratory training in perioperative rehabilitation programs for LC patients to optimize postoperative recovery outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03819-2.

Background

LC is a very common respiratory malignant tumor in thoracic surgery, among which non-small cell LC is the most common, accounting for 80–90% of all LC patients [1]. The common symptoms of LC are cough, chest pain, shortness of breath and fever, which have the characteristics of insidious onset, high morbidity, and high mortality. Surgical resection is the most direct and effective treatment for LC, including conventional thoracotomy and thoracoscopic radical resection of LC [2, 3]. However, lobectomy can cause lung volume reduction and surgical trauma, which will cause diaphragmatic dysfunction, and then affect the normal respiratory muscle function of patients, leading to dysfunction of pulmonary ventilation and gas exchange [4]. Lobectomy seriously affects the cardiopulmonary function of patients, and sputum retention after surgery increases the risk of pulmonary complications [5]. The status of pulmonary function after surgery directly affects the incidence of complications in patients [6]. Therefore, appropriate intervention measures after radical resection of LC are very important for promoting the rehabilitation process of patients and reducing the occurrence of postoperative complications.

Respiratory training, as a non-invasive adjunctive therapy with potential benefits, has gradually been incorporated into the treatment of LC patients. Breathing training can improve the exercise endurance of patients with radical resection of LC, and then improve the pulmonary function after surgery. Many goals of respiratory training are to optimize lung elasticity, maintain and improve the flexible range of motion of the thorax, strengthen the effect of cough to improve the ability to expectorate, exercise respiratory muscles, and improve the coordination of respiratory movements, to comprehensively improve the overall health of patients [7, 8]. Common respiratory training techniques include diaphragmatic breathing, pursed-lip breathing, and incentive spirometry training. These methods aim to enhance respiratory muscle strength (RMS), promote lung expansion, and improve gas exchange, thereby optimizing pulmonary function. Respiratory training has achieved good results in the treatment of patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [9–11]. Patients after LC surgery share certain similarities with those with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) in terms of respiratory function, both of which may experience ventilatory dysfunction. However, LC surgery patients also must contend with surgical trauma, pain, and other factors, which may impact adherence to and the effectiveness of respiratory training. To assess the impact of respiratory exercise on postoperative rehabilitation of patients undergoing radical resection of LC in thoracic surgery, relevant studies were included in this article for MA. It aims to systematically evaluate the impacts of perioperative respiratory training intervention on postoperative respiratory function, motor function, and postoperative pulmonary complications in patients undergoing radical resection of LC. It provides a reference for improving the postoperative rehabilitation effect of patients with radical resection of LC and improving pulmonary ventilation function.

Materials and methods

Literature search and screening criteria

A literature search was conducted across the following databases: China Wanfang, VIP Database, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Nature, Cochrane Library (CENTRAL), Springer, and ScienceDirect, covering publications from January 1995 to January 2024. The search keywords relevant to the topics were as follows: LC-related: “lung cancer,” “lung carcinoma,” “pulmonary cancer,” “pulmonary carcinoma,” “Pulmonary carcinoma,” “Bronchogenic carcinoma,” and “Malignant lung tumors”; Surgery-related: “radical resection of lung cancer,” “radical pulmonary resection,” “radical resection,” and “Pneumonectomy for lung cancer”; Respiratory training-related: “breathing training,” “respiratory training,” “perioperative breathing exercise,” “diaphragmatic breathing training,” and “pursed-lip breathing training.” The search keywords were combined using “or” and “and” for a comprehensive search. All relevant combinations of keywords were input into each database to retrieve targeted literature. For example, the search query for PubMed was: (“radical resection of lung cancer” OR “radical pulmonary resection” OR “radical resection” OR “Pneumonectomy for lung cancer”) AND (“breathing training” OR “respiratory training” OR “Perioperative breathing exercise”) AND (“lung cancer” OR “lung carcinoma” OR “pulmonary cancer” OR “pulmonary carcinoma” OR “Bronchogenic carcinoma” OR “Malignant lung tumors”). Additionally, manual searches were conducted through journal directories, conference proceedings, and professional organization websites to identify literature not indexed in electronic databases. No language restrictions were applied during the search.

Inclusion criteria: (1) the type of study was randomized controlled study; (2) The subjects were LC patients who underwent surgical resection; (3) In the perioperative intervention, one group was treated with breathing training. Exclusion criteria: (1) research types were retrospective studies, case reports, literature reviews, and basic animal experiments; (2) The subjects were treated with radiotherapy or chemotherapy alone; (3) Respiratory training combined with other intervention modes during perioperative period; (4) The outcome indicators were not clear, and the data could not be extracted.

Literature quality evaluation

Two authors independently assessed the quality of the included literature using the Cochrane risk of bias assessment tool 5.0 to guarantee the impartiality and accuracy of the quality evaluation of the literature. The instrument evaluated seven risk biases, including random sequence generation (selection bias), allocation concealment (selection bias), blinding of participants and researchers (execution bias), blinding of outcome assessment (detection bias), incomplete outcome data (loss to follow-up bias), selective reporting (reporting bias), and other biases. If the two authors have different opinions on the quality assessment of the literature, a third professional would be invited to participate in the discussion and make the final decision based on their professional judgment to ensure the accuracy and fairness of the assessment results.

Literature information and data extraction

Two authors independently extracted the corresponding data from a form that was created for data extraction. The basic data of the literature included: first author, publication year, surgical method, perioperative intervention method, and number of patients. Literature outcome data included: maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) and maximal expiratory pressure (MEP), pulmonary function indicators such as forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume of 1 s (FEV1), FEV1/FVC, and peak expiratory flow (PEF), exercise capacity index 6-minute walk test (6MWT), postoperative pulmonary complications. If the two authors have different opinions on the extracted data, a third professional would be invited to participate in the discussion and make the final decision based on professional judgment to ensure the accuracy and fairness of the extracted results.

Statistical analysis

Review Manager 5.3 software was employed. The I2 test was utilized for assessing the heterogeneity (Het). I2 > 50% was considered as visible Het, the random effect model (REM) was used for MA. I2 ≤ 50% was defined as no visible Het, the fixed effect model (FEM) was utilized for MA. 95% CI was used for the comprehensive analysis. The results of binary variables were expressed as OR, the results of continuous variables as MD. Funnel plot (FUP) was used to analyze publication bias. P < 0.05 was considered statistically meaningful.

Results

Literature screening process

Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, the literature was evaluated (Fig. 1). 603 items that were pertinent were found from PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and other databases. After removing 211 duplicate publications, 84 unqualified articles, and 47 articles for other reasons, 261 relevant articles were selected for the next stage. Secondly, 21 pertinent articles were chosen after 240 articles that did not fit the inclusion criteria were eliminated by looking through the literature’s title and abstract. In addition, the full text of the literature was downloaded and read, and 3 articles with unclear outcome indicators, 3 articles with non-surgical treatment, and 1 article with unclear treatment cycle were excluded. Finally, a total of 14 articles were included.

Results of MA

Contrast of RMS

A total of five studies were included in the analysis of MIP values between different patient groups. A MA was conducted to further assess the heterogeneity of MIP across the studies (Fig. 3). Significant heterogeneity was observed between the two groups in terms of MIP values (I2 = 80%, P = 0.0006). Therefore, a random-effects model was employed for the analysis. The results indicated a significant difference in MIP between the two groups (MD = −13.31, 95% CI: [−24.43, −2.19]; Z = 2.35, P = 0.02 < 0.05).

A total of four studies were included in the analysis of MEP values between different patient groups. A MA was conducted to further assess the heterogeneity of MEP across the studies (Fig. 4). No significant heterogeneity was observed between the two groups in terms of MEP values (I2 = 33%, P = 0.23). Therefore, a fixed-effects model was employed for the analysis. The results indicated no significant difference in MEP between the two groups (MD = −6.10, 95% CI: [−12.10, −0.11]; Z = 2.00, P = 0.05).

Discussion

LC is a common malignant tumor in thoracic surgery. With the deepening of population aging and the influence of smoking and other factors, the incidence and mortality of LC are increasing year by year [26]. Because there are no specific symptoms in early LC, most patients have progressed to middle and advanced stages when diagnosed. Radical resection of LC refers to a surgical procedure in which the primary lesion and metastatic lymph nodes are completely removed during the operation, and no cancer cells remain under the microscope [27]. Radical resection of LC is internationally recognized as one of the preferred options for the treatment of LC. Common surgical methods include lobectomy, segmentectomy and total pneumonectomy [28]. LC patients with surgical indications can achieve ideal curative effect, and postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy or chemotherapy can further prolong the survival time of patients [29, 30]. However, radical resection of LC can cause traumatic edema of the inner wall of the thoracic cavity, intercostal nerve injury and postoperative pleural adhesion, which can reduce the compliance of thoracic and lung tissue and lung volume to a certain extent [31]. After lobectomy, the reduction of the relatively effective diffusion area will cause atelectasis and lead to pulmonary edema. Factors such as incision pain after surgery will make patients give up effective deep breathing, affect normal lung ventilation and gas exchange function, and raise the risk of pulmonary problems after surgery [32]. Consequently, patients’ pulmonary function may be somewhat improved by using respiratory training during the postoperative phase after radical LC resection.

The core goal of respiratory training is to teach patients to master correct breathing skills, enhance the efficiency of alveolar air exchange by strengthening the activity of the diaphragm, to effectively promote the recovery and improvement of postoperative respiratory function [33]. Respiratory training also helps to alleviate the hypoxia of the body, correct the uneven distribution of gas in the lungs, and further improve the gas exchange efficiency of lung tissue. This study conducted a systematic review of the MA and found that perioperative respiratory training significantly improved MIP in LC patients compared to those receiving conventional measures. Perioperative respiratory training was shown to significantly enhance inspiratory muscle strength and lung capacity in patients undergoing radical LC surgery, indicating that the training effectively improved ventilation efficiency and pulmonary reserve capacity. This finding aligns with the mechanism by which respiratory muscle resistance training improves diaphragmatic contraction function [34]. Respiratory training can reduce sympathetic nerve excitability and increase skeletal muscle blood flow, so as to meet the energy demand of respiratory muscles and improve RMS. Therefore, the application of respiratory training during the perioperative period of radical resection of LC can markedly improve the postoperative RMS of patients with LC. Breathing training can enhance the pressure inside the airway by slowing down the speed of the expiratory process, which can effectively prevent the collapse or closure of peripheral small airways [35]. By promoting the effective emptying of air in the alveoli, increasing the volume of alveolar air exchange, and reducing the frequency of air exchange, breathing training helps to reduce the discomfort symptoms of patients with dyspnea. The postoperative FVC and FEV1 of LC patients who applied breathing training during the perioperative period were markedly higher than those who took routine measures. FVC and FEV1 are important indicators to evaluate lung function, which can truly reflect the pulmonary ventilation function of patients [36]. Respiratory training can enhance the activity of the diaphragm and intercostal muscles of patients, help patients build the correct breathing mode, increase alveolar ventilation volume, reduce respiratory power consumption, and improve lung function. 6MWT is the fastest walking distance that patients can withstand within 6 min through walking exercise, which can evaluate the functional status of patients [37]. In this study, perioperative respiratory training increased the 6MWT distance by 36.4 m (P = 0.03) in patients undergoing radical LC surgery, confirming that the training can accelerate physical recovery, shorten the time to mobilization, and reduce the risk of thrombosis. MEP showed only a marginal improvement, and there were no changes in FEV1/FVC or PEF, which may be attributed to the training’s focus on inspiratory muscles (e.g., incentive spirometry) while neglecting targeted training for expiratory muscles. The 6MWT distance of LC patients who used respiratory training during the perioperative period of radical resection of LC was markedly greater as against patients who used conventional measures. LC patients often experience significant impacts on their physical function following treatments such as surgery, leading to a decline in physical capacity and limitations in daily activities. An increase in the 6MWT distance indicates that patients are able to walk longer distances, enabling them to more easily perform daily activities such as shopping and climbing stairs, reducing dependence on others, and enhancing self-efficacy and confidence, thereby improving overall quality of life. The significant improvement in the 6MWT results in the IG suggests that perioperative respiratory training may facilitate the recovery of patients’ cardiopulmonary function. Respiratory training can enhance cardiopulmonary reserves by improving RMS, increasing lung ventilation, and enhancing gas exchange efficiency, allowing patients to better meet their body’s demands during physical activity. The results indicate that respiratory training can promote the recovery of motor function in patients undergoing radical resection of LC, thereby improving overall quality of life.

The common complications after radical resection of LC include atelectasis, emphysema, pneumonia, and pleural effusion. Atelectasis is due to the need for long-term bed rest after radical resection of LC, and the accumulation of sputum in the lungs will form sputum plugs, which will speed the bronchi and cause atelectasis [38]. Pneumonia is a hypostatic pneumonia caused by the increase of secretions and the difficulty of sputum excretion in patients with LC after surgery. To improve alveolar ventilation and gas exchange function, mechanical ventilation is very important for maintaining the normal respiratory tract, improving ventilation and oxygenation, and avoiding hypoxia and carbon dioxide retention. However, mechanical ventilation can also cause damage to the respiratory function of patients, and the duration of mechanical ventilation after surgery exceeding 48 h will greatly increase the incidence of complications [39, 40]. This study confirmed that perioperative respiratory training significantly reduces the incidence of pneumonia and overall complications in patients undergoing radical LC surgery, highlighting its core value in preventing respiratory tract infections. This effect may be achieved by enhancing cough and sputum clearance, thereby reducing the retention of secretions. Although atelectasis did not reach statistical significance, the observed trend towards reduced risk supports its potential benefit. On the other hand, pneumothorax and mechanical ventilation are more dependent on surgical techniques rather than respiratory interventions [41]. In addition, the forced exhalation movement in breathing training can change the pressure state in the chest of patients, thereby accelerating the discharge process of respiratory secretions and helping to improve the patency of the respiratory tract. Other studies have shown that respiratory training can relieve the psychological stress of patients and improve negative emotions such as anxiety and depression during perioperative period by relaxing muscles and maintaining regular contraction of cardiac muscles. In clinical practice, respiratory training should be incorporated as a core component of the enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocol. The program should be initiated three days prior to surgery and continue for two weeks postoperatively. It is recommended to combine inspiratory muscle resistance training with positive expiratory pressure (PEP) devices to cover the entire respiratory cycle. The preventive effect on pneumonia is particularly significant in patients with chronic pulmonary diseases and elderly individuals (> 70 years).

Conclusion

This MA confirmed that perioperative respiratory training can effectively optimize postoperative recovery in patients undergoing radical LC surgery. It significantly enhances MIP, FVC, and 6MWT, while reducing pneumonia risk by 62% and overall complication rates by 34%, establishing its core value in the context of ERAS. However, this intervention did not show statistically significant improvements in MEP, FEV1/FVC, and certain surgery-related complications (e.g., atelectasis, pneumothorax), suggesting that the current training protocol needs to target expiratory phase interventions more effectively. Additionally, individualized pulmonary rehabilitation strategies should be incorporated to overcome the limitations posed by structural lung damage. Based on the above evidence, we recommend that standardized respiratory training be incorporated into routine perioperative care for LC patients, initiated preoperatively and continued for two weeks postoperatively. The program should focus on integrating inspiratory muscle resistance and PEP techniques. Future studies should aim to develop individualized protocols targeting respiratory muscle strength (e.g., MIP ≥ 30 cmH2O) and assess their impact on long-term quality of life. This study provides the highest level of evidence for the application of respiratory rehabilitation within the thoracic ERAS pathway; however, the standardization of training protocols and their long-term effects need to be further validated through large-scale multicenter RCTs.
