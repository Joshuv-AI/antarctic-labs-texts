Alcohol Use and Attributable Burden of Disease in the World Health Organization Western Pacific Region
Authors: Jürgen Rehm, Sally Caswell, Dan Fang, Ari Franklin, Ahmed S. Hassan, Marian Saab, Ashley Wettlaufer, Xi Yin, Kevin D. Shield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThis article provides an overview of alcohol exposure and attributable mortality and burden of disease in the World Health Organization Western Pacific Region, and trends in alcohol exposure over the past two decades.MethodsWe employed a comparative risk assessment and trend analyses to evaluate alcohol exposure patterns and attributable burden.ResultsIn 2019, almost half a million deaths and more than 22 million disability‐adjusted life years lost were attributable to the consumption of alcohol in the Western Pacific Region, representing 3.5% (95% uncertainty interval 2.5, 4.8) of all deaths and 4.4% (95% uncertainty interval 3.6, 5.4) of all disability‐adjusted life years lost. This was caused by an average consumption of 6.06 litres of pure alcohol per year (APC; 95% confidence interval 4.46–7.84), higher than the global average of 5.45 litres. Trends in APC have varied across subregions: China saw increasing consumption up to 2016, with a marked decrease since then, likely due to an anti‐corruption campaign prohibiting consumption in public spaces and during official functions. In other Asian countries, consumption steadily increased until the COVID‐19 pandemic, then decreased during it, and has now partially returned to pre‐pandemic levels. APC in Oceania including Australasia was relatively stable between 2000 and 2020 and has decreased since.Discussion and ConclusionsOverall, alcohol use remains high and normalised in the region, necessitating robust alcohol control policies. Strengthening these efforts is particularly critical in some Asian countries that experienced the largest increases in levels of alcohol consumption between 2000 and 2024.

Introduction

The Western Pacific Region (WPR) of the World Health Organization (WHO) encompasses mainland and island countries in Asia, the Pacific Islands and Australasia; China represents 85% of the region's population. Economically, over the last two and a half decades, the WPR has had the second‐highest economic growth of all WHO regions—after the Southeast Asian Region–measured in gross domestic product based on purchasing power parity [1]. While this economic growth has been associated with increases in the level of alcohol consumption in many of these countries [2, 3], due to developments in China [4], the regional trend of yearly increases in alcohol consumption has stopped over the last decade. This contribution aims to examine these developments more closely. Specifically, we have the following objectives: To describe the level of alcohol use and attributable health burden in 2019, the last year before the pandemic, based on comparable Global Health Estimates [5, 6].To determine how the level of alcohol use developed in the WHO WPR and its subregions since 2000. We chose the year 2000 as a starting point, as we have comparable and country‐validated data from WHO monitoring [7] since that year.To study the impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic on the level of alcohol use and attributable harm, which was influenced by two major mechanisms: restrictions on availability (lockdowns) and affordability (financial hardship), both of which were predicted to reduce overall consumption and overall attributable burden [8, 9]. Additionally, increased stress, which was predicted to lead to increased consumption among people with prior heavy consumption, including those with alcohol use disorders [8, 9, 10, 11], resulted in an increase in 100% alcohol‐attributable burden [12].

Methods

Data Sources

We used WHO data for both exposure and burden. The main exposure variable was adult (≥ 15 years of age) alcohol per capita consumption (APC), which is the most accurate summary indicator for the level of alcohol consumption in a country [13, 14]. APC consists of three components [7]: recorded consumption, which denotes all alcohol registered in or taxed by the country, where it is sold and consumed; unrecorded consumption, which is a broad category comprising homemade and illegal alcohol, surrogates and alcohol not registered in the country where it is consumed [15, 16] and tourist consumption. While the WHO usually calculates APC as a three‐year moving average by the WHO [7, 17] (see Figure 1), it is preferable to report it on a yearly basis for trend analysis and prediction purposes (see Figure 4, based on [18]).

Indicators of harm including deaths, years lived with disability (YLD), disability‐adjusted life years (DALY) lost, and years of life lost (YLL) were obtained from the WHO's Global Health Estimates [5]. To correspond to the exposure estimates, all indicators refer to the population aged 15 years and older. Estimation of alcohol‐attributable harm was based on a comparative risk assessment framework using the population‐attributable fraction method integrating alcohol consumption data with corresponding relative risks [19].

Selection of Conditions Causally Related to Alcohol and Risk Relations

The following disease conditions were selected by the WHO based on recommendations of the Technical Advisory Group on Alcohol and Drug Epidemiology as being fully or partially caused by alcohol (Panel 1) [17, 20].

Relative risk estimates used to assess the alcohol‐attributable burden of disease were obtained from meta‐analyses [17]. To be selected, the source had to meet the selection criteria: outcomes had to match WHO‐reported disease and injury categories, lifetime abstainers had to be used as the reference group, and the continuous dose–response association between alcohol use and cancer had to be modelled while controlling for confounders. The WHO's standard population was used to calculate age‐standardised rates of mortality and disability per 100,000 people [21].

Calculation of Uncertainty Intervals

A Monte‐Carlo‐like approach was used to measure the 95% uncertainty intervals (UI) for the estimates of the alcohol‐attributable burden of disease [22]. By simulating 1000 estimates, the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles were constructed to determine the range of alcohol‐attributable harm.

Results

Alcohol Use and Attributable Burden of Disease in the WHO Western Pacific Region in 2019

Overall, the APC amounted to 6.06 L (L) of pure alcohol per year [95% confidence interval (CI) 4.46, 7.84], which is higher than the global average of 5.45 L. There was a high degree of normalisation of consumption: in 2019, 7 out of 10 males (72% [95% CI 69, 75%]), and nearly half of females (49% [95% CI 45, 53%]) consumed alcohol at least once in that year (total population: 60% [95% CI 57, 64%]), and on average 29, 11 and 21 g/day for males, females and total, respectively. The level of alcohol consumption was characterised by marked sex differences: male consumption (male APC: 9.62 L [95% CI 7.04, 12.49]) was almost four times higher than female consumption (female APC: 2.46 L [95% CI 1.82, 3.21]). As noted above, all indicators regarding consumption or attributable harm apply to populations aged 15 years and older.

Figure 1 gives an overview of alcohol use and attributable mortality by country, grouped by World Bank income level [23]. There is wide heterogeneity in APC between countries, even within income groups. While income is a major determinant of consumption levels [17], these levels are also influenced by the proportion of people of Muslim faith in the respective countries. Countries like Viet Nam or Lao PDR show the biggest increases since 2000 (see below and Figure S1), while China experienced stark increases up to 2016 before decreasing (see Figure 4 below) [4].

A total of 485,424 (95% UI 351,945, 671,381) deaths and 22.1 (95% UI 18.2, 27.5) million DALYs lost were attributable to the consumption of alcohol in the WPR in 2019. This represents 3.5% (95% UI 2.5, 4.8) of all deaths and 4.4% (95% UI 3.6, 5.4) of all DALYs lost within the region. For the region, the age‐standardised alcohol‐attributable burden of disease was 19.7 (95% UI 14.5, 26.6) deaths and 980.3 (95% UI 815.7, 1208.5) DALYs lost per 100,000 people. Overall, China accounted for 73% of all deaths and 61% of the alcohol‐attributable deaths, and 73% of all DALYs as well as 64% of alcohol‐attributable DALYs.

In terms of trends, alcohol‐attributable mortality rates in the WPR overall decreased by 31% between 2000 and 2019, matching the decline in all‐cause mortality rates (Table S1). Alcohol‐attributable mortality decreased less than all‐cause mortality in the first decade, and more in the second decade.

The DALYs attributable to alcohol consist of 16.2 (95% UI 12.9, 20.7) million YLL, representing 5.3% (95% UI 4.2%, 6.7%) of all YLL, and 5.8 (95% UI 5.2, 7.0) million YLD, representing 2.9% (95% UI 2.6%, 3.5%) of total YLD. Regionally, the age‐standardised alcohol‐attributable burden of disease was 714.1 (95% UI 569.8, 904.1) YLL and 266.3 (95% UI 241.5, 313.7) YLD per 100,000 people.

The three primary contributors to the reported alcohol‐attributable deaths in the WPR—in descending order—were haemorrhagic stroke, liver cirrhosis and road injuries. Road injuries, alcohol use disorders and liver cirrhosis were the leading causes of alcohol‐attributable DALYs lost in the region (for details, see Table S2).

As expected, for all burden indicators, the attributable fractions were much higher for males than for females across all ages, since at any age, the prevalence of alcohol consumption and the average amount consumed are higher for males and their drinking patterns are more dangerous. Alcohol proportionally affects mortality the most at younger ages (Figure S2).

As Figures 2 and 3 (and Figures S3 and S4) show, the alcohol‐attributable burden indicators—deaths and DALYs lost—also show wide heterogeneity. While there is some consistency with the global associations, that alcohol‐attributable burden decreases as income increases [17], this average relationship seems to depend on a few countries with disproportionately high burden, that is, Mongolia and Viet Nam in the upper‐middle‐income category, and Cambodia and Lao PDR in the lower middle‐income category (see Figures 2 and 3; for YLLs and YLDs, see Figures S3 and S4).

Discussion

In 2019, almost half a million deaths and more than 22 million DALYs were attributable to alcohol consumption in the WHO WPR, representing 3.5% (95% UI 2.5, 4.8) of all deaths and 4.4% (95% UI 3.6, 5.4) of all DALYs lost. This burden of disease was caused by an average consumption of 6.06 L of pure alcohol per year (95% CI 4.46, 7.84), surpassing the global average of 5.45 L. APC trajectories have differed for different subregions of the WPR: China's APC had increased until 2016, and then decreased dramatically, likely due to an anti‐corruption campaign, which prohibited alcohol use in public and during any official functions [7]. The level of alcohol use in Oceania and Australasia remained stagnant for the two decades preceding 2020, then decreased. Conversely, other Asian countries in the WPR (excluding China) experienced a consistent upward trend until the COVID‐19 pandemic, at which point their average APC began to decrease, with a partial recovery after the pandemic.

The WPR appears to be on track to meet the goals of the Global Alcohol Action Plan 2022–2030 [24] target of a 20% reduction by 2030 relative to the 2010 baseline. Between 2010 and 2024, the region achieved a 34% decrease, largely driven by a 46% reduction in China. However, progress in the other two subregions is less promising: since 2010, APC in other Asian countries within the WPR remained unchanged, while Oceania and Australasia saw a 12% decrease. This lack of progress prompted the WPR to adopt Resolution WPR/RC76.R4, which officially endorses the regional roadmap for accelerating the implementation of the WHO Global Alcohol Action Plan 2022–2030 [25].

Major Alcohol‐Attributable Disease Categories

Liver cirrhosis is the only disease category appearing in the top three conditions for both mortality and DALYs. Alcohol's contribution to the progression of liver diseases has been underestimated due to ICD definitions based on aetiology, which primarily recognise alcohol's role in disease incidence. However, alcohol's role in the course of all liver diseases, irrespective of their underlying aetiology, is significant [26, 27]. This can be quantified by the different, steeper exponential risk curves for mortality compared to morbidity in liver cirrhosis, derived from meta‐analyses [27]. Given the high burden of alcohol‐attributable liver cirrhosis on mortality and DALYs in the WPR, especially in Cambodia, Laos, and Mongolia, prevention of liver problems should be a priority. Such measures should be included not only in major alcohol control policies, but also in policies targeting other aetiologies, such as preventing liver cirrhosis due to hepatitis infection [28] and obesity [29].

The WPR has one of the highest incidences of haemorrhagic stroke globally [30]. Alcohol consumption, along with hypertension, smoking, air pollution, and metabolic risk factors, are the most important risk factors associated with stroke in general and haemorrhagic stroke in particular [30]. However, when examined across all CVDs, the age‐standardised rates of alcohol‐attributable CVD deaths and DALYs lost per 100,000 people are relatively small in the WPR when compared to other WHO regions. This is mainly because the detrimental impact of alcohol use on haemorrhagic stroke is offset by the estimated protective impact of alcohol use on ischaemic disease (Table S2).

However, in this paper, we argue that the protective effects of alcohol use on ischaemic disease may have been overestimated. Current estimates likely exaggerate these effects at the population level for the WPR. This is best exemplified by China, the country with the highest reported protective effect. First, Mendelian Randomisation and similar studies, including those from China [31], have cast doubt on the protective effect [32, 33]; second, some traditional cohort studies from China have shown no protective effects [34]; and third, many epidemiological studies employ designs that lead to biases that inflate the effect [35]. Consequently, future comparative risk assessments for the region should adjust their dose–response estimates to reflect the data from the most rigorous regional studies.

Before we interpret our results further and draw policy implications, we would like to stress the limitations of the current approach.

Limitations

The findings of our study are limited by several factors. First, there is a scarcity of data on the unrecorded use of alcohol, which is a drinking category included in a country's calculation of level of consumption [36], for most countries of the WPR. As it is suspected that approximately 20% of alcohol consumption in the WPR is unrecorded, alcohol exposure and its related burden may be underestimated. Second, road injuries have previously been under‐reported in the region's middle‐income countries due to a lack of comprehensive surveillance systems [37]. This poses a challenge for researchers and policymakers as it obscures the actual burden attributed to alcohol. Third, data for the region is missing on alcohol's harm to other than the drinker which has shown to be large [38]. Additionally, potential mechanistic interactions between alcohol use and other known risk factors that may increase the risk of developing various diseases and conditions were not considered (e.g., tobacco smoking) [39]. Third, due to the lack of data stratifying country‐level estimates of consumption and burden by socioeconomic status and subpopulations, the impact of alcohol use may vary across specific populations in each country. Finally, cultural differences in the accurate detection, diagnosis and reporting of alcohol use disorders may result in an underestimate of the alcohol‐attributable harms [11, 40].

Interpretation

Alcohol use and attributable burden in the WHO WPR are high. However, they are likely underestimated due to several factors: the effect of alcohol use on harm to others, which has not been taken into consideration [41], the potential overestimation of alcohol's protective effect on ischaemic diseases, and the underestimation of the role of unrecorded alcohol.

To accomplish the goals of international global action plans (e.g., [24]) aimed at reducing the detrimental effects of alcohol consumption, countries must implement alcohol control policies that curb consumption. Special attention should be given to countries that experienced near‐steady increases until the pandemic, and carry a high burden of disease attributable to alcohol use (e.g., Cambodia, Lao PDR, Mongolia and Viet Nam), as these countries disproportionately experience the highest rates of alcohol‐related harms per litre of alcohol consumed [42, 43]. These countries had not adopted sufficiently strong alcohol control policies until 2019, compared to some of their neighbours [2, 44, 45]. Since then, some alcohol policies have been implemented [e.g., [46]], but the overall level of alcohol consumption remains high (see above).

National and regional alcohol control policies should be informed by their respective epidemiological profiles. In most middle and high‐income countries in the WPR with a high prevalence of consumption and attributable harm, policies aimed at reducing consumption via a comprehensive ban on marketing [41, 47] along with the regulation of the supply and affordability are key (see also [48]). This is particularly important for countries that have increased their consumption significantly over the past two decades. In countries where the majority of the population abstains, policy opportunities will depend on the legal status; however, there may be a need to prevent marketing efforts, including in digital media, in order to minimise normalisation and to regulate supply, including measures against unrecorded alcohol [49]. In all contexts, there is a need to provide treatment opportunities for those experiencing alcohol use disorder [17].
