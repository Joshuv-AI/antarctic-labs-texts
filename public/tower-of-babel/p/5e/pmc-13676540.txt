Research progress on detection technologies for Mycoplasma pneumoniae
Authors: Huanhuan Wu, Fang Jiang, Siyu Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMycoplasma pneumoniae (M. pneumoniae) is a primary pathogen responsible for community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), particularly prevalent among children and adolescents. The recent global resurgence of infection cases, coupled with the rapid dissemination of macrolide-resistant M. pneumoniae (MRMP), underscores the critical clinical need for rapid and precise diagnostic technologies. This article systematically reviews the evolutionary trajectory of detection technologies, covering the transition from traditional culture and serological testing to modern molecular diagnostic techniques. It critically analyzes emerging platforms, including isothermal amplification, CRISPR/Cas-based diagnostics, microfluidic chips, and biosensors. It explores their potential in facilitating point-of-care testing (POCT) and simultaneous antimicrobial resistance profiling. Despite continuous technological advancements, challenges remain regarding the differentiation between active infection and colonization, as well as the balancing of cost-effectiveness. The future of M. pneumoniae diagnostics lies in the deep integration of multidisciplinary biotechnologies with artificial intelligence, aiming to construct intelligent “sample-to-answer” solutions to optimize clinical antimicrobial stewardship.Keywords: Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Diagnosis, Isothermal amplification, Point-of-care testing (POCT)

Introduction

Pathogenesis and clinical manifestations

Mycoplasma pneumoniae (M. pneumoniae), a prokaryotic microorganism with characteristics intermediate between bacteria and viruses, elicits respiratory tract infections and stands as a primary pathogen responsible for community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), particularly among children and adolescents [1]. Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection is primarily characterized by respiratory symptoms, though it can also affect multiple systems throughout the body. In terms of pulmonary manifestations, the infection is mainly characterized by fever and a dry, irritating cough, with mild physical findings but significant radiographic abnormalities. Regarding extrapulmonary manifestations, approximately one-quarter of children with M. pneumoniae infection may develop extrapulmonary complications. The most characteristic of these are Mycoplasma-induced rash and mucositis (MIRM). The nervous system is also a major site of involvement, and the infection may additionally affect multiple systems, including the cardiovascular, hematological, and digestive systems [1–3].

Epidemiology and disease burden

The epidemiology of M. pneumoniae exhibits marked periodicity and heterogeneity. Unfortunately, M. pneumoniae has demanding nutritional requirements and grows slowly, and the clinical positivity rate is often below 60%, making it difficult for susceptibility test results to guide treatment in real time during the acute phase of infection. While endemic globally, it undergoes epidemic outbreaks spanning extensive geographical areas every few years, with notable surges in case numbers recently observed across multiple European and Asian nations [1]. On a global scale, regional M. pneumoniae epidemics occur every 3 to 7 years, each lasting 1 to 2 years [4]. The disease burden of M. pneumoniae demonstrates considerable variation across age groups and regions. Among adults with CAP, detection rates typically range between 6.5% and 12.3% [8–10], though some studies (e.g., the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s (CDC) Etiology of Pneumonia in the Community (EPIC) study) report figures as low as 2% [11]. In contrast, infection rates are generally higher in the pediatric population. Although traditionally considered most prevalent among school-aged children (5–14 years) [11, 12], research indicates that younger children aged 4 years and below also face a substantial risk of infection [13]. Moreover, co-infections involving M. pneumoniae and other bacteria or viruses are particularly prominent in pediatric patients, with reported incidences as high as 60% [14, 15]. This underscores that pathogen detection focusing solely on a single agent may fail to comprehensively reflect a patient’s infectious status.

Diagnostic challenges and antimicrobial resistance

The clinical management of M. pneumoniae infection currently grapples with the dual challenges of diagnostic uncertainty and ambiguous therapeutic strategies. Clinically, the diagnosis of M. pneumoniae infection lacks specificity; neither clinical symptoms nor routine laboratory tests can reliably distinguish it from other respiratory pathogens such as influenza virus, respiratory syncytial virus, or Streptococcus pneumoniae [16]. Consequently, relying solely on clinical presentation for diagnosis is prone to error. Although the Japanese Respiratory Society (JRS) has developed a scoring system, its sensitivity remains limited [17].

Due to its lack of a cell wall, M. pneumoniae is intrinsically resistant to beta-lactam antibiotics commonly used in clinical practice, such as penicillins and cephalosporins. Therefore, antibiotic treatment options are confined to macrolides, tetracyclines, and/or fluoroquinolones. Given the potential side effects of tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones in young children, macrolide antibiotics (e.g., azithromycin) are consequently the preferred first-line therapy [18]. However, with the widespread use of macrolides, macrolide-resistant M. pneumoniae (MRMP) has rapidly emerged globally, especially in East Asia, with resistance rates exceeding 90% in some regions [19]. With the exception of macrolides, the overall resistance rate to fluoroquinolones is low, while tetracyclines remain highly sensitive, with resistance being rare [20]. The gold standard method for determining M. pneumoniae resistance is traditional bacterial culture combined with phenotypic susceptibility testing. This method determines the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) to assess the strain’s susceptibility to macrolides, fluoroquinolones, and tetracyclines, and can evaluate the synergistic effects of combination therapy [21, 22]. Culture-based susceptibility testing shows that the MIC for azithromycin is significantly elevated in resistant strains [21]. Regarding combination therapy (e.g., macrolides combined with tetracyclines or fluoroquinolones), culture-based methods can determine the combined antibacterial index in vitro, which preliminarily indicates synergistic activity; however, high-quality clinical evidence is currently lacking [23]. Unfortunately, M. pneumoniae has stringent nutritional requirements and grows slowly, with a clinical positivity rate often below 60%. Thus, susceptibility test results cannot guide acute-phase treatment in a timely manner. Under such circumstances, if the pathogen and its resistance profile are not identified early in the infection, empirical use of macrolides may expose patients to risks of protracted illness, increased severe cases, and difficulties in antibiotic selection [21]. Therefore, establishing rapid, accurate diagnostic methods capable of simultaneously providing resistance information is of irreplaceable and critical importance for guiding appropriate antibiotic use, improving patient outcomes, and curbing the spread of antimicrobial resistance.

Evolution of diagnostic technologies

The evolution of M. pneumoniae detection technology profoundly reflects the intrinsic logic of clinical needs driving technological iteration. Early diagnosis primarily relied on culture isolation and serological testing: the former is considered the microbiological “gold standard” but is severely hampered in acute-phase decision-making due to culture periods extending for weeks [24]; the latter, while widely applied, is limited by the antibody “window period” and interference from past infections, making early and precise identification difficult [25]. With advancements in molecular biology, nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs), represented by real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), have established a “new gold standard” for clinical diagnosis owing to their high sensitivity and specificity [26]. However, in response to the escalating crisis of macrolide resistance and the pursuit of timely diagnosis and treatment, current technological innovation is shifting towards a deep integration of speed and precision. On one hand, novel platforms such as isothermal amplification, CRISPR/Cas systems, and microfluidic biosensors are striving to break free from the constraints of complex laboratory environments, advancing diagnostics towards point-of-care testing (POCT) [24]. On the other hand, the application of concurrent resistance gene detection and high-throughput sequencing technologies provides new avenues for addressing mixed infections and guiding precise antimicrobial therapy. Following this trajectory of technological evolution, this article will systematically review the principles, transformations, and clinical applicability of methods ranging from traditional approaches to cutting-edge biosensing technologies. It will critically analyze the strengths and weaknesses of each technology in addressing clinical challenges such as resistance monitoring and distinguishing colonization from active infection, and offer perspectives on the future landscape of integrated diagnosis and treatment. Traditional detection methods for M. pneumoniae

Prior to the widespread adoption of molecular diagnostic techniques, laboratory diagnosis of M. pneumoniae primarily relied on pathogen isolation and detection of host immune responses. These traditional methods laid the foundation for the etiological understanding and epidemiological study of M. pneumoniae, yet their inherent limitations have become increasingly apparent.

Culture isolation

Pathogen culture isolation has long been regarded as the diagnostic “gold standard”, its core value lies in obtaining viable isolates, thereby providing an irreplaceable material basis for subsequent biological studies, epidemiological typing, and phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing [1]. Clinically, respiratory specimens such as nasopharyngeal swabs, oropharyngeal swabs, sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, and tracheal aspirates are commonly used for isolation and culture. Among these, nasopharyngeal swabs are the standard of choice for routine outpatient testing due to their ease of collection and high patient compliance.

Mycoplasma pneumoniae requires extremely demanding culture conditions. The most commonly used media are SP4 liquid and solid media [27], as well as PPLO broth and agar media [28]. All media require the addition of inactivated fetal bovine serum, highly active yeast extract, specific purine growth factors, and penicillin as an antimicrobial inhibitor, while strict measures must be maintained throughout the process to prevent contamination by other microorganisms [29].Under optimal conditions, M. pneumoniae grows slowly, forming colonies with a characteristic “fried-egg” morphology, a process often requiring 2 to 6 weeks of observation [30]. This testing method demonstrates extremely high diagnostic specificity under standardized quality control. When appropriate bacterial identification protocols are followed, specificity reaches 100% [31]. The advantage of the culture and isolation method lies in its ability to yield live bacterial strains, providing a solid foundation for subsequent quantitative determination of minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of antimicrobial agents, evaluation of the antibacterial efficacy of combination therapy, and molecular epidemiological surveillance.

However, the limitations of clinical application are even more pronounced, primarily due to excessively long proliferation cycles and delays in turnover, its inability to meet the urgent need for rapid diagnosis and therapeutic guidance during the acute phase of infection [32]. Furthermore, the sensitivity of culture is significantly influenced by specimen collection quality, transport conditions, and timing of inoculation. Its actual detection rate is often below 60%, and it is highly susceptible to overgrowth by faster-growing contaminating bacteria [33]. For instance, data from the University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB) Diagnostic Mycoplasma Laboratory indicated a culture positivity rate of only 5% among 821 oropharyngeal swabs [1]. Another retrospective analysis of 24,677 respiratory specimens showed that culture detected only 10 positive cases, indicating that this method has extremely low diagnostic performance in real-world clinical settings [34]. For these reasons, pathogen culture is generally not recommended as a routine method for rapid diagnosis or point-of-care susceptibility testing of M. pneumoniae during the acute phase of the disease.

Serological testing

Serological testing for M. pneumoniae infection primarily relies on the detection of the host’s humoral immune response to the pathogen. During infection, the immune system produces specific antibodies against glycolipid and protein antigens, including the P1 adhesin and the community-acquired respiratory distress syndrome (CARDS) toxin, the latter originally discovered by Kannan and Baseman [35]. ELISA methods based on CARDS toxin or its fragments demonstrate good sensitivity and specificity in serological diagnosis [36–38]. Typically, immunoglobulin M (IgM) antibodies become detectable approximately one week after symptom onset, while immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibody titers begin to rise 2 to 3 weeks post-infection, peaking at 3 to 6 weeks [29, 39].

Since the 1980 s, various serological assays have been developed, including enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (EIA), particle agglutination (PA), indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA), and the earlier complement fixation test (CF). With the widespread adoption of automated platforms, EIA and chemiluminescence immunoassays (CLIA) enable high-throughput testing with reduced turnaround times [40]. Additionally, immunochromatographic tests (ICTs) provide rapid, instrument-free alternatives for primary care settings, albeit with variable performance stability [41]. Despite advantages such as convenience in sample collection, the clinical utility of serology is constrained by limitations in sensitivity and specificity. The serological “gold standard” for diagnosis requires a fourfold or greater rise in antibody titer between paired serum samples collected at least 2 weeks apart (typically 3–4 weeks) [40]. Moreover, single-sample IgM testing shows low sensitivity, with positivity rates ranging from 14% to 45% in acute-phase specimens across different commercial kits, and overall sensitivity of only ~ 30–35% in confirmed cases [1]. This is partly attributable to early sampling before antibody production and absent or weak IgM responses in infants or reinfections [41].

Furthermore, the specificity of available assays is frequently compromised by false-positive results due to broad serological cross-reactivity. This cross-reactivity manifests in two primary ways: intra-genus and inter-genus. Within the Mycoplasma genus, shared protein antigens lead to significant cross-reactivity, particularly between M. pneumoniae and Mycoplasma genitalium [42, 43]. More importantly, marked cross-reactivity occurs among atypical respiratory pathogens. Clinical studies have demonstrated that patients with confirmed M. pneumoniae pneumonia often exhibit high rates of false-positive ELISA IgM reactions for Chlamydia pneumoniae [44] and Legionella pneumophila [45]. Additional false positives may arise from viral infections (e.g., EBV, CMV) or non-infectious immune activation. Therefore, serology alone is insufficient for etiological diagnosis, and combined molecular testing is recommended. Additionally, the diagnostic value of IgA remains controversial, as studies have not demonstrated clear superiority over IgM, particularly in pediatric populations [46–48]. To improve diagnostic performance, quantitative and automated platforms such as CLIA have been evaluated. In a pediatric cohort of 387 patients, CLIA-based IgM and IgG measurements correlated well with conventional titers, with IgG achieving a specificity of 87.0%, supporting its potential role in early diagnosis [49].

To enhance specificity, the use of recombinant proteins (e.g., in immunoblot assays) as antigens has become a research focus. The method developed by Dumke et al. demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity compared to PCR [50]. However, these approaches are not yet widely commercialized. Overall, although automated serological platforms have improved efficiency, serology remains limited in early-stage diagnosis due to the intrinsic delay in antibody production and seroconversion dynamics.

Molecular diagnostic technologies

The introduction of molecular diagnostic technologies has revolutionized the diagnostic landscape for M. pneumoniae infections. By directly detecting pathogen nucleic acids, these techniques circumvent the reliance of traditional methods on pathogen growth or host immune response, achieving a qualitative leap in sensitivity, specificity, and speed. Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs)

Conventional PCR and real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) were first applied to detect M. pneumoniae in the late 1980 s, PCR technology, with its superior analytical sensitivity compared to culture and serology and shorter turnaround time, has been regarded as the “new gold standard” for clinical diagnosis [40]. Early conventional polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was used to detect targets such as the ATPase operon and the P1 adhesion protein gene via agarose gel electrophoresis; however, due to its lack of quantitative capabilities, cumbersome procedure, and the risk of contamination from amplicon aerosols, it has largely been replaced by qPCR [51].

As the core technology of NAATs, qPCR enables real-time monitoring and quantification of amplification products within a closed system by incorporating fluorescent dyes (e.g., SYBR Green) or sequence-specific probes (e.g., TaqMan, molecular beacons). This significantly reduces the risk of cross-contamination and enhances specificity. To address the clinical need for differential diagnosis in community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), multiplex real-timide other major atypical pathogens (such as Chlamydia pneumoniae and Legionella), offering a practical, high-sensitivity, and high-throughput diagnostic tool for respiratory specimens [52, 53]. Targeting the three major atypical pathogens associated with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), U.S. CDC researchers developed a single-tube quadruple real-time PCR assay that simultaneously detects Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia pneumoniae, and Legionella. This assay offers a practical, high-sensitivity, and high-throughput method for detecting atypical pneumonia pathogens in respiratory clinical specimens [53].

The detection targets for this qPCR technology include single-copy genes such as the P1 and the CARDS toxin gene, as well as the repMp1 multi-copy repetitive sequence, which theoretically offers higher sensitivity [51, 54]. Performance evaluations of current commercial multiplex PCR kits (e.g., Genmark ePlex, QIAstat-Dx) indicate sensitivities typically above 92% and specificities reaching 99% to 100% [40]. Furthermore, real-time PCR offers the advantage of simultaneous pathogen identification, genotyping, and resistance mutation detection. For instance, a study utilizing molecular beacons not only established a highly sensitive P1 genotyping method (with a limit of detection as low as 1–100 copies/reaction) but also successfully identified the 23 S rRNA gene A2063G macrolide resistance mutation in all positive cases [55]. To ensure assay performance, modern laboratories often combine automated nucleic acid extraction systems to minimize human error and lower the limit of detection, while utilizing internal control systems to monitor for potential PCR inhibitors and prevent false-negative results.

Despite its excellent analytical performance, the clinical application of PCR remains limited by its inability to distinguish between asymptomatic carriage, non-infectious states, or persistent DNA remnants post-treatment. Therefore, interpreting a positive PCR result may still require correlation with paired serology to aid in differentiating colonization from active infection [56, 57].

Isothermal amplification technologies

To overcome PCR’s reliance on precise thermal cycling and relatively long turnaround times, isothermal amplification technologies, represented by loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), have emerged [58]. Isothermal amplification technologies—characterized by constant-temperature reactions—have emerged as pivotal tools for point-of-care testing (POCT) and resource-limited diagnostics for M. pneumoniae [40, 59].

Among these, loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) is most widely adopted, utilizing four to six primers to achieve high specificity [59]. Meta-analyses indicate that LAMP delivers a diagnostic sensitivity of 90% and specificity of 98% for M. pneumoniae [59]. Commercial platforms like the Illumigene system, which targets the protease-like protein gene using simplified heat treatment, and specialized assays targeting the CARDS toxin gene, both demonstrate excellent diagnostic performance [60, 61]. For RNA targets, nucleic acid sequence-based amplification (NASBA) uses a multi-enzyme system to amplify targets like 16 S rRNA under isothermal conditions. Although less common than PCR, NASBA provides unique clinical value by potentially reflecting pathogen viability with analytical sensitivity comparable to PCR [62–64].

Several advanced isothermal variants also show promise for M. pneumoniae screening. Multiple Cross-Displacement Amplification (MCDA), employing a 10-primer design, yields high detection rates in pediatric respiratory specimens and can be coupled with lateral flow biosensors or fluorescent probes to enable rapid, contamination-free diagnostics [65, 66]. Similarly, Multi-enzyme Isothermal Rapid Amplification (MIRA) offers rapid, high-sensitivity detection consistent with commercial qPCR [67]. Alternatively, enzyme-free signal amplification based on catalytic hairpin assembly combined with immunochromatographic strips provides an ultra-low-cost, equipment-free approach for screening in resource-constrained settings [68].

Although isothermal amplification technologies offer clear advantages for rapid screening, challenges remain regarding their capacity for multiplex pathogen detection, reagent stability, and contamination control. Currently, they are unlikely to completely replace PCR’s status as the “gold standard” for diagnosing complex cases [59].

Expansion into high-throughput and multiplex detection

Because differentiating M. pneumoniae from other respiratory pathogens based on symptoms alone is clinically challenging, and co-infections are common, single-pathogen testing often falls short. Consequently, a “syndromic testing” strategy that simultaneously detects multiple pathogens across diversified platforms has emerged. This has fostered a diversified diagnostic landscape ranging from commercial multiplex panels to fully automated high-throughput platforms and ultra-sensitive quantification technologies [40].

For rapid, acute diagnostics, commercial multiplex PCR panels (e.g., BioFire FilmArray, Genmark ePlex, and QIAstat-Dx) utilize “sample-to-answer” nested PCR to deliver comprehensive respiratory profiles within one hour, showing sensitivities above 92% and specificities near 100% [69–72]. Despite optimizing clinical workflows, their widespread adoption is constrained by high costs. Furthermore, certain panels rely on a single target for M. pneumoniae, which—unlike the multi-target designs used for influenza—may increase the risk of false-negatives due to sequence variations, alongside the technical challenge of managing primer-dimer interactions in multiplex architectures [73, 74].

To further enhance diagnostic efficiency and reduce the costs associated with repeated sampling and testing, researchers have conducted extensive exploration. For instance, Hu et al. presented a low-cost alternative by combining multiplex PCR with dipstick chromatography for visual readout. This multiplex PCR-dipstick DNA chromatography method enables simultaneous detection of M. pneumoniae and C. pneumoniae within 2 h, demonstrating high concordance with sequencing and a significantly higher positive detection rate compared to traditional serological IgM testing [75]. For large-scale epidemiological surveillance, laboratory-developed tests (LDTs) implemented on high-throughput, automated open platforms (such as the Hologic Panther Fusion system) have demonstrated strong clinical robustness during major public health outbreaks [76].

Recently, droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) has been applied to achieve absolute quantification and enhanced sensitivity. By partitioning reactions into tens of thousands of individual droplets, ddPCR exhibits superior tolerance to PCR inhibitors and lowers the limit of detection significantly compared to conventional qPCR. Applied to multiplex panels targeting M. pneumoniae and concurrent pathogens like S. pneumoniae, ddPCR effectively mitigates false-negatives in clinical samples with low pathogen loads [77].

Simultaneous detection of antibiotic resistance

As M. pneumoniae continues to circulate globally, macrolide-resistant M. pneumoniae (MRMP), driven by antimicrobial resistance (AMR), has emerged and spread widely, becoming a prominent public health issue and clinical challenge in the field of respiratory infections worldwide. Global AMR surveillance data indicate that macrolide resistance in M. pneumoniae follows a typical pattern of acquired resistance, exhibiting significant regional clustering. While overall AMR levels remain relatively low in Western countries such as Europe and the United States, East Asia is a key region for global AMR prevention and control efforts regarding M. pneumoniae. Against this backdrop, the development of rapid diagnostic technologies capable of simultaneously identifying the pathogen and assessing its resistance holds significant public health implications for the precise control of M. pneumoniae AMR outbreaks, the containment of the spread of resistant bacterial clones, and the guidance of early, targeted treatment.

Clinical context and resistance mechanisms

Since 2000, macrolide-resistant Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MRMP) infections have increased globally, with the Western Pacific Region (notably China, Japan, and South Korea) reporting the highest average resistance rate of 53.4%, and some areas exceeding 79.5% [78]. Clinically, while MRMP does not inherently increase disease severity, it significantly diminishes the efficacy of macrolide therapy. Compared to susceptible strains, MRMP infections result in prolonged fever and hospitalization, a 21.24-fold higher risk of persistent fever after 48 h, and a 4.42-fold increased likelihood of switching to second-line antibiotics, thereby escalating the overall healthcare burden [79]. At the molecular level, this resistance is primarily driven by point mutations in domain V of the 23 S rRNA gene, most predominantly A2063G [80]. In East Asia, MRMP dissemination is strongly associated with specific clonal lineages (e.g., P1 type 1, MLVA type 4–5-7-2, and ST3), highlighting the value of genotype monitoring for predicting regional resistance trends [80].

Research progress in methods for simultaneous resistance detection

Given that MRMP infection leads to treatment failure and prolonged illness, there is a pressing clinical need to determine resistance status at the initial diagnostic stage. Although culture combined with antimicrobial susceptibility testing remains the “gold standard” for resistance diagnosis, its extended turnaround time renders it nearly impractical for acute-phase clinical decision-making [81]. Consequently, nucleic acid amplification-based molecular diagnostic techniques have become the preferred strategy for achieving simultaneous resistance detection in clinical practice due to their speed and sensitivity.

Real-time PCR and melting curve analysis

Current expert consensus recommends employing PCR combined with fluorescent probe technology to directly detect resistance gene mutations in respiratory specimens such as throat swabs, sputum, or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid [21]. Among these, high-resolution melting (HRM) curve analysis is widely applied. This technique exploits the subtle differences in melting temperature (Tm) between amplicons from mutant (e.g., A2063G) and wild-type strains, stemming from their differing base compositions, allowing for direct analysis post-PCR amplification. This method not only qualitatively detects M. pneumoniae DNA but also effectively discriminates between MRMP and MSMP, significantly shortening the diagnostic window [80].

Pyrosequencing

In clinical practice, some patients may harbor mixed infections of resistant and susceptible strains or be undergoing a transition where the resistant population gradually becomes dominant. For such complex scenarios, pyrosequencing has proven to be an effective tool for quantifying the proportion of MRMP to MSMP within a sample [80]. Although this technique has higher equipment requirements, it can capture low-abundance resistance mutations, providing precise data for studying the in vivo evolution of resistance.

Commercial rapid molecular diagnostic kits

To meet the clinical demand for POCT, commercial kits for resistance detection have made substantial progress in recent years. For example, kits utilizing quenched probe (Q-probe) technology have entered clinical application [21]. This technology identifies point mutations in target DNA through specific fluorescence quenching. The entire process, including nucleic acid extraction and amplification/detection, requires only about 2 h [21]. This enhanced diagnostic capability enables clinicians to obtain resistance information on the same day of the patient’s visit. Consequently, evidence-based decisions can be made to initiate second-line drugs like doxycycline or fluoroquinolones (for children over 8 years or severe cases) at the outset of treatment, effectively avoiding the ineffective use of macrolides [21].

Therefore, with the evolution of molecular diagnostics, the shift from mere pathogen detection to simultaneous resistance detection has become an inevitable trend. Promoting the application of these rapid and precise molecular detection tools is of irreplaceable clinical value for optimizing antimicrobial stewardship, improving patient outcomes, and curbing the further spread of resistant strains, especially in regions with high resistance prevalence.

Next-generation detection technologies

The growing clinical demand for diagnostic precision and comprehensiveness has spurred the emergence of a series of novel technologies aimed at overcoming the limitations of existing molecular diagnostics. These technologies not only strive for superior performance but also seek to reshape diagnostic paradigms, propelling the diagnosis of M. pneumoniae infection towards earlier, smarter, and more integrated approaches.

CRISPR/Cas systems

The discovery of CRISPR/Cas systems (e.g., Cas12a, Cas13a) has revolutionized molecular diagnostics through their “off-target” (collateral) cleavage activity. Guided by gRNA, the Cas protein binds specifically to target nucleic acids, which activates its enzymatic ability to indiscriminately cleave reporter molecules and amplify fluorescent signals [82]. This single-nucleotide resolution enables rapid, sequencing-free identification of macrolide resistance mutations, such as the A2063G mutation in the 23 S rRNA gene [79, 82].

To meet the high sensitivity required for clinical diagnostics, Cas proteins are frequently coupled with isothermal amplification. Combining Cas12a with ERA achieves a limit of detection (LoD) of 1 copy/µL within 30 min—rivaling gold-standard qPCR but without complex equipment [83]. Utilizing MCDA lowers the LoD to 50 fg [84], while RPA-based platforms provide stable detection at human body temperature (37–39 °C), effectively eliminating the need for expensive thermal cyclers [85].

A major barrier to clinical adoption is aerosol contamination. To overcome this and enable true Point-of-Care Testing (POCT), miniaturized closed-tube systems have been developed. Innovations such as a “movable magnetic bead valve“ [86] and a palm-sized 3D-printed device capable of simultaneously screening four respiratory pathogens—directly addressing the clinical challenge of complex co-infections [87]—have successfully transformed CRISPR from a laboratory-bound technique into a field-deployable diagnostic tool.

Furthermore, conventional “one-pot” assays suffer from premature template cleavage by highly active Cas12a [88]. Researchers resolved this kinetic contradiction by utilizing the thermostable Cas12b. Coupling Cas12b with LAMP enables rapid, one-pot detection at a constant 57 °C [89]. Other advanced one-pot strategies leverage precise spatial-temporal control to prevent early template consumption. A thermally activated RPA-CRISPR-Cas12b system isolates the amplification (39 °C) and detection (66 °C) phases to achieve a 100-fold sensitivity increase [88]. Similarly, the HOLMESv2 platform uses a photolabile group on the gRNA to keep the system silenced during LAMP, subsequently activating Cas12b via UV irradiation to increase sensitivity 133-fold [90]. Integrated with lyophilized reagents and smartphone readers, these regulated approaches offer highly sensitive and robust POCT solutions.

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS)

For critically ill pneumonia patients with complex clinical presentations, or immunocompromised patients who test negative with conventional methods, mNGS offers an “unbiased” comprehensive diagnostic solution. Unlike the “preset target” logic of PCR, mNGS sequences all nucleic acids in a sample, enabling simultaneous screening for thousands of pathogens, including M. pneumoniae, in a single run [91].

In the diagnosis of M. pneumoniae infection, the value of mNGS lies in addressing complex mixed infections. It can not only detect M. pneumoniae but also simultaneously reveal co-infecting influenza viruses, adenoviruses, or drug-resistant bacteria, buying crucial time for managing critically ill patients. However, high testing costs, complex bioinformatics analysis pipelines, and overwhelming background interference from the host human genome (often constituting > 90% of sequences) remain major bottlenecks preventing its adoption as a first-line routine test [92].

Microfluidic chip systems and biosensors

Integration and automation in microfluidic chip systems

To achieve fully automated “sample-to-answer” workflows, lab-on-a-chip (LOC) or microfluidic chip technology has emerged. This technology integrates all steps of nucleic acid extraction, amplification, and detection onto a miniature chip, precisely controlling fluid movement through microchannels, thereby drastically reducing sample consumption and the risk of cross-contamination [93]. Centrifugal microfluidic chips are particularly effective at automating complex sample preparation. For example, a novel multi-valve system with a “delayed-release structure” optimizes magnetic bead washing to ensure high-yield extraction. This platform delivers M. pneumoniae test results in under 10 min, making it highly valuable for rapid emergency triage [94]. To meet the demands of high-throughput clinical screening, the CHAMP system scales this capability to process 48 samples simultaneously. By seamlessly integrating extraction with a one-pot CRISPR/Cas12b-LAMP reaction, it achieves an ultra-low LoD of 0.5 copies/µL. Validated on 427 clinical samples, CHAMP provides a robust, automated solution for managing large-scale disease outbreaks [95].

Furthermore, to address the clinical challenge of respiratory co-infections, pneumatic microfluidic cartridges excel in multiplexed diagnostics. A 10-channel closed-system chip enables the simultaneous differential diagnosis of M. pneumoniae, H1N1, RSV A, and SARS-CoV-2. By automating reagent distribution within a sealed environment, this platform eliminates cross-contamination risks, providing clinicians with a reliable tool to rapidly identify mixed infections and guide targeted treatments [96].

Biosensor systems

Biosensors convert biological recognition into measurable signals, driving the shift of M. pneumoniae diagnostics from centralized laboratories to point-of-care testing (POCT) [97, 98]. For primary healthcare settings, lateral flow biosensors (LFBs) are invaluable as they provide rapid, instrument-free visual results. Coupling LFBs with isothermal amplification (e.g., MCDA or LAMP) enables highly sensitive screening (LoD of 50 fg) within an hour, demonstrating significantly higher clinical detection rates than traditional culture and PCR [65, 97].

To overcome the risk of false positives caused by primer dimers in visual assays, a novel CRISPR/Cas9-based LFB uses the Cas complex solely as a “recognition probe” for secondary verification. This ensures highly reliable diagnosis (LoD of 3 copies) at room temperature, preventing clinical misdiagnosis [99]. To further simplify clinical workflows, a modified Cas12f_ge4.1 biosensor was engineered to directly recognize double-stranded DNA, eliminating cumbersome preprocessing steps and accelerating the “sample-to-answer” turnaround [100].

Beyond rapid screening, modern biosensors are advancing precision medicine and digitalization. Ion-sensitive field-effect transistor biosensors translate amplification-induced pH changes directly into electrical signals on a microchip. This label-free approach achieves single-copy sensitivity, laying the groundwork for real-time, handheld diagnostic devices [98]. Crucially, to address the severe clinical challenge of macrolide resistance, a highly flexible Argonaute (Ago)-based biosensor was developed. Free from CRISPR’s target sequence constraints, it accurately identifies the A2063G resistance mutation with single-nucleotide resolution and 100% clinical specificity, providing physicians with immediate, actionable data to guide targeted antibiotic therapy [101].

In summary, biosensor technology in the field of M. pneumoniae detection is undergoing a transition from “qualitative screening” to “precision diagnosis.” These innovative achievements collectively propel M. pneumoniae diagnostics towards being faster, more accurate, and more portable.

Challenges

Despite continuous innovation in detection technologies, a multi-dimensional comparison of different generations of methods reveals their varying clinical applicability. However, a profound disconnect persists between ideal laboratory performance and the complex, variable realities of clinical needs. These deep-seated contradictions represent current technological bottlenecks and point toward future research directions.

First, clinical diagnosis perpetually seeks a balance between sensitivity, speed, and cost, a triad often difficult to reconcile. High-sensitivity, high-specificity methods exemplified by qPCR come at the cost of heavy reliance on standardized laboratory environments, sophisticated instrumentation, and specialized personnel. This conflicts with the need for rapid, simple, and low-cost solutions in primary healthcare settings. Conversely, POCT technologies prioritizing extreme speed often necessitate compromises in accuracy or specificity. How to break this constraint through technological innovation is key to achieving equitable access to high-quality diagnostics. Second, with the global spread of MRMP strains, reporting “M. pneumoniae positive” alone is insufficient to guide precise antimicrobial therapy. However, traditional phenotypic susceptibility testing depends on pathogen isolation and culture, a process taking weeks, which lags entirely behind the clinical decision-making window for acute treatment. While existing molecular methods (e.g., qPCR) can rapidly detect common resistance gene mutations, their detection scope is limited, and they lack the capacity to identify rare or emerging mutations. Developing molecular technologies that can be performed concurrently with rapid pathogen nucleic acid detection and cover a broader spectrum of resistance determinants is an urgent need to achieve “integrated diagnosis and treatment.”

Furthermore, the lack of technical standardization and the dilemma in result interpretation constitute core obstacles affecting diagnostic consistency. Currently, there is a lack of unified standards for reagents (including primer/probe targets) from different manufacturers and for operational protocols across different laboratories, leading to poor comparability of test results. A more fundamental challenge is that highly sensitive nucleic acid detection (including qPCR and mNGS) struggles to effectively differentiate, at the molecular level, between asymptomatic respiratory colonization and active M. pneumoniae infection. This is also the key challenge currently hindering the precise clinical application of nucleic acid testing and complicating clinical diagnosis and treatment [102]. Carriage of low-level, non-pathogenic M. pneumoniae is not uncommon in children. Solely relying on a positive nucleic acid result can mislead clinical judgment, potentially leading to antibiotic overuse [102]. To address this key clinical challenge, domestic and international research has established a mature, practical, and multidimensional integrated diagnostic strategy for accurately distinguishing between asymptomatic carriage and active infection. First, quantitative detection of pathogen nucleic acid load serves as the primary basis for differentiation. The number of M. pneumoniae nucleic acid copies in respiratory specimens from patients with active infection is significantly higher than in asymptomatic colonized individuals. Clinically, by establishing standardized threshold values for nucleic acid load, the detection of high pathogen load, when combined with clinical manifestations, is more indicative of active infection rather than asymptomatic colonization, thereby significantly enhancing the diagnostic accuracy of nucleic acid testing alone. Second, replacing DNA testing with RNA testing is currently one of the most viable strategies. Since RNA is present only in live bacteria and degrades rapidly after cell death, a positive RNA result indicates a recent or active infection, whereas a positive DNA result may stem from residual DNA fragments of bacteria that have already been cleared. Finally, measuring antigen-presenting cells (ASC-ELISpot) can accurately distinguish between infection and carriage status with a single blood draw. Studies have shown that, with PCR testing as the gold standard, the detection of specific IgM-secreting cells demonstrates extremely high discriminatory ability in distinguishing between M. pneumoniae infection and carrier status. None of the carriers in the control group who were PCR-positive but clinically asymptomatic tested positive for IgM-ASC activity, highlighting the excellent positive predictive value of this technique [103].

Finally, the impact of specimen collection site on results is a critical, yet often overlooked, variable. For routine outpatients, throat swabs or nasopharyngeal swabs are the most common specimens. However, their detection rate is highly susceptible to sampling quality and may not accurately reflect the pathogen load in the lower respiratory tract. In patients with severe or refractory M. pneumoniae pneumonia, it is not uncommon for upper respiratory samples to test nucleic acid-negative while BALF tests positive [104]. BALF, obtained directly from the site of infection, holds far greater diagnostic and therapeutic evaluation value compared to throat swabs. Therefore, establishing a stratified clinical diagnostic pathway is crucial: rapid non-invasive throat swab screening can be used for mildly symptomatic suspected cases, while for severe or treatment-unresponsive patients, acquiring lower respiratory tract specimens via invasive methods like BALF should be actively considered to achieve precise diagnosis [104].

Future perspectives

The core challenges in current diagnostics fundamentally stem from the multifaceted contradictions between the limits of technological performance, real-world clinical needs, and economic cost-effectiveness. Additionally, seamlessly integrating resistance analysis into rapid diagnostic workflows and accurately distinguishing pathogen colonization from active infection remain unresolved clinical dilemmas. The future of M. pneumoniae detection technology will not be a linear upgrade of a single technology but rather a systematic innovation driven by multidisciplinary integration. Its development will be closely aligned with clinical scenarios, evolving toward greater precision, intelligence, and accessibility. Future detection platforms will exhibit a high degree of integration. CRISPR technology is expected to deeply integrate with lateral flow or microfluidic chips, combining the high specificity of gene editing with the portability for home-based self-testing. Furthermore, to address the high cost of mNGS, targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) is emerging as a compromise solution. By enriching and sequencing specific regions of common respiratory pathogens, tNGS significantly reduces cost and data redundancy, showing promise as a first-line test for severe infections [105]. The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) will transform the paradigm of result interpretation. By applying machine learning algorithms to integrate patient data such as chest CT imaging features, routine blood test indices, and nucleic acid test cycle threshold (Ct) values, AI models can assist physicians in more accurately assessing disease severity and even prospectively predicting resistance risk [106]. Looking further ahead, future diagnostics will transcend the mere identification of pathogens. By combining data from host metabolomics and transcriptomics, a dual “pathogen + host” diagnostic model could be constructed. This approach holds the potential to effectively resolve the clinical conundrum of differentiating colonization from active infection. Through the deep integration of cutting-edge biotechnologies like CRISPR and microfluidics with artificial intelligence, the future of M. pneumoniae diagnostics promises to provide the critical technological support needed to curb the spread of antimicrobial resistance and safeguard respiratory health, particularly in children.
