Oritavancin as rescue therapy in severe methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus pneumonia: A case report
Authors: Francesco Virzì, Giuseppe Giacopelli, Paolo Tutone, Sandro Tomasello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere pneumonia caused by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) represents a major therapeutic challenge due to the high risk of clinical failure and the limited efficacy of conventional antibiotics in critically ill patients.Case presentationWe report the case of a 65-year-old woman with severe MRSA pneumonia, rapidly progressing to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and apparently refractory to Linezolid and Ceftriaxone. Introduction

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) pneumonia represents a major cause of morbidity and mortality among hospitalized patients, especially those in Intensive Care Units (ICUs). Although relatively uncommon, its incidence has increased in recent years, paralleling the spread of multidrug-resistant strains. International guidelines (IDSA, ESCMID) recommend Vancomycin or Linezolid as first-line treatment [[1], [2], [3]]; however, therapeutic failure is not infrequent, particularly in cases with high bacterial burden, reduced pulmonary penetration, or severe systemic illness [[4], [5], [6]].

Oritavancin is a next-generation semisynthetic lipoglycopeptide [Fig. 1], derived from the vancomycin scaffold and modified by a lipophilic side chain that enhances antibacterial activity and confers unique pharmacokinetic properties. These structural modifications result in an exceptionally long plasma half-life (>200 hours), providing prolonged therapeutic coverage and sustained bactericidal activity after a single intravenous dose.

Oritavancin: Mechanism of action.

Oritavancin exerts a multimodal mechanism of action distinct from traditional glycopeptides. It inhibits peptidoglycan synthesis by binding to the D-Ala-D-Ala terminus of cell wall precursors, disrupts transpeptidase-mediated cross-linking, and—through its lipophilic moiety—interacts directly with the cytoplasmic membrane, increasing permeability and leading to depolarization and cell death. This triple mechanism provides potent bactericidal activity against Gram-positive organisms, including MRSA, vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus spp. (VRE), and other resistant pathogens [7,8].

Although currently approved for acute bacterial skin and skin structure infections (ABSSSIs), its pharmacodynamic profile and emerging evidence from in vitro and clinical observations suggest potential utility in more complex systemic infections, including MRSA bacteremia and endocarditis, though these indications remain off-label [9,10].

Pharmacokinetically, Oritavancin exhibits extensive tissue distribution and high plasma protein binding, contributing to its prolonged residence time. It undergoes minimal hepatic metabolism and is primarily excreted unchanged via non-renal pathways, predominantly fecal, with limited urinary elimination. Consequently, dosing is independent of renal function, and hepatic impairment generally does not necessitate adjustment.

Case presentation

A 65-year-old woman presented to the Emergency Department of Ospedale Civico di Partinico on September 14, 2025, with progressive dyspnea, fever (39 °C), and hypotension (BP 80/50 mmHg), partially responsive to fluid therapy. Her past medical history included chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS), a previous myocardial infarction (2023) treated with coronary stenting, and insulin-dependent type 2 diabetes mellitus. On examination, she was tachypneic, dyspneic, and diaphoretic, with oxygen saturation of 88% in room air. Chest auscultation revealed bilateral crackles, more prominent at the left base.

Initial laboratory tests showed leukocytosis (10,650/μL), elevated C-reactive protein (CRP 7.95 mg/dL), and markedly increased procalcitonin (35 ng/mL). Arterial blood gas analysis with oxygen mask (4 L/min) revealed PaO2 82 mmHg with normal pH, bicarbonates, and lactate. Chest CT showed extensive consolidation in the left lower lobe and bilateral ground-glass opacities [Fig. 2]. Empiric antibiotic therapy with Ceftriaxone 2 g/day IV was started, and the patient was admitted to the Internal Medicine ward.

Chest CT scan 14-09-2025, case presentation.

Over the following days, respiratory symptoms worsened, with persistent fever and increasing oxygen requirement. Biofire® molecular panel from sputum was positive for Staphylococcus spp. carrying mecA/C and MREJ genes, consistent with MRSA infection. Therapy was escalated to Linezolid 600 mg IV every 12 hours, maintaining Ceftriaxone.

On September 20 (day 6), despite high-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO) at 60 L/min, FiO2 95%, the patient developed acute hypoxemia (PaO2/FiO2 < 60) [Fig. 3]. She was intubated and placed on controlled mechanical ventilation (Psupp 20 cmH2O, FiO2 100%, PEEP 12 cmH2O) with sedation and neuromuscular blockade. Prone positioning for 12 hours provided mild improvement (PaO2 73 mmHg with FiO2 80% and PEEP 8 cmH2O), with norepinephrine support (0.6–0.8 μg/kg/min).

Chest CT scan 20-09-2025, pre-tracheal intubation.

Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) confirmed MRSA sensitive to linezolid, and ceftriaxone was therefore discontinued. Despite targeted therapy, the patient progressed to severe ARDS secondary to MRSA sepsis, with worsening respiratory function and marked systemic inflammation. Given the poor prognosis, the lack of clinical response to first-line therapy with linezolid - currently recommended as the antibiotic of choice for MRSA infections [11] - the extremely rapid clinical deterioration requiring an effective treatment within a very limited time frame, on September 21 the multidisciplinary team initiated off-label rescue therapy with oritavancin, administered as a single dose of 1200 mg IV over 3 hours diluted in 1000 mL of 5% dextrose. No adverse reactions were observed.

Within 24 hours, gas exchange improved significantly (PaO2 81 mmHg with FiO2 50%, PEEP 7 cmH2O) and norepinephrine requirement decreased (0.3 μg/kg/min). After 48 hours (September 23), the patient was extubated, hemodynamically stable, and breathing spontaneously with oxygen mask (8 L/min), Pro-Calcitonin, 0.54ng/mL and PCR 3.88mg/dl. Follow-up CT showed marked reduction of pulmonary consolidation, mainly in the left lower lobe [Fig. 4]. By September 29, she was transferred to the General Medicine ward, eupneic on spontaneous breathing (SpO2 96%) and afebrile. She was discharged home on October 8, 2025, in good clinical condition and without supplemental oxygen [Fig. 5] after a total of 14 days of linezolid therapy.

Discussion

Oritavancin is a semisynthetic lipoglycopeptide exhibiting potent bactericidal activity against multidrug-resistant Gram-positive pathogens, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). Its mechanism of action involves inhibition of peptidoglycan polymerization and cross-linking, together with disruption of the bacterial cell membrane. Currently, the Italian Medicines Agency (AIFA) has approved solely for the treatment of acute bacterial skin and skin structure infections (ABSSSI) in adult and pediatric patients aged ≥3 months. Nevertheless, increasing scientific interest has focused on the potential off-label use of oritavancin in severe invasive Gram-positive infections. Available evidence includes studies evaluating its use in Gram-positive infective endocarditis [12], as well as a retrospective observational study assessing the effectiveness, safety, and clinical outcomes of oritavancin as sequential (consolidation) therapy in adult patients with Gram-positive bloodstream infections, including infective endocarditis, following initial standard antimicrobial therapy [13]. In addition, in vitro investigations have demonstrated concentration-dependent bactericidal activity of oritavancin against MSSA, MRSA, VISA, and VRSA in time-kill assays, with more rapid bactericidal effects compared with vancomycin, teicoplanin, linezolid, and daptomycin [14]. Further experimental studies have shown that oritavancin is active against S. aureus in the stationary growth phase and within biofilms, including MRSA and VRSA strains, with comparable MIC and MBEC values, suggesting potential efficacy against bacterial populations tolerant to multiple antimicrobial agents [5]. Moreover, surveillance studies have consistently reported potent in vitro activity against clinical MRSA isolates, with MIC90 values of approximately 0.06–0.12 μg/mL and very high susceptibility rates (≈99.5–99.7%) [15].

In the present case, the patient failed to achieve a rapid or clinically meaningful response to linezolid despite confirmed in vitro susceptibility. This lack of efficacy may reasonably be attributed to inadequate pulmonary drug exposure in the setting of severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), marked systemic inflammation, and the presence of biofilm, all of which are known to impair effective antibiotic penetration and activity at the site of infection.

On this basis, treatment was escalated to oritavancin, a lipoglycopeptide structurally related to vancomycin that shares the pharmacophore responsible for inhibition of transpeptidation of terminal D-Ala–D-Ala peptide chains during bacterial cell wall synthesis. Oritavancin is derived from vancomycin through the addition of long lipophilic side chains, a modification that markedly increases its lipophilicity and results in a very large volume of distribution (Vd ≈ 0.8–1.5 L/kg, compared with vancomycin Vd ≈ 0.4–1 L/kg). These pharmacokinetic characteristics translate into extensive tissue distribution and excellent parenchymal penetration, as demonstrated in preclinical models.

In addition, oritavancin exhibits a prolonged terminal elimination half-life of approximately 250 hours, effectively qualifying it as a long-acting antimicrobial agent, with no clinically relevant nephrotoxicity reported at recommended dosages. These favorable pharmacokinetic properties, together with its additional bactericidal mechanisms — including insertion of the lipophilic side chain into the bacterial cell membrane with subsequent membrane depolarization and disruption of membrane integrity, as well as inhibition of protein synthesis — may be particularly advantageous in clinical scenarios characterized by rapid clinical deterioration despite the use of guideline-recommended first-line therapies.

The off-label administration of single-dose oritavancin in combination with linezolid as rescue therapy was temporally associated with a rapid clinical and radiological improvement within 48 hours, permitting successful extubation and complete clinical recovery. This observation is consistent with previously reported in vitro findings [16]. No treatment-related adverse events were observed, in line with the favorable safety profile described in clinical studies. Although clinical evidence supporting the use of oritavancin for methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) pneumonia remains extremely limited, this case adds to the emerging body of literature suggesting a potential role for oritavancin as a rescue therapeutic option in selected critically ill patients with refractory MRSA infections.

Conclusion

This case illustrates that oritavancin may represent a valuable rescue option in selected critically ill patients with MRSA pneumonia who are unresponsive to conventional antibiotic therapy and experience rapid, progressive clinical deterioration. The drug demonstrated satisfactory clinical efficacy, good tolerability, and practical advantages related to its single-dose intravenous administration—particularly relevant for patients with renal impairment. While current evidence is limited to isolated case experiences, these preliminary results highlight growing interest in the potential use of Oritavancin for severe Gram-positive respiratory infections, especially those refractory to standard therapies. Future prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings and define its role in MRSA pneumonia management.
