Tailoring Na⁺ Chelation Dynamics for Expedient Sulfur Redox Kinetics in Low‐Temperature Sodium–Sulfur Batteries
Authors: Min‐Hao Pai, Arumugam Manthiram
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSodium–sulfur (Na–S) batteries have attracted considerable attention due to their high theoretical energy density and the abundant natural availability of sodium and sulfur. However, sluggish kinetics of sulfur conversion, slow Na⁺ transport, and interfacial instability at low temperatures pose significant challenges for their operation and limit their practical application. Herein, three solvents with well‐designed molecular configurations are examined. We systematically investigate the impact of chelation effect on the desolvation behavior, sulfur conversion process, ion dynamics, and Na⁺ plating/stripping behavior. Compared with conventional linear ether solvents, the incorporation of methyl groups not only weaken the chelation capability and tailors the inner solvation sheath, but also reduces the energy barrier for Na⁺ transport, thus promoting enhanced sulfur conversion kinetics under low temperature conditions. This work elucidates the relationship among solvent molecules, Na⁺ desolvation behavior, and sulfur reaction kinetics, and offers a strategy for rational design of electrolytes for low‐temperature metal–sulfur batteries.Keywords: Chelation effect, Low‐temperature electrochemistry, Sodium–sulfur batteries, SPAN cathode, Sulfur redox kinetics

Introduction

Sodium–sulfur (Na–S) batteries have emerged as a promising candidate for next‐generation scalable energy storage, offering a high theoretical energy density of 1,274 Wh kg−1 and benefiting from the significantly greater natural abundance of sodium (2.3 wt%) compared to lithium (0.0017 wt%).[ 1 ] Nevertheless, several critical technological challenges hinder the performance of Na–S batteries, including the pronounced shuttling effect of highly soluble polysulfides and poor electrolyte–electrode compatibility. Furthermore, the sulfur conversion process in Na–S batteries exhibits slower kinetics compared to that in lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries. This is primarily attributed to the poor electronic conductivity of elemental sulfur and the final conversion product, Na2S, as well as the sluggish Na⁺ ion diffusion and lower Na⁺ ionic conductivity resulting from the larger atomic radius of sodium.[ 2 ] Crucially, Na⁺ ion diffusion is more temperature‐sensitive than Li⁺, making the solvation structure associated with Na⁺ at lower temperatures particularly relevant. At reduced temperatures, the solvation sheaths around Na⁺ ions become more stable and tightly bound, resulting in higher desolvation barriers that increase the charge‐transfer impedance and hinder the electrochemical kinetics.[ 3 , 4 , 5 ] Consequently, the electrochemical performance of Na–S batteries at moderate and especially at low temperatures remains a challenge in meeting the demand for deployment in extreme environments.

Modulating the solvation structure of electrolytes is a highly effective strategy for optimizing the sulfur conversion processes and improving the overall battery performance.[ 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 ] The composition and configuration of the solvation sheath play a crucial role in governing ion‐transport kinetics, influencing cation desolvation behavior at the electrode–electrolyte interface, ion migration across interfacial layers, and ionic diffusion within electrode matrices. Additionally, the solvation structure plays a critical role in determining the formation of electrode–electrolyte interphases by governing the preferential decomposition pathways of electrolyte components. The electrode–electrolyte interphase directly affects Na⁺ plating/stripping behavior and the cycling stability of the battery. Furthermore, in the Na–S system, polysulfide solubility is largely influenced by the solvation structure within the electrolyte, which dictates the spatial distribution and interaction dynamics of ionic species. A well‐defined solvation structure can effectively regulate polysulfide dissolution by altering the ionic environment and reducing the availability of free polysulfides in the electrolyte.[ 9 ] Therefore, by modulating the solvation structures, electrolytes can minimize undesirable polysulfide migration, thereby suppressing the shuttle effect and enhancing the overall cycling stability of the battery. As a result, exploration of electrolytes with ideal solvation behavior is of significant importance.

Concretely, tuning the solvation sheath in electrolytes can be achieved by adjusting salt concentration, selecting suitable solvents,[ 11 , 12 , 13 ] introducing functional anion additives.[ 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 ] Although high salt concentrations promote anion‐rich environments favorable for Na⁺ desolvation, they also lead to drawbacks like low salt dissociation, reduced conductivity, and increased viscosity, especially problematic at low temperatures.[ 21 ] Alternatively, weakly solvating solvents with tunable features like number of oxygen atoms and steric hindrance enable the design of favorable solvation structures, while avoiding high salt concentrations.[ 22 ] These approaches enhance ion transport and electrolyte performance. Importantly, the chelation effect, characterized by the formation of a stable five‐membered ring between bidentate ligands and the cation, exerts a profound influence on both the solvating capacity and the structural arrangement of the solvation sheath.[ 23 , 24 ]

Chelation chemistry has been extensively explored in lithium‐metal batteries,[ 23 , 24 ] demonstrating that the formation of stable coordination complexes between multidentate ligands and Li⁺ ions significantly influence the stability and performance of lithium‐metal anodes. Overall, chelation effects enable fine control of the cation solvation sheath, improving ionic transport, promoting electrochemical kinetics, and fostering uniform lithium deposition. Moreover, solvents with precisely tuned chelation chemistry can effectively suppress dendritic growth by influencing local ion distribution and interfacial electric fields. Additionally, the well‐designed solvation structure derived from the chelation effect guides the formation of robust solid‐electrolyte interphases enriched with inorganic components, which significantly enhance interfacial stability and cycling durability. Although the role of chelation interactions has been explored in lithium‐metal batteries, sulfur‐based systems present additional complexity arising from the formation and behavior of polysulfides, which are likely further modulated by molecular chelation effects. To the best of our knowledge, comprehensive studies examining the role of chelation chemistry in shaping solvation structures, governing sulfur conversion processes, and mitigating polysulfide dissolution in Na–S batteries have yet to be reported, despite their substantial promise for enhancing battery performance.

Herein, we pioneer the application of chelation chemistry in Na–S batteries, especially for low‐temperature conditions, presenting a tailored strategy to anchor and modulate sodium polysulfide species and improve the sulfur conversion kinetics through molecular chelation. We systematically investigate the effect of structure‐dependent chelation chemistry on the sulfur conversion process and explore the impact of solvent configuration on solvation environment and desolvation behavior. A series of chain ether molecules with well‐defined structures, including 1,2‐dimethoxyethane (DME), 1,2‐diethoxyethane (DEE), and 1,1‐diethoxyethane (CDEE), are employed as primary solvents in the electrolytes and thoroughly compared. The presented electrolyte is explicitly designed for low‐temperature operation, delivering stable Na plating/stripping and reliable Na–S cell performance at 0 °C. To the best of our knowledge, while most Na–S research still primarily focuses on room‐temperature operation, a systematic investigation of weakly solvating electrolytes for low‐temperature Na–S batteries has not been previously reported. Our results reveal that modifying chelation via terminal ethyl groups and introducing steric hindrance through central methyl groups markedly affects the solvation behavior of linear ether molecules, which in turn affects the electrode–electrolyte interphase, electrochemical performance, and sulfur conversion process. Consequently, the electrolyte formulated with the weak chelation capability, CDEE, enables Na||Al cells to exhibit a stable sodium stripping/plating behavior for over 300 cycles at 0 °C, maintaining a high Coulombic efficiency of approximately 97%. Low‐temperature Na–S half‐cells exhibit significantly enhanced cyclability at 0 °C, delivering 960 mA h gsulfur −1 after 150 cycles at a C/5 rate (1C = 1,672 mA h gsulfur −1). This approach not only mitigates the intrinsic shuttle phenomenon and promotes sulfur conversion kinetics in Na–S systems, but also offers a novel pathway for interfacial regulation and stability enhancement in broader metal–sulfur chemistries.

Results and Discussion

Design Concept of the Proposed Electrolyte

The chelation interaction between Na⁺ ions and solvent is significantly influenced by the molecular configuration of the solvent.[ 24 ] Even a single methyl substitution can introduce a pronounced steric effect that impacts the Na⁺ chelation capability. This steric hindrance governs the formation of solvation sheaths in the electrolyte, including solvent‐separated ion pairs (SSIPs), contact ion pairs (CIPs), and aggregates (AGGs). Such solvation structures modulate the interfacial chemistry and determine the desolvation behavior, affecting sulfur conversion kinetics and polysulfide solubility.[ 2 ]

The effect of methyl groups on the chelation chemistry is examined in this study. As shown in Figure 1a, the DEE molecule features two additional methyl substituents on the methoxy termini compared to the DME molecule. This leads to a weakened solvation ability of the two oxygen atoms in the DEE molecule due to increased steric hindrance. However, the bidentate coordination may remain strong, potentially inhibiting the desolvation process of Na⁺ and obstructing the formation of a stable electrode–electrolyte interphase at low temperatures.[ 23 ] Consequently, an additional methyl group is introduced at the central carbon within the CDEE molecule, resulting in a weak chelation effect and an ultralow solvation affinity toward Na⁺. According to density functional theory (DFT) calculations, the DEE and DME molecules exhibit comparable binding energies with Na⁺(Figure 1b), while the CDEE molecule displays the lowest binding energy of −42.1 kcal mol−1. Furthermore, the DEE and DME molecules show more negative minimum surface electrostatic potentials (ESPs), as indicated by the deeper red regions, whereas the CDEE molecule presents a higher minimum ESP, visualized as a dark brown area. These results suggest a weakest chelation interaction between the CDEE molecule and Na⁺, which may promote the formation of CIP and AGG within the well‐designed electrolyte as shown in Figure 1c. To further investigate the impact of structure‐dependent interfacial chemistry in the Na–S system, a series of electrolytes based on those three solvents were formulated. These electrolytes were prepared according to the procedures detailed in the experiment section and are denoted as DME, DEE, and CDEE, respectively.

Properties of the Electrolytes

Raman spectroscopy was employed to analyze the ion coordination behaviors and solvation structures within the electrolytes, as shown in Figure S1. Insights into the coordination environment of the FSI− anion can be obtained by analyzing the S─N─S vibrational mode in the range of 700–780 cm−1.[ 25 ] As shown in Figure 2a, the emerged peak exhibits a progressive blue shift across the series of electrolytes, following the order DME < DEE < CDEE, i.e., both DEE/CDEE‐based electrolytes can tune the solvation structure toward anion‐rich configurations due to the difference in the chelation effect.[ 23 ] In particular, the CDEE electrolyte exhibits an aggregation‐dominated cluster solvation structure with a high proportion of AGGs (74.7%), followed by CIPs (18.5%) and SSIPs (6.8%). While the DEE electrolyte exhibits 50.3% CIPs, 37.6% AGGs, and 10.1% SSIPs, the DME electrolyte is dominated by 74.6% CIPs and 25.4% SSIPs, with no significant presence of AGGs. This indicates that the methyl group at the central carbon within the CDEE molecule effectively modulates the chelation behavior and alters the solvation structure, primarily due to its pronounced steric hindrance.

Moreover, 2 3Na nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy was employed to probe the interaction strength between Na⁺ cations and FSI− anions within the solvation environment. As shown in Figure 2b, the 2 3Na NMR spectrum of the CDEE electrolyte exhibits an upfield shift relative to those of the DME and DEE electrolytes. Typically, a scarcity of solvent molecules permits the anions (FSI‐) to approach more intimately to the Na⁺ ions, thereby enhancing the shielding effect of sodium nucleus and causing an upfield shift in the NMR signal. As a result, this shift is attributed to an increased overall electron density around the Na⁺ ions, indicating a strengthened Na⁺–FSI‐ interaction and a weakened interaction between Na⁺ ions and solvent molecules.[ 9 ] Moreover, the presence of compact aggregation clusters solvation structure may lead to restricted ionic mobility and enhanced local electric field gradients. These conditions exacerbate quadrupolar interactions and expedite spin relaxation processes, thereby contributing to pronounced line broadening in the NMR spectra.[ 26 ] Therefore, the line broadening features in the NMR spectra provide further evidence that the solvation environments in CDEE and DEE electrolytes are primarily governed by anion‐rich coordination structures. Furthermore, the ionic conductivities of the DEE and CDEE electrolytes are presented in Figure S2. These results also support the conclusion that ionic mobility is restricted due to the aggregation‐cluster solvation structure, leading to the lower ionic conductivity observed for the CDEE electrolyte, which corresponds with the findings from the NMR spectra. Notably, while CDEE exhibits lower ionic conductivity, this behavior is consistent with the literature reports on weakly solvating electrolytes. However, the Na⁺ transference numbers in CDEE‐ and DEE‐based electrolytes, measured by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) combined with potentiostatic polarization, showed the opposite trend. As presented in Figure S3, the t Na⁺ value for the CDEE‐based electrolyte (0.81) is higher than that of the DEE‐based electrolyte (0.70). The higher Na⁺ transference number further demonstrates that the aggregation‐cluster solvation structure effectively restricts anion mobility, while Na⁺ ions, exhibiting weak interactions with CDEE molecules, can readily dissociate and migrate within the presented electrolyte.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted to further elucidate the coordination environments and solvation structures of Na⁺ ions in various electrolytes, as shown in Figures 2c,d and S4. The radial distribution function (RDF) provides insight into the spatial arrangement of anions and solvent molecules around Na⁺ ions in various solvation sheaths. Meanwhile, the coordination number (CN) of species surrounding the Na⁺ ion was determined through integration of the RDFs. In the CDEE electrolyte, the CN of the primary DEE solvent and CDEE solvent with Na⁺ are 0.98 and 2.33, respectively. These values are substantially lower than the CN of DME solvent in the DME electrolyte (5.26), and that of DEE solvent in the DEE electrolyte (3.59). This further highlights the limited solvating capability of the CDEE molecule in modulating the chelation effect within the designed electrolyte system. Likewise, the CN of the FSI− anion with respect to Na⁺ in the CDEE electrolyte is approximately 2.0, which exceeds that observed in the DME electrolyte at 0.58 and in the DEE electrolyte at 1.46. Furthermore, Figure 2e–g depicts the solvation structures within 4 Å surrounding the Na⁺ ion, derived from MD simulations across different electrolytes. Compared to the DME electrolyte, the solvation sheaths in both the DEE and CDEE electrolytes incorporate FSI− anions. Notably, the FSI− anion is positioned closer to the Na⁺ ion in the CDEE electrolyte. These findings further suggest that the CDEE molecule with the lower chelation effect and pronounced steric hindrance not only weakens the interaction between Na⁺ ions and solvent dipoles, but also promotes stronger Na⁺–FSI− associations, which may thereby facilitate the formation of an anion‐rich solvation environment.

In addition, to verify the alteration of the solvation structure under low‐temperature conditions, MD simulations and NMR measurements were performed at 0 °C. As shown in Figure S5, in the DEE electrolyte, the CN of the primary DEE solvent decreases from 3.59 at room temperature to 3.33 at 0 °C, while the CN of the FSI− anion with respect to Na⁺ increases from 1.46 to 1.92. Similarly, for the CDEE electrolyte, the CN of DEE and CDEE with Na⁺ decreases from 0.98 and 2.33 at room temperature to 0.90 and 2.31 at 0 °C, respectively. Meanwhile, the CN of FSI− in the CDEE electrolyte increases from 2.0 at room temperature to 2.2 at 0 °C. This indicates that the CDEE‐based electrolyte exhibits stronger Na⁺–FSI− associations at both room temperature and low temperatures, with the effect being more pronounced under low‐temperature conditions. Also, the variations in the CN of anions and solvent molecules suggest that CDEE effectively modulates the chelation effect within the designed electrolyte system under both conditions. Intriguingly, the low‐temperature NMR spectra (Figure S6) of the DEE electrolyte show similar chemical shifts at room temperature and 0 °C, implying that the decrease in DEE coordination and the concomitant increase in FSI− association at 0 °C balance each other, thereby maintaining a comparable electron density around Na⁺. However, the CDEE electrolyte exhibits an upfield shift at 0 °C compared to that at room temperature, indicating the formation of a more compact aggregation‐cluster solvation structure under low‐temperature conditions. Consequently, the CDEE electrolyte continues to reinforce Na⁺–FSI− interactions under low‐temperature conditions, supporting the formation of a solvation environment enriched in anions.

The polysulfide solubility tests in the electrolyte solvents (DME, DEE, and CDEE) were conducted to evaluate the interactions between polysulfides and solvent dipoles. 0.05 M Na2S6 was introduced into the pure solvents in the absence of electrolyte salt (NaFSI). As illustrated in Figure 2h, the DME solvent exhibits high polysulfide solubility and form a homogeneous dark solution, whereas the DEE solvent displays reduced polysulfide solubility, as evidenced by the presence of precipitates at the bottom. In particular, the introduction of the CDEE solvent leads to substantial precipitation of sulfur species, indicating its poor capacity to dissolve polysulfides. This implies a weak interaction between polysulfide and the CDEE molecule, which is also derived from the weak chelation behavior. Consequently, as depicted in Figure 2i, the presence of the CDEE molecule not only modulates the solvation structure toward an anion‐rich configuration, but also suppresses polysulfide solubility, which may in turn mitigate the shuttle effect during cycling.

Cycling Stability of Na Stripping/Plating

To evaluate the Na⁺ stripping/plating behavior and the stability of sodium metal in different electrolytes, Na || Al cells and Na || Na symmetric cells were tested at room temperature and 0 °C. As illustrated in Figures S7–S8, the electrolyte containing only CDEE solvent exhibits unstable Na⁺ plating behavior, highlighting the crucial roles of TFTFE as the outer solvent and DEE as the cosolvent. Furthermore, the Na || Al cell with the CDEE electrolyte exhibits more stable Na⁺ ion stripping/plating behavior compared to those with DME and DEE electrolytes at room temperature, indicating the formation of a robust SEI layer that facilitates Na⁺ ion transport and suppresses continuous solvent decomposition. Notably, such stable Na⁺ stripping/plating behavior is preserved even under low‐temperature conditions. At 0 °C (Figure 3a,b), the Na || Al cells utilizing the CDEE electrolyte exhibit markedly more stable and sustained Na⁺ stripping/plating behavior compared to the DEE‐based system at 0.5 mA cm−2/0.5 mA h cm−2. Moreover, it maintains a high Coulombic efficiency of 97.0% over 300 cycles. Remarkably, the CDEE‐based cell exhibits a flat and sustained current plateau, reflecting uniform Na⁺ stripping and enhanced interfacial stability at a low‐temperature of 0 °C. In contrast, the DEE‐based electrolyte leads to a poor reversibility of Na+ transport with pronounced current fluctuations and electrochemical instability, indicative of an inadequately formed interphase and persistent parasitic reactions. Furthermore, Na‖Na symmetric cells employing the CDEE electrolyte demonstrate overpotential and sodium‐metal stability comparable to that of DEE at room temperature at 0.5 mA cm−2/0.5 mA h cm−2, whereas DME results in unstable Na⁺ stripping/plating, as shown in Figure S9. However, under low‐temperature conditions, the cells with the CDEE electrolyte demonstrate significantly more stable cycling behavior with lower overpotential compared to the DEE‐based system, as presented in Figure 3c,d. These results demonstrate that the weakly chelating CDEE solvent tailors the solvation sheath, enabling efficient solvation/desolvation dynamics and forming a robust SEI layer, thereby improving the interfacial conductivity, electrolyte stability, and desolvation kinetics, even under low‐temperature conditions.

Furthermore, the stability of the electrode–electrolyte interface at the sodium‐metal anode and the charge transfer of Na⁺ ions were studied through EIS of Na‖Na symmetric cells. The Na‖Na symmetric cells were cycled at a low‐temperature of 0 °C and 0.5 mA cm−2/0.5 mA h cm−2 for 100 h before the EIS test. As depicted in Figure 3f, the cell utilizing the CDEE electrolyte exhibits lower interfacial resistance compared to that of the cells containing the DEE electrolyte across a range of testing temperatures. This observation correlates well with the superior cycling stability demonstrated by Na‖Na symmetric cells under low‐temperature conditions. The EIS results indicate that the sodium‐metal anode coupled with the CDEE electrolyte facilitates improved Na⁺ transport during cycling. Notably, at low temperatures, the process of Na⁺‐ion transport exhibits substantially lower impedance in the CDEE electrolyte, attributed to the combined effects of a reduced desolvation barrier relative to the DEE electrolyte and the establishment of a robust anion‐derived SEI layer on the sodium‐metal surface.

To gain deeper insight into the influence of solvation structure on the charge‐transfer processes and desolvation dynamics, distribution of relaxation time (DRT) analysis was applied to examine the EIS data as shown in Figure 3g. In general, DRT data can be classified into three distinct regions based on the characteristic relaxation time (τ), namely the contact, reaction, and diffusion regions, arranged in order from low to high τ.[ 27 ] Moreover, each characteristic peak in the DRT region responds to the specific physical process. The characteristic peak in the reaction region (τ around 10−1–10−6, depends on temperature) represents the interfacial resistance and ion transfer resistance according to the equivalent circuit (Figure S10).[ 28 ] Ultimately, DRT analysis revealed insights into the energy barriers during Na⁺ plating and stripping. The energy barriers, denoted as Ea1 and Ea2, are determined from interfacial and charge‐transfer resistances. They signify the activation energies required for Na⁺ migration through the SEI layer and for their release from the surrounding solvation sheath, respectively.[ 3 ] As shown in Figure 3h, the Ea1 values observed for the DEE and CDEE electrolytes are comparable, which is attributable to the presence of inorganic‐rich components in the SEI layers formed by both electrolytes. These components can facilitate Na⁺ migration across the electrode–electrolyte interface. Notably, the CDEE electrolyte exhibits a significantly lower Ea1 (17.47 kJ·mol−1) compared to the DEE electrolyte (33.67 kJ·mol−1). This is attributed to the weak chelation effect of the CDEE molecule, which not only effectively drives the formation of an aggregation‐rich solvation sheath, but also reduces the interaction strength between Na⁺ ions and solvent molecules, thereby promoting Na⁺ desolvation behavior. These findings are expected to significantly enhance the electrochemical performance and reaction kinetics of Na–S batteries.

Furthermore, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was utilized to investigate the sodium plating morphology in Na || Na symmetric cells cycled at 0 °C. As depicted in Figure 3i,j, the sodium‐metal surface in the presence of the DEE electrolyte demonstrates extensive cracking and fracturing, contributing to a highly non‐uniform interfacial structure. This is attributed to the sluggish desolvation behavior of Na+ ion in such electrolyte, leading to uneven sodium plating and stripping during cycling. In contrast, the sodium‐metal surface in the CDEE electrolyte demonstrates a markedly smoother and more compact plated morphology, a result attributable to its anion‐dominated solvation environment and the enhanced desolvation kinetics of Na⁺ ions within the CDEE‐based system.

Electrochemical Performance of Na–S Cells

Sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN) materials have attracted widespread attention in recent years due to their better electronic conductivity and smaller volume expansion compared to elemental sulfur cathodes.[ 29 ] The low expansion rate of SPAN enables their integrity during operation. As a result, they have been increasingly reported in the field of sulfur‐based battery electrolytes.[ 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 ] Nevertheless, the polysulfide shuttle effect persists for SPAN cathodes during cycling because of the intrinsic solubility of sulfur species and solid–liquid–solid conversion process, especially in ether‐based electrolytes. Furthermore, the kinetics of sulfur conversion at low temperatures have yet to be thoroughly investigated. Consequently, the prepared electrolytes were employed in Na–S cells with SPAN cathodes to investigate the influence of methylation‐induced weak chelation effects and to evaluate their potential for low‐temperature applications.

As illustrated in Figure 4a, the cell with the DME‐based electrolyte exhibits a pronounced capacity decay over 30 cycles at a C‐rate of C/2 at room temperature, whereas those employing the DEE and CDEE electrolytes demonstrate stable cycling performance. The degradation in the DME system is attributed to the formation of highly soluble polysulfides and the resulting unstable interphase between the electrolyte and the sodium‐metal anode. In the context of the DEE and CDEE electrolytes, the weak interactions between Na⁺ ions and polysulfides, particularly evident in the CDEE electrolyte, substantially inhibit the formation of soluble polysulfide species and alleviate polysulfide shuttling. Moreover, the anion‐rich solvation sheath promotes the development of a robust SEI layer, thereby enhancing the stability of the electrode–electrolyte interphase. Even at a C‐rate of C/2 (Figure S11), both electrolytes exhibit excellent cycling performance at room temperature. However, the electrolyte with pure CDEE solvent (Figure S12) exhibits significant capacity degradation at C/2 rate, primarily due to severe sodium‐metal anode erosion and the polysulfide shuttle effect. Notably, the CDEE‐based cells demonstrate higher and more stable performance, as evident from a capacity retention of 990 mAh·g−1 after 200 cycles. Additionally, cells with the CDEE electrolyte exhibit enhanced rate performance over those with DEE (Figure 4b), achieving reversible capacities of 1140, 1085, 1030, 995, and 960 mAh·g−1 at C/10, C/4, C/2, 3C/4, and 1C rates, respectively. This is attributed to the accelerated desolvation kinetics resulting from the weak chelation effect of the CDEE molecules. Moreover, the Na–S cells employing the CDEE electrolyte exhibit significant potential for long‐term operation under high‐rate conditions of 1 C rate at room temperature (Figure S13), achieving a capacity retention of 800 mAh·g−1 after 500 cycles.

To gain deeper insight into the influence of desolvation kinetics on the electrochemical performance, cells employing DEE and CDEE electrolytes were operated at a low temperature of 0 °C. As shown in Figure 4c, although the Na–S cells incorporating the CDEE electrolyte exhibit a slightly reduced discharge capacity in the initial cycles, attributable to the activation process, they deliver markedly improved cycling stability compared to cells utilizing the DEE electrolyte. The stable Na–S cells utilizing the CDEE electrolyte achieve a capacity exceeding 950 mAh·g−1 after 150 cycles at a C‐rate of C/5 and a temperature of 0 °C. Moreover, CDEE‐based Na–S cells demonstrate a significantly reduced overpotential of 0.67 V following cycling (Figure 4d) in comparison to DEE‐based electrolytes. This enhancement is attributed to the combined effects of an anion‐rich SEI and effective suppression of polysulfide shuttling, which together mitigate side reactions between the sodium‐metal anode and soluble polysulfides, a phenomenon facilitated by the weak chelation nature of CDEE molecules. Notably, the CDEE electrolyte enable Na–S cells achieve better rate capability at the low‐temperatures. As illustrated in Figure 4e,f, the CDEE‐based Na–S cells demonstrate a high capacity of over 350 mAh·g−1 when operated at a C‐rate of 1 C under a low‐temperature condition of 0 °C. These findings further highlight the significance of the CDEE solvent in enhancing the desolvation kinetics, which not only governs the rate capability performance, but also determines the operating potential under low‐temperature conditions.

To establish a clear link between the underlying design strategies and the resulting operational performance, a series of additional electrochemical tests were carried out. Self‐discharge test was applied to analyze the polysulfide shuttle effect during cycling process. Coulombic efficiency (CE) was determined by initially discharging the cell to either 1.5 or 2 V, allowing it to rest for 48 h, and subsequently continuing the discharge to a final cutoff of voltage of 0.8 V. As shown in Figure 4g, the incorporation of the CDEE electrolyte resulted in negligible self‐discharge, as evidenced by the Coulombic efficiencies of 99.6% and 100.1% following rest periods at 2 and 1.5 V, respectively. This performance highlights the crucial role of the CDEE solvent in reducing polysulfide solubility, which mitigates the shuttle effect and helps preserve the structural integrity of the sulfur cathode, while minimizing active material loss. Moreover, cyclic voltammetry (CV) at a low temperature of 0 °C was utilized to assess the electrochemical transformations and kinetic characteristics of the SPAN cathode, thereby clarifying the role of electrolyte composition in governing the cycling performance. Figure 4h reveals that the DEE‐based and CDEE‐based electrolytes both present two distinct reduction peaks at approximately 1.8 and 1.3 V when scanned at 0.05 mV s−1, corresponding to the sequential solid–solid conversion of sulfur into short‐chain Na2SX intermediates (x = 3–4) and ultimately into Na2S. This underscores the ability of anion‐dominated solvation structures to limit the polysulfide solubility and thus reduce the shuttle effect during cycling. Also, the CV curve of the CDEE electrolyte reveals a lower voltage polarization compared to that of the DEE electrolyte, implying a fast kinetic conversion process. Intriguingly, the first reduction peak observed for the CDEE‐based electrolyte is significantly higher than that of the DEE‐based electrolyte, which is attributed to the lower energy barrier associated with Na⁺ desolvation in the CDEE system. As a result, Na⁺ ions can more easily escape from the solvation sheath and engage in the reactions with active sulfur species. However, in the charged state, the trend is reversed. The stronger affinity of Na⁺ for DEE facilitates easier solvation with DEE molecules compared to CDEE. Consequently, the oxidation peak shifts to a slightly higher potential in the CDEE electrolyte. Although a slightly higher oxidation peak is observed in the CDEE electrolyte, the enhanced desolvation behavior exerts a significant influence on the sulfur conversion process. Moreover, as the scan rate rises from 0.05 to 0.25 mV s−1 (Figures 4i and S14), the DEE electrolyte displays a reduced first reductive peak current, whereas the CDEE electrolyte exhibits a stronger current response at higher speeds. This further suggests the impact of desolvation behavior on the kinetic conversion process and rate performance.[ 34 ]

Characterization of Cycled Electrodes in Na–S Cells

X‐ray diffraction (XRD) was applied to analyze the surface of cycled sodium‐metal anodes and SPAN cathodes, respectively. As shown in Figure S15, the XRD pattern of cycled sodium‐metal anodes within the DEE‐ and CDEE‐based electrolytes demonstrate similar diffraction peaks, assigned to sodium‐metal and the existence of NaF on the surface. Also, Figure S16 showcases the XRD pattern of cycled SPAN cathodes in discharged state, which only reveals a distinct broad peak at around 20° corresponding to the discharged SPAN. To further confirm the chemical component on the electrode surface, X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to characterize the electrode–electrolyte interphases formed on sodium‐metal anodes and SPAN cathodes. As presented in Figure 5a, the F 1s spectra were examined to elucidate the chemical composition of the SEI layer on the sodium‐metal surface. The anion‐derived solvation environments in the DEE‐ and CDEE‐based electrolytes result in a higher proportion of NaF (683.9 eV) relative to C/S–F (687.5 eV) in the outer SEI layer. After 120 s of Ar⁺ ion sputtering, as shown in Figure 5b, the NaF component remains dominant within the inner SEI layer, indicating the formation of an inorganic‐rich SEI derived from the tailored solvation structure. Furthermore, the C 1s spectrum was analyzed to investigate the solvent decomposition during cycling, which can be deconvoluted into three characteristic peaks corresponding to C─C/C─H (284.8 eV), C─O (286.4 eV), and C═O (288.4 eV). The intensity of the C─C and C─O peaks in the DEE electrolyte is significantly higher than that in the CDEE electrolyte. This suggests reduced solvent decomposition in the CDEE‐based system, likely due to the formation of an aggregation‐dominated solvation sheath. Consequently, XPS characterization of the sodium‐metal anode demonstrates that both electrolytes produce an inorganic‐rich SEI layer on sodium surface, as confirmed by the F 1s spectra, and further suggests that the CDEE‐based electrolyte experiences less decomposition throughout cycling. These findings correspond to the energy barriers associated with Na⁺ ion transport. Although the Ea1 values are comparable, reflecting the similar composition of the SEI layers on the sodium anode, the Ea2 values show significant differences attributable to distinct chelation effects, which exert a pronounced impact on Na⁺ desolvation dynamics. Moreover, XPS analysis was conducted for SPAN cathode. Notably, the SPAN cathodes paired with the CDEE electrolyte display a NaF‐rich CEI layer, unlike those with the DEE electrolyte, which show higher S─F/C─F signals compared to NaF in the F 1s spectra. Consequently, NaF effectively suppresses polysulfide dissolution and promotes sulfur conversion via solid–solid pathways, as illustrated in Figure 5e.

Additionally, the cycled sodium anodes from Na–S cells were examined by SEM, as shown in Figure 5f,g. Compared to the DEE‐based electrolyte, the sodium anode with the CDEE electrolyte also displays a more uniform and smoother surface morphology. Therefore, benefiting from the aggregation‐dominated solvation and rapid Na⁺ desolvation behavior, the cells with the CDEE‐based electrolyte exhibit outstanding electrochemical performance, especially at low temperatures, including long cycling stability, high rate capability, and high reversible capacity.

Conclusion

In summary, this work represents the first effort to elucidate the relationship between chelation chemistry dictated by molecular configuration and its influence on solvation structures, desolvation dynamics, and the kinetics of sulfur conversion. Owing to its well‐designed terminal ethyl groups combined with steric hindrance from central methyl groups, the CDEE molecule exhibits the weakest chelation capability compared to the commonly used linear ethers DME and DEE. This results in a weak interaction between Na⁺ ions and the CDEE molecule, thereby promoting the formation of an anion‐derived solvation environment. Such tailored solvation sheaths not only accelerate Na⁺ transport kinetics and enhance the desolvation process within the electrolyte, but also induce the formation of an inorganic‐rich CEI and SEI on the cathode and anode, respectively. This interfacial layer mitigates the erosion of sodium‐metal anode and facilitates the sulfur conversion reaction via a transformation pathway that minimizes the shuttle effect. Consequently, the electrolyte formulated with CDEE, which possesses a weak chelation capability, enables Na || Al cells to achieve stable sodium stripping/plating behavior for over 300 cycles at 0 °C, while maintaining a high Coulombic efficiency of approximately 97%. Moreover, low‐temperature Na–S half‐cells demonstrate significantly enhanced cyclability at 0 °C, delivering 960 mAh gsulfur −1 after 150 cycles at a C/5 rate. Overall, this study presents a rational design principle for achieving stable and practical Na–S batteries under low‐temperature conditions.
