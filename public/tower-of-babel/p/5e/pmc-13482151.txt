Coherent transformation of metal halide perovskites
Authors: Pinghui Yang, Haokun Shi, Zhen Wang, Shuaijun Yan, Xiliu Wang, Bo Xu, Junbo Wang, Kaidi Liu, Xuan Gao, Wenbo Liu, Fangfang Wang, Lin Zhu, Renzhi Li, Wei Huang, Jianpu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metal-halide perovskites offer high optoelectronic performance but translating laboratory efficiencies into controllable, reproducible processes remains challenging. Perovskite crystallization is tracked by in situ multiwavelength absorption to show that coherent, layer-by-layer conversion of low-n intermediates to three-dimensional perovskite, quantified by a transformation index (TI), correlates with device quality. TI displays a Sabatier-type dependence on additive–precursor interaction strength and concentration, defining an engineering window for reproducible film formation. Within this window, post-treatment-free devices reach a champion power conversion efficiency of 26.40% with improved stability and reduced device scatter at laboratory scale. TI is extractable from an inline optical readout and provides a measurable metric for additive screening and process tuning. Its applicability to scalable deposition is further validated through slot-die coated mini-modules, offering a practical pathway for inline process control.

Introduction

Metal-halide perovskite semiconductors, with their remarkable optoelectronic properties, have emerged as promising materials for a wide range of applications, including solar cells, light-emitting diodes, and photodetectors1–5. Turning laboratory efficiencies into controllable, reproducible processes remains a central challenge, since approaches that deliver best laboratory performance frequently show poor reproducibility between batches and under different deposition conditions6. Solution processing of metal-halide perovskites requires precise control of rapid crystallization kinetics, including solvent-coordinated complexes7,8, transient low-dimensional intermediates9, nucleation density, and subsequent grain growth10, so that solvated precursors reproducibly convert into phase-pure, low-defect three-dimensional lattices across different coating platforms and scales. A broad set of approaches has been developed to address these issues, including additive engineering11–20, solvent and antisolvent protocols21–24, single-crystal redissolution25,26, and low-temperature crystallization27–29. However, these methods are often treated as separate strategies rather than as a common nucleation-control principle. As a result, there is no practical, quantitative process-control paradigm that connects precursor chemistry and crystallization dynamics to scalable processing windows, inline monitoring metrics, and reproducible device yields across coating platforms.

Here we show that a coherent transformation, defined as a temporally ordered, sequential layer-by-layer evolution from transient low-dimensional (layered) intermediates into the final 3D perovskite, is the common microstructural signature of reproducible, low-defect films. This mechanism is not unique to additives: it also emerges when using single-crystal redissolution (SCR) and low-temperature crystallization, which each establish a single, ordered nucleation pathway that guides sequential phase evolution rather than overlapping, disordered transitions. To quantify this behavior, we introduce a transformation index (TI) derived from high-resolution, time-resolved absorption spectra; TI captures the temporal ordering of intermediate formation and conversion and serves as an inline, process-readable metric. Using TI as a control parameter, we find a Sabatier-type (volcano) dependence of device performance and defect density on additive–precursor interaction strength and concentration, defining an engineering window with practical tolerance. Operating inside this window yields post-treatment-free devices with ≈26% efficiency, improved operational stability, and tight device statistics across multiple chemistries and processing modes. Because TI can be measured during coating and annealing, it converts molecular design rules into actionable process control, providing a portable metric for additive screening and quality assurance in perovskite processing. Furthermore, we validate the practical scalability of this concept by integrating in-situ TI monitoring into a slot-die coating platform. Our results confirm that the coherent transformation mechanism remains fully applicable during large-area deposition, directly guiding the successful fabrication of mini-modules.

Results

In-situ observation of coherent transformation

We select the FA-based perovskite with an n-i-p device structure of fluorine doped tin oxide (FTO)/SnO2/perovskite/2,2’,7,7’-tetrakis-(N,N-di-4-methoxyphenylamino)−9,9’-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD)/Au as a model system for our study, which has demonstrated high photovoltaic performance (Supplementary Table 1). The perovskite layer was fabricated using a 1.5 M precursor solution with the composition of FA0.98MA0.02Pb(I0.98Br0.02)3. Consistent with previous reports30–32, the control perovskite film exhibits relatively small crystallites and disordered morphology (Supplementary Note 1), resulting in power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of around 23% with modest stability (Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 1, and Supplementary Note 2). To make comparison, we then used two strategies to enhance perovskite film quality, additive engineering using 2-amino-5-fluoropyridine (FPyA), and single-crystal redissolution (SCR) respectively. We note, since we focus on the relationship of the crystallization process and the resulting quality of perovskite films, we did not apply any post-treatment to the perovskite films. For additive engineering with FPyA, the resulting films displayed larger and more ordered crystallites and reduced trap densities of 7.67 × 1014 cm−3 (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Note 1), leading to a PCE increase to 26.40% (certified PCE = 25.43%, Supplementary Fig. 3) and enhanced stability (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Note 2). The SCR strategy also demonstrated comparable performance, achieving reduced trap densities of 1.15 × 1015 cm−3, a PCE of 25.82% and enhanced stability (Supplementary Figs. 4–5 and Supplementary Notes 3–4). Notably, these PCEs and stabilities are among the highest for perovskite films without post-treatment with a regular device structure33–35, indicating the quality of our perovskite films can be comparable with the state of the art. Below, we then focus on the mechanism of the formation of these high-performance perovskite films.

To elucidate the underlying mechanisms responsible for the enhanced performance and film quality for the two strategies, we employed home-built in-situ absorption spectroscopy. This technique, featuring a high time resolution of 10 ms and a spectral resolution of 0.79 nm, enabled us to capture the rapid evolution of chemical species throughout the crystallization process, with the aim of uncovering the common principles governing high-efficiency perovskite formation regardless of the specific synthetic route. Across all strategies, annealing-time-dependent absorption spectra revealed dramatic spectral shifts within the initial 20 seconds, with the absorption onset moving rapidly from ~500 to 800 nm (Fig. 1a–c). Intriguingly, and unexpectedly for nominally 3D perovskites, absorption features corresponding to layered perovskite structures (n1, n2, n3, n4) emerged in all films within the first 10 seconds of annealing. These features, observed at ~518 nm (n = 1 like layered perovskites, denoted as n1), ~567 nm (n2), ~606 nm (n3), and ~642 nm (n4), correspond to excitonic absorption peaks of layered perovskites with varying numbers (n) of inorganic [PbI6] octahedral layers36,37. This observation suggests a universal transient layered perovskite intermediate phase during 3D perovskite crystallization, intrinsic to FA-based perovskite formation, regardless of the specific strategy employed. In the control film, the time-dependent absorption intensity curve for the n1 component exhibits a noticeable plateau (with its first derivative approaches zero) between 5.6 and 7.2 seconds (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 6a). During this period, the first derivatives of the time-dependent absorption intensity curves of the n2, n3, and n4 components show simultaneous peaks, indicating the consumption of n1 species and their transformation into higher-n perovskite species. Subsequently, the absorption intensities of n1, n2, n3, and n4 reach their maximum values almost simultaneously, with their first derivatives concurrently approaching zero (Supplementary Fig. 6a). This observation suggests a near-simultaneous growth of various layered perovskites in the control film, even though high-n perovskites are converted from n1 species. Moreover, the characteristic absorption spectrum of 3D perovskites already appears before 9.5 s, indicating the concurrent formation of layered and 3D perovskites, which contributes to the disordered nature of the perovskite film (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Note 1). In contrast, the additive sample follows a markedly different transformation pathway. As shown in Fig. 1e, the time-dependent absorption intensity curves reveal that each layered perovskite reaches its maximum value sequentially, from low-n to high-n layered perovskites, with their first derivatives reaching zero sequentially as well (Supplementary Fig. 6b). This result suggests a collective and orderly layer-by-layer transformation in the additive sample. Notably, the characteristic absorption spectrum of 3D perovskites appears only after the saturation of the high-n absorption, further indicating an orderly transformation of high-n layered perovskites to 3D perovskites. As shown in Fig. 1a, b, once the layered perovskites begin transforming into 3D perovskites, their excitonic absorption peaks disappear, and the entire spectra exhibit the characteristic absorption of 3D perovskites. Moreover, since the absorption coefficient of the 3D perovskite is significantly lower than that of the layered perovskites38, the overall intensity of the absorption spectra decreases during the transformation (Fig. 1a, b). Therefore, the stabilization of the absorption spectra indicates the completion of the transformation from layered perovskites to 3D perovskites. Interestingly, the absorption spectra of additive sample stabilize at 17 s, compared to 12 s for the control sample. This suggests that the critical role of the additive during annealing is to induce a coherent, layer-by-layer, and slower growth process that promotes the ordered crystallization of 3D perovskites. This finding can be further verified by performing similar measurements with various additives reported in the literature for high performance 3D perovskites based solar cells (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9)16,17,19,31,39,40. Remarkably, we find that the SCR strategy also leads to a collectively and orderly layer-by-layer transformation during the crystallization process, as shown in Fig. 1c, f. Interestingly, the absorption spectra of SCR sample stabilize at 12 s, which is similar to the control sample (Supplementary Fig. 6c), indicating that a collectively layer-by-layer transformation is not necessarily due to a slow growth process, but other more fundamental mechanisms.

Mechanism of single nucleation pathway

To gain further insight into the origins of the different transformation behaviors, we measured the in-situ absorption spectra of the samples during the spin-coating process (Supplementary Fig. 7). The primary differences emerge after the antisolvent dripping step, which occurs at 30 s. For the control perovskite films, the main absorption peaks appear at 425 nm and 460 nm, corresponding to the absorption of [PbI4]2−, and [PbI4]2−·2DMSO complexes, respectively (Fig. 1g)41,42. In contrast, the additive significantly enhances the formation of [PbI6]4− (490 nm) and n1 (518 nm) species (Fig. 1h)36,37. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns and optical microscopy images reveal that the additive effectively suppresses the formation of the undesirable MA2Pb3I8·2DMSO intermediate phase due to its strong coordination (N:→Pb2+) and hydrogen bonding (F•••MA+) with perovskite precursors14,43. This suppression leads to abundant [PbI6]4−, and establish a single nucleation pathway and rapid nucleation of the n1 phase (Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Note 5). We believe that the establishment of a single nucleation pathway is essential for the coherently layer-by-layer transformation of perovskites during the annealing process.

Similarly, the SCR strategy achieves coherent transformation of layered perovskites during annealing by establishing the same initial state after spin-coating (Fig. 1i). This state is characterized by the rapid and dominant formation of pre-nucleated [PbI6]4− (490 nm) and n1 (518 nm) immediately after antisolvent dripping. Inherited from the perovskite single-crystal structure in the precursor, this pre-nucleation bypasses undesirable intermediate phases prevalent in the control sample. The resulting iodide-rich environment and abundant n1 nuclei act as seeds, guiding an ordered, layer-by-layer transition of layered perovskites into the desired 3D structure during subsequent annealing. In this case, even the nucleation and growth are much faster than the control sample, very high-quality perovskite films can still be achieved through the coherent transformation. Therefore, this observation confirms the importance of establishing a single nucleation pathway for the coherently layer-by-layer transformation of perovskites.

Interestingly, we can further confirm that the role of a single nucleation pathway for the coherently layer-by-layer transformation in the control sample is achieved by low-temperature spin-coating (LTC). DFT calculations show that the reaction energy of the detrimental MA2Pb3I8·2DMSO phase is higher than [PbI6]4− (Supplementary Note 6). At a reduced temperature of −10 °C, the formation kinetics of the MA2Pb3I8·2DMSO phase are drastically suppressed while [PbI6]4−can still grow, as shown in XRD (Supplementary Fig. 11a, b), optical microscopy (Supplementary Fig. 11c,d) and in-situ absorption spectrum measurements (Supplementary Figs. 12–14, and Supplementary Note 7). Remarkably, this film also shows a coherently layer-by-layer transformation during annealing process (Supplementary Note 7), and resulting in low trap-density films (9.82 × 1014 cm−3) and high PCE devices (25.74%) (Supplementary Figs. 15–16 and Supplementary Note 8). Quantification by transformation index

We then try to establish a correlation between the extent of coherent transformation and the resulting film quality. Based on the insights from in-situ absorption spectroscopy regarding the dynamics of layered perovskite evolution (Fig. 1d–f), we introduce a metric termed the “transformation index (TI)”. This index is calculated by multiplying the time interval between the absorption intensity maxima of n1 and n4 with the time interval for the transition from layered to 3D perovskites (Supplementary Note 9). We first explored the impact of additive engineering on coherent transformation and device performance by selecting various additives with different predicted binding energies (Eb) to uncoordinated Pb2+ ions (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 17, and Supplementary Note 10). In-situ absorption spectra reveal a general trend where stronger interactions between additives and PbI2 initially promote a more ordered transformation process as reflected in the TI (Supplementary Fig. 18–19). Figure 3a shows that the TI is positively correlated with binding energies. For instance, the TI for the control film is 0.57 s2, while for the FPyA-based film (representing an optimized interaction strength), it is 7.23 s2. Time-Resolved Photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements reveal that increasing TI leads to a significant reduction in defect state density, from approximately 1015 cm−3 to 1014 cm−3 (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 20). This positive correlation between TI and low defect density holds up to an optimal interaction strength. In the case of 2-aminopyridine, the excessively strong interaction with Pb2+ hinders the formation of perovskite nuclei (n1) (Supplementary Fig. 18). Consequently, the transformation process is hardly observable and its TI is very large, leading to an abundance of the yellow phase perovskites (Supplementary Fig. 21), high defect state density, and consequently poor device performance (Fig. 3a). The existence of an optimal additive interaction strength and TI is consistent with the scenario of single nucleation pathway induced coherently layer-by-layer transformation, since the weak interaction is not strong enough to suppress the formation of MA2Pb3I8·2DMSO phase, and the too strong interaction will hinder the formation of nuclei. Additionally, the optimal interaction strength for the coherent transformation aligns with the improved device efficiencies and operation stability, as shown in Fig. 3a and Supplementary Figs. 22 and 23. Notably, along with the high efficiency, in the 85 °C thermal stability measurement and maximum power point tracking (MPPT) measurements, FPyA-based devices consistently maintained best performance, retaining over 90% of their initial average efficiency after approximately 600 hours of aging, which is also among the best stable PSCs without post-treatments33,44–46.Fig. 3Impact of coherent transformation extent on perovskite crystallization and devices performance.a Plots of binding-energy (Eb, different additives with uncoordinated Pb2+ ions)-dependent transformation index (TI), device efficiency (PCE), and stability. b Plots of additive concentration-dependent TI, defect density (Ntrap), device efficiency, and stability. Similar to interaction strength, we find that the concentration of the additive also critically influences TI and film quality, exhibiting a concentration-dependent optimum. (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Figs. 24 and 25). From 1% to 5% FPyA concentrations, the in-situ absorption spectra display a growing trend in TI. However, at concentrations exceeding 10%, the additive begins to hinder the transformation. At a concentration of 50%, the n1 species no longer transforms to higher-n species. This trend of ordered transformation aligns with decreased defect density, improved device performance and stability (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Figs. 26–28). This concentration-dependent behavior again illustrates the mechanism of single nucleation pathway induced coherently layer-by-layer transformation, since the low concentration additive is not enough to suppress the formation of MA2Pb3I8·2DMSO phase, and the excess high concentration leads to detrimental over-coordination with Pb2+ ions that stagnates the structural rearrangement. At optimal concentrations, however, additives (even bulky ones) are dynamically expelled to grain boundaries without impeding the phase transition16,40. This explicitly reinforces the existence of a Sabatier optimum for both interaction strength and concentration. Unlike interaction strength, the importance of additive concentration is rarely discussed for optimizing perovskite films in literature47,48, our findings emphasize that both are critical for controlling coherent transformation and maximizing device performance.

Validation of optimal transformation window

From the above studies (Fig. 3), we find that an optimum TI is within the range of 4–12 s2 for high quality perovskite films. This result is consistent with SCR and low temperature spin-coating process, where TIs are 9.52 s2 and 7.98 s2, respectively. Crucially, this optimal TI window is largely insensitive to typical variations in heating conditions, maintaining its validity across different substrate thicknesses and minor annealing temperature fluctuations (Supplementary Figs. 29–31 and Supplementary Table 2). To further validate our findings, we investigated a diverse range of additives, including both additives developed in our lab (all showing PCEs > 25% without post-treatment, Supplementary Figs. 32–34, and Supplementary Table 3) as well as previously reported additives for high-performance PSCs in the literature (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9 and Supplementary Table 4)16,17,19,31,39,40. The results consistently demonstrate that these additives induce a similar coherent and ordered transformation from layered perovskites to 3D perovskites during crystallization (Supplementary Table 5), and TIs for high-performance additives fall into the range of 4–12 s2. Interestingly, we find that the relationship between the transformation index and device PCEs indeed exhibits a volcano shape, indicative of a Sabatier principle-guided interaction (Fig. 4). Consistently, Fig. 4 also suggests that the defect density shows an inverse correlation, reaching its minimum within the optimal TI range associated with peak PCEs. Discussion

In summary, we demonstrate that the coherent transformation of layered intermediates into three-dimensional perovskites during crystallization constitutes a unifying mechanism underlying diverse optimization strategies. This transformation is driven by a single, ordered nucleation pathway, and its extent, quantified by the transformation index, directly governs the film microstructure, trap density, and, consequently, device efficiency and stability. Framing crystallization in terms of nucleation pathway control and the Sabatier optimum yields a quantitative, generalizable principle for rational perovskite processing. The transformation index and its associated inline metrics provide practical levers for process control and monitoring, as demonstrated in scalable slot-die coating, with broader implications for the controlled synthesis of high-quality polycrystalline optoelectronic materials beyond perovskite photovoltaics.

Methods

Materials

Lead iodide (PbI2, 99.99%) and lead bromide (PbBr2, ≥98%), bis(triflfluoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt (Li-TFSI), 4-tertbutyl-pyridine (TBP) and 4-isopropyl-4’-methyldiphenyliodonium tetrakis(pentafluorophenyl)borate (TPFB) were purchased from TCI Shanghai (China). Methylammonium chloride (MACl, ≥99.5%), Methylammonium bromide (MABr, ≥99.5%), 2,2’,7,7’-tetrakis [N,N-di(4-methoxyphenyl)amine]-9,9’-spirobifluorene (Spiro-OMeTAD, >99.8%), FK209-Co(III)-TFSI ( ≥ 99%) and poly[bis(4-phenyl)(2,4,6-trimethylphenyl)amine] (PTAA) were purchased from Xi’an Ploymer Light Technology Corp. Formamidinium iodide (FAI, ≥99.9%) was purchased from Youxuan Tech, China. Chlorobenzene (CB, anhydrous), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, anhydrous), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, anhydrous), acetonitrile (ACN), Ethyl acetate (EA, anhydrous), stannous chloride dihydrate (SnCl2·2H2O, 99.99%), thioglycolic acid (TGA, 98%), and urea (≥99.5%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. All the materials were used without further purification.

Perovskite precursor solution

(FAPbI3)0.98(MAPbBr3)0.02: The precursor solution of perovskite was prepared by dissolving PbI2, FAI, MACl, MABr, PbBr2, and FPyA with a molar ratio of 1.09:1:0.35:0.02:0.02:0.051 at 52 wt% in a mixed solution of DMF and DMSO (v/v = 8:1). Additives with varying binding energies to uncoordinated Pb2+ were dissolved with a corresponding molar concentration relative to lead concentration in the perovskite precursors. The obtained precursor solution was then stirred at room temperature overnight and filtered through 0.22 µm polytetrafluoroethylene filters before use. The SCR perovskite precursor of (FAPbI3)0.98(MAPbBr3)0.02 was prepared by dissolving 868.45 mg FAPbI3 crystals and 13.24 mg MAPbBr3 crystals and 33.08 mg MACl in DMF/DMSO (8:1, V/V) solvents.

Solution and film characterization

XPS measurements were conducted by using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha+ XPS spectrometer with an Al Kα anode (E = 1486.6 eV). The pressure in the analysis chamber was maintained at ≈5 × 10–9 mbar. To prevent exposure to the atmosphere, all perovskite films were transferred into the XPS chamber through a glovebox. NMR measurements were conducted using a Bruker 400 MHz. XRD data were collected using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer. Optical micrographs were obtained with an LV100ND Nikon microscope. Steady-state absorbance spectra were measured by a UV-vis spectrophotometer with an integrating sphere (PerkinElmer, Lambda 950). SEM images were obtained using a JEOL JSM-7610F Plus instrument. For PLQE measurements, a three-step technique was used through the combination of a 445-nm CW laser, an optical fiber, a spectrometer, and an integrating sphere.

Film and device fabrication

FTO glasses were washed with dish soap, water, and ethanol, followed by ultrasonic cleaning for 15 min. Then, they were dried using a nitrogen gun and further treated with O2 plasma for 15 min. After depositing a compact stannic oxide (SnO2) film on FTO glass via chemical bath deposition (CBD): 10 mM SnCl2·2H2O in ethanol was mixed with thioglycolic acid (TGA) as a complexing agent, heated at 70 °C for 2 hours, followed by rinsing with deionized water and annealing at 180 °C for 30 min49. A diluted potassium chloride (KCl) solution was spin-coated on FTO-SnO2 substrates at 3000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 110 °C for 10 min in air. The substrates were then transferred into an air glovebox. Perovskite precursor solution was spin-coated onto the substrate at 1000 rpm for 10 s and 5000 rpm for 30 s. At 10 seconds before the end of spin-coating, 90 μL of ethyl acetate was deposited onto the substrate. A spin coater with adjustable chamber temperature was used for perovskite film preparation. For the −10 °C processing condition, the temperature parameter was set to −10 °C cooling mode. Both the substrate and precursor solution were pre-equilibrated in the spin coater chamber for 10 minutes prior to deposition. The resulting film was then placed on a hotplate at 100 °C for 60 min. The Spiro-OMeTAD solution was prepared by dissolving 30 μL of TBP, 18 μL of Li-TFSI solution (520 mg in 1 mL acetonitrile), 29 μL of FK209-Co(III)-TFSI solution (300 mg in 1 mL acetonitrile) and 73 mg of Spiro-OMeTAD in 1 mL CB. The PTAA solution was prepared by dissolving 30 mg of PTAA and 3 mg of TPFB in 1 mL CB. The solution was then spin-coated on the perovskite films at 3000 rpm (Spiro-OMeTAD) or 2000 rpm (PTAA) for 30 s, respectively. Finally, an 80-nm thick of gold layer was deposited using thermal evaporation.

Device characterization

All perovskite solar cells were measured under simulated AM1.5 G irradiation at 100 mW cm−2 using a solar simulator (Class AAA, SS-F5-3A, Enlitech), with current–voltage curves were recorded by a Keithley 2450 source meter. The light intensity was calibrated using a Newport standard silicon cell 91150. Current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics were measured in N2 using a scan rate of 0.02 V s−1. No preconditioning was applied to the devices before measurements. IPCE measurements were conducted using a QE-R-900AD system (Nanjing Ouyi Optoelectronics Technology). An LED was utilized as a bias light source to simulate the device operating conditions (~0.2 Sun). A Hamamatsu S1337-1010BQ silicon diode used for IPCE measurements was calibrated at the National Institute of Metrology, China. Steady-state power output measurements were carried out at a fixed voltage near the maximum power point identified in the J-V curve (Supplementary Fig. 37). For the moisture tolerance test, unencapsulated perovskite solar cells using Spiro-OMeTAD were stored in ambient air of 60% (±5%) and 20% (±5%) relative humidity in the dark. For thermal stability measurements, unencapsulated perovskite solar cells using PTAA were kept in a N2 glove-box under heat stress of 85 °C. In the maximum power point tracking measurement, unencapsulated devices with PTAA were kept in N2 at approximately 45°C and monitored using an MPP Tracking-4B system (Shenzhen Lancheng Technology Ltd.) under LED-based Sunlight Simulators (Newport Verasol) with an emission spectrum of 400–1000 nm.

In-suit UV-vis absorption spectroscopy measurement

In-suit absorption spectra were collected by an ISAS-HI001 system with a high temporal resolution of 10 ms and a spectral resolution of 0.79 nm (Nanjing Ouyi Optoelectronics Technology). Measurements were performed using an FTO/SnO2 substrate. The real-time absorbance (A) of the perovskite film during the spin-coating and thermal annealing processes was calculated using the equation A = lg(I0/I), where I0 and I represent the intensities of the reflected light from the bare substrate and the perovskite-coated substrate, respectively.
