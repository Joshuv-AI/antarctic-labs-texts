Hinge Strut: A New Septal Strut for Nasal Tip Projection, Definition, and Stability in Latino Noses
Authors: Wilder A. Pérez Willis, Gerardo A. Adrianzen Nuñez, Cynthia P. Mendoza Guerra, Wilder A. Perez Soto
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Obtaining ideal support to maintain the nasal tip has been investigated in several rhinoplasty studies. We aimed to describe a support technique, namely the use of a “hinge strut” (HS) for the nasal tip in Latino noses and to evaluate nasolabial angles at 6-months follow-up.Methods:In total, 46 Latino patients who had undergone rhinoplasty using the HS technique between June 2021 and June 2022 were recruited in Lima, Peru. All patients had nasolabial angle measurements taken preoperatively and at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively in active and passive positions to evaluate angle changes during this 6-month period.Results:We evaluated 38 women and six men aged 18–45 years (mean age, 25.74 years). In the passive position, the mean nasolabial angles were 91.5 degrees preoperatively and 106, 104, and 103 degrees at 1, 3, and 6 months, respectively, postoperatively. In the active position, the mean nasolabial angles were 85.5 degrees preoperatively and 102.6, 99.1, and 98.1 degrees at 1, 3, and 6 months, respectively, postoperatively (P < 0.0001, using Friedman chi-square test).Conclusions:The HS technique is safe and reproducible, with nasal tip maintenance and an adequate angle elevation trend observed at 6-months follow-up. Further studies and longer observation times are necessary to determine longer-term outcomes.

Takeaways

Question: Does the hinge strut (HS) technique provide nasal tip stability in the first 6 months?

Findings: The HS technique provides nasal tip stability, as corroborated by nasolabial angle measurements over 6 months with a statistically significant level (P < 0.0001).

Meaning: HS is a safe and reproducible technique that provides nasal tip stability, with results that are maintained for the first 6 months postoperatively.

INTRODUCTION

Rhinoplasty is in high demand globally because it provides both aesthetic and functional improvement. As a specialist surgical procedure, rhinoplasty requires extensive training, and the outcome often depends on a surgeon’s experience.1 In this context, work performed on the nasal tip is perhaps the most important aesthetically in terms of long-term outcomes for maintaining nasal tip rotation, projection, and definition.2,3

Obtaining ideal support to maintain the nasal tip has been studied extensively in rhinoplasty, with studies focusing on the development of grafts and the use of different techniques, such as the columellar strut, septal extension graft, tongue in groove, and suture maneuvers. Differing conclusions have been reported concerning these techniques, with no global consensus.4–12 Nasal tip stability is considered a fundamental component in rhinoplasty when seeking to obtain harmonious aesthetic results that are sustainable in the long term.13,14

Several North American and European studies have been undertaken involving study participants with the typical phenotypic characteristics of these countries. However, the study findings are not easily comparable to those of other ethnic groups with different phenotypic characteristics, particularly in Latin America, with multiple ethnic differences even within the same region. Notably, within Latin America, several variations of nose types have been reported according to location.15,16

The Latino nose is characterized with a deficient osseocartilaginous structure and wide nasal bones, a low radix, and thin, weak upper alar cartilages, with a tendency to be narrow in the middle-third of the nose. Therefore, the cartilaginous nasal valve is usually weak and narrow. In addition, the tip is characterized as bulbous and indefinite and may be more prominent in patients with thick skin.14,15

Given these specific Latino nose characteristics, obtaining ideal support for the nasal tip remains challenging. Moreover, owing to a lack of consensus, many surgeons prefer to perform structured rhinoplasty techniques in the tip area, using supports, such as septal extension grafts,14,15 with reportedly successful results; however, one study reported that these techniques can result in a rigid “frozen nose.”16

This study aimed to evaluate the stability of the nasal tip over a 6-month period through applying the hinge strut (HS) technique as an effective and reproducible alternative that could provide good nasal tip support while enhancing functional aesthetics and maintaining flexibility, independent of facial muscle dynamics, in Latino patients. We performed nasolabial angle measurements over time, to determine whether nasal tip stability was maintained during active facial movement.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This prospective, longitudinal, observational study was approved by a local ethics committee and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided written informed consent as well as respective authorization for the use of audiovisual material.

Study patients were recruited from June 2021 to June 2022, with a 6-month follow-up. The procedures were performed by the same plastic surgeon (W.P.W.), who was trained in the HS technique, at the Continental Clinic and Higuereta Clinic in Lima, Peru. In total, 46 patients who visited the outpatient plastic surgery clinics requesting evaluation for rhinoplasty procedures were included in this study. The author (W.P.W.) performed open rhinoseptoplasty on patients meeting the following inclusion criteria, namely patients of Latino ethnicity and Peruvian nationality who were aged 18–45 years. Exclusion criteria comprised patients with poorly controlled comorbidities, a Goldman surgical risk greater than II, secondary rhinoplasty, or severe caudal deformities.

MEASUREMENTS

A photographer used a Sony Alpha 7S III/24–105 mm F4 professional camera to obtain photographic images of all patients. These photographs were taken in active (smiling) and passive positions. They were then stored in a coded database to be subsequently analyzed by a photography specialist trained in CC 2020 (Adobe Systems, San Jose, Calif.), who used a goniometer to objectively measure nasolabial angles. Nasolabial angle measurements were taken preoperatively and at 1, 3, and 6 months, postoperatively by the same photography specialist. The chief author (W.P.W.) telephoned the patients to arrange follow-up visits, and photographs were taken during the medical appointments. SPSS statistical program software (version 25) was used for the statistical analysis, and measures of central tendency (mean) were evaluated using Friedman chi-square test to determine whether the findings were statistically significant.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Design of the HS

To perform the HS technique, adequate extraction of the septal cartilage is required. We considered the minimum required dimensions to include a height of 16 mm and a width of 8 mm, with ideal measures being a height of 20 mm and a width of 10 mm. These dimensions are recommended to obtain optimal outcomes, as smaller sizes are likely to be insufficient for the contact surface (Fig. 1).

HS design in three steps. Step 1: Remove the septal cartilage within the mentioned dimensions. Step 2: Use a scalpel to make a greenstick fracture, then turn it with your fingers to generate a pivot point. Step 3: Make a complete incision in the two-thirds section to obtain contact with the caudal septum. The dotted lines in the image represent areas where the total cut is made.

The optimal procedure involves adequate and intact extraction of the nasal septum graft. Therefore, we dissected only the area where the cartilage was to be extracted subperichondrically to preserve the remaining “L strut” (with 11-mm caudal and superior borders, minimally), while keeping the perichondrium intact to provide greater strength. After removing the cartilage graft, we proceeded to evaluate it to ensure it was of adequate and even thickness on both sides. If both sides differed in terms of thickness, the graft was shaved using a #15 scalpel to harmonize the cartilage (Fig. 1).

The next step was to determine the pivot point of the hinge, which is relevant to the design of the HS. The cartilaginous positional memory needs to be considered to prevent deformation of the HS as tension forces would be generated should be placed in a biconvex manner, thereby avoiding the formation of deviations. On certain occasions, placing the HS in a biconcave manner is also possible (Fig. 1).

The HS is formed through creating a greenstick fracture in the middle zone of the cartilage (previous measurement) with a #15 scalpel, in a very delicate manner, exerting a slight pressure with the fingers, such that a pivot point or hinge effect is produced when approaching both structures. When making the incision, consideration should be given to specify the caudal and cephalic portions because these positions may influence the outcome. Before fixation, superior and inferior portions need to be determined: the thicker part is usually the superior portion that will have the hinge turn, and the inferior portion is fixed to the septum.

Following the above criteria, the HS was placed while ensuring it was fixed on the caudal edge of the septum, ideally without exceeding this edge. The greater the contact with the cartilaginous surface, the greater the strength of the HS.

Fixation was performed using a nonabsorbable suture (nylon 5/0) through hinge-shaped fixation points, equidistant in an oblique manner, as shown in Figure 2A. We recommend placing at least two alignment sutures to ensure proper positioning (Fig. 2B and Video 2).

Domes and Tip

The domes were attached to the HS independently with a nonabsorbable suture (nylon 5/0) using a back-and-forth stitch and positioning the knot toward the domal face (Fig. 3A). We then placed a simple suture with the same suture material in the anterior face of the HS and fixed it in its medial portion. A marginal incision was made in the lateral crura, with sutures not being necessary (Fig. 3B). Safety Protocols

All patients received 1 g of tranexamic acid intravenously 15 minutes before surgery. Antibiotic prophylaxis was used in accordance with American Association of Plastic Surgeons standards.17 Antiembolic measures were undertaken based on patient evaluation.

Forty-six patients (38 women and eight men) aged 18–45 years (mean age, 25.74 years) were recruited. The surgery times ranged 180–240 minutes; all patients underwent general anesthesia (100%), and all had a Goldman grade I surgical risk (100%).18 No adverse events associated with surgery were reported during the 6-month follow-up period (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

Facial beauty implies harmony of the facial structures. One such structure is the nose, which is the most prominent part of the face due to the nasal tip. For measuring ideal preferences, angles and references, such as tip rotation and projection, are used.19

Concerning rotation, certain parameters are considered ideal. The standard nasolabial angle is 95–115 degrees for women and 90–95 degrees for men.19–21 However, there can be modifications due to the protrusion of the upper lip, creating acute nasolabial angles.22 For this reason, the nasolabial angle can have measures exceeding these ranges because a prominent maxillary arch generates acute measures, and these are frequent in Latino populations23 (Fig. 6).

The “ideal” nasal tip projection can be calculated using mathematical formulas.24–27 However, we consider that no general standard can be applied, as surgeons need to determine an ideal standard through taking the expectations and wishes of the patient into account, which may vary according to the types of noses such patients have.

The columella also plays a very important role in nasal aesthetics because it forms the tip slope. Sheen considered that its exposure in relation to the nasal ala should be approximately 2–3 mm in a lateral view.28 Sheen also presented several clinical cases that had a hanging columella defect and an alteration in the relationship with the nasal ala. We consider our HS technique would likely be effective in resolving this defect.

Most studies on nasal tip support include descriptions of patients of European ethnicity, in whom the surgical approach is likely to differ from that used in those of Latino ethnicity due to their anatomical differences.14,15 In Latino populations, these differences are marked due to a deficient osteocartilaginous structure (weak cartilage), as well as intermediate or thick skin. The nasal tip presents with poor projection and rotation, with a prominent maxillary arch giving acute nasolabial angles.14,15,23 In these patients, when moving from a passive to an active position (smiling), this angle increases slightly as the upper lip retracts (which is not usual in patients without this maxillary characteristic), thus maintaining a stable and firm tip.

We defined a septal strut graft as one that did not extend the septum at the caudal border. This term could be problematic because it could be confused with a septal extension graft, which usually extends the caudal border of the septum. However, these two close septal grafts are different. The HS is placed in the caudal portion of the septum and fixed to the septum bilaterally. Septal extensors are known to be more stable over time than columnar struts as they adhere to the septum itself.4

Currently, there has been a resurgence in dorsal preservation techniques, based on Saban et al’s study.29 Management is functional in terms of maintaining the ideal dorsum. However, not all dorsal preservation techniques have been applied to the same types of patients. In some situations where structure is needed for the nasal tip, especially in Latino patients with weak cartilage and thick skin, there can be a shortage of graft. For this reason, we developed the “five-S” rule for ideal tip support (Fig. 7).

Patient results showing HS stability. A, A preoperative three-fourths view in the active position. Note the large nasal tip droop in the passive position. B, A 6-month postoperative three-fourths view in the active position, with greater stability observed compared with preoperative stability.

Our five-S rule is described as follows, while detailing an HS category on a scale ranging from 1 to 5. We consider that this proposed HS categorization can be applied to any other type of nasal structure.

1. Stability: An ideal support should show more than firmness; that is, it should show stability and be maintained in the long-term in both passive and active positions. We aimed to obtain enhanced nasal tip stability and to evaluate how well stability had been maintained over a 6-month period through measuring the nasolabial angle. The average passive preoperative measurement was 91.5 degrees. At 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively, the average measurements were 106, 104, and 103 degrees, respectively. Changes of angle were clearly observed, and these changes had been maintained, which suggested that the stability of the nasolabial tip was maintained.

For active measurements, the patients were requested to smile. The average active preoperative angle was 85.5 degrees, and at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively, the nasolabial angles were 102.6, 99.1, and 98.1 degrees, respectively, showing an angle increase postoperatively, with postoperative stability maintained (P < 0.0001). Thus, in both positions, the angle measurements indicated that nasal tip stability at 6 months was adequately maintained, with major changes in the nasolabial angles occurring between the first and third month. The intraoperative nasolabial angle was maintained, giving a stable and predictable rotation, projection, and definition (HS: 4/5).

In Latino noses, good nasal tip support is highly recommended to maintain rotation and projection.14,15 In this study, the HS was shown to provide a double contact side and a strong attachment to the caudal border of the septum, making an ideal support for Latino noses. However, a further study is needed with a 12-month follow-up period to determine whether stability is maintained at 12 months.

2. Shape: Nasal tip definition is a key outcome of the HS technique. Cakir et al defined “the diamond-shaped tip,” which comprises two domal triangles, one interdomal triangle, two polygonal facets, and an infralobular polygon that collectively generate a reflection, with two luminous points observable on a frontal view photograph supported by two standard flashes.30 This feature was used to evaluate the HS technique, with a difference being that the interdomal triangle position was changed, inverting the base in the cephalic portion and the tip in the caudal portion, which provides a different design to the diamond and preserves the other triangles (Video 3 and Fig. 8). (See Video 3 [online], which shows the final result using the HS with a diamond-shaped tip.) To achieve this result, additional techniques can be used, such as crura steal, correction of the resting angle, and, in some cases, removal of the cephalic portion of the lateral crura. For patients with very thick or bulbous skin, additional grafts are required in the nasal tip, with the Peck onlay graft most commonly used.31 (See Video 3 [online].)

3. Save: maximum cartilage sparing should be sought (ie, the lesser the graft extraction the better) because removal of a large portion of cartilage tissue can lead to instability in the remaining septum.32 As the HS requires moderate graft extraction, a bilateral cartilaginous structure with a large size must be used. For this reason, we recommend taking a graft within the measurements described earlier (HS: 2/5).

4. Satisfaction: Both the patient and the operating surgeon need to be highly satisfied with the medium to long-term outcome. In our experience, patients who have undergone this technique have obtained optimal results. Subsequent studies are needed to evaluate long-term patient satisfaction rates more specifically (Fig. 7; HS: 4/5).

5. Simplicity: The HS technique is simple to perform. One of its main advantages is greater precision in relation to nasal tip projection and rotation through having control over the domes. Surgeons could use this simple technique worldwide. Moreover, this technique can also be applied in a closed approach. To control rotation in a simple way, we used a goniometer to measure the nasolabial angle preoperatively and at follow-up. A rotation test was then performed through placing a suture in the columellar incision, until reaching the rotation proposed preoperatively (Fig. 8; HS: 4/5).

A further characteristic of the HS is independence in each crura. Alar cartilages comprise three portions (medial, middle, and lateral). Each alar cartilage is independent of each other, when considering the angle of divergence or the interdomal angle between them arising in the medial crura, which is surrounded by soft tissue and Pitanguy’s ligament.19 Usually, when a support is placed in the nasal tip, all the force is concentrated at the same point, which involves the medial area of the septal extension graft or the columnar strut, causing loss of the natural divergence angle between both crura, resulting in a “frozen nose” or a “stiff nose.” To address this issue, the suture at each end of the HS is independent, which allows for slight movement on each side and gives the “hinge effect” (the dome’s hinge movement) as the final result. (See Video 4 [online], which shows the dome independence test with double suture.) This is likely to explain why the nose tip is flexible during active movement without descending. These moving structures have been previously described. We consider the anterior nasal septal angle (ANSA Banner) extension graft, which provides support to the lateral crura tensioning, to be highly effective in giving projection and in avoiding the rigid tip side-effect of septal extension grafts.16 The HS relies on the independence of each alar cartilage (dome independence) to generate the natural divergence angle with the hinge effect (movement referred to as the ANSA banner),16 resulting in a diamond shape (Baris tip),30 with support for each lateral cartilage (ie, double support). There is also a unilateral support, referred to as a teostrut,33 which is placed in the septum unilaterally. However, fixation to the domes differs because the HS makes each dome and each crura independent, giving a hinge effect (see Video 2 [online]). In addition, we suggest that this hinge effect continues postoperatively, giving a slight movement during breathing and during active facial movement (see Video 2 [online]).

Most patients are candidates for the HS technique, especially those who have a hanging columella, for whom the HS technique may be particularly helpful; however, we do not recommend this technique for patients with columellar retraction, severe caudal deviations, or a so-called crooked nose because the HS can be toned in an anomalous position, thus, generating nasal tip deviations.

CONCLUSIONS

The HS technique was found to be safe and reproducible, showing maintenance of nasal tip stability and flexibility at an adequate angle elevation at 6 months follow-up. Studies with longer observation times are necessary to evaluate longer-term outcomes.
