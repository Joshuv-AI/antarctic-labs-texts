Machine learning‐assisted clone selection for intensified cell culture processes
Authors: Nicolas Wolnick, John Schmitt, Christian Siltanen, Julia Armstrong, Shannon Hendrick, Ariel Atchley, Brandon Downey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntensified fed‐batch processes are becoming increasingly prevalent among biomanufacturers due to their superior space–time yields relative to traditional, non‐intensified fed‐batch processes. However, the shift towards intensified manufacturing has unexpectedly made optimal clone selection more challenging. Clone selection, traditionally an empirical screening of candidates on the basis of productivity, has been complicated by the observation that clonal productivity rankings can vary substantially between intensified and non‐intensified processes. In the absence of a small‐scale intensified empirical screen, clones destined for intensified manufacturing are selected based on their performance in the non‐intensified process. This mismatch risks selection of suboptimal clones, representing a missed opportunity to generate additional product at no extra cost. This study presents a machine learning approach to improve clone selection for intensified processes by predicting and ranking clonal productivity. Models were trained on non‐intensified clone performance data routinely collected during cell line development to facilitate seamless integration into existing capabilities. To evaluate generalizability and simulate real‐world application, independent clone panels expressing two distinct monoclonal antibodies were withheld from model training and used in comparative testing against the legacy clone selection method. The ranking model identified the most productive clone of those expressing the first mAb, representing a 68.5% higher titer than the clone selected by the legacy method. For the second mAb, the ranking model identified the second most productive clone, resulting in a marginally lower titer. These results demonstrate the potential of machine learning models as practical tools for improving the selection of high productivity clones in intensified bioprocessing.

INTRODUCTION

Since the launch of the first licensed monoclonal antibody (mAb) nearly 40 years ago, market demand for therapeutic proteins has grown rapidly. Numerous advancements throughout the industry have fueled this growth, with the US market size alone projected to reach nearly $500 billion by 2029. 1 , 2 However, the high cost of developing and manufacturing therapeutic proteins continues to limit broad patient access. In an effort to reduce the cost of goods (CoGs) for mAb production, many biomanufacturers have developed highly productive cell lines, faster screening tools, and more efficient upstream processes. 3 , 4 In recent years, intensified fed‐batch processes have become increasingly common because of their greatly improved space–time yields (STY) relative to traditional fed‐batch. Higher STY is often realized via sustained elevated viable cell densities, typically achieved through a high inoculation density (HID), relative to traditional low inoculation density (LID) fed‐batch processes. Many report greater than 70% improvements to STY with intensified processing. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13

Despite their promising gains in productivity, intensified fed‐batch processes face several implementation difficulties which complicate the full realization of their value potential. For example, legacy workflows such as clone selection for non‐intensified processes must adapt to better align with intensified process conditions. Clone selection is an empirical filtering process designed to identify top‐producing cell lines from an extensive starting pool of candidates. To sort candidates effectively, screening is performed in high‐throughput, small‐volume formats that mimic the upstream manufacturing processes at varying levels of fidelity. At each stage, a subset of the best clones, often judged by productivity and growth rate, is carried forward. While this approach has been effective for selecting clones suitable for traditional fed‐batch processes, its ability to identify top performers for intensified conditions is less consistent. Small‐scale models that faithfully replicate intensified process conditions are technically challenging for several reasons. These include limited control over critical parameters, such as pH and dissolved oxygen, which are more precisely and easily maintained at larger scales. Consequently, clones are often selected based on their performance in process mimics that differ from the intensified process they are ultimately destined for. This misalignment introduces risk; the selection of a suboptimal clone represents a missed opportunity to generate more product simply by making a different decision. In an era of rising productivity expectations, this cost‐free value potential is difficult to ignore.

In the absence of a high‐fidelity scale‐down model, clone selection for intensified fed‐batch processes requires tools that go beyond traditional empirical screening methods. This is a challenge ripe for machine learning, which can leverage complex performance data across process formats to predict clone productivity more accurately. We hypothesized that exploitable relationships exist between clonal performance in traditional fed‐batch and intensified fed‐batch processes, and that incorporating additional performance attributes beyond product yield would improve prediction accuracy. This study describes the development and validation of two machine learning models designed to enhance clone selection specifically for intensified fed‐batch processes. The development of a ranking model that outperforms the existing clone selection method was the primary objective of this study. The regression model was developed as a secondary objective with the intention of providing an estimate of clonal productivity in an intensified process. The models were trained on data from diverse clone panels expressing three unique mAbs, aiming to reflect the variability of clones and products encountered in deployment. To simulate future application to unseen products, the models were tested on small panels of clones expressing two distinct mAbs withheld from training. Compared to conventional selection methods, the ranking model identified a clone with a 68.5% higher titer for one mAb and a clone with a 6.6% lower titer for the other. Our results demonstrate how machine learning can help bridge the gap between clone performance in early‐stage screenings and in intensified fed‐batch production processes by leveraging routinely generated data to reduce the risk of missed productivity gains. This study represents a novel application of machine learning to clone selection for intensified processes and exemplifies a valuable application of predictive modeling in biomanufacturing.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Clones and processes

Panels of CHO clones (Lonza) expressing five different antibody products were cultured in small‐scale mimics of two Lonza fed‐batch platform processes in duplicate. The first process is a non‐intensified, LID, fed‐batch process run on the Ambr®15 (Sartorius) as part of the cell line selection workflow ahead of manufacturing scale production. The second process is an intensified, HID, fed‐batch process which was executed on Culture Biosciences' proprietary 250‐mL scale bioreactor system.

LID production bioreactors were inoculated at a target viable cell concentration of 0.5 × 106 cells/mL. pH was initially controlled at a mildly acidic setpoint between 6.8 and 7.2, then shifted to the more alkaline setpoint partway through the culture where it was maintained until harvest. Temperature was initially controlled at a setpoint of 36.5°C and was lowered partway through the culture and maintained there until harvest. Dissolved oxygen was controlled to a setpoint between 30% and 60% of air saturation for the duration of culture. Three supplemental feeds, rich in glucose, phenylalanine, and methionine, respectively, were added throughout the duration of the culture. The glucose feed was added as needed to maintain concentration within a suitable range. The phenylalanine feed was dosed according to an internally developed equation. The methionine feed was dosed as small bolus volumes on specific process days.

HID production reactors were inoculated at a target viable cell concentration of approximately 10.0 × 106 cells/mL. pH was initially controlled at a mildly acidic setpoint between 6.8 and 7.2, then shifted to the more alkaline setpoint partway through the culture where it was maintained until harvest. Temperature was initially controlled at a setpoint of 36.5°C and was lowered partway through the culture and maintained there until harvest. Dissolved oxygen was controlled to a setpoint between 30% and 60% of air saturation for the duration of culture. Three supplemental feeds, rich in glucose, phenylalanine, and methionine respectively, were added throughout the duration of the culture. The glucose feed was added as needed to maintain a low concentration setpoint equal to that maintained in the LID process. Both the phenylalanine and methionine feeds were supplemented according to equations derived to mimic the feeding strategy used for this process at larger scales.

Routine samples were collected from both LID and HID cultures for measurement of offline pH, metabolites, and cell concentration. Metabolite concentration measurements of glucose, lactate, ammonium, glutamate, glutamine, sodium, potassium, and calcium were analyzed via Nova Biomedical BioProfile® Flex2 Analyzer daily for HID cultures and solely on harvest day for LID cultures due to volume constraints. Cell concentrations were measured via Nova Biomedical BioProfile® Flex2 CDV module and Vi‐CELL™ XR. Cell culture supernatant collected on the final process day was analyzed for product concentration (titer) via high performance liquid chromatography. Using these measured data, the integrated viable cell concentration (IVCC) and cell‐specific productivity (qP) were calculated for all cultures.

Model development

The generated data were employed to develop two models: a ranking model to rank clones in terms of their expected performance in an intensified process and a regression model to provide a point estimate of the expected intensified process titer. The model development workflow is summarized in Figure 1 and explained further in the following subsections.

Overview of the model development process.

Training and testing dataset construction

A split‐sample methodology was employed for constructing the dataset, dividing it into two subsets: a training dataset for model development and a testing dataset for model evaluation. The split was performed at the product level, ensuring that all clones associated with a specific product were included in either the training or testing dataset, but never both. This splitting strategy prevented data leakage by ensuring that product‐specific patterns between LID input attributes and the associated HID performance output in the test set were not present in the training set. As detailed in Table 1, the split used in model development resulted in a training set of 22 clones across three products and a test set comprised of 12 clones across two products.

Data preparation and attribute engineering

Ranking and regression methods were used to model the relationship between LID‐scale input attributes at harvest and HID productivity, as measured by harvest‐day titer. LID Ambr®15 experiments produced input attributes including titer, IVCC, and qP (qP = titer/IVCC). To account for replicate measurements at both the LID and HID scales, all possible pairs of input LID attributes with output HID titer were included.

Attribute engineering techniques were employed to augment the input attribute set. For regression models, second‐order interaction attributes were created (excluding qP × IVCC to avoid redundancy). Gaussian mixture model (GMM) clustering, implemented via scikit‐learn's GaussianMixture function (v1.5.1), identified five and four clusters for ranking and regression models, respectively, based on Bayesian Information Criterion minimization. The Mahalanobis distance between each cluster centroid and instance was computed to serve as new attributes. 14 These distances capture how representative a clone is of distinct regions in the LID attribute space, potentially revealing structure predictive of HID productivity. To improve cluster attribute generalization, 65 LID‐only clones from four products were included during GMM training. All attributes were standardized using z‐score normalization based on the training data. The mean and standard deviation for each attribute derived from the training data were subsequently employed to standardize the attributes of the test dataset. This attribute engineering approach aimed to improve model generalizability, acknowledging that LID‐to‐HID relationships vary across and within products (see Figure 2).

Variation of HID titer and (a) LID qP, (b) LID titer and (c) LID IVCC color coded by product. HID, high inoculation density; IVCC, integrated viable cell concentration; LID, low inoculation density.

For regression models, HID titers were used directly as the output. For ranking models, titer values were converted to ordinal ranks on a per‐product basis to address inter‐product variability in maximum titers. Only the top four clones per product were assigned ranks from 4 (highest) to 1; all others were labeled 0. This focused model training on identifying and ranking top‐performing clones, consistent with downstream selection priorities.

Attribute selection, model training and evaluation

To ensure model generalizability was not compromised by non‐informative attributes, the Boruta all‐relevant attribute selection strategy was applied to identify the subset of attributes to retain for further model development. 15 The Boruta algorithm was executed using the BorutaPy package (v0.4) for a random forest model configured with a maximum tree depth of 10, for a maximum of 500 iterations, using a significance threshold of 0.05 for attribute inclusion. For the regression models, the random forest used in Boruta was a regression model with the continuous HID titer values serving as the output truth. For the ranking models, a binary classification random forest was employed instead. In this case, the model was trained to distinguish between non‐competitive clones (assigned a value of 0) and the top four clones for each product (identified as high performers and assigned a value of 1 for this purpose).

Ranking and regression models were developed using the relevant attribute sets identified via Boruta to determine relationships between LID attributes and HID productivity. Ranking models were implemented using the LambdaMART algorithm via the LightGBM package (v4.5.0). 16 A broad range of regression models available in scikit‐learn (v1.5.1) and other packages were considered, including: linear models (ridge, lasso), latent variable methods partial least squares (PLS), kernel‐based methods (support vector regression (SVR), kernel ridge), ensembles (random forest, extra trees, XGBoost v2.1.1, LightGBM), and neural networks (TensorFlow v2.10.1). Each model's hyperparameters, which govern the model's pattern detection capacity, were optimized to maximize performance while minimizing overfitting, as detailed below.

To ensure generalizability of model predictions to new products, cross‐validation with a customized product‐based group resampling strategy was employed during hyperparameter optimization. To avoid data leakage, each cross‐validation replicate was constructed by randomly assigning products of the training dataset, and all associated clones, to either the surrogate training or testing sets. Clones within each product were resampled with replacement to enhance diversity. For regression and ranking models, 20 and 40 replicates were employed, respectively. Each replicate in ranking models sampled 20 instances per product with replacement using stratified sampling to maintain balanced representation across ranks. This combined approach of group‐based and clone‐level resampling (and stratification) improved robustness, which is particularly important given the limited number of products in the training set.

Hyperparameter optimization was performed for each model using the MaxLIPO algorithm present in the functions find_max_global and find_min_global from the dlib package (v19.24.2). 17 This algorithm efficiently explores the hyperparameter space to identify values that optimize cross‐validation performance, computed as normalized discounted cumulative gain at rank 20 (NDCG@20) for ranking models and mean squared error (MSE) for regression models. For ranking models, the optimizer ran for 500 iterations with a solver epsilon of 10−6; for regression models, 1000 iterations with a solver epsilon of 10−2. The solver was designed to converge on a local optimum within the specified accuracy threshold (defined by solver epsilon), while continuing to explore the broader space for alternative optima, thereby balancing exploitation and exploration. After optimal hyperparameters were identified, models were retrained with these values using the full training dataset and evaluated on held‐out test data to assess generalization.

For Product D, the results illustrate a key limitation of the legacy, LID titer ranking approach (i.e. Ambr®15 harvest titer), as the clone with the highest LID titer had the lowest HID titer among all clones. While higher HID titer clones occupied the second and third spots in this strategy, the overall ranking was inconsistent with the true HID titer values. In contrast, the ranking model produced a more accurate order that better aligned with HID titer performance. Specifically, the ranking model correctly identified the top two HID titer clones as the first and second rankings. Although a lower performing clone occupied the third position, the remaining rankings closely mirrored the true HID titer order. Notably, the top clone selected by the ranking model achieved a 68.5% increase in HID titer compared to the top clone identified by the LID titer ranking strategy.

For Product E, the LID titer ranking strategy correctly identified the clone with the highest HID titer as the top performer. However, this strategy ranked the clone with the lowest HID titer as second best and the overall ranking was again inconsistent with true HID titer values. The ranking model, in contrast, successfully positioned the four highest HID titer clones in the top four spots, albeit in a slightly different order. The top three clones identified by the model corresponded to the second, third, and fourth highest HID titer clones, while the fourth‐ranked clone by the model was the true top performer. Importantly, all lower‐performing clones (e.g., C05 and C06) were ranked below the top performers, ensuring an effective separation of high and low HID titer clones. The top clone selected by the model achieved an HID titer only 6.6% lower than the actual maximum HID titer, a difference within the expected experimental error associated with replicate measurements.

The clone with the highest HID titer for Product E (C01) had only a single HID replicate due to quality control exclusions, while all other clones across both products had two replicates present to calculate the average HID titer used for determining the true ranking. The individual HID titer values for the two replicates of the second best clone (C02) for Product E had titer values of 9.31 and 7.63 g/L. It is possible that an additional replicate for C01 could have altered the average HID titer and associated truth ranking. Regardless, as observed for Product D, the ranking model demonstrated superior performance in producing an order reflecting the true HID titer values compared to the LID titer ranking strategy.

As shown in Table 4, nonlinear strategies such as tree‐based ensemble methods (e.g., LightGBM, XGBoost) and neural networks outperformed simpler linear models, even when interaction terms were introduced to account for basic nonlinearities. Among the nonlinear methods, LightGBM achieved the best cross‐validation performance. However, examining the gap between training and testing performance suggests that tree‐based models tended to overfit the training data, despite employing cross‐validation strategies aimed at mitigating overfitting. In contrast, the neural network model with dropout, while having comparable test performance to LightGBM, exhibited a smaller difference between training and testing performance, indicating better generalization to unseen data. Simpler models, such as ridge regression, partial least squares, and lasso regression, demonstrated comparatively poorer performance on both the training and testing datasets, suggesting that they are limited in their capacity to capture the complexity of relationships in the data. Ensemble models were also evaluated to determine if combining predictions from different modeling strategies could improve performance. The ensemble comprising predictions from the LightGBM and neural network models achieved the best performance among all ensembles and all individual models, with a smaller difference between training and testing performance compared to the individual LightGBM model. This suggests that these models complemented each other effectively, likely due to their differing architectures and strategies for capturing data patterns.

Figure 5 illustrates the predictions of HID titer for the training and testing datasets using the ensemble LightGBM and neural network model. While predictions for test clones generally tracked well with higher HID titer values receiving larger predictions, there were notable instances of underprediction for clones with high HID titers. This highlights the difficulty of using a pointwise approach, that is, regression, for reliably ranking clones in terms of expected HID performance. Pointwise approaches predict performance for each item independently and can underperform listwise ranking approaches, such as LambdaMART, which attempt to order items in a list directly by considering the impact of positional swaps during model training. Given these limitations, the ranking models are inherently better suited for clone ranking. The ranking models employ a pairwise comparison strategy that directly aligns with the goal of ordering clones by expected HID performance, unlike the pointwise regression approach. However, the pointwise predictions from the ensemble regression model could still serve as a useful adjunct to the ranking model results, providing a rough estimate of expected HID titer performance for clones of interest.

DISCUSSION

Intensified fed‐batch processes represent a promising solution to meeting the rising demand for biotherapeutics while maintaining favorable CoGs 13 , 18 , 19 . However, as biomanufacturers deploy intensified processes, legacy clone selection practices based on clone performance in non‐intensified processes have struggled to remain applicable. This mismatch in early screening process and final production process risks the selection of suboptimal clones, potentially forfeiting no‐cost productivity gains. In an attempt to seamlessly fit into existing capabilities and workflows, this study explored whether machine learning models could predict HID performance from routinely generated LID process data to guide more effective clone selection. To evaluate generalizability and simulate real‐world application, models were tested on small clone panels expressing two unseen mAb products. The ranking model identified the most productive clone expressing Product D and the second most productive clone expressing Product E; relative to the clones chosen by the legacy selection method, this translated to a 68.5% higher titer and 6.6% lower titer for the respective product groups. Though these findings are preliminary and imperfect, they demonstrate the potential for valuable application of machine learning in the biomanufacturing industry by offering a new approach to improving clone selection and maximizing productivity in intensified fed‐batch processes. The following Discussion focuses on insights into intensified bioprocessing suggested by these results, strategies for clone selection and their practical application, as well as key limitations and future directions.

Insights on clone performance in intensified fed‐batch

This study was partially motivated by the hypothesis that some clones are inherently more “fit” for intensified fed‐batch processes than others. Previous observations indicated that clones with similar product yields in the LID process occasionally diverged substantially in the HID process. This pattern was exemplified by clones C01 and C06 of Product D, which generated nearly identical titers in LID but differed by a substantial 3.31 g/L in HID (Figure 4). One potential mechanistic explanation of the variability stems from how process intensification alters the relative influence of key productivity attributes, specifically qP and IVCC, on harvest titer. Intensified fed‐batch processes are designed to achieve and sustain higher viable cell densities; a trend reflected in the results of this study by the consistent increase in IVCC across all clones with a median uplift of 109% (Figure 3b). In contrast, qP was less consistently affected by intensification, with 8 of 34 clones experiencing a decrease in HID relative to LID. Since final titer is a function of both IVCC and qP, clones with higher qP in LID may be more likely to benefit from the process‐driven boost in IVCC resulting from intensification. This relationship can be visualized in Figure 3a. Assuming a consistent increase to IVCC, clones with higher qP in LID would theoretically cross the isolines, and therefore increase harvest titer, more efficiently during intensification. This concept is more deeply explored alongside a synthetic illustration in the Supporting Information (Figure S5). This hypothesis was supported during model development when attribute importance from the LightGBM ranking model indicated that qP in LID was the most important attribute. These findings offer a plausible mechanistic explanation for the observed variability in clone performance between LID and HID processes and may represent one of the underlying patterns leveraged by the machine learning models.

In an attempt to interpret other selected attributes and engineered features, relevant clusters and the Mahalanobis distances to cluster centroids were examined. GMM Clusters 1, 2, 3, and 5, representing groups of similar clone subpopulations, were qualitatively sorted by percentile thresholds into “Low,” “Average,” or “High” bins for each LID attribute and the associated HID titer (Table S1). Analysis of cluster profiles revealed that Clusters 1 and 5 were binned identically (both classified as Low qP, Average IVCC, Low Titer) yet they corresponded to different HID titer bins (Low and Average Titer, respectively). This observation suggested a distinction between seemingly similar clone subpopulations identified by the GMM model. Similarly, the Mahalanobis distance attributes were analyzed in order to understand how cluster distances were leveraged during clone ranking. A partial dependence analysis revealed that the ranking model favored clones with minimal distance to Cluster 2 and maximal distance to Cluster 1, suggesting that the LID attribute profile bins defining these clusters are indicative of HID performance (Figure S2). Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis of cluster distance attribute utilization revealed their function as a penalization tool which enabled the model to better differentiate between high and low performers in HID. Rather than explicitly rewarding similarity to a known high‐performer group, the model penalized clones with varying severity based on their proximity to specific clusters associated with poor HID performance; the highest scoring clones were the least penalized because they were the most dissimilar to the defined low‐performer clusters. Though this limited dataset is too small for definitive conclusions, this could suggest that low‐performer subpopulations, clone groups that are not well suited for HID, were easier for the model to identify than high performers. These analyses are covered more thoroughly in the Supporting Information (Figures S1–S3 and Table S1).

Machine learning approaches to clone selection

While challenges remain, this study presents one of the first demonstrations of a machine learning model directly aiding clone selection for intensified fed‐batch processes. Although interest in applying machine learning in biomanufacturing has grown in recent years, few publications have focused specifically on clone selection. 20 Some prior studies have applied machine learning to cell characterization methods, such as image analysis, to shorten timelines, reduce labor demand, and inform clone selection. 21 , 22 Others have explored applying machine learning to omics‐based analyses in order to enable deep cell characterization and guide subsequent selection.

Even when favorable ML clone selection outcomes are achieved, it is important to recognize that there are benefits, limitations, risks, and tradeoffs associated with all approaches. For example, while omics‐guided approaches can offer deep cellular insights by more thoroughly interrogating the underlying fundamental biology, widespread adoption may be limited by high‐cost instrumentation required to generate the relevant model input data. 23 , 24 Approaches that rely on cell painting and/or imaging may face similar obstacles during implementation. In contrast, the models developed in this study were designed to seamlessly integrate with the existing cell line development workflow by leveraging routinely generated datasets, avoiding any added costs or time burdens that may have come from new instrumentation. However, by grounding input datasets in LID productivity readouts, the models were necessarily constrained in their ability to uncover and leverage deeper fundamental relationships intrinsic to clone performance across processes. From a pragmatic perspective, leveraging contextually relevant bioprocessing datasets has the advantage of being more immediately deployable while being grounded in meaningful process measurements, striking a context‐appropriate balance between near‐term practicality and future potential.

Limitations and future work

While the ranking model generalized well for withheld mAb Product D, it is not immune to challenges common to machine learning tools in real‐world application. First, this study was constrained by the size of the training and testing datasets. Though a dataset comprised of 34 clones across 5 products is thorough by process development standards, it is small relative to datasets typically used for machine learning. Due to dataset size alone, the modeling results shared in this study are preliminary and indicative, rather than definitive. The success of the ranking model, the primary objective of the study, was relative to the ranking performance of the existing clone selection method for each product. The existing selection method missed the top clone for Product D, but correctly identified the top clone for Product E. In contrast, the ranking model correctly identified the top clone for Product D, yet picked the second best clone for Product E. For its envisioned use case as a selection support tool during cell line development, this preliminary result represents mixed yet cautiously optimistic performance. Even without perfect predictive accuracy, the model provides value by mitigating the risk of selecting clones which ultimately underperform at larger manufacturing scales, when repeating cell line development and selection may be prohibitively expensive or time‐consuming. The regression model was the secondary objective of this study, and its performance was not held to a strict standard due to its expected use case. The regression model was not intended to influence clone selection, but rather to provide an estimate of intensified process performance when new clone selection is not an option. For established mAb therapeutics produced at the largest scales, regulatory obstacles all but eliminate changing clones as a possibility. As the biomanufacturing field evolves and market demand increases, process intensification is becoming an increasingly attractive strategy. The models developed in this study aimed to serve groups with different needs as biomanufacturers shift to intensified processes as the standard for mAb production.

Future development on these models would focus on the targeted expansion of the training and testing datasets, with an emphasis on increasing both clonal and product diversity to improve coverage of the relevant input space. Such expansion would help address current weaknesses and improve confidence in future generalizability by addressing one of the two largest risks facing these models: insufficient representation of future input data, undermining predictive accuracy. Though input domain coverage was a key consideration in the design of the models, future performance could be constrained by the scope of clone and product variation represented in the training dataset. As molecular formats evolve, this risk will become increasingly common if not proactively addressed. By expanding the dataset in both number and molecular diversity, the models would be better positioned for long‐term robustness.

This approach, however, does not address the possibility that substantial changes to the cell line construction workflow and/or upstream processes risk altering the input–output relationships the model depends on. In the event of a disruptive workflow change, the model would make predictions based on potentially outdated underlying patterns, likely to the severe detriment of accuracy. These challenges underscore the importance of continuous performance monitoring, and retraining if warranted, in preserving the model's value as process platform offerings and protein therapeutics evolve. Consistent with the practical application approach described earlier, future development of these models will likely be a balance between the selective reinforcement of the dataset to promote greater cross‐product generalizability and the real‐world limitations that constrain implementation and relevancy.
