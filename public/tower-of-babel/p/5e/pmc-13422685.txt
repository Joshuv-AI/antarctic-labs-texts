Association between research exposure and medical specialization choices among medical students and trainees in Saudi Arabia: A cross sectional study
Authors: Nasser E. Alotaibi, Mariam M. Albjadi, Naif S. Almuhaydili, Saja A. Alharbi, Shahad N. Alasmary, Abdulaziz A. Alhumam, Arwa M. Alahmari, Shouq S. Al-Awashz, Ibrahim H. Tawhari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Research exposure may shape specialty choice, but how its timing, duration, and perceived importance connect to career decisions is not well understood, particularly in Saudi Arabia. This study examined that link between medical students, interns, and junior residents in Saudi Arabia and the perceived high research impact on career decisions and identified factors affecting their prediction.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A cross-sectional survey conducted in Saudi Arabia in 2025, recruited 286 participants through convenience and snowball sampling. The questionnaire covered demographics, research experience, publication output, mentorship quality, and specialty preferences. The main outcome was whether research had high or low impact on career decisions. Association between the level of impact of research exposure on medical specialization choices and participants’ sociodemographic and research-related characteristics was examined using the Chi-square test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables.RESULTS:Of 286 participants, 55.2% were female, 47.6% were interns, and 26.2% rated research as highly influential in their choice of specialty. General surgery was the most preferred specialty (16.4%), followed by family medicine (7.7%) then pediatrics, ophthalmology, neurology, and other specialties (each 6.3%). The top influencing factors were research experience, mentor influence, and financial prospects. Male gender, longer research duration (≥6 months), and meaningful research experience correlated with greater impact in bivariate analysis (P < 0.05). After adjustment, only significant research experience and research involvement exceeding 6 months remained independent predictors.CONCLUSION:One in four trainees saw research as a genuine driver of specialty choice, with meaningful research experience and research involvement of more than 6 months as the only independent predictors. Embedding structured research and mentorship into the curriculum rather than leaving it to extracurricular chance may better support career decisions.

Introduction

Choosing a medical specialty is rarely as intentional as it appears. Students may claim that their choice was influenced by passion, vocation, or a specific rotation that resonated with them, but the reality of the decision process is far more complex. A realist evaluation of junior doctors in the United Kingdom found that students need multiple positive experiences before committing to a field, yet a single negative encounter, a dismissive attention, or a demanding overnight shift can permanently write off a specialty.[1]

Gender shapes these decisions in ways the field has documented for decades, without fully resolving them. In a multinational study across the Middle East and North Africa, males were more likely to choose surgery, and females more likely to choose internal medicine and pediatrics, with 42.6% of students remaining undecided.[2] In Pakistan, the same gender split appeared, and the largest barrier students identified was not workload or competitiveness, but the lack of mentorship compounded by gender inequity and family obligations, as reported by 76.3% of participants.[3] In the United States, male students are significantly more likely to pursue Doctor of Medicine/Doctor of Philosophy programs and indicate academic medicine as their primary goal. Female students, meanwhile, are more likely to lose their research ambitions entirely somewhere between year 1 and graduation.[4]

Other demographic and training-related factors shape specialty choice and get far less attention in the research-exposure literature. A systematic review found that specialty preferences are not fixed at entry; they shift across training as clinical exposure and role models accumulate, rather than crystallize in one defining moment.[5] Money matters just as much. A review of 52 studies tied medical student debt and anticipated income to a preference for higher-paying specialties.[6] So does the institution itself. A 2024 systematic review of 334 studies identified clinical exposure, role models, and voluntary offerings such as electives and interest groups as the factors most often linked to specialty preference. Discrimination, prejudice, and poor-quality teaching or clinical placements were the main deterrents.[7] In a Saudi survey of over 1000 medical students and interns, 80.4% of those who said a specialty’s procedures and techniques mattered to them were women, versus 19.6% of men. The split was even wider for research experience: 83.5% of women versus 16.5% of men cited a dissertation or research project as a key factor. It is worth noting that the sample itself was 78% of female, so some of that gap may just emanate from the composition of respondents, not necessarily a stronger pull on women specifically.[8]

Research exposure sits at the center of this. The bibliometric picture is striking: student-authored publications increased 15-fold over two decades. Their scholarly impact, measured by Relative Citation Ratio, exceeds resident-authored work and approaches the benchmark for National Institutes of Health -funded research.[9] Students who do research produce work that gets cited. The problem is getting them to do it. A cross-sectional study of Spanish medical students found that research interest declined as training progressed. Senior students reported weaker motivation, fewer skills, and little encouragement from faculty.[10] A systematic review of 27 studies put numbers to what many faculty already suspected: Barriers to student research far outnumber enablers. These include heavy workloads, scarce supervisors, and little institutional support. The authors were direct: extracurricular opportunities are not enough. Research needs to be integrated into the curriculum itself.[11]

Mentorship is among the few interventions that consistently yield measurable results. Longitudinal mentorship programs have been demonstrated to enhance specialty interest and professional confidence. Female students have consistently evaluated their mentors more favorably than male students, particularly in terms of emotional guidance and professional connection.[12] For students predisposed toward academic careers, teaching and research are the most robust predictors of sustained interest. Nonetheless, bureaucracy and a perceived lack of clinical control remain the most frequently cited deterrents.[13]

What remains absent is a comprehensive account of how the timing and intensity of research exposure during medical school specifically, and to what extent and when, rather than solely its occurrence relates to the specialty ultimately chosen by the student. We, therefore, aimed to assess the association between the timing and intensity of exposure to clinical research and the participants’ ultimate specialization choices. In doing so, we sought to identify the mechanisms through which research experience shaped career decisions, examine how that impact varied across demographic groups, and determine whether research exposure was linked to interest in academic medicine.

Materials and Methods

A cross-sectional survey study was conducted on medical students, interns, and junior residents in Saudi Arabia between June and July 2025. This design was appropriate for the study’s objective of characterizing the association between exposure to research and specialization preferences at a single point in time, without the need for a longitudinal follow-up. Consistent with the cross-sectional framework, the study measured all variables simultaneously and makes no causal claims; directionality of any observed associations remains to be tested in prospective work. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of King Khalid University vide Letter No. ECM #2024-3171 dated 08/12/2024. Participation was voluntary. Before accessing the questionnaire, all potential participants were presented with an electronic information sheet outlining the study’s purpose, procedures, expected time commitment, and intended data use.

The study, conducted in Saudi Arabia, was open to eligible medical students, interns, and junior residents nationwide. No restrictions were placed on institutional affiliation; the survey was distributed through channels with broad national reach, and participants were enrolled regardless of the medical college or health system they were affiliated with.

Eligible participants were: (1) presenior medical students; (2) senior medical students in the final clinical year; (3) medical interns; (4) recent graduates within 12 months of degree completion; and (5) junior residents up to the 3rd postgraduate year (R3). Interns and junior residents were included because they represent the stages of training immediately after undergraduate medical education, making it possible to examine how research exposure and its perceived influence on career decisions change early in professional development. Non-medical students, those completing medical degrees outside Saudi Arabia or in an unspecified country, and participants with incomplete responses were excluded.

Given the absence of a centralized registry of eligible students in Saudi Arabia, probability sampling was not feasible. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling with a snowball component. The survey link was distributed via WhatsApp groups, Twitter/X, and official university student portals. Participants were encouraged to share it with their peers.

The sample size was not predetermined; enrolment closed at the end of the predefined collection window in July 2025, and yielded 286 eligible responses. No a priori power calculation was performed, which the authors acknowledge.

Data were collected between June and July 2025 via a self-administered electronic questionnaire distributed in English via Google Forms. The research team developed and thoroughly revised the questionnaire for clarity, logical flow, and relevance. It included five sections of varied formats: categorical options, 5-point Likert scales, numeric entry fields, and free-text fields. To limit duplicate submissions, the Google Forms single-response-per-account setting was enabled.

The first section captured demographic and training background: age, gender, current training status (final-year medical student, intern, or recent graduate/junior resident up to 3 years postgraduation), country of medical school, and program duration. The second section addressed research exposure, covering the year of first engagement in clinical research, total duration of involvement, research productivity (number of peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations entered as numeric values), and self-rated mentorship quality on a 5-point scale (1 = poor; 5 = excellent). It also included the primary outcome item: Overall self-rated impact of research experience on career decisions (1 = no impact; 5 = significant impact). The third section addressed specialization choice: Intended specialty (free-text), confidence in that choice, the extent to which research influenced it (both on 5-point scales), and interest in pursuing academic medicine (yes/no/unsure). The fourth section asked participants to rank six factors by their influence on specialty choice: clinical rotations, research experience, mentor influence, lifestyle considerations, financial prospects, and personal interests and rate the frequency and influence of their online engagement with research content. The fifth section contained three open-ended items, each capped at 500 characters, covering a significant research experience that influenced career plans, the most important way in which research prepared participants for their chosen specialty, and suggestions on how medical schools could better support career decision-making through research. Responses to the open-ended items were analyzed using natural language processing to identify recurring themes and patterns.

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as mean and standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on distribution. Data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, which indicated that continuous variables were nonnormally distributed; therefore, nonparametric tests were applied. The association between the level of impact of research exposure on medical specialization choices and participants’ sociodemographic and research-related characteristics was examined using the Chi-square test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables.

All potential predictors, including mentor influence, financial considerations, personal passion, and clinical rotation experience, were initially evaluated in bivariate analyses. However, these variables were not included in the multivariate logistic regression model because they produced counterintuitive or unstable estimates when entered alongside other predictors, possibly as a result of conceptual overlap, multicollinearity, and their subjective nature. To ensure model stability and interpretability, only variables with strong theoretical justification, statistical robustness, and minimal collinearity were retained. The final multivariate model was, therefore, adjusted for age, current status, timing of first engagement in clinical research, and interest in pursuing a career in academic medicine. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 26.0 (SPSS, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY: USA).

Results

A total of 286 medical students, medical interns, recent medical graduates, and residents participated in the study. As shown in Table 1, 58.7% were younger than 25 years, more than half (55.2%) were females, and 47.6% were medical interns. Regarding academic research timelines, 30.1% of participants reported having engaged in clinical research for the 1st time in their 4th year of medical school, and 31.4% had a total research involvement duration of 6–12 months during their years of medical school. The most preferred specialty was general surgery (16.4%), followed by family medicine (7.7%) then pediatrics, ophthalmology, neurology, and other specialties (each 6.3%) [Figure 1].

Overall, 26.2% participants were classified as having a high level of impact of research exposure on their specialization choice, while the remaining 73.8% were classified as low impact [Figure 2]. Male participants (P = 0.011), those with a duration of 6 months or more of research involvement (P = 0.033), and those who had significant research experience that influenced career plans (P = 0.006) were more likely to exhibit a higher level of impact on medical specialization choices [Table 3]. No other significant differences were observed. The participants in the high-impact group reported significantly better research mentorship quality (median 4 [IQR: 3–5]) compared with those in the lowimpact group (median 3 [IQR: 2–4]; P < 0.001). Similarly, the extent to which research experiences influenced the choice of specialization was significantly higher for those who reported high impact (median 3 [IQR: 2–4] vs. 2 [IQR 1–3]; P < 0.001). Confidence in specialization choice and the influence of online engagement did not differ significantly between groups (P = 0.058 and P = 0.072, respectively) [Table 3].

Discussion

This study examined how exposure to research shapes the specialty selection of medical students and junior residents. The question is worth investigating on several fronts: it touches on how trainees make career decisions, whether early research experience draws them toward academic medicine, and whether it builds the critical thinking that sustains a physician’s career over time. There is also a workforce dimension. Research exposure may guide students toward underrepresented specialties, helping to ease persistent shortages while improving professional satisfaction along the way. What we learn from this area has direct implications for curriculum design, and offer to medical educators a basis for structuring training environments that better support career development and, ultimately, the quality of care delivered to patients.

Our findings show that roughly one-quarter of respondents reported that exposure to research had a substantial influence on their specialty choice. In this group, the first engagement with clinical research most commonly occurred during the 1th year of medical school, followed by the 15th and 3rd years, with most reporting involvement lasting 6–12 months or longer. Nearly half described their research experience as meaningfully shaping their career plans. These results align with prior work that reported that 63.4% of respondents with research experience intended to incorporate research into their future practice, and that graduates with stronger academic records were more likely to develop sustained research interests.[14] Comparable findings indicate that satisfaction with an intercalated research degree was generally high; 90.0% of participants considered it a worthwhile experience, and a career in research or academic medicine was the most commonly cited motivation for pursuing one.[15] Similarly, despite interest across a broad range of clinical specialties, 84.4% of respondents reported that they had plans to pursue careers in research, and 79.2% expressed a preference for an academic medical center setting.[16]

In contrast, a higher proportion of 4th-and 6th-year students showed less interest in research than 2nd-year students (50.4% vs. 28.1%, P = 0.042), and a similar pattern was observed for an interest in writing a doctoral thesis (75.0% vs. 50.9%, P = 0.079). Both groups also reported that their professors did not actively encourage participation in scientific research unlike the 2nd-year students.[10] A related discrepancy has been documented elsewhere: Although nearly all medical students surveyed (88.0%) expressed an interest in conducting research during their undergraduate years, actual participation was considerably lower, at 51.0%. Barriers such as a shortage of effective mentors and increased academic workload were cited as factors limiting students’ ability to engage in research activities.[17] Taken together, these variations in research interest and their influence on career planning are likely to be multifactorial, shaped by institutional policies, curricular structures, mentorship quality, student motivation, accessibility, cultural context, and differences in study methodology.

Male students, total duration of research involvement during medical school, and the presence of significant research experience were identified as factors associated with the impact of exposure to research on specialty choice. After adjusting for confounding variables, two factors remained independently associated: Significant research experience (AOR = 2.800; 95% CI = 1.333–5.880) and research involvement exceeding 6 months (AOR = 2.960; 95% CI = 1.271–6.894). Students with meaningful research experience were approximately 3 times as likely to rate research exposure as having a high impact on their specialty decision. Plausible explanations include the skills acquired through research, access to mentorship, expanded career opportunities, and competitive advantages, all of which may guide trainees toward specialties that align with their academic and professional goals. Prior work has similarly shown that participation in undergraduate research (UR) varied by gender, academic year, and grade point average, and that students with prior UR experience were more inclined to pursuing further research compared to those without it.[18] Consistent with this, multivariate regression analysis demonstrated that involvement in basic, translational, and clinical research, as well as in education, and the ability to identify a suitable mentor were each independently associated with intentions to pursue an academic career.[19]

In contrast to our findings, one study found that female students demonstrated more positive attitudes toward research than their male counterparts, suggesting potential gender-based disparities in access to research opportunities.[20] However, another study reported no significant associations between routine research activity and hospital size, gender, or career motivation.[21] These discrepancies probably stem from differences in study methodology, participant demographics, institutional context, and the time periods examined. As such, careful attention to each study’s design and setting is warranted when interpreting and comparing these results.

On a 1–5 scale, respondents gave at least average ratings across several domains: The influence of online engagement in shaping research interest or Medical Specialization (median 3 [IQR: 1–3]), and the extent to which research experiences influenced specialization choices (median 2 [IQR: 1–3]). Notably higher ratings were recorded for confidence in specialization choices (median 4 [IQR: 3–5]) and the quality of research mentorship (median 3 [IQR: 2–4]). The low median score for research influence on specialization is worth noting; research was not a primary driver of their specialty decision but rather one modest contributor among several. This does not contradict our main finding, but narrows it. Significant research experience predicted higher perceived impact among those who engaged with it meaningfully, but its reach across the broader sample remained limited. The relatively high confidence scores may reflect the combined effect of increased research exposure, growing self-awareness, mentorship, structured guidance, peer influence, sustained commitment, and external validation. The favorable mentorship ratings, in turn, are possibly attributable to structured programs, dedicated research time, motivated participants, strong faculty engagement, adequate resources, and smaller mentor-to-mentee ratios.[22] Together, these factors, particularly the close alignment between mentorship experiences and students’ career goals, may help explain why research mentorship is perceived as more effective and rewarding in medical training than in other academic fields.

Beyond research experience, participants in our study ranked financial prospects, mentor influence, lifestyle considerations, personal interests, and clinical rotations as the most influential factors shaping specialty choice [Figure 3]. This is consistent with prior reports that cited income as one of the strongest determinants of career plans.[223] Those same studies also identified career prospects, perceived competency, working hours, and posttraining lifestyle, and positive experiences with a clinician or teacher in a given specialty as important contributors to specialty preference. A systematic review similarly found that lifestyle, work-life balance, and genuine interest in the discipline were the principal factors driving specialty selection. However, notable variation was observed in different country contexts,[24] a pattern that is broadly consistent with our own findings.

Over 40% of participants had research experiences substantial enough to influence their career plans. Those who did noted three main benefits: a stronger grasp of research methods, sharper critical thinking, and improved patient care. When asked what would better support their career decision-making, the most common responses were more research opportunities, improved mentorship, and access to additional courses and workshops. Among Saudi medical students, the pull toward UR tends to be practical: the top three motivators were improving residency competitiveness, building a stronger curriculum vitae, and publishing in peer-reviewed journals.[18] What holds students back, on the other hand, is fairly consistent: inadequate faculty guidance and a timetable already stretched tight with academic demands.[25] Addressing these gaps requires action on several fronts. Ahmed et al., point to curriculum reform as a starting point, specifically, weaving research methodology vertically and horizontally through undergraduate training rather than treating it as an add-on. Alongside that, they call for institutions to dedicate adequate teaching time and resources, secure faculty involvement in research, and cultivate a culture, in which research is genuinely valued rather than merely tolerated by the administration.[26]

This study’s strength lies in its inclusion of trainees across the full training continuum, from pre-senior medical students through junior residents up to the third postgraduate year, rather than a single, narrowly defined group. The use of multivariate logistic regression further supports the finding that significant research experience and research involvement exceeding 6 months independently predict impact rather than reflect other factors picked up in bivariate testing.

This study has a few limitations. Because of the cross-sectional design, we can describe associations but cannot draw causal conclusions. Recruitment relied on convenience and snowball sampling, so the sample may not represent all medical trainees in Saudi Arabia. In addition, the sample size was not predetermined; although it proved adequate for the analyses conducted, the findings should be regarded as exploratory. All data were self-reported, which carries the usual risk of recall and response bias. Furthermore, information regarding participants’ institutional affiliations was not collected, which resulted in a limited understanding of differences between participants from different types of institutions.

Conclusion

Research exposure had a high impact on specialization choice for roughly one in four participants. Male sex and longer research involvement were associated with greater impact in bivariate analysis, but only significant research experience and research involvement exceeding 6 months remained independent predictors: participants with significant research experience had at least 2.8 times the chance of having a high level of impact on medical specialization choices compared to those with no significant research experience. Participants with research involvement exceeding 6 months had around 3 times the chance of having a high level of impact on medical specialization choices, than those with research involvement of <6 months. Structured, meaningful research experience can shape specialty decision-making, and mentorship and curricular integration are the most promising targets for support.
