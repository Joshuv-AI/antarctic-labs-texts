ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundErdheim-Chester disease (ECD) is a rare non–Langerhans cell histiocytosis that frequently involves the cardiovascular system.Case SummaryWe report the case of a 40-year-old man who presented with concurrent atrial flutter and severe sinus node dysfunction and was subsequently diagnosed with ECD. His medical history was notable for nonischemic cardiomyopathy and central diabetes insipidus. Initial evaluation demonstrated chest pain, elevated C-reactive protein levels, and endocrine abnormalities. History of Presentation

A 40-year-old man presented with a 3-month history of intermittent chest pain. He initially presented to an emergency department with cardiac-type chest pain and was admitted with a working diagnosis of unstable angina. He was discharged after coronary angiography demonstrated normal coronary arteries and serial troponin measurements were negative. One week later, he re-presented with recurrent chest pain accompanied by diaphoresis and syncope. Electrocardiography (ECG) demonstrated typical atrial flutter with 2:1 atrioventricular node conduction at a ventricular rate of 150 beats/min (Figure 1). Subsequently, he developed recurrent syncopal episodes, and repeat ECG revealed severe sinus bradycardia with prolonged sinus pauses, consistent with advanced sinus node dysfunction. A temporary pacemaker was inserted, and he was referred to our institution with the accompanying ECG findings (Figure 2). On physical examination, a right axillary lymph node was palpable. Laboratory evaluation showed an elevated C-reactive protein level of 30.2 mg/L, markedly reduced testosterone (0.60 nmol/L), and low cortisol (79.70 nmol/L).

Past Medical History

The patient's medical history was notable for nonischemic cardiomyopathy with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 45%, hypertension, and central diabetes insipidus.

Differential Diagnosis

The initial presentation of chest pain in a younger man prompted a broad differential diagnosis encompassing cardiac and noncardiac etiologies. Considering his medical background and evidence of systemic involvement, malignant and rare conditions were also considered. Cardiac causes included heart failure, valvular heart disease, coronary artery disease, and arrhythmias. Noncardiac considerations included muscular pain and gastrointestinal disorders such as severe gastroesophageal reflux disease. Malignancy and uncommon systemic diseases were included in the differential because of the patient's multisystem manifestations.

Investigations

Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed a mediastinal mass encasing the right atrium and distal superior vena cava (SVC), along with axillary lymphadenopathy (Figure 3). Abdominal CT demonstrated diffuse circumferential soft-tissue thickening encasing the abdominal aorta, with renal and perirenal involvement producing the characteristic “hairy kidney” appearance. Cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) showed a right atrial mass infiltrating all atrial walls and extending into the anterior atrioventricular groove, posterior wall, and interatrial septum, with mild pericardial effusion. The mass partially obstructed the SVC and extended approximately 4 cm above the SVC ostium, measuring 6 × 5.5 cm in the anteroposterior and transverse dimensions (Figure 4).

Core needle biopsy of the axillary lesion revealed adipose tissue with lymphoid aggregates and foamy histiocytes, without Touton giant cells (Figure 6). Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed CD68+/CD163+ histiocytes, CD1a positivity in scattered Langerhans cells, and S100 negativity (Figure 6). Flow cytometry demonstrated 92% mature T cells (CD4/CD8 ratio: 1.46), 4% natural killer cells, and 4% polyclonal B cells. Molecular testing using a polymerase chain reaction–based Idylla system (Biocartis) identified BRAF V600E/D and NRAS codon 12 mutations, which are consistent with ECD.

Management

A leadless pacemaker was implanted to treat severe sinus node dysfunction, and the patient's care was transitioned to the hematology service for initiation of targeted therapy.

Discussion

ECD is a rare non–Langerhans cell histiocytosis characterized by multisystem infiltration of CD68+, CD1a− foamy histiocytes, with approximately 1,500 cases reported since 1930.1 It most commonly presents between the fifth and seventh decades of life, although younger patients have been described, and it shows a male predominance.2 Skeletal involvement is nearly universal; however, ECD frequently affects extraskeletal organs, including the skin, lungs, central nervous system, kidneys, and cardiovascular system.

The etiology of ECD remains incompletely understood, but it is associated with marked dysregulation of the Th-1 (T cell helper 1) immune response. ECD is driven by clonal proliferation of myeloid progenitor cells, with somatic mutations in the MAPK signaling pathway identified in most patients. The BRAF V600E mutation is present in more than 80% of cases and is strongly associated with cardiac involvement, playing a central role in guiding targeted therapeutic strategies.3

Cardiac involvement is common and represents the most frequent manifestation after skeletal disease. Despite this, it is often underdiagnosed, and the true prevalence and long-term outcomes of cardiac involvement remain poorly defined.3 Cardiac manifestations include pericardial effusion, cardiac tamponade, conduction abnormalities, and heart failure. Advanced imaging modalities, particularly CMR and CT, are essential for detection, often demonstrating characteristic findings such as right atrioventricular sulcus infiltration and pericardial involvement.

This case is notable for the simultaneous presentation of atrial flutter and severe sinus node dysfunction, an exceptionally rare combination in ECD.4 Atrial flutter has been reported in only a small minority of patients with ECD, with one large series identifying it in just 1 of 200 cases, while sinus node dysfunction has been described only sporadically, including a report of reversibility with interferon alfa therapy.5,6 Cardiac arrhythmias in ECD are believed to result from histiocytic infiltration leading to fibrosis and disruption of normal conduction pathways. Atrial flutter likely reflects atrial myocardial involvement causing structural remodeling and electrical instability, whereas sinus node dysfunction suggests fibrosis or direct infiltration of the sinus node. The coexistence of tachyarrhythmia and bradyarrhythmia in this patient may therefore indicate advanced cardiac infiltration affecting both atrial myocardium and nodal tissue.

Imaging played a pivotal role in establishing the diagnosis. CMR demonstrated extensive right atrial infiltration, a predilection site in ECD reported in up to 63% of cases, while CT confirmed the classic “hairy kidney” and “coated aorta” signs.5 Histopathologic findings and molecular analysis, including identification of BRAF V600E/D and NRAS mutations, further supported the diagnosis and underscored the importance of genetic profiling in therapeutic decision-making.

Management required a multidisciplinary approach. A leadless pacemaker was implanted to treat severe sinus node dysfunction while minimizing the risk of worsening SVC stenosis and potential SVC syndrome.4 The patient was initiated on mutation-directed targeted therapy based on BRAF V600E/D and NRAS mutation status, which has demonstrated efficacy in controlling both systemic and cardiac manifestations of ECD.7 This case also indicates that significant cardiac involvement may occur in younger patients, emphasizing the need for early and comprehensive cardiac evaluation. Such assessment should include echocardiography, CMR, and FDG PET/CT to detect both overt and subclinical disease. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for both tachyarrhythmias and bradyarrhythmias in patients with ECD, as their presence may signal advanced cardiac infiltration. Optimal care relies on close collaboration among cardiology, hematology, and radiology teams. Targeted therapies guided by molecular findings, particularly BRAF mutation status, may significantly improve both systemic and cardiac outcomes.

This case adds to the limited literature on ECD-associated arrhythmias and highlights the role of individualized interventions, including advanced pacing strategies. In conclusion, ECD may present with rare dual arrhythmias, such as atrial flutter and severe sinus node dysfunction, necessitating thorough and multimodal cardiac evaluation.
