Active vitamin D acts in vitro as an adjuvant to anti-TNFα treatment in a psoriatic synovial fibroblast activation model by modulating human Th17 activity
Authors: Yi He, Bennie Van Heeswijk, Adriana M C Mus, Nadine Davelaar, Radjesh Bisoendial, Erik Lubberts
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPsoriatic arthritis (PsA) is an IL-23/IL-17/TNF-driven inflammatory arthritis, commonly accompanied by vitamin D deficiency. Here, we investigate the interaction between PsA synovial fibroblasts (SFs) and human memory CCR6+Th17 (hmemCCR6+Th17) cells and evaluate the therapeutic effects of TNFα and/or IL-17A inhibitors, and the adjunctive therapy potential of 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 (1,25(OH)2D3).MethodsSF derived from PsA biopsies were co-cultured with hmemCCR6+Th17 cells to construct a PsA SF activation model. SF phenotypes were analysed by flow cytometry. Treatments included adalimumab (anti-TNFα), secukinumab (anti-IL-17A), and/or 1,25(OH)2D3, applied alone or in combination. A transwell system was used to assess treatment effects on SF and hmemCCR6+Th17 cells. Cytokines and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) were quantified by ELISA.ResultsPsA SF were composed of heterogeneous subpopulations and constructed a pro-inflammatory feedback loop with hmemCCR6+Th17 cells. Anti-TNFα and/or anti-IL-17A significantly suppressed proinflammatory cytokines and tissue-destructive mediators in the PsA SF activation model, but showed limited effect on IL-22 and IFNγ. Adding 1,25(OH)2D3 to anti-TNFα treatment effectively overcame the limitations of single anti-TNFα in suppressing Th17 cytokines, while significantly enhancing the inhibition of IL-6, IL-8 and MMPs. In addition, 1,25(OH)2D3 promoted the production of IL-10.ConclusionsTNFα or IL-17A inhibition alone does not completely inhibit the proinflammatory loop between hmemCCR6+Th17 cells and PsA SF, although the combination shows additive effect on suppressing proinflammatory and tissue-destructive mediators. Importantly, 1,25(OH)2D3 plays a complementary role in the treatment of this proinflammatory loop alongside anti-TNFα therapy and significantly induces IL-10. Clinical PsA studies are needed to explore the additional therapeutic potential of this combination.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Further clinical PsA studies are needed to explore the additional therapeutic potential of this combination.

Introduction

Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a chronic inflammatory disease with diverse clinical manifestations that affect the joints, entheses and are commonly observed in patients with psoriasis (PsO).1 A recent meta-analysis of global clinical studies found that PsA affects approximately 15%–25% of individuals with PsO.2 One of the primary manifestations of PsA is psoriatic synovitis, a condition characterised by synovial angiogenesis, immune cell infiltration and the activation of synovial fibroblasts (SFs).3

Investigating the interaction between immune cells and stromal cells in inflamed joints is crucial for understanding the pathogenesis of PsA. Increased numbers of IL-17-producing cells and elevated levels of related cytokines have been observed both in the circulation and synovial fluid of PsA patients.4 In the inflamed joints, IL-17+T helper cells,4 IL-17+CD8+ T cells5 and type 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3)6 are abundant with high levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-22, IFNγ and TNFα. Those cytokines independently or synergistically activate stromal fibroblasts and osteoclasts, contributing to tissue and bone damage.3 7

As a common chronic inflammatory disease, PsA shares several clinical features with rheumatoid arthritis (RA).7 However, differences in serological markers, radiographic features and responses to biological disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (bDMARDs) distinguish PsA from RA.7 A single-cell transcriptomic comparative study by Floudas et al8 reveals that distinct interaction between immune and stromal cells in RA and PsA defines their different pathogenesis. In inflammatory arthritis, the interaction between SFs and Th17 cells plays a critical role in disease pathogenesis.9 In RA, studies have shown that SFs comprise heterogeneous populations with distinct functions, identified by surface markers such as CD34, FAP and THY1.10 11 However, research on phenotypes and functions of PsA SFs remains limited. This underscores the need for further investigation into the specific interactions between PsA SFs and Th17 cells.

TNFα and IL-17A are two key pro-inflammatory cytokines driving PsA pathogenesis. In recent years, bDMARDs targeting TNFα and IL-17A have significantly improved PsA treatment outcomes.12 However, about 30% of PsA patients fail to respond to initial anti-TNFα therapy or experience adverse effects leading to treatment discontinuation with TNFα or IL-17A inhibitors.13 14 These challenges highlight the urgent need for more effective therapeutic strategies.

Emerging studies are exploring new strategies for PsA treatment, including inflammatory pathway inhibitors such as JAK and TYK2 inhibitors, multitarget drugs and the combined use of DMARDs.15 The effectiveness and benefits of dual targeting TNFα and IL-17A in PsA remain a topic of debate. Our previous study, using a co-culture system of PsA SFs and allogeneic synovial fluid-derived CD4+T cells provided preliminary evidence supporting the effectiveness of anti-TNFα/IL-17A.16 In addition, a phase II clinical trial evaluating a dual-target immunoglobulin against TNFα and IL-17A in PsA showed efficacy comparable to adalimumab (TNFα inhibitor), without a notable increase in adverse events.17 Given that PsA is primarily driven by the IL-23/IL-17 axis,1 we aim to develop an in vitro model by co-culturing Th17 cells with PsA SFs. This model provides a more targeted platform to investigate the efficacy of potential treatments.

The potential benefits of complementary therapies, such as vitamin D supplementation, for PsA management remain controversial. Vitamin D is well-known for its broad immunoregulatory effects on human immune cells, particularly Th17 cells.18 19 Several observational studies have reported a correlation between vitamin D deficiency and psoriatic diseases. A meta-analysis of nine studies revealed that serum 25(OH)D3 levels were significantly lower in PsA patients compared with healthy controls (mean difference: −6.42).20 Similarly, a retrospective study found that hypovitaminosis D (<30 ng/mL) was highly prevalent in PsO patients with PsA (82.2%).21 Supplementation with vitamin D has been shown to significantly improve disease activity in PsO22 and inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD),23 particularly when combined with DMARDs in cases of vitamin D deficiency. These findings suggest the potential of vitamin D as an adjunctive therapy for PsA, which warrants further investigation.

Therefore, we investigated the characteristics of a positive feedback loop between human memory CCR6+Th17 cells and PsA SFs, and the effect of TNFα and/or IL-17A inhibitors with 1,25(OH)2D3 (the active form of vitamin D) on regulating inflammation. To accomplish the above objectives, an in vitro model was constructed by co-culturing PsA SFs and allogeneic peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) sorted memory CCR6+Th17 cells. The phenotypes of PsA SFs were analysed by flow cytometry. The efficacy of treatments was assessed by levels of cytokines and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). Additionally, the potential regulatory targets of 1,25(OH)2D3 were also explored. This research put more insight into better understanding the pathogenesis of PsA and contributing to the advancement of PsA treatment.

Methods

PsA SF isolation and culture

To obtain SFs for culture, synovial tissue biopsy specimens were collected from inflamed knee joints of PsA patient during synovectomy. Informed consent was obtained from patients prior to synovectomy. Synovial tissue biopsy specimens were fractionised and cultured with Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Thermo Fisher) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal calf serum (FCS, Thermo Fisher) and penicillin/streptomycin (100 units/mL, LONZA). Passages 3–8 of the SF were used for experiments.

PsA SFs phenotyping

PsA SF phenotypes were analysed before co-culture. PsA SFs were detached by 5 mM EDTA and stained with monoclonal antibodies as shown in online supplemental table I. Cell viability dye (eFluor506, eBioscience) was stained after antibody cocktail incubation. Samples were measured with the five-laser Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometer and analysed by FlowJo V.10.10.0 software. The gating strategy for identifying SFs is illustrated in online supplemental figure 1a. The number of populations was quantified and shown as percentages of the whole population in dot plots.

Memory CCR6+Th17 cells sorting

PBMCs were isolated from healthy donors’ buffy coats (Sanquin, study approval number NVT0291) using Ficoll density gradient for cell sorting. After CD4+cells pre-enrichment with CD4 microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec), cells were stained by monoclonal antibodies shown in online supplemental table II. Cell viability dye (DAPI) was added before sorting. Target T cell populations were sorted from PBMCs using FACSAria or FACS Fusion Cell Sorting system (BD Bioscience). CD25+CD127− (Treg) cells were excluded during sorting.

Th17 cells and PsA SFs co-culture model

In vitro model was constructed by co-culturing PsA SFs and allogeneic memory CCR6+Th17 cells sorted from PBMCs (figure 1a). 1×104 PsA SFs were seeded in plate overnight and co-cultured with 2.5×104 memory CCR6+Th17 cells with Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium supplemented with 10% FCS, penicillin/streptomycin (100 units/ml), and β-mercaptoethanol (5×10-5M) in a 5% CO2, 37°C incubator for 72 hours. Cells were activated with soluble anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 (400 and 600 ng/mL, respectively, Miltenyi Biotec), with or without the presence of anti-TNFα (adalimumab, 1 ug/mL), anti-IL-17A (secukinumab, 100 ug/mL), isotype control (IgG1/kappa), 1,25(OH)2D3 (100 nM, STEMCELL) and/or solvent control (ethanol).

Transwell system

A HTS transwell membrane system (Corning) was used to investigate the effects of biologics with or without 1,25(OH)2D3 on PsA SFs and memory CCR6+Th17 cells in the absence of physical interactions. The duration of the culture is similar as to that described above for the co-culture model. 1×104 PsA SFs were seeded in the basolateral chamber overnight. 2.5×104 allogeneic memory CCR6+Th17 cells were plated in the inserted chamber. The transwell system was cultured in a 5% CO2, 37°C incubator for 72 hours. Culture medium, T cell activators and treatments have been described in the ‘CCR6+Th17 and PsA SFs co-culture model’ part.

Assessment of cytokine levels

Cell culture supernatant from co-culture or transwell system was collected and used to measure the levels of cytokines and MMPs. TNFα, IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-10, IL-22, IFNγ, IL-6 and IL-8 expression levels were determined with human quantitative ELISA (Invitrogen). MMP-1 and MMP-3 expression levels were determined using human Duoset ELISA kits (R&D systems).

Statistical analysis

Graphs display individual data points with the mean±SE of the mean overlaid, unless otherwise indicated. GraphPad Prism (V.9.1) was used for creating graphs, with SPSS (V.26) and R (V.4.1.1) software used to perform statistical analysis. Group comparisons were conducted using paired t-tests, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, mixed linear model or robust linear mixed model as appropriate. Mixed models were fitted with treatment as a fixed effect and donor as a random effect using the lmer() function from the lme4 package. When model assumptions were violated, robust linear mixed-effects models were used with the rlmer() function from the robustlmm package. Post hoc comparisons were based on estimated marginal means using available paired data with the emmeans package. Data were transformed (log or square root) for modelling, but raw values are shown for interpretability. A p<0.05 was considered as statistically significant. Detailed statistical methods and tests for each experiment were illustrated in the respective figure legends.

Results

Characterisation of PsA SFs phenotypes and interaction with memory CCR6+ Th17 cells

Psoriatic synoviocytes and Th17 cells are key mediators in the development of psoriatic synovitis. Prior to co-culture, we conducted flow cytometry analysis to characterise the phenotypes of PsA SFs and investigate the stability of markers during in vitro culture.

As shown in figure 1b, in vitro cultured SFs exhibited a heterogeneous population. Notably, over 99% of these fibroblasts expressed podoplanin (PDPN), although CD34 expression varied among the populations. Both CD34− and CD34+ fibroblast subsets exhibited high levels of fibroblast activation protein (FAP) and thymocyte antigen 1 (THY1), with only minor differences observed between the two groups. The expression of PDPN and FAP remained stable, whereas CD34+ and CD90− populations diminished in later passages of PsA SFs (online supplemental figure 1b).

The interaction between memory CCR6+Th17 cells and PsA SFs was investigated. Allogeneic memory CCR6+Th17 cells were observed to migrate towards and cluster around the SFs after 72 hours of co-culture (figure 1c). To further assess the interaction between memory CCR6+Th17 cells and PsA SFs, we measured levels of T cell-derived cytokines (TNFα, IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-22 and IFNγ), fibroblast-derived pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and IL-8), and MMPs (MMP-1 and MMP-3) in the co-culture supernatant. Compared with Th17 cells alone, co-culturing with PsA SFs significantly enhanced the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines from memory CCR6+Th17 cells (figure 1d). Moreover, memory CCR6+Th17 cells significantly increased IL-6 and IL-8 production by SFs (figure 1e), aligning with their immune effector phenotype. MMP-1 and MMP-3 were also highly induced after co-culturing (figure 1f), indicating that these fibroblasts exhibit tissue-destructive capabilities when activated by memory CCR6+Th17 cells.

In summary, the above findings revealed that memory CCR6+Th17 cells from PBMCs and PsA SFs interacted to establish a pro-inflammatory feedback loop, highlighting their potential role in psoriatic synovitis and providing insights into possible therapeutic targets.

Comparative and combined effects of anti-TNFα and anti-IL-17A therapies on PsA synovial inflammation

Adalimumab and secukinumab were used to neutralise TNFα and IL-17A, respectively, at pretitrated concentrations that achieved approximately 60%–70% cytokine suppression in our previous study.16 These suboptimal concentrations were chosen to demonstrate possible additional combination effects. The treatment workflow is illustrated in figure 2a. Both antibodies significantly suppressed respective cytokine levels (figure 2b, c). Notably, the combination of anti-TNFα and anti-IL-17A did not yield a further reduction in TNFα and IL-17A levels beyond that achieved with each antibody alone. Unexpectedly, anti-IL-17A led to increased TNFα production (figure 2b).

Both anti-TNFα and anti-IL-17A showed limited effect on IL-17F (figure 2d). IFNγ production was inhibited by anti-TNFα or dual anti-TNFα/IL-17A treatments, whereas the anti-IL-17A showed no suppression (figure 2e), suggesting a potential correlation between IFNγ levels and TNFα. Importantly, IL-22 level remained unchanged with either anti-TNFα or anti-IL-17A treatment alone, and the combination treatment exhibited a trend towards increased IL-22, although this was not statistically significant (figure 2f).

Both anti-TNFα and anti-IL-17A effectively suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and IL-8), MMP-1 and MMP-3 production from PsA SFs (figure 2g–j). The combined anti-TNFα/IL-17A treatment demonstrated a greater inhibitory effect on the activity of PsA SFs, leading to a significant reduction in cytokines and MMP production compared with each treatment alone.

In summary, suppression of either TNFα or IL-17A effectively reduced Th17-driven fibroblast activity, while dual treatment resulted in enhanced effects.

Additional value of 1,25(OH)2D3 in modulation of PsA beyond TNF-α and/or IL-17A inhibition

To assess whether 1,25(OH)2D3 could offer additional benefits in managing the activity of memory CCR6+Th17-driven PsA SF activation, sole 1,25(OH)2D3 effect on the proinflammatory loop between Th17-PsA SF in vitro was investigated (online supplemental figure 2). 1,25(OH)2D3 broadly suppressed the production of IL-17A, IFNγ and IL-22 (online supplemental figure 2b, d, e), together with IL-6, IL-8, MMP-1 and MMP-3 (online supplemental figure 2f-i). However, 1,25(OH)2D3 did not affect TNFα (online supplemental figure 2a) and showed limited impact on IL-17F (online supplemental figure 2c).

Next, the effects of 1,25(OH)2D3 in combination with anti-TNFα/IL-17A treatments in PsA co-culture model were examined. Consistent with the findings in online supplemental figure 2), 25(OH)2D3 did not suppress TNFα, as this effect was solely attributable to the TNFα inhibitor (figure 3a). However, the addition of 1,25(OH)2D3 to anti-IL-17A treatment showed a further decrease in IL-17A levels, although we cannot exclude that this is a direct 1,25(OH)2D3 effect (figure 3b). Similarly, IFNγ level was significantly reduced when 1,25(OH)2D3 was combined with either anti-TNF or anti-IL-17A antibodies (figure 3d). Strikingly, IL-22 was only suppressed when 1,25(OH)2D3 was added (figure 3e). Nevertheless, IL-17F levels remained unaffected by the induction of 1,25(OH)2D3 (figure 3c).

The effect of 1,25(OH)2D3 on reducing Th17-driven PsA SF activation was also observed. Interestingly, 1,25(OH)2D3 showed enhanced suppression of IL-6 and IL-8 only in the presence of anti-TNFα or dual anti-TNFα/IL-17A (figure 3f–g). Additionally, the complementary effect of 1,25(OH)2D3 together with the TNFα inhibitor was also observed for MMP-1 (figure 3h), but not MMP-3 (figure 3i).

To conclude, the introduction of 1,25(OH)2D3 did not interfere with the efficacy of anti-TNFα/IL-17A. Instead, it provided enhanced and complementary effects on controlling Th17 cell-derived inflammatory cytokines and showed additional value on suppressing IL-6, IL-8 and MMP-1 when combined with anti-TNFα.

Regulatory effects of 1,25(OH)2D3 on the proinflammatory loop between memory CCR6+Th17 cells and PsA SF: more prone to regulating pro-inflammatory cytokines instead of tissue destruction enzymes

To investigate how 1,25(OH)2D3 exerts effects on controlling the proinflammatory loop between memory CCR6+Th17 cells and PsA SFs, we used a transwell system (figure 4a). In this setup, PsA SFs were seeded in the basolateral chamber, while memory CCR6+Th17 cells were placed in the insert chamber and cultured for 72 hours. Soluble anti-CD3/CD28, anti-TNFα/IL-17A, with/without 1,25(OH)2D3 were applied as in normal co-culture system. This arrangement facilitated the exchange of secreted factors between the cells but prevented direct physical interactions between the memory CCR6+Th17 cells and PsA SFs.

The absence of physical interaction led to a significant reduction in cytokine production derived from both memory CCR6+Th17 cells and PsA SFs (online supplemental figure 3a). Individual 1,25(OH)2D3 treatment effectively suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-17A, IL-22, IL-6 and IL-8 (online supplemental figure 3c, e, f, g), but not TNFα, MMP-1 and MMP-3 secretion (online supplemental figure 3b, h, i).

The effects of anti-TNFα/IL-17A in combination with 1,25(OH)2D3 were investigated in the transwell system. Neutralising TNFα and IL-17A significantly suppressed respective cytokines. The levels of IL-17A and IL-22, but not TNFα, were strongly reduced by 1,25(OH)2D3 (figure 4b–d). IL-17A inhibition significantly downregulated IL-6, but not IL-8 (figure 4e–f). However, the combined usage of 1,25(OH)2D3 resolved the inadequacy of anti-IL-17A on IL-8 suppression (figure 4f). In contrast, anti-TNFα fully blocked IL-8 levels with no window for further suppression when combined with 1,25(OH)2D3 (figure 4f). Interestingly, no additive effect of 1,25(OH)2D3 was found for the concentration of IL-6 in combination with dual TNFα and IL-17A blockade (figure 4e).

Regarding tissue destruction enzymes, only TNFα neutralisation effectively reduced both MMP-1 and MMP-3 secretion, while IL-17A inhibition did not (figure 4g, h). Furthermore, the co-administration of 1,25(OH)2D3 with either anti-TNFα or IL-17A did not exhibit a synergistic effect on controlling MMPs (figure 4g, h), which was consistent with the findings in online supplemental figure 3h,i.

Additional regulatory effects of 1,25(OH)2D3 on IL-10 induction

Since anti-TNF treatment in patients with RA can increase IL-10 induction (41), we investigated whether the treatments of anti-TNFα/IL-17A and/or 1,25(OH)2D3 are effective in enhancing IL-10 in the in vitro memory CCR6+Th17-driven PsA SF activation model.

In the co-culture system, neutralising TNFα and/or IL-17A resulted in a moderate increase of IL-10 levels compared with the isotype control group (figure 5a). Notably, treatment with 1,25(OH)2D3 alone significantly enhanced IL-10 production compared with the ethanol solvent control group (figure 5b). Furthermore, combining either anti-TNFα or anti-IL-17A with 1,25(OH)2D3 resulted in a greater induction of IL-10 compared with antibody neutralisation alone (figure 5c). Of note, also the combination of anti-TNFα/anti-IL17A markedly induced IL-10 which was not further increased by adding 1,25(OH)2D3 (figure 5c).

In the transwell system, basal IL-10 levels were significantly lower than in the co-culture system (online supplemental figure 3). Nevertheless, 1,25(OH)2D3 alone retains the ability to elevate the IL-10 level, though the increase is not statistically significant (figure 5d). Neutralising TNFα and/or IL-17A did not significantly elevate IL-10, which was overcome when combined with 1,25(OH)2D3 (figure 5e).

Together, these findings confirmed the significant benefit of 1,25(OH)2D3 in increasing IL-10 levels as an adjunctive treatment to TNFα or IL-17A inhibition.

Discussion

In this study, we characterised the phenotypes of in vitro cultured PsA SFs and found a heterogeneous population. Co-culturing these cells with activated memory CCR6+Th17 cells resulted in a positive inflammatory feedback loop with an increase of both Th17-derived cytokines and PsA SFs derived proinflammatory cytokines and MMPs. Treatment with anti-TNFα and anti-IL-17A antibodies separately does not lead to complete inhibition of especially the Th17-derived cytokines but does show suppression of SFs produced inflammatory factors. Furthermore, a clear enhancing suppressive effect can be seen in the combination therapy on the pro-inflammatory cytokines and tissue destructive mediators. Importantly, our findings showed that active vitamin D acts in vitro as an adjuvant to anti-TNFα treatment in a PsA SF activation model by modulating human Th17 activity. This suggests a potential complementary role for 1,25(OH)2D3 in PsA management alongside anti-TNFα therapy, since anti-TNFα does not significantly suppress IL-17A, IL-17F and IL-22 but suppresses TNF and 1,25(OH)2D3 does not suppress TNFα but significantly inhibits Th17 cytokines. Notably, our results show that the anti-inflammatory effects of 1,25(OH)2D3 are primarily mediated through the regulation of Th17 cells rather than direct modulation of SFs.

SFs are central to PsA pathogenesis, as their interactions with immune cells drive sustained inflammation, cartilage degradation and bone erosion.3 24 Emerging evidence suggests that in arthritis, SFs can be classified into pro-inflammatory and tissue-destructive subsets based on the expression of fibroblast activation protein (FAP) and THY1.10 Recent single-cell sequencing research has identified differential enrichment of FAP and THY1 in SFs from PsA and RA.8 Additionally, fibroblast subpopulations in PsA and RA showed distinct responses to TGF-β and IL-1β.8 Fibroblast activation identified by increased synovial and entheseal FAP uptake has been shown as a significant risk factor for PsA development.25 A study using PET/CT to trace 68Ga-FAPI-04 signals in synovial and entheseal sites of inflammatory joint disease patients showed that the enrichment of FAP was correlated to erosive lesions, where MMP3+/IL-6+ pro-inflammatory fibroblasts were high in FAP internalisation.26 Our results were consistent with these findings as flow cytometry analysis of PsA SF showed high levels of FAP, which suggested that they possess pro-inflammatory potential.

In our co-culture model, the PsA fibroblasts predominantly consisted of the FAP+THY1+subset, exhibiting high levels of IL-6, IL-8 and MMPs on activation by memory CCR6+Th17 cells, even after several passages in vitro cultures. These results suggest that these fibroblasts maintain their pathological characteristics over time and possess both immunomodulatory and tissue-destructive capabilities. We also observed heterogeneity within the PsA SF population, comprising both CD34+and CD34− subsets, with a higher proportion of FAP+THY1+ cells in the CD34+fraction. Previous studies have indicated that CD34, along with THY1, may be a marker for fibroblast invasiveness and differentiation potential.11 However, further research is needed to explore the functional roles and clinical relevance of these fibroblast phenotypes, particularly their association with different clinical manifestations and disease progression in PsA.

TNFα plays a critical role in amplifying inflammation and promoting joint damage.3 The strong involvement of the IL-23/IL-17 axis in PsA further underscores the potent pro-inflammatory effect of IL-17A.27 In clinical practice, anti-TNFα and anti-IL-17A show similar efficacy in reducing joint symptoms, though they differ in their effectiveness on skin manifestations.12 Recent studies have revealed that TNFα and IL-17A can synergise to promote inflammation and tissue destruction via ELF3 mediator and NF-κB pathways.28 29 Given the synergistic effects between TNFα and IL-17, we explored whether dual inhibition of these cytokines would provide enhanced benefits compared with monotherapy at the cellular level.

Our findings suggested that the combined inhibition of TNFα and IL-17A resulted in superior control of PsA SF activity compared with inhibiting each cytokine alone in vitro. While anti-TNFα and anti-IL-17A therapies were effective independently, combination therapy provided more comprehensive regulation of the inflammatory response. This aligns with prior studies, including a rat spondyloarthritis model where dual blockade reduced inflammation and tissue destruction significantly.30 A phase II trial assessing a dual-targeting immunoglobulin (ABT-122) showed efficacy similar to TNF inhibitors in PsA patients, but without substantial differentiation.17 As our results indicated that the pro-inflammatory profile cannot completely be suppressed by inhibition of TNFα or IL-17A, 1,25(OH)2D3 was introduced to evaluate its potential added value.

1,25(OH)2D3, the active form of vitamin D, exerts broad immunomodulatory effects, influencing several immune cell types and potentially impacting autoimmune diseases.18 Vitamin D deficiency is common among both PsO and PsA patients.20 While topical vitamin D analogues are effective and safe in PsO treatment,22 the impact of vitamin D on PsA progression is less defined, with clinical trials on vitamin D supplementation and autoimmune diseases yielding mixed results.31 Nevertheless, exploring vitamin D as a complementary therapy in PsA is of interest. One study found that PsA patients with low vitamin D levels (25(OH)D≤20 ng/mL) had a higher risk of discontinuing anti-TNFα treatment.32 However, there is limited research on the effect of combining bDMARDs with vitamin D supplementation in PsA.

In our in vitro experiments, we observed that combining 1,25(OH)2D3 with TNFα inhibition, or with dual TNFα and IL-17A inhibition, provided better regulatory effects on inflammation compared with IL-17A inhibition. 1,25(OH)2D3 exerted a comprehensive effect by modulating the Th17 pro-inflammatory cytokine profile, suppressing IL-17A, IFNγ and IL-22, but not TNFα, while enhancing IL-10 production. This is consistent with our previous ex vivo study using a Th17-driven RA SF activation model in which 1,25(OH)2D3 showed limited effect on TNFα, while neutralising TNFα showed limited effect on Th17 cytokines.19

Additionally, 1,25(OH)2D3 significantly reduced fibroblast-derived inflammation when combined with TNFα inhibition. 1,25(OH)2D3 with TNFα/IL-17A blockade showed better suppression of IL-6, IL-8 and MMPs compared with dual cytokine inhibition, which was consistent with the results of 1,25(OH)2D3 with TNFα inhibitor. However, adding 1,25(OH)2D3 to anti-IL-17A did not yield significant additional benefits. These findings suggest that combining 1,25(OH)2D3 with TNFα inhibitor, but not IL-17A inhibitor, may provide complementary effects, enabling more comprehensive regulation of psoriatic synovial inflammation. Additionally, given the potential for an increased risk of infection with combined TNFα and IL-17A blockade, careful consideration is necessary when evaluating this combination therapy.

Our data show that TNFα suppression in the memory CCR6+Th17-driven PsA SF activation model can only be achieved with a TNFα inhibitor, as neither 1,25(OH)2D3 nor IL-17A inhibition was effective. Previous research from our lab revealed that 1,25(OH)2D3 had no effect on TNFα levels in a Th17-RA SF co-culture system.19 A clinical trial in IBD patients similarly found that while vitamin D supplementation significantly increased serum 25(OH)D levels, it did not reduce serum TNFα levels.33 Given the critical role of TNFα in PsA pathogenesis, controlling TNFα remains essential. Studies in Crohn’s disease, a condition also linked to the IL-23/IL-17 axis, suggest that vitamin D supplementation may enhance the effectiveness of TNFα inhibitors,23 34 encouraging further clinical investigation of this combination therapy in PsA.

A unique advantage of 1,25(OH)2D3 is its robust suppression of IL-22 and IFNγ, cytokines not adequately targeted by either anti-TNFα or anti-IL-17A therapies. IL-22, a pro-inflammatory cytokine co-regulated with IL-17, is significantly elevated in PsA synovial fluid, and its receptor (IL-22Rα1) is highly expressed on fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS), promoting FLS proliferation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production.35 IFNγ is another critical pro-inflammatory cytokine in PsA, synergising with IL-17 to amplify IL-6, IL-8, ICAM and NO production.36 In PsA synovial tissue, IFNγ+ polyfunctional T cells are increased and correlate with disease activity.37 Another cross-sectional study also found the frequency of circulating IFNγ producing CD8+T cells was significantly correlated to the number of swollen joints.38 The ability of 1,25(OH)2D3 to suppress IL-22 and IFNγ suggests potential benefits in controlling psoriatic synovitis.

IL-10 plays a multifaceted regulatory role in controlling inflammatory arthritis.39 However, in PsA, IL-10 producing regulatory cells were decreased and functionally impaired.40 Studies have shown that TNFα blockade promotes IL-10 production from IL-17+CD4+ T cells,41 while 1,25(OH)₂D₃ has a broader effect, both suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines and inducing IL-10.42 Our findings suggest that in a human in vitro PsA model, combining 1,25(OH)₂D₃ with anti-TNFα or anti-IL-17A not only reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines but also strongly enhances IL-10 production. Given IL-10’s role in inhibiting osteoclastogenesis and inflammasome activation,43 this increase could be beneficial for PsA treatment. Additionally, a novel finding is that IL-10 can also inhibit the trained immunity of monocytes, a key mechanism in autoimmune diseases like systemic lupus erythematosus.44 Whether IL-10 can similarly regulate innate immune cell activity in PsA requires further investigation.

None of the treatments, including anti-TNFα, anti-IL-17A, 1,25(OH)2D3 or combination, effectively suppressed IL-17F. IL-17F is coexpressed with IL-17A in disease-relevant cells in PsA patients and can independently drive pro-inflammatory responses.45 Dual neutralisation of IL-17A and IL-17F has proven more effective in controlling synovial inflammation compared with IL-17A blockade alone.45 46 However, IL-17F monotherapy did not impact pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-8) or chemokines (CXCL1, CXCL5, CCL20).46 The cytokine measuring methods applied in this study primarily detect IL-17F homodimers. The regulation of IL-17A/F heterodimers and the potential role of IL-17F as homodimer and heterodimer requires further investigation.

As indicated in our study, 1,25(OH)2D3 showed potential added value in controlling Th17-driven PsA SF activation in vitro, particularly in combination with a TNFα inhibitor. However, the precise mechanisms by which 1,25(OH)2D3 exerts its effects and the specific cellular targets involved remain unclear. Using the transwell system, we were able to separately evaluate the effects of various treatments on SFs and memory CCR6+Th17 cells while maintaining a homogenous environment. Our initial findings confirmed that physical interactions between Th17 cells and PsA SFs are essential for the development of the pro-inflammatory feedback loop.47 Nevertheless, a higher level of IL-10 in co-culture system indicates the importance of cell-cell interaction between Th17 cells and PsA SF to reach the full potential of this increase under these specific inhibitory conditions. Notably, 1,25(OH)2D3 was able to suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine production, but it had limited impact on MMPs.

There are also limitations of our study. While our in vitro results provide valuable insights into the effects of 1,25(OH)2D3 in Th17-driven PsA SF activation, the differences between in vitro and in vivo settings must be considered. First, we built this in vitro model by co-culturing PsA SFs and Th17 cells, effectively demonstrating the pro-inflammatory feedback loop between them. While SFs are the primary stromal cells in the synovium and memory T cells are the important source of pro-inflammatory cytokines, other types of immune cells, such as macrophages, endothelial cells, ILCs and other subsets of T cells can also play roles in the pathogenesis and need further investigation. Second, in this study, 1,25(OH)2D3 was used directly, whereas in vivo, vitamin D must undergo metabolic conversion to its active form to exert biological effects.48 This raises challenges in monitoring effective supplementation and bioavailability in tissues. Unlike its role in calcium homeostasis, the immunomodulatory effects of vitamin D are thought to depend on localised intracrine or paracrine conversion by immune cells, such as dendritic cells and macrophages, through 1α-hydroxylase.18 49 Additionally, genetic factors like VDR polymorphisms may influence vitamin D’s activity in PsA.50 These considerations highlight the complexity of translating in vitro findings into clinical application and underscore the need for further in vivo studies.

In conclusion, TNFα or IL-17A inhibition alone does not completely inhibit the proinflammatory loop between human memory CCR6+Th17 cells and PsA SF. The combined inhibition of TNFα and IL-17A exerted additive effects on suppressing proinflammatory and tissue-destructive mediators but remained insufficient, particularly in suppressing IFNγ and IL-22. Notably, the addition of 1,25(OH)2D3 to TNFα blockade provided a complementary immunomodulatory effect, disrupting a broader inflammatory circuit and significantly enhancing IL-10 production. These findings highlight a potential adjunctive role for active vitamin D alongside anti-TNFα in modulating pathogenic Th17 responses in PsA. Further investigation is warranted to elucidate its precise cellular targets and assess its clinical utility in the broader context of PsA management.
