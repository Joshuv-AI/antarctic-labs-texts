Orchestration of Skin Pathology in Cutaneous Lupus Erythematosus by HLA Class I Down‐Regulated Senescent Epidermal Basal Cells
Authors: Sena Yamamoto, Yuki Saito, Tsukasa Sato, Seina Nakano, Dain Kasseki, Ayaka Nagao, Norihiro Miura, Kentaro Nagaoka, Arisa Kita, Maki Miyajima, Shogo Ijima, Koji Taniguchi, Atsushi Niida, Takako S Chikenji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study investigates the role of senescent epidermal basal cells in cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE) pathogenesis using skin samples from patients with CLE and a mouse model of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).MethodsCellular senescence profiling used datasets from the NCBI GEO database and Accelerating Medicines Partnership (AMP) Phase 1‐Metro. Gene array data from GSE184989 (CLE, n = 68; control, n = 4), single‐cell RNA sequencing data from GSE186476 (CLE, n = 7; control, n = 14), and AMP Phase 1‐Metro (SLE, n = 17) were used. In vitro experiments further examined the expression of p21WAF1/CIP1, Type I interferon (IFN), HLA class I (HLA‐I), and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signaling. Pharmacological clearance of senescent cells was performed using the senolytic drug fisetin in the SLE mouse model.Resultsp21WAF1/CIP1‐high senescent epidermal basal cells in patients with CLE exhibit high Type I IFN expression. These cells enhance IFN signaling in surrounding normal epidermal basal cells, leading to increased HLA‐I expression. In contrast, senescent epidermal cells up‐regulate epidermal growth factor receptor signaling, which downregulates HLA‐I expression, allowing them to evade immune surveillance. This heterogeneity of HLA‐I expression promotes CD8‐positive T‐mediated toxicity against normal epidermal basal cells, resulting in their apoptosis. Pharmacological clearance of senescent epidermal basal cells improved SLE‐like skin lesions.ConclusionSenescent cells create a microenvironment that directs cytotoxic T cell‐mediated responses against normal epidermis in patients with CLE, contributing to disease pathology. Targeting senescent cells and their signaling pathways may offer novel therapeutic strategies for patients with skin lesions from CLE and SLE.

INTRODUCTION

Cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE) can occur as an isolated skin disease or as a manifestation of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). 1 In patients with SLE, skin involvement is observed in 70% to 80% of patients and is present in 20% to 25% of patients during the initial stages of the disease; 1 however, the treatment of patients with CLE remains challenging owing to the absence of US Federal Drug Administration‐approved therapies and the common occurrence of resistance to conventional treatments. 1 , 2 Recent research has focused on targeting immune cells, particularly T and B cells, or proinflammatory mediators, such as Type I interferon (IFN) and JAK/STAT signaling pathways. 1 Despite these efforts, evidence supporting most treatments for patients with CLE remains limited, with few therapies meeting the high evidence‐based medicine criteria. 2 Therefore, elucidating the pathologic mechanisms underlying CLE is crucial for the development and approval of new therapeutic targets.

Skin lesions in patients with CLE are characterized by an IFN‐mediated cytotoxic response against epidermal cells, which promotes the release of cell debris and activation of innate immune pathways. 1 , 3 This establishes in a proinflammatory amplification cycle. 1 A primary trigger, such as UV light, induces keratinocyte death, leading to the exposure of nuclear antigens on the cell surface, which are subsequently recognized by circulating autoantibodies. 4 Evidence suggests that these events might be mediated by cytotoxic T cells, playing a critical role in mediating immune responses against the epidermis. 5 Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that keratinocytes may perpetuate the lesion cycle through the production of Type I and III IFNs and IFN‐regulated proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines. In patients with established SLE, IFN activity is closely linked to cutaneous involvement and serves as a predictor of future flares and disease severity. 6 Historically, investigations into IFN production in patients with SLE have focused on plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs). 7 However, a recent preclinical study revealed that pDCs do not function as effector cells and are incapable of producing toll‐like receptor‐mediated cytokines or activating T cells, regardless of disease activity or circulating interferon levels. 8 , 9 This suggests that an alternative cell source may be responsible for the production of interferon. 8 Nonetheless, the precise mechanism underlying continuous IFN production remains elusive.

Cellular senescence, characterized by irreversible cell cycle arrest, is triggered by various stressors and leads to the secretion of inflammatory factors known as the senescence‐associated secretory phenotype (SASP). 10 Immunohistochemical analysis of skin lesions from patients with SLE has identified senescent epidermal basal cells, positive for p21WAF1/CIP1 and p16INK4A. 11 Senescent cells exhibit derepression of long interspersed nuclear element‐1 (LINE‐1) retrotransposable elements, resulting in the cytoplasmic accumulation of LINE‐1 complementary DNA and the activation of an IFN‐I response. 12 This response contributes to SASP and age‐associated inflammation, which can be mitigated by inhibiting LINE‐1 reverse transcriptase. 12 Although the involvement of cellular senescence in the pathogenesis of patients with CLE has been proposed, the role of senescent cells in the production of IFNs and their function in the proinflammatory amplifying cycle remain poorly understood.

To address these questions, we conducted gene array and single‐cell RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) analysis of skin tissues of patients with CLE. Our results show that CDKN1A (p21WAF1/CIP1)‐positive epidermal basal cells up‐regulate Type I IFN expression. Type I IFN from these senescent cells increases classical HLA class I (HLA‐I) expression in nonsenescent epidermal basal cells. In contrast, senescent basal cells reduce their HLA‐I expression through the epidermal growth factor (EGF) autocrine effect, avoiding CD8+ T cell recognition. Eliminating senescent epidermal cells in a mouse model with SLE improved skin lesions. These findings indicate that senescent epidermal basal cells promote the targeting of normal basal cells by cytotoxic T cells while avoiding destruction themselves, contributing to the pathogenesis in patients with CLE.

RESULTS

Cellular senescence‐associated factors are transcriptionally up‐regulated in skin tissues from patients with CLE

Gene expression data from patients with CLE were obtained from the GEO database (GSE184989). 13 To investigate cellular senescence in the skin tissue of patients with CLE, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), 14 focusing on cellular senescence‐associated gene sets (SenMayo) 15 and hallmark gene sets. 16 GSEA revealed significant enrichment of the SenMayo gene set in skin tissue from patients with CLE compared with that in normal skin tissue (Figure 1A). Further analysis of acute, subacute, and chronic discoid lupus erythematosus disease subtypes classified using CLE pathomorphology 13 showed significant enrichment of the SenMayo gene set in all subtypes (Figure 1A). The gene expression data also indicated significant enrichment of genes associated with the inflammatory response and those encoding components of the complement system (Supplementary Figure 1). We further observed significant up‐regulation of CDKN1A and CDKN2A, which are representative cellular senescence genes, in all types of skin tissues with CLE compared to normal skin (Figure 1B). Additionally, analysis of epidermal basal cells revealed significant downregulation of COL17A1, ASS1, and POSTN, epidermal stem cell factors abundant in the epidermal basal cells around the dermal papilla (Figure 1C), whereas other basal cell markers, including KRT14, PTTG1, CDC20, RRM2, GJB2, HELLS, UHRF1, PCLAF, KRT16, and KRT6A, did not exhibit significant differences in their expression (Figure 1C). These findings suggest that in the skin of patients with CLE there is an accumulation of senescent cells and a decrease in the proportion of epidermal stem cells.

Single‐cell analysis reveals CDKN1Ahigh epidermal basal cells up‐regulate Type I IFN and EGF signaling

To identify the cell types involved in cellular senescence in SLE skin lesions, we analyzed a single‐cell RNA‐seq dataset from the skin of patients with SLE (Accelerating Medicines Partnership Phase 1‐Metro) 17 and performed single‐sample GSEA (ssGSEA). 18 We extracted skin‐derived keratinocytes and identified epidermal basal cells expressing KRT5 and KRT14 (Supplementary Figure 2A and B). Dimensionality reduction using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) identified five clusters of epidermal basal cells (Figure 2A), including COL17A1‐high (COL17), JUN and CDKN1A‐high (JUN_CDKN1A), KRT6A and CDKN1A‐high (KRT6_CDKN1A), MKI67‐high (MKI67), and nonspecific (BAS) clusters (Figure 2A and B). The ssGSEA with a cellular senescence‐associated gene set confirmed that the JUN_CDKN1A cluster was highly enriched, identifying it as a senescent cell population, 15 suggesting that UMAP clustering identified senescent cells (SEN) in this study (Figure 2C). DecoupleR analysis showed increased p53, NF‐κB, and MAPK signaling pathways, along with vascular endothelial growth factor, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), transforming growth factor β, hypoxia, and androgen activity in the SEN cluster (Figure 2D). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of epidermal development in the COL17 cluster and viral processes and negative regulation of the cell cycle in the SEN cluster (Figure 2E and F and Supplementary Figure 3). GO enrichment analysis also showed that the SEN cluster had a significantly increased expression of the DNA damage response signature (Supplementary Figure 4), which contributes to senescence. 10 When investigating Type I IFN‐associated gene sets, we found their significant enrichment in the SEN cluster (Figure 2G). These results suggest that senescent epidermal basal cells in the skin of patients with SLE may contribute to Type I IFN production.

Senescent cells form a microenvironment in chronic inflammatory pathologies by communicating via the SASP. 10 Using CellChat, which analyzes intercellular communication networks from single‐cell RNA‐seq data, 19 we found that the SEN clusters had more active intercellular communication than nonsenescent clusters, with EGF signaling being the strongest secreted signal (Figure 3A). The SEN cluster showed the most enriched ligand expression for EGF signaling, with SEN, BAS2, BAS3, and COL17 clusters showing higher receptor expression (Figure 3B and C). Among EGF pathways, heparin‐binding EGF‐like growth factor (HBEGF)–EGFR and amphiregulin (AREG)–EGFR interactions were significantly increased in the SEN cluster, with HBEGF‐EGFR showing a stronger interaction (Figure 3D and E). Although the EGF signaling network comprising SEN, BAS2, and BAS3 showed increased interactions (Figure 3D), the signaling network showed the strongest interaction in the SEN cluster via autocrine signaling (Figure 3D).

To study the characteristics of senescent epidermal basal cells, we used ultraviolet B (UVB)‐irradiated human immortalized HaCaT keratinocytes. UVB exposure, a determinant factor in the onset and progression of CLE and SLE 1 induces cellular senescence through DNA damage. 10 UVB‐irradiated HaCaT cells showed increased SPiDER‐β Gal‐positive cells (Supplementary Figure 5A and B), indicating β‐galactosidase expression, a marker of senescent cells. 10 UVB‐irradiated HaCaT cells exhibited up‐regulation of CDKN1A and downregulation of COL17A1 (Supplementary Figure 5C), consistent with senescent epidermal basal cells. These cells showed increased expression of the EGF signaling ligands HBEGF and AREG (Supplementary Figure 5D), and up‐regulation of IRF5, IRF7, IFNA, and IFNA10 (Supplementary Figure 5E and F), which are members of the interferon regulatory transcription factor family involved in the innate immune response and SLE pathogenesis. 20 These results suggest that in SLE pathogenesis, EGF derived from Type I IFN‐producing CDKN1A‐high epidermal basal cells acts on these cells in an autocrine manner.

CDKN1Ahigh senescent epidermal basal cells modulate HLA‐I expression

Type I IFN induces long‐lasting HLA expression (I and II) crucial for the development and progression of SLE. 21 Next, we analyzed a single‐cell RNA‐seq dataset from skin lesions of patients with CLE and healthy controls (GSE186476). 22 This dataset, which includes both lesional and nonlesional skin regions from patients with CLE, was subjected to UMAP analysis, revealing 13 distinct cell clusters. Focusing on Type I IFN production, our analysis demonstrated a significantly elevated proportion of IFN‐I production‐high epidermal cells in both lesional and nonlesional skin compared to healthy controls; however, no significant difference was observed between lesional and nonlesional skin in patients with CLE. Given the increased IFN‐I signaling in lesional skin and our focus on CLE pathogenesis, subsequent analyses concentrated on lesional skin from patients with CLE and skin from healthy controls (Supplementary Figure 6A and B). First, we clustered cells derived from the skin using UMAP and identified epidermal basal cells that expressed KRT5, KRT14, and COL17A1 (Figure 4A). Epidermal basal cells from CLE skin lesions showed high senescence scores and Type I IFN‐associated gene sets compared with healthy controls (Figure 4B and C). These cells also showed an up‐regulation of HLA‐I (HLA‐A, HLA‐B, HLA‐C, and HLA‐E) (Figure 4D) and HLA‐II (HLA‐DRA, HLA‐DRB1, and HLA‐DQB1) (Supplementary Figure 7A). UMAP identified two clusters: CDKN1A‐low COL17A1‐high nonsenescent (NSEN) and CDKN1A‐high COL17A1‐low SEN (Figure 4E and F). The SEN cluster exhibited high senescence scores (Figure 4G) and decreased expression of classical HLA‐I molecules (HLA‐B and HLA‐C) and major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC‐I) antigen‐processing and presentation machinery (APM) molecules (β2 microglobulin [B2M]), 23 compared with the NSEN cluster (Figure 4H). In contrast, nonclassical HLA‐I molecules, HLA‐E were more abundant in the SEN cluster (Figure 4H). The expression of the HLA‐II molecules HLA‐DRA and HLA‐DQA1 was also up‐regulated in the SEN cluster compared to the NSEN cluster (Supplementary Figure 7B).

Next, we performed ssGSEA 18 for Type I IFN production, IFN response, and EGFR signaling, as EGF signaling is known to suppress HLA‐I expression upon IFN stimulation. 24 The SEN cluster showed higher enrichment scores for Type I IFN production but lower IFN response and higher EGFR signaling compared to the NSEN cluster (Figure 4I and J). EGFR was the predominant signal in the SEN cluster, whereas IFN α/β receptor (IFNAR) and Type I IFN responses were predominant in the NSEN cluster (Figure 4K and L).

Further classification of NSEN clusters based on IFN response levels revealed that the IFN response‐high cluster had elevated expression of HLA‐A, HLA‐B, HLA‐C, HLA‐E, and B2M (Figure 4M and N). Additionally, the IFN response‐high cluster had higher enrichment scores for MHC‐I‐mediated antigen processing and presentation, as well as immune system processes, but no difference in MHC‐II‐mediated antigen presentation (Figure 4O–Q). To elucidate the differences in cell–cell communication of CD8+ T cells with SEN and NSEN clusters, we performed NichNet analyses. 25 Supplementary Figure 8 shows the identified ligand‐receptor pairs between CD8+ T cells and COL17A1‐high nonsenescent or CDKN1A‐high senescent basal cells. The number of identified ligand‐receptor pairs between COL17A1‐high nonsenescent basal cells and CD8+ T cells was equivalent in both directions. Conversely, interactions between CDKN1A‐high senescent basal cells and CD8+ T cells were biased, with more pairs originating from senescent basal cells toward CD8+ T cells, suggesting a stronger influence of senescent cells on CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Figure 8A–F). These results indicate that nonsenescent epidermal basal cells with up‐regulated HLA‐I and APM may be targeted by CD8‐positive T cells in the skin of patients with CLE, whereas senescent epidermal basal cells may evade immune detection similar to tumor cells. 26

Influence of SASP factors on HLA‐I expression and immune evasion

The effect of the SASP on the microenvironment is mediated by autocrine and paracrine mechanisms; 10 therefore we speculated that SASP factors might contribute to differences in HLA‐I expression in senescent and nonsenescent cells. Testing SASP conditioned medium (CM) on epidermal and UVB‐irradiated senescent epidermal cells (Figure 5A) showed that UVB‐irradiated senescent HaCaT cells had significantly lower HLA‐I fluorescence intensity, whereas SASP CM‐treated nonsenescent HaCaT cells showed significantly higher fluorescence intensity (higher expression; Figure 5B and C). Gene expression analysis confirmed the downregulation of HLA‐I expression (HLA‐A, HLA‐B, HLA‐C, and HLA‐E; Figure 5D and E) and B2M (Supplementary Figure 9A) in UVB‐irradiated senescent HaCaT cells compared with SASP‐treated nonsenescent HaCaT cells. Likewise, UVB irradiation of primary normal human keratinocytes increased CDKN1A and HBEGF expression; however, HLA‐I expression (HLA‐A, HLA‐B, HLA‐C, and HLA‐E) was significantly increased (Supplementary Figure 10), unlike the single‐cell RNA‐seq data from patients with SLE and HaCaT cells.

Next, to explore the SASP‐mediated mechanism of the HLA expression and immunogenicity of epidermal basal cells, we tested the effect of neutralizing antibodies (Abs) for HBEGF and type 1 IFNs, identified in this study as key SASP factors derived from senescent epidermal basal cells. Neutralizing Abs for HBEGF and Type I IFNs demonstrated that HBEGF reduces HLA‐I levels in senescent cells autocrinely, whereas Type I IFN enhances HLA‐I expression in nonsenescent cells paracrinely (Figure 5D–G). This observation was corroborated by single‐cell RNA‐seq analysis, which identified EGFR as the predominant signaling pathway in the senescent epidermal basal cell cluster; in contrast, the IFNAR and Type I IFN signaling pathways dominant in the nonsenescent epidermal basal cell cluster (Figure 4K). To investigate whether HBEGF inhibition restores HLA expression by enhancing autocrine IFN‐I signaling or through an independent mechanism, we examined STAT1 signaling, a downstream component of the Type I IFN pathway. 27 In‐cell Western assays showed that HBEGF‐Abs did not inhibit STAT1 and pSTAT1 expression in either control or UVB‐irradiated senescent HaCaT cells. These results suggest that an HBEGF block restores HLA expression independently of autocrine IFN‐I effects (Supplementary Figure 11).

Co‐culture experiments demonstrated that the presence of UVB‐induced senescent cell or CD8+ T cells alone did not affect the viability of HaCaT cells; however, when CD8+ T cells were co‐cultured with senescent HaCaT cells or exposed to SASP factors, there was an increased the expression of activated caspase 3/7 compared to CD8‐positive T cells co‐cultured with nonsenescent HaCaT cells co‐cultured or CD8‐positive T cells alone (Figure 5H and I). Additionally, we confirmed that neither co‐culture with senescent HaCaT cells nor exposure to SASP factors affected the survival of CD8+ T cells, as indicated by unchanged expression levels of apoptosis‐associated genes CASP3, CASP8, and CASP9 (Supplementary Figure 9B).

Fisetin reduces p21WAF1 /CIP1 ‐positive senescent cells and ameliorates skin lesions in MRL/lpr mice

To assess the involvement of senescent cells in SLE pathogenesis, we investigated whether eliminating senescent cells ameliorated SLE skin lesions. We investigated the expression of p21WAF1/CIP1 and collagen17α1 (Col17) using immunohistochemical staining in 18‐week‐old MRL/lpr mice, a mouse model of SLE. Histologic analysis showed that MRL/lpr mice had significantly greater epidermal thickness and worse skin lesion scores than MRL/MpJ mice (Supplementary Figure 12A–C). The proportion of p21WAF1/CIP1‐positive epidermal cells was higher in MRL/lpr mice compared to MRL/MpJ mice (Supplementary Figure 12D and E), which also exhibited increased fluorescence intensity for p21WAF1/CIP1 and a decreased fluorescence intensity for Col17 (Supplementary Figure 12D and F). A 2.5‐dimensional diagram of the epidermal basal layer and fluorescence intensity histogram indicated low Col17 expression in p21WAF1/CIP1‐positive cells in MRL/lpr mice (Supplementary Figure 12G and H). Epidermal cells with loss of lamin B1 exhibited p21WAF1/CIP1 and 53BP1 expression (Supplementary Figure 12I), which are characteristics of senescent cells with DNA damage. 10 Additionally, MRL/lpr mice showed increased nuclear loss of high‐mobility group box protein 1 (HMGB1) (Supplementary Figure 12J and K), which is known to orchestrate stimulator of interferon genes (STING)‐mediated senescence. 28 MHC‐I expression in p21WAF1/CIP1‐positive cells from MRL/lpr mice was significantly decreased compared to that in p21WAF1/CIP1‐negative cells (Supplementary Figure 12L and M). MRL/lpr mice showed increased phospho‐EGFR (pEGFR) expression (Supplementary Figure 12N and O), and the levels of pEGFR expression were significantly correlated with the presence of p21WAF1/CIP1‐positive cells (Supplementary Figure 12P). Furthermore, MRL/lpr mice showed a higher proportion of immune cells, including CD4‐positive T cells, CD8α‐positive T cells, and F4/80‐positive macrophages in the dermal layer (Supplementary Figure 12Q and R). To elucidate the involvement of senescent cells in skin lesions of patients with SLE, we conducted in vivo experiments using the senolytic drug fisetin. 29 This drug inhibits NF‐κB and EGFR signaling pathways up‐regulated in senescent epidermal basal cells. 30 MRL/lpr and MRL/MpJ mice were treated with fisetin or 20% polyethylene glycol‐400 (vehicle) orally for five days each week for four weeks (Figure 6A). Before the in vivo experiment, the effect of fisetin treatment was validated in vitro using UVB‐irradiated senescent HaCaT cells, in which fisetin significantly reduced SPiDER‐β Gal fluorescence intensity (Supplementary Figure 13A).

Vehicle‐treated MRL/lpr mice exhibited a progressive worsening of skin scores, whereas fisetin‐treated MRL/lpr mice retained stable skin scores over time (Figure 6B and C). Histologic analysis revealed that fisetin treatment ameliorated epidermal thickening (Figure 6D and E). The proportion of p21WAF1/CIP1‐positive cells and the nuclear loss of HMGB1 in epidermal cells significantly decreased in fisetin‐treated MRL/lpr mice compared to vehicle‐treated MRL/lpr mice (Figure 6E–H and Supplementary Figure 13B and C). Fisetin treatment also reduced pEGFR and IFN‐α expression and the number of CD4‐positive T cells, CD8α‐positive T cells, and F4/80‐positive macrophages, which were increased in vehicle‐treated MRL/lpr mice (Figure 6I–L). These results indicate that p21WAF1/CIP1‐positive senescent epidermal basal cells are involved in the pathogenesis of skin lesions in patients with SLE.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we found that CDKN1A (p21WAF1/CIP1)‐positive senescent epidermal cells accumulate in the skin of patients with SLE, communicating with various types of cells, including normal epidermal cells, via SASP. Normal epidermal cells exhibit up‐regulated classical HLA‐I expression and APM via EGF and Type I IFN signaling, suggesting that they may be targeted and killed by CD8‐positive T cells, as in SLE skin lesions. CD8‐positive T cells, binding to classical HLA‐I, become cytotoxic, releasing granules like perforin and granzyme B, 31 inducing caspase‐driven apoptosis in target cells. This mechanism aligns with CD8‐positive cytotoxic T cells infiltration and granzyme B expression already identified in the skin lesions of patients with CLE, suggesting that these cells at the dermo‐epidermal junction contribute to keratinocyte apoptosis. 32

Senescent epidermal cells down‐regulate HLA‐I expression, likely due to EGF autocrine function. The EGF pathway reportedly suppresses IFN‐stimulated HLA‐I expression. 26 Our single‐cell RNA‐seq analysis data showed that senescent epidermal basal cells had increased Type I IFN production but did not respond to Type I IFN. This finding is supported by in vitro experiments where classical HLA‐I expression in senescent epidermal cells increased when HBEGF‐Abs were added. These results suggest that while senescent cells can evade immunity, they promote antigen presentation by normal epidermal cells, contributing to the immunogenicity of normal cells in SLE skin pathogenesis.

In our study, CDKN1A (p21WAF1/CIP1)‐positive senescent epidermal cells were present in both patients with SLE and a SLE mouse model, exhibiting decreased COL17 expression. p21WAF1/CIP1‐positive cells inhibit E2F transcriptional activity, persistently activating the retinoblastoma family and regulating cell proliferation by inhibiting cyclin‐dependent kinase 2. 10 Cellular senescence is often initiated by p16Ink4a or p21WAF1/CIP1. 10 Recent research shows that eliminating senescent cells with dysfunctional telomeres, specifically those expressing p21WAF1/CIP1 but not p16Ink4a, can reduce several proinflammatory factors associated with radiation‐induced SASP. 33 COL17, an epidermal stem cell marker and a Type II transmembrane protein, functionally connects the epidermis to the dermal interface and localizes cell adhesion molecules in the basement membrane. 34 The DNA damage response in stem cells can cause COL17 (COL17A1/BP180) proteolysis. 35 Therefore, DNA damage in patients with CLE may be responsible for the observed decrease in COL17 expression. 1 We also demonstrated that fisetin eliminated senescent epidermal cells in MRL/lpr mice and ameliorated skin lesions. 36 Senescent cells depend on senescent cell antiapoptotic pathways (SCAPs) for survival, 29 and senolytic drugs induce selective apoptosis in senescent cells by blocking SCAPs without affecting nonsenescent cells. 29 We selected fisetin as a senolytic drug because of its positive impact in patients with central nervous system lupus and lupus nephritis symptoms in a mouse model of SLE. 36

The accumulation of senescent cells in the epidermis and the amelioration of lesions by senolytic drugs suggest that cellular senescence is involved in SLE skin pathology. Single‐cell RNA‐seq of skin epidermal basal cells of patients with SLE and in vitro analysis of human keratinocytes suggest that CDKN1A‐high COL17A1‐low senescent epidermal basal cells may up‐regulate the expression of Type I IFN and EGF signaling ligands, already identified as SASP factors. 10 , 15 The up‐regulation of AREG, a ligand of EGFR, has also been found in the blood cells of patients with SLE. 37

Our single‐cell RNA‐seq data also showed that BAS2 and BAS3, epidermal basal cell clusters, have elevated scores for senescence‐associated gene set, although not as high as the scores of CDKN1A‐high clusters. BAS2 cluster highly expresses KRT16 and KRT6A. These Type I and II keratins regulate early inflammation and innate immunity in the skin. 38 KRT16 overexpression in basal cells leads to hyperproliferation, altered keratin filament networks, and decreased cell–cell adhesion, suggesting a role in keratinocyte activation. 39 Skin from Krt16 transgenic mice resembled skin with activated EGFR‐mediated signaling. 39 KRT16 expression is also increased in the skin of patients with discoid lupus erythematosus. 40 BAS3 highly expresses RRM2 and HELLS. RRM2 accumulation is promoted by the ATR–Chk1 pathway, stabilizing E2F1, which in turn provides deoxyribonucleotides (dNTPs), the building blocks for DNA synthesis, to prevent DNA damage and cell death. 41 RRM2 maintains dNTP pools during keratinocyte differentiation. As shown in Supplementary Figure 3, BAS3 was associated with keratinocyte and epidermal differentiation‐related GO terms. EGFR signaling inhibits E2F1‐induced apoptosis by activating retinoblastoma tumor suppressor. 42 BAS3 may differentiate and exhibit antiapoptotic characteristics in epidermal basal cells. BAS2 and BAS3 also had increased CDKN1A expression, but since the negative regulation of the cell cycle was enriched in the CDKN1A‐high cluster, this study focused only on the CDKN1A‐high cluster. However, EGF signaling in BAS2 and BAS3 may also affect SLE pathology, and further verification is required in future studies.

The characteristics of HaCaT cells and primary normal human keratinocytes differ. Similar to senescent HaCaT cells, UVB‐irradiated senescent human keratinocytes up‐regulated the expression of HBEGF, AREG, and Type I IFN‐related genes; however, HLA‐I expression was significantly increased in senescent human keratinocytes, but not in HaCaT cells. Although HaCaT has been used as a substitute model for human keratinocytes, biologic differences between HaCaT cells and primary human keratinocytes have been described. 43 Eight hours after UVB irradiation, primary human keratinocytes showed p21 and p16 protein expression, whereas p16 protein expression was undetectable in HaCaT cells. 44 The increase in cellular senescence markers in HaCaT cells appears to be relatively slow after stimulation, increasing the expression of p16, p21, and senescence‐associated β‐galactosidase one to five days after UVB irradiation. 45 , 46 It is unclear how these differences in UVB stimulation responses between HaCaT cells and primary human keratinocytes, but it is known that cellular senescence markers appear at different time points and change the SASP state. 12 These differences in senescence responses to stimuli may facilitate an understanding of the pathogenesis of SLE.

This study has several limitations that must be addressed. HLA‐E expression was more abundant in the SEN cluster. Senescent dermal fibroblasts express the nonclassical MHC molecule HLA‐E, which interacts with the inhibitory receptor natural killer cell receptor group 2 A on natural killer (NK) cells and highly differentiated CD8+ T cells, inhibiting immune responses against senescent cells. 47 HLA‐E levels are elevated in SEN clusters; thus, increased HLA‐E expression may inhibit the cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells or NK cells. NKT cells are the conserved MHC class I‐like molecule CD1d‐dependent, which are called invariant NKT cells (iNKT) or Type I NKT cells because there is an invariant T cell receptor α chain on the surface of the cells to recognize CD1d molecules, unlike conventional T cells. 48 Although NKT cells have a subtype of CD1d‐independent NKT cells, the involvement of CD1d‐dependent iNKT cells in the pathology of patients with SLE has been suggested. 48 In our single‐cell RNA‐seq analysis, CD1d gene expression was not determined in either the SEN or NSEN clusters; however, the relationship between NKT cells and senescent cells has not been verified.

This study did not identify the specific factors prompting cellular senescence. However, UVB irradiation induces senescence and activates EGFR signaling and interferons, suggesting it may function as a trigger for cellular senescence in skin lesions with patients with CLE. UVB exposure can induce DNA damage, leading to activation of the cGAS–STING pathway, a response observed in various cell types and not limited to SLE pathophysiology. 10 , 28 , 49 In patients with SLE, cellular senescence may also be triggered by fragmented nuclear DNA released into the extracellular space, providing another stimulus for the cGAS–STING pathway. 50 Because the cGAS–STING pathway induces cellular senescence, further investigation is needed to clarify SLE senescence triggers.

Although our study revealed that fisetin, which possesses senolytic properties, affects senescent epidermal basal cells, it cannot be ruled out that it may also affect senescent bone marrow mesenchymal cells. Therefore, future research should consider the systemic dynamics of senescent cells in patients with SLE.

In conclusion, our study showed that the accumulation of p21WAF1/CIP1 CDKN1A‐high senescent epidermal cells might contribute to the pathogenesis of SLE skin lesions in both human patients and mouse models. A schematic representation of these findings is provided in Supplementary Figure 14.
