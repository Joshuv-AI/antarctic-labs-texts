Temporary Blood Donor Deferrals: Analysing their impact on donor retention and future re-donations
Authors: Ambreen Kashif, Rana Hasanato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjectives:Temporary blood donor deferral can be a challenge in retaining donors and to maintain a stable blood supply. This study aimed to analyse the pattern of temporary donor deferral and its impact on their future return for donationMethods:This retrospective study included deferred blood donors from the Blood Bank department of King Khaled University Hospital, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, and included data from January 2021 till December 2023 with subsequent donors follow up till August 2025 were recorded. Effect of demographics, causes of temporary deferrals and gender wise distribution were compared between new and regular blood donors and their return for future re-donations.Results:A total of 47,522 whole blood donors were included in this study; 5,182 donors were deferred, of whom 97.3% experienced temporary deferral. Among these, 46.8% were new and 53.1% were regular donors. The leading causes of deferral were low haemoglobin/haematocrit (Hb/Hct; 45.9%), recent medication use (9.3%) and low blood pressure (8.8%). Donors deferred due to low Hb/Hct demonstrated lower return rates compared to those deferred for low blood pressure or medication use. Return rates among new donors ranged from 0–40%, whereas among regular donors it ranged from 37.5–100%, depending on the deferral category. Overall, the return rate was significantly lower in new donors and female donors (P <0.001 each).Conclusion:Temporary donor deferral has an effect on the future donations of the donor. This study highlights the need for stronger interventions, particularly donor education, reminder messages and follow-up to encourage the deferred donors to return, thereby supporting a consistent supply of safe blood for patients.Keywords: Blood Donation, Blood Donor, Donor Selection, Hemoglobin, Risk Factors, Follow-up Studies, Hypotension, Medication Review

1. Introduction

Transfusion medicine is a rapidly evolving field, and blood transfusion services represent a cornerstone of modern healthcare, supporting complex surgeries, organ transplantation, trauma care and chronic disease management. As clinical demands continue to surge, the requirement for blood and blood products also rises, emphasising the importance of proactive strategies to recruit and retain healthy donors.[1] Blood donation plays a crucial role in supporting healthcare services. Therefore, a sustained engagement of donors is essential to ensure a consistent and reliable blood supply. A comprehensive donor history, physical examination and laboratory tests are key to ensure a safe and adequate blood supply and are essential to maintain patient safety. Assessing donor deferral patterns within specific populations provides insight into regional characteristics that are important for guiding the development of effective national policies.[2,3]

Donor selection is the first critical step in this safety framework. Worldwide, there are different criteria for donor selection; individuals who do not fulfil the defined eligibility criteria are deferred, with varying deferral durations depending upon the underlying cause. These deferrals may be temporary or permanent, and their primary goal is to protect both donor health and recipient's safety.[4] Temporarily deferred donors represent a valuable reservoir of potential donors and their successful re-engagement plays a significant part in sustaining an ample donor inventory and blood supply.[5] Deferring a donor can be an uncomfortable experience for both the donor as well as blood centre and may contribute to shortages in the blood supply. Deferred donors often feel discouraged or uncertain about returning, which further impacts future donation rates. Understanding the factors that lead to donor deferral is therefore crucial, as it helps maintain donor satisfaction and strengthens their appreciation of the safety measures essential for blood and transfusion practices.

While there are diverse causes for temporary deferral of donors, the leading cause of short-term donor deferral is low haemoglobin/haematocrit (Hb/Hct).[6] There are regional variations in the primary causes of temporary donor deferrals with some studies identifying low Hb/Hct as the leading factor while others report low blood pressure (BP) as the most common reason for deferral.[7] A multi-centre study conducted in Saudi Arabia reported that the most common reasons for temporary rejection included low BP, underweight donors and low Hb/Hct. Less frequent causes included fever, recent tattoos and hijama. These causes increase the risk of exposure and transmission of transfusion transmitted infections.[8]

Numerous studies from different regions have demonstrated that temporary donor deferral can negatively impact a donor's likelihood of returning, with regular donors consistently showing higher return rates than first-time donors.[9,10] Temporary deferrals may discourage the donors, reduce their engagement and ultimately affect their long-term donation behaviour. To improve donor return and engagement, targeted interventions are essential. Supportive communication, timely follow-up, clear guidance and counselling of donors about cause of deferral as well as deferral period are key strategies to encourage the deferred donors to return and help in maintaining a sustainable blood supply. In light of this, this study aimed to analyse the patterns of temporary donor deferral, its impact on the future donations and the effect of demographic variables on donor deferral and their return.

2. Methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in the Blood Bank department of King Khaled University Hospital, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. All allogenic whole blood donors who were temporarily deferred base on pre-donation history and assessment at the blood donation centre between January 2021 and December 2023 were included; in addition, the subsequent donors return visits following deferral were reviewed for the period from January 2021 to August 2025 with a minimum follow-up period of 8 months or more for every donor deferred. Follow-up period was calculated after completion of their deferral period. Temporary deferral was defined as a donor who was not eligible to donate blood due to a condition or risk factor for a specific period of time. Indefinite deferral was defined as a donor who was not eligible to donate blood due to a condition or risk factor for an indefinite period of time/permanently. All information was retrieved from the HEMATOS blood bank information system. Donor deferral causes and deferral period was based on the donor deferral criteria by Association of Advancement of Blood & Biotherapies.[9] Demographic characteristics including age, gender, weight and ethnicity were also recorded; records with incomplete demographic data were excluded. Deferred donors were classified into two categories: (1) new donors who presented for their first donation at the blood donation centre and (2) regular donors with a history of at least 1 previous donation. Donor return was defined as the presentation of a deferred donor for blood donation after the completion of the prescribed deferral period, regardless of whether the donor was subsequently found eligible and accepted for donation.

The lower cut-off for Hb/Hct for males was 13.5g/dL and 39%; for females, the cut-off was 12.7 g/dL and 38%. The upper threshold for Hb for both genders was >18.0 g/dL. Upper and lower cut-off for BP were systolic 90–180 mmHg and diastolic 60–100 mmHg. Weight limit for both genders was ≥50 kg. Age limit for the selected donors was 18–70 years for both genders. Data was stratified based on the specific deferral causes including low or high Hb/Hct, low or high BP, low body weight and donor left due to second thought/could not wait. Other causes included a history of recent medication/infection/other medical illness, vaccination, hijama/tattoo/piercing, recent travel to a malaria endemic area, major/minor surgical procedure, dental procedure, poor venous access and other rare causes.

Deferral period for these categories varied widely (range: 1 day to 1 year). Some deferrals extended up to 1 year due to travel to malaria-endemic areas or hijama/recent tattoo or piercing (from unregistered facility) or major surgery. However, these longer deferrals (1 year) were calculated from the date of the risk exposure rather than the donation visit. Therefore, in most cases, the actual deferral period was less than 1 year. Individual deferral period was calculated for each donor based on the date of the deferral and the date when the deferral ended and follow-up for donor return was started after the end of their deferral period.

Influence of these deferral causes on subsequent donor return was evaluated by comparing new and regular donors. Age and weight were summarised using means and standard deviations; gender and ethnicity were reported as frequencies. Prevalence and pattern of donor return were compared based on genders, ethnicity and donor type to identify the factors affecting donor return after temporary deferral.

Data were analysed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software, Version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). Categorical variables were reported as percentages and frequencies. Descriptive statistics were applied on quantitative variables and were summarised as means and standard deviations. Chi square/Fisher's exact test were applied to derive associations between different categorical variables, where appropriate. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to predict the donors return based on their demographic data and deferral causes. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). P <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

A total of 47,522 whole blood donors were screened during the study period. Among these donors, 5,182 (10.9%) were deferred following pre-donation history review, clinical assessment and examination. There were 5,045 (97.3%) pre-donation temporary deferred donors and 137 (2.7%) indefinitely deferred donors. Within the temporary deferral cohort, 2,363 (46.8%) were new donors and 2,682 (53.2%) were regular donors [Fig. 1]. A total of 2,303 temporarily deferred donors (45.6%) returned for a subsequent donation attempt during the follow-up period.

Flowchart of the allogenic blood donors included in this study.

Analysis by ethnicity showed that Saudis constituted the majority of temporarily deferred donors (86.6%). The next most common nationalities were Yemeni (2.2%) and Sudanese (1.7%), followed by smaller proportions of Indian, Egyptian, Syrian, Ugandan and other expatriate groups. A total of 74.1% of the deferred donor population were 18–40 years, 25.5% of donors were 41–65 years and 0.4% of donors were >65 years old. Among the deferred donors, 66.6% (n = 3,362) were male and 33.4% (n = 1,683) were female [Table 1]. The mean age of deferred female donors was 31.2 ± 10.5 years and a mean body weight of 71.7 ± 12.2 kg. The deferred male donors had a mean age of 34.7 ± 11.7 years and a mean body weight of 79.7 ± 12.7 kg.

The most frequent cause of temporary donor rejection was low Hb/Hct (45.9%). Other common causes included antibiotic or prescription medication use (9.3%), low blood pressure (8.8%), second thoughts/could not wait (6.1%) and elevated blood pressure (5.5%). Additional causes were recent infections (4.3%), hijama/tattoo/piercing (4.0%), recent surgery/transfusion (2.9%), being underweight (2.9%), elevated Hb (2.4%), recent vaccination (2.1%), travel to malaria-endemic regions (1.8%) and recent dental procedures (1.8%). Less frequent causes included antiplatelet/anticoagulant use (0.8%), other medical conditions under treatment (0.5%), intravenous drug use or unsafe sexual practices (0.4%), poor venous access (0.3%), exceeding donation limits or <8 weeks since the last donation (0.2%) and pregnancy or lactation (0.1%) [Fig. 2].

The causes of donor deferral were further analysed by gender. The most common 3 reasons for deferral among females in descending order were low Hb/Hct (61.3%), low BP (9.7%) and antibiotic/medication intake (5.5%). In male donors, the 3 leading causes of deferral were low Hb/Hct (38.2%), antibiotic/medication intake (11.1%) and low BP (8.4%). However, more females were deferred based on low Hb/Hct, low BP and low body weight compared to males. Deferral causes that were more prevalent in males compared to females were recent antibiotic/medication intake (11.1%), second thought/could not wait (6.8%), increased BP (6.5%) and increased Hb/Hct (3.5%) [Fig. 3]. The comparative analysis performed to evaluate gender differences in post-deferral return behaviour, stratified by donor type (new donors versus regular donors), demonstrated that male donors showed a significantly higher likelihood of returning compared to female donors (72% versus 28%; P <0.001).

Analysis of donor return patterns among new donors and regular donors demonstrated a consistently higher likelihood of return in the RD group across all major deferral categories, for example, low Hb, antibiotics/prescription medication use, recent hijama/tattoo, low blood pressure and hypertension. Overall, the return rate among regular donors was 68 % compared with only 20.3% in the new donor group [Table 2].

A multivariate regression analysis revealed a significant association of gender, donor type (regular versus new donor), ethnicity and different causes of temporary deferral. Female donors were less likely to return compared to males (OR = 0.672, 95% CI: 0.584–0.773; P <0.001). Regular donors had significantly higher odds of return compared to new donors (OR = 7.807, 95% CI: 6.819–8.937; P < 0.001). Saudis were less likely to return compared to non-Saudis (OR = 0.751, 95% CI: 0.620–0.911; P = 0.004). Donors deferred due to infection (OR = 1.603, 95% CI: 1.158–2.219; P = 0.004) and low blood pressure (OR = 1.285, 95% CI: 1.017–1.634; P = 0.036) were more likely to return than those with low Hb. However, donors deferred due to change of mind, hijama and other less frequent causes were less likely to return to donate. High blood pressure and medication appeared significant in univariate analysis but did not maintain the statistical significance in the multivariate model [Table 3].

4. Discussion

This study analysed the impact of temporary donor deferral on donor return. The results of this analysis showed that different causes of deferral lead to different likelihoods of donors returning to donate blood following the expiration of their deferral. In addition, specific donor demographic characteristics were strongly associated with the likelihood of returning during the follow-up period.

Pre-donation deferral continues to be an important challenge in donor retention and their future donation behaviour. In the current study, the overall pre-donation deferral rate was 10.9%, which corresponds to findings from Saudi Arabia, where deferrals accounted for 11.7% of the prospective donors as well as other Asian countries such as China (9.26%) and India (12.6%).[6,12,13] These regional similarities indicate that temporary deferrals represent a substantial barrier to maintaining an adequate donor pool across diverse healthcare systems.

This study found that low Hb/Hct were the most frequent cause of donor deferral in both males and females. This is comparable with the findings from a multi-centre study in the USA, where low Hb/Hct was the leading cause of temporary donor deferral.[14] However, a study from Dharan, Saudi Arabia, reported low Hb as the second most frequent cause while a history of recent medication intake was the most common cause.[15] The second most frequent cause of temporary deferral was the use of antibiotics or medications while low BP was found to be the third commonest cause. In contrast, a study from Nigeria identified low Hb/Hct, high BP and low body weight (<50 kg) as the 3 leading causes of temporary deferral.[16] Furthermore, a single-centre study from Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, found that the most common cause of deferral was failed vital signs (low or high BP), followed by low Hb/Hct.[17] These variations in regional studies highlight the differences in population health, nutritional patterns and medication practices. The difference in the most common deferral causes among the Saudi population may be attributed to differences in donor recruitment strategies or pre-screening criteria. These findings emphasise the need to tailor the donor management strategies according to local demographic and epidemiological characteristics of donors.

Common causes of female donor deferral included low Hb/Hct, low BP and medication use. These findings are partially consistent with a study from Turkey, where low Hb, common cold/fever and low body weight were the leading causes of deferral among female donors.[18] Among male donors, the main causes of deferral in the current study were low Hb/Hct, medication use and low BP. In contrast, a study from Malaysia reported upper respiratory tract infection, high BP and low Hb as the most frequent causes of deferral among males.[19] These regional differences may be explained by variations in donor recruitment strategies and differences in pre-donation screening criteria and haematological thresholds.

The pattern of higher proportion of female donors deferred due to low Hb/Hct compared to male donors is consistent with other studies. This observation aligns with a Croatian study, which reported deferral rates of 16.4% for males and 36.5% for females due to low Hb/Hct levels.[20] Such gender-based differences may reflect underlying biological, nutritional and physiological factors.

Temporary donor deferral in the current study had a negative effect on subsequent donor return, predominantly among new donors and female donors. New donors deferred due to low Hb/Hct had a low return rate compared to regular donors. Similar findings were reported in a study conducted on an Australian population where donor return after deferral due to low Hb was 20.9% for first-time donors and 64% for repeat donors.[10] The current study's observation is also supported by the conclusions of Clement et al., who noted that temporary deferral disproportionately affects new donors and reduces their likelihood of returning for future donations.[4] Regular donors, on the other hand, demonstrated a significantly higher probability of returning after a temporary deferral. This finding aligns with the work of Custer et al., who reported that first-time donors are less expected to return after experiencing a temporary deferral.[21] A study conducted in Iran similarly reported a noticeable decline in donor return rates following deferral, with the effect particularly pronounced among women, individuals with lower educational levels and first-time donors.[22] Conversely, another Iranian study demonstrated higher return rates among regular donors, male gender, older individuals and rhesus-negative donors.[23]

These observations highlight the importance of targeted interventions including donor education, individualised counselling and structured follow-up programmes to improve the donor retention, especially among new donors and female donors who are more vulnerable to disengagement after a rejection experience. Specifically, a simple intervention pathway for donors with low Hb/Hct could include iron counselling with dietary advice at the time of deferral, followed by a scheduled Hb recheck (e.g., 4–8 weeks) and reminder messages and phone calls once donors become eligible again. Such targeted interventions can address modifiable causes of deferral, enhance donor confidence and improve re-engagement in blood donation.

This is a single center study within a specific region, which may limit the external validity and generalisability of the findings to other donor populations and practical settings. In addition, the retrospective design relied on the completeness and accuracy of existing records, and important factors influencing donor return, such as donor perceptions, educational status and satisfaction with counseling, were not assessed. Future multicentre prospective studies are recommended to better understand barriers to donor return and to evaluate interventions, such as enhanced counseling and reminder systems, to improve donor retention following deferral.

5. Conclusion

Donor recruitment and long-term retention remain major challenges for blood donation centers, and temporary deferral can significantly reduce the likelihood of donor return. This study demonstrated that temporary deferral had a substantial negative impact on subsequent donor visits, particularly among new donors, female donors, and those deferred because of low hemoglobin. These findings highlight the need for targeted retention strategies focused on these donor groups. Interventions such as clear communication, brief counseling, and automated reminder messages may help address barriers to return and improve donor retention following temporary deferral.
