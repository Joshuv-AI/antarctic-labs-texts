YTHDF2 alleviates the radioresistance of rectal cancer cells by targeting methylated MYC
Authors: Yeying Fang, Shijie Shang, Gang Chen, Dawei Chen, Jinming Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractYTHDF2, a N6-methyladenosine (m6A) recognition protein, is involved in the occurrence and progression of various malignancies. The impact of YTHDF2 on the radiosensitivity of rectal cancer cells remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the effect and potential mechanisms of YTHDF2 on radiotherapy sensitivity in rectal cancer. Acquired radioresistant colorectal cancer (CRC) cell lines (HCT-116-R and CX-1-R) were established through accumulative X-ray exposure. YTHDF2 was exogenously overexpressed or endogenously knocked down using lentivirus systems, and the radiosensitivity of the cells was analyzed both in vitro and in vivo. High-throughput transcriptome sequencing identified MYC as a downstream target of YTHDF2. RNA stability assays revealed that YTHDF2 facilitated the decay of MYC messenger (mRNA) through an m6A-dependent mechanism. Western blot analyses demonstrated that YTHDF2 modulated MYC expression and the Hippo signaling pathway, enhancing p-MST1/2, p-LATS1 and p-YAP levels while reducing nuclear YAP. Functional assays showed that YTHDF2 overexpression improved radiosensitivity by promoting radiation-induced apoptosis and G2/M phase arrest. MYC overexpression reversed these effects, suggesting a competitive regulatory relationship between YTHDF2 and MYC. These findings indicate that YTHDF2 modulates radiosensitivity through MYC and the Hippo signaling pathway. YTHDF2 enhances the radiosensitivity of rectal cancer cells by facilitating the degradation of MYC mRNA and activating the Hippo signaling pathway. Targeting YTHDF2 may provide a promising therapeutic strategy for overcoming radioresistance in rectal carcinoma.Keywords: YTHDF2, rectal cancer, radioresistance, methylation, MYC, Hippo signaling pathway

INTRODUCTION

Colorectal cancer is the most prevalent type of gastrointestinal cancer and is the third most common cancer worldwide, with rectal cancer accounting for about one-third of all cases [1]. Radiotherapy, a medical procedure that uses radiation to kill tumor cells, plays a pivotal role in the treatment of rectal carcinoma. The standard treatment approach for patients with locally advanced rectal carcinoma is a combination of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, followed by total mesorectal excision and adjuvant chemotherapy [2]. However, the response of individuals to radiation varies greatly; only one-third of patients respond completely to neoadjuvant radiation, even in the era of precise radiation and intense treatment regimens [3]. The mechanism by which cancer cells escape the biological effects of radiation is complex. Uncovering this mechanism could help to improve the outcomes of radiation treatment in rectal carcinoma.

Among various regulatory mechanisms, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification—a dynamic and reversible post-transcriptional modification—has gained attention. This modification is orchestrated by three main classes of enzymes: methyltransferases, demethylases and RNA-recognition proteins. YTHDF2 is the first identified m6A recognition protein. It can selectively bind the m6A core sequence of the RNA molecules, transfer RNA molecules with the m6A modification into the lysate body of the cytoplasm and then recruit the deadenylase complex to degrade various methylated RNA molecules [4]. It has been reported that YTHDF2 participates in the initiation and progression of various malignancies and affects the prognosis [5]. As the functional executor of methylation regulation, the role of YTHDF2 in the sensitivity of rectal cancer cells to radiotherapy remains unclear.

In the radiotherapy of rectal cancer, MYC, as a classical oncogenic transcription factor, is closely associated with cell proliferation and survival [6, 7]. Previous studies have demonstrated that MYC participates in the regulation of radioresistance through various pathways [8]. However, its specific role in the m6A modification mechanism remains unclear. Given the potential regulatory relationship between MYC and YTHDF2, it is crucial to explore the interaction mechanism between the two in radiotherapy sensitivity.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the regulatory role of YTHDF2 in the radiosensitivity of rectal cancer and to elucidate its molecular mechanism in regulating MYC and the Hippo signaling pathway through m6A modification. The findings are expected to provide novel insights and potential therapeutic targets for overcoming radioresistance in rectal cancer.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell lines and grouping

This study used the HCT-116 and CX-1 colorectal cancer cell lines, along with their radiation-resistant derivatives, HCT-116-R and CX-1-R, established through long-term cumulative X-ray exposure [9]. Briefly, human CX-1 and HCT-116 parental cells were conventionally cultured and received repetitive fractional radiation, with an accumulated dose of up to 80 Gy within 4 months. The following experimental groups were established: ShNC group (HCT-116 cells transfected with control shRNA lentivirus), Sh69 group (HCT-116 cells transfected with YTHDF2 shRNA lentivirus), OE-NC group (HCT-116 cells transfected with control lentivirus), OE-Y2 group (HCT-116 and CX-1 cells transfected with recombinant YTHDF2 lentivirus), OE-MYC group (HCT-116 cells transfected with recombinant MYC lentivirus) and OE-Y2 + MYC group (HCT-116 cells co-transfected with recombinant YTHDF2 and MYC lentiviruses). For HCT-116-R cells, the same groups were established: ShNC, Sh69, OE-NC, OE-Y2, OE-MYC and OE-Y2 + MYC groups.

YTHDF2 expression alteration with lentivirus

The recombinant YTHDF2 (Genebank accession number: NM_016258) lentivirus, MYC (Genebank accession number: NM_001354870) and short hairpin RNA lentivirus were both designed and constructed by Genechem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The GV341 vector was used to elicit the overexpression of YTHDF2. For YTHDF2 knockdown, three target sequences were initially designed, then 5,-AGGCTCCTGGCATGAATACTA-3, and 5,-TAGCGGGTCCATTACTAGTAA-3, which were capable of knocking down >80% of YDHDF2 expression, were selected and spliced into the GV112 vector. After sequencing verification, the lentivirus was packed following the standard procedure and cryopreserved at −80°C. On the day before transfection, 1 × 105 cells were seeded into the six-well plate. After infection in serum-free medium for 12 h, cells were further cultured in a complete medium for 48 h. Puromycin (Solarbio, Beijing) was employed for positive screening at a concentration of 4 μg/L, and the surviving cells were used in the subsequent experiments. The stable overexpression or knockdown of YTHDF2 was confirmed by both polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and western blot.

High-throughput transcript sequencing and m6A sequencing

The Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA) was used to harvest total cellular RNA according to the manufacturer’s conventional procedure. After measuring the quality and quantity, >200 μg of eligible total RNA was subjected to isolate Poly (A) mRNA with poly-T oligo attached magnetic beads (Invitrogen). The poly(A) mRNA fractions were fragmented into ~100 nt-long oligonucleotides using divalent cations under elevated temperature. Then, the cleaved RNA fragments were incubated for 2 h at 4°C with m6A-specific antibody (No. 202003, Synaptic Systems, Germany) in IP buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 750 mM NaCl and 0.5% Igepal CA-630). The mixture was then incubated with protein-A beads and eluted with elution buffer (1 × IP buffer and 6.7 mM m6A). After precipitation with 75% ethanol, eluted m6A-containing fragments (IP) and untreated input control fragments were converted to a final complementary DNA library utilizing a strand-specific library preparation method based on dUTP. The average insert size for the paired-end libraries was ~100 ± 50 bp. The paired-end 2 × 150 bp sequencing was conducted on an Illumina Novaseq™ 6000 platform at LC-BIO Bio-tech Ltd (Hangzhou, China). For sequencing, the unqualified reads were first removed using Cutadapt [10] and Perl scripts, and fastp was used for quality assurance. HISAT2 was used to match reads to the genome of Homo sapiens (Version: v96) with default settings [11]. Mapped reads of IP and input libraries were dealt using the R package exomePeak [12], which identifies m6A peaks in the bed or bam format, which can be adapted for integrative genomics viewer (IGV) visualization software (http://www.igv.org/). MEME [13] and HOMER [14] were used to find motifs and the location of the enrichment region, and ChIPseeker was employed to annotate the identified peaks based on the intersection with gene architecture [15]. The level of mRNA was calculated with the fragments per kilobase million (FPKM) method using StringTie software [16]. The differentially expressed mRNAs were selected using the edge R package, with the threshold of the absolute value of log2 (fold change) >1 and a P-value <0.05 [17].

Real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction

A total of 10 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (TAKARA, Dalian, China). Approximately 0.4 μl of cDNA template was subjected to two-step polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using a TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ (TAKARA) with gene-specific primers on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The relative RNA expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method, with the levels normalized to the internal reference glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). Western blot

Culture cells were lysed in RIPA Lysis Buffer (Beyotime, Nanjing, China, P0013B) supplemented with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonylfluoride, and the protein concentration was measured using a standard bicinchoninic acid assay. Normalized lysates (~40 μg) were separated by electrophoresis in a prewired 10% ~ 15% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (Gsebio, Xian, China) and then transferred onto the 8 μm polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Millipore, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked in the Tween Tris-buffered saline (TTBS) with 5% milk at room temperature for 1 h and then incubated with primary antibodies on an oscillating shaker in a moist incubator overnight. The membranes were subsequently washed three times using the TTBS buffer for 8 min. Secondary antibodies diluted at a suitable dilution ratio were then incubated with the membranes for 45 min at room temperature. After the membranes were washed with the TTBS three times for 8 min, the blots were visualized on a Gel Imager System (Biorad, CA, USA) by chemiluminescence using the Immobilon electrochemiluminescence (ECL) Ultra Western horseradish peroxidase (HRP) Substrate (Millipore, MA, USA).

Clonogenic cell survival assay

Cells infected with the indicated lentivirus were seeded in triplicate into six-well plates and exposed to gradient doses (2–8 Gy) of X-ray radiation, followed by 24-h incubation. Cells infected with the indicated lentivirus were seeded in triplicate into six-well plates and exposed to gradient doses (2–8 Gy) of X-ray radiation, followed by a 24-h incubation. The cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline and fixed with methyl alcohol at room temperature. Then, they were stained with a 0.1% crystal violet staining solution (Biosharp, China), and the colonies comprising >50 cells were counted, reflecting the colony-forming ability.

Cell cycle and apoptosis detection

For cell cycle detection, an equal quantity of CRC cells, HCT-116 cells and HCT-116-R cells were scraped from the culture plates 24 h after radiation and fixed with 70% ice-cold ethanol at −20°C for 72 h. Cell pellets were washed with iced phosphate buffer saline (PBS) three times and then cocultured with 0.25 mg/ml RNase A (Solarbio, China) and 50 μg/ml propidium iodide (PI) for 45 min at 37°C in the dark. Samples were analyzed by flow cytometry (CytoFlex, BD Biosciences, USA), and the data were analyzed using ModFit 5.1 software. To analyze apoptosis, cells were collected with EDTA− trypsin, washed twice with iced PBS and double-stained with 5 μl of Annexin V-FITC and 5 μL of PI (Annexin V, FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit, BD, Germany) in 500 μl of binding buffer for 15 min at 37°C in darkness. Pretreated cell samples were subjected to flow cytometry using the typical parameters, and the results were analyzed using FlowJo 10.0 software. The Annexin-V+/PI− cells were in the early phase of the apoptotic process; Annexin-V+/PI+ indicated late apoptosis. The percentages of both groups of cells were computed, and each experiment was repeated in triplicate.

RNA immunoprecipitation

RIP (RNA immunoprecipitation) assays were conducted using the Magna RIP RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Millipore, 17-700) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 10 × 106 cells were lysed with IP lysis buffer, and 10% volume was set as the input. The cell lysates were incubated with magnetic beads coated with 5 μg of YTHDF2 antibodies overnight at 4°C. After IP, the phenol/chloroform method was used to isolate RNA from the input and IP fractions. Reverse transcription and quantitative real-time PCR were performed to determine the mRNA abundance, as described above. The enrichment of target genes associated with YTHDF2 was normalized based on the baseline input abundance.

RNA stability measure

Cells were seeded in six-well plates, and YTHDF2 knockdown or overexpression was achieved by lentivirus infection for over 48 h. The cells were treated with 5 μg/ml Actinomycin D (MedChemExpress) for 0, 1, 2 and 4 h, respectively. At the indicated time points, cells were harvested, total RNA was isolated and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed to measure the relative expression levels of target mRNAs.

Xenograft model

Five-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were purchased from the Model Animal Research Center of Nanjing University (Nanjing, China). HCT-116, HCT-116-R, CX-1 and CX-1-R cells transfected with empty vector, YTHDF2 overexpression or YTHDF knockdown lentivirus were trypsinized into a single-cell suspension. A total of 2 × 107 cells were injected into the axilla of each nude mouse. The inoculation position was 2 mm lateral and 2 mm posterior to the anterior fontanel. Tumor growth was monitored twice a week beginning in the third week and calculated as follows: V = length×width2/2. Then, the tumors were exposed to a single 8 Gy X-ray radiation using an RS2000 X-ray linear accelerator (Precision X-Ray, Madison, USA) on Day 14, when the masses were palpable. The mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation on Day 42, and the tumors were harvested, weighed and then embedded in neutral paraffin for further assays. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Shandong University Cancer Center (Approval Number: SDTHEC2024001011).

Immunohistochemical staining

The paraffin-embedded tissues were cut into 4 μM sections and deparaffinized following the conventional procedure. After hydration and antigen retrieval, the sections were blocked with 5% fetal bovine serum. The sections were then incubated with the antibodies for YTHDF (Proteintech, China), Ki-67 (TA800648, ORIGENE, Beijing) and MYC (Proteintech) at the optimal dilution ratio overnight at 4°C. After washing with PBS three times, the sections were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:300, ZB-5301, ORIGENE) for 45 min at room temperature. The stained slices were washed three times in PBS and reacted with the DAB chromogenic kit (ZLI-9018, ORIGENE). The immunoreactive score was calculated by multiplying the values of the positive percentage and the staining intensity of cancer cells.

Statistics analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compiled using. SPSS 24.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL). A one-way analysis of variance was used to determine. statistical and significant differences between the groups. In this study, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

The establishment of the acquired radioresistant CRC cell lines

To identify genes involved in radioresistance, radioresistant CRC cell lines were established by cumulative doses of X-ray radiation. The radiosensitivity of the radioresistant cells was compared with that of the parental cells at 48 h after 4 Gy X-ray exposure. The acquired radioresistant cells exhibited resistance to radiation compared with the parental cell lines. In detail, the proliferation ability of the cells after radiation was enhanced (Fig. 1A), while the apoptosis rate (Fig. 1B) and G2/M phase arrest (Fig. 1C) were both decreased. The acquired radioresistant cells were, namely, HCT-116-R and CX-1-R, respectively.

(A) Plate clone formation experiment. (B) Annexin V/PI double-staining apoptosis detection. (C) Cell cycle distribution detection. **P < 0.01 compared with CX-1 group and HCT-116 group.

YTHDF2 was down-regulated in acquired radioresistant cells

The transcript profiles of the acquired radioresistant cells and their corresponding parental cells were analyzed, and the differences were visualized using heat maps. In the radioresistant HCT-116-R cells, 47 genes showed downregulated expression compared to the parental cells (Fig. 2A), while in CX-1-R cells, 61 genes were downregulated (Fig. 2B). Among these, HSPA8, PYCR1, MORF4L2 and YTHDF2 were consistently downregulated in both HCT-116-R and CX-1-R cells. Further analysis confirmed that YTHDF2 expression was reduced at both the mRNA and protein levels in both radioresistant cell lines (Fig. 2C and D). Since the change in radiosensitivity of HCT-116 cells was more significant, and transcriptome analysis showed substantial differences between the radioresistant cells (HCT-116-R) and the parental cells, HCT-116 and its corresponding radioresistant cells (HCT-116-R) were selected as the models for subsequent phenotypic experiments.

YTHDF2 facilitated HCT-116 cell response to radiation

Since the expression of YTHDF2 was negatively related to the radiosensitivity of CRC cells, we knocked down YTHDF2 and performed clone formation assay, cell cycle detection and apoptosis assay to explore its impact on radiosensitivity. Among three candidate short hairpin RNA lentivirus vectors, Sh69 exhibited the most powerful knockdown effect, both at the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3A). The colony-forming ability of HCT-116 cells increased after the knockdown of YTHDF2 at radiation doses of 2 Gy and 4 Gy (P < 0.05) (Fig. S1A). The apoptosis rate of HCT-116 cells declined (Fig. S1B), while the G2/M phase arrest of HCT-116 increased (Fig. S1C). After YTHDF2 knockdown, the in vivo growth of HCT-116 cells remained sustainable, even at a radiation exposure of 8 Gy (Fig. 3B and C).

(A) YTHDF2 knockdown efficiency in HCT-116 cells was confirmed at both the mRNA and protein levels using qRT-PCR and western blot. (B) Growth curve of xenograft tumors derived from HCT-116 Sh69 and ShNC cells (n = X per group). (C) Representative tumor images from mice injected with HCT-116 Sh69 or ShNC cells. (D) Western blot showing overexpression of YTHDF2 in HCT-116 and CX-1 cells following lentiviral transduction. In contrast, YTHDF2 overexpression led to an inverse effect. Infection of YTDHF2-recombinant lentivirus led to successful exogenous overexpression of YTHDF2 in both HCT-116 and CX-1 cells (Fig. 3D). YTHDF2 overexpression rendered HCT-116 cells more responsive to radiation at the doses of 2 Gy and 4 Gy (P < 0.05) (Fig. S1D). The upregulation of YTHDF2 had no influence on the intrinsic apoptosis of HCT-116 cells but facilitated apoptosis at 48 h after 4Gy radiation exposure (Fig. S1E). In addition, the overexpression of YTHDF2 affected the cell cycle distribution of HCT-116 cells. The G2/M arrest increased from 25.98 ± 3.31 to 41.195 ± 2.01 (Fig. S1F). The upregulation of YTHDF2 also positively impacted the in vivo radiosensitivity of HCT-116 cells, as can be seen in the volume growth curve in Fig. 3E. Tumor volumes in the YTHDF2-overexpressed group were negligible, while the parental cells sustainably increased (Fig. 3F).

YTHDF2 alleviates acquired radioresistance in CRC cells

We investigated the effect of YTHDF2 expression on the radiation sensitivity of acquired radiation-resistant CRC cells. Upon infection with YTHDF2-carrying lentivirus, the expression level of YTHDF2 was significantly upregulated in HCT-116-R cells (Fig. 4A). Consistent with the results in parental cells, YTHDF2 overexpression significantly enhanced the radiation sensitivity of HCT-116-R cells, as evidenced by a marked decrease in clonogenic survival (Fig. S2A). Furthermore, YTHDF2 overexpression induced a higher rate of radiation-induced apoptosis in resistant cells (Fig. S2B) and increased G2/M phase arrest, similar to the trend observed in parental cells (from 17.25 ± 0.13% to 24.00 ± 2.00%, Fig. S2C). in vivo, although HCT-116-R cells exhibited notable tumor growth after radiation exposure, YTHDF2 overexpression significantly suppressed tumor volume growth (Fig. 4B–C). In contrast, YTHDF2 knockdown significantly enhanced radioresistance in HCT-116-R cells both in vitro and in vivo (Fig. S3). These results indicate that YTHDF2 is a key regulator of radiation sensitivity in acquired radiation-resistant CRC cells.

YTHDF2 regulates MYC expression and affects radiation sensitivity in HCT-116 and HCT-116-R xenograft tumors

To explore the role of MYC in radiation-resistant CRC cells and its relationship with YTHDF2, western blot analysis revealed that MYC protein expression was significantly higher in HCT-116-R cells compared to HCT-116 cells (P < 0.01, Fig. 5A). The regulatory effect of YTHDF2 on MYC expression was validated by experiments involving YTHDF2 knockdown and overexpression. In both HCT-116 and HCT-116-R cells, YTHDF2 knockdown significantly upregulated MYC protein levels, whereas YTHDF2 overexpression markedly reduced MYC protein expression (Fig. 5B and C). These findings suggest that YTHDF2 negatively regulates MYC expression, thus influencing the radiation sensitivity of CRC cells with acquired resistance.

In vivo experiments further demonstrated a negative correlation between YTHDF2 and MYC. When YTHDF2 was knocked down, the staining intensity of Ki-67 significantly increased, indicating enhanced cell proliferation. Conversely, when YTHDF2 was overexpressed, Ki-67 staining intensity was markedly reduced (Fig. 5D). Additionally, MYC protein levels were inversely correlated with YTHDF2, further supporting the hypothesis that YTHDF2 modulates radiation sensitivity in CRC cells by suppressing MYC expression.

YTHDF2 modulates radiation sensitivity by regulating MYC expression, apoptosis and cell cycle arrest

To investigate the mechanism by which YTHDF2 enhances radiation sensitivity, clonogenic survival assays demonstrated that the colony-forming ability of HCT-116 and HCT-116-R cells significantly decreased with increasing radiation doses (Fig. 6A and B). In HCT-116 cells, the colony-forming ability was significantly reduced in the OE-Y2 group compared to the OE-NC group, while MYC overexpression (OE-MYC group) significantly reversed this reduction (P < 0.01). The colony-forming ability in the OE-Y2 + MYC group was intermediate between the OE-Y2 and OE-MYC groups. A similar trend was observed in HCT-116-R cells, indicating that YTHDF2 overexpression significantly reduced radioresistance, while MYC overexpression partially reversed this effect.

Additionally, flow cytometry apoptosis analysis showed that YTHDF2 significantly promoted radiation-induced apoptosis (Fig. 6C and D). In both HCT-116 and HCT-116-R cells, the apoptosis rate in the OE-Y2 group was significantly higher than in the OE-NC group, while the OE-MYC group exhibited a significantly lower apoptosis rate than the OE-NC group (P < 0.01). The apoptosis rate in the OE-Y2 + MYC group was significantly lower than in the OE-Y2 group but higher than in the OE-MYC group (P < 0.01). Cell cycle analysis revealed that YTHDF2 significantly enhanced radiation sensitivity by inducing G2/M phase arrest (Fig. 6E and F). In both HCT-116 and HCT-116-R cells, the proportion of cells in the G2/M phase was significantly higher in the OE-Y2 group than in the OE-NC group, while it was significantly lower in the OE-MYC group (P < 0.01). The proportion of G2/M phase cells in the OE-Y2 + MYC group was significantly lower than in the OE-Y2 group but higher than in the OE-MYC group (P < 0.01).

YTHDF2 facilitated the decay of MYC

To further explore the mechanism through which YTHDF2 enhanced the radiosensitivity of CRC, high-throughput sequencing was performed. Based on the transcriptome sequencing, 618 transcripts were upregulated while 556 transcripts were downregulated (Fig. 7A). The 29 transcripts that were both downregulated and enriched with the m6A motif were regarded as the downstream targets of YTHDF2 (Fig. 7B). Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) verified that the expression levels of MYC was negatively regulated by YTHDF2 (Fig. 7C). RIP showed that MYC mRNA was captured by YTHDF2 antibody in both HCT-116 cells (Fig. 7D) and HCT-116-R cells (Fig. 7E). The RNA stability measure indicated that YTHDF2 overexpression accelerated the decay of MYC mRNA, while YTHDF2 knockdown delayed the process of MYC mRNA degradation (Fig. 7F).

YTHDF2 regulates signal transduction in HCT-116 and HCT-116-R cells by modulating the Hippo signaling pathway

Western blot analysis revealed that knockdown of YTHDF2 (sh69 group) significantly reduced the phosphorylation levels of MST1/2, LATS1 and YAP in HCT-116 cells, while the total expression levels of MST1, LATS1 and YAP remained unchanged. In contrast, the nuclear expression level of YAP was significantly increased in the sh69 group (Fig. 8A–C). Conversely, overexpression of YTHDF2 (OE-Y2 group) significantly increased the phosphorylation levels of MST1/2, LATS1 and YAP, while markedly reducing nuclear YAP expression (P < 0.01). No significant differences were observed in these indicators between the control groups (shNC and OE-NC). In HCT-116-R cells, YTHDF2 knockdown also led to a significant reduction in the phosphorylation levels of MST1/2, LATS1 and YAP (P < 0.01), without changes in total protein levels of MST1, LATS1 and YAP. Additionally, nuclear YAP expression was significantly increased in the sh69 group (Fig. 8B and D). Overexpression of YTHDF2 (OE-Y2 group) restored the phosphorylation levels of MST1/2, LATS1 and YAP and significantly reduced nuclear YAP expression (P < 0.01). No significant differences were observed between the shNC and OE-NC groups. These results indicate that YTHDF2 plays a key regulatory role in HCT-116 and HCT-116-R cells by activating the Hippo signaling pathway (increasing MST1/2, LATS1 and YAP phosphorylation) and inhibiting nuclear translocation of YAP. Knockdown of YTHDF2 suppresses Hippo signaling, while its overexpression enhances the pathway’s activity.

YTHDF2 influences MYC-mediated effects by regulating the activity of the Hippo signaling pathway

To investigate whether YTHDF2 affects the function of MYC through the regulation of the Hippo signaling pathway, we analyzed the expression levels of key proteins in the Hippo pathway and the nuclear localization of YAP in HCT-116 and HCT-116-R cells. Western blot analysis showed that overexpression of YTHDF2 (OE-Y2 group) significantly increased the levels of p-MST1/2, p-LATS1 and p-YAP in both HCT-116 and HCT-116-R cells (P < 0.01, Fig. 9A and B), while simultaneously reducing the nuclear levels of YAP (P < 0.01, Fig. 9C and D). Conversely, overexpression of MYC (OE-MYC group) markedly reduced p-MST1/2, p-LATS1 and p-YAP levels, while increasing nuclear YAP levels (P < 0.01). Further experiments revealed that in the OE-Y2 + MYC group, where both YTHDF2 and MYC were overexpressed, the levels of p-MST1/2, p-LATS1 and p-YAP were significantly lower than those in the OE-Y2 group but higher than those in the OE-MYC group (P < 0.01). Similarly, the nuclear YAP levels in the OE-Y2 + MYC group were intermediate between those observed in the OE-Y2 and OE-MYC groups (P < 0.01). These findings collectively suggest that YTHDF2 exerts its function by activating the Hippo signaling pathway and reducing nuclear YAP levels. However, overexpression of MYC can partially counteract these effects. This indicates a potential competitive regulatory mechanism between YTHDF2 and MYC in modulating the Hippo signaling pathway, thereby jointly influencing cellular behavior.

DISCUSSION

Radioresistance limits the outcomes of rectal carcinoma therapy and causes clinical concern. In this study, the acquired radioresistant CRC cells were established by means of accumulated radiation exposure, and the crucial genes involved in radioresistance were then screened out using high-throughput sequencing. YTHDF2, the first authenticated m6A reader, was found to be negatively related to the radiosensitivity of CRC cells. After the expression of YTHDF2 was exogenously upregulated or endogenously silenced, the radiosensitivity of the acquired radioresistant CRC cells and the corresponding parental cell lines was determined both in vivo and in vitro. Transcriptome sequencing and MERIP-seq indicated that MYC, whose transcript was enriched in the m6A motif, was negatively related to the expression of YTHDF2. RIP demonstrated that MYC directly combined with the YTHDF2 protein, while the RNA stability measure revealed that the decay of MYC mRNA was impacted by YTHDF2 alteration.

Radiation is an essential treatment strategy for CRC, especially in patients with locally advanced rectal carcinoma. Radiotherapy can not only increase the chance of complete resection but also vastly reduce the risk of postoperative recurrence [18]. However, radio resistance weakens the therapeutic effect and imposes extra physical and economic burdens. The response of tumor cells to radiation depends both on their intrinsic traits and the surrounding microenvironment. In turn, the alteration in gene expression profiles or epigenetic modifications during radiation affect radiation sensitivity. As the most abundant RNA modification, m6A is involved in various physiological processes, including spermatogenesis, embryonic development, fat metabolism, stem cell differentiation, immune response and tumor occurrence and development [19].

The role of m6A in radiosensitivity is gradually being recognized. It has been shown that methyltransferase enzyme METTL3-mediated m6A modification increases the cellular survival ratio after ionizing radiation, thus aggravating the radiosensitivity of glioma, pancreatic cancer and non-small-cell lung cancer cells [20–22]. Huang et al. demonstrated that m6A eraser fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) potentiates the radio resistance of nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) cells by inhibiting radiation-induced ferroptosis [23], while another demethylase, ALKBH5, reduces the radiosensitivity of hepatocellular carcinoma by reversing the modification of TIRAP mRNÀ [24]. As the executor of various m6A modifications, methylated recognition proteins also make biological sense in radiation therapy. YTHDC2 facilitates the radioresistance of NPC cells by activating the IGF1R/ATK/S6 signaling axis [25], while YTHDF3 mediates cervical cancer radioresistance by promoting m6A-dependent RAD51 translation [26]. Thus, the effect of m6A in regulating the course of radiation may vary based on the specific disease or pathological pattern. For rectal cancer, a malignancy with a high incidence and broad radiotherapy indications, the role of m6A modification has been less studied. Our findings revealed that YTHDF2 promoted the response of CRC cells to radiation, similar to the findings of a previous study [27]. In a study conducted by Shao et al., YTHDF2 was observed to enhance the radiosensitivity of cell lines HCT-116 and HT-29. In our study, we generated an acquired radioresistant cell model through repeated radiation exposure and further selected YTHDF2 as the candidate gene with the help of high-throughput sequencing. The upregulation of YTHDF2 in HCT-116-R and CX-1-R cells implies its participation in the development of acquired radioresistance. The alteration of YTHDF2 affected both primary and radiation-triggered radioresistance in both acquired radioresistant cells and the corresponding parental cells, suggesting the potential of YTHDF2 as a radiosensitizer. As the most well-known reading protein, YTHDF2 has been shown to be involved in the occurrence and development of various tumors, while its role is discrepant in different malignancies [28]. One possible reason for this phenomenon is that YTHDF2 performs different functions at different stages of disease progress. Considering the crucial role of radiation in CRC treatment, understanding the role of YTHDF2 in radiosensitivity regulation is of great clinical significance. Although YTHDF2 knockdown enhanced in vivo tumor growth of HCT-116 cells (Fig. 3), this did not correlate with increased radiosensitivity. While it is generally true that highly proliferative tumors are more radiosensitive due to their increased susceptibility to DNA damage, radiosensitivity is a multifactorial trait that also depends on apoptosis regulation, DNA repair capacity and cell cycle dynamics [8, 29]. In our study, YTHDF2 knockdown reduced radiation-induced apoptosis and attenuated G2/M phase arrest, key processes that contribute to radiation response. Moreover, YTHDF2 modulates radiosensitivity via destabilizing m6A-modified MYC mRNA and regulating the Hippo-YAP pathway, mechanisms that exert strong effects independent of proliferative rate. Thus, the observed enhancement in tumor growth coexists with reduced radiosensitivity due to the dysregulation of post-transcriptional and signaling networks orchestrated by YTHDF2. Notably, although MYC was identified as a downstream target of YTHDF2 and was functionally validated to be negatively regulated by YTHDF2 through m6A-mediated mRNA degradation, it was not listed among the top upregulated genes in the radioresistant clones depicted in Fig. 2. This discrepancy may be attributable to the selection criteria for the heatmap visualization, which focused only on the most statistically significant differentially expressed transcripts with consistent downregulation (e.g. HSPA8, PYCR1, MORF4L2 and YTHDF2) in both HCT-116-R and CX-1-R cells. Given that MYC may exhibit moderate but biologically meaningful changes, or that its upregulation might be context- or time-dependent in the radiation response, it may not have passed the statistical threshold for inclusion in Fig. 2A and B. Nevertheless, our subsequent mechanistic studies (Figs 5–7) clearly demonstrate that MYC protein levels are elevated in radioresistant cells and are inversely regulated by YTHDF2. These results underscore the importance of integrating transcriptomic screening with targeted validation when dissecting complex regulatory pathways such as m6A-mediated control of radiation response.

MYC, a classical transcription factor and oncoprotein, is frequently dysregulated in human cancers and participates in numerous tumorigenesis biological activities, driving cancer progression [30]. MYC interacts with various proteins and dominates various processes, including promoter binding, epigenetic modifications, initiation and elongation, as well as post-transcription modification. It has been shown that the expression of MYC in CRC is regulated by the Wnt signaling pathway [31], mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway [32], various microRNAs [33] and long-chain non-coding RNAs [34], thus affecting CRC apoptosis [35], proliferation [36], migration [37] and chemoresistance [38]. The role of MYC in radiotherapy has also been reported but is less well understood. MYC regulates the glutamine metabolism of prostate cancer (PCa), thus rendering cancerous cells resistant to radiation, and high expression of MYC is significantly associated with unfavorable progression-free survival in PCa patients undergoing radiotherapy [39]. Cytolethal-distending toxin-mediated MYC downregulation sensitizes PCa cells to radiation by attenuating the radiation-induced autophagy pathway [40]. It was also reported that MYC reverses the radiosensitizing effect of NS1-binding protein in esophageal squamous cancer [41]. The interplay between LINC00958 and MYC could form a positive feedback loop, which also facilitates squamous cell carcinoma of head and neck resistance to radiotherapy [42]. Our study demonstrates that YTHDF2 negatively regulates MYC expression in CRC cells. YTHDF2 reduces the stability of MYC by promoting the degradation of its m6A-modified transcripts. Functional studies revealed that YTHDF2-mediated MYC downregulation enhances radiation-induced apoptosis and G2/M phase arrest, thereby reducing CRC radioresistance. Conversely, overexpression of MYC partially reverses the radiosensitizing effects of YTHDF2. These findings suggest a potential competitive relationship between YTHDF2 and MYC in regulating CRC radiosensitivity.

Furthermore, we investigated the role of YTHDF2 in modulating the Hippo signaling pathway, which plays a crucial role in regulating cell proliferation, apoptosis and tissue homeostasis. When the Hippo pathway is activated, its core kinases MST1/2 phosphorylate and activate LATS1/2, which, in turn, phosphorylate the transcriptional coactivator YAP [43]. Phosphorylated YAP is retained in the cytoplasm and degraded, thereby suppressing pro-proliferative and anti-apoptotic gene expression. Conversely, inactive Hippo signaling leads to nuclear translocation of unphosphorylated YAP, promoting tumor growth and therapy resistance. Our results showed that overexpression of YTHDF2 increased the phosphorylation levels of MST1/2, LATS1 and YAP, while significantly reducing nuclear YAP expression, indicating activation of the Hippo pathway. In contrast, YTHDF2 knockdown suppressed Hippo pathway activity and promoted YAP nuclear localization. Moreover, overexpression of MYC partially counteracted YTHDF2-induced Hippo pathway activation, suggesting that MYC negatively regulates Hippo signaling. These findings support a model in which YTHDF2 enhances radiosensitivity by repressing MYC and thereby restoring Hippo pathway activity, revealing a competitive regulatory mechanism between YTHDF2 and MYC in controlling CRC cell behavior.

The clinical significance and limitations of this study warrant further exploration. Our findings suggest that YTHDF2 could be a potential therapeutic target for overcoming CRC radioresistance. By targeting m6A-modified MYC and regulating the Hippo signaling pathway, YTHDF2 significantly enhances radiation-induced apoptosis and G2/M phase arrest. However, several limitations remain. First, although high-throughput sequencing identified 29 potential targets of YTHDF2, their direct interactions require further validation through rescue experiments. Second, whether the effects mediated by YTHDF2 are entirely m6A-dependent needs deeper investigation, such as functional gain or loss experiments focusing on the YTH domain of the YTHDF2 protein. Finally, the potential impact of YTHDF2 on the prognosis of rectal cancer patients receiving radiotherapy should be further verified through clinical cohort studies.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that YTHDF2 enhances CRC cell radiosensitivity by promoting the decay of m6A-modified MYC mRNA, reducing MYC stability and modulating apoptosis and cell cycle progression. YTHDF2 also activates the Hippo signaling pathway, increasing MST1/2, LATS1 and YAP phosphorylation while reducing nuclear YAP levels, further contributing to radiosensitization. Importantly, MYC overexpression partially reverses these effects, suggesting a competitive regulatory mechanism. These findings highlight YTHDF2 as a potential radiosensitizer and therapeutic target for overcoming radioresistance in rectal carcinoma. Future studies should investigate YTHDF2’s broader mechanisms and clinical applicability in radiotherapy.
