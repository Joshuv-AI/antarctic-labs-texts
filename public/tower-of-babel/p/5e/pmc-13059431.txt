Positive psychology approaches to alleviating foreign language classroom anxiety among university students: insights from self-efficacy, flow, and self-compassion
Authors: Fan Jia, Yiyan Li, Zhuofan Yang, Shujun Wang, Yanhui Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent research in second language acquisition (SLA) has increasingly applied positive psychology to improve learning outcomes and reduce foreign language classroom anxiety (FLCA). However, the field still lacks an integrative framework that captures how cognitive appraisals and affective resources jointly shape the emotional processes that lead to reduced FLCA. Guided by Control-Value Theory and Broaden-and-Build Theory, this study examines the roles of self-efficacy, flow experience, and self-compassion in shaping FLCA among English as a foreign language (EFL) learners.MethodsA total of 1,352 EFL learners from multiple universities in China completed a cross-sectional survey. Structural equation modeling was used to test the mediating role of flow experience in the relationship between self-efficacy and FLCA, as well as the moderating role of self-compassion in the link between self-efficacy and flow experience.ResultsSelf-efficacy showed a significant adverse effect on FLCA, and flow experience mediated this association. Furthermore, self-compassion moderated the relationship between self-efficacy and flow experience, with the association being weaker among learners with lower self-compassion.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that fostering self-efficacy, promoting flow, and nurturing self-compassion may help reduce classroom anxiety and enhance learning outcomes, thereby contributing to more supportive language-learning environments.Keywords: Foreign language classroom anxiety (FLCA), Self-efficacy, Flow, Self-compassion, Control-value theory, Broaden-and-build theory

Introduction

Early research on foreign language classroom anxiety (FLCA)—defined as the nervousness or tension learners experience during foreign language learning and communication—primarily investigated its adverse impacts on learners [1, 2]. These studies underscored how anxiety could disrupt the language acquisition process and lead to poorer academic performance. In response to these detrimental effects, researchers have increasingly turned to positive psychology in recent years. Rather than focusing solely on reducing negative emotions, this approach emphasizes the cultivation of positive psychological resources—adaptive personal traits and emotional states that support human flourishing and optimal functioning [3]. Specifically, this positive psychology framework emphasizes that activating personal strengths and fostering positive emotional experiences can not only buffer the impact of anxiety but also enhance language-learning outcomes [4, 5]. This shift has opened new avenues for exploring how psychological factors contribute to more emotionally supportive and practical language-learning experiences.

Self-efficacy, defined as an individual’s belief in their capacity to accomplish specific tasks [6], is a central construct in positive psychology and has gained increasing attention in second language acquisition (SLA). Self-efficacy has been shown to predict learning-related emotions and metacognitive strategies, which in turn mediate academic performance [7]. In English language learners, self-efficacy beliefs have been found to foster greater academic enthusiasm and vitality [8]. These benefits have prompted scholars to explore further the role of self-efficacy in managing negative affective factors in language learning, such as its potential to alleviate emotional barriers, including FLCA. Indeed, research shows that learners with higher self-efficacy approach tasks with greater confidence, leading to reduced anxiety and improved outcomes [9–11]. Although extensive research has documented the significance of self-efficacy, work exploring its relationships with other psychological constructs—particularly in the context of foreign language learning—remains comparatively limited. Among existing works, recent scholarship has shifted from merely establishing such relationships to examining the mechanisms underlying them, although most studies have tended to focus on individual pathways or specific associations rather than on multiple processes together. The present study responds to this trend by addressing the need for a more integrated perspective to understand how these mechanisms interconnect in the language-learning context.

Another construct receiving growing attention in SLA research is flow—a deeply engaging and intrinsically rewarding state associated with heightened concentration, enjoyment, and optimal performance [12, 13]. Flow has been linked to greater willingness to communicate, reduced anxiety, and increased persistence in language learning [14, 15]. These positive outcomes underscore the importance of educators’ understanding of which factors can effectively promote flow experiences in their students. Among the factors contributing to flow, self-efficacy has proved to be a promising antecedent. Longitudinal studies suggest that learners with stronger self-efficacy beliefs are more likely to experience flow over time [16]. However, the precise relationship between the two remains theoretically underexplored. Clarifying this gap is essential for designing pedagogical interventions that simultaneously reduce anxiety and promote learner engagement.

Language learning is an emotionally demanding process that often involves challenges such as errors, setbacks, and self-doubt, which can trigger negative emotions like frustration and anxiety, ultimately hindering learners’ progress and engagement [3, 17]. One construct that may help learners navigate such emotional challenges is self-compassion, defined as treating oneself with kindness and understanding when facing difficulties [18]. Self-compassion helps learners manage self-critical thoughts and facilitates emotional recovery, enabling them to acknowledge their imperfections, interpret mistakes as a natural part of the learning process, and ultimately enhance learning outcomes [19]. While previous research has primarily explored the role of self-compassion in supporting teachers [20, 21], its potential benefits for language learners remain underexplored. Given the emotional challenges inherent in SLA, investigating the influence of self-compassion on language learners is both timely and essential.

To address these gaps, the present study proposes an integrated model grounded in Control-Value Theory (CVT) [22] and the Broaden-and-Build Theory (BBT) [23]. According to CVT, learners’ achievement emotions stem from their perceived control over learning tasks and the value they assign to those tasks. Recent evidence from second language writing contexts has supported this view, showing that task-specific control and value appraisals predict emotions such as enjoyment and anxiety [24]. Self-efficacy can be seen as a key factor that strengthens learners’ control appraisals, thereby fostering positive emotions such as flow. In turn, self-compassion acts as a moderator by helping learners regulate emotional distress and self-critical thoughts when control perceptions are low, facilitating entry into the flow state. BBT complements this framework by suggesting that positive emotions, such as flow, broaden learners’ cognitive and behavioral repertoires, thereby reducing anxiety and supporting long-term psychological resilience [25, 26]. By integrating CVT and BBT, the present study proposes a theoretically grounded framework that elucidates how a cognitive appraisal process (self-efficacy) initiates a positive experiential state (flow), which in turn contributes to the reduction of negative emotional experiences (FLCA), with self-compassion moderating the transition from cognitive appraisal to positive emotion. This integration delineates a sequential affective–cognitive pathway that offers a process-oriented account of how these constructs interconnect dynamically. In doing so, it extends beyond the predominantly correlational evidence reported in previous research.

Specifically, this study aims to examine: (1) whether self-efficacy is associated with FLCA; (2) whether flow mediates the relationship between self-efficacy and FLCA; and (3) whether self-compassion moderates this mediated pathway. From a theoretical standpoint, this study contributes to the positive psychology turn in SLA research by elaborating on the complex interplay among cognitive, emotional, and attitudinal factors that shape learners’ psychological states during foreign language learning [27]. By scrutinizing how self-efficacy, flow, and self-compassion interact through their distinct yet interrelated components, the study delineates the functional roles of these three key affective constructs and situates them within a coherent developmental sequence relevant to language-learning contexts. In doing so, it refines the conceptual boundaries and illuminates how they jointly contribute to learners’ broader affective architecture, thereby extending existing theories by specifying the conditions under which positive psychological resources interact to influence affective dynamics in SLA. In practice, it provides actionable insights for EFL pedagogy by emphasizing the need to cultivate both self-efficacy and self-compassion in learners, thereby fostering emotionally supportive classrooms that enhance engagement, reduce anxiety, and promote sustainable language development.

Literature review

Foreign language classroom anxiety: definition, components, and impact

FLCA was first defined as “a distinct complex of self-perceptions, beliefs, feelings, and behaviors related to classroom language learning arising from the uniqueness of the language learning process” [1]. At its core, FLCA is an emotional phenomenon characterized by nervousness and fear in the context of SLA. Horwitz et al. [1] identified three distinct types of anxiety: communication apprehension, test anxiety, and fear of negative evaluation. Communication apprehension is characterized by concern or worry about interactions with others; test anxiety refers to a fear of failure, often accompanied by unrealistic self-expectations; and fear of negative evaluation involves apprehension regarding others’ assessments, avoidance of evaluative contexts, and anticipation of negative judgments. While moderate FLCA may, in some cases, enhance student focus and effort, it is generally found to harm L2 learners’ emotions and performance, posing a significant challenge to foreign language education [28]. Specifically, high levels of FLCA can lead to paralysis and muteness, impede concentration, hinder the assimilation of new knowledge [2], and negatively affect language test performance [29, 30]. Furthermore, compared to other emotions associated with SLA (e.g., boredom), FLCA has a more pronounced adverse effect on academic achievement [31, 32]. As a result, applied linguists have emphasized the urgent need for effective strategies to mitigate the adverse effects of FLCA.

Self-efficacy and FLCA: a negative relationship

Self-efficacy, a concept within Bandura’s social cognitive theory, refers to individuals’ beliefs in their ability to achieve specific performance goals, which, in turn, affects their motivation and persistence in tasks [6, 33]. Individuals with high self-efficacy typically possess greater confidence in their capacity to control outcomes and confront challenges, a trait consistently linked to academic success, particularly college GPA [6, 34]. Similarly, in the EFL context, self-efficacy is a crucial determinant of successful learning outcomes. It enables students to remain engaged and consistent over a longer period, despite the turmoil and difficulties of language learning [35]. Furthermore, individuals with a high level of self-efficacy typically exhibit a substantial commitment to learning, possess confidence in their language acquisition skills, and experience predominantly positive emotions (enjoyment, pride) while encountering few negative emotions (anger, anxiety, shame) [11].

Several studies have demonstrated a negative correlation between self-efficacy and FLCA (e.g. [36–38]), . Notably, this correlation is robust and consistent across various factors, including school type, gender, and national income [39]. One study identified self-efficacy as a key mediator between classroom anxiety, learning motivation, and English achievement, underscoring its critical role in managing classroom emotions and enhancing learning experiences [40]. Similarly, Gao and Zuo [41] described a cyclical dynamic in which heightened anxiety undermines self-efficacy, leading to poorer academic performance and diminished motivation, which in turn further impairs learners’ ability to meet educational demands and intensifies their anxiety. Taken together, these findings suggest a reciprocal relationship between self-efficacy and FLCA, where low self-efficacy both contributes to and is reinforced by higher levels of anxiety.

Flow: a mediating pathway from self-efficacy to reduced FLCA

Flow, as defined by Csikszentmihalyi, is a deeply enjoyable psychological state that occurs when individuals are fully immersed in an activity that matches their skills [12, 42, 43]. It is characterized by nine key elements: (1) clear goals, (2) immediate feedback, (3) a balance between skills and challenges, (4) merging of action and awareness, (5) focused concentration, (6) a sense of control, (7) loss of self-consciousness, (8) altered perception of time, and (9) an autotelic experience. Among these, the balance between skills and challenges is crucial, as it drives individuals to perform at the edge of their abilities, leading to the highest levels of engagement and fulfillment [12]. Previous research has indicated that flow often occurs in L2 classrooms, where it tends to boost learner engagement and support the achievement of favorable learning outcomes [44–46]. Moreover, early flow experiences in L2 learning tend to be brief and self-focused, particularly when learners feel insecure or lack confidence within the group [15]. However, as learners’ proficiency improves, flow becomes a more frequent and intense experience, contributing to deeper and more sustained engagement in the learning process [15].

A growing body of research has documented a positive association between self-efficacy and flow across diverse domains, including SLA (e.g. [47–49]), . In the context of EFL, Gong and Xu [50] examined both the independent and interactive effects of academic self-efficacy and teacher support on learners’ flow experiences, highlighting their synergistic role in fostering deeper engagement. Furthermore, a longitudinal study provided evidence that self-efficacy consistently serves as a robust predictor of flow over time, while flow influences self-efficacy only intermittently [16]. These findings highlight the pivotal role of self-efficacy in facilitating flow experiences in SLA and underscore its importance as a foundational factor in sustaining learner engagement and motivation.

In addition to the connection between self-efficacy and flow, recent studies have explored the negative relationship between flow and FLCA. Characterized by the merging of action and awareness, flow has been found to alleviate anxiety and increase learners’ willingness to communicate in a foreign language [51]. Building on this, Almukhaild and King [52] demonstrated that fostering flow experiences enables anxious learners to engage more fully in language tasks, experience positive emotions, and momentarily suppress anxiety during task execution. These findings suggest that flow not only supports emotional regulation in foreign language learning but also fosters a psychologically optimal state that may help mitigate the adverse effects of FLCA.

Collectively, although the existing studies have demonstrated a close relationship between self-efficacy, flow, and FLCA, the mediating role of flow in the relationship between self-efficacy and foreign language classroom anxiety remains underexplored. Given that self-efficacy enhances the likelihood of experiencing flow, and that flow, in turn, helps alleviate FLCA, it is theoretically plausible to position flow as an intervening mechanism.

Self-compassion: a moderator between self-efficacy and flow

Self-compassion involves offering oneself support during times of distress, whether due to personal shortcomings or external challenges [18]. Neff identifies three core components of self-compassion: (a) self-kindness, the ability to treat oneself with care rather than harsh self-criticism; (b) common humanity, the recognition that imperfection is part of the human experience, rather than feeling isolated by one’s difficulties; and (c) mindfulness, the capacity to maintain a balanced perspective on one’s experiences, rather than dwelling on suffering. A substantial body of research has linked self-compassion to reduced psychopathology, enhanced well-being, positive emotions, work engagement, improved physical health, and positive traits such as optimism, resilience, and curiosity [21, 53–55]. Furthermore, self-compassion plays a critical role in promoting effective autonomous language acquisition [56]. It is a valuable psychological resource that helps students manage academic challenges and boosts positive emotions related to language learning outcomes [57]. Nevertheless, its precise contribution to language acquisition has yet to be thoroughly examined.

Given its role in promoting positive emotions and resilience, self-compassion may facilitate flow experiences in language learning. Although empirical evidence remains limited, existing research suggests several indirect pathways linking the two. Self-compassion fosters resilience, a known predictor of flow in learning contexts [20, 58]. Additionally, a positive, cyclical relationship exists between self-compassion and coping self-efficacy, with both factors mutually reinforcing each other in everyday life [59]. Chishima et al. [60] found that self-compassion promotes adaptive coping by reducing perceived threat and enhancing perceived controllability of stressful events, thereby enhancing perceived control. These findings suggest that self-compassion may strengthen the link between self-efficacy and flow by enhancing learners’ emotional resilience and sense of control.

Theoretical foundation

This study integrates CVT and BBT to construct a dual-pathway framework for explaining the emergence of FLCA and identifying potential mechanisms for its mitigation. According to CVT, learners’ achievement emotions are primarily shaped by two cognitive appraisals: perceived control over learning activities and the subjective value attributed to learning outcomes [22, 61]. In the context of SLA, self-efficacy serves as a critical indicator of control appraisals. Learners with high self-efficacy perceive themselves as capable of managing language learning tasks, thereby experiencing reduced anxiety and increased positive emotions such as flow and enjoyment [39]. CVT also acknowledges the influence of individual antecedents—such as self-compassion—on learners’ appraisals. Self-compassion involves treating oneself with kindness and acceptance during difficult times, which helps reduce self-criticism and emotional reactivity, allowing learners to maintain psychological stability when facing setbacks. By fostering a more balanced emotional response, self-compassion enhances perceived control and reinforces self-efficacy, creating more favorable conditions for experiencing flow and reducing anxiety.

Complementing this cognitive-evaluative perspective, BBT offers an affective-functional account by emphasizing the adaptive value of positive emotions in learning contexts. According to BBT, positive emotions like flow broaden individuals’ momentary thought-action repertoires and contribute to the development of lasting cognitive, psychological, and social resources [23]. Flow has been shown to reduce anxiety by redirecting attention away from threat-related cognitions and toward sustained task engagement [62]. Thus, while CVT explains flow as a product of favorable appraisals (e.g., high self-efficacy and self-compassion), BBT positions flow as a mechanism for emotional regulation and psychological growth. Furthermore, BBT posits that positive emotional states can disrupt or reverse the physiological and cognitive responses typically associated with negative emotions like anxiety [63], thereby diminishing avoidance tendencies and enhancing resilience in SLA.

Together, CVT and BBT provide a comprehensive theoretical model that captures both the cognitive antecedents and emotional consequences of key affective experiences in language learning. This integrated framework proposes that self-efficacy and self-compassion jointly promote appraisals of control, which in turn facilitate flow experiences. Flow functions not only as a reflection of optimal cognitive appraisals (as emphasized by CVT), but also as an affective resource that helps learners manage anxiety and build psychological resilience (as emphasized by BBT). By elucidating the reciprocal relationships between cognition, emotion, and behavior, this model provides a nuanced explanation of how affective variables interact to influence FLCA, offering a theoretically grounded foundation for emotional interventions in SLA contexts.

In conclusion, drawing on Control-Value Theory and the Broaden-and-Build Theory, the present study proposes an integrative conceptual model to elucidate the interrelationships among self-efficacy, flow, self-compassion, and FLCA, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Grounded in the foregoing literature review, the following hypotheses are tested: (H1) self-efficacy is negatively associated with FLCA; (H2) flow mediates the relationship between self-efficacy and FLCA; and (H3) self-compassion moderates the relationship between self-efficacy and flow, such that this relationship is stronger for learners with higher levels of self-compassion.

Method

Participants

A total of 1,693 university students from mainland China were recruited through convenience sampling across multiple universities in different provinces to ensure diverse geographical representation. Participants were native Chinese-speaking undergraduates learning English as a foreign language, all of whom had passed the College English Test Band 4 (CET-4), a standardized assessment widely used in Chinese universities, demonstrating good English proficiency. Before entering university, students had typically received approximately 9–12 years of formal English instruction, as English is a compulsory subject throughout primary and secondary education in China. Most students had also completed major English examinations during secondary schooling—for example, the English component of the National College Entrance Examination (Gaokao)—which involve sustained preparation and extensive engagement with English learning tasks.

During the data screening process, cases with improbably short response times (< 200 s) or inattentive responses (e.g., selecting identical ratings throughout the survey) were excluded to ensure data quality. After filtering, 1,352 valid responses were retained, yielding an effective response rate of 79.86%. The final sample consisted of 757 females (56.00%) and 595 males (44.00%), with a mean age of 19.16 years (SD = 1.24). Participants were enrolled in two academic disciplines: science (n = 472, 34.91%) and humanities (n = 880, 65.09%).

Measures

Foreign language classroom anxiety

Foreign language classroom anxiety was measured using the Foreign Language Classroom Anxiety Scale (FLCAS) [1], which has high reliability and validity and is widely used in emotional research related to language learning contexts. The scale comprises 33 items, covering aspects like communication apprehension (CA), test anxiety (TA), and fear of negative evaluation (FNE). A sample item includes: “I start to panic when I have to speak without preparation in English class.” Responses were given on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating higher levels of FLCA. In this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.895 for CA, 0.903 for TA, and 0.814 for FNE. The overall scale yielded a Cronbach’s α of 0.956.

Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy was measured using six items from the self-efficacy subscale of the Psychological Capital Questionnaire-24 [64]. The measure has been previously validated among Chinese university students [65], demonstrating its suitability to gauge self-efficacy in this specific demographic. The example items include “I feel confident analyzing complex English learning problems to find effective solutions” and “I feel confident expressing my opinions in English during classroom discussions.” Participants rated these items on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”). The higher the score, the stronger the self-efficacy. In the present research, Cronbach’s α was 0.926.

Flow

Flow was measured using the 8-item Flow Experience Scale [66] from the Personally Expressive Activities Questionnaire (PEAQ). This 8-item flow scale was designed to evaluate the frequency and intensity of participants’ flows in the process of learning English in the classroom, which has shown robust reliability and validity in prior research [67]. Sample items in this study included “I have clear and specific goals when engaging in English learning tasks” and “I feel in control of my learning process when studying English.” During the factor analysis, one item (“I seem to lose self-consciousness when engaged in English learning activities”) was excluded due to its negative factor loading. This may be attributed to cultural differences in interpreting self-consciousness loss in the Chinese EFL context, where students might associate losing self-consciousness with adverse learning outcomes rather than the immersive flow experience intended initially. Participants rated their actual experiences on a 7-point Likert scale, where 1 represented “completely inconsistent” and 7 represented “completely consistent.” Higher scores indicated a greater tendency to experience a state of flow. Cronbach’s α in this study was 0.840.

Self-compassion

We employed the Self-Compassion Scale–Short Form (SCS–SF) to measure self-compassion, which consists of 12 items derived from six dimensions: self-kindness, self-judgment, common humanity, isolation, mindfulness, and over-identification [68]. The reliability and validity of the SCS–SF have been well-established across various cultural contexts and populations (e.g. [69]), . A sample item is: “When I encounter difficulties in learning English, I treat myself with care and kindness.” Participants responded on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “almost never,” 5 = “almost always”), with higher scores indicating greater self-compassion.

In this study, we excluded six items from the three negative subscales—self-judgment, isolation, and over-identification—as they were reverse-scored and showed weak or negative correlations with the remaining items. A confirmatory factor analysis further indicated that these subscales had very low factor loadings on the overall self-compassion construct, suggesting that they did not align well with the latent structure. After their removal, Cronbach’s α improved from 0.763 to 0.875, indicating better internal consistency.

Procedure

Prior to the survey administration, a translation and back-translation procedure were conducted to ensure the accuracy and cultural relevance of the survey instrument. Ethical approval was obtained from the first author’s affiliated institution. Participants were informed that their responses would be kept anonymous and confidential and used exclusively for research purposes, with no impact on their standing. Additionally, the study’s purpose was explained to all participants, who were also informed about the consent process, the voluntary nature of their participation, and their right to withdraw at any time. The survey was then conducted online using Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn/vm/PzzcLW2.aspx#), the most widely used questionnaire platform in China. Data were collected over approximately one month, from late November to December 2024.

Data analytic strategy

We first conducted tests to assess common method bias, followed by descriptive statistics and correlation analyses using SPSS 27.0. To minimize potential measurement error and enhance model parsimony, we employed an item parceling strategy in SPSS 27.0 prior to proceeding with further analyses [70]. Specifically, FLCA and self-compassion were parcelled into three distinct sets based on their theoretical dimensions, whereas flow and self-efficacy were parcelled into three groups using the factorial algorithm.

Subsequent analyses were conducted using Mplus 8.3. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to assess construct reliability and validity. Model fit was evaluated using several indices with established cutoff criteria: CFI and TLI compared the proposed model to a null model, with acceptable values above 0.95; RMSEA accounted for model complexity, with values below 0.06 indicating a good fit; and SRMR reflected residuals between observed and predicted covariances, with values below 0.08 considered acceptable [71]. Additionally, the chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio (χ2/df) was used to assess overall fit relative to model complexity, with values below 5 indicating adequate fit [72].

Next, to test the mediating role of flow in the relationship between self-efficacy and FLCA, we utilized bias-corrected percentile bootstrapping with 5,000 samples to estimate the indirect effects and their 95% confidence intervals. This robust method does not rely on normality assumptions and provides more accurate confidence intervals in structural models [73]. Finally, we examined the moderated mediation effects of flow at varying levels of self-compassion using the latent moderated structural equation (LMS) method. The significance of the mediation and moderation effects was assessed by checking whether the bootstrapped confidence intervals excluded zero.

Preliminary analyses

Test of the common method bias

Given that our data were primarily collected via self-reported questionnaires, Harman’s single-factor method was employed to assess common method bias [74]. The analysis identified seven factors with eigenvalues greater than 1; the first factor accounted for 33.773% of the total variance, well below the critical threshold of 40%. Therefore, common method bias is unlikely to pose a significant issue in this study.

Mediational results

After controlling for sex and majors, the structural equation model (SEM) based on theoretical assumptions demonstrated a good fit to the data, with the following indices: χ2/df = 4.427, RMSEA = 0.050, SRMR = 0.034, CFI = 0.987, and TLI = 0.983. As shown in Fig. 3, self-efficacy was negatively correlated with FLCA, supporting Hypothesis 1. Furthermore, Hypothesis 2 was supported, as flow significantly mediated the relationship between self-efficacy and FLCA (indirect effect = -0.319, 95% CI = [-0.393, -0.262]), accounting for 67.4% of the total effect. Regarding the magnitude of these relationships, the calculated Cohen’s f 2 effect sizes were 0.75 for the path from self-efficacy to flow, 0.13 for the path from flow to FLCA, and 0.01 for the direct path from self-efficacy to FLCA.

Test of moderated mediation

In line with the methodological guidelines proposed by Sardeshmuk and Vandenberg [75], the LMS approach, implemented using Mplus 8.3 software, was employed to test Hypothesis 3. Notably, the LMS method is preferred over traditional regression techniques for evaluating moderated mediation because it yields more accurate estimates, narrower confidence intervals, greater flexibility for complex models, and superior statistical power [76]. Initially, we tested the baseline model, which excluded the latent interaction term. The results of the data analysis indicated that the structural equation modeling (SEM) model provided a good fit to the data, as evidenced by the following fit indices: χ²/df = 3.817, CFI = 0.986, TLI = 0.982, SRMR = 0.034, and RMSEA = 0.046. These values indicate that the baseline model offers an adequate representation of the empirical data.

Subsequently, the moderated mediation model, incorporating the latent interaction term as specified by the hypothesized theoretical framework, was estimated. It is important to note that the LMS method does not provide traditional model fit indices such as CFI and RMSEA. To address this limitation, log-likelihood ratio tests were conducted to compare the fit of the baseline and moderated mediation models. The results of the log-likelihood ratio test indicated a significant difference between the moderated mediation model and the baseline model (p < 0.001), with the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) of the moderated mediation model decreasing by 43.599 compared to the baseline model (see Table 3). This finding provides empirical support for the superiority of the moderated mediation model, highlighting the critical role of self-compassion as a moderating factor in enhancing the model’s explanatory power.

Discussion

This study aimed to develop and test a moderated mediation model to examine the relationships among FLCA, self-efficacy, flow, and self-compassion in a sample of Chinese EFL learners. Our findings empirically support a model in which flow mediates the relationship between self-efficacy and FLCA, while self-compassion moderates the relationship between self-efficacy and flow.

The alleviating effect of self-efficacy on FLCA

This study confirmed the critical role of self-efficacy in alleviating foreign language classroom anxiety. This finding aligns with previous literature [39], highlighting that learners’ positive beliefs about their foreign language abilities can effectively reduce anxiety. Our study corroborates the central tenets of CVT among Chinese EFL learners, reinforcing the stability of these theoretical relationships across educational contexts. For EFL learners, motivation and anxiety are key predictors of academic performance. Higher self-efficacy can alleviate anxiety and enhance motivation, thereby improving language learning outcomes [40]. When FLCA is defined as a type of achievement emotion that results from high task value and low learning abilities, students who highly value a task often evaluate whether their abilities are sufficient to achieve their goals; if they find their abilities insufficient, the conflict between high values and low abilities has adverse effects. According to CVT, control judgments are primarily based on the perceived likelihood of achieving success [22]. Students with higher self-efficacy anticipate greater success and feel more in control of their learning, which in turn leads to more positive emotions and fewer negative emotions (e.g., anxiety). In contrast, those with lower self-efficacy expect more failure, feel less in control, and are more prone to negative emotions [77]. Validating this link in the current EFL context reaffirms self-efficacy as a primary “control appraisal” mechanism, as posited by CVT, thereby establishing a necessary theoretical foundation for examining subsequent mediation and moderation processes.

The mediating role of flow between self-efficacy and FLCA

The present study also revealed that flow mediated the relationship between self-efficacy and FLCA. While prior research has predominantly examined direct self-efficacy-anxiety associations [39], our findings reveal an additional indirect pathway via flow. Consistent with earlier studies [16, 78], our results indicate that self-efficacy enhances students’ flow experiences. A plausible explanation is that enhancing self-efficacy can facilitate individuals’ positive emotional and activity experiences, such as flow [79]. Specifically, when faced with tasks and challenges in the English classroom, students with higher self-efficacy are more confident in their language abilities and perceive a better alignment between their abilities and task demands, thereby meeting a key prerequisite for entering a flow state [42, 44].

Additionally, the finding of a significant path from flow to FLCA is consistent with previous research [15]. Students in a state of flow will focus their attention on the current classroom tasks and experience positive emotions, while their cognitive resources are mobilized to temporarily set aside anxiety [52]. Crucially, this finding supports the “undoing hypothesis” of BBT in a language-learning context, suggesting that the positive emotional resources generated by flow can help counteract the physiological and psychological grip of classroom anxiety.

Synthesizing these paths, flow plays a pivotal mediating role in the effect of self-efficacy on FLCA. Rather than treating anxiety reduction as a direct outcome of self-efficacy, the identified mediation pathway provides empirical support for an integrative view of CVT and BBT [22, 23, 61]. This enriches the field by identifying flow as an important operational pathway through which “control” facilitates anxiety reduction. As proposed by CVT, students with higher self-efficacy perceive greater competence, leading to enhanced control over classroom performance. Such a mechanism complements BBT by demonstrating how this sense of control cultivates positive emotions (flow) that expand learners’ cognitive and behavioral resources, enabling them to manage evaluative pressures more effectively. This partial mediation pattern extends prior CVT-based research by demonstrating that control appraisals reduce anxiety through dual routes—direct cognitive effects and indirect experiential engagement via flow—a mechanism not previously examined in SLA contexts.

The positive moderating role of self-compassion between self-efficacy and flow

We have verified that enhancing self-efficacy increases the likelihood of generating positive experiences, thereby helping students immerse themselves in current activities and temporarily alleviate anxiety. However, although self-efficacy can enhance a sense of control and contribute to the generation of pleasant experiences, positive emotions elicited solely by improving self-efficacy may not be sufficient to enable students to enter a state of flow. Significantly, the introduction of self-compassion underscores a key boundary condition for the relationship between self-efficacy and flow and differs from prior work by focusing on its interaction with self-efficacy rather than treating it solely as a direct emotional predictor, providing a more specific account of its role in supporting flow experiences. Self-compassion can enhance the experience of positive emotions by fostering a positive, caring attitude toward oneself when facing difficulties [80], thereby facilitating students’ entry into a state of flow. A possible reason is that self-compassion can serve as a self-regulatory resource, fostering kindness and self-acceptance when confronted with perceived inadequacies and providing an adaptive, flexible coping approach [57, 81]. These self-regulatory processes empower students to approach foreign language learning with greater confidence, thereby facilitating immersion.

According to the CVT, emotional experiences are jointly influenced by an individual’s sense of control and sense of value [18]. The current results extend the application of CVT by proposing that “control” judgments (self-efficacy) function more robustly when supported by emotion-regulatory resources (self-compassion). In the context of foreign language learning, self-compassion helps students maintain a sense of control over their language learning by reducing self-criticism and negative emotions in response to setbacks. At the same time, it enhances the sense of value by reframing challenges and fostering intrinsic motivation. This dual function not only strengthens students’ perceived mastery of foreign language learning and their recognition of its importance but also effectively reduces anxiety and self-doubt, thereby amplifying positive emotions. This finding refines existing theoretical models by suggesting that, in the face of linguistic challenges, the cognitive belief in one’s ability must be catalyzed by a supportive self-attitude to fully elicit the immersive state of flow. Consequently, students are more likely to sustain feelings of enjoyment and immersion. Overall, self-compassion not only reinforces the positive effects of self-efficacy but also integrates the dimensions of control and value, providing more comprehensive psychological support for students to enter the state of flow in foreign language learning.

Contributions and implications

FLCA has been observed among many students who need to learn a foreign language, creating obstacles to their foreign language learning and ultimately affecting their foreign language grades [82]. The findings of this study are of great theoretical and practical significance for understanding the potential mechanisms among self-efficacy, FLCA, flow, and self-compassion in the SLA context.

From a theoretical perspective, the findings verified the mediating role of flow in the relationship between self-efficacy and FLCA, indicating that learners with higher self-efficacy are more likely to experience flow and thus exhibit a reduction in FLCA. It supports the view that flow, as a state of deep engagement and pleasure, can serve as a psychological buffer against negative emotional experiences associated with language learning [83]. In addition, the moderating role of self-compassion emphasizes its importance as an emotional regulation resource [84]. Specifically, self-compassion helps learners cope with the stress and self-critical thoughts arising from perceiving low self-efficacy or difficulties in language learning, ultimately creating a more positive learning experience. CVT and BBT can account for these findings, and the inclusion of self-compassion as an emotional variable extends the existing CVT framework.

From a pedagogical perspective, the results offer valuable insights for language educators seeking to reduce FLCA from a positive-psychology perspective. First, given that higher self-efficacy was associated with lower FLCA through enhanced flow, our findings underscore the importance of fostering students’ confidence in their language abilities. One essential approach is to implement scaffolded learning experiences that provide appropriate challenges while ensuring success is achievable, allowing students to build confidence gradually through consistent progress [85]. Another effective strategy is adopting flipped classroom approaches that would enable students to control their learning pace and prepare comfortably before classroom activities [86]. Additionally, feedback practices play a crucial role in building student confidence. Regular formative assessment that emphasizes effort and progress rather than focusing solely on errors helps reinforce students’ developing sense of competence [87]. Teachers should provide encouraging feedback that helps learners view mistakes as learning opportunities, thereby maintaining their self-belief throughout the language learning process.

Second, it is crucial to facilitate learners’ entry into flow states in language classrooms. These conditions involve aligning task difficulty with students’ skill levels, establishing clear and meaningful goals, and delivering timely feedback [12]. Teachers are advised to assess students’ proficiency beforehand, develop suitably challenging tasks, and provide guidance that maintains learners’ concentration.

Third, to further sustain learners’ engagement and emotional regulation, cultivating self-compassion in the language classroom is advisable. Learners who adopt a self-kind attitude are more likely to tolerate setbacks and maintain focus during challenging tasks [88]. Educators can support this by incorporating reflective practices that encourage students to reinterpret mistakes as integral to the learning process, fostering peer environments that normalize vulnerability, and modeling nonjudgmental responses to their learning difficulties. Such strategies may help mitigate fear of failure, reduce perfectionistic tendencies, and enhance students’ persistence in emotionally demanding contexts.

Limitations and future directions

This research has limitations requiring further investigation. First, the cross-sectional design hinders causal inference among self-efficacy, flow, FLCA, and self-compassion; longitudinal or experimental designs are needed to validate causal pathways and temporal dynamics. Additionally, while self-compassion was examined as a moderator, other factors, such as resilience or emotional regulation, may also influence FLCA mechanisms. Expanding the model to include such variables could yield a more holistic understanding of anxiety in language learning. Third, some items were removed during factor analysis due to low factor loadings. While this approach is supported by methodological guidelines (e.g. [89]), , it may reduce the comparability of our findings with those from studies that employed the full versions of the scales and may slightly affect the conceptual coverage of the measured constructs. Addressing these limitations in future research would strengthen theoretical foundations and enhance the applicability of findings across diverse language learning contexts.

Conclusion

Based on the CVT and BBT, this study reveals the complex relationships among self-efficacy, FLCA, flow, and self-compassion. The findings indicate that flow mediates the relationship between self-efficacy and FLCA, suggesting that learners with higher self-efficacy are more likely to experience flow, which reduces anxiety. Additionally, self-compassion serves as a moderating factor, highlighting its crucial role as an emotional resource in language learning. This study contributes to the field by providing empirical evidence for integrated psychological mechanisms underlying language learning anxiety, extending theoretical understanding of how positive psychological constructs work synergistically and dynamically to mitigate negative emotional states [90, 91]. Moreover, this paper contributes to the future of language education by demonstrating the importance of cultivating learners’ self-efficacy, flow, and self-compassion to develop psychologically sustainable learning environments suited to the evolving emotional and cognitive demands of contemporary language education. These insights enable educators to design evidence-based interventions that foster positive psychological states while reducing foreign language anxiety.
