Intensification of marine heatwaves and their climate drivers in the Arabian Gulf
Authors: Alfiya Fathima Paradan, Fahim Abdul Gafoor, Maryam Rashed AlShehhi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMarine heatwaves (MHWs) are extreme sea surface temperature (SST) phenomena that can profoundly impact marine ecosystems but remain largely unexplored in the Arabian Gulf. We use a high resolution satellite SST and atmospheric reanalysis datasets to perform an extensive study of spatial and temporal variations of MHWs and their potential driving factors. Significant positive trends in MHW days, duration, and frequency are observed, particularly in the southeastern Gulf and the Sea of Oman during the summer. The Gulf experienced an increase of 0.6 MHW days/yr with an increasing intensity of C/yr in summer while summer SST also shows an increase of C/yr, indicating a notable warming trend. The first two EOF modes explain 80% of MHW variability, showing a Gulf-wide pattern in the first mode (62%) and contrasting Gulf versus Sea of Oman behavior in the second (18%), reflecting different underlying physical processes. Weakened Shamal winds and strengthened Kaus winds are observed during major MHW years, suggesting their potential influence on surface warming. MHWs often coincide with negative wind stress curl in upwelling zones and reduced latent heat loss, indicating weakened ocean cooling. While positive mean sea level (MSL) pressure anomalies are frequently observed, their inconsistency suggests that multiple ocean–atmosphere processes contribute to MHW development. Understanding these trends helps predict future extremes and mitigate their impacts, contributing to global efforts to protect marine ecosystems and coastal communities in a warming world.Keywords: Ocean warming, Coral bleaching, Marine heatwave, Climate change, Extreme eventsSubject terms: Ocean sciences, Physical oceanography

Introduction

Climate change due to anthropogenic activities are impacting oceans and the consequences are already evident in the form of sea surface temperature (SST) increase1, rapid sea ice loss2, altered upwelling systems3 and overturning circulation4 along with imbalances in nutrient dynamics of the oceans5. Moreover climate change has also resulted in intensification of extreme events around the globe such Marine heat waves (MHW)6, acidification events7 and hypoxia dead zones8. The term marine heat wave was first coined by Pearce et al. (2011)9, referring to anomalous warm seawater events that can have substantial effects on marine life. An MHW is a specific period of prolonged anomalously warm water at a particular location based on ocean temperatures exceeding a specific threshold. Various attempts have been made to define this threshold, Frölicher and Laufkötter (2018)10 defined MHW based on a fixed threshold, while Eakin et al. (2010)11 proposed a cumulative threshold. However, the most widely used definition of MHW is of Hobday et al. (2016)12 which follow a seasonally varying threshold. An anomalously warm event is said be a MHW if it’s duration is greater than 5 days and it’s temperature is warmer than the 90th percentile temperature based on historical baseline period. As an extreme, abnormally warm event, a MHW can cause sudden and severe impacts on the marine environment, which can be more intense than those caused by gradual long-term warming. Studies have reported mass mortality, fish kills13 and coral bleaching14. With several studies reporting intensification of MHWs duration and frequency in future climate15,16, their impacts will significantly perturb future marine ecosystems.

MHWs are influenced by several factors, both local and global such as air-sea interactions, atmospheric conditions like wind speed, oceanic preconditions and remote large scale climate connections17,18. Local processes like heat fluxes and ocean advective and mixing processes are sensitive to the ocean state19. However, it can also be influenced by climate variability or global warming and may vary regionally and seasonally. In the extra-tropics, intense MHWs are often linked to persistent atmospheric highs, resulting in increased insolation and decreased wind speeds, reducing turbulent heat losses and vertical ocean mixing.19. Heat budget analyses by Vogt et al. (2022)20 show that increased insolation and reduced evaporative cooling dominate the build-up of MHWs, while decay is driven by increased turbulent heat losses. They also proposed that MHWs in subtropics and mid-to-high latitudes are caused by net ocean heat uptake, reduced latent heat loss, increased shortwave heat absorption, and reduced sensible heat loss and dampened by reduced vertical mixing. In the tropics, reduced ocean heat uptake and lowered vertical local mixing cause warming. While increased ocean heat loss to the atmosphere and enhanced vertical local mixing dominated the decline of MHWs. Large scale modes of climate variability like El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO)21 and Indian Ocean Diapole (IOD)22 can affect the probability of occurrences of MHW regionally by modulating the local drivers and the upper-ocean stratification. However, the characteristics of MHWs, such as duration and frequency, as well as the drivers are equivocally agreed to be changing regionally, emphasizing the need for regional characterization of MHW.

Our study region, the Arabian Gulf and the Sea of Oman, is a marginal sea of Arabian Sea. The Arabian Gulf, hereafter called ”the Gulf” is a shallow evaporative basin with depth less than 90m, which is located in one of the world’s extreme climatic location23. The Gulf is connected to the deeper Sea of Oman (depth >4000m) through the Strait of Hormuz24. Being a marginal sea, and with extreme SSTs in summer, the Gulf is expected to warm at a rate greater than the global average25,26 which will have devastating effect on its rich flora and fauna. Coral reefs are one such ecosystems that suffer large-scale mortality due to extreme SST. A study of eight reefs in the Arabian Gulf found coral bleaching events associated with high SST during an extended summer season27. Recent study by Riegl Jr et al. (2024)28 reveals that thermal stress events in the Arabian Gulf have led to extensive coral mortality, resulting in reef degradation and biodiversity loss in recent decades. Moreover marine biodiversity and fisheries in the Arabian Gulf exhibited high rate of local extinction29,30. Also, Alosairi et al. (2020)13 reported an extreme SST of 37.6C in the northern Gulf. This MHW event driven by neap tides, and high humidity northwesterly winds led to large-scale intermittent fish kill incidents along Kuwait’s coastline and in Iraq’s Shatt Al Arab stretch, with varying species found during the incident. A review by Kawiyani et al. (2024)31 evaluates selected habitats and organisms, including mangroves, sea grasses, and coral reefs in the Arabian Gulf reveals a general decline in these areas.

However, a very few attempts have been made to study the MHW characteristics and trends in the Gulf. Kashkooli et al. (2022)32 studied the spatiotemporal variability of the Gulf and Sea of Oman using SST products (AVHRR-only version with spatial resolution of 0.25) of National Center for Environmental Information (NCEI), but have not looked into the drivers of the MHWs. Another study by Hamdeno et al. (2022)33 used higher-resolution satellite-based SST observations (0.05) from 1982 to 2020; however, their analysis of MHW drivers was limited to events associated with low chlorophyll-a (Chl-a) concentrations. As MHWs can also be linked to positive Chl-a anomalies19,34, their study does not capture the full range of drivers influencing all MHW events, not just low Chl-a events. Shaltout and Eladawy (2024) recently explored how MHW intensity may be influenced by large-scale drivers, such as ENSO events and changes in the sea surface radiation budget. While their study provides valuable insights, it did not focus on local-scale influences such as atmospheric wind and coastal upwelling35, which are also important in shaping MHW characteristics in the region. Hence, in this study we attempts to look at the properties, spatiotemporal variability, trends and drivers of MHW using a high resolution (0.01) satellite data from Group for High Resolution Sea Surface Temperature (GHRSST) datasets36. The Empirical Orthogonal Function (EOF) analysis has been previously applied to understand SST variability and its link to MHWs and it’s drivers in various regions37. In this study, we apply EOF decomposition to identify the dominant drivers of MHWs in the Arabian Gulf and Oman Gulf, which has not been previously examined in this region. EOF analysis allows for the decomposition of MHW variability into spatial modes and their associated temporal patterns, helping to isolate dominant structures of variability that may be linked to physical drivers such as local oceanographic processes. This provides a more nuanced understanding of the spatial coherence and temporal evolution of MHWs in the Arabian Gulf, beyond what is possible through simple trend analysis.

To better understand regional MHW characteristics, we focus on two sub regions: the northern Gulf (above 28N; outlined by the black box in Fig. 9) and the UAE coast (within the black triangle) for contextualizing their geographic location as they follow the discussion. The selected regions are characterized by shallow bathymetry, ecological sensitivity, and pronounced trends in marine heatwave indices identified in our analysis. These areas also overlap with locations where multiple MHW events have been documented in previous studies13,27. Among them, the UAE coast stands out due to its exceptionally high summer sea surface temperatures and the presence of vulnerable habitats such as coral reefs and seagrass beds28. In addition, the region hosts critical coastal infrastructure41 and supports important fisheries29, increasing its exposure and sensitivity to the impacts of marine heatwaves. The UAE coast experiences 2-2.5 MHW events per year (Fig.3a), where duration of each MHW is between 8-12 days (Fig.3c), making a total of 20-25 MHW days per year (Fig.3e). Whereas northern Gulf experience longer duration of MHW, but fewer events and much higher intensity of around 2-2.5C compared to the UAE coast where the mean intensities are around 1.5C (Fig.3f). However even this mean intensity will add up the SST to above 37 C in summer which is above the tolerance limit of many ecosystems such as corals28. The mean MHWs matrices in Sea of Oman are more or less similar to the indices along the UAE coast, but being a deeper sea, their impact on ecology will be lesser than that compared to the shallow coast along the UAE where the water depth is around 20m. The region around Strait of Hormuz also experiences long duration MHWs lasting over 25 days per year and having mean intensity of 1.5C (Fig.3e,g). The spatial variability in MHW trends across the Gulf is evident and further explored using Theil–Sen estimators (Fig.3e-h). These maps show a clear and significant increase in MHW count, duration, and total MHW days over large parts of the region. Strong positive trends are particularly pronounced along the UAE coast, the southern Gulf, and the Sea of Oman. In many areas, MHWs are increasing by up to 2.5 days per year, with approximately 0.5 additional events annually. The duration of each MHW event is also increasing at a rate of 0.5 days per year in these regions. In contrast, trends in MHW intensity are comparatively weak and spatially inconsistent across the region (Fig.3h).

Major drivers of marine heatwaves

The Arabian Gulf’s thermal regimes are strongly influenced by regional wind patterns, which play a key role in modulating SST39. The climatological annual mean SST over the Gulf and Sea of Oman is plotted in Fig.5a, along with the mean annual wind derived from ERA5. The northwestern gulf has a low SST, showing a clear meridional gradient with SST increasing towards the southeastern Gulf along the UAE coast. The major wind blows from the northwestern Gulf towards the southeast direction. These northwesterly winds, known as Shamal Winds, originate from high-pressure systems over Turkey, Iraq, and Iran and blow towards the southeast, affecting the Gulf region, cooling the SST, and enhancing upwelling in summer and winter24. Meanwhile, another major wind is Kaus winds, southeasterly winds from the Arabian Sea, bringing warm, humid air to the Arabian Gulf, increasing moisture levels in coastal regions. They occur before the Indian summer monsoon and can appear briefly in autumn causing increased humidity, fog, and warmer SST13. However, their intensity in the annual mean is very low, as evident from the climatology plot in Fig. 5a. Nevertheless these two wind systems play a significant role in modulating the MHWs. Previous studies, such as Alawad et al. (2023)39, have shown that weakened Shamal winds can contribute to anomalous SST warming by reducing cold air advection, lowering evaporative cooling, and suppressing upwelling due to enhanced stratification. For instance, the SST anomalies overlaid with wind anomalies for the year 2003 (Fig. 5b) suggest a possible contribution from the strengthening of the Kaus wind, which may have supported the development of MHW conditions that year. In contrast, the year 2004 (Fig. 5c) shows a deflection of the Shamal winds away from the UAE coast, which may have reduced wind-driven mixing and surface cooling, thereby creating conditions more favorable for MHW development. No clear wind anomalies were observed in 2005, suggesting that any marine heatwave (MHW) conditions during that year, if present, were likely driven by other factors. In 2006, moderate strengthening of Shamal winds was observed, which may have inhibited the development of MHW conditions. The years from 2007 to 2014 (Fig. 5f-h) showed highly anomalous Shamal winds, making them non-MHW events in both the Gulf and the Sea of Oman. But there are exceptions for the years 2010 and 2015, when there was an anomalous Kaus wind, which made 2010 an MHW year, while the negative SST anomaly in 2015 made it a non-MHW year. From there on, there is either a weakening of Shamal winds and/or strengthening of Kaus winds, which caused an increasing trend in the MHW index and SST.

An EOF analysis was further conducted on the MHW Index, a more comprehensive metric that combines the frequency, duration, and mean intensity of MHWs. The spatial distribution of first and second EOF modes is given in Fig.6 along with the corresponding standardized principal components (PC) PC1 and PC2. To provide context for the EOF-derived patterns, the spatial climatology of the annual mean MHW index (2003–2024) is shown in Fig. 6e. This highlights the baseline spatial distribution of MHW activity across the region before applying the decomposition. The spatial patterns of other EOF modes are less prominent, resulting in a variance of less than 6% and hence omited from this study. The first mode of EOF explains 62% and the second modes of EOF explains 18%. The spatial distribution of EOF1 (Fig. 6a) is negative throughout the Gulf and Sea of Oman, with the northern Gulf being more negative. The amplitude of PC1 (Fig. 6b) tends to be negative during MHW years and positive during non-MHW years, as seen from the combined MHW index for the study region (see Fig.4a). This inverse relationship suggests that the first EOF mode captures basin-wide variability linked to the suppression or enhancement of MHW conditions. The spatial patterns of EOF2 show a clear zonal dipole pattern, with negative along the northwestern Gulf and turning positive along the southeastern Gulf, and it intensifies further east towards the Sea of Oman (Fig.6c). But the PC2 does not show a clear pattern over the years, with both positive and negative values.

The wind speed and SST anomaly highly influence the wind stress and the curl of the wind stress. The wind stress curl is a mathematical formula that determines the rotational component of the wind stress field, which influences Ekman pumping and upwelling/downwelling in the ocean. Positive wind stress indicates counterclockwise (cyclonic) rotation in the Northern Hemisphere, leading to upwelling. Negative wind stress indicates clockwise (anticyclonic) rotation, causing downwelling. Zero wind stress curl indicates no rotational force, resulting in minimal upwelling or downwelling.42. The climatological wind stress curl is plotted in Fig.7a. The wind stress curl in the Gulf shows positive values near the Iranian coast extending south till the UAE coast, indicating upwelling, which cools SST and enhances biological productivity. Conversely, negative values indicate downwelling, which pushes surface water downwards, leading to warmer SST and low nutrient levels. Wind-driven downwelling dominates in the western Gulf, causing higher SST and lower nutrient levels. The comparison of annual wind stress curl anomalies (Fig. S2) and SST anomalies (Fig.5) shows that positive wind stress curl anomalies in generally aligns with cooler SSTs, while negative curl anomalies corresponds to warmer SSTs. This supports the role of wind-driven processes in shaping SST variability, though some exceptions suggest other regional factors like regional circulation, or atmospheric heat fluxes may also be involved. The correlation between wind stress curl and PC1 of the EOF analysis (Fig. 7b) is significant only in certain regions, rather than across the entire domain. Notably, some areas exhibit high correlation coefficients of around 0.8, indicating a strong local influence of wind stress curl on the leading mode of SST variability. However, the lack of widespread significance suggests that additional factors also contribute to SST variability elsewhere in the Gulf. The first mode of EOF doesn’t show significant correlation with the air-sea heat fluxes when they are regressed onto PC1. However, when the heat flux components are regressed onto the PC2 of the MHW index(Fig.7c–f), distinct spatial patterns emerge, indicating varying degrees of association across the region. PC2 is the second most dominant mode of variability in the MHW index, capturing secondary patterns linked to regional processes such as external forcings. A spatial regression plot of the MHW index’s PC2 against air-sea heat fluxes indicates how variations in fluxes are related to the variability captured by PC2 at different locations. The magnitude of the regression coefficient indicates the strength of the relationship between the MHW index PC2 for a particular year and heat flux at that location. Larger values suggest a stronger influence of heat flux on MHW variability, while smaller values indicate a weaker relationship. While positive values suggest that increases in heat flux are associated with positive anomalies in the PC2 pattern, negative values indicate negative anomalies, suggesting a cooling or suppressive effect, assuming PC2 is positive that year. The spatial distribution of shortwave radiation (SWR) and long wave radiation (LWR) fluxes are opposite in sign when regressed onto PC2 (Fig.7c,d). The significant negative SWR anomaly linked to PC2 in the Sea of Oman (Fig.7d) suggests that MHW variability here is not driven by surface solar heating, but likely by ocean-atmospheric processes. However, the most notable heat flux anomaly here is that of latent heat flux (LHF); being an evaporative basin, the latent heat flux is always a cooling flux in the region24. The latent heat flux anomaly is the prominent flux in the region, for both the Gulf and the Sea of Oman (Fig.7e)43. The yearly mean anomalies of latent heat flux, upward long wave radiation flux, and short wave radiation flux are plotted in supplementary figures (Fig. S3-S5). Since the region is having a negative latent heat flux, a positive anomaly value indicates a reduced loss of latent heat from the ocean to the atmosphere. Most MHW years have positive latent heat flux anomalies; however, there are exceptions such as the years 2020 and 2024. Similarly, upward long wave radiative fluxes are also a cooling flux in the region and have experienced positive anomalies (reduced heat loss from the ocean) during most of the MHW year. Here also, there are certain exceptions, the years 2010 and 2021. However, these years experienced an increased shortwave heat flux anomaly, which is the major heating flux, thus explaining the MHWs. However, sensible heat flux (SHF) does not play much role here.

We next looked at the mean sea level (MSL) pressure anomaly over an extended region around the study region covering major Arabian sea (Fig. S6). MHW strength can be influenced by MSL pressure anomalies where positive anomalies, higher than the long-term average, can indicate stronger and more persistent MHWs, reduced surface cooling, weaker winds or upwelling, and atmospheric pressure influence. The positive anomalies suggest a stable, warm ocean environment, prolonging and intensifying MHW events in a region44. While a negative MSL pressure anomalies, lower than the long-term average, indicate weaker or fewer MHWs, stronger winds and enhanced upwelling, cyclonic atmospheric patterns, and stronger surface mixing. These lower MSL pressure can also be associated with low-pressure systems, which enhance cloud cover and reduce incoming solar radiation, leading to ocean cooling. Additionally, stronger surface mixing can limit extreme surface warming. In our case, most of the identified MHW years exhibit positive MSL pressure anomalies, with the exception of 2021, 2022, and 2024. Additionally, the years 2014 to 2016, though not classified as MHW years, still show high positive mean sea level anomalies. These cases indicate that while positive MSL pressure anomalies are commonly observed during MHW years, they are not a necessary condition, suggesting that other ocean-atmosphere processes may also influence the occurrence of MHW.

Discussion

This study investigates the characteristics and variability of MHWs in the Arabian Gulf and the Sea of Oman, discusses the physical mechanisms that are presumed to be causing the MHW are also investigated. We noticed an increasing trend in the duration and the count of MHWs occurring in the region. Moreover a seasonal shift MHWs form winter to Summer was also observed, along with an increasing trend in SST. Also we observed a meridional shift in the locations of MHW where the MHWs are increasing along the southern Gulf and in Sea of Oman. Some of the recent studies also agrees with our findings. Study by Kashkooli et al. (2022)32 projected a doubling of spatial extent of MHWs over the past 24 years, with a 19-fold increase in the average number of MHW days since 1997 compared to 1982–1997. The highest intensities were observed in the northern and coastal regions, with temperatures 1.8 to 2C above the long-term SST mean. Additionally, there has been a significant rise in both the average number of MHW days and cumulative intensity, with about 90% of the Arabian Gulf experiencing an increase of more than 4C days per decade. In another study by Hamdeno et al. (2022)33, the Gulf exhibited long-lasting MHWs, with the highest annual MHW frequency trend, up to 1.5 events per decade, was observed in the northern part of Sea of Oman.

Moreover, the observed SST increase, likely influenced by global warming, along with the increasing frequency of marine heatwaves, may contribute to heightened risks for Gulf ecosystems and marine life. The major threats are towards the indigenous species and the fragile ecosystems like corals. An Extreme SST of 37.6 C was reported in Kuwait Bay and Alosairi et al. (2020)13 found that the MHW event was accompanied by major fish kill incidents. Nevertheless, corals suffer the greatest impact since they currently inhabit the future thermal conditions projected for the majority of tropical reefs45. The Gulf corals underwent bleaching following exposure to temperatures exceeding 34C for a cumulative period of 8 weeks, with 3 weeks surpassing 35C14. The prolonged MWH in the Gulf will lead to significant fluctuations in coral population levels and compromising their ability to build reefs. Another notable effect of MHW is on the biogeochemistry of the Gulf. The Chl-a, a measure of surface productivity was correlated with MHW by Hamdeno et al. (2022)33. The low Chl-a event happened 11 days after the onset of the MHW event and concluded as the MHW started to diminish. The peak intensity of the low Chl-a and MHW events was recorded at -0.90 mg/m and 5.3C, respectively during a period 1982 to 2020.

The EOF analysis conducted for the MHW index revealed that the first mode explains 62% and the second mode accounts for 18% of the variability in MHWs. The spatial distribution of the first EOF mode exhibits a general single-sign pattern across the Gulf and Sea of Oman, with more negative values in the northwest Gulf, indicating a coherent regional variability in MHW strength. The second EOF mode shows a zonal shift, with negative values over most of the Gulf and positive values along the Sea of Oman and UAE coast, suggesting contrasting MHW behaviors between these regions likely driven by differing physical processes. Wind plays a key role in the development of MHWs, along with other atmospheric and oceanic factors. During MHW years, the cooling Shamal winds tended to weaken, while the warming Kaus winds often strengthened. The Kaus wind carries warm air into the Gulf, creating conditions that may favor the formation of MHWs. The correlation between wind stress curl and PC1 is strong but significant only in specific regions, indicating a localized influence on the MHW index. This limited spatial significance suggests that other factors also contribute to SST variability in the Gulf. Notably, several major MHW years coincide with negative wind stress curl anomalies over upwelling-prone areas, indicating a potential link between weakened upwelling and MHW development. The analysis indicates that reduced latent heat flux is associated with stronger MHWs in the region, suggesting that suppressed surface cooling contributes to increased surface warming during these events. Positive MSL pressure anomalies are frequently associated with MHW events in the region, but their inconsistent presence across all years suggests that multiple ocean-atmosphere processes contribute to MHW development.

Limitations of the study

While a 30-year baseline is recommended for climatological analyses to capture the full range of natural variability, this study employs a 22-year dataset (2003–2024) to define the baseline climatology for MHW detection. The choice was dictated by data availability of highest spatial resolution and the objective to focus on the most recent period of accelerated warming. We acknowledge several limitations associated with the use of a shorter (22-year) climatological baseline. A limited baseline is more sensitive to the influence of anomalously warm or cold years, which increases the uncertainty in estimating thresholds for detecting marine heatwave (MHW) events12. Additionally, since the climatology is based on more recent years that are generally warmer due to ongoing climate change, the calculated MHW thresholds are likely biased high. This can result in an underestimation of the frequency, duration, and intensity of moderate MHWs, as less-severe events may no longer exceed the elevated threshold46,47. The shorter timeframe also limits the ability to capture decadal or multi-decadal climate variability, which may affect the representativeness of the detected MHWs12,46, especially in light of strong long-term warming trends that contribute substantially to uncertainty in MHW characterization48. While this approach may reduce comparability with studies that use the conventional 30-year reference period, it aligns with recent regional methodologies47,49,50 that prioritize high-resolution, contemporary climatologies. Coarse-resolution datasets tend to underestimate MHW frequency and intensity, whereas high-resolution data have been shown to better resolve the spatial structure and variability of MHWs as evident from the study of Pilo et al., 201951, particularly in coastal and shallow regions where fine-scale processes are critical52. Hence, we consider our choice of 22 years appropriate for analyzing recent trends; however, all findings should be interpreted in light of this limitation.

Nevertheless, this study provides valuable insights into the spatial and temporal characteristics of MHWs in the Arabian Gulf and Sea of Oman, identifying key atmospheric drivers and regional variability. It establishes a baseline for understanding MHW trends in a climatically and ecologically sensitive region. However, the current analysis does not account for seasonal variability in atmospheric and oceanic drivers, which may influence MHW development at finer temporal scales. Additionally, the role of subsurface ocean processes–including vertical mixing, heat content, stratification, and oceanic heat advection–was not explored due to data constraints. These factors are critical for a full understanding of MHW dynamics. Furthermore, while the influence of large-scale climate modes such as ENSO and IOD is acknowledged, their potential contributions were beyond the scope of this study. A comprehensive statistical or dynamical analysis of these interconnections would be an important future step. The outlined concrete directions for future research will enhance the impact of our findings rather than diminishing them. Despite the limitations, this study makes a significant contribution toward advancing regional MHW understanding and supports the development of future mitigation and adaptation strategies.

Methods

The Arabian Gulf is a semi-enclosed marginal sea located between 23 to 30N and 48 to 57E, with average depth of 40m and maximum width of 338 km. The Oman Sea is located between 22 to 27N and 56 to 65E23,24 (Fig. 9). The Gulf experiences an arid and subtropical climate with annual rainfall along the southern coasts less than 50 mm/year and the northern coasts being less than 200 mm/year32. The freshwater inflow to the Gulf supplied by the northern rivers are very much limited26. The hydrography of the Gulf shows a extremely hot (> 35C) and saline waters (> 40 psu) in summer, while there is a gradient of temperature in winter of above 10 C, and hence it is much cooler with SST dropping below 16C, but equally saline24,53. The Strait of Hormuz experiences an inflow along the northern coast and a southeast flow from the central region towards it. Surface currents form a cyclonic gyre in the central Gulf, centered at 53E. Water entering the Gulf is modified by air-sea fluxes and mixing processes, resulting in different properties of the water exiting the Gulf (hotter, saltier, and denser) from that which enters24. The Arabian Gulf has the highest SST records globally due to its regional hydrographic features as well as from global warming13. Coastal developments, increasing effluent discharge, seawater desalination54 and power plants41 have caused ecological stresses30, particularly in shallow coastal areas, making the region more vulnerable to thermal stresses.

The geography and bathymetry of the Arabian Gulf and Sea of Oman. The red box marks the northern regions of the Gulf and the red triangle shows the UAE coastal regions. The map was generated using the Python Basemap toolkit (https://matplotlib.org/basemap/).

The GHRSST Level 4 Multi-scale Ultra-high Resolution (MUR) Global Foundation SST Analysis (v4.1) is a high-resolution SST dataset produced by NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL). The dataset is gap-free, daily global SST fields with a spatial resolution of 1 km ( 0.01 grid spacing). MUR v4.1 integrates SST observations from multiple sources including the NASA Advanced Microwave Scanning Radiometer-EOS (AMSR-E), the JAXA satellite Advanced Microwave Scanning Radiometer-2 on GCOM-W1, the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometers (MODIS) on the NASA Aqua and Terra platforms, the US Navy microwave WindSat radiometer, the Advanced Very High Resolution Radiometer (AVHRR) on several National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) satellites, and in situ SST observations from the NOAA In situ Quality Monitor project36. Our dataset spans 22 years of daily SST from 2003 to 2024. Even though, there are satellite data products covering longer intervals of time, such as NOAAs Optimum Interpolation SST (OISST) V2 from AVHRR satellite data26,32,55, these datasets have significantly lower spatial resolutions, typically ranging from 0.25 to 0.05. Moreover, the suitability of GHRSST in the study region is well studied by Nesterov et al. (2021)56 compared GHRSST with 2-year seawater temperature measurements at ten offshore buoys in the shallow coastal waters, revealing good agreement with estimated root mean square deviations ranging from 0.5 to 0.9C. Even though GHRSST data are available from May 2003, we exclude this year due to incomplete coverage of a full calendar year. The other variables used in this study: 10 m wind components, surface net solar radiation, surface net thermal radiation, surface latent heat flux, surface sensible heat flux and mean sea level pressure, are downloaded from ERA557. ERA5 is the fifth-generation European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) with hourly and daily estimates of many climate variables from 1979 to present and a resolution of 0.2558. Sevaral studies have established the applicability of ERA5 atmospheric data in the Gulf33

To evaluate long-term trends in SST, we employed a combination of parametric and non-parametric approaches. Chang et al. (2024)38 provide a detailed comparison of these methods, highlighting their sensitivity to data length, noise, and seasonal variability. In our study, we applied both linear regression and STL decomposition to assess trend robustness, following the approaches of Bonino et al. (2023)59, Dash et al. (2025)60 and Eladawy et al. (2017)61. First a spatially averaged daily SST time series over the region of interest and applied Seasonal-Trend decomposition using LOESS (STL)62 to isolate the long-term trend component. We then estimated linear trends using multiple methods for robust comparison. Ordinary Least Squares Regression63 (OLSR1) was applied in the conventional form (SST as a function of time), from which the slope, standard error, and p-value were derived. A non-parametric Mann-Kendall64 trend test was also performed to evaluate the significance of monotonic trends, and the associated Sen’s slope65,66 estimator was used to quantify the rate of change. Additionally, we employed alternative regression techniques including inverse regression (OLSR2)67, Geometric Mean Regression (GMR), and Orthogonal Regression (OR)68 based on Principal Component Analysis (PCA)69. Since these methods do not inherently provide uncertainty estimates, a bootstrap resampling approach70 was used to empirically derive standard errors for their trend estimates. All trend estimates were converted to C per decade for consistency and visualized together with the STL-derived trend for comparative analysis. The summer months considered through out this analysis are months June, July and August, while winter months are January, February and December

This study examines temperature extremes MHWs as abnormally warm events lasting five days or longer, exceeding the 90th percentile following the definition by Hobday et al. (2016)12. The climatological threshold for 2003 to 2024 was estimated from data centered on an 11-day window and then smoothed using a 31-day moving average. The frequency or count of MHWs is defined as the number of MHWs per year, while the total number of MHW days is the sum of all MHW days in that year. The mean MHW intensity is the average SST anomaly during MHWs. We used the python package developed by Oliver (2016)71 available at https://github.com/ecjoliver/marineHeatWaves?tab=readme-ov-file for the detection of MHWs. The trend of the MHW parameters is estimated by the Theil-Sen estimator65,66 which 95% interval of the sampled slope. The Theil-Sen estimator is an unbiased metric that is much less susceptible to outliers. Then Mann-Kendall rank test (95 % interval of the significance level) is conducted to determine the significance of the trend64,72. Four MHW matrices were used to describe MHW strength and characteristics. They are (a) MHWs per year, which is the total number or Count of MHWs occured in a year, (b) the mean duration of MHWs, (c) total MHW days, which is the sum of all MHW days in a year and (d) mean MHW intensity. Finally we define a cumulative index to quantify the strength of MHW. The MHW index is defined as the product of number of MHW events, the mean duration and the mean intensity of MHW. The MHW index have also been used in the study by Yao and Wang (2021)37. The mathematical definition of each are given in Table.S1.
