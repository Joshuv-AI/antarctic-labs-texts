Patient preference predictors revisited: technically feasible, ethically desirable, yet must be clinically relevant
Authors: Andrea Ferrario, Beatrix Göcking, Giovanna Brandi, Emanuela Keller, Nikola Biller-Andorno
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough goal-concordant care is central to patient-centered medicine, determining treatment preferences for incapacitated patients remains a challenge. Nearly two decades ago, algorithms were proposed to estimate the most likely treatment preferences in the absence of advance directives, aiming to support surrogate decision-making. This idea has evolved into a race toward increasingly complex models, driven by the assumption that expanding data collection and refining predictive methods will yield more accurate approximations of patients’ unknown treatment preferences. Despite extensive debate on the epistemic, ethical, and clinical challenges of these algorithms, none have been successfully implemented in clinical practice. We contend that this failure does not stem from any of these challenges but, rather, from conceptualizing these models simply as technically sophisticated replicas of advance directives, abstracting a few high-level treatment preferences across all clinical contexts while ignoring setting-specific, temporal, and relational factors. The barrier to the implementation of these models is fundamentally a technology design problem that requires a novel design perspective to ensure their clinical relevance. We discuss this perspective using neuro-intensive care as a case study and examine how algorithmic models could support time-sensitive decision-making for patients with severe acute brain injury. The success of patient preference predictions depends not only on their being technically feasible and ethically promising but on ensuring clinical relevance.Keywords: Goal-concordant care, Decision-making, Goal of care preferences, Patient preference, Artificial intelligence, Machine learning, Brain injuries, Neurological intensive care unit, Critical care

Background

Goal-concordant care—ensuring that medical treatments align with a patient’s values and preferences—is a key principle of patient-centered medicine, particularly in high-stakes, preference-sensitive decisions [1]. However, determining preferences for incapacitated patients remains a persistent challenge, involving notable epistemic and normative challenges. The absence of advance directives (ADs)—here meant as a document used to record living wills—and the limitations of surrogate decision-making have led different authors to propose the use of algorithms—namely, a set of rules run by computer systems to predict an output of interest given input data—to estimate patient’s most likely treatment preferences in case of their incapacitation [2, 3].

Since their proposal, these algorithms, originally called patient preference predictor (PPP or P3) have risen to a broader technological competition—a ‘race for Px’—where Px denotes increasingly personalized predictors, ranging from the original P3 to the more recent P3.5 and P4, each reflecting a higher degree of predictive personalization [4, 5]. In fact, the underlying hypothesis driving the race is that expanding the granularity of data and implementing methods from artificial intelligence (AI) will yield more accurate approximations of patient’s true, albeit often unknown, treatment preferences. As a result, while early algorithms suggested population-based rules to predict category-level treatment preferences, more advanced approaches suggest considering machine learning incorporating large-scale data integration and natural language processing to compute individualized preferences [4, 5].

Our deliberate use of the verb ‘suggest’ underscores the theoretical nature of this endeavor. Despite extensive discourse on the epistemic and ethical challenges posed by Px, which span a broad and growing body of literature [2, 5–12], after 18 years since its original proposal, no Px has been adopted in real-world clinical environments. The original promise of the PPP—to enhance goal-concordant care by aiding clinicians and surrogate decision-makers in making more informed treatment decisions—remains unfulfilled.

In this paper, we argue that this implementation gap descends from a conceptual challenge that we name ‘substitutive design fallacy’ affecting the Px proposition since its inception. Under this fallacy, Px models are simply conceptualized as technically complex replicas of ADs that compute few, high-level treatment preferences and make them available across clinical scenarios, regardless of care setting, decision-making timelines, or relational dynamics among human participants. While this conceptualization may have initially served as a useful rhetorical instrument to promote the idea of algorithmically enhanced preference elicitation [2, 3], it has condemned Px to replicate the well-known limitations of ADs (e.g., context insensitivity), neglecting the procedural and social dimensions required to meaningfully integrate Px into clinical decision-making that are key to the design of this technology. Thus, we contend that the barrier to the clinical implementation of Px models is fundamentally a technology design problem, rather than the result of the ethical challenges that are extensively discussed in the literature.

To solve this problem, we believe that Px models can become clinically viable only if tailored to the constraints of clinicians’ and surrogates’ interactions and designed to be embedded in those interactions. Drawing on clinical pragmatism, we stipulate that the design of Px models should follow three steps: (1) identifying which decisions are most relevant and preference-sensitive in each clinical context along different clinical trajectories; (2) select the decisions that would benefit from algorithmic predictions in that context; and (3) defining the procedures for how Px should be invoked, its output presented, and the system interact with human deliberation. Our approach implies the development of distinct Px systems, each trained on context-appropriate data, producing decision-specific outputs, and deployed at well-defined moments of clinical uncertainty—in summary, tailored to the needs of particular care scenarios.

We explore this approach through a neuro-intensive care use case within the context of incapacitation due to severe acute brain injury (SABI) [13]. Paraphrasing Earp et al. [5], even if Px prove technically feasible and ethically desirable, their success ultimately hinges on ensuring their clinical relevance.

Personalized patient preference predictors: a primer

The challenge of determining medical preferences for incapacitated patients remains a critical issue in clinical decision-making. In the absence of ADs, clinicians and surrogates are tasked with approximating patient’s preferences and make treatment decisions respecting them. However, an increasing body of research has shown that surrogates often struggle with accuracy, leading to decisions that may not fully reflect the patient’s true preferences. Eighteen years ago, scholars began exploring whether rule-based approaches could offer a solution to this problem [14]. After Rid and Wendler’s [2] proposal, this approach was named the ‘patient preference predictor,’ in what follows: PPP or P3 [2, 6]. The PPP approach promotes the use of algorithms (either deterministic or probabilistic) to implement these rules. Since its inception, PPP has undergone extensive scrutiny, with efforts focused on enhancing their personalization by expanding data collection and leveraging more and more advanced modelling techniques. Despite technical differences, all these efforts, which we collectively denote as ‘Px,’ aim to predict treatment preferences across a wide range of clinical scenarios where ADs are not completed or not available, spanning sudden incapacitation due to multi-trauma or stroke to progressive decision-making impairments seen in conditions such as dementia, multiple sclerosis, or anorexia nervosa. In the following sections, we examine three prominent examples, highlighting what distinguishes them conceptually. Shalowitz et al.’s [14] and Rid and Wendler’s [2] P3

In 2007, Shalowitz et al. proposed the ‘population-based treatment indicator,’ a method to predict patient treatment preferences in emergencies without an AD, using the known preferences of individuals similar to the patient [14]. They motivated it by drawing on clinical cases like the Quinlan and Cruzan, both involving patients in persistent vegetative states reliant on life-sustaining interventions, such as artificial nutrition and ventilation [15, 16], as well as vignettes involving Alzheimer’s disease and major stroke [14].

In 2010, Rid and Wendler elaborated on the Shalowitz et al.’s proposal introducing the P3, an algorithmic method to estimate patient preferences based on statistical patterns [2]. Drawing on individualized sociodemographic and physical, psychological, and social functioning factors—e.g., perceived quality of life, attitudes and values, and relevant personal experiences [6]—the P3 would compute category-level treatment predictions, allowing answering questions such as “should a patient with severe Alzheimer disease be resuscitated?” by enforcing rules like “if patient X is classified into category Y—based on their personalized data—then their predicted outcome is ‘do not resuscitate,’ since this is the most common outcome among individuals in Y.” P3’s lack of personalized outcomes raised concerns about relying on group-level patterns to infer individual preferences, a limitation that subsequent models aimed to overcome.

Personalizing predictions with machine learning: Ferrario et al.’s [4] P3.5

Ferrario et al.’s [4] ‘P3.5’ advances P3 by proposing the use of machine learning and natural language processing on the same data as P3 and clinical notes to compute personalized predictions of goal-care preferences [4]. By collecting individuals’ perceived burden, outcome, and the likelihood of that outcome, the P3.5 would also predict the minimum or maximum likelihood at which individuals would consent to treatment and, when applicable, the minimum number of years of survival for which they would accept an invasive treatment [4]—see footnote vi and Section ‘Data Collection’ therein. Because this would require multiple models, aggregating outcomes may be needed to reduce complexity [4]. Digital tools improving ADs are envisioned to collect such data at scale [17]. Finally, while Ferrario et al. propose intensive care a use case for the P3.5, the model is broadly framed and applicable across various contexts, including emergency care, palliative care, and dementia [4].

More data and predictions for higher personalization: Biller-Andorno and Biller’s [10] proposal and Earp et al.’s [5] P4

In 2019, Biller-Andorno and Biller introduced the idea of using vast amounts of individual-level data to train an AI to make predictions on individual care preferences [10], proposing to improve these personalized predictions comparing patterns within an individual’s past decision-making to patterns and decisions of other people [10]. They outlined three use cases—self-reflection on advance care choices, surrogate deliberations on presumed will, and clinician decisions in the absence of stated preferences—and discussed the ethical and methodological challenges of this approach [10].

The use of massive datasets and advanced AI methods to predict treatment preferences has recently found its latest expression in the P4 model proposal by [5], which would leverage Large Language Models trained on person-specific corpora of text to create a ‘digital psychological twin’ of an individual [5]. Building on previous models like the P3, P3.5 and Biller & Biller-Andorno’s proposal, P4 could retrieve the patient’s most likely treatment preferences in the event of a healthcare emergency, estimating also how the patient would prefer treatment decisions to be made on their behalf during periods of incapacitation, and the level of confidence the individual expresses in their stated preferences [5]. In particular, to fine-tune the LLM, (re)train the P4 and generate these predictions, Earp et al. [20] outlined an extensive range of potential training data sources—including personal communications, surveys, and surrogate input, see Table 1 in [5]—that could be integrated in hospital electronic health record systems. To motivate the design of P4, Earp et al. [20] discussed a case involving a patient in a persistent vegetative state dependent on artificial nutrition [5]. This integration of different data sources, prediction tasks, and modeling capabilities, establishes P4 as the most sophisticated Px model proposed to date.

The challenge of replacing ADs with Px-generated treatment predictions

Where do we stand in the Px race?

Since its inception, Px has ignited lively debates in epistemology, ethics, and human-AI interactions. The literature on the topic is now rather extensive [2, 4–6, 8–12, 18, 19]. Discussed challenges include the management of (racial) bias and discrimination in data collection and modelling, the ‘naked statistical evidence’ issue, the empirical validations of Px-based systems with patients, surrogates, and clinicians, the need for transparency and safety, and the impact on patient autonomy among others [9, 12, 18–20]. These challenges affect all Px proposals to varying degrees and are particularly relevant for its more recent expressions, e.g., the P3.5 and P4.

In general, however, this debate closely mirrors the broader discourse on AI ethics and trustworthy AI. Most Px discussions offer scenario-independent recommendations drawn from general medical AI literature. As a result, the debate often lacks clinical specificity and remains focused on generic machine learning or AI challenges rather than issues arising from real-world implementation of Px models in specific care contexts.

That said, the goal of the Px model proponents remains simple: by properly addressing these challenges, a well-designed Px implementation in settings where ADs are unavailable and patients are incapacitated could improve predictive accuracy, thereby promoting goal-concordant care and reducing the risk of under- or over-treatment, and alleviate the emotional burden on surrogates [4, 5]. In what follows, we examine whether this promise holds in practice.

Why do we still have no Px implementation in clinical practice? The “substitutive design fallacy”

The original proposal by [14] is now 18 years old of age. Given the intense work on Px models, a critical question remains: Why has no Px model been successfully implemented in clinical practice?

Rather than risking the pursuit of even more complex Px models—e.g., by adding neurodata streams or multi-system AI agents—it is time to confront the barriers to the clinical implementation of Px-based systems. These barriers include a few unresolved epistemological and ethical concerns, but also pragmatic demands: interdisciplinary teams, engaged clinicians, patients and surrogates, and a a robust infrastructure. Clinician skepticism and limited public acceptance—e.g., predicting Do-Not-Attempt-To-Resuscitate status [11], further complicate deployment. Yet these challenges reflect broader obstacles to clinical AI adoption, not unique flaws of Px.

We focus on a conceptual barrier to Px clinical implementation: the ‘substitutive design fallacy.’ This fallacy frames Px models as digital replica of ADs designed to predict a limited set of high-level, context-independent treatments for use when ADs or surrogates are unavailable. While this framing has initially helped position Px as an alternative to inaccessible ADs and flawed surrogate decisions, as shown by Shalowitz et al. [14] and, later, Rid and Wendler [2, 14], it has since constrained Px design. This would risk reproducing ADs’ limitations and overlooking the factors that make preference-sensitive decisions both clinically actionable and ethically sound.

First, the substitutive design fallacy limits Px models to the same narrow set of high-level treatment preferences found in ADs, e.g., resuscitation, ventilation, and artificial nutrition. However, as Shalowitz et al. [14] acknowledge no advance directive can account for all clinical scenarios [14]. Despite this, the Px literature lacks critical reflection on which treatments should be predicted and why.

Further, under this fallacy, Px models remain largely clinical context-agnostic, treating treatment preferences as transferable across clinical scenarios. Yet preferences vary depending on by whom, when, where and how are formed. For instance, cultural and belief system-related barriers affect the effectiveness of ADs, limiting their use among different ethnicities and social groups [21, 22]. Preferences expressed while healthy are vulnerable to projection bias and affective forecasting errors—healthy individuals often mispredict how they will value life with serious disability (“disability paradox”) [23, 24]. If Px tools are to be used simply as proxies for ADs, these interrelated biases and challenges must be addressed during model training and after deployment. At present, doing so is difficult, and no definitive solutions are available. Further, ADs can be filled out at home after witnessing a neighbor’s stroke, in primary care with minimal guidance, or in structured advance care planning sessions in long-term care facilities. Each setting shapes different levels of reflection, understanding, and interpersonal input, making the relevance and applicability of those preferences context-dependent. Ignoring this variability risks producing misleading predictions. This is because clinical decision-making is not monolithic; the demands of making urgent decisions in neuro-intensive care differ from those involved in managing progressive dementia, psychiatric illness, or end-of-life care. Each setting entails distinct stakeholders, uncertainties, and ethical challenges. A Px system designed to predict whether a patient wants ‘mechanical ventilation’ may be irrelevant without reference to the prognosis, potential reversibility, or quality of life implications of that decision. Such contextual factors must be embedded upstream in the design of Px models, particularly during data collection and preference elicitation phases. This, in turn, requires explicitly specifying in detail the clinical scenario in which a given Px model is intended to be used. Otherwise, we face a ‘chicken-and-egg’ dilemma: without clarity about the intended point of application of a Px model in clinical practice, it becomes impossible to determine which data to collect, which preferences to elicit and why. Ultimately, the consequence is a mismatch between what Px predicts and what clinicians and surrogates must actually deliberate as decision regret often stems not from the treatment choice itself, but from its downstream consequences—functional outcomes, complications, or diminished autonomy [25]. Most recent Px proposals hint at this by suggesting that preferences could be anchored in likely outcomes [4, 5, 26], but this idea remains largely unexplored in practice.

Giving Px a chance: from general treatment preference tool to a contextualized design

To support to clinical decision-making, Px models must do more than mimic the informational content of ADs albeit with advanced algorithms. Predicting a few, context-independent treatments risks making Px a theoretically promising, yet practically ineffective solution that fails addressing the limitations of ADs.

The substitutive design fallacy is a clinical system design problem. Thus, the path forward lies in improving our design methodologies to align Px systems with both ethical requirements and practical demands of real-world clinical decision-making. Rather than replicating ADs, we envision Px models should serve as decision-support tools offering clinical support to individuals who, for various reasons, did not complete an AD, by facilitating shared decision-making with their surrogates and clinicians. We argue that this goal can be achieved in three design steps:

Identifying which decisions are most relevant and preference-sensitive in a given clinical context and along different clinical trajectories;

Identifying which decisions are most relevant and preference-sensitive in a given clinical context and along different clinical trajectories;

Selecting the decisions benefitting from algorithmic predictions in that context; and

By following (1)-(3) we can then guide the more technical steps, e.g., how to elicit preference, collect data, enforce transparency and fairness, that are discussed in the P3.5 and P4 literature, albeit in a clinical context-independent way [4, 5]. Note that, here, the term ‘decision’ refers to medical interventions, i.e., treatments, that are single or compounded and that need to be administered at a given point of time. For instance, in emergency medicine, a Px model might be limited to predicting only a personalized “Do Not Attempt Resuscitation” status whenever the decision ‘to resuscitate (or not)’ needs to be made. This model could be trained on AD-like data collected from the general population, as this decision often must be made rapidly under severe time constraints. In contrast, a Px system designed for long-term care settings, such as nursing homes treating patients with dementia, might be oriented toward supporting quality-of-life-related decisions over time, due to the chronic nature of cognitive decline and the longitudinal involvement of caregivers, surrogates, and clinicians. It may intervene in decisions such as whether to combine antibiotic treatment and hospital transfer during recurrent infections, helping estimate the patient’s likely preferences regarding the overall care trajectory rather than isolated treatments.

This system-design-informed perspective on the future of Px resonates with the principles of human-centered AI, which emphasizes usability, ethical alignment, and human agency throughout the design process, while remaining attentive to the specificities of the socio-technical contexts in which these systems are intended to be used [27, 28]. Frameworks such as Value Sensitive Design [29] and the adherence to the International Patient Decision Aid Standards [30] can help follow the design steps (1)-(3) by guiding the design of Px systems that fulfill their promise of promoting goal-concordant care and easing the emotional burden on surrogates.

To illustrate this perspective, we examine a specific use case: how Px can be adapted to support real-world decision-making in neuro-intensive care—a domain where time-sensitive medical decisions demand a Px that is not just theoretically sound but clinically actionable. We show this in the forthcoming section.

Use case: designing Px to support severe acute brain injury decision-making

Severe acute brain injury: an overview

Severe Acute Brain Injury (SABI) refers to sudden, serious neurological events such as stroke, traumatic brain injury, infections, and status epilepticus [13, 25]. Patients are typically unconscious and require ICU care [25, 31]. Severe traumatic brain injury affects 5.5 million people annually, and ICU admissions for stroke are rising, including among younger patients [32–35]. Survivors often face long-term cognitive and functional impairments, including memory loss, decision-making difficulties, and reduced mobility [36–39]. These disabilities severely affect quality of life due to the extensive rehabilitation needs, psychological stress, increased demand for caregiver support, and substantial associated costs [40, 41].

Making decisions about treatments in neuro-intensive care

Respecting the preferences of individuals with SABI is clinically and ethically complex. A gap between preferred and received care has been documented [25, 42–44]. Below, we examine three interrelated factors contributing to this gap.

First, SABI encompasses diverse conditions, each with distinct clinical trajectories and evolving decision points. Identifying when preference-sensitive decisions arise and which of them are suitable for Px-based support is critical yet remains an open challenge. For instance, in aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH), a series of rapid, high-stakes decisions must be made at various stages [45]. A decision timeline for aSAH—a structured depiction of preference-sensitive decision moments [45]—illustrates that following the initial hemorrhage, urgent neurosurgical or neuroradiological interventions are often required. While these interventions are essential for survival, they carry long-term implications for neurological recovery. As the condition progresses, complications such as rebleeding, delayed cerebral infarctions, cerebral vasospasms, and extracerebral complications necessitate reassessments of prognosis and treatment pathways [45]. These timelines reveal multiple decision points where patient preferences could guide care yet determining which of these qualify for Px-based interventions remains an unresolved question.

Second, SABI care involves a wide range of medical interventions. For instance, in the case of aSAH, these include neurosurgical or neuroradiological procedures such as clipping, coiling to secure ruptured aneurysms, as well as providing respiratory and cardiovascular support, including mechanical ventilation and hemodynamic stabilization. Intracranial pressure management, avoiding secondary injuries, antibiotic therapy, temperature management, and nutritional support are also essential components of care [46]. SABI treatments varies significantly in terms of invasiveness, clinical needs, and routine applications. While some interventions, such as hemodynamic monitoring, are considered standard practice, others, like neurosurgical procedures, are disease-specific, invasive and preference sensitive. In general, ADs and Px studies investigating the accuracy of surrogates at predicting next-of-kin’s preferences focus on major, high-stakes decisions—such as cardio-pulmonary resuscitation, dialysis, or artificial feeding—see, for instance, Table 1 in [14]. As a result, Px literature typically frames these cases in terms of predicting patient preferences. However, a challenge remains: determining which treatments or treatment combinations should be explicitly considered in a Px model for SABI patients. It is neither practical nor meaningful to elicit preferences for every possible intervention—along with their likelihood and burden—given the highly variable clinical trajectories of SABI patients [45]. Further, some treatments require detailed explanations, which conflicts with the need for digital tools eliciting preferences to remain clinically actionable yet understandable to all their users [4, 17, 26, 47]. Consequently, not only the timing of Px interventions for SABI patients but also the scope of decision-making they support remains an open avenue for research.

Lastly, decision-making for individuals with SABI involves multiple stakeholders under time pressure. Clinicians must act swiftly, while surrogates may be emotionally overwhelmed and lack clear knowledge of the patient’s wishes [46]. In many cases, there is no opportunity to gather detailed information about the patient’s values and preferences, such as through standardized questionnaires or structured interviews, which could otherwise help mitigate uncertainty in the absence of an AD. However, Px models are promoted primarily to predict treatment preferences, not to provide guidance on the broader implications of those treatments. How Px-based tools can effectively support decision-making while balancing the need to select a treatment while considering the future realities of a SABI patient remains an open question.

Supporting decision-making for SABI with Px: Recommendations

The unique demands of neuro-intensive care for SABI patients necessitate adapting existing Px approaches and overcoming the substitutive design fallacy. Below, we present two key recommendations aimed at bridging the gap between general design principles for Px and the needs of their clinical implementation by following the three design steps we introduced in Sect. The Challenge of Replacing ADs with Px-generated Treatment Predictions.

I. Using timelines to identify preference-sensitive decisions and when using Px

Timelines—such as Göcking et al.’s for aSAH [45]—can map SABI patient journeys to identify key moments when Px model’s preferences should inform care. These moments include the transition from curative to palliative care or the decision to initiate long-term life-sustaining interventions. This approach allows for a more targeted application of Px, shifting from predicting treatment preferences whenever this decision may arise to identifying when these predictions are most appropriate. Timelines also support longitudinal modeling that reflect the evolving prognosis throughout the acute phase. Since uncertainty is typically highest early on, and the expected level of disability can often only be estimated later (often after a wake-up trial), repeated reassessment is essential. This helps ensure that Px tools remain clinically relevant over time and can contribute to avoiding self-fulfilling prophecies, where early, uncertain predictions shape downstream decisions. In fact, when care is withdrawn based on early prognostication, poor outcomes may follow not from the illness alone but from the treatment decision itself. As a result, datasets used to train Px models may embed biased outcome labels, reflecting early decisions made under uncertainty rather than clinical trajectories. Strategies such as deferred prognostication, re-evaluation after clinical milestones in the timeline, and the inclusion of cases where intensive treatment was continued despite poor early prognosis can help mitigate this bias in both model development and clinical practice.

II. Focus on predicting outcomes and levels of disabilities with Px

Following steps (2)-(3), instead of asking individuals to speculate on complex, technical interventions they may not fully understand, a more meaningful approach is to elicit outcome-based preferences, focusing on what levels of cognitive and functional impairment they or their loved ones would find acceptable after a SABI.

Px can be trained on data collected via digital tools like SABI-specific plug-ins of the Advance Care Compass [26, 47], capturing cognitive and functional impairment levels at scale. Cognitive disability can be described using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment [48], enriched with daily-life descriptions inspired by the American Stroke Association’s post-stroke cognitive challenge and validated by ICU and rehabilitation experts [49]. Functional disability can be described using the modified Rankin Scale [50], widely used across SABI cases for its strong correlation with the Glasgow Outcome Scale for traumatic brain injury [51]. Digital tools for data collection used by Px would allow individuals to explore different levels of cognitive and functional disability before expressing (1) the maximum levels of impairment they would personally accept because of undergoing treatments for SABI, (2) their expected quality of life, and (3) confidence in their choices [4, 5, 11]. Further, in designing Px tools for SABI, integrating the patient’s perspective is critical. Evidence from studies such as [25, 40] highlights priorities expressed by SABI patients following discharge and during rehabilitation, particularly regarding long-term outcomes such as functional recovery and disability levels and decisional regret. In particular, the systematic collection of their cultural and religious preferences as well as racial background—well established as relevant to end-of-life decision-making [52, 53] and critical to avoiding bias and algorithmic discrimination—can be integrated into periodic nursing assessments with surrogates. Altogether, this approach would allow individuals to reflect on what they consider an acceptable post-treatment existence, rather than making choices about isolated, and often overly technical medical procedures. In fact, in neuro-intensive care, clinicians must make decisions about specific treatments. However, these discussions with surrogates are often framed around the potential long-term functional and cognitive impairments that may arise if the treatment is successful. Then, integrating outcome-sensitive Px predictions at key decision points (see Point I) supports structured yet human-centered interactions with surrogates that align with how people naturally consider their own and their loved ones’ future well-being. We present an example of this interaction in Box 1, where we elaborate on the case of a conversation between neuro intensive clinicians and the next-of-kin of a hemorrhagic stroke patient.

“Clinicians assess the condition of a patient who has suffered a hemorrhagic stroke and requires intensive care. After surgically reducing the brain hemorrhage, they note clinical deterioration and the need for further interventions. They inform the patient’s next of kin, explaining that certain treatments are necessary to stabilize the situation. However, they also clarify that the most likely outcome of these interventions would be a moderately severe disability or worse.

They describe this prognosis to the next of kin in concrete terms, including the inability to walk independently and the need for assistance with basic bodily functions. At this point, the clinicians introduce the Px prediction for the patient, given their current clinical situation, to their next of kin. They explain that, according to the model, the patient would likely assess the outcome of a future moderately severe disability or worse as associated with (1) a low quality of life, but (2) still hold a preference for a (mildly) curative approach. Based on this, they state that proceeding with the treatments aligns with the patient’s presumed preferences. They then invite the next of kin to discuss the case together.”

Conclusions

Despite their promise and multiple technical expressions, personalized patient preference predictors have yet to see clinical adoption. As we have argued, this results from a lack of clinical relevance stemming from a conceptual problem that we named substitutive design fallacy. In contexts like SABI, the challenge is not just predicting preferences of overly technical or even high-level treatments, but providing timely, outcome-sensitive insights that reflect what patients would consider an acceptable quality of life. While our work does not present new quantitative data, clinically valid and ethically grounded evidence cannot be gathered until foundational conceptual issues are addressed. Our approach aims to close this gap by clarifying the design premises and clinical role of Px in the ICU for SABI cases. Rather than additional Px proposals, we need empirical studies that determine what model works, for whom, and under what conditions. These studies should prioritize usability and effectiveness with careful evaluations that include clinicians, (ex-)patients and their surrogates. Here, caution is essential: hasty implementations or even premature commercialization would quickly undermine trust and patient autonomy. However, with thoughtful design and clinical grounding, the goal of improving goal-concordant care with algorithmic solutions can move closer to reality.
