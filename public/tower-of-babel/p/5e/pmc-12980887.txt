Genetic blood disorder status awareness and factors influencing willingness or reluctance to undergo premarital screening among young adults in Ahafo Region, Ghana: a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Ma. Lydia Sarpong, Richard Joshua Tetteh, Desmond Kuupiel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGenetic disorders like sickle cell disease (SCD), Thalassemia, and Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency significantly impact social, health, financial, and healthcare systems, affecting 2%-5% of live births, 30% of pediatric hospitalisations, and 50% of juvenile deaths. Premarital carrier screening (PMCS) is a critical preventive measure, yet it remains unexplored in Ghana’s Ahafo Region. This study examined young adults’ knowledge and factors influencing willingness or unwillingness to undergo PMCS for genetic blood disorders.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 460 young adults (18–35 years) in health facilities in the Ahafo Region, Ghana, from January to March 2024. Data was collected using a validated and translated questionnaire to capture socio-demographic characteristics, knowledge of genetic disorders, and factors influencing willingness to undergo PMCS. Data analysis was performed using Stata v14, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Chi-square tests were used to assess associations between socio-demographic factors and willingness to undergo PMCS. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify predictors of willingness, presenting results as odds ratios (OR) with corresponding p-values.ResultsAmong the 460 respondents, most (n = 294; 63.9%) knew their SCD status, but fewer were aware of their G6PD (n = 418; 90.9%) and Thalassemia (n = 441; 95.9%) statuses. A significant number had not tested for SCD (n = 303; 65.9%), G6PD (n = 421; 91.5%), or Thalassemia (n = 446; 97.0%). Among the 460 respondents, 88.5% expressed willingness to undergo PMCS. A chi-square test revealed that sex (χ² = 11.481, p = 0.0032)., education level (χ² = 8.428, p = 0.0379), and parental consanguinity (χ² = 14.336, p = 0.0136) were significantly associated with willingness to undergo PMCS. Logistic regression analysis revealed higher willingness among females [OR = 4.3, p = 0.002], cohabiting individuals [OR = 0.3, p = 0.021], married individuals [OR = 0.1, p = 0.001], self-employed [OR = 0.2, p = 0.047], unemployed [OR = 0.2, p = 0.037], and those informed through school subjects [OR = 0.2, p = 0.036].ConclusionDespite low awareness of genetic disorders, willingness to undergo PMCS is high among young adults in the Ahafo Region, highlighting the need for integrated, culturally tailored public health strategies, particularly through school-based education to improve uptake and reduce the burden of hereditary conditions.

Background

Hereditary disorders, such as Sickle Cell Disease (SCD), Thalassemia, and Glucose 6 Phosphate Dehydrogenase deficiency (G6PD), remain a major global public health concern due to their widespread geographical distribution, large affected population, and significant health and socio-economic consequences ADDIN EN.CITE [1, 2]. A Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study suggests a persistent rise in the incidence of inherited anemias over 44 million new cases in 2019 with Central Sub-Saharan Africa recorded the highest incidence rates, whereas Central Latin America showed the greatest decline over time ADDIN EN.CITE [1].

The increasing incidence of hereditary blood disorders is partially attributed to marriages between carriers or affected individuals [3]. One proposed strategy to reduce the occurrence of these genetic abnormalities is premarital screening [3]. Premarital screening is highly recommended to prevent genetic disorders from being passed to offspring [4]. However, many couples choose not to undergo screening before marriage, which can have serious implications for their children’s health [4]. In addition to preventing hereditary diseases, premarital screening is recognised as a method for promoting the health and well-being of both mothers and future children [4]. It also serves as an early detection tool for genetic conditions like hearing loss and other disorders [4].

Hereditary conditions like SCD, Thalassemia, and G6PD deficiency often require extensive medical care, which can be costly and time-consuming, impacting patients’ productivity and education [5]. For example, a study in southern Ghana found that 10.6% of school-age children had phenotypical G6PD deficiency ADDIN EN.CITE [6]. These conditions can often be fatal but are preventable if couples undergo premarital screening to determine their genetic status before marriage ADDIN EN.CITE [7].

Despite the recognised benefits, premarital screening remains underutilised. In 2024, a meta-analysis revealed that the overall uptake of premarital sickle cell trait screening across Africa was approximately 48%, with considerable variation between countries ADDIN EN.CITE [8]. Factors such as lack of awareness, the cost of screening, and socio-cultural beliefs significantly impact the uptake of screening services. The study found a positive relationship between SCD awareness and the likelihood of screening, where a unit increase in awareness corresponded to a 0.729 rise in the odds of undergoing screening (β = 0.729, p = 0.004) ADDIN EN.CITE [8]. These findings are consistent with other research showing that awareness plays a critical role in the decision to undergo premarital screening even when a policy exists [9].

In Ghana, approximately 2% of newborns are diagnosed with SCD annually ADDIN EN.CITE [10]. Also, a previous study indicated that the prevalence of phenotypical G6PD deficiency among school-age children was nearly 11% in Southern Ghana ADDIN EN.CITE [6]. In the Ahafo Region of Ghana, a total of 3117 new cases were diagnosed between 2021 and 2023 (1,293 in 2021; 1,117 in 2022; and 767 in 2023 [11]. To help develop a socio-culturally acceptable intervention for promoting genetic blood disorder awareness and testing before marriage in the context of Ahafo Region, this study seeks to assess awareness of genetic blood disorder status and the factors influencing the willingness or reluctance to undergo premarital screening among young adults in the region.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted between January and March 2024 to assess awareness of genetic blood disorders and factors influencing the willingness or reluctance to undergo premarital screening among young adults in the Ahafo Region.

Study setting

This study was conducted in the Ahafo Region of Ghana, which was officially established on February 13, 2019, with Goaso as its capital. Geographically, the region is bordered by the Bono Region to the north and west, the Ashanti Region to the east, and the Western North Region to the south. It lies within Ghana’s forest belt and is characterised by fertile grasslands, drought-resistant trees such as acacias and baobabs, and a predominantly agricultural economy. The region spans an approximate location of latitude 6.8125° N and longitude −2.6167° W.

According to the 2021 population and housing census, the region has an estimated population of 588,633, with an annual growth rate of 2.1%. [12]. While no official breakdown of the youth population is available at the regional level, national estimates suggest that roughly 32% of Ghana’s population is between the ages of 15 and 34, which would imply an estimated 180,000–190,000 young adults residing in Ahafo Region constituting the main target population for this study [12].

The region consists of six districts and 27 sub-districts, with 117 health institutions, including 13 hospitals (6 government, 2 Christian Health Association of Ghana (CHAG), 1 Islamic, and 4 private), 22 health centres (21 government and 1 private), 17 clinics (4 government, 5 CHAG, and 8 private), 3 private maternity homes, and 62 Community-based Health Planning and Service (CHPS) compounds. Despite this network, access to specialised genetic screening services remains limited ADDIN EN.CITE [13]. Based on records from the Ahafo Regional Health Directorate, only a few hospitals [4] are equipped to provide genetic counselling or laboratory services for screening Sickle Cell Disease (SCD), Thalassemia, or G6PD deficiency. Most residents seeking genetic testing must travel to tertiary centres outside the region, such as Sunyani or Kumasi. The doctor-to-population ratio in the region is approximately 1:13,307, while the nurse-to-population ratio stands at 1:339, reflecting significant disparities in skilled healthcare workforce availability for preventive services like premarital genetic screening.

Sources of public knowledge on genetic disorders in the Ahafo Region are also varied. Findings from a previous study reveal that the most common source of information on premarital screening is family and friends (50.7%), followed by healthcare services (27.8%), the media (14.6%), and school subjects (6.9%). These patterns suggest that informal networks and community interactions are critical conduits of health information in the region ADDIN EN.CITE [14].

The 2021 Population and Housing Census (PHC) report suggest that the literacy rate for the Ahafo Region was 68.6%, which is just below the national average literacy rate of 69.8% for Ghana (based on persons 6 years and older) and lower than more urbanised regions such as Greater Accra and Ashanti (where literacy rates exceed 75%) per the 2021 PHC literacy and education report [12].

Study population and sample size

The study targeted young adults aged 18 to 35 years and residents in the Ahafo Region, who were willing to participate and were attending health facilities (hospitals clinics, health centres, and maternity homes) across different districts and subdistricts during the study period. Participants were included after being informed about the research purpose and communicated in either the local dialect or English.

The sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula for a single population proportion: n = Z²pq/d² [15], where:

For this study, p was set at 0.5 (50%) since there were no prior population-level studies in the Ahafo Region reporting awareness or willingness to undergo premarital genetic screening. Using 50% as the proportion provides the maximum variability and yields the largest required sample size, thereby ensuring sufficient statistical power [16].

Based on these parameters, the initial sample size was calculated to be 384 participants. To account for non-response, the sample size was increased by 20%, which is a commonly accepted adjustment in community-based surveys in sub-Saharan Africa where response rates vary due to literacy, consent hesitancy, or logistical barriers [12, 17]. The adjusted sample size became approximately 460 (that is, 384 × 1.2).

In addition, considering that a multi-stage sampling technique was employed, a design effect (DEFF) of 1.5 was also factored in to account for potential clustering within health facilities and districts. Therefore, the final calculated sample size became 576 (that is, 384 × 1.5). However, due to resource and logistical constraints, we surveyed 460 young adults, which was deemed sufficient based on the initial calculation and non-response adjustment. This was also supported by the relatively high homogeneity of the target population (young adults aged 18–35 years accessing health facilities within the selected districts), which reduces intra-cluster variability.

Sampling strategy

To ensure a representation from a diverse range of healthcare facilities, a multi-stage sampling approach was employed to select participants from various health facilities in the Ahafo Region.

¬ Stage One: Three out of the six districts in the region were randomly selected using a simple random sampling method (ballot). The districts chosen were Tano North, Asunafo North, and Asutifi South.

¬ Stage Two: Ten health facilities representing different levels of care were randomly selected by ballot from three districts. These facilities included 2 hospitals, 2 clinics, 5 health centres, and 1 private maternity home.

¬ Stage Three: From each of the selected facilities, 46 participants were included in the study using a convenience sampling method. The first 46 adults, aged 18–35, who visited the health facilities, agreed to participate and provided informed consent were enrolled in the study.

Data collection

Data for this study were collected using a structured and validated questionnaire adapted from previous research by Brown et al. [16]. The questionnaire was slightly modified to suit the current study’s context, with minor adjustments made to the demographic-related questions. Trained research assistants supported the lead investigator in administering the structured questionnaire to respondents.

To ensure clarity, the survey questions were translated into the local Twi dialect and then back-translated into English to maintain the accuracy of the content. The questionnaire collected sociodemographic information, including gender, age, ethnicity, marital status, educational level, monthly income, employment status, and the nature of family and parental relationships. Additionally, respondents were asked about their understanding of premarital carrier screening (PMCS), covering topics like SCD, G6PD, and Thalassemia, as well as their knowledge of their own genetic status and their willingness or reluctance to undergo premarital screening for genetic blood disorders.

The lead investigator or research assistants approached potential participants who met the inclusion criteria, explained the study’s purpose, and invited them to participate. Those who agreed were then given the questionnaire to complete.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using Stata version 14 (Stata Corp. LLC, Texas, USA) [18]. Prior to analysis, all entries were double-entered, and random spot checks were performed to ensure data accuracy and integrity. All 460 respondents who completed the questionnaire were included in both descriptive and inferential statistical analyses. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarise participants’ socio-demographic characteristics, awareness of genetic blood disorders, screening history, and willingness to undergo premarital genetic screening (PMCS). Associations between socio-demographic variables and willingness to undergo PMCS were assessed using Chi-sq uare (χ²) tests. Variables with p-values %3 C 0.2 from bivariate analysis were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to determine independent predictors of willingness. Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with all values below 2.5, indicating no concern. Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (p > 0.05), and Nagelkerke R² was reported to assess the model’s explanatory power. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and all tests were two-tailed.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study file 2023-33 was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Kintampo Health Research Centre (KHRC IEC) to obtain ethical clearance. Permission from Ahafo Regional Directorate and the managers of health facilities involved in this study. Each respondent also provided written informed permission and was made aware of his or her freedom to leave the study at any time without facing repercussions.

Results

A total of 460 structured questionnaires were administered to eligible young adults across the Ahafo Region, and all were fully completed and returned, yielding a 100% response rate. The findings presented below reflect this full sample (N = 460) and provide insights into the socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents, their awareness of genetic blood disorders, and the factors influencing their willingness or reluctance to undergo premarital genetic screening (PMCS).

Socio-demographic characteristics of the study respondents

Table 1 presents the socio-demographic characteristics of the 460 respondents. Most participants were female (n = 394; 85.6%), with most aged between 18 and 25 years (n = 272; 59.1%), and a mean age of 24.7 ± 4.7 years. More than half of the respondents were single (n = 264; 57.4%), and the majority were of Akan ethnicity (n = 379; 82.4%). Most respondents had attained tertiary education (n = 203; 44.1%) and were unemployed (n = 242; 52.7%). Regarding income, 63.7% of respondents reported earning less than 500 Ghana cedis per month.

Respondents’ willingness or reluctance to undergo PMCS for genetic blood disorders and reasons

Of the 460 respondents, 407 (88.5%) expressed willingness to undergo PMCS. Among respondents who expressed willingness to undergo PMCS for genetic blood disorders, the primary reasons cited were to prevent transmission of genetic conditions to future offspring (52.4%), to prevent transmission to oneself (17.4%), to ensure fitness for marriage (15.8%), and to ensure a healthy partner (14.4%). These reasons varied modestly across socio-demographic, personal, and informational characteristics.

Notably, individuals with tertiary education more frequently reported prevention of transmission to offspring (66.5%) as their main reason, while those with junior high school or lower education more often selected ensuring fitness for marriage (18.0%) and partner health (16.4%). Participants with a personal history of hereditary blood disorders overwhelmingly chose offspring protection (72.0%) as their motivation. Similarly, those aware of a family history were more likely to prioritise preventing transmission to offspring (64.4%).

Sources of information also influenced motivations. Respondents who learned about PMCS through school or healthcare services prioritised prevention of hereditary transmission to offspring (65.4% and 60.2%, respectively), whereas those informed via media or family/friends showed more distributed motivations.

Variations were also observed by marital status, income level, and relationship between respondents’ parents (i.e., degree of consanguinity). For example, participants with first- or second-degree parental consanguinity leaned more towards preventing transmission to offspring (66.0% and 62.5%, respectively) than other motivations.

Among those willing to undergo screening, the primary motivation was to prevent the transmission of hereditary diseases to their offspring (n = 212; 52.1%). In contrast, the main reasons for reluctance among the 24 respondents were concerns that their families would oppose the continuation of marriage (n = 6; 25%) and fear of social stigma (n = 6; 25%) (Table 3).

In the Ahafo Region of Ghana, among the 24 respondents (5.2%) who expressed reluctance to undergo PMCS for genetic blood disorders, the main reasons were concerns about the social and familial consequences of a positive test result. Specifically, 25.0% feared family opposition to marriage if unfavorable results were disclosed, while another 25.0% cited potential social stigma. Additionally, 20.8% were apprehensive that test outcomes might negatively influence their marital prospects or prevent continuation of marriage. A further 8.3% believed that PMCS would interfere with divine will, indicating religious or spiritual resistance to the practice.

Notably, 29 respondents (6.3%) indicated that they did not know whether they would be willing or unwilling to undergo PMCS, suggesting a need for greater public education.

These findings suggest that, although overall willingness to undergo PMCS was high (88.5%), a small but notable proportion of young adults are influenced by sociocultural, familial, and spiritual concerns, which could undermine the implementation and uptake of genetic screening programmes.

Association between socio-demographic characteristics and willingness to undergo PMCS for genetic blood disorder among young adults in Ahafo Region, Ghana

A chi-square test of independence was performed to examine the association between respondents’ socio-demographic characteristics and their willingness to undergo PMCS for genetic blood disorders (Table 4). The analysis revealed that sex, education level, and parental relationship were significantly associated with willingness to undergo PMCS.

Specifically, females were significantly more likely than males to express willingness to undergo screening (χ² = 11.481, p = 0.0032). Education level also showed a significant association (χ² = 8.428, p = 0.0379), with respondents having tertiary education being more willing to screen than those with lower education levels. Furthermore, parental consanguinity (i.e., relationship between respondents’ parents) was significantly associated with willingness (χ² = 14.336, p = 0.0136), with those from consanguineous unions demonstrating greater likelihood to opt for screening—potentially reflecting heightened perceived risk or awareness.

Other variables such as marital status (χ² = 6.010, p = 0.198), employment status (χ² = 4.954, p = 0.084), and monthly income (χ² = 6.612, p = 0.085) did not show statistically significant associations at the 0.05 level, although employment and income demonstrated borderline significance. Variables including age, personal and family history of hereditary disease, and source of information were not significantly associated with willingness to undergo PMCS (p > 0.1).

These findings underscore the importance of tailored health communication strategies that consider educational level, gender, and cultural factors such as family history and consanguinity in promoting premarital screening uptake.

Discussion

This study assessed the awareness of genetic blood disorder status and the factors influencing willingness or reluctance to undergo premarital screening (PMCS) among young adults in the Ahafo Region of Ghana. The results revealed that most respondents were single females from the Akan ethnic group, with a high level of tertiary education, yet low income and high unemployment. Additionally, most respondents (88.5%) expressed willingness to undergo premarital genetic screening, primarily to prevent the transmission of hereditary diseases to their children. A small percentage of participants were reluctant, citing concerns about family opposition to marriage and fear of social stigma. The study also demonstrated significant associations between sex, educational level, and parental relationship and willingness to undergo PMCS (p < 0.05). Moreover, gender, marital status, employment, and information sources were key determinants of willingness, with females and employed individuals being more likely to participate, while married individuals and those receiving information from school subjects showed lower willingness. These results reinforce that individual health behaviors regarding PMCS are shaped by complex demographic, social, and informational factors.

These results are consistent with previous studies across Africa and the Middle East. For instance, Dilli et al. reported a pooled screening participation rate of 47.8% across Africa, attributing low uptake to poor awareness, cultural beliefs, and limited access ADDIN EN.CITE [8]. Similarly, Oluwole et al. and Edwin et al. found that even when individuals were aware of genetic conditions such as SCD, actual screening remained low due to stigma and misinformation [19, 20]. From a public health standpoint, this highlights the urgent need to enhance early genetic literacy and routine screening, particularly among adolescents and young adults before marriage.

While the high willingness (88.5%) among participants suggests readiness for PMCS, the public health challenge lies in converting this willingness into actual uptake, especially considering that 95.9% were unaware of their Thalassemia status, and 90.9% did not know their G6PD status. Previous studies in Ghana showed similar patterns despite high prevalence, with Amoah et al. finding 12.4% of children in southern Ghana positive for the A– allele of G6PD ADDIN EN.CITE [6], and Owusu-Sarpong reporting about 29% among pregnant women [21]. The public health implication is that individuals may unknowingly pass on preventable hereditary disorders due to the absence of screening infrastructure and education.

Notably, 5.2% of respondents were unwilling or reluctant to undergo PMCS. Their concerns such as fear of marital rejection, social stigma, and religious fatalism mirror those observed in Oman and Nigeria ADDIN EN.CITE [20, 22]. These findings call for culturally sensitive educational strategies that go beyond scientific messaging and actively engage community leaders, religious institutions, and civil society organisations to shift social norms around genetic diseases and premarital screening.

Interestingly, receiving information from school subjects was associated with lower willingness to screen, suggesting that current health education curricula may be failing to resonate. This finding diverges from studies in Oman [23], where school-based genetic health education improved screening uptake. The Ghana Education Service (GES) should therefore consider revising school curricula to include practical and socially relevant genetic counselling modules, ideally integrated into sexuality education and reproductive health.

In addition, the gap in Thalassemia awareness was striking. Basu reported that 53% of Saudi Arabian participants were aware of their Thalassemia status, compared to less than 5% in this study [24]. Meanwhile, a Ghanaian study found that 19.9% of participants were heterozygous and 6.8% homozygous for alpha-Thalassemia [25]. These numbers justify routine population-level screening and public awareness campaigns on non-SCD hereditary conditions, especially since G6PD and Thalassemia are often underrepresented in public discourse.

Implications for public health practice and research

To effectively address the growing burden of hereditary blood disorders, it is imperative that the Ghana Health Service (GHS) and Ministry of Health (MoH) institutionalise PMCS as a standard component of routine pre-marital, antenatal, and adolescent health services. This integration should be linked with genetic counselling services, diagnostic testing, and structured follow-up mechanisms to ensure continuity of care and informed reproductive decisions. Embedding PMCS into existing primary healthcare structures will enhance early identification of at-risk individuals and reduce the incidence of inherited disorders.

In addition to health system integration, education and community engagement must be strengthened. The Ghana Education Service (GES) should revise school curricula to include comprehensive, age-appropriate, and culturally sensitive content on genetic blood disorders such as Sickle Cell Disease (SCD), G6PD deficiency, and Thalassemia. Simultaneously, the National Commission for Civic Education (NCCE) in collaboration with District Assemblies, faith-based organizations, and community leaders should spearhead public awareness campaigns aimed at destigmatizing genetic conditions and promoting the acceptability of PMCS. These efforts should target not only young adults but also families, religious groups, and traditional authorities who often influence marital decisions.

Improving access to screening services is also essential, particularly in rural or underserved communities. The Municipal and District Health Directorates should prioritize decentralisation by equipping Community-Based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) compounds, mobile outreach clinics, and government laboratories with PMCS capabilities. Where possible, testing should be made free or subsidised, with prioritisation for low-income and unemployed individuals to reduce economic barriers to access.

Lastly, further implementation and behavioural research is needed to inform culturally appropriate interventions. Future studies should investigate how partner communication, religious beliefs, family dynamics, and community norms influence decisions about genetic screening. Additionally, implementation research should evaluate the effectiveness of school-based PMCS programmes, point-of-care testing models, and community-led interventions tailored to the Ghanaian context. Such research will guide policy development and help scale up effective interventions nationwide.

Strengths and limitations of the study

A key strength of this study lies in its large, diverse sample (n = 460), which captured both urban and rural populations in the Ahafo Region. The structured questionnaire was adapted from validated instruments and translated into Twi, enhancing comprehension and cultural relevance. However, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference. The use of self-reported data may also introduce recall and social desirability bias, particularly in responses related to willingness and past screening. Although rural–urban inclusion was achieved, stratified sampling by geographic zone was not conducted, which may limit direct comparisons between rural and urban subgroups. Nonetheless, the study provides important baseline data to inform policy initiatives, education, and service delivery reforms in genetic blood disorder prevention in Ghana.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that while awareness of genetic blood disorders such as SCD, G6PD deficiency, and Thalassemia remains low among young adults in the Ahafo Region, willingness to undergo PMCS is high. Socio-demographic factors including sex, marital status, education, employment, and information source significantly influence willingness, with females and employed individuals more likely to participate. Public health stakeholders should act swiftly to leverage this willingness by integrating PMCS into routine services, enhancing genetic literacy through schools and communities, and expanding screening access, especially in underserved regions. Normalising PMCS through culturally responsive strategies and policy integration is key to breaking the cycle of inherited blood disorders in Ghana and similar LMIC settings.
