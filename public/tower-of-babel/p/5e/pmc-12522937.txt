Differential humidity effects on seed viability and oxidative stress responses in Quinoa (Chenopodium Quinoa Willd.) during short-term storage
Authors: Muhammad Hamzeh, Syed Riaz Ahmed, Iram Ijaz, Mahak Naveed, Aftab Ahmad, Anisa Umer, Naeem-Ud Din, Jahangir Khan, Muhammad Ejaz, Munir Ahmed Khetran, Nadeem Sadiq, Yongming Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the effects of relative humidity (RH) and storage duration (SD) on quinoa seed viability, biochemical composition and physiological traits. A factorial experiment in a completely randomized design (CRD) was conducted at 50 °C with two RH levels (30% And 60%) and three storage durations (30, 60, And 90 days). The objective was to evaluate how RH and storage time influence seed viability and identify varietal differences in storage tolerance. Higher RH (60%) accelerated seed viability decline during prolonged storage, as reflected in reduced germination percentage, vigor index, and sharp losses in tetrazolium (TZ) viability, particularly in V5 (91.67% to 9%). Electrical conductivity increased with storage time, indicating membrane damage. Morphological traits (root length, shoot length and biomass) photosynthetic pigments decreased more markedly under 605 RH, with V1 performing poorest and V6 showing relatively better resilience. Antioxidant enzyme (SOD, POD, CAT, APX) were more active under 30% RH, suggesting an adaptive defense response, while proline accumulation was highlighted in V6 (42.73 µmol/g) at 305 RH after 90 days. Overall, prolonged storage under high RH severely compromised quinoa seed quality, whereas 30% RH preserved viability and biochemical integrity. Statistical analyses (ANOVA, PCA and heatmaps) confirmed superior seed performance at 30% RH. These findings provide practical guidance for optimizing quinoa seed storage and highlight V6 as a promising genotype for a long term preservation.

Introduction

Quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa Willd.) is a climate-resilient crop with exceptional nutritional value, gaining global recognition for its adaptability to diverse environmental conditions [1, 2]. The area under quinoa crop cultivation is increasing day by day and therefore, the effective post-harvest handling, storage protocols, seed quality and maintaining genetic diversity is the need of time. Seed storage is a critical determinant of agriculture productivity, germplasm conservation and food security. Maintaining quinoa seed viability and physiological integrity during storage remains a major challenge, as improper storage conditions can lead to biochemical degradation, reduced germination, and impaired seedling establishment, ultimately affecting crop productivity [3, 4]. The key environmental factors influencing seed quality, relative humidity, and temperature play a crucial role in modulating seed longevity by altering moisture dynamics and oxidative stability [5, 6]. Even short-term exposure to suboptimal storage conditions can speed up seed deterioration and leads to reduced germination rate and seedling vigor. Therefore, understanding how seeds respond to fluctuating temperature and humidity conditions, is important for maintaining crop nutritional quality and yield [3].

Seed deterioration is closely associated with oxidative stress, a process triggered by the excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radicals and peroxidase (POD). ROS are known to play signaling roles during seed germination, uncontrolled accumulation during storage can result in lipids, proteins and DNA degradation [7, 8]. Besides antioxidant enzymes, seed also possess some other important molecules like ascorbate and glutathione that mainly act in mitigating oxidative damage [9]. While previous studies have investigated temperature effects on seed storage and reported that long term storage under a constant temperature effected quinoa seed germination by reducing seed water content, significantly reduced chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, carotenoid contents and antioxidant enzymes activities including peroxidase, superoxide dismutase and catalase. Moreover, these studies have also reported that days to germination increased with a significant decreased in germination percentage compared to control [10, 11].

A deeper understanding of quinoa oxidative stress responses under differential humidity is essential for designing effective storage protocols [12, 13]. Even recent studies have suggested that quinoa seeds exhibit variable storage performance depending upon the underlying genetics (genetic background) and port-harvest handling [14], but the better physiological and biochemical understanding of these responses still remains unclear [9, 15]. By connecting humidity induced changes in viability with shifts in oxidative stress markers and antioxidant enzymes activities, new useful insights can be gained into how quinoa seeds balance it metabolic, enzymatic and photosynthetic resilience with environmental vulnerability. Moreover, seed Lifespan decreases significantly with even a 1% rise in moisture content [16]. While seeds can be stored at 5–8% moisture for several years, a content of 14% is too high for most crops [17, 18]. Therefore, the identification of suitable humidity level and temperature for a long and short term storage of quinoa seed is important [19].

While previous studies on seed storage have largely emphasized on germination percentage as the principal indicator of seed viability and such single metric approaches often overlook the biochemical events that precede visible deterioration. To our knowledge, no work has systematically examined how relative humidity interacts with short term accelerated aging to shape quinoa oxidative stress responses. This study addresses that gap by combining controlled HR treatments with accelerated storage to capture early shifts in enzymes (SOD, POD, CAT, APX) and primary and secondary metabolites (reducing sugar, non-reducing sugar, TSP, proline) antioxidant defenses. In addition, we employ heatmap based multivariate analysis to visualize interactive stress response patterns across humidity and duration gradients (an approach not previously applied in quinoa seed research). By integrating germination assays with biochemical profiling, this work move beyond conventional correlation based assessment to reveal humidity specific metabolic adjustment. In doing so, it not only challenges prevailing assumptions about uniform humidity effects but also offers a new framework for rapid evaluation of quinoa seed storage ability.

Materials and methods

Experimental site

Seeds of six accessions, obtained from King Abdullah University of Science and Technology (KAUST), Saudi Arabia, were cultivated at the experimental area of the Department of Agronomy, University of Agriculture Faisalabad, Pakistan.

Experimental layout

Seeds were harvested at the end of April (2022) and immediately subjected to threshing. The cleaned seeds were then placed in hermetically sealed containers with two controlled relative humidity levels (30% And 60%), which were maintained using saturated salt solutions of magnesium chloride (MgCl₂) and aluminum nitrate (Al(NO₃), respectively. Each RH condition was subjected to three different storage durations (SD) namely 30, 60, And 90 days separately (Table 1). The temperature for all humidity levels in all storage durations was maintained at 50 °C. The experiment was laid out in a completely randomized design (CRD) with a factorial arrangement comprising two relative humidity (RH) levels (30% And 60%) and three storage durations (30, 60, And 90 days), each replicated three times. A total of 100 fresh seeds (for each replication, treatment, genotype and days interval) were harvested and exposed to six different treatments (Table 1). During the experimental period the average monthly temperature during April was 42 °C (maximum) 24 °C (minimum) in May was 44 °C (maximum), 29 °C (minimum), And in June was 43 °C (maximum) 30 °C (minimum). The average monthly humidity during April, May And June was recorded by 29%, 29% And 42%, respectively.

Measurement of electrical conductivity (EC)

The EC of the seeds was checked by following the [20] method, the seeds were presoaked in water to ensure the release of different primary And secondary metabolites, sugars, And various ions. Fifty seeds were weighed and washed for 5–10 min with HgCl2 And then washed thoroughly with double distilled water. After that seeds were placed in 50 mL of distilled water at 25 ᴼC for 20 h. Then seeds were taken out with the help of forceps and the EC of water (seed leachates) was checked with the help of a digital EC meter. The EC meter was dipped in each sample and the indicated value on the digital meter was recorded.

Measurement of tetrazolium

The seed tetrazolium level was confirmed to check the viability of seeds. The viability of the seeds mainly relies on the dehydrogenase enzyme activity that curtails the tetrazolium salt and aids in the formation of formazan. Active and viable seeds have more amount of formazan which gives them a red color, while dead seeds have less amount of formazan and appear colorless. This change in color or color pattern gives information about the seed’s health and viability [21]. To assess the viability of seeds, a 1% solution of 2, 3, 5-triphyenyl-2 H-tetrazolium chloride was made in a measured amount of distilled water. After that, the seeds were dipped in that solution at 30 °C for 22 h. Then the seeds were taken out with the help of forceps from the tetrazolium solution and data was noted by placing the stained seeds on the filter paper.

Final germination

A germination test was performed for 100 harvested seeds by following ISTA rules (1995). The seeds were germinated on filter paper in a petri plate under all six treatments with three replicates. 1 st And 2nd count were considered as per the rules of ISTA. The germination % was calculated according to the following formula:

Measurement of growth parameters

After 14 days of growth, the plants germinated in petri plates were harvested for measurement of primary root and shoot length. The primary root and shoot length were measured with the help of a scale. Shoot and root fresh weight and dry weight were noted with the help of a weighing balance.

Measurement of primary and secondary metabolic compounds

For the measurement of chlorophyll, 0.1-gram leaves were taken And homogenized with 80% acetone, And centrifuged at 10,000 rpm And the supernatant was collected. The absorbance of the supernatant was recorded at 663, 645, And 470 [22].

Where V = volume of extract (mL) and W = fresh weight of the sample (g).

Reducing sugar

Reducing sugar was ascertained by the DNS method established by [23]. For this purpose, 0.1 g of plant tissue was immersed in 80% ethanol And then centrifuged at 1200 rpm, And then the supernatant was separated. 1 ml of the extract was then mixed with DNS (3, 5 dinitrosalicylic acid) reagent And heated in the water bath for 5 min. After cooling the absorbance was noted at 540 nm and reducing sugar was calculated by comparing it with the glucose standard curve.

Non-Reducing sugar

Non-reducing sugars were computed by employing the anthrone sulphuric acid reagent method. Anthrone reagent was made by dissolving in sulphuric acid (0.2% in sulphuric acid). The plant extract was obtained by homogenizing 0.1 g of plant part in 80% ethanol. The extract of the plant was then mixed with Anthrone reagent And then heated in the water bath for 10 min at 100 °C And absorbance was taken at 620 nm [24].

Total soluble proteins

TSP was measured using the [25] assay. For this purpose, 0.1 g tissue of plants mixed with a phosphate buffer of 7 pH. The Mixture was then centrifuged And the supernatant was collected. 0.1 ml of extract was then added to 5 ml of Bradford reagent And incubated for 10 min. After that, the absorbance was recorded at 595 nm and compared with the standard curve of bovine serum albumin (BSA).

Antioxidant activities measurement

To discern the Antioxidant enzyme activity 0.25 g of plant samples were homogenized with a phosphate buffer of pH 7. The mixture was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant was separated and then used for different assays. To determine the superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity the inhibition of NBT (nitro blue tetrazolium) was observed at 560 nm. SOD one unit can inhibit 50% of NBT reduction [26]. In addition, Peroxidase (POD) was quantified against the oxidation of guaiacol oxidation. The plant extract was combined with guaiacol and H2O2 for peroxidase activity measurement. An increase in absorbance was then recorded at 470 nm [27]. Nevertheless, for the estimation of catalase (CAT) activity, the extract was assessed by the decline in absorbance at 240 nm as the hydrogen peroxide gradually degraded [28]. Moreover, Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) was determined by following the [29] protocol where a decrease in absorbance at 290 nm was observed as the oxidation of ascorbate continued.

Measurement of proline activity

The proline was quantified by using the method given by [30]. For this purpose, 0.5 g of primary leaf tissues were homogenized or mixed with sulfosalicylic acid (3%), centrifuged, and then the supernatant was collected. After that glacial acetic acid (2 ml) and ninhydrin (2 ml) were added in 2 ml of extract And heated for 1 h at 100 °C. After that it was then extracted with toluene (4 ml) And the absorbance of the toluene layer was observed at 520 nm.

Measurement of malondialdehyde content

Malondialdehyde content were measured using leaf tissue by homogenizing it in 20% trichloroacetic acid And 0.5% thio-barbituric acid. The homogenized solution was then exposed to boiling at 100 °C around 30 min And was cooled down using ice And made ready for measurement. The solution was then taken to spectrophotometer to take absorbance at 532 nm and was applied for calculating MDA content.

Statistical analysis

The analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to find out the significance of treatments and the means were compared using the Tukey’s HSD test. PCA analysis was carried out to discern the variability among studied traits and growing quinoa varieties under different storage duration while heatmap analyses assists in better comprehension regarding quinoa varieties clustering and their positive and negative interplay with observed key traits.

Results

Effect of relative humidity and storage duration on growth parameters

Analysis of variance revealed highly significant effect of relative humidity and days to storage duration on most of the evaluated morpho-physiological traits (Table 2). RH was found to had a strong influence on vigor index (Fig. 1A), final germination percentage (Fig. 1B), days to 50% germination (Fig. 1C), moisture content (Fig. 1D), electrical conductivity (Fig. 1E), TZ% (Fig. 1F), root length, shoot length root fresh weight, shoot fresh weight, root dry weight and shoot dry weight. Storage duration showed similarly strong effects, being highly significant (p < 0.001) for almost all the traits except shoot dry weight, where the effect was non-significant (Table 2). Varietal differences were also significant across all traits, though the magnitude of their effects was generally lower than those of RH and storage duration. On the other hand, interaction effects including RH x variety (RH x V), RH x duration (RH x D), variety x duration (V x D) and the three-way interaction (RH x V x D) were non-significant for all the traits indicating that the influence of RH and storage was largely independent of genotype. Overall, the ANOVA suggest that humidity and storage period are the dominant factors determining seed vigor, germination and seedling growth traits in quinoa whereas, varietal and interaction effects played a relative minor role (Table 2). Vigor index declined sharply with the increase in storage duration under both RH levels (Fig. 1A) Variety V5 mainained the highest values, decreasing from 1316.63 to 23.73 at 30% RH And from 1316.63 to 13.37 at 60% RH. V1 showed the lowest vigor index across both conditions, declining from 1212.17 to 27.13 at 30% RH And from 1212.17 to 6.53 at 60% RH (Fig. 1A). Final germination percentage (FG%), also declined markedly with storage (Fig. 1B). At 30% RH, V1 retained the highest FG%, decreasing from 95% to 9.67% over 90 days, while V6 showed the lowest, decreasing from 95% to 18%. At 60% RH, the trend was reversed where V1 showed the lowest germination (95% to 5%), while V4 maintained the highest (97.33% to 10.33%). Moreover, days to 50% germination increased under storage stress (Fig. 1C). V1 consistently recorded the shortest days to 50% germination, rising from 1.97 days in the control to 22.33 days at 30% RH And 12.33 days at 60% RH. On the other hand, V5 displayed the longest duration for 50% germination, escalating from 2.1 days to 19 days at 30% RH And to 9 days at 60% RH. However, moisture content decreased across storage periods (Fig. 1D and). V1 had the lowest moisture content under both RH levels, dropping from 12.25% to 5.17% at 30% RH And from 12.25% to 5.4% at 60% RH. V6 showed a similar trend at 30% RH decreasing from 13.23% to 4.87%, but maintained the highest moisture content at 60% RH increasing from 13.23% to 5.5%. Despite this, electrical conductivity (EC) increased steadly with the storage duration (Fig. 1E). At 30% RH, V1 showed the highest increas from 12.07 µS/cm to 32.38 µS/cm, while V5 remained lowest ranging from 12.87 µS/cm at control to 32.16 µS/cm at 90 days. At 60% RH, V1 again showed the highest EC rising from 12.07 µS/cm to 50.07 µS/cm, while V6 exhibited the lowest from 13.63 µS/cm to 41.30 µS/cm. Tetrazolium viability (TZ%) also declined with the storage (Fig. 1F). V6 maintained the highest viability across both RH levels, dropping from 93% at control to 37.3% at 30% RH And to 70.3% at 60% RH. Conversely, V5 had the lowest TZ%, decreasing from 91.67% to 19% at 30% RH And to 9% at 60% RH.

Effect of relative humidity and storage duration on morphological parameters

The results highlighted significant variations in root length (Fig. 2A), shoot length (Fig. 2B), root fresh weight (Fig. 2C), and root dry weight (Fig. 2D) at different RH levels and storage periods. V1 showed the most significant reduction for shoot length at both RH levels, dropping from 7.53 cm to 1.50 cm at 30% RH And from 7.53 cm to 0.77 cm at 60% RH. V6 exhibited the least decline, with dropping from 7.97 cm to 2.30 cm at 30% RH And from 7.97 cm to 1.60 cm at 60% RH (Fig. 2A). Similarly, V1 stored at 30% RH exhibited better root length retention over time, with a gradual decline from 5.23 cm at control to 1.33 cm at 90 days. In contrast, seeds stored at 60% RH showed a more significant decline in root length, especially for V1, which dropped to 0.53 cm at 90 days. Overall, lower RH (30%) was divulged to preserve root growth better than higher RH (60%) (Fig. 2B). Root fresh weight followed a similar trend, with V1 showing the highest decline at both RH levels from 4.23 g at control to 0.56 g at 30% RH And from 4.23 g to 0.42 g at 60% RH. V6 showed the least decline from 4.54 g to 0.83 g at 30% RH And from 4.54 g to 1.02 g at 60% RH (Fig. 2C). Shoot fresh weight also recorded the highest decrease in V1 at both RH levels from 5.08 g to 1.12 g at 30% RH And from 5.08 g to 0.76 g at 60% RH. V6 experienced the least decline dropping from 4.91 g to 1.01 g at 30% RH And from 4.91 g to 0.91 g at 60% RH (Fig. 2D). V1 showed the greatest decline in root dry weight at both RH levels decreasing from 0.83 g at control to 0.12 g at 30% RH And from 0.83 g to 0.12 g at 60% RH (Fig. 2E). V6 showed the lowest decline from 0.88 g to 0.16 g at 30% RH and from0.88 g to 0.19 g at 60% RH. Shoot dry weight exhibited the most significant decrease in V1 at 30% RH from 1.01 g to 0.21 g, And at 60% RH, from 1.01 g to 0.17 g, while V5 had the least decline from 1.10 g to 0.16 g at 30% RH And from 1.10 g to 0.12 g at 60% RH (Fig. 2F). Concurrently, V1 showed the most significant declines across most parameters, particularly at higher RH and longer storage durations (Fig. 2).

High relative humidity and long term storage reduced pigment contents

At 30% RH, V1 showed significant (p < 0.05) declines in chlorophyll a (Chl. a), chlorophyll b (Chl. b), And carotenoids, with Chl. a dropping from 2.71 µg to 0.29 µg (Fig. 3A), Chl. b from 1.23 µg to 0.11 µg (Fig. 3B), And carotenoids from 0.92 µg to 0.10 µg (Fig. 3C). In contrast, V6 and V5 maintained relatively higher pigment stability, showing the least reductions across all three parameters. At 60% RH, the reductions were more severe, with V1 again showing the largest decrease in Chl. a (2.71 µg to 0.61 µg), Chl. b (1.23 µg to 0.20 µg), and carotenoids (0.92 µg to 0.20 µg), while V5 showed the smallest decline (Fig. 3). The observed pigment loss under high RH suggests oxidative degradation of chlorophyll and carotenoids, which are critical for maintaining photosynthetic efficiency and seedling vigor. The sharper declines in V1 indicates greater susceptibility to storage induced oxidative stress, while the resilience of V5 and V6 highlights their superior protective mechanisms against ROS. These results imply that RH not only alters pigment concentration but also directly impacts physiological functions like light harvesting, energy transfer and overall seedling establishment.

Antioxidant enzymes activities altered under both RH regimes

The antioxidant enzymes were significantly (P ≤ 0.05) influenced by imposed relative humidity regimes and storage days duration (Table 3). At 30% RH, V1 exhibited the greatest increase in superoxide dismutase (Fig. 4A), peroxidase (Fig. 4B), catalase (Fig. 4C) and ascorbate peroxidase (Fig. 4D) activities, with SOD rising from 131.63 U/g to 324.83 U/g, POD from 68.96 U/g to 183.02 U/g, CAT from 23.78 U/g to 110.36 U/g, And APX from 69.96 U/g to 211.30 U/g. V6 had the highest SOD And POD activities at 90 days, reaching 349.93 U/g And 214.21 U/g, respectively. At 60% RH, all enzyme activities declined, with V1 showing the largest reductions in SOD (131.63 U/g to 265.75 U/g), POD (68.96 U/g to 152.76 U/g), and CAT (23.78 U/g to 83.12 U/g). APX activity also decreased in all varieties at 60% RH, with V1 decreasing from 69.96 U/g to 164.34 U/g (Fig. 4).

Secondary metabolites reduced under both RH regimes and storage conditions

Secondary metabolites were significantly (P ≤ 0.05) influenced by alteration in humidity levels and change in seed storage duration (Table 3). For estimation at 30% RH, V6 displayed the highest proline accumulation, rising from 6.01 µmol/g to 42.73 µmol/g, followed by V2 (6.14 µmol/g to 33.58 µmol/g) and V1 (6.03 µmol/g to 27.46 µmol/g). Proline levels were lower at 60% RH, with V1 and V2 showing moderate increases (Fig. 5A). TSP levels decreased across all varieties, with V1 showing the greatest decline (9.23 µmol/g to 1.58 µmol/g), while V6 had the least decrease (Fig. 5B). Reducing sugars increased, with V6 accumulating the most at both RH levels, though it decreased by 90 days (Fig. 5C). Non-reducing sugar showed significant increases at 30% RH, with V6 peaking at 74.15 °Brix at 30 days. At 60% RH, non-reducing sugar was lower, with V6 reaching 81.31 °Brix at 30 days before dropping (Fig. 5D). In addition, MDA levels rose for all varieties, with V6 showing the largest increase, from 1.20 mg/g to 11.66 mg/g at 30% RH, indicating notable stress accumulation over the storage period (Fig. 5E).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that prolonged storage under high RH significantly affected quinoa seed viability, biochemical composition, and physiological traits. Specifically, higher RH (60%) accelerated seed deterioration, whereas lower RH (30%) helped maintain seed quality [31, 32]. The observed increase in electrical conductivity (EC) over storage duration indicated progressive membrane damage resulting from Lipid peroxidation, with the highest values recorded at 60% RH [33, 34]. These findings are consistent with earlier reports that report increased EC as a reliable indicator of membrane disruption and ion leakage in aging seeds [35].

The steep decline in tetrazolium viability under high RH further confirmed accelerated loss of seed viability, particularly in V5, which exhibited the most pronounced reduction [36]. Comparable trends have been reported in oilseeds and cereals, where increased humidity accelerates the loss of seed vigor and germination potential due to oxidative stress [37]. The vigor index also showed substantial reductions, with higher RH exacerbating these reductions [38]. Germination percentage followed a similar pattern, decreasing more drastically at 60% RH, suggesting that elevated moisture levels promote metabolic imbalance and oxidative stress, thereby reducing seed viability [37, 39]. Prolonged storage also delayed the attainment of 50% germination, with the longest time times at higher RH [40]. These delays imply impaired metabolic activation, consistent with findings in other stored seed species where storage induces dormancy-like conditions through metabolic slowdown [41]. Moisture content also decreased under storage conditions, likely due to metabolic degradation and seed desiccation processes, further compromising seed viability [42, 43].

Prolonged storage caused significant reductions in root and shoot length as well as fresh and dry biomass, with higher RH accelerating these declines [44]. V1 exhibited the most severe reductions, particularly at 60% RH, which likely reflects greater metabolic stress and cellular degradation [45]. In contrast, the relatively better performance of V6 suggests superior tolerance to storage-induced stress, making it a promising candidate for long term storage [46]. Similar results have been reported in wheat and barley, where prolonged storage under high RH compromised seedling growth due to reduced energy reserves and impaired metabolic function [47, 48].

Pigment concentrations (chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoid) also declined across all varieties, with V1 experiencing the most significant reductions [49]. The observed pigment loss indicates oxidative damage and impaired photosynthetic efficiency, which were further exacerbated by prolonged storage and high RH. This aligns with the notion that chlorophyll degradation under stress conditions correlates with reduced seedling vigor and compromised photosynthetic machinery [50]. Antioxidant enzyme activity increased over time, particularly at 30% RH, indicating an adaptive stress response. Activities of SOD, POD, CAT and APX were higher under lower RH, with V6 showing the highest activity at 90 days. In contrast, enzyme activity declined at 60% RH, suggesting an overwhelmed antioxidant defense system unable to mitigate oxidative damage, thereby accelerating seed viability loss [51].

Proline accumulation was highest in V6 at 30% RH, suggesting its role as An osmoprotectant under storage induced stress. However, proline levels were markedly lower at 60% RH, indicating compromised stress tolerance under elevated humidity [52]. This observation aligns with previous studies that demonstrated proline role in maintaining cellular homeostasis under abiotic stress [53]. TSP levels declined in all varieties, with V1 showing the greatest reduction, reflecting increased protein degradation under storage stress. Reducing sugars and non-reducing sugars initially increased, suggesting carbohydrate mobilization to support metabolism but declined at lateral stages, indicating progressive metabolic exhaustion [54]. Concurrently, MDA levels increased, particularly in V6, confirming oxidative damage and accumulation of stress markers during prolonged storage. Elevated MDA levels are indicative of lipid peroxidation, a key marker of oxidative stress in aging seeds [55, 56].

Statistical Analyses further clarified trait interactions, PCA performed separately for both RH regimes highlighted variability among the studied traits And their response to storage. The PCA biplot for 30% RH revealed positive and negative coherent interactions among the studied traits. Varieties stored under control conditions displayed higher plant biomass viz., FG, SDW, SFW, VI, SL, RDW, MC, and RFW underscoring the benefits of optimal RH for seed preservation. Under such conditions, desirable metabolic adjustments supported sustained plant growth and higher germination percentage [7, 57]. Intermediate storage durations (30 And 60 days) showed moderate performance for all the analyzed traits, coupled with higher levels of reducing and non-reducing sugars. These trends indicate enhanced synthesis of secondary metabolites that contribute to improved plant growth and development [58, 59]. Intriguingly, seed stored for 90 days under RH 30% displayed elevated antioxidant enzymes activities (SOD, POD, CAT, APX, MDA) And extended days to 50% germination, showcasing active stress mitigation mechanisms that support better performance compared to 60% RH. However, the PCA biplot for 60% RH revealed a contrasting scenario, with both positive and negative trait interactions. Under control conditions, varieties maintained higher pigment contents (Chl.a, Chl.b), and plant biomass including TZ, RDW, RFW, SFW, SL, SDW, and MC, suggesting superior photosynthesis efficiency and germination performance under lower stress [60]. At intermediate durations (30 And 60 days), varieties again showed moderate performance And increased reducing And non-reducing sugars, reflecting temporary metabolic adjustment. However, by 90 days at 60% RH, elevated antioxidant enzyme and proline levels were observed (Fig. 6B), reflecting a defensive response to oxidative stress. Nonetheless, the marked decline in growth traits and pigment concentrations under higher humidity suggests that antioxidant upregulation was insufficient to counterbalance the severe effects of excessive MDA accumulation [61]. Similarly, heatmap analysis further clarified clustering patterns among quinoa varieties under different storage conditions. Varieties such as V3 and V4 grouped together across both RH regimes, showcasing similar physiological responses to storage stress. At 30% RH, clustering patterns exhibited superior maintenance of biomass and pigment concentrations [62, 63]. This reinforces the conclusion that 30% RH provides a more favorable storage environment compared to 60% RH, where negative effects such as biomass reduction, pigment degradation and excessive ROS (e.g., MDA) accumulation predominated. These stress induced imbalances disrupted cellular redox status and destabilized membranes, ultimately accelerating quinoa seed deterioration under prolonged high RH storage [64, 65].

Conclusion

The results suggest prolonged storage under high RH (60%) severely impairs quinoa seed viability, biochemical stability, and physiological integrity, while lower RH (30%) is comparatively more effective in preserving seed quality. The findings highlight the critical importance of controlled humidity conditions in maintaining seed viability, with V6 demonstrating relatively higher resilience to storage-induced stress while V1 exhibited poor performance. This study provides novel insights into how RH regimes influence oxidative stress, antioxidant defense, and metabolic balance in quinoa during short-term storage. Beyond confirming the detrimental effects of high RH, our findings also highlight varietal differences in tolerance, indicating that genetic background plays a pivotal role in storage resilience. Future studies should investigate the genetic, biochemical, and molecular mechanisms underlying varietal differences in storage tolerance. Particular emphasis should be placed on identifying stress-responsive genes, antioxidant pathways, and Osmo protectants that contribute to improved seed storability. Moreover, integrating omics approaches (transcriptomics, metabolomics, and proteomics) could provide a comprehensive understanding of storage induced metabolic reprogramming. From an applied perspective, evaluating seed storage performance across a wider range of RH and temperature conditions, as well as under natural storage systems, will be critical for developing practical seed management strategies. Identifying quinoa genotypes with enhanced storage tolerance may facilitate targeted breeding programs aimed at improving post-harvest stability. In addition, optimizing storage protocols through the use of humidity-controlled facilities, seed treatments, and biochemical markers for early detection of deterioration holds promise for ensuring long-term seed viability in germplasm conservation and sustainable agriculture.
