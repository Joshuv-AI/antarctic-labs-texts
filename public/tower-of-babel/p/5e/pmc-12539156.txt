Assessment of the ligamentous stress distribution in the pathomechanics of the boutonniere deformity through a computational 3D model
Authors: Giovanni F Solitro, Diego Barragan Echenique, Mark H Gonzalez, Farid Amirouche
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe boutonniere deformity of the digit, resulting from a flexion deformity of the proximal interphalangeal joint (PIPJ) and an extension deformity at the distal interphalangeal joint, is a well-documented condition. However, the association between alterations in forces and damage to the extensor mechanism that causes progression to a boutonniere deformity remains a gap in our understanding. To bridge this gap, we took a unique approach. We used a custom-built specialized apparatus to evaluate digital extension in eight cadaveric hands before and after transecting the central slip, oblique fibers, and triangular ligament. This novel approach allowed us to develop a detailed finite element model of the digit, providing a fresh perspective on the biomechanics of the boutonniere deformity. The model’s accuracy was further validated when it successfully predicted the behavior of the digit under specific scenarios. In the cadaveric study, a positive Elson’s test was replicated by tensioning the extensor tendon after dividing the central slip while the PIP was maintained at 90 degrees of flexion. The DIP joint consequently extended. The model replicated this extension and successfully predicted the extension of the DIP joint during Elson’s test as observed in the cadaveric study, thereby validating its predictive capabilities. This successful validation reassures the readers about the reliability of our research.Keywords: Boutonnière deformity, Bio-kinematics, Elson test, Finite element analysis (FEA), Hand deformity, Ligaments

Introduction

The extensor mechanism of the digit is composed of a complex system of tendons derived from the intrinsic and extrinsic musculature. The balance of this system coordinates the extension of the distal interphalangeal joint (DIP) and the proximal interphalangeal joint (PIP). Insults to this system often result in deformities, such as a boutonniere. The boutonniere finger deformity commonly results after traumatic hand injuries [1, 2], congenital disorders [3], rheumatoid arthritis [4], and as an undesirable result of the Fowler tenotomy used in the surgical repair of the mallet finger [5]. A boutonniere deformity is characterized by DIP joint hyperextension combined with paradoxical flexion at the PIP joint during finger extension. The deformity involves the disarrayed interaction of the central slip, lateral bands of the extensor digitorum communis (EDC) tendon, and the support structures of the lateral bands (Fig. 1) including the triangular ligament (TL), transverse oblique ligament (TOL), and transverse retinacular ligament (TRL); all of which have been considered crucial to the development of the deformity [6–10]. The deformity forms secondary to an insult to the central slip, which releases the tension applied to the middle phalanx by the extensor digitorum communis (EDC) tendon. Subsequently, the lumbricals apply tension to the lateral bands, causing them to act as flexors of the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint and extensors of the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint. Over time, the tension results in the attenuation of the TL, which progresses to the palmar displacement of the lateral bands. The displacement aggravates DIP extension and amplifies flexion at the PIP joint along with the unopposed pull of the flexor digitorum profundus (FDP); the result is the distinguishable morphology of the boutonniere deformity [11]. The literature indicates that an isolated laceration or a closed rupture of the central slip will not immediately result in a boutonniere deformity, but instead will develop gradually and typically appear within 10–14 days after the initial injury, particularly in closed traumatic injuries [12, 13].

The pathophysiology of the deformity has been well-documented in the literature [11, 14, 15] and is characterized as occurring in stages [16]; however, no biomechanical explanation has been reported. A detailed biomechanical understanding of many disease processes is limited by challenges in experimental design or the inability to measure disease processes in an in vivo model [17]. To address this issue, finite element model analysis has gained popularity, as it enables the measurement of parameters that would otherwise be difficult to obtain. Finite element analysis (FEA) has been utilized to investigate a range of orthopaedic biomechanical questions. It has gained popularity in the study of the shoulder and knee joints, as evidenced by finite element analysis (FEA) studies [18, 19]. Given that cadaveric experiments are not able to provide direct measures of the stresses experienced by the structures of the finger during the formation of a boutonniere deformity, FEA of this pathology offers a viable avenue to study these characteristics, as it can provide insight into the relationship between ligamentous forces and the kinematics of the digit with a boutonniere deformity.

This study is significant as it utilizes a finite element model of the index finger to provide a detailed understanding of how altered loading distributions following transection of the central slip lead to the attenuation of transverse and oblique fibers in the interosseous hood and the triangular ligament. These alterations are crucial in the stages observed during the development of the boutonniere deformity in the early post-injury period. The model simulates acute lesions resulting from transverse cut injuries or central slip avulsions, which are commonly observed in the emergency department. The findings of this study are essential in our quest to understand and potentially prevent the boutonniere deformity.

Methods

Finite element model

A finite element model of an index finger randomly selected from a group of 8 donors, aged 71 ± 11 years, was developed for this study. The bony structure of the model generated using Materialize Mimics (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) was based on a 3D bone geometry rendering of an index finger obtained from computed tomography (CT) scan images obtained with a Brightspeed scanner (GE Medical Systems) at 0.625 mm slice thickness (pixel size 0.422 mm). In light of the resolution adopted regarding the bone dimensions, the reconstructed geometry was imported into Rhinoceros 3D (Robert McNeel & Associates, Seattle, WA), where the distal aspects of the bones were modeled using profiles of constant radius. At the same time, NURBS curves were drawn on the CT-obtained cross-sections to reconstruct the bones (Fig. 2a), and were then wrapped with a NURBS surface (Fig. 2b). Proximal and distal sections of each bone were used with loft functions to create the capsular geometry, which was then used to reconstruct the volar plates and lateral bands (Fig. 2c). An offset of the surface (Fig. 2d) was then used to establish a second layer, onto which the other ligaments were drawn based on anatomical data from the literature [20–24] (Fig. 2e).

Development of the finger model in stages: a NURBS reconstruction of the bones from CT data, b wrapping of the bones, c isolation of the capsular regions, d offsetting of the wrapping surfaces, and e isolation of the ligamentous structures on the wrapping surface

The extensor and flexor tendons were modeled on these two layers as resting on two tendon sheaths as previously described in literature by Walbeehm and McGrouther [25]. The final model included the central slip, lateral bands of the extensor digitorum communis tendon, and support structures of the lateral bands, including the triangular ligament, as well as the transverse and oblique fibers of the interosseous hood. The structures were considered isotropic and elastic, with moduli obtained from previously reported values in the literature and detailed in Table 1. The model was created with a (DIP), proximal interphalangeal (PIP), and metacarpophalangeal (MCP) flexion angles of 3°, 27°, and 17°, respectively, which were used in the simulations as the initial unloaded configuration.

Model validation

The model was validated by simulating the Elson test in two consecutive steps. First, the flexor superficialis tendon was actuated for a 25 mm excursion [11]. Subsequently, the extensor tendons were activated for a 20 mm excursion. Given the model’s precise versatility, the test was conducted by simulating eight different structural alterations (Fig. 3C).

Description of the configurations considered for cadaveric testing and computer modeling according to the Nalebuff and Millender classification: a Nalebuff & Millender staging of extensor damage necessary to cause lateral band sublation seen in BD of rheumatoid patients, b cadaveric experiment attenuation damage of structures, c computer simulation attenuation damage of structures

The experiment model simulated the expected clinical patterns of an intact finger, a finger with a CS transection, a finger with a CS transection combined with either a partial (50%) or total (100%) resection of the oblique fibers, and a finger with CS transections and either a 25, 50, 75 or 100% attenuation of the triangular ligament.

Finite element analysis of angular displacement

The finite element model was used to simulate the angular displacement of the PIP joint during extension under the configurations outlined in Fig. 3C. The configurations included an intact finger (A), CS detachment (B), CS detachment accompanied by either a 50% attenuation of the oblique fibers of the interosseous hood (C1) or a complete attenuation of the oblique fibers of the interosseous hood (C2), CS detachment along with complete attenuation of the extensor hood and either a 25% (D1), 50% (D2), 75% (D3), or a 100% (D4) longitudinal attenuation of the triangular ligament.

In brief, eight cadaveric hands were secured on a custom-built test apparatus as previously described [34, 35] (Fig. 4). The hands were secured onto the apparatus by placing two Steinman pins through the volar surface of the hand without damaging or interfering with the extensor tendons. The extensor communis tendon of either the ring or the long finger was then secured to a computer-controlled winch-type servomotor with a tensiometer connected in series. A potentiometer secured to the soft tissue of the PIP joint recorded the angular displacement from a starting PIP flexion of 35° ± 5°, and a constant tendon displacement force of 30 mm/min was applied (Fig. 5) while the potentiometer recorded angular displacement. The results were compared to those of a separate biomechanical study by the authors, conducted independently, which evaluated the fidelity of the computer model in reproducing finger kinematics [36]. The experimental setup and apparatus design for the present study were adapted from the methodology described in that earlier work to ensure consistent loading and measurement conditions.

Recordings were sequentially obtained with the finger intact (A) after detachment of the CS [15] (B) after detachment of the central slip accompanied by sectioning of the transverse and oblique fibers of the interosseous hood on either side of the CS (C), and following longitudinal sectioning along the triangular ligament combined with the previous insults (D) (see Fig. 3B).

Finite element analysis of stress distribution

The cadaveric studies, while insightful, lacked the means to monitor specific variables that the model could measure. Therefore, the model was used to monitor the stress values of the lateral bands, the oblique fibers of the extensor hood, and the triangular ligament.

The simulation of the Elson test was performed by applying sequentially displacements of 25 mm and 20 mm to the extensor and flexor tendons, respectively. At the same time, the proximal end of the metacarpal bone was fully constrained.

The position of the long bones (LBs) relative to the volar plane was examined to evaluate the transition from a finger to a non-reducible boutonniere deformity. At the same time, they passed through the midpoint of a line drawn transversely across the distal portion of the proximal phalanx and the midpoint of a plane drawn transversely across the proximal portion of the middle phalanx. This position was measured across all eight different configurations (Fig. 3B).

Statistical analysis

We performed an ANOVA test (single factor, p = 0.05) on the relative three cumulative alterations, assuming unequal variances. The statistical tests were developed on the relative amplitudes measured at one, two, and three-thirds of tendon excursion. For each configuration, the magnitude of the relative displacement was defined as the difference between the observed and intact PIP extension angle divided by the latter. The results are reported for comparison.

Results

Cadaveric experiments

The mean extension angle of an intact finger after 10 mm tendon excursion starting from an initial flexion angle of 35° was 15.4° ± 9.5°. After transection of the central slip, the mean extension angle decreased to 11.9° ± 7.4°. Subsequent attenuation of the triangular ligaments and the Transverse and oblique fibers of the interosseous hood resulted in 11.8° ± 9.1° of flexion (− 11.8° ± 9.1° of extension). Comparison of the extension angles of configurations (B), (C), and (D) at one third of the excursion, two thirds of the excursion, and at the whole excursion showed a statistically significant difference in angular position (p < 0.001). Paired comparisons demonstrated no statistical significance between configurations (B) and (C). Still, they showed a statistically significant difference between configuration (C) and (D) at one-third, two-thirds, and full tendon excursion (Table 2).

Stress analysis

The stress experienced by the central slip and the lateral bands was measured using the finite element (FE) model in the intact configuration (configuration A). Measurements were taken during finger flexion and finger extension (Fig. 6). The flexion angles at the PIP and DIP joints were 56.2° and 31.5° from the initial reference position (PIP: 27°, DIP: 3°), respectively (Fig. 6B). The extension angles at the PIP and DIP were 31.7° and 18.4°, respectively, relative to the unloaded configuration (Fig. 6C). The stress on the central slip was evaluated at 15 MPa, which was higher than the stress experienced by the lateral bands, evaluated at 6.6 MPa. Subsequent alteration of the central slip (configuration B) caused a modest increase in the PIP flexion angle to 57.6°, but reduced the extension angle at the PIP joint to 12.5°. The central slip alteration resulted in a 7.2° flexion deficit at the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint and a 27.2° increase in the DIP extension angle.

Discussion

The development of a boutonniere deformity is well-documented in the literature, but little is known about the detailed biomechanical implications that lead to this deformity. In this study, we employed a finite element model to characterize the biomechanical steps that lead to the deformity. The model was compared to one of our prior cadaveric studies, which experimentally replicated the three pathoanatomical stages of the boutonniere deformity on cadaveric specimens to evaluate its fidelity and assess the stress distribution during each stage [37]. Our cadaveric experiments found a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05) in the PIP flexion angle following the division of the triangular ligament, which followed the sequential division of the central slip and the oblique fibers of the interosseous hood (configuration d). They also demonstrated that damage to the central slip leads to an extensor lag, which the model accurately predicted (Table 2). The model was also within one standard deviation (SD) of the experimentally measured angular displacements from 0 to 8 mm of excursion, before diverging during the last 2 mm of excursion. Therefore, while not perfect, the model predicted the biomechanical parameters we observed during our cadaveric studies with sufficient accuracy to adequately predict the parameters we measured during subsequent finite element analyses (FEAs).

During our stress simulations, we observed a 6.7 MPa increase in stress on the lateral bands and a slight decrease in stress on the oblique fibers after transection of the central slip (configuration b). The partial transection of the oblique fibers (configuration C1) led to almost 3.5 MPa increase in stress experienced by the oblique fibers. Still, the stress on the lateral band remained relatively constant, albeit slightly lower (Table 3). The sequential longitudinal attenuation of the triangular ligament leads to a progressively higher stress level experienced by the remaining fibers, but diminishes the stress experienced by the lateral bands. The stress alteration we observed during the study provides a plausible explanation for the stepwise evolution of the boutonniere deformity.

The complete rupture of the central slip prevents the extensor tendon from transmitting force to the middle phalanx. Instead, the tension of the extensor tendon is transmitted to the lateral bands and triangular ligament, which accounts for the increased stresses observed on the lateral bands and triangular ligaments after isolated division of the central slip.

Clinical studies have demonstrated that an isolated central slip division is insufficient to cause an immediate boutonniere deformity [37]. We suggest that the altered stress distribution on the triangular ligament progressively leads to its dysfunction and subsequent failure, thus explaining the delayed development of the boutonniere deformity.

While the model provides valuable information, it has limitations. The main limitation is that the results are extrapolated for a single individual. While this is true for previously published finite element models of the finger, it is essential to acknowledge that the extension of our results to clinical practice remains qualitative in nature. The model doesn’t consider the intrinsic tendons involved in the extensor mechanism. Therefore, it lacks input from the radial and ulnar interossei and lumbrical muscles [38]. The model predicted PIP values increased linearly for the intact case, while experimental measurements did not follow this pattern. This is likely due to a lack of key structures that restrict flexion during larger excursions, or that a more accurate characterization of the material properties should be performed. Adding the lumbrical muscles would likely increase the stress experienced by the lateral bands. Furthermore, we only analyzed the formation of a BD on the index finger; however, the forces exerted by the extrinsic and intrinsic muscles of the 3rd-5th digits likely differ from those experienced by the 2nd digit, which reduces the model’s applicability. It is also known that the transverse fibers of the interosseous hood play an essential role during the formation of the BD, a role which we did not evaluate in our model. The kinematic behavior of the model closely resembled the average values recorded in our biomechanical experiments for all configurations, except for the complete resection of the triangular ligaments (D3) (Table 2). Given that our study focuses on the evolution of the deformity, we did not find any discrepancies, other than the one for configuration D3, to be relevant.

Following the early post-injury period, as modeled in this study, there is a permanent shortening of the collateral and retinacular ligaments [11], which leads to joint stiffness and constant PIP flexion—a phenomenon not modeled in this study [39]. Our model dose have advantages, other models of human fingers presented in the current literature, are limited in anatomical fidelity [31, 40, 41], are often planar, and are constituted by four rigid elements created using geometrical primitives in which articular joints are modeled as a revolution joint [22, 42].To overcome the limitations of these parameters, our model was based on CT scan data and provided an accurate geometrical representation of the three-dimensional structure of soft tissues on the finger. It comprises four deformable bodies surrounded by flexible membrane elements, providing no constraint limitations to the joint’s degrees of freedom.

When examining the relationship between lateral band position and flexion angle (Fig. 9a) in the intact configuration (A), the lateral bands exhibit a volar displacement of 0.5 mm. During the extension phase for the same configuration, the dorsal displacement was found to be 0.9 mm (data not presented). After resection of the central slip (B), the corresponding volar displacement of the lateral bands becomes 0.6 mm. Additional rupture or damage to the extensor mechanism increases volar displacement of the lateral bands until they reach a threshold at which they are no longer reducible. This threshold is reached when the triangular ligament experiences a longitudinal resection greater than 50%. At this point, the lateral bands are converted into paradoxical flexors of the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint, as previously described in the literature [11]. The volar displacement of the lateral bands is achieved at 67° of flexion at the PIP Joint when the triangular ligament is attenuated by 50%. At 43° of PIP flexion, the triangular ligament is completely attenuated.

Several tests are available to diagnose central slip injuries. Carducci et al. [43] proposed a physical maneuver by which the wrist and metacarpophalangeal (MP) joint are fully flexed. The patient is asked to extend the suspect’s proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint while the examiner flexes the metacarpophalangeal (MP) joint. The test is considered positive when there is an extension lag of over 15°–20° at the PIP joint. The Elson test [32] assesses the ability to extend the digit when the proximal interphalangeal joint (PIP) is flexed at 90 degrees [44]. Smith and Ross studied a central slip tenodesis test in 1994 and claimed that it can be used on patients with minimal discomfort. According to them, the test can monitor the progress of conservative management of central slip disruption. During this test, the patient flexes their wrist, and the examiner applies pressure on the dorsal aspect of the proximal phalanx of the suspect finger, inducing flexion. When the examiner applies pressure on the finger, an intact central slip will cause passive extension of the PIP joint due to the tenodesis effect. When the central slip is torn, this maneuver will instead induce an extensor lag [45]. These tests rely on the subluxation phase of the lateral bands, which reduces their contribution to the moment arm, and an extension phase that evaluates the reduction of this moment arm. When comparing several diagnostic techniques, Rubin et al. [9], identified that the Elson could characterize the integrity of the central slip. Our results further validate the Elson test and support the claim that a 90° PIP flexion is required. Our results also highlight a significant variation in lateral band displacement when the PIP flexion angle becomes more important than 40° in a finger with a triangular ligament attenuation that exceeds 75%. Furthermore, our results indicate that a boutonniere deformity will start to appear at a 66° PIP flexion when there is a 50% partial rupture of the triangular ligament.

Our model provides a means to investigate the progression of a central slip injury into a boutonniere deformity, including an understanding of this progression. An endeavor that is inherently difficult in a cadaveric model, given the structural complexity and the difficulty of recreating the dynamic processes of the finger. This study exemplifies the difficulty of early diagnosis of a central slip injury, as the ligaments substitute for their function in the acute setting. We believe that this substitution triggers an increase in the stress experienced by the LBs and TL that evolves into TL failure and volar displacement of the LBs. The volar displacement of the LB changes their center of rotation, turning them into paradoxical PIP flexors and DIP extensors when 50% of the triangular ligaments are attenuated, culminating in the characteristic boutonniere deformity.

This study also illustrates the importance of early diagnosis and intervention for this condition. Early intervention may reduce stress redistribution onto the lateral bands and protect the triangular ligament and oblique fibers from progressive attenuation. Furthermore, the biomechanical and finite element components of this study suggest that surgical intervention should incorporate central slip reattachment, along with lateral band repair or reconstruction to restore their anatomical position. The information from this study provides evidence for surgeons to plan their surgical interventions and intervene early when encountering a central slip injury.

Future work of clinical issues related to stress distribution in the lateral bands beside the central slips injuries and boutonniere could be studied with our FEA model and could focus on the following pathologies:

Pain and Dysfunction: Stress redistribution due to injuries can lead to pain on the dorsal aspect of the MCP joint, affecting grip strength and the overall functionality of the hand.

Tendon Ruptures: Increased stress on the lateral bands can contribute to chronic irritation or acute tears of the tendons associated with the extensor mechanism, leading to further clinical complications.

Chronic Inflammation: Abnormal stress distribution can cause persistent inflammation of the surrounding tissues, contributing to conditions such as tenosynovitis, which can result in pain and stiffness in the affected digit.

Reduced Range of Motion: Ongoing mechanical imbalances and stress concentration on the lateral bands can limit the range of motion in the affected joints, affecting the overall dexterity of the digit.

Post-Surgical Complications: In cases of surgical intervention for central slip injuries or other related conditions, improper alignment or fixation of the extensor mechanism can lead to altered stress distribution, potentially resulting in poor surgical outcomes or the recurrence of deformities.

Rehabilitation Challenges: Patients with altered stress distribution patterns may face difficulties during rehabilitation, necessitating a careful evaluation of their progress and the development of tailored intervention strategies to restore proper hand function.

Conclusion

This study offers critical insights into the biomechanical factors that contribute to the development of boutonniere deformity. Using a finite element model, we have successfully characterized the intricate stress distributions and biomechanical steps associated with the deformity, validating our findings against prior cadaveric studies to ensure the model’s reliability. The significant differences observed in PIP flexion angles following the sequential division of key structures, such as the central slip and oblique fibers of the interosseous hood, underscore the importance of these components in maintaining normal finger function.

Our findings elucidate how the complete rupture of the central slip leads to altered stress transmission to the lateral bands and triangular ligament, propelling the evolution of the boutonniere deformity. Notably, the delayed nature of this dysfunction underscores the importance of meticulous monitoring and intervention following such injuries, as early surgical attempts at isolated central slip division may not be sufficient in preventing long-term complications.

While our model provides a more accurate representation of the anatomical structures involved, it does have limitations, including its focus on the index finger and the exclusion of intrinsic muscle contributions. Future research should address these gaps by expanding the model’s parameters to incorporate the unique forces exerted by different digits and considering the role of intrinsic muscle activity in the deformation process.

Overall, this study not only enhances our understanding of the biomechanical evolution of boutonniere deformity but also points toward more effective strategies for prevention and treatment. By enhancing our understanding of these mechanisms, clinicians can more effectively anticipate and manage the progression of this complex hand condition, ultimately leading to improved patient outcomes.
