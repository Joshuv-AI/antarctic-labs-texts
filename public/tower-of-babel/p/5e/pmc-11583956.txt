Latent Archetypes of the Spatial Patterns of Cancer
Authors: Thaís Pacheco Menezes, Marcos Oliveira Prates, Renato Assunção, Mônica Silva Monteiro De Castro
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe cancer atlas edited by several countries is the main resource for the analysis of the geographic variation of cancer risk. Correlating the observed spatial patterns with known or hypothesized risk factors is time‐consuming work for epidemiologists who need to deal with each cancer separately, breaking down the patterns according to sex and race. The recent literature has proposed to study more than one cancer simultaneously looking for common spatial risk factors. However, this previous work has two constraints: they consider only a very small (2–4) number of cancers previously known to share risk factors. In this article, we propose an exploratory method to search for latent spatial risk factors of a large number of supposedly unrelated cancers. The method is based on the singular value decomposition and nonnegative matrix factorization, it is computationally efficient, scaling easily with the number of regions and cancers. We carried out a simulation study to evaluate the method's performance and apply it to cancer atlas from the USA, England, France, Australia, Spain, and Brazil. We conclude that with very few latent maps, which can represent a reduction of up to 90% of atlas maps, most of the spatial variability is conserved. By concentrating on the epidemiological analysis of these few latent maps a substantial amount of work is saved and, at the same time, high‐level explanations affecting many cancers simultaneously can be reached.Keywords: cancer maps, low‐rank approximation, nonnegative matrix factorization, singular value decomposition, spatial statistics

Introduction

Cancer is a group of heterogeneous diseases whose main common behavior is the disordered growth of cells, which tend to be aggressive, uncontrollable, and invade different systems, tissues, and organs of the human body. It is one of the main causes of death in the world. On a global level, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) estimated that 19.3 million in new cancer cases occurred in 2020, together with 10.0 million in cancer deaths. Epidemiological studies are the main tools to identify risk factors that act by modifying the cell genetic code and triggering cancer. They have found that avoiding tobacco use, the maintenance of a healthy weight and diet, the practice of physical activities, and alcohol use limitation can help prevent cancer. As prevention is the main strategy for cancer control, the results of these studies are very important to reduce the risk of developing the disease.

As early as 1975, the publication of a geographically detailed atlas of cancer mortality revealed the strong spatial patterns in the occurrence of cancer cases [1, 2]. Since then, many National Cancer Atlases have been published, either as printed matter or, more recently, as web‐based interactive platforms covering large regions or entire countries [3, 4]. Prominent examples include the cancer atlas of the whole world [5], the USA [6, 7], Europe [8], China [9], Scotland [10], Brazil [11, 12], Australia [13, 14], Spain and Portugal [15], Argentina [16], Saudi Arabia [17]. These atlases have invariably shown that almost all cancers present a large variance in risk across regions, as well as considerable variation when comparing different cancers in the same region [18]. Each cancer type is analyzed individually in search of plausible risk factors with a clear potential biological mechanism to trigger cancer cells. For instance, spatial clusters of high mortality risk for liver cancer may be explained by the concomitant occurrence of high alcohol consumption or chronic hepatitis B and C [3], while high lung cancer risk is associated with heavy smoking habits. This type of ad hoc interpretation for the diverse maps collected in any cancer atlas has led to a large number of papers, each analyzing an individual cancer type map [19, 20, 21, 22]. This common practice of analyzing cancer atlas maps individually, one at a time, represents an enormous burden on researchers. It also creates scattered knowledge, lacking connections between risk factors common to several different cancers.

There have been occasional exceptions to this general norm when more than one cancer type is analyzed simultaneously. The main approach adopted in these works is the shared component model proposed by Knorr‐Held and Best [23], which assumes the presence of one latent risk factor simultaneously affecting the risk model of two diseases. Later, their work was extended to jointly model the variation of more than two diseases [24]. However, the applications of this model have been restricted to a small number of diseases that share a clear and well‐known common risk factor. Wang and Wall [25] introduces a spatial factor analysis model to detect common spatial factors for cancer diseases. Held et al. [24] analyze cancer mortality rates on oral, esophagus, larynx, and lung cancers for males in Germany, all of them located in the upper digestive and upper respiratory systems, and sharing smoking and excessive alcohol consumption as common risk factors. Downing et al. [26] analyses the incidence of esophagus, stomach, pancreas, lung, kidney, and bladder cancers using three latent components to represent variation in smoking (common to all six sites), bodyweight/obesity (for esophagus, pancreas, and kidney cancers) and diet/alcohol consumption (for esophagus and stomach cancers) finding evidence only for the first component. As smoking is strongly associated with lung cancer and smoking exposure data is hard to obtain, Best and Hansell [27] used the bivariate shared latent spatial factor to model the chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) mortality rates and lung cancer rates. The aim is to identify the areas in which patterns of lung cancer and COPD mortality are discordant and assign this discordance to risk factors other than smoking. The shared spatial latent model was used by Ancelet et al. [28] for a different purpose. They have three different data sources on one disease and the shared component model was used to assess and correct for spatially structured sources of bias in the data collection. Botella‐Rocamora, Martinez‐Beneito, and Banerjee [29] introduce two Bayesian models, named QR‐based and M‐based, for analyzing multiple cancers, offering a comprehensive perspective by providing posterior means of correlations between various cancer types. This is a sophisticated and flexible model that may represent one of the best approaches available in the literature. Gómez‐Rubio and Palmí‐Perales [30] showed how to use the integrated nested Laplace approximation (INLA) technique to carry out Bayesian inference in the single component version of the shared spatial latent component. They analyzed cancers that are known to have common risk factors (lip, oral cavity, and pharynx tumors, esophagus tumors, and stomach tumors). Recently, Azevedo, Prates, and Bandyopadhyay [31] extended the works of Knorr‐Held and Best [23] and Gómez‐Rubio and Palmí‐Perales [30] to alleviate spatial confounding in this family of models.

A common denominator among these previous works is the use of a very small number of diseases that are well‐known to share impactful risk factors. A noticeable exception is the work Botella‐Rocamora, Martinez‐Beneito, and Banerjee [29]. Their M‐based model is used to model 21 cancers observed in 540 municipalities simultaneously. However, a major problem with this approach is the scalability problem as its processing time grows quadratically with the problem size. For this particular case study, while their QR‐based model cannot be fitted to more than three diseases, the M‐based model took more than 13 h to be fitted (see tab. 1 in their paper). In contrast, the exploratory method we propose in this article takes only a few seconds for a problem of the same size. In their results, Botella‐Rocamora, Martinez‐Beneito, and Banerjee [29] found that the random effects associated with widely different cancer sites are strongly correlated. For example, they found correlations larger than 0.45 between the pairs (leukemia, bladder), (leukemia, liver), (stomach, larynx), and (lymphatic, liver).

Another exception to the use of a very small number of simultaneous diseases is the work presented in Assunção and Castro [32] where the authors calculated the Pearson correlation between the incidence rates of very different cancers, apparently without a common causal factor. They considered the raw and Bayesian smoothed incidence rates of the six most common cancers in men and six most common cancers in women in 18 Brazilian cities located in São Paulo state. The cities were far away from each other and hence no spatial analysis was carried out. The age‐sex standardized rates of different cancers showed a surprisingly high correlation between cancers that have no clear common risk factor. For example, for men, the correlation between the pairs (larynx, colon), (prostate, lung), (prostate, larynx), and (larynx, lung) are above 0.58 while, for women, those between the pairs (colon, breast), (stomach, colon), (stomach, ovary), and (breast, ovary) are above 0.52. Even for quite different cancer sites in men and women, there are highly correlated cancer pairs, such as male lung and female breast with a correlation equal to 0.74 and male colon and female ovary with a correlation equal to 0.61.

The high correlation between very different cancers found in these works was primarily explored to obtain more accurate estimates of the incidence of 12 types of cancer in the region. One unexamined consequence is the possibility that a more sparse structure might approximate the variability present in the rates of over a hundred cancers. If such a consequence is an empirically verifiable fact, it could lead to a significant epidemiological insight. Instead of relying solely on the analysis of individual cancers, they could be viewed as combined instances of a few latent, not directly observable, factors. This is one of the main motivations for this article.

A second motivation is the recent debate over the causal factors associated with cancer. It is widely held that risk factors such as smoking, diet, and lifestyle are some of the main causal factors triggering cancer cells [33]. In two papers, Tomasetti and Vogelstein [34, 35] challenged this view stating that completely random mutations that appear during the healthy cells' DNA replication are responsible for two‐thirds of the variation in cancer risk. The randomness is complete in the sense that the probability of occurring cancer is not affected by any factor, whether individual, population, or environmental [36]. Their claim is based on the empirical correlation between the lifetime risk of particular types of cancer and the number of stem cell divisions occurring in the corresponding tissues from which these cancers arise. This has been called the bad luck hypothesis as it assigns a minor role to lifestyle, environment, and heritable genetic variation as sources of cancer risk [37]. Recently, Stare, Henderson, and Gorenjec [36] estimated an upper limit to the probability that cancers develop randomly and they found that six out of nine estimates agree with those found by Tomasetti and Vogelstein. A heated debate continues without a prevailing outcome [38, 39, 40, 41, 42]. The main empirical evidence against the bad luck hypothesis is the strong temporal and spatial patterns in cancer risk observed in many populations [40]. These patterns point out the likely existence of underlying, latent risk factors that are geographically correlated. It is difficult to reconcile this observed spatial structure of risk with the hypothesis of bad luck. To contribute to this debate, we carry out an exploratory multivariate analysis of a large number of cancer sites simultaneously trying to find common spatial patterns in them. If these common spatial patterns are responsible for a substantial portion of the risk variation, this would add evidence against the bad luck hypothesis as the number of cell divisions is quite different in different tissues.

We hypothesize that the geographic variation of all cancers, even those occurring in distinct and poorly connected regions of the body, can be explained by the appropriate combination of a small number of maps representing latent geographic patterns.

Finding empirical evidence for the validity of this hypothesis should stimulate epidemiological research into simultaneous risk factors involving a greater number of cancers than we have had so far, as well as into discovering similarities and differences in the spatial distribution of different cancers. Of course, this hypothesis is not expected to fit perfectly the data. What we intend is to measure quantitatively the amount of the spatial risk variation in the many individual cancer maps that can be assigned to common latent maps and how these common factors combine to produce the observed patterns. Inspired by the Greek philosopher Plato, we call these small number of latent maps of archetypes in the sense that they represent ideal forms of which real cancer maps are shadows made by combining the archetypes in different proportions and adding some degree of noise.

We organize the relative risks of a large number of cancers as a matrix with rows corresponding to the spatial locations in the cancer atlas and columns representing the different cancers. We use low‐rank approximation techniques to decompose this matrix of relative cancer risks into a sum of spatially structured latent factors. As disease risks are positive variables, we were particularly interested in studying low‐rank representation that we obtain by using the singular value decomposition (SVD) as well as nonnegative matrix factorization (NMF) [43, 44]. The latter is a matrix decomposition that constrains the latent factors to have positive values. A main issue in our analysis is the selection of the number of common latent factors. Gavish and Donoho [45] studied this problem and suggested a simple solution for the SVD decomposition and we apply this work to the NMF decomposition. We applied our method to a large collection of cancer maps from different regions in the world: the USA, England, France, Australia, Spain, and Brazil. We conclude that with a few latent maps—representing up to a 90% reduction in the number of maps compared to the original number—a large amount of spatial variability is conserved. Furthermore, the latent maps created using our method reveal pronounced spatial patterns that can provide insights into the incidence or death rates of a large number of cancer types.

Methodology

LetX be an×q matrix in whichn is the number of regions andq is the number of cancers. TheXij element is a mortality or incidence risk estimate for cancerj in areai, possibly standardized for age and sex population distribution. Each column ofX can be mapped to show the spatial variability of specific cancer risk and this is the typical input data to each sheet in a cancer atlas. Our interest is to consider the largest possible numberq of cancers. Some cancers are so rare that not enough data is available to allow for a geographically detailed analysis. Our main exploratory hypothesis is the existence ofk<<q latent maps that, duly combined, can retrieve approximately allq observed maps inX. We decided to avoid complex modeling that usually requires a large number of untestable assumptions. Rather, we adopt an exploratory data analysis approach to evaluate the degree to which our hypothesis finds evidence in the data from several different parts of the world.

The importance of the singular‐value decomposition for data analysis is that it organizes the information contained in an arbitrary numerical matrixX. This is done in terms of the orthonormal basis of the eigenvectors that generate theX row space as well as in terms of the basis that generates its column space. The eigenvectors of these two bases are ordered as a function of the common singular valuesσl of the cross‐product matrices or empirical second‐order moment matrices given byXXt andXtX. When these singular values have a natural break, with few relatively large values separated out from the close to zero remaining values, we are in the ideal situation of information reduction. The variability present inX is well approximated by the first few eigenvectors in the form expressed in (1). This is the property that makes SVD so useful in data analysis: we can drastically reduce the number of columns (which is the number of cancers in our problem) while at the same time preserving the similarity structure between the rows of the matrixX (which represent the areas in our maps).

The approximate representation in Equation (3) of each cancer mapxj as a linear combination of thek latent factorsml, withl=1,…,k, and with the weightsWl,j, is key to the interpretation of our results. The spatial pattern of cancerxj is explained by the latent factors with the largest absolute value weights. For interpretative purposes, we may choose to invert the sign of the latent factorml. This is due to the sign invariance property of orthonormal eigenvectors. Specifically, ifv is an eigenvector of a matrixA with real eigenvalueλ, then−v is also an eigenvector with the same real eigenvalueλ (becauseA(−v)=λ(−v)). By flipping the sign of a latent factor to−ml, its corresponding map displays essentially the same spatial pattern as that ofml. In some situations, there may be a preference for a specific orientation of this eigenvector due to contextual interpretation. In our decomposition of cancer risk, an eigenvector or latent factorml may be inverted so that an increase in risk in a given area corresponds to an increase in the correspondingml coordinate.

However, the interpretation can become trickier because the weightsWl,j can be positive or negative. After doing the multiplication of the weights by the latent values, as shown in (4), the final map has only positive values asXi,j is a risk estimate. The presence of negative weights can make it harder to interpret the results. If each latent mapml is composed of nonnegative values, its contribution changes when a weight is negative: regions where the latent map has high positive values contribute a negative term bringing the risk to a lower final value. To avoid this potentially convoluted interpretation, we can use NMF methods. They have been created to guarantee that all the weights are nonnegative in an SVD‐like decomposition. These methods are a group of algorithms built from (2) with the restriction that all three matrices (X,M andW) have no negative elements. As with SVD, the matrixM in NMF corresponds to the matrix with the latent maps whileW is the weight matrix. In addition to that, NMF usually produces sparser results than SVD, meaning that the mixture coefficients have only a few nonzero entries.

In the literature, several NMF alternative implementations can be used to create cancer latent maps. The NMF package [47] in R is a very complete package that includes many NMF algorithms. Therefore, we use the methods available at the NMF package to create the latent maps and their respective weights for all the countries analyzed in this article. The following algorithms were considered: Brunet [48], KL and Frobenius [49, 50], Lee [50], and snmf/r and snmf/l [51].

Preparing the Data

TheXi,j element is a risk estimate of cancer incidence or mortality in a small area. Given that we want to analyze several cancers simultaneously, the best strategy is to start with the standardized mortality (or incidence) rate (SMR). Different cancers have widely different values for the mean level risk in a given map as well as their variation range across areas. Different scales and high variability in the data can make similar spatial behavior to be identified as distinct. It is necessary to reduce such variability in the data and to use comparable scales between maps. The SMR is a measure of relative risk varying around 1 and hence we can deal with cancers having widely different incidence levels. LetYi be the random variable representing the number of cases of a given cancer in theith region with observed and expected values given byyi andei,i=1,…,n, respectively. It is well known that cancer risk varies substantially according to age and sex and theseei expected values should allow for that. Assuming that the risk in each age‐sex category is spatially uniform, we denote the expected number of cases in each area byei=πiθi whereπi is the number of individuals at risk or population size in theith region andθi is the underlying relative risk. The SMR has a comparable scale across cancers with highly different incidence rates. We calculate an SMR for the total data combined over regions, within each type of cancer. For each map and cancer, we obtained the global age‐sex rates and applied them to the corresponding population sizes in every single small area. Under the hypothesis that the risk is spatially homogeneous, this provides the expected number of cases in each age‐sex class in the small area. Adding them up, we obtain the expected total number of cases in each area. The ratio between the observed count and the expected value provides a simple empirical estimate of the relative risk. As we used the global age‐sex specific rates calculated using the total population in each country, the relative risks vary around 1 providing a unique reference value to compare risks of cancers with highly different incidence rates. One of the main advantages of the use of SMRs is that they equalize this reference value for different cancers and maps.

However, a major problem withSMRi is its large variance when theith area population is small. The most extreme SMRs are also the least reliable ones, usually occurring in rural areas with sparse populations and with widely different values if one or two cases are added or subtracted fromyi. This extreme variation is not due to the risk variation and it should be smoothed out. There are several ways to deal with this problem, usually called disease mapping [52]. As our study is exploratory, we avoid the use of methods that may add spatial artifacts and patterns to the observed data. That is, we avoid methods that require untestable assumptions or impose prior knowledge that may strongly affect the final risk estimates.

Because of that, we decide to resort to a highly effective but also simple risk smoothing method, the empirical Bayes proposed by Marshall [53] and Leyland and Davies [54]. The smoothed relative risk estimator is given byθ^i=wiSMRi+(1−wi)mi wheremi is an estimate ofEθ(θi) and0≤wi≤1 is an estimate of the variance ratio𝕍θ(θi)/𝕍y(SMRi) between the underlying relative risk variation and the unconditional variance of the SMRs. The weightwi is inversely proportional to the region's population, ensuring that the risk estimate is shrunk towards the global estimate in regions with smaller populations. This method reduces the impact of random fluctuations that are not necessarily associated with the true risk, particularly in regions with smaller populations.

Number of Maps

The selection of the numberk of latent maps in (1) capable of explaining the spatial variability of the diseases is a key aspect of our proposal. A common practice to selectk is by plotting the singular valuesσi ofX in decreasing order and looking for an elbow shape to identify that integer. A more principled way was proposed by Gavish and Donoho [45] who study a threshold to recover noisy data from singular values in random matrices. Their method is asymptotically robust to unknown rank and noise being the optimal choice for thresholding when performing hard truncation to approximate a low‐rank matrix under the asymptotic mean squared error. Approximation Measure

We define a measure that expresses the amount of variability on the real maps that are explained by the proposed approach in (1). The goal is to use the linear combination ofk latent maps to capture the actual variability of the observed one.

Stare et al. uses a cancer data‐based approach to estimate the amount of variability of cancer that is due to genetic and/or environmental factors. After their analysis, they conclude that some parts of the cancers' occurrence are exclusively due to randomness. A continuing debate discusses how much of the total variance can be assigned to this random element [34, 35, 38, 39, 40, 41]. Notwithstanding the exact value of this proportion, we should not expect that few spatial latent traits explain most of the cancer variability since there are cases that occur purely at random. These cases can have a high impact on the spatial patterns of rare cancers over regions with a small population. Their occurrence can change drastically the relative risks estimates and this reinforces the need to carry some previous smoothing on the rates.

Simulation Results

The simulation study focuses on verifying the method's capacity to recover the number of latent maps, to recover the explained variability, and to compare the SVD with the NMF under different scenarios. Further, a final section is included to study the method's performance under model misspecification.

The derivation (5) of the optimal thresholdτ is based on several assumptions that do not hold in our application such as the Gaussian noise and independent data assumptions. Hence, in this section, we study the capacity ofτ to detect the number of relevant latent maps (k) and how the metricVE(k) in (6) can recover the explainable variation in the data. The method in Section 2.2 assumes an unknown but unique noise varianceσ2 for the entire matrixX. In our cancer application, there is no guarantee that the different cancers' noise is the same. To overcome this limitation, before findingτ, all columns inX are standardized. As will be shown, this simple data transformation to select the threshold has an adequate performance as the spatial signal in the data increases.

To generate realistic simulated data, we used the SVD in (2) to the datasetX from Brazil composed of 557 regions and 30 cancers. A more detailed description of the original data is provided in Section 4. The SVD decomposition producedX=MSVt. Holding fixed the obtained matrixV and the estimated maps inM as the ground truth latent maps, we altered the singular values inS using the newS˜k diagonal matrix. We also added some noise to build simulatedX˜ matrices. We set the firstkT singular values as positive values and made the remaining ones equal to zero takingkT equal to2,4, or 7. We considered three different scenarios for thek positive singular values inS˜k: (1) the same positive singular values for all maps:σ=σ1=…=σkT; (2) linearly decaying singular values:σ1,σ2=(kT−1)σ1/kT,…,σkT=σ1/kT, and (3) the same singular values as the ones obtained in the Brazilian data decomposition. Having a larger number of true latent maps makes the task of determining their true number more difficult. In this case, when the spatial signal contributes25% to the total variance, each latent map contributes with approximately only3.6% of the explained variability. Therefore, it is hard to detect all of them. As the spatial signal increases to90%, the individual latent map contribution is around12.85%, making the signal strong enough for the correct detection of the number of latent maps present.

The results in Table 1 need to be complemented with the resulting quality, that is, the capacity to identify the spatial signal in the data. Having 100% detection capability of the correct number of latent maps does not guarantee that the latent maps to be estimated by our method will have the same explained variability as the true latent maps in the simulated maps. To do that, we calculatedVE(k) for the results found by the method in each simulation. In low signal‐to‐noise ratio simulated maps, we cannot expect to have large values forVE(k).

Hence, we estimate the real variability (spatial signalp) by using the formula in (6) using the estimated weights and estimated latent maps in theXk numerator. Thus, we have a method with good performance if we seeVE(k)≈p for allk. In Figure 3, we can see the box plots of the explained variabilityVE(k) found by our method using the SVD or NMF decompositions. These are shown for each level of the real variabilityp, with a short horizontal dash indicating the real variability level. For lower real variability levels, there is some overfitting in the inference procedure, which reduces as the signal increases. When the spatial signal is weak, the method tends to select latent maps that produce an explained variabilityVE(k) above the real levelp, but this bias is small. Additionally, comparing the SVD and NMF methods, both closely recover the real explained variability, with SVD generally explaining more of the variability than SVD.

We conclude that under this scenario, the recovered explained variability by the metric in (6) provides close results to the real valuep and improves as the signal increases. With the increase of the spatial signal, the number of recovered latent maps by (5) also improves and is approximately equal to the true number of latent maps. Usually, there is an underestimation of the true number of latent maps. This is clear in the case with 7 latent maps. But even when only 25% of the signal is due to the 7 latent maps, the recommended number of latent maps is equal to 3 or larger. When the explained variability is 50%, the number of selected latent maps isk=5 or 6 in 99.6% of the cases.

Model Misspecification

To assess the performance of our proposed approach, we conducted simulations under a misspecification setting. By doing so, the cancer risks are sampled from a multivariate spatial Conditional Autoregressive (MCAR) model opposed by Martinez‐Beneito [55]. The goal of this simulation study is to check if the proposed approach recovers the correct number of latent maps, performs well in explaining spatial variability, and can approximate the observed maps using the selected latent maps.

Following the general MCAR proposed by Martinez‐Beneito [55], we create three latent maps to build the disease counts. In this evaluation, we separate the generated data into three distinct groups:

Group 1 (Cancers 1 to 5): This group has a common latent Conditional Autoregressive [56, CAR] covariance function with very strong spatial dependence (ρs=0.999) and very strong correlation (ρ=0.99) among them.

Group 2 (Cancers 6 to 15): This group exhibits moderate spatial dependence (ρs=0.975) and strong correlation (ρ=0.9) among its members.

Group 2 (Cancers 6 to 15): This group exhibits moderate spatial dependence (ρs=0.975) and strong correlation (ρ=0.9) among its members.

Group 3 (Cancers 16 to 30): This group shows weak spatial dependence (ρs=0.95) and moderate correlation (ρ=0.8) among its members. Remember that even with the spatial correlationρs close to one, the observed empirical correlation between neighboring areas tends to be small.

Group 3 (Cancers 16 to 30): This group shows weak spatial dependence (ρs=0.95) and moderate correlation (ρ=0.8) among its members. Remember that even with the spatial correlationρs close to one, the observed empirical correlation between neighboring areas tends to be small.

Due to its relatively small size, the map and observed expected cancer values of Spain were used to manage the computational burden associated with the dimension of the MCAR necessary to generate the simulated data. This construction results in a joint covariance matrix∑ for all areas and cancers. Subsequently,∑ is converted to its correlation matrixΨ.

Considering this structure, the risks for each type of cancer are generated from a multivariate normal distribution, specificallyN(1,0.02Ψ). The mean1 and the variance factor of 0.02 was chosen to ensure that the relative risks predominantly range from 0.5 to 1.5. Using these generated risks along with the observed expected values of the diseases in each area, we can sample each disease count using a Poisson model. From the generated data, we compute the SMRi for the simulated dataset. Subsequently, we fit the SVD and NMF methods. The number of latent maps is estimated using the method described in Section 2.2. Following this, we calculate the explained variability (Equation 6) and the absolute relative bias.

It is important to highlight that each group has its CAR structure with different spatial dependencies to generate its latent map. Therefore, in the end, we have three unknown and non‐orthogonal latent maps that impact the diseases. The correlation among the diseases in the group shares the latent map of its group with different associations between them as described in the previous paragraph. The experiment was replicated 1000 times.

Our results indicate that in 79.7% of the cases, 3 latent maps were detected, with the number of latent maps ranging from 2 to 5. The second most frequent occurrence was the identification of 4 latent maps, observed in approximately 16% of the iterations. Notably, the extreme cases of identifying either 2 or 5 latent maps were rare, occurring in less than 5% of cases. Additionally, Figure 6 shows the plot representing the explained variability achieved when approximating the data using the number of latent maps detected as per the method described in Section 2.2 for each iteration. The bars on the plot represent the average explained variability for each approximation method, while the lines indicate the 95% confidence intervals.

Plot of the explained variability obtained when approximating the data using the total number of latent maps detected by the method explained at Section 2.2. The bars represent the average value while the lines are the95% confidence interval of the explained variability.

The SVD approximation achieves higher levels of explained variability compared to the NMF algorithms. Using the SVD method, the latent maps collectively explain an average of 67.52% of the spatial variation in the data. In contrast, the NMF algorithms, specifically the Frobenius and KL approaches, exhibit the highest average explanation rates at approximately 66.20% and 65.26%, respectively. The narrow 95% confidence intervals indicate that, for each algorithm, the percentage of explained variability remains consistent with low variability when approximating the simulated data using a specific number of latent maps.

Real Data Analysis

Data

We consider cancer databases from 6 countries: Brazil, the United States, Spain, England, Australia, and France. To have enough data for the rare cancers and to ensure stability in the estimated rates, we aggregate cancer counts over a period of several years for each country.

Mortality data for Brazil, the USA, and Spain have been collected over the internet. The USA's data was collected using the SEER*Stat software [57], covering the years 1999 to 2017 and segmented by 3107 counties in the US. Brazilian data was sourced from DataSUS [58], spanning from 2000 to 2010 at the micro‐regional level, encompassing 557 areas. Spain's data was acquired from the Spain statistical office [59], detailing information by province from 2010 to 2018, with a total of 47 provinces. For England, Australia, and France, cancer incidence data is available. For England, the data is accessible through the Office for National Statistics [60], categorized into 195 “National Health Service Regions” with incidences recorded from 2001 to 2017. Australian data was sourced from the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare [61], covering the years 2006 to 2010 across 87 “Statistical Areas Level 4.” Lastly, France's data was reported by the “Observatoire Géodes” [62] at the department level (totaling 94 departments) with data spanning from 2007 to 2016.

For most countries, except Australia and France, the database had an extensive number of cancer types, but many of them were very rare. To have well‐defined geographic patterns, this study considers the 30 most common cancers by mortality or incidence, depending on the country. The Australian data contains information for the 22 most common cancers while the France data has information on only 9 types. The Supporting Information has a complete list of all cancers for each country considered in our analysis.

Figure 8 showcases the heatmap‐colored correlation matrix encompassing all the countries included in our data application. This correlation landscape reveals a diverse array of relationships, allowing us to identify clusters of cancers that exhibit high correlations. For instance, we observe cases of high correlation between cancers of the same type but occurring in different genders, such as esophagus cancer in both females and males. Additionally, cancers affecting organs within the same physiological system, such as the colon and stomach (both part of the digestive system), tend to exhibit elevated correlation values.

Heatmap‐colored correlation matrix of the cancers for each country considered in the data analysis.

Beyond these well‐known relationships, our analysis unveils intriguing insights. Notably, there is a remarkable correlation, exceeding 0.8, between pancreas cancer and bronchial and lung cancer—aconnection not easily explained by typical shared risk factors or human behaviors that would concurrently increase the risk of both cancers.

As will be shown in the following sections, the strength of our approach lies not only in detecting correlated cancers but also in its capacity to explore the latent spatial maps that underpin these distinct cancer types. These latent spatial maps provide an examination of the spatial patterns and variations associated with these cancers. In doing so, they hold the potential to reveal hidden factors or localized influences that may contribute to the co‐occurrence of these cancers.

The first latent map exhibits notably high and positive weights for all 30 cancers, with minimal variation, making it the predominant factor in the Brazilian context. This map shows the lowest values in the North and Northeast regions.

This spatial distribution is typical when analyzing Brazil's socioeconomic inequity and strongly correlates positively with regional economic development levels. The North region has living standards below the national average and the lowest population density in the country, while the Northeast region has 28% of the total population living with the lowest levels of most social indicators in Brazil. Even within the relatively wealthier South and Southeast regions, there is still a positive correlation between the first latent factor and socioeconomic indexes. This appears counter‐intuitive because the negative correlation between socioeconomic conditions and several cancer mortality risks is well documented [80, 81, 82]. The explanation for this counter‐intuitive result lies in the low quality of vital statistics in the country. Although improving over time, especially after 2005 [83, 84], the situation remains suboptimal, particularly in the North and Northeast regions [85]. A major aspect impacting our analysis is the high number of underreported deaths and the significant proportion of deaths recorded with ill‐defined causes in the less developed areas of the country. Recently, it was found that most states in Brazil have more than15% of deaths with ill‐defined causes [85]. Specifically for mortality from neoplasms, seven states in the North and Northeast regions reported more than35% of deaths with ill‐defined causes between 2009 and 2019, and others had rates exceeding17%.

Our conclusion is that this first latent factor reflects this major determinant of registered cancer rates, the quality of the mortality information system. A significant consequence is the difficulty in interpreting the remaining latent factors. The recorded cancer rates reflect an entangled mix of actual mortality risks and the quality of the registration system, affecting the latent factors identified. It isn't easy to separate these two influences to isolate the true mortality risks in the secondary latent factors. With this important caveat, we proceed with the analysis of the second and third latent factors, ignoring the impact of registration quality on the mortality information system.

For latent factor 2, we separated the 10 largest cancers, with weights larger than 5, from the 10 smallest weights cancers. The first group, with the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD) codes attached, is composed of male and female bronchus or lung (C34.9), stomach (C16.9), and colon (C18.9) cancers, female breast (C50.9), pancreas (C25.9), esophageal (C15.9) cancers, and acute lymphoblastic leukemia (C91.0). Considering the second group, with the 10 smallest weights, we found male and female liver (C22.9), and rectum (C20) cancers (C50.9), female cervix uteri (C53.9) and larynx (C32.9) cancers, and the male breast (C50.9) and spleen cancers (C26.1). The contribution of this second latent factor to the rates is to positively boost the first cancer group while decreasing the rates for the second group. In interpreting this latent factor, we observe that the first group includes cancers with higher incidence in the Brazilian population, while the second group comprises rarer cancers. The first group predominantly emphasizes lifestyle factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and diet, whereas the second group includes cancers that may be influenced by viral infections and genetic factors. Genetic factors could be relevant to both groups. Additionally, the spatial distribution of the second latent factor shows decreased prominence in wealthier regions.

For latent factor 3, positive values seem to be associated with acute lymphoblastic leukemia, acute myeloid leukemia, cervix, stomach, and ovary cancers, while negative values are associated with esophagus, bronchi, lungs, and pancreas cancers. Among the ten most significant cancers associated with the third latent factor, two are present in both males and females: acute myeloid leukemia (C92.0) and bronchus and lung (C34.8) cancers. Five are predominantly female: esophageal (C15), acute lymphoblastic leukemia (C91.0), cervix uteri (C53.9), and ovarian (C56). Only one is predominantly male: stomach (C16.9). It is not easy to find a simple explanation for the spatial pattern of this latent factor or for the set of cancers that have significant weights associated with it. Interpreting this factor is challenging because it is heavily influenced by the misregistration of causes of death in Brazil. The inaccuracy in recording specific cancer types can obscure the true associations, making it difficult to draw clear conclusions about the underlying risk factors.

Given the high degree of explanation these latent spatial patterns have for 30 widely different cancers in the 2 case studies, it seems worthwhile to suggest that our results may have two positive consequences. First, they reduce the amount of epidemiological interpretation, a work demanding specialized training and creativity, by concentrating the interpretation and analysis on a small number of maps. Second, they suggest high‐level epidemiological explanations for the many observed patterns of the large number of cancers in an atlas.

Corroborating the conclusions obtained in Section 3, it is possible to notice that the percentage of explained variance is similar when comparing the NMF algorithms with SVD. In general, the dispute for the best NMF algorithm is between Frobenius and KL, each being the one with the greatest explained variability for the 3 countries. In a similar conclusion from our simulation studies, it is possible to say that although the criteria in (5) were originally designed for the SVD framework, the chosen number of latent mapsk in the approximation is also reasonable for the methodology NMF since the explained variability obtained is similar.

However, it is important to highlight some disadvantages of the NMF methods: (1) the computational time is much higher in relation to the SVD, which can become a problem when the studied database is large; (2) these methods are very sensitive to initial values with the algorithms not converging if an appropriate choice is not provided; and (3) NMF methods cannot be adjusted if the selected number of maps is one. None of these characteristics are observed in the SVD method. On the other hand, the NFM method provides a direct interpretation of latent maps, since all weights are nonnegative.

Overall, the SVD consistently stands out as a compelling option. Across various datasets and scenarios, the SVD tends to either lead as the top performer or closely trail behind in terms of explaining variability. Our results indicate that the NMF+KL method holds a slight edge over the SVD for the two smallest datasets, Australia and France. However, given the marginal differences in performance and the higher computational efficiency of the SVD, it remains a solid choice for summarizing cancer spatial patterns, especially in extensive spatial epidemiological investigations. While researchers may opt for the NMF approach in cases where non‐negativity constraints are crucial, the SVD generally serves as a reliable and versatile solution for capturing intricate spatial relationships in cancer incidence or mortality data.

The possibility of negative weights in the SVD method can, at first, be seen as a disadvantage, since the interpretation of latent maps becomes disease‐dependent and must be done with caution. A disease with a negative weight is, in fact, using the inverted pattern of what is directly observed on the map. On the other hand, this allows the latent map to represent two opposing behaviors, just reversing the disease burden, thus requiring fewer maps to obtain greater explained variability, as generally noted in Section 3.

In conclusion, it is possible to say that the two methods have similar performance in detecting disease spatial patterns. Moreover, although the idea to find the total number of maps introduced by Gavish and Donoho [45] considers only the SVD framework, our empirical results suggest that it provides reasonable and reliable results for the NMF methods.

Conclusion

This article introduces a novel approach by applying the SVD in a unique context. The main idea involves breaking down the data matrix of cancer rates into latent maps and utilizing the calculation method proposed by Gavish and Donoho [45] to determine the optimal number of latent maps capable of elucidating the spatial patterns within the data. The fundamental idea is that thek latent maps employed in the approximation are instrumental in explaining the majority of the data's variability, with the remaining portion attributed to random noise. In practical terms, these latent maps serve as the foundation for a data generation model and should be the focal point of specialized studies aiming to comprehend how diseases propagate spatially on a map. Such studies can aid in devising strategies to address this issue effectively and reduce the incidence of cancer or associated mortality rates.

The simulation study demonstrated that the adopted strategy effectively identifies the correct number of latent maps when moderate levels of noise are present in the data. However, in scenarios where random noise accounts for the majority of data variability (exceeding 50%), accurately detecting the optimal number of maps becomes challenging due to the predominant randomness in the data. This outcome is not surprising in this situation since much of the data is inherently random.

Moreover, although the method for selecting the optimal value ofk was initially tailored for SVD, our empirical findings indicate its applicability to NMF algorithms as well. Additionally, a model misspecification analysis was conducted, revealing that even under a different data generation system, the method successfully identified an appropriate number of latent maps, elucidating over 65% of the data variance. Notably, the reconstruction of observed maps yielded highly satisfactory results with minimal relative errors.

Analysis across multiple real databases revealed a substantial portion of data variability could be effectively explained using a significantly reduced number of latent maps. The reduction in the total number of maps required ranged from 76% to 90%. For instance, in the case of the Brazilian dataset, the methodology identified only 6 latent maps capable of explaining 78% of the data variability. Detailed examination of the latent maps and their weights provided valuable insights that can be derived from such analyses.

A comparative assessment between SVD and NMF indicated similar performance levels, concluding that the number of latent maps determined by the approach proposed by Gavish and Donoho [45] is adequate for NMF. Additionally, both methods demonstrated comparable total explanatory power through the latent maps. There is little distinction between the latent maps of the two methods and the SVD execution notably outpaces NMF in speed, making it more suitable for handling extensive maps or a large number of diseases.

The purpose of our SVD approximation is not to replace individual maps but to organize the map collection, enhancing the exploration of cancer relationships beyond simple pairwise correlations or individual analyses. While the concept that a cancer site incidence can aid in estimating the incidence of a specific similar cancer site is not new, our article represents one of the first to implement this idea on a large scale encompassing numerous cancers simultaneously, including cancers with no apparent relation, such as pancreas cancer and bronchial and lung cancer.

Consequently, the latent maps function as a tool for encapsulating the common features that underlie the occurrence of multiple cancers, enabling a comprehensive summary. Given the specialized nature of cancer research, most studies focus on a singular cancer type at a time. While epidemiological studies may analyze the incidence or mortality of various cancers and compare different regions, they often do not explore multiple correlations among different cancers as undertaken in our study. These approaches are viewed as complementary to our proposal.

We compared our proposal with the one presented by Held et al. [24], which enables the simultaneous modeling of multiple cancers. While our proposal is primarily exploratory, the latent map derived from the SVD and the shared component model exhibit striking similarity, leading to the same conclusions regarding the latent spatial structure. Additionally, other model strategies such as those by Azevedo, Prates, and Bandyopadhyay [31] or Botella‐Rocamora, Martinez‐Beneito, and Banerjee [29] could serve as secondary stages for simultaneously modeling multiple cancers after leveraging the initial insights gained from our approach. This proves especially beneficial considering the lengthy processing times associated with these more sophisticated models. For instance, fitting 20 cancers across 540 areas using the M‐based model proposed by Botella‐Rocamora, Martinez‐Beneito, and Banerjee [29] demands over 13 h. Hence, having prompt exploratory results to guide this arduous analytical process proves invaluable.

One aspect that we have explored in this article is the possibility of using other cancer incidences to estimate a given cancer incidence when reliable rates are difficult to obtain due to the small number of expected cases, as was done by Assunção and Castro [32]. This approach would reintroduce the analysis into the domain of estimating individual cancer risks, but this time, by harnessing data from correlated cancers to enhance the precision of our individual cancer estimates.

Several potential avenues for future work stand out. One crucial direction involves integrating uncertainty into our framework. While this enhancement would significantly enhance the model's versatility, it may also lead to increased processing times. This trade‐off could potentially limit the expansion of our model beyond its current exploratory scope. Additionally, considering the extension of our method for mapping estimated trends presents an intriguing opportunity to gain fresh insights. Another avenue for future research is the exploration of alternative response metrics. We adopted the SMR as a comparable quantity among different cancers. However, we can use direct negative‐binomial [87] in the response to improve the interpretation of the latent maps in the original scale of the data. Extending the methodology to other contexts such as high‐dimensional multivariate time series or functional data is of interest to broaden the impact of the method.
