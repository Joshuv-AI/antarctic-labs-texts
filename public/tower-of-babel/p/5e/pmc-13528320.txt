Predicting cardiac index from coronary angiogram videos: a novel application of 3D convolutional neural networks
Authors: Shih-Sheng Chang, Behrouz Rostami, Gerardo Lo Russo, Chia-Hao Liu, Eunjung Lee, Mohamad Alkhouli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveData on cardiac index (CI) in patients undergoing coronary angiography (CAG) can enhance decision-making and improve clinical outcomes. However, this necessitates an additional invasive procedure, and therefore, it is frequently omitted. This study explores the novel application of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to predict CI from routine CAGs.Methods and analysisPatients who underwent CAG and simultaneous right heart catheterisation at Mayo Clinic between 2002 and 2023 were included. A three-dimensional (3D)-CNN model (X3D architecture) was developed to predict binary CI categories (normal: CI≥2.2 vs abnormal: CI<2.2 L/min/m²) from CAG videos. CAGs were randomly split into training (70%), validation (15%) and test (15%) sets. All available videos from each study were entered into the model. The model’s performance was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity and F1 score.ResultsA total of 15 297 CAG studies were considered. After applying a quality-check algorithm to exclude low-quality or irrelevant CAGs, 11 475 studies from 9103 patients were included. CI was abnormal in 25.2% of studies. The model achieved an AUC of 0.83 (95% CI 0.81 to 0.85), sensitivity of 0.72 (95% CI 0.68 to 0.77), specificity of 0.78 (95% CI 0.75 to 0.80) and F1 score of 0.62 (95% CI 0.58 to 0.65) on the test dataset. Limiting the input to 4, 3 or 2 prespecified CAG videos representing standard views did not significantly affect the model’s performance, supporting its applicability in varied clinical workflows.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the feasibility of leveraging deep learning models to predict CI from CAG videos. The proposed 3D-CNN model shows promise as a tool for providing physicians with real-time data to aid decision-making during CAG procedures.

Introduction

Cardiac index (CI) quantifies blood volume pumped by the heart per minute and is calculated as the product of heart rate (HR) and stroke volume (SV) normalised to body surface area (BSA).1 A reduced CI (<2.2 L/min/m²) indicates low cardiac output and is one of the haemodynamic criteria used in the assessment of cardiogenic shock (CS), although the diagnosis of CS requires additional clinical, biochemical and perfusion criteria. Therefore, real-time access to CI data in patients undergoing coronary angiography (CAG) can influence procedural decisions and subsequent care (eg, percutaneous vs surgical revascularisation, mechanical circulatory support use). However, calculating the CI requires an additional invasive procedure: right heart catheterisation (RHC). Although RHC is available in most Cath labs, it is infrequently used, even in academic centres. In one study, <10% of CS patients underwent RHC during their index hospitalisation.2 The limited use of RHC is attributed to the additional time, cost and risks associated with the procedure.3

Deep learning (DL) has become increasingly popular in medical image analysis, enabling researchers to extract valuable data from routinely collected images.4–6 Three-dimensional convolutional neural networks (3D-CNNs) have demonstrated excellent performance in estimating left ventricular ejection fraction from routine CAG with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.87-0.91.7 8 An advanced 3D-CNN architecture (X3D) recently gained attention for its spatial-temporal resolution capabilities and efficiency, making it particularly relevant for real-time video CAG analysis and prediction. In addition, X3D can achieve excellent performance with considerably fewer computational resources compared with prior architectures. We hypothesised that this X3D CNN architecture can be used to accurately predict CI from routinely obtained CAG videos.

Methods

Data selection

Only data from patients who had given their consent for medical research purposes were collected. The study data were sourced from the Mayo Clinic databases in Rochester, Arizona and Florida, covering records of adult patients who underwent CAG and RHC between September 2002 and July 2023. Patients ≥18 years of age who completed CAG and RHC with CI measurement (using either Fick or the thermodilution methods) during the same catheterisation session were included. Baseline characteristics were obtained from electronic medical records using the International Classification of Diseases, 9th Revision (ICD-9), ICD-9 Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM), ICD-10 and ICD-10-CM codes (online supplemental table S1).

Data preprocessing

We conducted extensive preprocessing on the CAG Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) files to ensure data quality and consistency. Initially, the DICOM images were converted to PNG format without additional windowing or contrast adjustments, and resized to 160×160 pixels using bilinear interpolation. Aspect ratio was not explicitly preserved, though CAG images are typically square. Based on the DICOM header information, videos with a frame rate of 15 frames per second were selected, and strategically, 48 frames starting from the 13th frame of each video clip were chosen. Videos shorter than the required length were padded with blank frames. This range was selected to cover typical scenarios effectively encountered in cine angiographic sequences. To prevent data leakage across different subsets from the same patient, we uniquely and randomly allocated CAG based on Patient ID into training, validation and test sets with proportions of 70%, 15% and 15%, respectively.

A pretrained auto-selection deep neural network (DNN) model and manual screening were used to select the most appropriate images for X3D model development.9 This model achieved an AUC of 0.95 and an accuracy of 0.92 on an independent validation dataset. This excluded graft vessel angiograms from patients with prior bypass graft surgery, non-CAG images, videos with insufficient contrast filling and videos containing medical devices that significantly hindered the visualisation of the coronary arteries. Because left coronary artery (LCA) and right coronary artery (RCA) labels are largely absent in DICOM headers, a separate pretrained DL model was developed to automatically classify each video clip as RCA or LCA. This model achieved a validation accuracy of 0.98 and an AUC of 0.99 on 5000 manually reviewed clips, enabling reliable vessel-type labelling for subsequent model training. The primary and secondary angle values extracted from the DICOM metadata were converted into the following angle labels: primary angles (Right Anterior Oblique (RAO), RAO-Lateral (RAO-Lat), Left Anterior Oblique (LAO), LAO-Lateral (LAO-Lat) and AnteroPosterior) and secondary angles (cranial, caudal and neutral). Detailed definitions of conversion are provided in online supplemental table S2. Each video was labelled with LCA or RCA and primary and secondary angle information. Various combinations of these labels were used for model development and performance evaluation. Data augmentation techniques such as random horizontal and vertical flips, random perspective transformations and rotations, Gaussian blurring and image normalisation were extensively applied to enhance the model’s performance and generalisation capability.

Algorithm development

Our model is a video-based DNN built on X3D architecture. It was trained to accept video CAG for binary prediction of CI. The X3D-S architecture was selected among the six available versions10 (from XS to XXL) and subsequently modified to align with our binary prediction requirements by adjusting the last fully connected layer. The model was initialised with pretrained weights from Kinetics-400, and all layers were unfrozen and jointly fine-tuned on our dataset. We explored alternative strategies, including freezing the backbone layers during fine-tuning and training from scratch without pretrained weights; however, neither approach yielded improved performance compared with full fine-tuning with pretrained weights. We classified CAG videos into the normal CI group (CI≥2.2 L/min/m2) and the abnormal CI group (CI<2.2 L/min/m2) based on the prevailing consensus in the literature.11 12 Our model generates predictions for each video clip and averages them within the same angiographic study to obtain the study-level outputs. The model was trained for 50 epochs with a batch size of 32 using cross-entropy loss. We used the Adam optimiser with an initial learning rate of 1×10⁻⁵ and weight decay of 1×10⁻³, with a StepLR scheduler (step size of 30 epochs). The dropout rate was set to 0.5. The model was trained using cross-entropy loss without class weighting, as exploratory analyses with weighted loss did not yield performance improvement. The best model checkpoint was selected based on the highest validation AUC across training epochs, rather than validation accuracy, to account for class imbalance in the dataset. The optimal classification threshold was determined on the validation set by identifying the point on the ROC curve that minimised the Euclidean distance to the top-left corner, and subsequently applied to the SoftMax outputs of the test set for binary classification. Training was executed in Python V.3.10 with PyTorch V.1.12 on NVIDIA V100 GPUs.

Sensitivity analyses

First, to assess the model’s performance across various CI thresholds, we first conducted sensitivity tests using CI cutoffs of 1.6, 1.8, 2.0, 2.2 and 2.5 L/min/m2. Second, we assessed the minimum required views and angle projection to attain acceptable model performance, as the index analysis included all available videos per case. For this analysis, we tested the model’s performance with predetermined combinations of two angles (LCA: RAO-caudal and RCA: LAO), three angles (LCA: RAO-caudal, LAO-cranial and RCA: LAO) and four angles (LCA: RAO-caudal, LAO-cranial and RCA: LAO, RAO). We only considered the first occurrence if multiple videos exist for the same projection. We then aggregated the predictions by the angiographic study to deliver model prediction at the study level.

Model evaluation

The model’s ability to distinguish between normal and abnormal CI was evaluated using a range of metrics, calculated with Python V.3.10 and the Scikit-learn library V.1.3.13 Given the class imbalance between normal and abnormal CI groups, AUC and F1 score were selected as the primary evaluation metrics, as these measures are more robust to class imbalance than overall accuracy. The AUC was used to evaluate the model’s ability to distinguish between classes at different thresholds. A higher AUC value, closer to 1, indicates better discrimination by the model. Other calculated metrics included sensitivity, specificity and F1 score. Sensitivity (recall), defined as Sensitivity=True Positives/(True Positives+False Negatives) (1), measures the proportion of actual positive cases that are correctly identified by the model. Specificity, defined as Specificity=True Negatives/(True Negatives+False Positives) (2), measures the proportion of actual negative cases that are correctly identified by the model. The F1 score, defined as F1 Score=2 * (Precision * Recall)/(Precision+Recall) (3), is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, providing a balanced evaluation of the model’s performance. Precision, a component of the F1 score, is defined as Precision=True Positives/(True Positives+False Positives) (4) and measures the proportion of positive predictions that are actually correct.

Model calibration was evaluated using the Brier score, calibration-in-the-large (comparing mean predicted probability to the observed event proportion), and calibration slope derived from a logistic regression of observed outcomes on predicted probabilities.14 Decision-curve analysis15 was performed to assess the net clinical benefit of the model compared with the ‘treat all’ and ‘treat none’ default strategies across a range of threshold probabilities (0.1–0.5).

To assess model interpretability, Vanilla Gradient Saliency Maps and Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) were applied to representative cases to visualise the input regions most influential to model predictions.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted in Python V.3.10, using SciPy V.1.1116 for evaluation. We analysed the dataset to discern differences between normal and abnormal CI patient groups, applying two-sample t-tests for continuous variables and χ² tests for categorical ones. For the subgroup analyses, we employed bootstrap resampling17 to replicate the original composition of the test dataset, maintaining the same number of samples in each subgroup. This process was repeated 100 times to estimate the mean and 95% CIs for diagnostic metrics, including AUC, sensitivity, specificity and the F1 score. Metric comparisons across subgroups employed two-sample t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests based on normality. Significance was determined by a two-sided p value threshold of 0.05.

Results

Data selection and patient characteristics

From 1 September 2002 to 31 July 2023, a total of 15 297 CAG examinations from the Mayo Clinic database met our inclusion criteria. After rule-based preliminary screening and automatic quality selection via a pre-trained DL model, 11 475 examinations comprising 71 710 video clips from 9103 patients were deemed of qualifying quality, while 20 405 clips (22.2%) were excluded (online supplemental figure S1). The test set comprised 1651 studies from 1366 patients (91.6% with a single study; mean 1.21 studies per patient). To assess potential selection bias, we compared characteristics of patients whose clips were retained versus excluded by the automated quality filter. As shown in online supplemental table S3, the two groups were similar in age, sex, BSA and CI distribution (2.80 vs 2.71 L/min/m²), suggesting that the filtering process did not preferentially exclude clinically complex or haemodynamically unstable cases.

Table 1 lists the characteristics of the study cohort. The normal CI group comprised 6516 patients, encompassing 8579 angiographic studies (74.8%), while the abnormal CI group included 2587 patients and 2896 angiographic studies (25.2%). The mean CI was 3.12 L/min/m² (median 2.91, range 2.20–9.90) in the normal group and 1.82 L/min/m² (median 1.88, range 0.62–2.20) in the abnormal group. The class distribution was consistent across dataset splits, with 25.0% abnormal CI in the training/validation sets and 26.4% in the test set. Mean CI values were similarly distributed across splits (normal group: 3.12 vs 3.09 L/min/m²; abnormal group: 1.82 vs 1.84 L/min/m², for training/validation vs test sets, respectively; online supplemental table S5). The mean age was higher in the abnormal CI group (68.2 vs 64.0 years, p<0.01). No significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding sex ratio, BSA and number of videos selected per study. Heart failure, valvular heart disease and pulmonary circulation disorder were more prevalent in the abnormal CI group (p<0.01).

Performance evaluation of the X3D model on the test dataset

The performance of our optimised X3D model on the test dataset, as summarised in table 2, is reported as the average across all video clips within each angiographic study. The model achieved an AUC of 0.83 (95% CI 0.81 to 0.85), with sensitivity, specificity and F1 scores of 0.72 (95% CI 0.68 to 0.77), 0.78 (95% CI 0.75 to 0.80) and 0.62 (95% CI 0.58 to 0.65), respectively. To provide users with clear guidance on using the model, we determined the minimum required input projection combinations. We conducted inference testing on predefined combinations of the angle projections and presented the results for the best-performing ones. When considering four projections, consisting of two orthogonal views for the LCA—the LAO-cranial and RAO-caudal views—and two orthogonal views for the RCA—the LAO and RAO views—the model achieved an AUC of 0.82 (95% CI 0.79 to 0.85), with sensitivity, specificity and F1 scores of 0.79 (95% CI 0.73 to 0.83), 0.72 (95% CI 0.67 to 0.75) and 0.62 (95% CI 0.55 to 0.65), respectively. Narrowing the selection to the combination of three projections—the LAO-cranial and RAO-caudal views for the LCA, along with the LAO view for the RCA—the model demonstrated an AUC of 0.82 (95% CI 0.78 to 0.84), with sensitivity, specificity and F1 scores of 0.80 (95% CI 0.74 to 0.83), 0.72 (95% CI 0.69 to 0.75) and 0.62 (95% CI 0.58 to 0.65), respectively. Further reducing the input to two projections—the RAO-caudal view of the LCA and the LAO view for the RCA—yielded an AUC of 0.81 (95% CI 0.78 to 0.84), with sensitivity, specificity and F1 scores of 0.79 (95% CI 0.75 to 0.83), 0.69 (95% CI 0.65 to 0.72) and 0.60 (95% CI 0.55 to 0.63), respectively. Online supplemental figure S2 presents confusion matrices illustrating the model’s performance under different scenarios, including using all video clips, the two-angle combination, the three-angle combination and the four-angle combination. The confusion matrices provide a detailed breakdown of the model’s predictions, allowing for the assessment of true positive, true negative, false positive and false negative rates in each scenario.

Subgroup and temporal validation analysis

To further investigate the predictive capability of our model, we conducted a comprehensive subgroup analysis. We compared patients aged ≥65 years versus those <65 years, patients with a BSA above the median value versus those below the median, individuals with extreme CI (>4.5 or <1.8 L/min/m2) versus those with mid-range CI (1.8–4.5 L/min/m2), and patients with or without key comorbidities. Table 3 outlines the model’s performance across different subgroups. The AUC values for all subgroups remained ≥0.80, indicating the model’s robustness across varied subgroups. Temporal validation further supported this robustness, with consistent model performance between early (2006–2019) and late (2020–2023) periods and no evidence of performance degradation over time (online supplemental table S4).

Exploratory analysis across different CI thresholds

We conducted an exploratory sensitivity analysis to assess the model’s performance at various CI thresholds. We created new binary labels for five CI thresholds: 1.6, 1.8, 2.0, 2.2 and 2.5 L/min/m2, and retrained the model accordingly. The AUC values for each threshold on the test dataset are shown in online supplemental figure S3. The model achieved AUC values of 0.79, 0.80, 0.80, 0.83 and 0.77 for CI thresholds of 1.6, 1.8, 2.0, 2.2 and 2.5 L/min/m2, respectively. These results demonstrate that the model maintains good predictive performance across a range of CI thresholds, with the highest AUC of 0.83 observed at the 2.2 L/min/m2 threshold. Confusion matrices for each model are shown in online supplemental figure S4.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to document the successful application of DL methods using a novel 3D-CNN architecture to predict CI from routine CAG videos (figure 2). The application of ML to predict CI non-invasively from various physiological signals has been a growing area of interest. Moon et al applied CNN frameworks to predict SV from arterial pressure waveform analysis in liver transplant surgery patients, achieving a significant correlation coefficient (r=0.84).18 However, another retrospective study analysing arterial pressure data from 2057 surgical patients for similar predictions reported a lower correlation coefficient (r=0.64).19 Ansari et al used a CNN model to analyse waveforms generated by rings made from polyvinylidene fluoride film to predict low CI (<2 L/min/m2), attaining an excellent AUC of 0.88.20 Our study focused on the novel application of ML to extract non-coronary data from routine CAGs. Until recently, research on ML applications on CAG mainly aimed at estimating the physiological significance of coronary artery disease.4 21–23 Our groups and others have recently shown the feasibility of predicting LVEF from routine angiograms.7 8 24 The current study shows the ability of ML models to extract additional key data from CAGs beyond LVEF.

The incorporation of our model in clinical practice has potential important applications. First, it can aid decisions on the revascularisation strategies. Selected patients with multivessel disease may be considered for a high-risk percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) rather than surgical bypass if they are in CS. Patients referred for a high-risk PCI may be further considered for adjunctive mechanical circulatory support if they have low CI. Considering the documented underuse of RHC nationwide, even in patients with clinical signs of CS, our model may provide valuable supplementary haemodynamic information to support clinical decision-making, although it does not replace the comprehensive assessment offered by RHC. Furthermore, the model performed exceptionally well in excluding low CI (negative predictive value=88.7%) for predicting CI <2.2 L/min/m2, which may help identify patients who are less likely to benefit from further invasive haemodynamic assessment, although this requires prospective validation before clinical implementation. We also attempted to train a regression model to predict CI as a continuous variable; however, this approach yielded poor performance at the study level (R²=−0.20, correlation coefficient=0.37, mean absolute error=0.72 L/min/m²), and when converted to binary classification using the 2.2 L/min/m² threshold, achieved an AUC of only 0.71 compared with 0.83 for the binary classification model. This suggests that while angiographic video data contains sufficient information to distinguish between normal and abnormal CI states, it may not provide enough granularity for precise quantitative prediction of CI values.

We acknowledge the considerable variability in CAG performance across sites. Therefore, in our investigation, we aimed to ensure the applicability of our methods across different sites by testing the impact of varying angiographic projection angle combinations on the accuracy of CI prediction. Using all video data in each case, the model achieved the highest AUC of 0.83. However, using various combinations of limited views did not significantly reduce the model performance. For example, using three projections—two orthogonal views of the LCA and one view of the RCA—achieved a comparable accuracy, with an AUC of 0.82. Furthermore, employing either two or four projection angles yielded a similar level of AUC, underscoring the model’s robustness to different sets of angles. These additional analyses confirmed the applicability of the model to a wide range of practices.

Previous studies have identified that the accuracy of invasive CI measurements may decrease under certain conditions, such as heart failure, tricuspid regurgitation, intracardiac shunt, pulmonary hypertension and in obese patients.25 Therefore, we performed subgroup analyses employing bootstrap methods. In these analyses, we observed no significant differences in AUC between genders and in F1 scores across age groups split at 65 years. Notably, our model demonstrated superior predictive capabilities for groups with CI below 1.8 or above 4.5 L/min/m2, achieving an AUC of 0.91 and an F1 score of 0.84, significantly outperforming the subgroup with CI levels between 1.8 and 4.5 L/min/m2. Additionally, subgroups under 65 years of age, with BSA below the median, without valvular disease, without heart failure and without pulmonary circulation disorders, exhibited better AUC values, ranging from 0.82 to 0.85. However, their counterparts also maintained a good AUC range between 0.80 and 0.81 (table 3). This analysis not only clarifies our model’s performance across specific subgroups but also confirms its clinical applicability and robustness across diverse patient groups.

To further assess the incremental value of video-based DL over conventional clinical assessment, we conducted an exploratory comparison using a logistic regression model with readily available clinical variables (age, sex, BSA, HR, blood pressure and haemoglobin). This model showed substantially inferior performance (AUC 0.69 vs 0.83; sensitivity 0.10 vs 0.72), suggesting that the 3D CNN captures haemodynamic information beyond what is reflected in routine clinical measurements.

Implementation of future directions

Our study demonstrated that low CI can be predicted with good accuracy and excellent NPV using a minimum of 2 angiographic views during routine CAG. We have explored the incorporation of this ML algorithm for real-time display in a standard Cath lab viewer (online supplemental figure S5). This technical experiment illustrated the feasibility of real-time transmission of the angiograms to a preselected cloud environment in which the algorithm can be used immediately and provide instantaneous predictions for display on the Cath lab viewer in the angiography suite. This ‘ML’ icon can display the output of multiple models at once in the future, such that it will provide immediate predictive values for LVEF, CI and other important data currently being investigated.

Prior research has shown that CI can be predicted from other sources of bio data with reasonable accuracy. These include ML-based prediction of CI from haemodynamic waveforms and ECG.26 In future studies, we aim to expand the input from a unidimensional input (ie, using CAG videos only) to a multimodal input (ie, adding ECG and haemodynamic waveform tracings) and test whether this multimodal input approach would result in further improvement in the model’s performance. In addition, the use of novel ML methods such as foundational models can be explored to further enhance the predictive performance.27 28 Future prospective studies should also include recalibration of the model’s predicted probabilities to optimise performance across new clinical settings and patient populations. Finally, the impact of incorporating such a model on clinical practice needs to be assessed in prospective pragmatic trials comparing AI-assisted decision-making with standard care.29–31

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, both the Fick and thermodilution techniques were used to measure CI, and the specific method was not recorded as a discrete variable, precluding subgroup analysis. Although discrepancies between these techniques have been documented,11 32 particularly in low-flow states, we anticipate that their impact on our binary classification task is limited. second, the lack of perfect synchronisation between our CAG images and CI measurements introduces potential confounding factors. Variations in HR at different time points and the use of contrast agents during CAG may influence the results. To minimise the impact of these factors, we selected only patient data from those who underwent CAG and RHC during the same catheterisation session. Thirdly, our study also faced the challenge of data imbalance, where only 1 in 4 patients had reduced CI. To mitigate the potential effects on model training and performance evaluation, we specifically selected AUC and F1 scores as our preferred model evaluation metrics. Additionally, performance metrics were computed at the study level, which does not fully account for within-patient correlation in patients with multiple studies. However, 91.6% of patients in the test set had only a single study, limiting the impact of this clustering on our estimates. Fourthly, an automated quality filter was used to exclude low-quality clips, which could potentially introduce selection bias. However, comparison of retained versus excluded clips showed similar patient characteristics including CI distribution (online supplemental table S3), suggesting the filtering did not preferentially exclude clinically complex cases. Fifthly, external validation outside the Mayo Clinic network was not performed. However, the data were sourced from three geographically distinct Mayo Clinic sites (Rochester, Florida and Arizona), providing some diversity in patient demographics and practice patterns. Temporal validation showed consistent model performance between early (2006–2019) and late (2020–2023) periods (online supplemental table S4). Given the uniqueness of this dataset comprising 9103 patients with simultaneous CAG and RHC, identifying a comparable external dataset is challenging; future multicentre studies are warranted. Finally, while Saliency Maps and Grad-CAM were applied to representative cases to illustrate model attention on coronary and myocardial structures, current explainability methods for 3D video models have known limitations in stability and interpretability. These visualisations should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of the model’s decision-making process.

Conclusions

We documented the feasibility of a novel ML algorithm in predicting reduced CI by analysing routinely obtained CAG videos. The model performance was robust across different subgroups and maintained accuracy even with limited angiographic projections, supporting its potential applicability in busy catheterisation laboratories. Before clinical implementation, external validation across diverse angiography systems and acquisition protocols, as well as prospective evaluation, is needed to confirm generalisability and clinical utility.

Impact on daily practice

The integration of the present 3D-CNN model into clinical practice holds significant potential to enhance decision-making during CAG. By providing real-time CI predictions directly from routine angiograms, this tool can offer supplementary haemodynamic insights without additional invasive procedures, thus streamlining workflows and potentially supporting clinical decision-making in high-risk patients. This advancement could lead to more informed choices regarding revascularisation strategies and the use of mechanical circulatory support, particularly in high-risk patients.
