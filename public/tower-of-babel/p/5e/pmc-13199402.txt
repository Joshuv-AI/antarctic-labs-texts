Emergent surface magnetic ordering and surface electronic dipole layers in a two-dimensional spin=1/2 La2CuO4 film
Authors: Anjali Jain, Caozheng Diao, Bin Leong Ong, Angga Dito Fauzi, Kaushik Jayaraman, Muhammad Avicenna Naradipa, Sai Prashanth Josyula, Xiao Chi, Thomas James Whitcher, Muhandis Shiddiq, Mark B H Breese, Eng Soon Tok, Andrivo Rusydi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe surfaces of strongly correlated materials can exhibit exotic properties that differ substantially from the bulk. Investigation of these surface effects requires both high quality surfaces, and an experimental technique capable of disentangling surface and underneath layers or bulk. Herein, we overcome these two challenges, using newly developed grazing-incident-angle resonant soft x-ray and magnetic scattering techniques to study ultrathin films of Spin(S) = 1/2 La2CuO4, the parent compound of high-critical-temperature (high-Tc) superconductor. We observe emergent surface magnetic ordering and surface-like electronic dipole layers at room temperature. As a function of temperature, intrinsic holes from Cu2+ and O2- ions are mitigated from the surface into subsurface layers and back into the surface. The intrinsic hole and oxygen ion mitigation have different rates, yielding strong temperature hysteresis. Supported with theoretical calculations, these surface orderings consist of a mixture of Cu2+ 3d9 (S = 1/2) and Cu1+ 3d10-like (S = 0) with strong magneto-electronic coupling through the many-body upper Hubbard band. Our results highlight intrinsic holes and oxygen ion mitigations, yielding surface-like electronic dipole and magnetic orderings and the potential of grazing-incident-angle resonant soft X-ray and magnetic scattering to elucidate, layer-by-layer, magnetic and electronic structures.”Subject terms: Surfaces, interfaces and thin films; Electronic properties and materials; Magnetic properties and materials; Electronic properties and materials; Magnetic properties and materials

Introduction

Surfaces are believed to contain rich exotic properties, which are different from that of the bulk1. Recent efforts are to understand surface magnetic and electronic properties in strongly correlated electron systems. Of particular interest is the well-known Heisenberg model for S = 1/2 system, a two-dimensional (2D) La2CuO4 layer. The La2CuO4 is the parent compound of high-critical-temperature (high-Tc) superconductor2 and its bulk exhibits antiferromagnetic3 insulator4 with a Neel temperature around 325 K5. The La2CuO4 is a model of a CuO2 layered system, i.e., […-CuO2-LaO-CuO2-…], where the Cu site is surrounded by the six O sites. The two oxygen atoms are located above and below the CuO2-layer, known as apical oxygen, while the remaining four oxygen atoms are in-plane oxygen and lie within the CuO2-layer, forming an octahedron arrangement3. Due to strong on-site Coulomb repulsion6, the Cu 3d9 (S = 1/2) band splits into a localized occupied lower Mott-Hubbard band (LHB) and a localized unoccupied upper Mott-Hubbard band (UHB), which are separated by broad oxygen p bands. The parent compound La2CuO4 is classified as a charge transfer insulator in the Zaanen-Sawatzky-Allen scheme7. In the CuO2-layer, the many-body UHB has mostly Cu dx2−y2 character hybridized with O 2pxy8. While CuO2 -layers are believed to be the main place for those exotic properties, of, however, till today, magnetic and electronic structures at the surface of the CuO2-layer of La2CuO4 ultra-thin film remain far from being understood.

With today’s advancing growth techniques, it is now possible to fabricate high-quality surfaces and ultra-thin films via atom-by-atom methods. Optimizing and controlling the surface is critical for fundamental studies as well as for the development of new technologies and applications. However, directly measuring surface magnetic and electronic properties of unconventional insulators, such as Mott insulators or charge transfer insulators in strongly correlated electron systems, has been far more challenging than probing their bulk properties, especially with conventional techniques9–17. It has been documented that when devices and films reach nanometer and even atomic-scale thicknesses, such as surface states or single-layer films, conventional bulk measurement techniques are not successful in directly detecting such weak signals from surfaces or single-layer films, which then led to fundamental discrepancies11,14,16. A number of techniques have been developed for probing surface magnetism, including spin-resolved angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy18,19, low-energy muon spin rotation spectroscopy20, and diamond quantum magnetometry21. Detecting surface states is, though, primarily challenging for the following reasons. First, surface magnetic and surface electronic signals are becoming extremely significantly weak, while bulk signals are dominating in most techniques. Second, the surface of cuprates and strongly correlated electron systems, in general, consists of unconventional insulators and/or unconventional metals17, yielding severe surface charging problems, which hinder conventional electron-based spectroscopy techniques. Therefore, a technique that is non-destructive, spin- and site-specific, and directly probes surface magnetic and electronic properties without charging problems is highly demanded.

In this Article, we report the emergence of novel, emergent room-temperature surface magnetic ordering and surface and subsurface electronic dipole layers (or surface-like electronic dipole layers) with strong surface magneto-electronic coupling through many-body upper Mott-Hubbard band (UHB) in 2D Spin = 1/2 Mott-Insulator La2CuO4 ultrathin film. We map layer-by-layer magnetic and electronic structures in energy and real (reciprocal) spaces from the surface to all subsurface layers using newly-developed non-destructive element-specific with highly surface-sensitive experimental techniques, namely a new grazing-incident-angle resonant Soft X-ray and Magnetic Scattering (GA-RSXMS), a new grazing-incident-angle X-ray absorption spectroscopy (GA-XAS), and the newly-developed grazing-incident-angle-resonant soft X-ray scattering (GA-RSXS).

We prepare epitaxially ~ 4 unit cell (uc) La2CuO4 ultrathin film on LaAlO3 using ultra-high vacuum molecular beam epitaxy pulsed laser deposition (UHV MBE PLD). We design all main experiments in UHV conditions (1 – 3 × 10−9 mbar) to ensure the cleanliness of the surface. Our UHV MBE PLD is equipped with in situ reflection high-energy electron diffraction (in situ RHEED) to measure real-time epitaxial film growth (Supplementary Information Fig. S1) and in situ X-ray and ultraviolet photoemission spectroscopies. The surface of the film is measured using atomic force microscopy (AFM), while the crystal structure and orientation of La2CuO4/LaAlO3 ultra-film are measured using high-resolution synchrotron radiation X-ray diffraction (Supplementary Information Figs. S1 and S2). The GA-RSXMS, GA-XAS, and GA-RSXS measurements are performed in a unique UHV 5-circle diffractometer at the soft X-ray ultraviolet (SUV) beamline at the Singapore Synchrotron Light Source (SSLS)22. Experimental geometry set-up and schematic of electronic and spin transitions of these techniques are discussed in the Supplementary Note. 1, Supplementary Table. S1, and Supplementary Fig. S3–S5. For GA-RSXMS, GA-RSXS, and GA-XAS measurements, the experiments are performed as follows. Right after the films are grown, we transfer quickly and keep the samples in the UHV chamber until all GA-RSXMS, GA-RSXS, and GA-XAS measurements are completed. To check the reproducibility and stability, we make measurements at different times and spots within the same sample. Even after several months, the results are reproducible, revealing that no changes, no contaminants, and no new phases are observed, and the stoichiometry of the sample remains. Furthermore, the temperature-hysteresis loop at surface-UHB reveals the reversible loop, indicating that the surface sample response to heating and cooling is stable and shows no degradation of properties. Details of the experimental techniques and various other characterizations are in the Materials and Methods section and Supplementary Information.

Our new strategy is the following. Magnetic and electronic structures of UHB at the CuO2 surface and subsurface (or underneath) CuO2 layers of La2CuO4 ultra-thin film are directly probed and mapped using A-RSXMS, GA-RSXS, and GA-XAS at Cu L3,2 and O K edges. At Cu L3,2 edges (Cu 2pj → 3 d, with j = 3/2 and 1/2 for Cu L3 and Cu L2, respectively), the UHB is directly revealed via Cu 2pj→UHB (Cu 3dx2−y2) transition probing spin and electronic orderings at the Cu site. At O K edge (O 1 s → 2p), the UHB is observed via O 1 s → O 2pxy hybridized with UHB (Cu 3dx2−y2) revealing spin and electronic orderings of Cu within an oxygen environment. By tuning the angle of incidence from grazing-incident-angle to high-incident-angle in RSXMS, RSXS, and XAS, we map magnetic and electronic structures layer-by-layer from the surface to all subsurface CuO2 layers, which were never previously explored. The RSXS measurement is performed in reflectivity mode using a linearly polarized photon (LnP), denoted as RLnP(ℏω,θ). The RSXMS measurements are performed in reflectivity magnetic dichroism mode using circularly polarized photons. Results

Our main observation is the emergent surface magnetic ordering and surface-like electronic dipole layers at room temperature in S = 1/2 ultra-thin La2CuO4 film. We start our discussion with surface magnetic ordering. To reveal the surface magnetic ordering, we perform GA-RSXMS measurements at Cu L3,2 edges as shown in Fig. 1. We observe significant RMCD(ℏω~ 931,θ) at 300 K and in the range of θ from 2° to 4.7° (Fig. 1a). The RMCD(ℏω~ 931,θ) strength decreases beyond 4.7° and becomes infinitesimally small near the angle of incidence 14° and above. The photon penetration depth of our film from 2° to 4.7° is 19 Å ± 10 Å to 38 Å ± 10 Å, which are around 2-3 top CuO2 layers of La2CuO4 ultra-thin film, while our La2CuO4 film is 52 Å thick (Supplementary Fig. S6). Therefore, the grazing-incident-angle, even up to 4.7° is dominated by the surface. Such a strong RMCD at resonance occurs due to the matrix element of magnetic scattering, where the RSXMS signal has momentum transfer, Q ≠ 0 and is orders of magnitude higher compared to that of the X-ray magnetic circular dichroism (XMCD)23, which has Q = 0. This makes GA-RSXMS very sensitive to surface magnetic orderings and even weak magnetic signals.

Figure 1b shows temperature-dependent RMCD(ℏω,θ) with varying θ. The inset of Fig. 1b shows the full range of θ, which is divided into the five regions based on photon penetration depth. The RMCD( ~ 931 eV,~ 4.7°) is the highest at 300 K. It decreases by a factor of six upon increasing the temperature to 320 K, and decreases with cooling below 300 K, and diminishes at 200 K and 37 K. We observe weak RMCD(ℏω,θ) in Region II for all temperatures, supporting the strong magnetic ordering arises from the surface and weak bulk sub-layer magnetism. In contrast, regions III to V show significantly weak RMCD(ℏω,θ) and exhibit the onset of anti-ferromagnetism in the sub-layers akin to bulk La2CuO4.

To rule out other possible low-angle effects, we compare the RMCD(ℏω,θ) at resonance with off-resonance energy (see Supplementary Note. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S9). During GA-RSXMS measurements, we only change the polarization from left circularly (RLCP(ℏω,θ)) to right circularly (RRCP(ℏω,θ)) polarizations. Therefore, RMCD(ℏω,θ) is only sensitive to spin ordering or magnetism, while other low-angle phenomena, such as fringes from thin films and birefringence, remain zero or negligible in the RMCD(ℏω,θ) signal. Intriguingly, we observe nearly a factor of ~4 enhancement in RMCD(ℏω,θ) at the maximum of the absorption compared to at the edge of the absorption (see also Supplementary Note 4 and Supplementary Fig. S10). Furthermore, significant dichroism still occurs as large as ~ 12°. Therefore, the magnetic ordering at a low angle arises from the sample’s intrinsic magnetism at resonance.

Figure 2a displays RLnP(ℏω,θ) for 3∘<θ<20∘. The RLnP(ℏω,θ) measurement is conducted as a function of temperature with a linearly polarized photon, where the electric field, E || CuO2-layer. Varying θ or ℏω results in a momentum transfer QHKL along (00 L), where H, K, and L refer to intercept along the a-axis, b-axis, and c-axis in reciprocal lattice units (r.l.u.), respectively. The RLnP(ℏω,θ) is measured at the Cu L3 edge with resonant photon energy, ℏω ~ 931 eV (UHB within Cu2+ configuration) for three temperatures (320 K, 300 K, and 37 K). We observe a new phenomenon of anomalously changing of RLnP(ℏω,θ) as a function of temperature, ℏω, and θ. Interestingly, RLnP(~ 931 eV,~ 4.7°) shows a new peak at 300 K. This peak is higher at 320 K and significantly reduced at 37 K. Based on resonance profiles as shown in Fig. 2b and XAS as shown in Fig. 2c, d, the anomalous reflection at RLnP(~ 931 eV,~ 4.7°) reveals the presence of an electronic dipole-like ordering between surface and subsurface layers or named surface-like electronic dipole layers. The surface-like electronic dipole layers occur between the 2 - 3 top CuO2 layers of La2CuO4 ultra-thin film (see Supplementary Note 5 and Supplementary Fig. S11). Below the surface, the CuO2 layers behave similar to that of bulk La2CuO4 as discussed above. As discussed later, at 300 K, the surface CuO2 layer contains a mixture of Cu2+ (S = 1/2) and Cu1+-like (S = 0), and the CuO2 sub-layers contain Cu2+ (S = 1/2) and an additional hole, yielding electronic polarization along the c-direction. For θ > 14°, which is probing CuO2 layers of the entire film, RLnP(~ 931 eV, θ > 14°) is significantly reduced and shows nearly temperature-independent. This further supports that the surface electronic structure is unique and different from the bulk electronic structure. Furthermore, at off-resonant photon energy, RLnP(~ 920 eV,~ 4.7°), which has a bulk-like photon penetration depth of 70 ± 10 Å, is significantly weak. This further supports that RLnP(~ 931 eV,~ 4.7°) at 300 K is directly related to the surface-like electronic ordering of UHB.

By employing RSXS and RSXMS over the full range θ, we observe the contrast between surface and bulk RLnP(ℏω,θ) as a function of temperature, ℏω, and θ. The reflectivity across the broader θ range, from 3° to 88° is shown in the inset of Fig. 2a. At a high θ ~ 80° (region V), we observe (002) Bragg peak feasible at resonant Cu L3 edge and very weak at off-resonance (see Supplementary Fig. S12) measured using RSXS. This reveals that at resonance UHB, the intensity of (002) Bragg peak is dominated by the electronic ordering of UHB in CuO2 layers of the entire La2CuO4 film and is also affected by the surface-like electronic dipole layers of UHB. Note that the photon penetration depth of RLnP(~ 931 eV,~ 80°) is ~ 891 ± 10 Å (Supplementary Fig. S6). The intensity from lattice ordering is less significant due to the scattering matrix element23,24. Upon cooling from 320 K to 300 K, the intensity of the (002) Bragg peak is enhanced significantly, supporting the creation of surface-like electronic dipole layers (as further discussed later).

To reveal the origin of the dramatic changes in surface reflectivity, we measure the resonance profile, RP(ℏω) of RLnP(ℏω,θ) at region I (Fig. 2b). The details of the resonance profile are in the Methods. We take RP(ℏω) for three different temperatures of 320 K, 300 K, and 37 K to be consistent with RLnP(ℏω,θ) shown in Fig. 2a. The RSXS and RSXMS at Cu L3,2 edges directly detect and distinguish between the presence of Cu2+ (~ 931 eV) and Cu1+-like (~ 931.9 eV)23. Figure 2b shows the RP(ℏω) for region I and the extinction coefficient, K(ℏω,θ) measured from XAS. The RP(ℏω) for other regions, II, III, IV, and V are shown in Supplementary Fig. S14. At 320 K, K(ℏω,θ = 20°) shows two main peaks, Cu L3 (∼ 931 eV) and Cu L2 (953.1 eV) edges (shown in the inset of Fig. 2b), revealing the presence of Cu2+ in the CuO2 layers of cuprates23. The RP(ℏω) resonates at Cu2+ and shows a significant intensity from Cu1+-like, unraveling that the anomalously high reflectivity at low-θ is due to Cu2+ at the surface (c.f. Fig. 2b). Upon cooling, the RP(ℏω) changes abruptly. At 300 K, surprisingly it shows enhancement from both Cu2+ and Cu1+-like, while generally, the resonance profile of Cu2+ at 300 K reduces compared to that of the resonance profile at 320 K, which is consistent with surface reflectivity (c.f. Fig. 2a). This reveals new electronic dipole layers consisting of the surface CuO2 layer, which has a strong mixture of Cu2+ and Cu1+-like, and the underneath CuO2 layer, which has Cu2+, resulting in an electronic polarization along the c-direction. At 37 K, we observe an infinitesimally small contribution of RLnP(ℏω,θ) in RP(ℏω). This shows that most Cu2+ transforms into Cu1+ 3 d10-like, where Cu 2p → 3d transition is somewhat forbidden due to X-ray dipole selection rules. Clearly, upon cooling, the charge state of Cu at the surface exhibits a change from mostly Cu2+ (3d9) at 320 K to a mixture of Cu2+ and Cu1+-like at 300 K and then to mostly Cu1+ (3d10)-like at 37 K, while underneath CuO2 layers, remain mostly Cu2+ with additional hole due to charge migration from the surface (see discussion below).

Figure 2c, d show the temperature-dependent extinction coefficient, K(ℏω,θ) measured through XAS at Cu L3,2 edges for θ = 6°, primarily probing the surface, and for θ = 20°, primarily probing the entire film, respectively. Interestingly, temperature-dependent K(ℏω,θ) shows an opposite trend between θ = 6° and θ = 20°. The effective photon penetration depth of K(ℏω∼ 931 eV,θ) for θ ~ 6° and 20° is ~ 40 ± 10 Å and 160 ± 10 Å, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S6). The XAS shows two peaks, at 931 eV and 953.1 eV, which reveal UHB of Cu2+ (3dx2−y2) in CuO2-layers (see discussion above). The K(ℏω,θ = 6°) shows a decrease, revealing the decrease of UHB at the surface as the temperature decreases from 320 K to 300 K, followed by the disappearance of UHB at 37 K. This reveals that holes are migrating from the surface to the sublayers. The valence state of Cu at the surface significantly changes, i.e., from mostly Cu2+ at 320 K to a strong mixture of Cu2+ and Cu1+-like at 300 K, and eventually mostly Cu1+-like at 37 K (see further discussion below and Supplementary Fig. S8). In contrast, K(ℏω,θ = 20°) increases as the temperature decreases from 320 K to 37 K, revealing that additional holes are migrated into the sublayers. Combining temperature-dependent RP(ℏω), K(ℏω,θ = 6°) and K(ℏω,θ = 20°) reveals that upon cooling, holes are migrating from the CuO2 surface to CuO2 sub-layers. Such hole migration yields the creation of Cu1+-like on the surface and changes the valence state of Cu differently between the surface and sub-layers. Note that the XAS at Cu L3 keeps increasing at lower angles and high temperatures, revealing that they are not affected by saturation (see Supplementary Note. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S15).

The surface-like electronic dipole layers and hole migration (from the surface into the sub-layers) also affect the (002) Bragg peak, as shown in the RP(ℏω) of region V (c.f. Supplementary Fig. S14d). At 320 K, RP(ℏω) follows XAS at Cu L3,2 and as the temperature decreases the RP(ℏω) significantly increases and shifts toward Cu L3’ (931.9 eV). Following the matrix element of scattering23, the significant enhancement of the intensity of the (002) Bragg peak and the shift are due to the hole migration into the sub-layers and the formation of surface-like electronic dipole layers. This also further supports the anomalous RLnP(ℏω,θ) in region I. It is worth noting that the temperature behavior of the (002) Bragg peak is opposite to Templeton-Templeton scattering25. In the Templeton-Templeton scattering, the RP(ℏω) of the forbidden Bragg peak would follow polarization anisotropy of anomalous X-ray scattering or absorption. In contrast, RP(ℏω) of (002) Bragg peak and K(ℏω, θ) at Cu L3 shows the opposite. First, upon cooling, the surface K(ℏω, θ=6°) decreases (c.f. Fig. 2c), while the RP(ℏω) of (002) Bragg peak increases (c.f. Supplementary Fig. S14d). Second, at a high temperature of 320 K, the RP(ℏω) of the (002) Bragg peak enhances at Cu2+, while at a low temperature, 37 K, it enhances at both Cu2+ and Cu1+-like. This reveals that the surface-like electronic dipole plays a significant role in increasing the (002) Bragg peak. Altogether, RLnP(ℏω,θ), RP(ℏω) and K(ℏω,θ) at region I reveal surface-like electronic dipole layers consisting of a strong mixture of Cu2+ and Cu1+-like at the CuO2 surface and Cu2+ with additional hole (or Cu2+L-like, with L is ligand hole) in CuO2 sub-layers.

We would also like to highlight the following observations and comparisons. First, it is worth noting that the shape of the Bragg peak is qualitatively similar when the photon energy changes (Supplementary Fig. S13). Therefore, the enhancements are due to the resonance effect of Cu2+ and Cu1+-like (or surface-like electronic dipole layers of UHB), while the interference process between the Bragg peak and absorption is negligible. Second, we compare XAS at the Cu L3 edge for our 4 uc La2CuO4 film at θ = 20° with bulk La2CuO4 from ref. 26 and strain La2CuO4 film from ref. 27 our La2CuO4 film behaves similar to bulk La2CuO4 (Supplementary Fig. S7).

At 320 K and ℏω ∼ 530.1 eV, in region I, RLnP(ℏω∼ 530 · 1 eV,θ∼ 5.7°) shows a peak, which slightly decreases as the temperature is reduced to 300 K and eventually vanishes at 37 K. This trend of temperature-dependent RLnP(ℏω∼530 · 1 eV,θ∼ 5.7°) is similar to that of RLnP(~931 eV,~ 4.7°), supporting that they come from the same origin. The RLnP(ℏω∼530 · 1 eV,θ∼ 5.7°) reveals the importance of oxygen in determining surface-like electronic ordering in the CuO2 layers. The peak at ~ 532.1 eV and ~ 536.1 eV is due to the transition from O 1 s → O 2pxy hybridized with eg↓(3d3z2−r2) in CuO2-layer and O1s → apical O 2pz hybridized with La 5 d in LaO-layer, respectively3,4. We, therefore, assign them as oxygen in the CuO2-layer and apical oxygen, respectively. At a temperature of 320 K and in the region I, both RLnP(ℏω∼ 532 · 1 eV,θ∼5.7°) and RLnP(ℏω∼ 536 · 1 eV,θ∼ 5°) shows a peak and it diminishes upon cooling down to 300 K and completely vanishes at 37 K. This reveals that oxygen in CuO2-layer and apical oxygen are migrating moving away (or migrating) from the surface toward sub-layers upon cooling. On the contrary, bulk-like region III shows no considerable variation in RLnP(ℏω,θ) as a function of temperature for all three photon energies. We observe temperature-dependent RLnP(ℏω,θ) in region II, revealing that the surface still contributes, in addition to the bulk.

Based on RSXMS, RSXS, and XAS, we obtain emergent magnetic and electronic phase diagrams of the surface and sub-layers of La2CuO4 ultra-thin film.

We measure and identify distinct magnetic orderings from RMCD(ℏω,θ) at Cu L3 of the surface (region I) and the entire film (region V) as a function of temperature (Fig. 4a). To qualitatively obtain the magnetic strength corresponding to surface and bulk, we integrate RMCD(θ) strength at each region (regions I and V). Note that in our experimental set-up, RMCD(θ) is measured without applying the magnetic field; therefore, the magnetic scattering dichroism obtained is a remnant, intrinsic magnetism from the sample. Surprisingly, we find that the RMCD(θ) strength in region I shows a remarkable increase at 300 K, revealing an extremely strong surface magnetic ordering. As the temperature increases to 320 K, the RMCD(θ) strength decreases by a factor of six, assigned to surface anti-ferromagnetic-like ordering. Upon cooling below 300 K, it drops dramatically and then further decreases monotonically until 200 K. Further cooling, it ultimately suppresses to zero, named weak surface magnetic ordering and no surface magnetic ordering, respectively (see Supplementary Note 6 and Supplementary Fig. S16). Conversely, in region V, the RMCD(ℏω,θ) strength is relatively stronger at lower temperatures till 200 K and decreases monotonically at higher temperatures (see details in Supplementary Note 6 and Supplementary Fig. S17).

To reveal the effects of the holes and oxygen migrations on the surface-like electronic dipole layers as a function of temperature, we conduct temperature hysteresis (heating-cooling cycle) of RLnP(ℏω= UHB,θ = 6°), both during warming up from 37 K to 330 K and during cooling down (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Note. 7). The temperature hysteresis RLnP(ℏω= UHB,θ = 6°) is performed at both ~ 931 eV (UHB of Cu L3 edge) and ~ 530.1 eV (UHB of O K edge), which are related to holes and oxygen ion migrations, respectively. Surprisingly, we observe strong hysteresis at ℏω ∼ 530.1 eV and still feasible hysteresis at ℏω ∼ 931 eV. As temperature increases, RLnP(ℏω,θ = 6°) at both UHB follows the same trend, with an onset transition occurring ~ 200 K. Prior to 200 K, RLnP(ℏω,θ) is zero at both photon energies, and subsequently, as the temperature rises above 200 K, RLnP(ℏω,θ) increases gradually with the increase in temperature till 330 K and decreases on cooling down, resulting in RLnP(ℏω,θ = 6°) temperature hysteresis. Notably, upon cooling, RLnP(ℏω= 931 eV, θ = 6°) decreases at a faster rate compared to RLnP(ℏω= 530 · 1 eV, θ = 6°). Details of fitting and calculation on the transition rate of Cu2+ and Cu1+-like based on RLnP(ℏω,θ = 6°) temperature hysteresis for both the UHB of Cu L3 edge and O K edge are shown in Supplementary Note. 7 (Supplementary Figs. S18–20). Note that the temperature hysteresis RLnP(ℏω,θ = 6°) at the O K edge has been performed a few times, which are reproducible.

To have a deeper understanding of the surface magnetic and electronic structures, we construct a four-unit cells (4 uc) model of La2CuO4 and perform density functional theory (DFT) with Hubbard U to calculate the projected density of states (PDOS) and magnetic moments (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Figs. S21–23). The details of the theoretical calculations are shown in Materials and Methods and Supplementary Figs. S21–23. The relaxed crystal structure results in a surface reconstruction in which the length of the c-axis is extended by 2.56% (13.56 Å), whereas the Cu-O-Cu bond angle is 151.42° at the surface. To induce a ferromagnetic configuration, the total magnetization is constrained to be 1 μB/unit cell. This results in a ferromagnetic ground state, where magnetic moments of the Cu atoms in each layer are observed. In Fig. 5a, the collinear FM DFT calculations show a large enhancement of the magnetic moments at the surface, both in the unrelaxed (c-axis = 0) and relaxed surface atoms (c-axis = 46.16 - 53.28 Å). Meanwhile, the magnetic moments of bulk-like La2CuO4 are small. This surface magnetic enhancement agrees with GA-RSXMS.

To further explore the surface electronic structure, we calculate the spin-dependent projected density of states (PDOS) for Cu 3 d and O 2p orbitals for surface and bulk atoms (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. S22). We observe that the surface electronic structure of La2CuO4 is indeed different from the bulk, especially near the Fermi level. Both Cu 3 d and O 2p surface atoms show significant broadening, creating new spin-polarized mid-gap states localized at the surface. By examining Cu 3 d orbitals, we can observe the origin of the surface spin-polarized mid-gap states. The PDOS bulk La2CuO4, i.e., Cu 3 d orbitals located below the relaxed surface, show states that are separated by a band gap. These states are dominated by Cu2+ and behave like the antiferromagnetic ground state of La2CuO4. Interestingly, the surface Cu 3 d shows spin-polarized and localized midgap states. The surface spin-polarized mid-gap states are due to the mixture of Cu2+ and Cu1+-like and the symmetry broken, hosting the surface electronic dipole layers as well as surface magnetic ordering as observed in GA-RSXS and GA-RSXMS.

Discussion

Based on our experimental data supported with theoretical calculations, we draw the schematic of spin and electronic structures from the surface into sub-layers of S = 1/2 ultra-thin La2CuO4 film as a function of temperature (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. S24 for details). At 300 K, the co-existence of surface magnetic ordering and surface-like electronic dipole layers emerges through many-body UHB. The electronic dipole-like layers consist of two different CuO2 layers, i.e., (1) the surface CuO2 layer, which has a strong mixture of Cu2+ and Cu1+-like, and (2) the underneath CuO2 layer, which has Cu2+ with an additional hole, resulting in the creation of a charge dipole along the c-direction depicted as P. When only Cu2+ (S = 1/2 at 320 K) or Cu1+-like (S = 0 at 37 K) at the surface of the CuO2 layer is observed, magnetic ordering significantly weakens. The movement of holes and oxygen results in broken inversion symmetry and the emergence of surface magnetic ordering, which further leads to the onset of surface-like electronic dipole layers (as further discussed below). Interestingly, RP(ℏω) and RLnP(ℏω,θ=5°) at 536.1 eV, which track the apical oxygen (or oxygen in La-O layer), show a prominent peak at 320 K and reduce drastically at 300 K (Fig. 3a and b), revealing the movement of apical oxygen from the surface to sub-layers (Supplementary Fig. S24). Note that interstitial and staging of oxygen have been observed in La2CuO4+y with an oxygen excess system, supporting that the oxygen moves into sub-layers28–30. Similarly, RP(ℏω) and RLnP(ℏω, θ = 5 · 7°) at 532.1 eV, which probe O in CuO2-layer, show a significant reduction in reflectivity on lowering the temperature from 320 K to 300 K (Fig. 3a and b), showing the movement of CuO2-layer oxygen into sub-layers (as shown in schematic Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. S24). The GA-RSXS at UHB ( ~ 530.1 eV), both RP(ℏω) and RLnP(ℏω, θ = 5 · 7°) remain the same as a function of temperature, signifying that the system maintains charge transfer at 300 K (Fig. 3a and b). The movement of holes and oxygen in the apical and CuO2-layer from the surface results in the mixture of Cu2+ and Cu1+-like, generating surface-like electronic dipole layers with electronic polarization along the c-direction (Fig. 5c).

The RMCD(ℏω,θ) strength of UHB at Cu L3 edge in GA-RSXMS exhibits an enormous increase in surface magnetic ordering at room temperature (c.f. Figure 4a). Supported by theoretical calculations, we ascribe the magnetic transition at 300 K as a complex, non-collinear magnetic ordering due to the mixture of Cu2+ (S = 1/2) and Cu1+-like (S = 0) in the surface CuO2-layer (Fig. 5c). Based on RMCD(ℏω,θ) strength (Fig. 4a), we infer the presence of robust surface magnetic ordering at 300 K, and albeit weaker, but still noticeable magnetic ordering at 320 K, indicating that the Néel temperature likely exceeds 320 K. At temperatures higher than the Néel temperature (TN)5, Cu2+ spins in La2CuO4 are arranged in the paramagnetic phase. As the temperature is lowered to the Néel temperature, an anti-ferromagnetic transition occurs, causing Cu2+ in the CuO2-layers to develop a long-range anti-parallel spin order5. On going down to 320 K (below Neel temperature), the Cu2+ dominates at the surface, resulting in a weak magnetic ordering that suggests the onset of weak competing magnetic interactions between adjacent Cu2+ spins, leading to spin canting. Upon cooling down to 300 K, we argue that a mixture of Cu2+ (S = 1/2) and Cu1+-like (S = 0) results in a strong competing magnetic interaction between antiferromagnetic and ferromagnetic, causing spin frustration between adjacent Cu2+ (S = 1/2) spins and resulting in a somewhat non-collinear spin structure. Note that the interplay of Cu2+ and Cu1+ has been shown to play an important role in the multiferroic LiCu2O2 (ref. 30), and a somewhat similar but more complex phenomenon may occur at the surface of La2CuO4. Upon cooling to 200 K and beyond, surface magnetism in the La2CuO4 significantly reduced (c.f. Figs. 1b, 4a). This is due to the presence of a majority of non-magnetic Cu1+-like (S = 0) at the surface, in which, unlike Cu2+ (3d9), the Cu1+(3d10)-like does not have any unpaired d electrons and therefore does not contribute to the observed magnetism31.

We next investigate the temperature hysteresis of UHB using GA-RSXS at Cu L3 and O K edges (Fig. 4b). The many-body UHB of La2CuO4 ultra-thin film is shown to occur in the CuO2-layer, and we observe copper atoms undergo changes in the valence state for varying temperatures, and the hole (charge) and oxygen moving out of and into the surface (Fig. 5c). At the UHB of Cu L3 edge, the observation of GA-RSXS temperature hysteresis provides insights into different valence states of Cu in UHB. Upon cooling, Cu2+ is reduced to Cu1+-like, resulting in a decrease of RLnP(ℏω,θ). Upon heating, Cu1+-like changes its valence state to form Cu2+, increasing the RLnP(ℏω,θ). Interestingly, this increase in RLnP(ℏω,θ) do not follow the same path (or have different rates) upon subsequent cooling, resulting in a hysteresis loop in the GA-RSXS measurements, where RLnP(ℏω,θ) remains higher even upon cooling until a certain temperature is reached. This behavior shows the presence of Cu2+ that has not fully reduced back to Cu1+-like upon cooling.

At the UHB of O K edge, GA-RSXS temperature hysteresis provides insights into UHB in the oxygen environment. Interestingly, it shows a broader hysteresis as compared to that of GA-RSXS at UHB of Cu L3. This reveals that the dynamic behavior of UHB is associated with oxygen environments. Therefore, the interplay of the migration of holes and oxygen plays an important role in determining the temperature hysteresis of UHB during heating and cooling cycles.

We next construct the correlation between surface magnetic ordering, surface-like electronic dipole layers, and migration of hole and oxygen across three distinct temperatures 320 K, 300 K, and 37 K, encompassing both heating and cooling processes (Fig. 5c). At 320 K, Fig. 4a reveals a discernible yet weak magnetic ordering, while Fig. 4b shows the presence of Cu2+ at the surface as shown in Fig. 5c (A). This is the state where the presence of canted spins arises from the broken symmetry at the surface of the ultra-thin film, resulting in weak magnetic dichroism (marked as A in Fig. 5c).

Upon cooling down to 300 K, the system has the following conditions: (1) the UHB at the surface remains as revealed by RP(ℏω) of GA-RSXS at ~ 530.1 eV (c.f. Fig. 3b); (2) The pronounced surface magnetic ordering emerges revealed by GA-RSXMS (c.f. Figs. 1a and 4a); (3) The surface-like electronic dipole of a mixture Cu2+ and Cu1+-like occurs revealed by GA-RSXS (c.f. Fig. 2a, b) and GA-XAS (c.f. Fig. 2c, d and see also marked as B in Fig. 5c); (4) The activation energy for Cu2+ → Cu1+-like at UHB of Cu L3 edge is similar to the activation energy at UHB of O K edge (Supplementary Table S2); and (5) the migration of holes and oxygens mainly in apical from the surface into sub-layers revealed by XAS at grazing- and high-incident angles of Cu L3,2 edges (c.f. Fig. 2c, d) and RP(ℏω) at O K edge (c.f. Fig. 3). Therefore, this is the state, where the surface UHB exhibits both strong surface magnetic ordering and surface-like electronic dipole layers. The interplay of the mixture of Cu2+ and Cu1+-like and charge transfer (or hole (h+) migration), accompanied by O2- (apical oxygen) migration from surface to sub-layers, determines the surface magnetic and electronic orderings (Fig. 5c (B)). Note that it is relatively easy for the apical oxygen to move as compared to O in-CuO2 layer32,33.

Further cooling down to 37 K induces a significant reduction in magnetic ordering seen from GA-RSXMS (Fig. 4a) and surface-like electronic dipole layers in GA-RSXS intensity (Fig. 4b), due to the fact that the surface primarily consists of a Cu1+-like state. This reveals that holes and O2- (apical and CuO2-layer) further migrate from the surface to sub-layers of the crystal lattice [Fig. 5c (C)]. Subsequently, upon raising the temperature back to 300 K, the movement of holes occurs from sub-layers to the surface as depicted in D (Fig. 5c). Further increase in temperature to 320 K, we get back to the initial state with Cu2+ prevailing at the surface. Based on temperature hysteresis data, we find that the data are highly reproducible; therefore, the surface of La2CuO4 is an active layer (see details in Supplementary Note 8).

It is worth highlighting other unique strengths of GA-RSXMS and GA-RSXS to directly map real and energy spaces layer-by-layer and resolve magnetic and electronic structures from the surface to sub-layers generally. We also show the difficulty of detecting surface properties using bulk-type experimental geometry of RSXS measurements (c.f. Supplementary Fig. S14c and inset of Fig. 2a). The RP(ℏω) of RSXS in region IV, which is probing bulk properties, reveals that even RSXS cannot detect the surface states. This further supports the main challenge of using conventional bulk measurements to measure surface states, and thus GA-RSXMS and GA-RSXS are powerful and unique to probe surface properties even in ultra-thin films.

Methods

Sample preparation

La2CuO4 ultra-thin films are prepared epitaxially on 1 cm × 1 cm LaAlO3(001) substrates using a custom-made ultrahigh vacuum molecular beam epitaxy pulsed laser deposition (UHV-MBE PLD) system. The growth process involved the utilization of a solid-state ablation Nd:YAG laser with an output wavelength of 266 nm and growth monitoring using in situ reflection high-energy electron diffraction (in situ RHEED). The LaAlO3 (001) substrate is rinsed in isopropanol and blown-dried in the air before being loaded into the UHV-MBE PLD system with a base pressure of 5 × 10−9 Torr. The substrate is first outgassed in a vacuum at 310 °C for 45 mins before being annealed at 750 °C for 60 mins under 50 mTorr O2 to obtain a clean LaAlO3 (001) surface as verified using in situ RHEED as depicted in Supplementary Fig. S1. The La2CuO4 film is then pulsed laser deposited onto LaAlO3 (001) substrate at 750 °C under 50 mTorr O2. Subsequently, the sample underwent an additional annealing step at 750 °C under 300 Torr O2 for another 60 mins. The pulsed laser utilizes an energy of 3.25 J cm−2. Prior to the deposition, the La2CuO4 target is pre-ablated at 10 Hz with 2000 pulses.

Resonant Soft X-ray and Magnetic Scattering (RSXMS)

Resonant soft X-ray and magnetic scattering (RSXMS) are performed at the Soft X-ray Ultraviolet (SUV) beamline, Singapore Synchrotron Light Source (SSLS), Singapore22,23. The RSXMS is an element-sensitive, non-destructive, high-energy resolution, and polarization-sensitive experimental technique (Supplementary Fig. S5). Depending on the scattering geometry (or angles), the probing depth varies from a few to a hundred nanometers, therefore, we can map magnetic and electronic structures making it powerful to probe spin, charge, and orbital modulations from surface to sublayers as well as buried interfaces. The scattering chamber consists of an ultrahigh vacuum (UHV) 6-circle diffractometer with a based pressure of 10-9 mbar. The beamline covers a broad energy range from ultraviolet photons ( ~ 3.5 eV) to soft X-ray photons (~ 1500 eV). The X-ray scattering intensity is measured for linearly polarized light (LnP) with a degree of polarization ~ 95% for RSXS measurement and using left and right circularly polarized X-rays, (LCP and RCP, respectively) with a degree of circular polarization ~ 80% for RSXMS measurements. The reflectivity magnetic dichroism, RMCD(ℏω,θ), is calculated by taking the difference between RLCP(ℏω,θ) and RRCP(ℏω,θ) as shown in Eq. 1. Note that in our previous study, we observe a strong magnetic dichroism in low-dimensional Au/La1.80Ba0.20CuO4/LaAlO3 films with 80% circular polarization, revealing in-plane and out-of-plane ferromagnetism using resonant soft X-ray and magnetic scattering (RSXMS)22. The RSXMS intensity is ~ 50 times stronger than X-ray magnetic circular dichroism (XMCD), enabling it to measure infinitesimally small magnetic signals in the materials. The X-ray scattering intensity is measured by tuning X-ray energy to the O K edge and Cu L2,3 edges and then scattered photons are collected for varying scattering angles using a photodiode to get the total fluorescence yield (TFY) from the sample. The collected spectra have been normalized to the incident X-ray photon intensity first and then with respect to off-resonance energy.

X-Ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS)

The X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) spectra (Supplementary Fig. S3) are collected at the Soft X-ray Ultraviolet (SUV) beamline, Singapore Synchrotron Light Source (SSLS), Singapore22. The XAS is a non-destructive, element-specific, high-energy resolution and offers sensitivity to the local environment. The XAS measurements are taken in total electron yield mode (TEY), and the incident photon energy is varied to follow RSXS and RSXMS (see above). In a very grazing incidence geometry, the sensitivity of XAS is enhanced on the surface region of the material as we probe the electronic and structural properties of the top few nanometers (Supplementary Fig. S3). The measurements are conducted using linearly- and/or circularly polarized X-ray photons. Reflection high-energy electron diffraction (RHEED), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and atomic force microscopy (AFM)

Figure S1 presents the results of the reflection of in situ high-energy electron diffraction (RHEED) (Supplementary Fig. S1a–d), RHEED intensity oscillations during the growth of the sample in pulsed laser deposition (Supplementary Fig. S1e) and high-resolution X-ray diffraction (HR-XRD) (Supplementary Fig. S1f) obtained from the growth and characterizations of 4 uc La2CuO4/LaAlO3 (001). The sample is prepared under ultrahigh vacuum conditions using PLD-MBE. The in situ RHEED intensity oscillations (Supplementary Fig. S1e) together with the in situ RHEED pattern at azimuth angle 45° and 90° exhibiting the same (1 × 1) surface symmetry pattern before and after the growth of La2CuO4 on LaAlO3 (001) with streak pattern, indicate two-dimensional (2D) layer-by-layer epitaxial growth during in-situ pulsed laser deposition (Supplementary Fig. S1a–d). The XRD θ − 2θ scans show strong diffraction patterns corresponding to the La2CuO4 film and LaAlO3 substrate peaks. The crystal structure analysis using a four-circle X-ray diffractometer shows a pseudo-tetragonal crystal structure with the c-axis normal to the substrate surface. The LaAlO3 (001) and (002) substrate peaks are at 23.51° and 47.92°, while La2CuO4 (004), (006), and (008) film peaks are at 26.67°, 41.03°, and 55.65°, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S1f). Density functional theory (DFT)

We perform DFT calculations for the La2CuO4 thin film. We construct a 4-unit cell (4 uc) model of La2CuO4 to calculate the projected density of states and magnetic moments. The model is shown in Supplementary Fig. S21, where a 1 × 1 × 4 La2CuO4 supercell is calculated using the Quantum Espresso simulation package35. Spin-dependent generalized gradient approximation36 (GGA) with Hubbard U (GGA + U) is used with SG15 Optimized Norm Conserving Vanderbilt (ONCV) with frozen-core Projected Augmented Wave (PAW) pseudopotentials37–39. The Hubbard U values are set as Ueff,La=11 eV and Ueff,Cu=7 eV, as conducted by previous theoretical works40. The lattice parameters are a = b = 3.7899 A¨ and c = 13.221 A¨, respectively, which are obtained from high-resolution synchrotron-XRD measurement at the Singapore synchrotron light source (SSLS). To avoid interaction between neighboring structures, 25 Å of vacuum is inserted. The structure relaxation uses a Monkhorst-Pack k-point grid of 2×2×1, where the xy-positions of all atoms are kept stationary, whilst the z-positions are allowed to relax only for the top two-unit cells (surfaces). The fixed lattice parameters in the ab-axes replicate the 4 μc thin film observed experimentally and verified through the Synchrotron-XRD. The relaxation is conducted until the forces on each atom are less than 0.02 eV/ Å. The self-consistent calculations and non-self-consistent calculations are conducted with 5×5×1 and 10×10×1 Monkhorst pack k-point grid, respectively. The cutoff energy for the plane wave basis is set at 50 Ry (~ 680 eV), and the kinetic energy cutoff is set at 200 Ry. To induce a ferromagnetic configuration, the total magnetisation is constrained to be 1 μB /unit cell. The spin-dependent projected density of states (spin-PDOS) and charge density plots are calculated using the projwfc.x and pp.x programs, respectively, provided in the Quantum Espresso package.
