Transcranial focused ultrasound modulates spiking, LFP, and BOLD activity in the primate thalamus
Authors: Ning Zheng, Pai-Feng Yang, M. Anthony Phipps, Jiro Kusunose, Arabinda Mishra, Jixin Xia, William Rodriguez, Allen T. Newton, Benoit M. Dawant, John C. Gore, Charles F. Caskey, Li Min Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transcranial-focused ultrasound (FUS) is a non-invasive neuromodulation technique capable of targeting deep brain regions with high precision. However, its mechanisms of action – particularly how it modulates neuronal activity and relates to fMRI BOLD signals – remain poorly understood. Using the macaque monkey thalamus and insular cortex as a model system, we show that low-intensity FUS evokes localized BOLD increases and preferentially modulates low-frequency (Delta, Theta, and Alpha) local field potentials and single-unit spiking in the ventroposterior lateral (VPL) nucleus, as well as through remote stimulation of the insular cortex. Compared with vibrotactile stimulation, FUS-evoked responses exhibit slower peak latencies. Importantly, we observe strong spatial correspondence between ultrasound-induced changes in spiking, LFP activity, and BOLD signals. These findings define the temporal and spatial characteristics of FUS neuromodulation and demonstrate the feasibility of precise, non-invasive targeting of deep brain circuits, supporting its translational potential for neuroscience and therapeutic applications.

Introduction

Transcranial focused ultrasound (tFUS) has emerged as a powerful noninvasive neuromodulation technique with the unique ability to target deep brain structures with high spatial precision1,2. Over the past decade, a growing body of animal and human neuroimaging studies has demonstrated that low-intensity tFUS can modulate hemodynamic signals measured with functional MRI (fMRI)1–6. Early work showed that tFUS delivered to cortical targets produces localized BOLD signal changes, establishing the feasibility of concurrent tFUS–fMRI in humans and revealing modulation of sensorimotor and visual circuits. Subsequent studies extended these findings to ultra-high-field 7 T fMRI, showing that stimulation of the motor cortex alters BOLD responses both at the target site and in connected regions. More recently, sonication of deep structures has been shown to modify perfusion and functional connectivity3,7–10, and emerging work has begun to characterize non-intuitive or “paradoxical” patterns of tFUS-evoked BOLD activity, including negative BOLD signals that may not straightforwardly reflect decreases in neural activity11,12, highlighting the complexity of neurovascular coupling in the context of ultrasound neuromodulation.

Together, these studies provide important evidence that tFUS can influence human brain activity as measured by fMRI, but the underlying neuronal correlates of these signals remain largely unresolved. A major obstacle to clinical translation, however, is the limited understanding of how ultrasound pulses modulate the electrophysiological activity of a single population of neurons in deep brain regions—either directly or through network pathways—and how these effects relate to fMRI blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) signals widely used to assess human brain function.

Growing preclinical and clinical evidence has demonstrated that low-intensity FUS can modulate neural activity13–17, alter behavior4–7,18–20, and holds strong potential both as a noninvasive tool for neuroscience research21 and as a therapeutic modality for neurological and psychiatric disorders5,22–25. At the neuronal level, FUS has been shown to modulate spiking activity—predominantly in excitatory cortical neurons—in vivo in mice26. Despite these advances, relatively few studies have investigated how FUS affects spiking activity and local field potentials (LFPs) within deep brain structures (for recent reviews13,27). This gap is particularly significant because LFPs reflect neuronal population-level activity and mesoscale neuronal computations28,29. Although several animal studies have individually documented FUS-induced spiking and LFP responses, their relationship to BOLD signals hasn’t been examined within a unified experimental framework. For translational purposes, establishing how neuronal spiking and LFP activity correspond to fMRI signals—particularly within deep brain structures, such as the thalamus—is essential for optimizing tFUS parameters and for bridging preclinical results into human applications, where fMRI remains the dominant tool for studying human brain function in diseases.

This study aims to directly compare BOLD, LFP, and spiking activity within a single experimental setting in a nonhuman primate model. To achieve this goal, we developed an anchor-based MR-guided FUS (MRgFUS) system that integrates (a) real-time validation of sonication accuracy via MR acoustic radiation force imaging (MR-ARFI), (b) in-scanner monitoring of target engagement and neuromodulatory effects using BOLD fMRI, and (c) electronically steered, high-precision FUS delivery aligned with intracranial microelectrodes for electrophysiological recordings outside the scanner. Using this system at 3 T in macaque monkeys, we have previously demonstrated that tFUS can modulate fMRI signals at both targeted cortical and deep-brain (thalamic) regions and their interconnected networks and alter activity in their broader functional networks during painful and nonpainful tactile processing1,30–32. Building on this foundation, the present study has three main objectives: (1) to directly quantify how FUS influences spiking and LFP activity in the ventroposterior lateral (VPL) thalamic nucleus and how these electrophysiological changes relate to FUS-evoked BOLD signals; (2) to determine whether FUS-evoked spiking and LFP activity differ from those elicited by tactile stimulates; and (3) to test whether neuromodulation of the VPL can also be achieved through remote FUS stimulation of the posterior insula cortex - a cortical region exhibited BOLD signal changes during thalamic VPL FUS stimulation. Together, these studies will provide important mechanistic insight into the neuronal underpinnings of tFUS-evoked BOLD signals, inform the optimization of stimulation protocols, and enhance the interpretation of tFUS-based causal mapping and behavioral modulation. They also offer a crucial translational bridge between electrophysiology and fMRI, supporting the development of ultrasound neuromodulation as a precise, noninvasive tool, and guiding the translation of focused ultrasound neuromodulation to human neuroscience and therapeutic applications.

Results

High precision targeting based on MR-ARFI within and outside the MR environment

Integration of the MR-guidance, which combines MR-ARFI, BOLD fMRI, and an anchor-based treatment planning system, enabled high-precision and repeatable transcranial FUS targeting and stimulation of the thalamus33, outside MRI, following an initial MRI calibration scan34. Prior to performing FUS neuromodulation and electrophysiological studies outside the MRI environment, we first used fMRI to identify the VPL and insular cortex that respond to tactile stimulation as FUS targets in each animal. Ultrasound stimulation of the targeted thalamic nucleus elicits robust whole-brain fMRI BOLD activation

In addition to the BOLD activation detected at the targeted thalamic VPL, we found that delivery of ultrasound pulses (parameters described in the “Methods” section) to the thalamic VPL nucleus elicited robust fMRI activation at 27 off-target brain regions, including the insular cortex. The fMRI activation focus identified in the insular cortex was subsequently used as the remote target for FUS stimulation during electrophysiology recordings in these subjects. The group-level whole-brain fMRI activation map is presented in Supplementary Fig. S1. Event-averaged fMRI BOLD time courses extracted from both the VPL nucleus and the posterior insular cortex exhibited hemodynamic responses (Fig. 1d) similar to those observed during tactile or heat stimulation of the hand36,37. BOLD signal increases in the thalamic VPL were stronger in amplitude (mean ± SEM: 0.342 ± 0.015 for VPL and 0.26 ± 0.014 for posterior insular cortex) and reached their peak faster (time to peak: 6.32 ± 0.04 s) than those in the insular cortex (8.157 ± 0.039 s), both evoked by FUS stimulation of the VPL target. We observed robust FUS modulation of BOLD signals at the VPL and off-target effects in the posterior insular cortex and other brain regions.

In contrast, FUS pulses elicited different LFP signal changes, with a marked increase in inward current amplitude (valley or negative-going deflection), followed by a rebound in outward current (peak or positive-going deflections; Fig. 2d). The FUS-induced LFP signal evolved continuously throughout the 500-ms stimulation period (red trace in Fig. 2d), whereas the signal during the resting, no-FUS condition fluctuated near the baseline (blue trace).

At the group level, the z-scored valley-to-peak amplitude of the tactile response (mean ± SEM: 15.33 ± 2.00) was significantly larger (p < 0.001) than that of the FUS responses (10.37 ± 2.89, compare yellow and red columns in Fig. 2e). For FUS stimulation, the latency (time-to-valley) of the primary LFP responses was 144.49 ± 45.30 ms, and the average time-to-peak measure was 341.00 ± 99.01 ms after FUS stimulation onset (Fig. 2f). In contrast, the time-to-valley and time-to-peak for tactile responses were much shorter—approximately 17.00 ± 2.00 and 23.80 ± 4.38 ms, respectively. Overall, both time-to-valley and time-to-peak measures during tactile stimulation were significantly shorter (p < 0.001) than those during FUS stimulation (Fig. 2f), indicating neuron activity occurred much faster in response to tactile stimulation than to FUS.

Temporal features of ultrasound-induced LFP power spectrum in different frequency bands

We next examined the temporal features of the LFP power spectra across five frequency bands (delta:1–4 Hz, theta:5–8 Hz, alpha:9–12 Hz, beta:13–30 Hz, and gamma:30–80 Hz). As shown in the averaged LFP power spectra plots, FUS-induced LFP power changes occurred predominantly in the low-frequency range (<10 Hz; Fig. 3a). During the 500-ms FUS stimulation period, power increases were more pronounced during the early response phase (0–250 ms). In contrast, the LFP power spectra during the noFUS control condition showed no apparent power increases (Fig. 3b). Quantification of the percentage changes in LFP power at a fine temporal resolution (50 ms) (Fig. 3d–i, top panels) confirmed these observations. Compared to the noFUS condition (blue columns), significant LFP power increases (red columns; *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01; two-tailed two-sample t-test) were observed for broadband and all frequency bands except the high-frequency gamma band. Ultrasound-evoked spiking activity in thalamic neurons

Figure 4 shows examples of isolated single-unit spiking activity that responded to FUS pulses across the 32 channels (with 100-µm spacing) of the electrode, which samples electrical activity within the thalamic VPL nucleus (Fig. 4a). During FUS stimulation (indicated by pink background), robust increases in spiking rates, as indicated by peri-stimulus time histogram (PSTH) plots, were detected at nearly all electrode channels (Fig. 4b). The spike waveform isolated at each channel is displayed alongside the PSTH plots. Figure 5 shows the group-level quantification and comparison with tactile stimulus-evoked spiking activity changes. Increased spiking activity—shown in the spike raster plots and PSTH plot of the spiking rate—was sustained throughout the 500-ms FUS stimulation period (Fig. 5a). In contrast, tactile stimulation of the same duration elicited phasic spiking at stimulus onset and offset (Fig. 5b). Figure 5c directly compares the firing curves of FUS versus tactile-responsive units. The baseline firing rate of FUS-responsive neurons is significantly lower than that of tactile-responsive neurons, whereas their maximum firing rate is significantly higher (Fig. 5d, e, p < 0.001). Correspondence between spike, LFP, and fMRI in the context of the brain network

At the network level, FUS delivered to the VPL (Fig. 1) elicited BOLD signal increases in both the VPL and posterior insular cortex, paralleled by increased LFP power and spiking in the VPL when the insular cortex was stimulated (Fig. 6). These findings provide evidence of a direct correspondence between increases in fMRI signals and electrophysiological activity during the same FUS stimulation condition within the same VPL nucleus in each animal. This observation is significant because it reveals a direct and causal functional connection between the posterior insular cortex and the thalamic VPL—two key hubs for processing and integrating tactile and nociceptive information. Discussion

Summary of findings

This work provides an integrated characterization of how transcranial focused ultrasound (FUS) modulates deep-brain activity across single-neuron, population-level, and whole-brain network scales within the same primate preparation. By directly comparing spiking, LFP, and BOLD fMRI signals in the thalamic VPL nucleus, we show that FUS engages thalamic circuits in a manner distinct from natural sensory input, producing unique temporal dynamics and selective modulation of low-frequency LFP power. The strong correspondence between electrophysiological and BOLD responses confirms the neuronal origin of FUS-induced imaging signals. Importantly, we demonstrate that FUS modulation can also occur remotely through functional network connections, as stimulation of the posterior insular cortex robustly altered thalamic activity. These findings provide critical mechanistic insights into how FUS engages deep brain circuits and offer essential guidance for optimizing ultrasound parameters and stimulation protocols for future translational and clinical neuromodulation applications.

Ultrasound pulses elicited low-frequency LFP signals and clinical implications

While spikes—also known as action potentials—are the primary output signals of individual neurons and represent the fundamental unit of neural communication, LFPs mainly reflect input signals—particularly excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs and IPSPs)38. LFPs, recorded from thousands of neurons residing near the electrode, are generated by transmembrane current flow in ensembles of neurons and represent the summated synaptic inputs to a local region38–40. Its importance in systems and cognitive neuroscience has reemerged over the past two decades because it is linked to the neural underpinnings of hemodynamic signals41,42, magnetoencephalographic (MEG), and scalp EEG signals43. It is important to note that subthreshold synaptic activities require metabolic and energy support, which is one main signal source of the BOLD44. Good spatial agreements between LFP, spiking, and BOLD have been reported in multiple systems by many groups, including our own45–47. Here, our results support such correspondence within the VPL in the context of FUS neuromodulation.

In the present study, 650 kHz FUS predominantly induced low-frequency LFP power increases (delta, theta, and alpha), most pronounced in the early temporal phase (<250 ms) of stimulation (Fig. 3). FUS’s modulation of specific LFP frequency bands has been reported previously29,48,49. Similar FUS-induced theta (4–8 Hz) power enhancements have been reported in hippocampal CA1 in mice48, thalamus, and motor cortex of mice28,50. These changes likely arise from subthreshold synaptic interactions between FUS pulses and thalamic neurons, as the thalamus is a major contributor to low-frequency LFP oscillations in the brain51,52.

The mechanism by which ultrasound evokes low-frequency LFP signal changes is an important subject for future investigation. Established mechanisms of action, including modulation of mechanosensitive channels and TRP channels53–56 (Piezo1/2, TRP channels), may contribute to the detected LFP signal changes, which reflect integration of subthreshold postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs and IPSPs) populations rather than from action potentials themselves38,39,44. Subthreshold potentials are membrane potential changes that do not produce spikes but contribute substantially to the extracellular field. The ability of FUS to modulate low-frequency LFPs has important clinical implications. Delta power is associated with deep sleep, while theta and alpha power are linked to cognitive and sensory processes. Modulating low-frequency LFP activity with FUS could provide a valuable approach for influencing brain function and restoring altered LFP power in disease conditions.

FUS-induced LFP activities are slower than tactile processing

The markedly slower LFP signal changes in the thalamus during FUS stimulation, compared to tactile processing, seem to align with previous reports, where FUS-induced cortical activity and behavioral responses were slower than those produced by natural motor56 or optogenetic stimulation57. These slower responses suggest a fundamentally different cellular mechanism for ultrasound neuromodulation. Because LFP signals reflect synaptic activities rather than outgoing spiking activity, their slow onset and peak may result from the additional time required to modulate subthreshold synaptic processes and neuronal excitability13,58,59. It is also possible that FUS-induced neuronal spiking activity is less synchronized and/or engages a more heterogeneous population of local neurons compared with the highly synchronized activity seen in homogenous populations during tactile processing. The distinct phasic responses to tactile stimuli confirm that FUS-evoked neuronal activity in the same region and with the same electrode is genuine and not an artifact. Differences in LFP and spiking activity between FUS and tactile stimuli may also reflect whether neurons are activated directly by ultrasound or indirectly via ascending inputs from downstream regions, such as the spinal cord. Both FUS- and tactile-responsive neurons also exhibited distinct baseline tonic firing rates (Fig. 5), with FUS pulses not disrupting this ongoing activity, which likely supports thalamocortical communication.

In this study, the latency of measurable LFP or spiking changes is typically ≥100 ms. This slow response could be explained by a few factors. Low-intensity FUS primarily operates via mechanical perturbation (see supporting temperature simulation in Supplementary Fig. S7) that acts either directly or indirectly on neurons. This may involve mechano-electrical or mechano-chemical delays when FUS interacts with neurons, or synaptic delays following modulating astrocytes54,56,60. Unlike electrical or optogenetic stimulation, which directly injects current into the extracellular space or cell membrane or uses light-sensitive ion channels or pumps to rapidly depolarize neurons and evoke spiking within 1–10 ms57,61, ultrasound engages mechanosensitive ion channels15,53,54,56, whose activation is inherently slower, as it involves mechanical deformation, membrane stress or capacitance changes, and downstream biochemical cascades, and possibly astrocytic or vascular responses62–64. These processes do not immediately depolarize neurons but modulate excitability (i.e., change the likelihood of firing), alter synaptic activity, or shift network state.

The relatively low proportion of FUS-responsive units (~8.3%) compared to tactile-responsive units (~50%) likely reflects differences in their neuron recruitments. FUS-responsive and tactile-responsive neurons may represent partially distinct neuronal populations. Peripheral tactile stimulation robustly engages large glutamatergic thalamocortical relay neurons in the thalamus and activates as well as local GABAergic interneurons. In contrast, FUS neuromodulation may recruit a more limited or selective subset of neurons. Emerging evidence suggests that FUS, depending on parameters, may preferentially modulate smaller or inhibitory neuronal populations. To more fully characterize the cellular specificity of FUS neuromodulation, future studies employing techniques capable of monitoring both excitatory and inhibitory populations (e.g., two-photon calcium imaging or cell-type-specific recordings) will be necessary.

FUS neuromodulation of the brain network

The ability to modulate the network is a desirable feature for neuromodulation since many mental and neurological disorders are characterized by network changes, and perturbation of the network has become a therapeutic approach. In the present study, we purposely selected the thalamic VPL and the insular cortex as FUS targets for several reasons. First, we have consistently detected fMRI activation and strong functional connectivity in both regions during tactile and thermal nociceptive stimulation of the hand, and during FUS stimulation of the VPL itself1,2,36, indicating strong functional connectivity and suggesting that the poster insular cortex serves as an effective remote driver of thalamic activity. Second, our previous graph-theory-based network analysis identified the posterior insula as a key hub connecting the somatosensory circuit to higher-order cognitive and emotional networks—a finding supported by human fMRI and FUS modulation studies24,37,65. Importantly, ultrasound stimulation of the posterior insula has been shown to reduce pain ratings and temporal summation in human subjects24,66, highlighting its translational relevance. Third, our extensive fMRI mapping of these networks in macaques has delineated functional subregions within both the VPL and the insular cortex, along with their anatomical connections, establishing a firm foundation for the present investigation focused on thalamic VPL, a primary relay station for processing and integrating tactile and painful information.

That remote FUS stimulation of the insular cortex produced thalamic responses similar to those evoked from direct VPL stimulation (Fig. 6) supports the conclusion that FUS-induced electrophysiology changes are genuine, arising from direct neural modulation rather than artifacts. Robust LFP and spiking activity recorded in the thalamic VPL nucleus, where BOLD signal increases were detected (Fig. 1), during remote FUS stimulation of the posterior insular cortex confirmed that FUS can modulate not only the activity of the targeted region but also downstream areas via network connections.

From a neuroscience perspective, the observation that FUS applied to the VPL elicits responses in the posterior insula, and vice versa, supports a causal functional influence between the VPL and the insular cortex (Fig. 1). To explore the directionality between these two regions. We have conducted ongoing anatomical tracing studies in these animals. AAV tracer injections into the thalamic VPL nucleus revealed direct anatomical projections from the VPL to the posterior insular cortex (unpublished observations). These findings provide anatomical support for a direct pathway consistent with the functional effects observed here. In addition, prior anatomical tracing studies in non-human primates have demonstrated direct connections between the insular cortex and the thalamus67–70 supporting the existence of reciprocal structural connectivity between these regions. Taken together, our FUS perturbation results, along with the anatomical evidence, support a bidirectional relationship between these regions. Future studies (e.g., pathway-specific manipulations or simultaneous recordings) are required to establish directionality and circuit mechanisms more definitively.

Spatial agreement between FUS-evoked BOLD, LFP, and spiking signals

Ultrasound-induced BOLD, LFP/EEG, or spiking activity has been reported in previous independent studies. But variability in FUS parameters and model systems has limited direct comparison. Our experimental design enabled assessment of these three signals in the same animals. Detection of FUS-elicited activations of neurons by in vivo electrophysiology confirms the neuronal basis of the fMRI signal changes observed in the targeted thalamus VPL and off-target posterior insular cortex (Fig. 1). Further studies are needed to better understand neurovascular coupling during FUS neuromodulation, which will be essential for optimizing protocols, especially when using BOLD fMRI to monitor treatment effects. To our knowledge, this is the first direct within-subject link between fMRI and electrophysiological measures in the context of FUS neuromodulation of a deep brain structure. Because fMRI is widely available clinically, understanding its relationship to LFP and spiking activity will facilitate the translation of FUS neuromodulation to patient care and guide FUS parameter selection for disorders when fMRI can serve as a monitoring tool.

Technical considerations and limitations of the study

We achieved precise FUS targeting and steering within the thalamus and posterior insular cortex using an MRI-guided 128-element transducer array with MR-ARFI validation and a bone-anchor-based frame system. By being rigidly affixed to the skull, the frame-based system overcomes known fundamental limitations with non-invasive fiducial-based solutions, such as optical tracking, that have errors on the order of multiple millimeters, which would not be sufficient to ensure accurate targeting for our present study that uses a transducer that creates a focal ellipsoidal beam with full-width at half maximum dimensions of 2.2 × 2.2 × 9.3 mm[3,72–74. The simulation of full free field pressure maps at the VPL on three different image planes is presented in Fig. S2.

FUS induced comparable spiking waveforms to tactile stimulation, with no millisecond-scale temporal locking to pulses (Fig. S5), arguing against recording artifacts. Remote stimulation of the insular cortex produced thalamic responses without direct ultrasound–electrode interaction, confirming that observed effects are neuronal. These findings show that FUS can modulate both local targets and downstream regions, with region-specific effects. Limitations of the present study include a small subject number and unbalanced sampling sizes between tactile and FUS conditions, and slight variations in sampling locations. Nevertheless, the effects were statistically significant.

The fundamental pulse characteristics were comparable across the fMRI and electrophysiological experiments, consisting of a 500 ms focused ultrasound (FUS) burst with a 50% duty cycle and a 1 kHz pulse repetition frequency (PRF). The primary differences were the use of a higher estimated acoustic pressure and a longer inter-sonication interval (ISI; time between bursts) in the electrophysiological experiments. In the fMRI studies, ultrasound pulses were delivered as part of a block design paradigm, with pulses occurring every 2 s during a 16-s stimulation block. In contrast, the electrophysiology experiments consisted of repeated single pulse trials separated by a 5-s ISI, with 20–50 trials per run and multiple runs conducted per day. These parameter choices were guided by empirical considerations related to signal detectability for electrophysiology versus BOLD fMRI and experimental safety. While the fMRI protocol reliably produced measurable BOLD responses at the pressures used, we found that higher acoustic pressures were necessary to obtain robust electrophysiological signals. Additionally, the longer ISI used during electrophysiology was implemented to mitigate potential thermal risk, given both the higher pressure levels and the longer overall pulse trains relative to the fMRI experiments (20–50 pulses per run versus 8 pulses per block). As shown by our thermal simulations (Supplementary Material), the chosen parameters did not result in significant tissue heating.

Because the mechanisms underlying FUS neuromodulation are not fully understood, it is possible that differences in stimulation parameters could lead to differences in neural effects. Importantly, our intent was not to make direct quantitative comparisons between the fMRI and electrophysiological outcomes, as these data were acquired in separate sessions under modality-specific optimized conditions. Rather, the goal was to obtain complementary readouts of FUS-induced neural modulation using two different measurement approaches.

We acknowledge that isoflurane anesthesia can influence thalamocortical activity, LFP spectral properties, and neurovascular coupling. In the present study, all data were acquired under anesthesia, and our experimental design—particularly the separation of fMRI and electrophysiological recordings—does not allow us to directly assess how brain state affects FUS-induced responses or their cross-modal coupling. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted within the anesthetized condition. At the same time, our primary conclusion focuses on the spatial correspondence of FUS-evoked responses across modalities, rather than on precise response magnitude, temporal correspondence, or coupling strength. Prior work from our group suggests that isoflurane predominantly reduces response amplitude and functional connectivity strength, while preserving the overall spatial organization of cortical activity74–78. Consistent with this, our previous electrophysiological studies indicate that the spatial distribution of cortical LFP activity remains largely intact under anesthesia, despite reduced signal magnitude. Based on these considerations, we expect that anesthesia may affect the strength and spectral characteristics of the responses but is less likely to fundamentally alter the spatial patterns that are central to our conclusions. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that this cannot be directly tested within the current study. Future experiments in awake animals, particularly with simultaneous multimodal recordings (e.g., combined fMRI, electrophysiology, and ultrasound stimulation), will be important to further evaluate state-dependent effects.

Methods

Macaque monkeys and study approval

Four monkeys participated in fMRI experiments, and two macaque monkeys (two female Macaca fascicularis and two male Macaca mulatta) participated in electrophysiology studies. The monkeys weighed between 4.1 and −12.8 kg and were aged 13–19 years. Throughout the study, researchers and animal care staff monitored the monkeys daily to ensure their health and well-being. All animal care, housing, and experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee at Vanderbilt University. Vanderbilt University is an AALAC-accredited research institution.

Animal preparation

Animals were pre-anesthetized with ketamine hydrochloride (10 mg/kg)/atropine (0.05 mg/kg) and then anesthetized with 0.5–1.2% of isoflurane delivered with either pure oxygen for fMRI scans or a 70/30 N2O/O2 gas mixture to maintain a stable physiological condition for electrophysiological experiments. The anesthetized animals were intubated and artificially ventilated. The animal was placed in a stereotaxic frame with its head secured using ear bars and eye bars. Lactated Ringer’s solution was infused intravenously (2–3 ml/h/kg) to prevent dehydration during the course of the study. Arterial blood oxygen saturation and heart rate (Nonin, Plymouth, MN), electrocardiogram, end-tidal CO2 (ET-CO2; 22–26 mmHg; Surgivet, Waukesha, WI), and respiration (SA Instruments, Stony Brook, NY) were externally monitored and maintained. Body temperature was monitored (SA Instruments) and maintained between 37.5 and 38.5 °C via a circulating water blanket (Gaymar Industries, Orchard Park, NY). Real-time monitoring was maintained from the time of induction of anesthesia until full recovery. Detailed procedures were similar to those previously reported79–81. All procedures were conducted in accordance with National Institutes of Health guidelines and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committees at Vanderbilt University.

Stimulation protocol

Animals’ fingers were secured by gluing small pegs to the fingernails and fixing these pegs firmly in plasticine, leaving the glabrous surfaces available for vibrotactile stimulation of a distal finger pad. The vibrotactile stimulation was generated by a round plastic probe (2 mm in diameter) controlled by a piezoelectric device (Noliac, Kvistgaard, Denmark). The piezo was driven by Grass S48 square wave stimulators (Grass-Telefactor, West Warwick, RI). The probe was in light contact with the skin before the vibrotactile stimuli were delivered. For fMRI data acquisitions, vertical indentations of two probes (in 0.48 mm displacement) on two distal finger pads at 8 Hz rate (with 20 ms pulse duration) were presented as 30 s on/off blocks. Seven blocks were typically presented within one imaging run. Typically, 3–5 runs were collected in each fMRI session (day).

In electrophysiological recording sessions, vertical indentations of a probe at 8 Hz were presented on a distal pad for 500 ms, followed by a 5.0 s baseline period and repeated 20-50 times per run. Typically, in each session, 10–30 runs were collected, and within each run, 20–50 trials were acquired and averaged to increase the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).

FUS parameters and presentation paradigm for fMRI

We employed a custom-designed, MR-compatible, 128-element transducer (radius of curvature = 72 mm, outer diameter = 103 mm, element diameter = 6.6 mm, free-field full-width half max 9.3 and 2.2 mm in the axial and lateral directions)35,71, manufactured by Imasonic (Besancon, France) was used in the current study. A driving system (Image Guided Therapy, Pessac, France) controlled the sonication parameters. The transducer was positioned around the midline of the head. The scalp was shaved, and transmission gel was applied to the scalp to ensure optimal acoustic coupling. A custom water cone was filled with a degassed solution of approximately 90% D2O (also called heavy water or deuterium oxide) and 10% H2O. The D2O was used to replace most of the H2O to reduce signal intensities and geometric distortions in MR images due to ineffective shimming associated with the water concentration in the cone.

The FUS stimulation blocks consisted of trains of 650 kHz pulses82, 500 ms pulse train duration, pulse repetition frequency (PRF) of 1 kHz, pulse duration of 0.5 ms, and duty cycle of 50%. This pulse train was repeated at 0.5 Hz for 16 s, followed by a minimum 30-s rest period. Individual pulses were ramped up and down (ramp duration 50 μs) to reduce the spectral content in the auditory range (16 to 35–45 Hz for macaque monkeys)83,84. Our targeted in vivo pressure was 550 kPa. For each targeted location (thalamic VPL nucleus or posterior insular cortex), the free-field pressure was estimated using a calibrated hydrophone (HNC 0400, Onda Corp., Sunnyvale, CA), and one example of the simulated target at the thalamic VPL nucleus is shown in Fig. S2. Calibrations of the transducer were performed to compensate for pressure reductions during steering by steering the beam in 1 mm steps, in the transducer’s lateral direction, steering out to 7 mm at low pressure (MI < 1). Based on previous water tank measurements with an ex vivo NHP skull cap, we estimated transmission to be 39% of the free field value. Steering compensation was performed based on hydrophone measurements of the beam during lateral steering of the transducer33. The FUS parameters used in this study have previously been shown to increase BOLD response at rest and to inhibit BOLD response during tasks1,31,85,86.

FUS parameters and presentation paradigm for electrophysiology

For electrophysiological recording sessions, a bone-anchor-based high-precision FUS targeting and steering system (Fig. 2a) described and characterized elsewhere34 was employed to position and secure the transducer to the animal’s skull. Prior to the transducer securement, the animal’s scalp was shaved, and a coupling gel was applied. Unlike the fMRI studies, the custom cone was filled with degassed, deionized water. FUS pulses were delivered with a 500 ms pulse train duration, a PRF of 1 kHz, a pulse duration of 0.5 ms, and a 50% duty cycle. The pulse train was repeated with an inter-stimulation interval (ISI) of 5 s and a burst duration (BD) of 500 ms (see supplemental Tables S1–S4). The targeted in vivo pressures used for subject 1 ranged from 1.00 to 1.05 MPa, and for subject 2, a range of 0.70 to 1.45 MPa. These pressures were estimated by assuming 39% transmission of the free field pressure through the skull based on previous ex vivo skull data. The pressure at each steering location was compensated by using a free-field steering calibration. Our transcranial acoustic simulations (described below) at the geometric focus (unsteered) of the transducer showed transmission percentages of 45.7% in subject 1 and 53.8% in subject 2. This would result in in vivo pressures of 1.17–1.23 MPa and 0.97–2.00 MPa in the brain for subject 1 and subject 2, respectively.

Two targets were considered for this study: the right thalamus (VPL region) and the right insula. Both targets were functionally localized using tactile stimulation prior to the ultrasound experiment (Fig. 1b). For the VPL target, we sonicated at the estimated target location and a grid of points around that target up to 4 mm in each direction. LFP and spike analyses were performed on data acquired from a subset of target locations (thalamus: 35 out of 109 locations and 41 runs (30–50 trials per run); Insula: 3 out of 3 locations and 4 runs). For each run, 20–50 trials of FUS stimulations were delivered, and the electrophysiological recordings were averaged to increase the SNR. FUS electronic steering was calibrated by acquiring MR-ARFI (described below) before electrophysiological studies were conducted.

FUS simulations

Ultrasound simulations were performed to estimate in vivo pressures from the driving free-field pressure. All simulations were performed using the MATLAB acoustic toolbox, k-Wave87, and followed the methods detailed in previous work with modifications described below88. Ultrasound pulses were simulated using the k-Wave createCWSignals function with the amplitudes associated with the free-field pressure. Simulations were performed with a grid size of [450, 450, and 400] and a voxel size of 0.25 mm3. Subjects’ CTs were registered to the MR image space using the software, Slicer, and its “General Registration (BRAINS)” module89. The CT-data were used to estimate the speed of sound, density, and attenuation through the skull90. Estimates of temperature deposition were simulated in k-Wave using the kWaveDiffusion class. Voxel sizes of thermal simulations were the same as pressure simulations, and heterogeneous medium parameters were used based on CT images as described in Supplementary Information (Table S5). The same procedure was performed on two different subjects.

Transducer elements were registered to the transducer surfaces in T2-weighted MR images through fiducial registrations based on subject-specific stereotactic frames described in previous work34. Pressure amplitudes were recorded throughout the simulation grid. 10 × 10 × 10 mm ROIs were selected around the natural focus to determine the maximum pressure amplitudes in the brains, as opposed to the absolute maximum pressure, which is likely to occur in the skulls35. The maximum in vivo peak negative pressure was estimated to be 2.00 MPa based on simulations (see Supplementary Materials for full simulation results). We computed the Thermal index at the cranial bone (TIC) to be 7.4 based on the following. The total highest electrical power of the multi-channel amplifier was 45.2 W transmitted via a matching network to a transducer with a reported efficiency of 60%. The duty cycle of the pulse during the 150-s neuromodulation block was 5%, giving a time-average power of 1.36 W. The transducer was approximately 7.0 cm away from the skull, resulting in an equivalent active aperture of 4.6 cm. The TIC is therefore (1000 × 45.2 × 0.6 × 0.05)/(40 × 4.6) = 7.4. ITRUSST does not recommend TIC above 6.0, so we additionally performed thermal simulations (Fig. S7) for both experiment subjects with the highest transmit power used in the study91. With subject 1, we measured an estimated 2.46 °C rise in the skull during the 150-s block with 2.69 MPa free-field pressure. For subject 2, we performed two sonication runs at 3.72 MPa free-field pressure, which induced an estimated 2.36 °C rise in temperature in the skull. Maximum temperature increases in the brain remain below 2 °C for both subjects.

MRI data acquisition and analysis

Four monkeys underwent five MRI and FUS stimulation sessions. MRI scans were conducted using a Philips 3 T Ingenia CX, equipped with a pair of FLEX surface coils (inner diameter = 12 cm) positioned on either side of the head. We acquired three types of MR images: (1) whole-brain high-resolution T1-weighted images (TE = 4.6 ms, TR = 9.9 ms, 500 × 512 × 512 matrix, voxel size of 0.5 × 0.49 × 0.49 mm3, and a flip angle of 8°). (2) MR-ARFI (acoustic radiation force imaging): MR-ARFI measures transient tissue displacements induced by a brief FUS pulse35, offering a real-time visualization of the sonication field. For the MR-ARFI data acquisition, we implemented sonication at a frequency of 650 kHz for 8.5 ms, with a maximum estimated in vivo pressure of 3.5 MPa and a duty cycle of 0.85%. Our previous research35,71 described in detail the equations that relate to these image volumes (optically tracked FUS beam, MR-ARFI displacement image, and anatomic brain image). (3) fMRI data: BOLD fMRI time series data were acquired using a single-shot GE-EPI sequence (TE = 30 ms, TR = 2 s, voxel size of 1.43 × 1.43 × 2 mm3, 112 × 112 × 36 matrix size). Each fMRI run consisted of 493 imaging volumes, with three experimental conditions (heat alone, heat + FUS, and FUS alone) presented 7 times (epochs) for each condition in a randomly interleaved manner. Each stimulus on/off circle consisted of 16 s of FUS on and 30 s of FUS off (baseline) periods. A total of 8 pulse trains consisting of 500 individual FUS pulses were delivered during the 16-s stimulation period. Typically, 3–4 runs were acquired per session. The median fMRI (EPI) image was registered to higher-resolution T1-weighted images acquired in the same geometry for display. FMRI EPI data underwent standard pre-processing steps, and activation maps and time courses were derived from GLM model-based statistical analysis. Resulting statistical maps were thresholded and then linearly interpolated and registered to the high-resolution T1-weighted anatomical images for display. All fMRI activation maps were later registered to the macaque monkey atlas (NMT v2) for group-level quantification using SPM12/MATLAB19. Details of the procedures are similar to what has been described in our previous publications1. For ROI-based time-course analyses, BOLD signal change was quantified as the mean percentage signal change during the 16-s ultrasound stimulation period relative to baseline (6 s, 3 data points before stimulus onset). Effect sizes were quantified as Cohen’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${d}_{z}$$\end{document}dz based on these stimulation-minus-baseline differences. Exact p-values were obtained using paired t-tests, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for the mean paired difference.

Identification of hand representation within the thalamic VPL for recording

In each animal, hand representation was mapped first before data acquisition. We used pre-acquired fMRI maps and stereotaxic coordinates of the hand regions of the thalamic VPL nucleus in macaque monkeys to guide the placement of the electrode deep into the thalamus (Fig. S3). Under isoflurane anesthesia, a craniotomy was performed, and the dura was removed for mapping and recording. Single microelectrodes (FHC Inst., Bowdoinham, ME) that were epoxylite-coated tungsten with exposed standard sharp tip (<3 μm) of ~1 MΩ impedance (measured at 1 kHz) penetrated through brain regions and advanced into the targeted territories. At each electrode penetration site, the microelectrode was advanced in 300–500 µm increments, and its tip depth was tracked and logged. Preferred stimulus type (i.e., stroke, squeeze, and light touch), response strength (scored in six levels), and receptive field properties were characterized. Receptive fields of neurons were identified by palpating skin areas on the contralateral hand, arm, and face to the recording side while listening to the audio amplifier for spike activity and viewing traces of action potentials on a display. After the completion of the mapping, we were able to identify the hand and arm regions within the VPL nucleus based on the characteristics of receptive field properties, preferred stimuli, and somatotopic organization of the digits92–94.

Electrophysiological data acquisition and analysis

Data acquisition

Electrophysiological recordings were performed on two subjects under light anesthesia that was similar to that for fMRI data acquisition. Spiking and LFP activity were then recorded by a 32-channel linear electrode array with a Multichannel Acquisition Processor System (Blackrock Neurotech, Salt Lake City, Utah). Electrophysiological signals were passed through a unit-gain head stage and a pre-amplifier through which each input channel was separated into two output channels that underwent different analog filtering, with one channel recording the higher frequencies of neuronal spikes and the other channel recording the lower frequency LFP signals. Spike events were detected online using predefined amplitude thresholds (current deflection) and waveform shape criteria, and recorded as multiunit activity with timestamps, rather than as continuous broadband signals. This approach reduced data volume and computational demands during simultaneous recordings.

Analysis

Raw LFP data were notch-filtered at 60 and 120 Hz to remove the power line frequency interference, and then a band-pass filter between 1 and 150 Hz was applied. After filtering, LFP temporal and spectral analyses were performed using the FieldTrip toolbox95 and custom-written codes in Matlab (The MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). For the LFP temporal signal, the data were separated into trials of the peri-event period, which was identified as between 1 s before and 1.5 s after stimulus onset time. For each run, event-averaged LFP time series were first computed across trials, and the valley-to-peak amplitude and latency measures were extracted from the run-averaged tracer. Time series at channels (out of 32 channels in total) with the strongest LFP responses within each run were selected for group analysis. Power spectra were computed by ft_freqanalysis program with hanning window apodization and 0.25 s window length. The period from 500 ms before stimulus onset to the beginning of the stimulus was considered the baseline. For presentation, the baseline normalization of power spectra was conducted by subtracting and then dividing their corresponding baseline signals. For quantification and group analysis, the raw power values were normalized to the total power as a sum across the frequency ranges 1–80 Hz and further represented as % of the total sum96 LFP signals were separated into delta band (1–4 Hz), theta band (5–8 Hz), alpha band (9–12 Hz), beta band (13–30 Hz), and gamma band (31–80 Hz)47,97. LFP power was quantified separately within predefined frequency bands. For each band, run-level normalized power values were compared between FUS and noFUS control, or between FUS and tactile stimulation.

For spike analysis, the spike data containing the time stamps and waveforms were imported into Offline Sorter software (Plexon, Dallas, TX, USA), and spike sorting was performed using the Valley Seeking algorithm and outlier waveforms were manually removed. A total of 1233 units were sorted during FUS stimulation, and 102 units were responsive to FUS pulses (8.27%). A total of 452 units were sorted during tactile stimulation, and 227 units were tactile responsive (50.2%). The firing rates of the units were computed using NeuroExplorer (bin size = 20 ms). The average firing rate during baseline (250 ms pre-stimulus to stimulus onset) and maximum firing rate during stimulation (500 ms duration of stimulus) were measured and compared between FUS- and tactile-responsive units. Spiking activity was analyzed at the level of single units. Spike trains were used to construct peri-stimulus time histograms (PSTHs), and firing rates were quantified within defined time windows. Units were classified as responsive if their firing rates during stimulation exceeded the upper bound of the 95% confidence interval of baseline activity. For population-level comparisons (e.g., FUS-responsive vs tactile-responsive units), analyses were conducted at the neuron level with neurons treated as independent observations.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of LFP and spiking data was conducted with GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, USA). Data were compared between the two groups according to their distribution. Normally distributed data were analyzed using Welch’s t-test. The effect size for parametric comparisons was reported as Cohen’s d, calculated as the mean difference divided by the pooled standard deviation. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. The effect size for nonparametric comparisons was reported as the superiority probability, defined as the probability that a randomly selected value from one group exceeds a randomly selected value from the other group. All the data were represented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). p < 0.05 was considered to indicate a significant difference. “ns” represents “p > 0.05,” “∗” represents “p < 0.05,” “∗∗” represents “p < 0.01,” “∗∗∗” represents “p < 0.001,” “∗∗∗∗” represents “p < 0.0001.” Sample sizes (n) and statistical methods are included in the respective figure legends.
