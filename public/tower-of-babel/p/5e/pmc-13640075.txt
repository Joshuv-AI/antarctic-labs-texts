Retrospective radiographic assessment of marginal bone level changes and peri-implant trabecular bone patterns around implants healed with healing abutments or cover screws
Authors: Mehmet Gümüş Kanmaz, Elif Aslan, Burcu Kanmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to describe changes in marginal bone levels (MBL) and peri-implant trabecular radiomorphometric patterns during the osseointegration period in posterior mandibular implants healed with either healing abutments or cover screws.MethodsThis retrospective radiographic cohort study included 53 posterior mandibular implants in 36 patients, classified into the healing abutment group (n = 22) and the cover screw group (n = 31), based on the immediate postoperative panoramic radiographs. Digital panoramic radiographs obtained before implant placement (T0), immediately after implant placement (T1), and after a 3-month osseointegration period (T2) were analyzed. Marginal bone level (MBL) changes and radiomorphometric indices, including fractal dimension (FD), lacunarity, bone area fraction (BAF), and mean gray value (MGV), were calculated.ResultsMBL values decreased significantly from T1 to T2 in both groups (p < 0.05). No statistically significant between-group difference in MBL change was detected at either the mesial or distal surface (p > 0.05). In the overall cohort, mean lacunarity decreased, whereas mean FD, BAF, and MGV increased. Group-based analyses showed significant longitudinal changes in all radiomorphometric parameters in the cover screw group, while only FD changed significantly in the healing abutment group (p < 0.05). For the overall mean radiomorphometric values, no statistically significant between-group differences were detected at T2 (p > 0.05).ConclusionsPosterior mandibular implants showed MBL reductions and radiomorphometric changes during the 3-month osseointegration period. MBL changes did not differ significantly between groups, while radiomorphometric trajectories differed descriptively. These findings provide descriptive radiographic information on early peri-implant bone changes and should be interpreted within the limitations of the retrospective cohort design.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09238-x.

Background

Dental implants have become a predictable and widely adopted treatment modality for the rehabilitation of partial and complete edentulism [1]. However, the long-term clinical success of this therapy depends on stable osseointegration and the preservation of peri-implant hard and soft tissues [2–5]. Peri-implant tissue stability is influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including patient-related characteristics, effective supportive care, implant macro- and micro-design features, and the specific surgical protocols employed during placement [6–9].

Dental implants are placed using either a one-stage or two-stage surgical protocol [10]. In the two-stage protocol, the implant is initially submerged with a cover screw and uncovered at a subsequent surgical procedure, whereas in the one-stage protocol, a healing abutment is connected at the time of implant placement. The choice between these approaches is generally based on clinical judgment and may be influenced by factors such as primary stability, perceived local bone conditions, soft-tissue conditions, implant site characteristics, and the perceived risk of early loading [11, 12].

Radiographic examination is routinely used to monitor peri-implant marginal bone levels during implant follow-up, with intraoral and panoramic radiographs being commonly used in clinical practice [13, 14]. In addition to conventional linear measurements, radiomorphometric and texture-based analyses may provide supplementary information on the trabecular bone pattern around implants. In this regard, fractal analysis (FA) has been used as a non-invasive method to evaluate radiographic trabecular complexity through fractal dimension (FD), while lacunarity provides information on spatial heterogeneity within the trabecular pattern [15–17]. Furthermore, bone area fraction (BAF) and mean gray value (MGV) have also been used as radiographic texture-related parameters to characterize peri-implant trabecular bone patterns [18].

Although marginal bone level (MBL) measurements are widely used in implant follow-up, data combining linear marginal bone assessment with radiomorphometric indices during the early osseointegration period remain limited. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to describe early MBL changes and peri-implant trabecular radiomorphometric patterns around posterior mandibular implants healed with either healing abutments or cover screws during the 3-month osseointegration period.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This retrospective radiographic cohort study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) recommendations for observational cohort studies. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Izmir Tinaztepe University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2026/28). Ethics approval was obtained before retrospective record screening, data extraction, radiographic measurements, and statistical analyses were performed for research purposes.

Patient and implant selection

A systematic screening of patient records and digital panoramic radiographs was performed for patients who received dental implants at the Faculty of Dentistry, Izmir Tinaztepe University, between 01/2024 and 01/2026. To reduce heterogeneity related to implant macro-design and anatomical region, the study was limited to a single implant brand and model (Grand Morse; Neodent, Curitiba, Brazil) placed exclusively in the posterior mandible. According to the available surgical records, all included implants were placed by a single experienced periodontist using the manufacturer’s recommended drilling sequence under copious sterile saline irrigation. Following elevation of a full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap and osteotomy preparation, implants were positioned approximately 1–2 mm subcrestally relative to the adjacent alveolar bone crest, consistent with the routine placement protocol for the Morse taper implant system used.

Study groups

Based on the immediate postoperative panoramic radiographs and surgical records, implants were classified into two retrospectively defined groups according to the healing component used after implant placement. Implants with a cover screw were classified as the cover screw group, whereas implants with a healing abutment were classified as the healing abutment group. In the present study, implants in the cover screw group were managed with a submerged/buried healing approach, whereas implants in the healing abutment group were managed with a non-submerged/transmucosal healing approach. Therefore, the healing method was consistent within each retrospectively defined group and did not vary as an additional subgroup-level factor. The component used at implant placement was based on the clinician’s surgical decision at the time of surgery, mainly considering implant-site conditions such as primary stability, perceived local bone conditions, soft-tissue characteristics, and local anatomical factors. These criteria were not predefined or consistently quantified in the retrospective records.

Radiographic acquisition and assessment

All digital panoramic radiographs had been acquired using the MyRay Hyperion X5 system (Cefla, Imola, Italy) with standardized exposure parameters of 76 kV, 7 mA, and an exposure time of 14.2 s. Radiographic measurements and image analyses were performed on these digital radiographs by a single oral and maxillofacial radiologist with six years of clinical experience. All evaluations were conducted using Fiji software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). For linear radiographic measurements, dimensional calibration was performed separately for each implant image using the known actual implant length obtained from the surgical records. In Fiji software, the implant length was first measured along the long axis of the implant on the panoramic radiograph using the straight-line measurement tool. The measured pixel distance was then calibrated to the corresponding actual implant length in millimeters using the “Set Scale” function, thereby generating an image-specific pixel-to-millimeter conversion factor. All subsequent MBL measurements on the mesial and distal implant surfaces were performed after this calibration. This procedure was used to reduce measurement error related to panoramic magnification; however, it could not eliminate distortion related to projection geometry or two-dimensional image acquisition.

Marginal bone level (MBL) analysis

Marginal bone levels were measured at the mesial and distal aspects of each implant on T1 and T2 panoramic radiographs. The implant shoulder was used as the reference point. The radiographic vertical position of the marginal bone crest adjacent to the implant surface was recorded as a signed value relative to the implant shoulder. Bone crests positioned coronal to the implant shoulder were assigned positive values, whereas bone crests positioned apical to the implant shoulder were assigned negative values. MBL change was calculated as the T2 value minus the T1 value; negative change values indicated an apical shift of the marginal bone crest, corresponding to marginal bone loss, whereas positive values indicated a coronal shift.

Radiomorphometric and fractal analysis

All radiographic evaluations were conducted in a dimly lit room using a high-resolution diagnostic monitor to minimize visual artifacts. To improve longitudinal comparability of the radiographic evaluations, T0, T1, and T2 digital panoramic radiographs were spatially aligned using the TurboReg plugin prior to region of interest (ROI) selection, as previously applied by Tözüm et al. [19] in a peri-implant fractal analysis study using longitudinal panoramic radiographs.

In the T0 radiographs, the corresponding future implant site was identified by superimposing the spatially aligned T0 image with the T1 postoperative image, on which the implant position and long axis were clearly visible. The “Add Image” function in Fiji was used to overlay the aligned images and determine the mesial, distal, and apical peri-implant locations on the T0 radiograph. After the ROI dimensions and coordinates had been defined on the superimposed images, the Edit > Selection > Specify command was used to reproduce ROIs of identical size and position on the individual T0, T1, and T2 images. Thus, the longitudinal radiomorphometric measurements were obtained from anatomically corresponding regions across all three time points, as described in previous peri-implant radiographic studies [20, 21].

To assess radiomorphometric changes in the trabecular bone pattern, rectangular ROIs were selected in the peri-implant bone at three specific locations: mesial, distal, and apical to the implant. The ROI selection procedure was adapted from the peri-implant panoramic radiomorphometric approach described by Dündar et al. [22]. The ROIs were standardized to a width of 15 pixels, while the length varied between 80 and 150 pixels depending on the available anatomical bone height, carefully avoiding the mandibular canal, the implant body, and the root spaces of adjacent teeth. Prior to ROI selection, panoramic radiographs were rotated for each ROI so that the mesial and distal surfaces of the implant were perpendicular, and the apical surface was parallel to the ground plane (Fig. 1).

The selected ROIs underwent a standardized sequence of image processing steps in Fiji software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) to isolate the trabecular pattern, adopting the well-established method described by White and Rudolph [17]. The sequence was performed as follows:

Images were converted to 8-bit grayscale. A blurring filter (Gaussian blur, sigma = 3) was applied to a duplicated image, which was then subtracted from the original to remove large-scale variations in background brightness caused by varying tissue thickness. A gray value of 128 was added to each pixel, followed by binarization using a threshold value of 128 to distinguish trabecular bone (white) from marrow spaces (black). Erosion, dilation, inversion, and skeletonization processes were sequentially applied to reduce noise and prepare the images for the following textural analyses:

Fractal dimension (FD): Calculated on the skeletonized images using the box-counting method to quantify the complexity of the trabecular bone structure. The grid consisted of squares with box sizes of 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 32, and 64 pixels.Lacunarity: Measured on the binary images using the FracLac plugin to assess the heterogeneity and size distribution of the marrow spaces within the trabecular network.Mean gray value (MGV): Calculated as the average gray-level intensity within the non-binary grayscale ROIs before binary conversion. In Fiji, the mean gray value measurement was selected under Analyze > Set Measurements, and the values were recorded using Analyze > Measure.Bone area fraction (BAF): Calculated on the binary images as the ratio of the area of trabecular bone (black pixels) to the total area of the ROI, expressed as a percentage. Measurements were performed using the area fraction measurement in Fiji. Since ImageJ software analyzes foreground black pixels in a binary image, BAF was calculated using inverted binary images where trabecular bone areas were represented by black pixels [18].

Statistical analysis and sample size calculation

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and median (Q1–Q3) according to their distributional characteristics. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages.

Normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For within-group comparisons across the three time points, repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied when the assumption of normality was met; the Friedman test was used otherwise. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni-corrected repeated-measures ANOVA contrasts or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction, with p < 0.017 considered significant for three pairwise comparisons. Between-group differences at each time point were assessed using the independent-samples t-test for normally distributed data and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Two-time-point intragroup MBL comparisons were evaluated using paired-samples t-tests, and a significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted.

For the primary analyses, the implant was used as the unit of analysis. Because some patients contributed more than one implant, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the main findings to patient-level clustering. Generalized estimating equations (GEE) were used with patient treated as the clustering unit, using an exchangeable working correlation structure and robust standard errors. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated to describe the degree of within-patient correlation for the main outcomes.

To support interpretation beyond p values, effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals were reported where appropriate. Cohen’s dz was used for paired comparisons, and Hedges’ g was used for between-group comparisons. Mean differences with 95% confidence intervals were also presented for the main comparisons.

To assess intra-observer reliability, the examiner re-evaluated a randomly selected subset of 20 panoramic radiographs two weeks after the initial assessment. The ICC was calculated to determine measurement consistency.

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.4; Kiel University, Kiel, Germany) based on the study by Kış and Güleryüz Gürbulak [23], who investigated peri-implant trabecular bone changes using fractal analysis and reported an effect size of 1.45 for the FD1 parameter. Assuming a two-tailed significance level of 0.05, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 8 cases per group to achieve 80% power and 10 cases per group to achieve 90% power.

Results

A total of 142 patient records comprising 332 implants were screened for eligibility. After application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 106 patients with 279 implants were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria or fulfilled one or more exclusion criteria. The final study sample included 36 patients (mean age, 36.75 ± 7.47 years; 19 females and 17 males) with 53 posterior mandibular implants. Of these implants, 31 were classified into the cover screw group and 22 into the healing abutment group. No patient had implants with both healing components. Most implants were 10.0 mm in length and 4.00 mm in diameter, and the mandibular first molar regions were the most common placement sites (Table 1). Intra-examiner reliability was excellent (ICC, 0.87–0.93). The screening, exclusion, and inclusion process of patients and implants is presented in Fig. 2.

Across the entire study cohort, MBL showed a statistically significant decrease from T1 to T2. Overall mean MBL decreased from 2.31 ± 0.80 mm to 1.89 ± 0.89 mm at the mesial aspect, and from 2.07 ± 0.79 mm to 1.63 ± 0.89 mm at the distal aspect (p < 0.001 for both) (Table 2). In the group-based analysis, both groups showed significant longitudinal reductions in measured MBL. The healing abutment group showed a significantly lower distal MBL value at T2 than the cover screw group, with a mean difference of 0.56 mm for cover screw minus healing abutment (95% CI: 0.07 to 1.06; p = 0.022). However, the amount of MBL change from T1 to T2 did not differ significantly between groups at either the mesial surface (mean difference: 0.24 mm; 95% CI: −0.06 to 0.54; p = 0.118) or distal surface (mean difference: 0.31 mm; 95% CI: −0.08 to 0.70; p = 0.068) (Table 3; Fig. 3).

Discussion

The present study described changes in marginal bone levels and peri-implant trabecular radiomorphometric patterns around posterior mandibular implants during the 3-month osseointegration period. Implants were classified according to the healing component visible on the immediate postoperative panoramic radiograph. Because component selection was not randomized and reflected routine clinical decision-making, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive radiographic observations rather than evidence of direct protocol-related effects. To our knowledge, this is one of the few studies combining linear MBL assessment with peri-implant trabecular radiomorphometric analysis during osseointegration, thereby providing complementary radiographic information on early peri-implant bone changes.

In the present cohort, MBL values decreased significantly between T1 and T2, indicating longitudinal MBL changes during the osseointegration period. The overall decrease in MBL is consistent with previous reports indicating that the greatest proportion of peri-implant marginal bone remodeling often occurs during the early healing phase, before or shortly after functional loading [5, 9]. Maghsoudi et al. [24] similarly observed that most peri-implant bone changes occurred before definitive prosthetic loading, whereas subsequent loss during loading was comparatively limited.

In the group-based analysis, the healing abutment group showed a significantly lower distal MBL value at T2 than the cover screw group. However, the amount of MBL change from T1 to T2 did not differ significantly between groups at either the mesial or distal surfaces. Similarly, Gheisari et al. [25] reported no significant difference in marginal bone loss between implants treated with healing abutments and those treated with cover screws. In contrast, Siadat et al. [10], in a split-mouth randomized controlled trial (RCT), reported greater early crestal bone loss in the two-stage group at 3 months, although this difference was no longer evident after functional loading. The discrepancy between their early findings and ours may be related to methodological differences, particularly the use of interim prostheses and a split-mouth design in that study.

The Cochrane systematic review by Esposito et al. [12], which included five RCTs with a total of 239 patients, found no statistically significant differences for prosthesis failures or implant failures between one-stage and two-stage implant placement. The only study [26] within the Cochrane review that used implant placement values as the baseline for MBL assessment reported no significant difference in bone loss between these healing approaches. In a subsequent RCT, Tallarico et al. [27] compared 38 one-stage and 51 two-stage early-loaded implants in 47 partially edentulous patients and found no statistically significant differences in mean MBL between the groups at any follow-up interval. In the present study, the slightly greater, although non-significant, MBL change observed in the healing abutment group may be considered in light of possible biological factors, such as immediate exposure of the implant to the oral environment, early peri-implant soft-tissue establishment, and bacterial colonization, as previously discussed by Weber et al. [28]. However, because these mechanisms were not directly assessed, they should be regarded only as possible biological considerations rather than explanations for the observed MBL changes in the present study.

Regarding trabecular bone pattern, the combined cohort showed significant longitudinal changes in radiomorphometric parameters during the osseointegration period. Mean lacunarity decreased from T0 to T2, whereas BAF, FD, and MGV increased over time, suggesting radiographic alterations in the peri-implant trabecular pattern during early healing. This overall increase in FD is consistent with several previous studies [16, 21, 29]. However, the temporal pattern in our study diverged somewhat from the immediate postoperative findings of Soylu et al. [29]; while they reported an initial FD decrease during the first week followed by a gradual recovery, our findings demonstrated an immediate and progressive increase from T0 to T1 that continued throughout osseointegration. This steady increase aligns with Sansare et al. [21] and is also broadly consistent with Karabağ et al. [16], who reported significant FD increases after prosthetic loading and discussed these changes in relation to functional adaptation. FD has also been investigated in relation to implant prognosis. Kış and Güleryüz Gürbulak [23] demonstrated that early FD values were significantly lower in failed implants than in successful implants. Despite these findings, contradictory results regarding FD changes exist in the literature. In contrast to these reported increases, Zeytinoğlu et al. [30] reported significantly reduced FD values at 6 months after prosthetic loading. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the evaluation timeline, as our study assessed the pre-loading osseointegration period, whereas Zeytinoğlu et al. [30] evaluated the post-loading phase. Additionally, variations in implant systems, anatomical locations, and ROI selection methodologies may contribute to these variable outcomes, as further illustrated by the non-significant decrease in mean FD values observed 6–12 months after loading in the short implant cohort of Kış and Güleryüz Gürbulak [23]. Ultimately, these conflicting reports underscore the need for standardized protocols for fractal analysis in peri-implant bone evaluation, as recommended by Kato et al. [31]. Furthermore, because FD values in the present study were derived from two-dimensional panoramic radiographs and were not validated against three-dimensional micro-CT or histological findings, they should be interpreted as surrogate radiographic texture markers rather than as direct indicators of trabecular microarchitectural changes.

Group-based analyses also showed different descriptive trajectories in trabecular radiomorphometric parameters. The cover screw group showed significant longitudinal changes across all texture parameters, including decreased lacunarity and increased BAF, FD, and MGV, indicating measurable radiographic changes in the peri-implant trabecular pattern during the observation period. In contrast, the healing abutment group showed relatively stable values for lacunarity, BAF, and MGV, with only FD demonstrating a significant increase over time. These different trajectories should be interpreted in light of baseline differences between the groups, as the healing abutment group presented with higher initial BAF and MGV values at T0 and T1. By T2, no statistically significant between-group differences were detected in the overall mean values of the assessed texture parameters. However, the observed differences, as well as the absence of significant between-group differences at T2, should not be attributed solely to the healing component. The clinician-based selection of a healing abutment or cover screw may have reflected baseline implant-site conditions, representing a potential confounding by indication.

The use of panoramic radiography in this study, while providing broad anatomical coverage and standardized imaging across all time points, presents certain limitations that merit discussion. Panoramic radiographs inherently suffer from magnification, distortion, and superimposition of anatomical structures, which may influence quantitative measurements. However, Gutmacher et al. [32] demonstrated that panoramic radiographs were as reliable and reproducible as periapical radiographs for the assessment of proximal bone height around dental implants, with a very high positive correlation between the two modalities. Furthermore, existing evidence suggests that FD is relatively insensitive to variations in image density, projection geometry, and ROI placement across all digital non-standardized clinical images, including panoramic radiographs, supporting its reliability as a tool for assessing peri-implant trabecular bone structure [18, 33, 34]. Although similar conclusions have not yet been clearly established for lacunarity and BAF, these parameters may still offer complementary insights into trabecular bone structure. While we acknowledge that peri-implant trabecular bone pattern is more appropriately assessed using standardized intraoral radiographs, the present findings indicate that calibrated panoramic radiographs may provide exploratory information on FD and related texture parameters during the osseointegration period. Additionally, TurboReg spatial alignment and dimensional calibration using known implant dimensions were used to reduce, but not eliminate, measurement errors attributable to image distortion.

The present study has several limitations. First, the study was based on existing clinical and radiographic records; therefore, the healing component used at implant placement reflected the clinician’s routine surgical decision rather than a study-defined or randomized allocation protocol. This decision may have been influenced by implant-site conditions, primary stability-related factors, perceived local bone and soft-tissue characteristics, and clinical preference. Because these factors were not consistently recorded or quantified, statistical adjustment for treatment selection bias could not be performed, and confounding by indication cannot be excluded. In addition, baseline differences in some radiomorphometric parameters, particularly BAF and MGV, suggest that the healing-component groups may not have been fully comparable at baseline. Second, additional unmeasured surgical, biomechanical, periodontal, and soft-tissue factors, together with possible temporal variability across the inclusion period, may have influenced the observed radiographic outcomes. Although radiographic baseline placement depth was estimated from the immediate postoperative measurements, exact intraoperative placement conditions and peri-implant soft-tissue and hygiene-related parameters were not consistently available in the retrospective records. Third, the sample size was relatively modest, particularly in the healing abutment group, and the follow-up period was limited to the 3-month osseointegration phase before prosthetic loading. Fourth, although the implant was used as the unit of analysis in the primary analyses, patient-level clustering was addressed through an additional GEE sensitivity analysis using robust standard errors. Nevertheless, because some patients contributed more than one implant and the study had a retrospective design, the exploratory p values should still be interpreted cautiously. Fifth, due to the two-dimensional nature of panoramic radiography, buccal or lingual bone changes could not be evaluated. Although image calibration and spatial alignment were applied to improve measurement consistency, magnification, projection geometry, and superimposition remain inherent limitations of panoramic radiography. Therefore, the radiomorphometric findings should be considered exploratory and should not be interpreted as a direct three-dimensional assessment of trabecular microarchitecture. In particular, FD should be regarded as a two-dimensional surrogate radiographic texture marker, while BAF and MGV should be considered radiographic texture-related parameters rather than true bone mineral density measurements. Finally, all implants were from a single implant system and were placed exclusively in the posterior mandible, which may limit the applicability of the findings to other implant systems, anatomical regions, or clinical conditions.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this retrospective radiographic cohort study, posterior mandibular implants showed significant marginal bone level reductions and changes in peri-implant trabecular radiomorphometric parameters during the 3-month osseointegration period. The amount of MBL change from T1 to T2 did not differ significantly between the healing abutment and cover screw groups at either the mesial or distal surfaces. Group-based radiomorphometric analyses showed different descriptive trajectories: the cover screw group demonstrated longitudinal changes in all assessed texture parameters, whereas the healing abutment group showed a significant change only in FD. However, no significant between-group differences in mean radiomorphometric parameters were detected at T2. Because healing-component selection was based on routine clinical decision-making rather than predefined or randomized allocation criteria, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory observational radiographic findings rather than evidence of healing-component-related effects. Further prospective studies with predefined clinical criteria, larger samples, standardized radiographic protocols, and post-loading follow-up are needed to clarify the clinical relevance of these observations.
