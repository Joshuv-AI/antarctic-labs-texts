Two-way 5G NR FSO-HCF-UWOC converged systems with R/G/B 3-wavelength and SLM-based beam-tracking scheme
Authors: Stotaw Talbachew Hayle, Hai-Han Lu, Hsiao-Mei Lin, Chia-Peng Wang, Chung-Yi Li, Tsai-Man Wu, Chih-Hong Lin, Wei-Xiang Chen, Jia-Lian Jin, Yan-Zhen Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A two-way fifth-generation (5G) new radio (NR) free-space optical (FSO)-hollow-core fibre (HCF)-underwater wireless optical communication (UWOC) converged systems with a red/green/blue (R/G/B) 3-wavelengths and spatial light modulator (SLM)-based beam-tracking scheme is practically built. It is the first to practically build a two-way FSO-HCF-UWOC converged system with high-speed and long-distance optical wireless-wired-underwater wireless communication characteristics. It shows a 5G NR FSO-HCF-UWOC convergence from drone or buildings to undersea, using R/G/B 3-wavelengths and an SLM as a demonstration. The R/G/B 3-wavelengths are used to enhance the downstream and upstream aggregate transmission rates. An SLM with electrical comparator is used to adjust the laser beam and mitigate laser beam misalignment caused by drone movement or ocean flow. Over a hybrid of 1-km FSO, 10-m HCF, and 10.44-m ocean water-air-ocean water medium, downstream/upstream 5G-millimeter-wave (MMW) 9.1-Gb/s/24-GHz signals are transmitted with satisfactorily low bit error rates and error vector magnitudes, as well as distinct constellations. This demonstrated that the 5G NR FSO-HCF-UWOC converged system exhibits promising potential as it advances the scenario implemented by the 5G-MMW signals over FSO, HCF, and UWOC convergence, paving the way for high-speed and long-distance communications across diverse media.

Introduction

Fifth-generation (5G) communications bring about a significant enhancement in data transport across systems, driven by the larger bandwidth and advanced communication technologies enabling seamless connectivity across various environments, including space, aerial, terrestrial, and underwater domains1–5. Terrestrial networks, which use microwave links, fibre, and free-space optical (FSO) systems, provide low latency, high data rates, and reliable performance, particularly in urban areas5,6. In contrast, satellite networks offer essential connectivity to remote and underwater areas where terrestrial infrastructure is either impractical or costly5. These networks provide broad coverage and can reach challenging geographic areas, serving as a valuable alternative to terrestrial systems. Integrating satellite networks with terrestrial infrastructure, especially for 5G, brings several benefits. This hybrid approach extends coverage to remote regions where terrestrial networks might be lacking, achieves reliable communication, and offers a backup for terrestrial systems, enhancing network resilience during outages or peak times5. Combining both types of networks enhance connectivity, supporting applications such as 5G in moving vehicles, Internet of Thing devices, and remote sensing7. Satellite networks use satellites to transmit signals across vast geographical areas, enabling global reach and seamless long-distance communication5. However, this integration presents challenges, including spectrum overlap and interference, higher latency in satellite communications, and seamless handovers between networks. Effective spectrum management and advanced interference mitigation strategies are necessary to address spectrum overlap and interference between satellite and terrestrial networks8. Additionally, higher latency in satellite communications, particularly with geostationary satellites, can impact real-time applications. Deploying low Earth orbit satellites, which operate at lower altitudes and offer reduced latency, can mitigate this issue4,5. Ensuring seamless handovers between satellite and terrestrial networks is also challenging due to differing architectures and protocols. Machine learning algorithms can enhance these transitions9. Despite these challenges, the convergence of 5G new radio (NR) technology with FSO, hollow-core fibre (HCF), and underwater wireless optical communication (UWOC) technologies presents a robust solution for seamless communication across aerial, terrestrial, and underwater domains (see Fig. 1). This convergence enables data transmission to underwater facilities or submerged vehicles, and is associated with a new era of interconnectedness across diverse environments10. Here, a drone equipped with 5G NR capabilities or a stationary building serves as a communication hub, connecting terrestrial devices and transmitting data to underwater installations11. These hubs act as pivotal points for data exchange and coordination. From the drone or building hub, data are transmitted via FSO links to nearby submerged vehicles.

FSO communication systems have increasingly emerged as a high-speed and cost-effective alternative to traditional fibre connections12,13. Operating within the unlicensed optical spectrum, these systems deliver high data rates while providing robust security and immunity to electromagnetic interference. One of the prominent advantages of FSO technology is its ease of installation, which typically involves less time and cost compared to optical fibre links13,14. This feature makes FSO an attractive solution, especially in scenarios where fibre cabling is impractical or unfeasible. In the FSO links, incorporating triplet lenses rather than doublet lenses offers significant advantages in terms of power consumption and energy efficiency. Triplet lenses provide superior aberration correction compared to doublet lenses15. This enhanced aberration correction reduces optical distortions and aberrations, improving signal quality and reducing the need for additional signal processing and correction. Consequently, this leads to lower power consumption. Moreover, triplet lenses offer improved light focusing capabilities, resulting in clearer and more accurate signal transmission over longer distances. This improved focusing means that FSO communication systems equipped with triplet lenses require less power to maintain high data rates, as the enhanced signal quality reduces the need for higher transmission power. As a result, power requirements are minimized, translating into significant energy savings and cost reductions14. In addition to their superior aberration correction and light focusing, triplet lenses extend the maximum free-space transmission coverage compared to doublet lenses. This extended range allows for reliable communication over longer distances without excessively increasing power levels, further enhancing the system’s energy efficiency. Thus, the use of triplet lenses in FSO links contributes to greater energy efficiency and cost-effectiveness by improving aberration correction and transmission coverage, all of which reduce power requirements and enhance overall system performance. However, FSO technology is limited in underwater environments due to various factors, such as turbulence, absorption, and scattering. To address this, hollow-core fibre (HCF) is utilized to extend the reach of the FSO link into underwater installations. HCF offers low attenuation, low latency, and high-bandwidth transmission16, making it ideal for underwater communication. As illustrated in Fig. 1, HCF is utilized to transmit optical signals through water between submerged devices or installations. Due to its attractive properties, it is suitable for maintaining reliable communication links undersea or underwater.

Upon reaching the coastal region, the data are transmitted to underwater installations using UWOC technology. UWOCs have a wide range of applications by providing high bandwidth and data rates, such as underwater petroleum exploration, disaster prevention, and seafloor resource mining17–19. It is essential for real-time video transmission, environmental monitoring, and underwater infrastructure inspection, which are crucial for oceanography, oceanic animals’ study, scientific data collection, offshore oil exploration, and marine research20,21. In the military and defense sectors, UOWC ensures secure, high-speed data transfer for tactical operations, thereby enhancing communication capabilities in underwater environments and significantly advancing subsea monitoring efforts22. Besides, laser-based UWOC systems offers a substantial improvement compared traditional underwater communication technologies such as radio frequency (RF) and acoustic communication systems23–25. RF communication systems, while capable of transitioning between terrestrial and underwater environments, are constrained by limited bandwidth and data rates in ranging of kilobits per second, with moderate latency due to the slower propagation speed and significant attenuation in water23. Consequently, RF communication systems are not suitable for underwater communication due to their significant signal attenuation23,24. Acoustic communication systems, known for their long-range communication capabilities, offer higher data rates in megabits per second range compared to RF communication systems but are still constrained by limited bandwidth and experience high latency23. The unique underwater environment and the properties of sound waves make it challenging to achieve high-speed, stable communication. Additionally, acoustic communication systems face issues such as multipath propagation and environmental noise from marine life24. In contrast, the UWOC system offers superior performance with high bandwidth, and high data rates in the gigabit-per-second (Gb/s) range and low latency, thanks to the high transmission speed of light25. Its ease of deployment, fast data transmission, low power consumption, and enhanced security are making UWOC a favored option20. Although UWOC systems offer significant advantages in speed and real-time communication, they are highly sensitive to water clarity, where increased absorption and scattering can degrade signal quality26. To overcome these challenges, integrating low-latency HCF with UWOC in underwater environments significantly enhances signal quality and extends transmission coverage by mitigating attenuation and dispersion. Thus, the proposed FSO-HCF-UWOC converged system provides superior performance compared to RF and acoustic communication systems. By leveraging optical signals to penetrate the water, this converged system enables high-speed communication between submerged devices and terrestrial environments, facilitating seamless and efficient communication across aerial, terrestrial, and underwater domains. This type of demonstration illustrates how advanced convergence frameworks can overcome geographical obstacles or constraints and facilitate communications across ocean water-air-ocean water medium. However, achieving a successful realization of a converged system requires addressing several key technical aspects. One of the key technical aspects that requires attention is the enhancement of data transmission capabilities. Previous studies have achieved data transmission through water-air-water mediums with data rates of 1.08 Gb/s27, 5.5 Gb/s28, and 500 Mb/s29. However, these data rates are relatively low. On the other hand, the utilization of FSO technology for UWOC links, which employs blue-light emitted diodes (LEDs) has been demonstrated30. However, the transmission distance and data rate are restricted by the LED bandwidth. To address these limitations, a four-level pulse amplitude modulation (PAM4) FSO-plastic optical fibre (POF)-UWOC convergent system12, an integrated high-speed FSO-UWOC system designed for long-range transmission31, and an optical wireless communication (OWC) system that employs wavelength division multiplexing (WDM)-based PAM4 modulation32 with a doublet lens have been demonstrated. Here, the transmission coverage of the systems is limited due to doublet lenses. However, the coverage of the FSO link in the system can be expanded by using triplet lenses instead of doublet lenses.

Moreover, various high-speed optical communication systems have been demonstrated, including an 11-Gb/s WDM link with a high colour supplying index (CRI) red-green-blue (RGB) laser lighting system33, a 40-Gb/s WDM system using polarization-multiplexing red, blue and green laser diodes (LDs)34, and a 35-Gb/s visible laser light communication (VLLC) system using violet, red, and green LDs35. However, these systems are currently built solely using VLLC links and do not incorporate FSO, HCF, or UWOC links. Additionally, they are not directly integrated with advanced wireless technologies or 5G signals for long-distance communication across both terrestrial and underwater mediums. 5G communication across both underwater and terrestrial media (as illustrated in Fig. 1) carries significant consequences for the growing convergence of systems. Focusing on the advancing of 5G NR-based 3-R/G/B wavelength FSO-HCF-UWOC links rather than separate VLLC links ensures smooth compatibility with 5G signal transmission across terrestrial and underwater environments. Furthermore, our previous study established an UWOC system17, convergent system12,18, and OWC system32 by employing PAM4 modulation. However, in these systems, direct association with 5G is not feasible due to the use of OFDM signals in the 5G physical layer1. To establish a direct link with 5G communication, the FSO-HCF-UWOC converged system could utilize OFDM signals instead of PAM4. In the other direction, a PAM4 UWOC system16 and WDM-based PAM4 OWC system32 are demonstrated by using a spatial light modulator (SLM) integrated with an electrical controller. However, integrating 5G millimeter-wave (MMW) technology into the proposed converged system offers a distinct advantage over traditional electrical controllers, as it enables significantly higher data transmission rates and bandwidth. MMW signals propagate at extremely high speeds, resulting in reduced signal transmission delays compared to electrical signals. The 5G MMW link integrated with electrical comparators, ensures reliable performance in high-interference environments by sustaining signal integrity over longer distances and in challenging conditions.

In this study, we demonstrate a two-way 5G NR FSO-HCF-UWOC converged system that achieves a total data rate of 27.3 Gb/s/24-GHz (i.e., 9.1-Gb/s × R/G/B 3-wavelengths for downlink/uplink transmissions) total data rate using 16-quadrature amplitude modulation (QAM) orthogonal frequency division multiplexing (OFDM) over a cascaded of 1-km FSO link, 10-m HCF, and 10.44-m UWOC links by employing the WDM-based R/G/B 3-wavelengths and SLM-based beam-tracking scheme to address the aforementioned limitations. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first practical demonstration of a two-way converged system implemented by 5G MMW signals as feedback through FSO, HCF, and UWOC convergence, paving the way for high-speed and long-distance communications across various mediums. In the newly proposed converged system, the WDM-based R/G/B 3-wavelengths are used to increase channel capacity and transmission rates from 9.1-Gb/s to 27-Gb/s. WDM facilitates the simultaneous transmission of optical signals at different wavelengths over a single channel, thereby increasing the transmission capacity in proportion to the number of wavelengths employed31. Here, to achieve diffraction efficiency and high angular dispersion, we employ two transmission gratings with a dense arrangement of grooves and a broadband anti-reflection coating. The two consecutive transmission gratings in the system are designed to combine the R/G/B lights at the transmitter side, and disperse them at the receiver side for down- and uplink signal transmission36. On the other hand, leveraging the low absorption property of ocean water, which ranges from 400 to 700 nm in wavelength can lead to significant advancements in UWOC18,19. Utilizing R (660.2 and 670.4 nm)/G (520.3 and 530.6 nm)/B (450.4 and 458.2 nm) LDs with two-stage light injection and 5G MMW signal through an electrical comparator feedback schemes for both down- and uplink signal transmission allows for accurately controlling the emitted wavelengths and enhances optical power. The two-stage light injection and 5G MMW signal through electrical comparator feedback schemes used in the system have achieved significant improvements in both the bandwidth and LDs’ optical output power efficiency and stability18. In addition, to maintain adaptability and resilience of communication links across changing conditions or environments for down- and upstream signal transmission, we have employed an SLM-based beam-tracking scheme with a 5G MMW wireless link integrated with an electrical comparator. By dynamically adjusting the direction of optical beams, the SLM-based beam-tracking scheme integrated with a 5G MMW wireless link and electrical comparator, ensures robust communication and helps stabilize the LDs’ output wavelengths, ensuring consistency and reliability throughout the transmission system, even in the face of environmental challenges. If quality falls below acceptable levels, a comparator can trigger alarms to mitigate issues such as signal degradation. Thus, this mechanism continuously monitors and adjusts the operation of the LDs, compensating for environmental variations in the converged system. To evaluate the signal quality and performance of the demonstrated 5G NR FSO-HCF-UWOC converged system, we conducted measurements in a number of scenarios using various metrics, including associated constellation patterns, bit error rate (BER), and error vector magnitude (EVM). In the first scenario, we evaluated the downlink signal transmission performance utilizing R/B wavelengths with feedback input signals and evaluated the uplink signal transmission performance utilizing R/B wavelengths with and without feedback input signals, all based on BERs. In the second scenario, we evaluated the downlink signal transmission performance utilizing G/B wavelengths with feedback input signals, and also evaluated the uplink signal transmission performance utilizing G/B wavelengths with and without feedback input signals, all based on EVMs. In the third scenario, we evaluated the performance of the converged system based on constellation patterns at different received MMW powers. The results indicate that incorporating the 5G-MMW feedback input signal through the electrical comparator within the 5G NR FSO-HCF-UWOC converged system achieved significantly lower BERs and EVMs, as well as clear constellation patterns. This confirms the significance of the 5G-MMW wireless feedback input signal through the electrical comparator in the proposed system for enhancing the performance of the high-speed optical wireless link in the long-reach transmission converged system. Therefore, by employing WDM techniques and integrating the 5G-MMW wireless feedback input signal through the electrical comparator with SLM and a triplet lens for the FSO link, the proposed converged system over a hybrid medium can achieve high data capacity, low power consumption, cost-effective, flexibility, and long-distance communication capabilities in optical wireless-wired-underwater wireless link characteristics for both down- and uplink signal transmission. These findings demonstrate the successful implementation of a practical two-way 5G NR FSO-HCF-UWOC converged system with R/G/B 3-wavelengths together with SLM beam tracking. Results

Impact of environmental factors on UWOC and FSO link

Environmental factors are pivotal in determining the performance of FSO-HCF-UWOC converged systems. Ocean turbulence presents a significant challenge, leading to optical signal scattering and absorption, which in turn causes attenuation, distortions, and signal fading, ultimately degrading signal quality. Variations in water salinity can alter the refractive index of seawater, potentially causing the optical beam to bend, resulting in misalignment between the transmitter and receiver37. This misalignment increases the BER and leads to signal loss. Additionally, the simulation results confirmed that as the concentration of salt in ocean water increases, the received power decreases at various transmission distances26. Thus, higher salinity levels intensify light absorption and scattering, leading to reduced overall system performance. On the other hand, weather conditions, such as fog, rain, and aerosols, introduce further complications by scattering and absorbing optical signals in the FSO link, weakening signal strength and increasing transmission performance errors. Changes in atmospheric pressure can affect the refractive index, causing the optical beam to diverge or converge, which in turn affects signal focus and alignment38. Similarly, temperature variations in the water can create thermal gradients, leading to fluctuations in the refractive index and disrupting optical signal propagation26. High humidity increases the absorption of optical signals, particularly in the FSO link, leading to a reduced effective communication range. Water clarity is also crucial, as suspended particles such as plankton, sediment, and organic matter in the UWOC link can scatter and absorb light, leading to decreased transmission distance and increased signal attenuation.

Moreover, the classification of water types is critical for the effectiveness of underwater wireless optical communication, as the optical properties of water significantly influence signal transmission performance. Water can be categorized into four types, which are pure seawater, clear ocean water, coastal ocean water, and turbid harbor water, each presenting distinct challenges for UWOC2,39. These challenges arise from varying levels of absorption and scattering, which are key factors affecting signal transmission. Pure seawater, characterized by minimal scattering and a low absorption coefficient, provides the most favorable conditions for UWOC, thereby facilitating relatively good signal transmission performance39. Light in the 450–550 nm wavelength range, including blue to green wavelengths, undergoes relatively low attenuation in ocean water due to lower absorption compared to longer wavelengths26,39. In contrast, clear ocean water, while slightly more challenging due to higher concentrations of particles, still allows for effective communication, albeit with increased scattering and absorption23,39. Coastal ocean water, with its higher concentration of suspended particles, results in greater scattering and absorption, reducing the propagation ability of optical signals and making it less suitable for long-distance communication40. The most challenging environment is turbid harbor water, where the high concentration of suspended particles leads to severe absorption and scattering, drastically limiting both the distance and clarity of optical signal transmission26,40. Here, the variations in absorption and scattering coefficients across different water types further emphasize the differences in UWOC performance. According to these variations, pure seawater is the most conducive to effective communication, while turbid harbor water presents the greatest challenges. The absorption coefficient (a(λ)) and scattering coefficient (b(λ)) together determine the beam extinction coefficient, c(λ) = b(λ) + a(λ), which quantifies total signal attenuation and reflects how these factors reduce the signal’s intensity as it transmits through water. For a wavelength of 532 nm, the coefficients were as follows: in pure seawater, the absorption coefficient was 0.053, the scattering coefficient was 0.003, and the attenuation coefficient was 0.056; in clear ocean water, these values were 0.069, 0.08, and 0.151, respectively; in coastal ocean water, they increased to 0.088, 0.216, and 0.305; and in turbid harbor water, they were significantly higher, with the absorption coefficient reaching 0.295, the scattering coefficient reaching 1.875, and the attenuation coefficient reaching 2.172,40. These environmental factors impact signal transmission by affecting signal quality, BER, and transmission distance. To address these challenges, employing adaptive optics and advanced coding, can significantly enhance system performance under varying conditions38.

On the other hand, FSO communication systems are significantly impacted by atmospheric conditions such as heavy rain, fog, and snow. These environmental factors introduce substantial attenuation of optical signals as they propagate through the atmosphere. Atmospheric attenuation in FSO links is highly variable, ranging from about 2 dB in clear weather to up to 50 dB in severe weather conditions over a 1-km distance41. In our demonstration, we measured an attenuation of approximately 2.1 dB over a 1-km FSO link in clear weather. A 1-km FSO link equipped with triplet lenses at both the transmission and reception ends provides high link availability in clear weather. However, under adverse conditions, such as heavy rain or fog, the link’s performance can degrade significantly. To mitigate this, the performance of the FSO link can be improved by using an advanced zero-cross correlation code during severe weather conditions42. Extending the FSO link from 1-km to 1.6-km increases both atmospheric attenuation and environmental noise. During the extension of the FSO link to 1.6-km, it experienced a total loss of 5.1 dB, which included 2.7 dB from atmospheric attenuation and 2.4 dB from environmental noise. In such conditions, the MMW backup is essential for maintaining reliable performance under varying environmental conditions31. Additionally, in our previous research, we designed a bidirectional convergent system integrating various transmission media, consisting of a 20-km SMF, a 500-m FSO link, and a 1-m/4-m RF wireless link, to evaluate the system’s performance under different weather conditions43. We used a laboratory-designed weather simulator to replicate adverse conditions such as heavy rain, fog, and snow within the 500-m FSO link employed doublet lenes in both transmission and reception sides. The results showed that, in clear weather conditions, the FSO link maintained high signal quality with low BERs, clear eye diagrams, and distinct constellation patterns, indicating strong link reliability. In contrast, under adverse weather conditions, the FSO link’s performance deteriorated, with increased BERs, blurred eye diagrams, and distorted constellation patterns, indicating reduced link reliability. Furthermore, our observations showed that the FSO link’s coverage was more limited when using double lenses compared to the coverage achieved with triplet lenses. The maximum free-space transmission coverage using double lenses was 595.4-m43, whereas triplet lenses can extend the coverage to 1.6-km due to their superior ability to minimize signal dispersion.

The downlink/uplink optical transmittance of R/G/B 3-wavelength channels and optical spectra of B-light in different conditions

The optical transmittance of the R/G/B 3-wavelength channels for down- and uplink signals was measured at a particle concentration of 6.74 g/m³ in ocean water, as illustrated in Fig. 2a. As shown in the figure, the optical transmittances of the downlink signal at a wavelength of 450.4 nm (B-light) transmitting approximately 97%, 520.3 nm (G-light) transmitting approximately 97.14%, and 660.2 nm (R-light) transmitting approximately 97.09% are nearly identical. Similarly, the optical transmittances of the uplink signal at a wavelength of 458.2 nm (B-light) transmitting approximately 97.07%, 530.6 nm (G-light) transmitting approximately 97.16%, and 670.4 nm (R-light) transmitting approximately 97%, are nearly identical. These results reveal that the optical transmittance values for downlink/uplink signals at these wavelengths are quite similar, indicating that utilizing R/G/B lights is appropriate for transmitting signals over the FSO-ocean water-air-ocean water mediums. This consistency across wavelengths suggests that a combination of R/G/B is suitable for transmitting signals over the FSO-ocean water-air-ocean water communication at 6.74 g/m³ particle concentration. Figure 2b illustrates the optical spectrum observed during the free-running state. Figure 2c shows the optical spectrum of the B-light LD (550.4 nm) with light injection and a 5G-MMW signal, locked at 450.52 nm using electrical comparator feedback techniques. The spectrum of light emitted by the injection-locked B-light LD shifts slightly to a longer wavelength, from 450.4 to 450.52 nm. Moreover, Fig. 2d illustrates the spectrum of light emitted by the injection-locked B-light LD with light injection and a 5G-MMW signal, locked at 450.67 nm for the second time using electrical comparator feedback techniques. The optical spectrum of the injection-locked B-light LD shifts to a longer wavelength from 450.4 to 450.67 nm. Light injection and the application of the 5G-MMW signal are facilitated through electrical comparator feedback actions, wherein the master laser (B-light LD1) is finely adjusted to emit light at a wavelength slightly longer than that of the slave laser (B-light LD2), while the slave laser (B-light LD2) is detuned to a wavelength greater than that of the master laser (B-light LD3). The wavelength factor at the injection wavelength has significant influence. As a result, the peak power of the injection-locked B-light LD2 is enhanced while its linewidth is reduced, compared to the free-running state. Similarly, both the linewidth and peak power of the injection-locked B-light LD3 are reduced and enhanced, compared to the injection-locked B-light LD2 and the free-running state. Consequently, employing light injection and a 5G-MMW signal through an electrical comparator as feedback amplifies the optical power of the slave laser while minimizing its linewidth. This leads to an injection-locked slave laser with enhanced optical characteristics, thereby bolstering the system’s overall performance.

The SLM-based beam-tracking scheme with a 5G-MMW signal through electrical comparator

To maintain adaptability and resilience across changing conditions, such as transitioning from ocean water to air and vice versa across a cascade of 1-km FSO, 10-m HCF, and 10.44-m UWOC links for downstream/upstream communication, we employed a 5G-MMW signal through a 25-GHz electrical comparator integrated with SLM technology as feedback, as illustrated in Fig. 3. In an SLM employing liquid crystal on silicon technology, the liquid crystal is manipulated by a calculated voltage transmitted from an electrical comparator. An electrical comparator is used to compare two input signals and generate output based on the comparison. The electrical comparator receives the reference signal on one input and the signal from the 5G-MMW wireless link on the other input. It then compares these two signals and generates an output based on the result of the comparison. Then, by utilizing the output from the electrical comparator in these ways, the converged systems can adapt and optimize their operation in real time to maintain robust communication and ensure reliable transmission of the signal, even in challenging environmental conditions. Additionally, incorporating polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) into the 5G MMW wireless link plays a crucial role in preserving integrity and minimizing attenuation signal. Thus, the integrated 5G-MMW wireless link and electrical comparator with an SLM-based beam-tracking scheme help lock the LDs’ output wavelengths, ensuring consistency and reliability throughout the 5G NR FSO-HCF-UWOC converged transmission system. Hence, zero-order diffraction efficiency and response time are definitely crucial factors for SLMs. Enhancing zero-order diffraction efficiency is essential in SLMs to maximize the intensity of the desired diffraction orders while minimizing losses due to lost light or unwanted diffraction orders. The mirror coating applied to the back plays a key role here by optimizing reflectivity at the desired wavelength (671 nm in our case). Achieving high reflectivity (> 95%) ensures that a significant portion of the incident light is efficiently reflected and utilized by the SLM. Additionally, the reflective SLM’s response time is mainly determined by the liquid crystal’s speed. With a response time of greater than 5 ms, it can effectively adjust spatial light, even with a natural wind speed of approximately 5 m/s. Even in strong wind conditions (11–14 m/s), the SLM’s response time remains sufficient for spatial light adjustments. This suggests that wind variation has minimal impact on UWOC systems over the ocean water-air-ocean water interface. In UWOC systems, the lack of adaptive adjustment between the plane mirror and the SLM presents a significant challenge, particularly in the presence of ocean flows. Laser beam misalignment due to these ocean flows can result in unreliable links. However, an SLM with an integrated 5G-MMW wireless link and electrical comparator can function as a flexible mirror, effectively mitigating laser beam misalignment caused by ocean flows.

Measured BERs and EVMs of the two-way 5G NR FSO-HCF-UWOC converged systems in different scenarios

Figure 4 illustrates the BERs and EVMs of the 9.1-Gb/s/24-GHz 16-QAM-OFDM R- and B-light transmitted in the downlink/uplink direction through the converged system in different scenarios. Figure 4a illustrates the BERs of the 16-QAM-OFDM R- and B-light transmitted in the downlink direction through the converged system with the feedback input signal, as well as the same R- and B-light transmissions in the uplink direction through the converged system with and without the feedback input signal. These measurements are shown at various received MMW powers across hybrid medium consisting of a 1-km FSO link, a 10-m HCF, and a 10.44-m UWOC link. When the R- and B-light are transmitted in the downlink direction through a converged system with the feedback input signal, it achieves identical BERs of 2.08 × 10−4 at received MMW powers of -16.58 dBm (660.2 nm, R-light) and − 16.71 dBm (450.4 nm, B-light), respectively. On the other hand, when the R- and B-light are transmitted in the uplink direction through the same converged system with feedback input signal, it achieves BERs of 2 × 10−4 and 2.04 × 10−4 at the received MMW powers of -16.94 dBm (670.4 nm, R-light) and − 17.07 dBm (458.2 nm, B-light), respectively. Consequently, the obtained BERs of the R- and B-light transmitted in the downlink/uplink direction through a converged system with the feedback input signal are approximately the same and significantly below the FEC standard of 3.8 × 10−3. However, when the R- and B-light are transmitted in the uplink direction through a converged system without the feedback input signal, it achieves BERs of 2.82 × 10−2 and 2.88 × 10−2 at received MMW powers of -15.84 dBm (670.4 nm, R-light) and − 15.9 dBm (458.2 nm, B-light), respectively. These obtained BERs are notably higher than the FEC standard of 3.8 × 10−3, indicating that the feedback input signal to the converged system significantly reduces the BER of the R- and B-light transmitted in the uplink direction. Specifically, the BERs are decreased from 2.82 × 10−2 to 2 × 10−4 for R-light transmission and from 2.88 × 10−2 to 2.04 × 10−4 for B-light transmission through the same converged system. Moreover, when R- and B-light are transmitted in the downlink direction through a converged system with the feedback input signal at the BER of 3.8 × 10−3, the received MMW powers of R- and B-light are about − 18 dBm and − 18.08 dBm, respectively. This yields a power penalty of 0.08 dB for R-light transmission compared to B-light transmission through the same system. Similarly, in the uplink direction under the same conditions, the received MMW powers of R- and B-light are about − 18.34 dBm and − 18.51 dBm respectively, resulting in a power penalty of 0.17 dB for R-light transmission compared to B-light transmission through the converged system. This finding confirms that the sensitivity of the received MMW power gradually decreases as the 9.1-Gb/s/24-GHz 16-QAM-OFDM signals shift toward longer wavelengths44. The power penalty differences can be attributed to several factors, including atmospheric attenuation across the 1-km FSO link, signal dispersion within the 10-m HCF, attenuation over the 10.44-m UWOC link, wavelength-dependent losses, and coupling losses during the laser beam’s transition into the fibre ferrule.

Figure 4b presents EVMs of the 9.1-Gb/s/24-GHz 16-QAM-OFDM G- and B-light transmissions across a hybrid medium, consisting of a 1-km FSO link, a 10-m HCF, and a 10.44-m UWOC link. This includes G- and B-light transmitted in the downlink direction through a converged system with feedback input signal, as well as G- and B-light transmitted in the uplink direction through the converged system with and without feedback input signal at various received MMW powers. When the G- and B-light are transmitted in the downlink direction through a converged system with feedback input signal, it achieves EVMs of 10.94% and 10.89% at received MMW powers of − 14.74 dBm (520.3 nm, G-light) and − 14.8 dBm (450.4 nm, B-light), respectively. On the other hand, when the G- and B-light are transmitted in the uplink direction through a converged system with the feedback input signal, it achieves EVMs of 10.35% and 10.13% at received MMW powers of − 14.95 dBm (530.6 nm, G-light) and − 14.96 dBm (458.2 nm, B-light), respectively. However, when the G- and B-light are transmitted in the uplink direction through a converged system without the feedback input signal, achieving the same EVMs of 13% at received MMW powers of − 17.81 dBm (530.6 nm, G-light) and − 17.98 dBm (458.2 nm, B-light), respectively, the obtained EVM of 13% significantly exceeds the 3GPP requirement of 12.5%. This indicates a substantial reduction in the EVMs of G- and B-light transmitted in the uplink direction through a converged system, from 13 to 10.35% and 10.13%, respectively, when the system has the feedback input signals. Moreover, when G- and B-light are transmitted in the downlink direction through a converged system with the feedback input signal at the EVM of 12.5%, the received MMW powers of G- and B-light are − 19.92 dBm and − 20.00 dBm, respectively. This results in a power penalty of 0.08 dB for G-light transmission compared to B-light transmission through the same system. Similarly, in the uplink direction under the same conditions, the received MMW powers for G- and B-light are − 20.20 dBm and − 20.29 dBm, respectively. This leads to a power penalty of 0.09 dB for G-light transmission compared to B-light transmission through the converged system. These power penalty differences arise because B- and G-light may have distinct wavelengths. Besides, factors contributing to these penalties could include atmospheric attenuation across the 1-km FSO link, signal dispersion within the 10-m HCF, attenuation over the 10.44-m UWOC link due to turbulence and absorption in ocean water, and coupling losses during the laser beam’s transition into the fibre ferrule. These are contributed for both BER and EVM performance degraded. As shown in the measured BER results in Fig. 4a and the measured EVM results in Fig. 4b, the system demonstrates better received MMW power sensitivity with shorter wavelength light. Thus, B-light has better received MMW power sensitivity compared R-light and G-light. Moreover, laser light alignment is a crucial factor in ensuring the performance and reliability of FSO links. Achieving precise alignment involves directing the laser beam entirely into the fibre ferrule input, a challenging task due to the natural spreading of the laser light over distances. To address this, a triplet lens is used at the receiving end to focus and direct the laser beam accurately into the fibre ferrule. A triplet lens, consisting of two convex lenses and one concave lens, provides better aberration correction compared to a doublet lens, which combines only one convex lens and one concave lens15. This advanced design ensures that the laser light is efficiently concentrated on the fibre ferrule input, making it especially effective for maintaining high-quality FSO links over long distances. For optimal performance, the fibre ferrule input must be precisely aligned at the focal point of the triplet lens. Misalignment can lead to significant performance degradation, including increased BER and EVM due to large coupling losses1. A large misalignment of the laser light results in higher coupling loss, which degrades both BER and EVM performance. To mitigate these issues, techniques such as network rerouting, adaptive optics, multiple beam transmissions, packet retransmission, and integrated FSO/MMW systems are employed to maintain high link availability38. Moreover, to address the challenges in UWOC, such as turbulence, absorption, scattering, and pointing loss due to transceiver misalignment, we have implemented SLM-based beam-tracking system integrated with a 5G MMW link and an electrical comparator. This system dynamically adjusts the direction of optical beams to counteract misalignment, thereby ensuring consistent and reliable communication. The SLM-based tracking significantly enhances signal quality and stabilizes the wavelengths of LDs, while the electrical comparator continuously monitors system performance and triggers alarms if signal quality deteriorates. This integrated approach improves the SNR and overall system reliability, resulting in more robust communication and reduced BER even under challenging environmental conditions. Furthermore, it has been observed that the BER performance across a converged system with feedback input signals shows only small differences, indicating that upstream transmission does not significantly influence downstream transmission due to the large wavelength spacing. There is no crosstalk between down- and upstream transmissions, as the wavelength spacings of 7.8 nm, 10.3 nm, and 10.2 nm for B-, G-, and R-light, respectively, are carefully chosen to ensure effective separation and prevent interference in the system. Therefore, based on the findings illustrated in Fig. 4a,b, the incorporation of the 5G-MMW wireless link integrated electrical comparator feedback input signal into the converged system significantly enhances system performance.

Determining the maximum FSO link distance coverage using triplet lenses

The coverage of the FSO link is expanded to 1-km using triplet lenses at both ends of the FSO link. Each triplet lens features a 146-mm focal length and 88.6-mm diameter. It is composed of three lenses (convex, concave, and convex) assembled together. Methods

Experimental setup of the two-way 5G NR FSO-HCF-UWOC converged systems

Figure 6 illustrates the architecture of a demonstrated two-way 5G NR FSO-HCF-UWOC converged system. This system utilizes a R/G/B 3-wavelength and SLM-based beam-tracking scheme across a cascade of 1-km FSO, 10-m HCF, and 10.44-m UWOC links. To transmit a signal in the downlink direction, the OFDM transmitter generates the 9.1-Gb/s/10-GHz 16-QAM-OFDM signal after being driven by a modulator driver. This signal undergoes up-conversion into the 9.1-Gb/s/24-GHz signal employing a mixer and a 14-GHz electrical local oscillator (LO) signal. Subsequently, the signal is split by a power splitter and transmitted towards LDs with R (λc = 660.2 nm)/G (λc = 520.3 nm)/B (λc = 450.4 nm) pigtails, where it utilizes a two-stage injection locking approach. The 16-QAM-OFDM signal is characterized by 128 subcarriers and a 512-point FFT size, which operates at a sampling rate of 10 gigasamples per second (Gsps), and has a bandwidth of 10 GHz. Then, the three R/G/B lights, each transmitting the 9.1-Gb/s/24-GHz 16-QAM-OFDM signals are multiplexed using two sequential transmission gratings. After being transmitted through two consecutive transmission gratings, the R/G/B lights combine to generate white light in suitable ratios. Note that these consecutive transmission gratings are designed to combine and disperse the R/G/B lights at the same time. Then, the white light is transmitted wirelessly towards a convex lens. Subsequently, the signal is conveyed through a 1-km FSO link utilizing a pair of triplet lenses. A triplet lens, composed of convex, concave, and convex lenses, is crucial for guiding laser light between fibre ferrules and free space. Reducing devices are essential to overcome challenges in a 1-km FSO link. The triplet lens at the reception end serves as such a device, exactly guiding laser light to the fibre ferrule input by reducing its diameter. For optimal transmission performance, it is crucial that the laser light be exactly focused onto the reception end triplet. Therefore, the diameter of the reception end or left triplet lens must be bigger than that of the dispersed laser light to minimize connecter loss and ensure efficient communication. After passing through the left triplet lens, the light is transmitted over a 10-m HCF under ocean water inside the water tank. The water contains ocean water with a particle concentration of 6.74 g/m3, achieved by adding suspensions of Al (OH)3 and Mg (OH)2 got from a commercial antacid preparation18,45. The water turbulence inside the tank is induced by a current generator. Next to this, two highly reflective plane mirrors (with 99% reflectivity) positioned 2-m apart, are strategically located within the tank to efficiently reflect the laser light through the ocean water-air-ocean water medium. Additionally, an SLM, positioned 2.8-m above the water tank, is utilized to regulate the laser beam’s direction. Subsequently, the laser light is directed through a convex lens towards the left-plane mirror. Upon passing through the convex lens, the laser light travels 10.44-m over the ocean water-air-ocean water medium, including the 2-m distance between the two plane mirrors and twice the 4.22-m distances between each plane mirror and the reflective SLM, as described in Fig. 6. The transmission distances of ocean water-air-ocean water interfaces are extended using a combination of SLM and reflective plane mirrors. After transmission through 10-m of HCF and 10.44-m of ocean water-air-ocean water medium, laser light or white light undergoes dispersion over two consecutive transmission gratings, separating it into R/G/B-3 wavelength light. The larger wavelength of R-light results in a larger diffraction angle compared to G- and B-light, while the shorter wavelength of B-light causes it to have a smaller diffraction angle than G- and R-light. The separated R/G/B-light, which carries the 9.1-Gb/s/24-GHz 16-QAM-OFDM signal, is received by a photodiode (PD) with a 25-GHz bandwidth after passing through the second transmission grating. To enhance further transmission, the signal strength is magnified by a low-noise amplifier (LNA). Finally, an OFDM receiver evaluates the performance of the system by analysing BERs alongside their associated constellations and EVMs using MATLAB.

To transmit a signal in the uplink direction, the OFDM transmitter generates the 9.1-Gb/s/10-GHz 16-QAM-OFDM signal after being driven by a modulator driver. This signal is then up-converted into the 9.1-Gb/s/24-GHz signal employing a mixer and a 14-GHz LO signal. Subsequently, the signal is split by a power splitter and transmitted towards LDs with R (λc = 670.4 nm)/G (λc = 530.6 nm)/B (λc = 458.2 nm) pigtails, where it employs a two-stage injection locking approach. After the two-stage light injection, R/G/B laser light passes through two consecutive transmission gratings to combine the R/G/B-3 wavelength light into white light. Then, the white light is transmitted toward a right-reflected plane mirror through the water tank. Similar to the signal transmitted in the downlink direction, two reflective plane mirrors with 99% reflectivity are positioned 2-m apart in the water tank, effectively reflecting the laser light through the ocean water-air-ocean water medium. Additionally, a reflective SLM is positioned 2.8-m above the water tank to alter the direction of the laser light. Here, the laser light travels 10.44-m over the ocean water-air-ocean water medium, including the 2-m distance between the two plane mirrors and twice the 4.22-m distances between each plane mirror and the reflective SLM, as described in Fig. 6. The transmission distances of ocean water-air-ocean water media are extended using a combination of reflective plane mirrors and SLM. After that, the light is reflected by the left reflective plane mirror towards a convex lens. After transmitting through the convex lens, the laser light is transmitted over a 10-m HCF and a 1-km FSO link via a pair of triplet lenses. After passing through the right triplet lens, it is captured by a convex lens. Then, the white light is separated into R/G/B 3-wavelength light as it transmits through two consecutive transmission gratings. The separated R/G/B light carrying a 9.1-Gb/s/24-GHz 16-QAM-OFDM signal is received by a PD. To enhance further transmission, the signal is amplified by an LNA. Finally, an OFDM receiver evaluates the link’s performance by analysing BERs, EVMs, and their associated constellations using MATLAB.
