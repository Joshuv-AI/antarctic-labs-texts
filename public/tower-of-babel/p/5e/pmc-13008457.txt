Effect of supervised exercise training on objectively measured physical activity in patients during anthracycline therapy
Authors: Caroline Schneider, Laura Stuetz, Annika Dierks, Kristin L Campbell, Matthias Wilhelm, Prisca Eser
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIt is unknown what the benefits of cardio-oncologic rehabilitation programmes on cardiorespiratory fitness and cancer fatigue during anthracycline-based chemotherapies (AC) are with supervised exercise training (ET), compared to PA advice and tracking only.MethodsPatients with breast cancer or lymphoma were recruited from four cancer centres and randomly assigned to three months supervised ET during (EXduringAC) or after (EXpostAC) AC. All patients were counselled on physical activity (PA) and PA was objectively measured with an activity tracker. Primary endpoints were peak VO2, fatigue and quality of life (QoL) after AC (AC-end) and at follow-up (3 months after completion of AC). Secondary endpoints were daily PA and daily steps during AC and follow-up phase, which were compared between days with and without centre-based training sessions. All analyses were performed by linear mixed models.ResultsFifty-seven patients (median [1st and 3rd quartiles] age 47 years [38, 57 years]; 95 ​% women) consented to participate, of whom data from 51 patients were available. Despite the fact that PA on days with centre-based training sessions was 28 (95 ​% confidence interval 24–32) min higher with 4382 (3995–4768) more steps, neither PA nor steps differed between groups in neither AC nor follow-up phase, nor were there between group differences in peak VO2, QoL or fatigue at any time point.ConclusionIn physically active patients with cancer, PA advice and using an activity tracker was equally effective on changes in peak VO2, fatigue, or QoL as enrolling in centre-based ET performed during or after AC.Trial registrationNCT03850171, February 21, 2019.Keywords: Physical activity, Exercise therapy, Step count, Activity tracking, Breast cancer, Lymphoma

Introduction

Exercise training (ET) therapy is an adjunct strategy following a cancer diagnosis. It has been shown to be feasible and associated with significant improvements in symptom-related outcomes like exercise tolerance, as well as multiple patient-reported outcomes such as fatigue, quality of life, and physical functioning during conventional adjuvant therapy [1,2]. Moreover, preclinical as well as clinical evidence supports exercise modulation on tumor response to radiation, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy [3]. Higher levels of physical activity (PA) are associated with improved cancer-specific and all-cause mortality in breast, colorectal and prostate cancer [4]. Likewise, in patients with lymphoma, PA has been correlated with higher survival rates [5]. ET has been recommended since 2010 for cancer survivors [6] and recently, more detailed exercise recommendations have been published in a roundtable consensus paper by international experts [7]. However, PA levels have been found to be reduced upon diagnosis with breast cancer, particularly in patients undergoing chemo- or radiation-therapy [8]. Less than half of the patients with early breast cancer [9] or also other cancers [10] reached the recommended PA levels of 150 ​min moderate to vigorous PA (MVPA).

Peak oxygen uptake (VO2), an objective quantification of physical fitness, has been found to increase rather than decrease with ET compared to standard care without ET when performed during [11], and even more so after cancer treatment [12] with a benefit of most often 3–4 ​ml/kg/min from ET. An observational study on a 4-month exercise intervention in cancer patients found a beneficial association between the degree of prevention of loss in exercise capacity with the degree of increase in fatigue and reduction in QoL [13].

The potential of PA trackers to increase the amount of PA and meet step goals has been demonstrated in general populations [14,15] and in patients with cardiometabolic conditions [16]. A recent meta-analysis in cancer survivors showed moderate to large effects of wearing PA trackers on daily PA and steps [17]. No study to date has compared the effect of supervised ET versus PA advice with supplied activity tracker on objectively measured PA and steps in patients with cancer during or following cardiotoxic therapies.

The aim of this randomized cross-over study was to assess whether performing centre-based ET during anthracycline-based therapies (AC) versus after AC reduces the loss of cardiorespiratory fitness, QoL, as well as the increase in fatigue. Over the whole study period all patients received an activity tracker together with PA recommendations. The choice of wearing an activity tracker as a control treatment was based on the fact that many people nowadays use some form of activity tracking [18] and it is a low-cost intervention strategy to increase PA [19]. Further, we quantified the effects of ET on objectively measured daily PA and steps during AC and over the 3 months following AC. Specifically, we quantified the additional PA and number of steps achieved on days with centre-based training session compared to days without centre-based training sessions.

Methods

Study design and population

This analysis was conducted as part of the CAPRICE-Study, a prospective, randomized controlled multicentre study, investigating the importance of ET timing with regard to cardiotoxicity and patient preference in early breast cancer and lymphoma patients (clinicaltrials.gov number NCT03850171). The study was approved by the Ethic Committee of the Canton of Bern, Switzerland and conducted in four large Swiss cancer referral centres including one university hospital. Results of the primary endpoint (left-ventricular function) assessed at 3 months have been published elsewhere [20]. For the secondary outcomes reported in this study, the study design was a randomised cross-over trial with the intervention allocated to the control group after the assessment of the primary outcome and reporting results at 3 and 6 months.

Patients were recruited by their treating oncologist with the help of flyers, which were also available in the waiting rooms of the oncologic clinics of the tertiary hospitals. Adults with histologically confirmed breast cancer and lymphoma who underwent anthracycline-based chemotherapy (AC) for the first time were included in the study. Patients were informed about the experimental design and risks related to the project and provided written consent form before entering the study. Group allocation has been described previously [20]. Breast cancer patients received 4 doses of AC either in a 14-day interval (dose-dense) or in a 21-day interval whereas lymphoma patients underwent 2–6 cycles of either R–CHOP or O–CHOP, escalated BEACOPP or ABVD-therapy with doxorubicin-administration every 3 weeks. A complete list depicting all administered AC is shown in the Supplemental Digital Content (Supplemental Table 1).

Assessments

Patients attended the laboratory for three visits: before initiation of AC (baseline, week −1 to 2), immediately upon AC completion (AC-end, week 8–14) and 12 weeks after AC completion (follow-up, week 20–26, Supplemental Fig. 1). During all visits, patients completed an echocardiogram, a cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET) for assessment of cardiorespiratory fitness (peak VO2) and a bio-impedance scan to obtain their body composition (Supplemental Fig. 1). CPETs were performed on a cycle ergometer with an individualized ramp protocol, which was kept constant at all three visits, aiming to achieve exhaustion within 8–12 ​min of ramp duration. From January 2014 until the end of February 2020 gas exchange was measured using the spirometry system Jaeger Oxycon Pro (Masterscreen CPX, PanGas Healthcare GmbH) which was thereafter replaced by the Quark spirometric system (Cosmed, Rome, Italy). Peak values from the CPET were determined and included power, heart rate as well as ventilatory and gas exchange parameters measured breath-by-breath and averaged over the last 30 ​s before exhaustion.

Health-related quality of life was assessed using the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy-General (FACT-G) and fatigue was assessed using the validated Functional Assessment of Chronical Illness Therapy-Fatigue (FACIT-F) questionnaire, while self-reported PA was evaluated with the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) [21]. All patients were encouraged to perform leisure time activities and to document their activities in a training diary (see physical activity assessment).

For the present study, all participants of the CAPRICE-Study from the Swiss centres who completed the second visit upon AC-completion and collected valid activity tracking data until the end of October 2022 were included.

Supervised exercise training

The intervention group participated in a centre-based cardio-oncologic rehabilitation (ET) programme during AC (EXduringAC) while the control group (EXpostAC) performed the ET programme after AC. The naming of the intervention and control group is based on the assessment of the primary endpoint at 3 months before the cross-over. Supervised ET was embedded in a 12-week ambulatory cardio-oncological rehabilitation programme, including 24 supervised centre-based and 12 non-supervised home-based exercise sessions, counselling on PA, psychological aspects, nutrition, cardiovascular risk factors and pain management. The ET was offered twice weekly to groups of approximately 10 cancer patients with exercise sessions lasting 90 ​min, supervised by experienced exercise therapists. Sessions started with approximately 30–40 ​min of cycling on an ergometer at a workload corresponding to the patient's individual first ventilatory threshold measured during the CPET (see below) of the baseline visit, increased on a weekly basis targeting to achieve a score of 13 on the Borg scale [22]. After the cycling training, patients continued with approximately 40–45 ​min of strength (at 70–80 ​% of 1 repetition maximum, 2–3 sets of 8–12 repetitions for all major muscle groups), coordination and/or balance training. During the first COVID-19 lockdown in Switzerland (March to May 2020), training sessions at the centre were suspended. In June 2020, group exercise sessions resumed once a week in smaller groups and patients completed two additional weekly training sessions at home. From July to September 2020, training sessions were offered twice weekly with up to 10 patients. From October 2020 to May 2021, during the second Covid-19 wave, group exercise sessions were replaced by individual sessions once weekly, which were scheduled according to patients' preference and availability of the exercise therapist. During weeks where no centre-based trainings were possible, patients were contacted by the physiotherapists on a weekly basis and counselled with regard to their ET in order to maintain 3 ​ET sessions per week.

Control intervention

The control intervention consisted of wearing the Fitbit Zip device and the simple verbal recommendation of performing a minimum of 150 ​min of at least moderate exercise per week. However, no instructions or planning in how to achieve this was provided. Patients were also instructed to fill in a training diary to record voluntarily performed training sessions.

Physical activity tracking and data preparation

During the first visit patients were provided a Fitbit Zip PA tracking device (Fitbit, San Francisco, CA) and asked to wear this device mounted to their trousers at the hip or at their bra on their sternum every day during the waking hours over the five to six months of study duration. Fitbit Zip devices contain a tri-axial accelerometer to capture body motion in 3 dimensions and were shown to be within the 10% equivalence zone around the indirect calorimetry estimate [23]. Good to excellent agreement has been found between accelerometry derived gait parameters for the sternum und lumbar location [24]. Proprietary algorithms were used to estimate activity summary data [25]. These included daily steps, as well as daily minutes of low, medium and high activity. Further, metabolic equivalents of task (METs) were determined based on activities sustained over at least 10 ​min of continuous moderate-to-vigorous walking corresponding to a level of 3 METs or higher [26]. At each visit, data from Fitbit Zip, such as the date of the day, number of steps per day, daily sedentary minutes, minutes of low, medium and high activity, were downloaded.

Additionally, participants kept a training diary in which they documented modality, duration and rate of perceived exertion (RPE) according to the Borg Scale [27] of all training sessions they completed as part of the ET programme but also individual exercise sessions performed at home. Training modalities recorded in the diary but not reflected by Fitbit Zip due to insufficient gravitational reaction forces (GRF), such as cycling, swimming or strength training, or when the Fitbit Zip device was not worn, were added to the daily Fitbit activity data if their duration was at least 10 ​min and Borg scale ≥12 using the following customised formulas:

Formula (1) converting endurance minutes into steps was based on a study by Tudor-Locke who identified the thresholds of 100 steps/min for moderate intensity (3 METs) and 130 steps/min for vigorous intensity (6 METs) [28]. Formula (2) is based on the RPE of 11–13 on Borg scale corresponding to 3–6 METs [29].

All documented training sessions were further categorized into centre-based ET (supervised ET completed within the ET programme) or home-based ET (unsupervised training completed outside the centre). A binary variable was created to characterise each day according to presence or absence of a centre-based training session.

Activity data was indicated as steps/day or PA min/day. Days with less than 500 steps were considered invalid days and were classified as non-wear days. AC administration was characterised by the number of cycle (AC1-AC4) and the number of the day within each cycle. Further, the 6-month study phase was dichotomised into the AC and post-AC phase, with AC phase referring to the 8–12 weeks of AC administration and post-AC phase to the weeks from completion of AC until the follow-up visit. During the post-AC phase all breast cancer patients received other chemo- or radiotherapies or underwent surgery whereas lymphoma patients did not receive additional therapies.

Fitbit wear compliance was classified on a daily basis using the following categories: 1) valid wear day; 2) Fitbit battery empty/Zip not working; 3) non-wear; and 4) steps from another device. For the analysis of daily steps, steps from 1 to 4 were added, whereas for the evaluation of PA only data from 1 was used.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using R studio (R studio, Version 2021.09.2 ​+ ​382). Baseline data of the two study groups (EXduringAC and EXpostAC) was compared using Wilcoxon two-sample tests or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. The following parameters were calculated separately for each day of each patient: recorded steps (steps), steps including converted non-step based PA from diaries (steps including non-step based PA), PA (moderate and vigorous intensity) minutes, PA including non-step based PA from diaries (PA including non-step based PA). Subsequently, means were calculated for each patient and each phase, namely AC phase, post-AC phase, and individual AC cycles.

Peak VO2 of predicted was calculated according to the Wassermann [30,31] as well as Friends [32].

The effect of the exercise intervention on changes in peak VO2 was analysed using a linear mixed model whereas cumulative linked mixed models were performed for analysis of changes in the FACT-G and FACIT-F. Predictor variables were the interaction of study group (EXduringAC and EXpostAC) with visit, patients were set as random intercepts, and models were adjusted for age, BMI and baseline values, where appropriate. The effect of the exercise intervention on PA and steps per day was analysed with linear mixed models with the following predictor variables: interaction of study group with AC-cycle (AC1-AC4 and post-AC phase) and patients as random intercepts, adjusted for age, BMI and days with centre-based ET.

Sample size calculation was based on the primary outcome of left ventricular function [20]. All analyses were performed on an Intention-To-Treat (ITT) and Per-Protocol (PP) basis. PP was defined according to which phase (AC or post-AC phase) patients performed more centre-based training sessions in, with the requirement that at least 60% of scheduled centre-based trainings were attended. All models were performed for ITT and PP analyses, except for the models on PA and steps with days with versus without centre-based training sessions, which were only performed with the PP group allocation. Missing data was not imputed.

Results

Study flow and baseline characteristics

Between May 2019 and June 2022, 143 patients were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 86 patients were excluded from the study because they declined to participate (n ​= ​52) or met exclusion criteria (n ​= ​34). 57 patients consented to participate in the study, of whom 28 were allocated to EXduringAC and 29 to EXpostAC (Fig. 1). Six patients (10.5%) discontinued the study (4 in the EXduringAC group and 2 in the EXpostAC group) before the end of AC.

Study flow with groups as analysed by Intention-to-Treat (ITT) and Per-Protocol (PP). Criteria for PP analysis was the requirement of 60% compliance with centre-based training sessions, therefore fewer patients were included in the PP analyses for visit data and training data. Analysis of training data was based on fewer patients than analysis of visit data due to some patients not having worn the Fitbit Zip tracker on more than 50% of study days.

Participants who dropped out of the study before the AC-end visit were excluded from the analysis of visit and training data. Three and five patients did not have recorded training data in the EXduringAC and EXpostAC group, respectively (Fig. 1) and hence were not included in the analyses of the training data. In the EXpostAC group one patient did not attend the AC-end visit because the appointment could not take place due to the Covid-19 lockdown in March/April 2020. Fig. 1 patients who dropped out of the study before the AC-end visit were 11 years older than patients who remained in the study (p ​= ​0.007) but otherwise did not differ with regard to baseline characteristics (Supplemental Table 2). One lymphoma patient received only two doses of AC and consequently started the control phase already after 6 weeks. For the PP analysis of the EXduringAC group, 8 patients did not satisfy the criteria of completing 60% of the prescribed centre-based training sessions. One patient from the EXduringAC group completed more training session in the post AC phase and was analysed in EXpostAC for the PP analysis. Five patients who were allocated to the EXpostAC group decided to start the ET during the AC phase and were hence analysed in the EXduringAC group for the PP analysis (Fig. 1). Five patients in EXpostAC did not fulfil the requirement of 60% compliance with centre-based training sessions and were excluded from the PP analysis.

The two study groups did not significantly differ regarding baseline characteristics, tumor-site, cancer stage, timing of AC and risk factors (Table 1). VO2 peak of predicted values according to Friends were 10–15% lower than those according to Wassermann, and were slightly above norm values according to Wassermann but below norm values according to Friends (Table 2).

Changes in VO2, QoL and fatigue

As displayed in Fig. 2 and Table 2, peak VO2 tended to decrease from the baseline to the AC-end visit and increased again at follow-up in both groups. There were no significant group ​× ​time interactions indicating that there was no treatment effect for peak VO2, with group allocation according to ITT and PP resulting in similar effects (Supplemental Table 3). FACT-G showed a similar pattern without significant group ​× ​time interactions. FACIT-F was significantly improved at follow-up according to ITT analyses (p ​< ​0.01). PP analyses revealed a significant time ​× ​group interaction at follow-up with significantly worse fatigue observed at AC-end (p ​< ​0.001) (Fig. 2, Table 2 and Supplemental Table 3).

Interaction plot of effects of group and visits (adjusted for standardised age and body mass index) on peak VO2 (top). Box plots are shown for FACIT-F (middle) and FACT-G (bottom) of the two groups and three visits. Left panels show group allocation according to Intention-to-Treat and right panels according to Per-Protocol. Asterices (∗) indicate significant (p ​< ​0.05) main effects of time.

Effect of intervention and AC cycle on physical activity and steps per day

PA and step data were available in 21 of the EXduringAC group and 22 patients of the EXpostAC group (Table 3). Reasons for missing PA data were: unwillingness to wear the activity tracker (n ​= ​1), device lost (2), technical problems (1), non-compatible phone (1), and incomprehensible exercise dairy (1). PA was recorded on a median 141 days [102, 165 days] reflecting a compliance of 87.4 ​% [63.6, 95.6 ​%]. One patient recorded steps on another digital device and therefore had no PA data in the AC phase, while this was the case in four patients in the post-AC phase. Steps were recorded by Fitbit Zip or another digital device on a median 155 days [117.5, 165.0 days], reflecting a compliance of 92.3 ​% [72.9, 96.9 ​%]. Battery or device failure occurred on a median of 4 days [1, 22.5 days], and the Fitbit Zip was not worn on a median of 5 days [1.5, 9 days]. During the AC phase 32 out of 42 patients with available PA data (76.2 ​%) reached the recommended 150 ​min/week PA goal (Table 3). During the post-AC phase this was the case in 29 out of 36 (80.6 ​%).

Physical activity on days with and without centre-based training

Days with centre-based trainings resulted in significantly higher PA and steps within both groups (Supplemental Fig. 2). Namely, the main effect for days with centre-based training sessions was 28 ​min (95% confidence interval 24–31 ​min) more PA and 4382 steps (3996–4768 steps) more (p ​<0.001 for both models, Supplemental Table 5). However, since days with centre-based trainings were fewer (usually 2 per week) than days without centre-based training sessions (usually 5 per week), for the comparison between groups this surplus in PA could not outweigh the lesser PA performed on days without centre-based trainings, and consequently did not lead to an intervention effect. There were some significant group x AC phase interaction effects, however, they were mainly related to the EXpostAC group reducing PA and steps from AC1 to AC4 while PA and steps were kept more constant in the EXduringAC group (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This is the first randomized controlled trial on ET in patients with cancer that objectively tracked PA continuously over 6 months during and after AC therapy. With regard to change in peak VO2, QoL and fatigue, centre-based ET was not superior to PA advice and activity tracking only in our sample. PA on days with centre-based ET sessions was 28 ​min (95% confidence interval 24–31 ​min) higher and patients performed 4382 steps (3996–4768 steps) more on these days compared to days without centre-based ET sessions. The absence of between group differences, however, suggests that the surplus of PA and steps on days with centre-based ET was compensated during the days without centre-based ET. Only when investigating PA and steps of individual AC cycles did we find that the drop in PA and steps during AC cycles 3 and 4 was attenuated in EXduringAC compared to EXpostAC, particularly when groups were analysed according to PP group allocation, which however, did not affect cardiorespiratory fitness, fatigue or QoL after AC. In our physically active sample, randomisation to centre-based ET did not increase objectively measured PA nor steps over the provision of PA recommendations combined with self-monitoring by PA tracker only.

The absence of a beneficial effect of ET on QoL is largely in agreement with previous findings [1,33]. Several previous studies have also documented the absence of a beneficial effect of ET on FACT-G [34]. As expected, fatigue was significantly improved at follow-up. PP analyses revealed a significant between group difference with lower FACIT-F scores at follow-up for EXduringAC and significantly worse fatigue observed at AC-end in both groups.

Our results of peak VO2 and FACIT-F are in contrast to the findings of two meta-analyses who found benefits of centre-based ET during adjuvant therapy with regard to cardiorespiratory fitness or physical functioning and fatigue in breast cancer patients compared to usual care [1,2]. Median decreases of 1.0 ​ml/kg/min in the EXduringAC and 1.1 ​ml/kg/min in the EXpostAC compare well to previous studies [11,35]. The absence of a benefit of centre-based ET in our study may have been caused by the fact that all patients received a Fitbit Zip activity tracker over the whole 6-month duration of the study and were physically very active. The PA tracking allowed the detailed information gathering of daily PA and steps, but confounded the centre-based ET treatment in that both groups had access to their personal daily steps which they could see on the tracker or on the Fitbit App. While many studies and meta-analyses have assessed the combined effect of an exercise intervention with an activity tracking device versus no intervention, fewer studies have assessed the effect of an exercise intervention combined with an activity tracking device versus the use of an activity tracking device alone [36]. A meta-analysis assessing the effect of pedometer use in community-based adults on objectively measured daily steps found no additional benefit of exercise counselling or financial incentives over pedometer use alone [19]. Pedometer use was associated with approximately 1000 additional steps per day in the short term (<6 months) and 400 steps in the long-term (12 months) [19]. Most of the included studies in cancer patients showed that implementing an exercise intervention (with at least setting PA goals) or monetary rewarding [37] had additional benefits in terms of increasing objectively measured PA levels compared to the wearing of the tracking device alone [[38], [39], [40]]. Patients of our control group (EXpostAC) only received information on guideline recommendations for PA, such as performing 150 ​min/week of MVPA. Nevertheless, encouragement by self-monitoring their daily steps may have led to an increase in daily PA that competed with the effect of centre-based ET. The absence of a between group difference in change in peak VO2 and performed daily PA and steps implies that the surplus of PA and steps on days with centre-based training sessions was simply compensated on the following das(s) without centre-based training sessions. This is in agreement with a recent study in young adults with cancer [41].

Compared to a study from the U.S.A. who used the same Fitbit Zip activity tracker in patients of comparable age with early breast cancer [42], our patients were more physically active. Their patients reached a mean of just under 4000 steps/day during the 12 weeks of adjuvant treatment, while our population reached a median of 6654 steps/day during the AC phase (and even 7459 steps/day when including non-step based PA) and 86% of our patients exceeded the threshold of 5000 steps/day for sedentary lifestyle [43]. This aligns with the high PA level of the Swiss population confirmed by a WHO report from 2016, which reported a low prevalence of inactivity in Switzerland (20–29%), compared to 30–50% in other European countries or North-America [44].

As far as the pattern of PA and steps over the AC cycles is concerned, we found a slight increase between AC1 and AC2 followed by a continuous decrease until completion of the last AC cycle in both groups. PA and daily steps during the 3-month post-AC phase was comparable to levels during AC2 with negligible differences between groups and between ITT and PP analyses, except that in EXduringAC the decrease after AC2 was attenuated (Fig. 3). This is in slight contrast to a recent study in patients with breast cancer who wore a wrist-worn Fitbit Charge HR device on a mean of 102 days during their chemotherapy, which found a steady decline from about 15 ​min of MVPA per day to 7.5 ​min until 50% of chemotherapy was completed [45]. Our patients were more active with a median 32.3 ​min MVPA during AC and 36.4 ​min in the post-AC phase. Comparison of MVPA minutes across studies is difficult when exact definition of how MVPA was calculated (type and location of tracker, sampling windows, averaging, thresholds, required minimal bout duration, etc.) is missing [46]. Fitbit uses device-specific and unpublished algorithms for PA threshold determination. The only information available was that the 10-min criteria for minimal bout duration was used [47]. This definition leads to much lower daily MVPA durations than those based on the current WHO recommendations that omitted the bout criteria [46,48]. Therefore, comparisons between studies are only valid for the same MVPA definitions, which, unfortunately, are sometimes missing. Despite being based on old, more stringent MVPA criteria, in our population, 76% and 81% of all patients reached the recommended 150 ​min/week of MVPA during and following the AC phase, respectively.

Few studies have compared PA or steps on days with supervised ET with days without supervised ET. In a study of patients with HIV and sedentary controls without HIV, approximately 2000 steps were performed more on days with supervised ET compared to days without supervised training [49]. We found that our patients had 28 more minutes of MVPA and 4382 more steps on days with compared to days without centre-based training sessions. These differences were similar in the two groups and during the AC and post-AC phases. Given that there was no effect of group in the models for PA and daily steps, it is likely that the surplus of exercise during days with centre-based training sessions was compensated on days without centre-based training sessions.

The main strength of our study is the detailed objective assessment of PA. No previous study using ET interventions in cancer patients during adjuvant therapy has documented the effects of ET on their objectively measured activity pattern. Further, our assessment of PA and steps included activities such as cycling and swimming that are not normally recorded by accelerometry by using training durations recorded in the patients’ training diaries, converting them to steps and adding them to PA and steps recorded from the Fitbit Zip. The training diaries and recordings of centre-based training sessions by the centres themselves allowed the separation into days with and without centre-based training sessions. Last but not least, our population was quite homogenous as we only included patients undergoing AC therapy, allowing us to perform a detailed assessment of PA and steps over individual AC cycles.

Our study likely suffered from selection bias. Patients interested in a study necessitating ET participation were likely to have been physically active before their cancer diagnosis. Namely, willingness to exercise and educational level were found to be associated with PA level before entering an exercise programme in cancer patients [50]. This may explain the rather high levels of PA and steps in our patients. Also, the fact that five patients performed ET during AC despite the fact that they were randomised to EXpostAC indicates that patients included in this study were motivated to start the ET as soon as possible.
