Multiparametric MRI as predictors for evaluating renal ischemia reperfusion injury and protective effect of Mito-TEMPO in rat model
Authors: Jin-Lu Song, Zhen Xu, Yu-Hong Wang, Yu-Xin Qin, Wei Cui, Zhong-Yuan Cheng, You-Zhen Feng, Zi-Dong Xie, Xiang-Ran Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe aimed to evaluate multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), including blood oxygen level dependent (BOLD), arterial spin labeling (ASL), and intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) for noninvasively monitoring acute kidney injury (AKI) to acute kidney disease (AKD) progression following renal ischemia reperfusion injury (IRI) and the subsequent response to Mito-TEMPO (MT) therapy.MethodsPhase 1: 42 Sprague Dawley (SD) rats (36 IRI and 6 control). Phase 2: 144 SD rats (IRI/control + MT treatment/saline, 36 per group). IRI group: left renal ischemia reperfusion procedures, control group: sham abdominal surgery. Animals underwent MRI scanning to obtain effective transverse relaxation rate (R2*), renal blood flow (RBF), apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC), pure molecular diffusion (D), perfusion-related diffusion (D*), and perfusion fraction (f) values of kidney. After sacrifice, serological and histological analysis were performed.ResultsAll MRI parameters (R2*, RBF, ADC, D*, D, f) showed significant, time-dependent alterations following IRI compared to controls. These changes were effectively reversed by MT treatment, with most parameters recovering to near-baseline levels.ConclusionBOLD, ASL, and IVIM can be used as noninvasive radiologic markers for detecting the progression from renal IRI to AKI-AKD and recovery after MT treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-04949-3.Keywords: MRI, Ischemia reperfusion injury, Acute kidney injury, Acute kidney disease, Mitochondria-targeted antioxidant

Introduction

Ischemia reperfusion injury (IRI) primarily drives acute kidney injury (AKI) [1]. Acute or subacute kidney impairment and/or functional decline that persists for 7 to 90 days following an AKI-triggering event is recognized as acute kidney disease (AKD) [2], and AKI is a subtype of AKD [3]. Recent evidence demonstrates that AKI progressing to AKD correlates with 1-year mortality and the emergence of new chronic kidney disease (CKD) [4]. This makes AKD a key target for intervention.

Mounting evidence identifies oxidative stress as a key factor in IRI-induced AKI [5]. IRI disrupts cellular redox balance, triggering excessive production of renal reactive oxygen species (ROS). This initiates a cascade of pathological events, including cytoplasmic calcium overload, cellular energy depletion, apoptosis/necrosis, and inflammation, which collectively contribute to kidney injury [6]. Approximately 90% of intracellular ROS originate from mitochondria [7]. Mitochondria ROS (mtROS) contribute significantly to ischemic diseases [8]. Consequently, targeting mtROS is a promising therapeutic strategy. The mitochondria-targeted antioxidant Mito-TEMPO (MT) is a combination of antioxidant piperidine nitroxide (TEMPO) and lipophilic cation triphenylphosphonium (TPP+) [9, 10]. MT has demonstrated protective effects on glomerular endothelial cells and podocytes by reducing the accumulation of 8-oxoguanine [11]. Furthermore, MT alleviates mitochondrial dysfunction and endoplasmic reticulum stress [10]. MT also mitigates kidney injury in CKD models by inhibiting mtROS and increasing mitochondrial mass [9]. However, its efficacy in modulating the progression from AKI to AKD remains unclear.

Monitoring this transition is challenging. Renal biopsy is invasive. Conventional markers such as serum creatinine (Scr) levels can be confounded by muscle mass and volume status [12], whereas BUN often remains within the normal range until glomerular filtration rate (GFR) is reduced by over 50%. While the novel biomarker kidney injury molecule 1 (KIM-1) is upregulated post-injury and shows promise for ischemic AKI, its clinical application remains to be established in larger, well-designed studies [13, 14]. Non-invasive magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques have emerged as valuable tools for early detection of AKI progression [15]. Intravoxel incoherent motion-diffusion weighted imaging (IVIM-DWI) can simultaneously provide information on tissue perfusion and diffusion, enabling the detection of early renal changes in acute ischemic AKI [16]. The key quantitative parameters are as follows: the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC), reflecting overall water mobility; the pure diffusion coefficient (D), representing the diffusion of free water molecules; the pseudo-diffusion coefficient (D*), associated with microcirculatory perfusion; and the perfusion fraction (f), corresponding to the fractional volume of microvascular flow. Arterial spin labeling (ASL) is a perfusion MRI technique that utilizes magnetically labeled blood water as an endogenous tracer. It enables quantitative assessment of tissue perfusion, with renal blood flow (RBF) serving as its primary metric. Blood oxygen level dependent (BOLD), a functional MRI (fMRI) technique, assesses tissue oxygenation by measuring the effective transverse relaxation rate (R2*). The R2* value is inversely related to tissue oxygen tension, as it increases with deoxyhemoglobin concentration. This method has been utilized to assess renal tissue oxygen tension in the early phases of ischemia [16] (Table S1).

Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the renoprotective effects of MT in a rat IRI model and to employ non-invasive MRI techniques to monitor kidney injury and recovery changes, thereby elucidating its potential role in the progression from AKI to AKD.

Materials and methods

Animal experiments

The prospective experimental study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Jinan University (IACUC Number: 20230720-01. Approved Date: 2023-07-20). All experiments followed animal ethics guidelines in our institutional guidelines, which conform to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (Eighth Edition) and current Chinese regulations. 186 healthy male Sprague Dawley (SD) rats (aged 6–8 weeks and weighing 200–250 g) were purchased from Beijing Huafukang Experimental Animal Technology Co., Ltd. The rats were housed in a specific pathogen free (SPF)-grade facility at Jinan University’s animal center. Acclimated for 1 week, the experimental design comprised two phases. In the first phase, 42 rats were randomly assigned to two groups: IRI group (n = 36) and Sham-operated group (Sham group, n = 6), to assess the efficacy of MRI in monitoring renal IRI progression in rats. In the second phase, 144 rats were randomly divided into four groups: control group + saline (CON group, n = 36), control group + MT (MedChem Express, Monmouth Junction, NJ) (COM group, n = 36), IRI group + saline (IRS group, n = 36), IRI group + MT (IRT group, n = 36), to evaluate the utility of MRI in monitoring renal changes in IRI rats after MT treatment. After 8-hour fasting, all rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 3% sodium pentobarbital (prepared from pentobarbital sodium powder dissolved in sterile saline) at a dosage of 1 mL/kg to induce surgical anesthesia. According to the warm IRI protocol, rats were placed in a supine position on a thermostatically controlled heating pad, which maintained the core body temperature at 37 °C throughout the procedure. The left kidneys in IRI, IRS, and IRT groups were subjected to 45 min of pedicle clamping, then released the clamp (Figure S1). The Sham, CON, and COM groups underwent laparotomy only, without clamping. As a preventive treatment, rats in COM and IRT groups received an intraperitoneal injection of MT (2.5 mg/kg dissolved in 0.9% saline), while rats in CON and IRS groups received intraperitoneal injection with 0.9% saline only (2.5 mg/kg) at 15 min before operation. And each group received intraperitoneal injection with the same dose of the drug or saline every day from day 1 to 14.

MRI protocols

6 rats were randomly selected from each of IRI and Sham groups to undergo MRI scanning before surgery (day 0, baseline) and on day 1, 3, 5, 7, and 14 after IRI induction (Time set to the AKI-AKD phase). 6 rats from each of CON, COM, IRS, and IRT groups underwent MRI scanning at baseline and after injection of MT or saline at aforementioned time points. MRI scans were performed on a 3.0T clinical-grade superconducting scanner (Discovery MR750w; GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL) equipped with an HD Wrist Array upper coil to optimize signal reception. Prior to performing coronal scanning of left kidney, the rats were fasted for 4 h. They were then anesthetized for MRI scanning by intraperitoneal injection of 3% sodium pentobarbital (prepared from pentobarbital sodium powder dissolved in sterile saline) at a dosage of 1 mL/kg. Routine MRI sequences included T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), BOLD, ASL, and IVIM-DWI. The scanning sequence and parameters were as follows (Table 1).

Image analysis

The original BOLD and IVIM-DWI images were transferred to a workstation (Advantage Workstation 4.5; GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL), to generate R2*, D, D*, f, and ADC maps. The original ASL images were analyzed using the open-source Quantiphyse package (FMRIB, Oxford, Oxfordshire), which is designed for quantitative MRI analysis, to obtain RBF images. Three regions of interest (ROIs), including cortex (CO), outer stripe of the outer medulla (OSOM), and inner stripe of the outer medulla (ISOM) [17, 18] were delineated in left kidney with T2WI images as a reference by two radiologists with 5 years’ experience and blinded to group designations. The measurements were repeated three times to calculate the average value (Figure S2).

Serum examination

While the rats were under deep anesthesia, a midline laparotomy was performed to expose the abdominal aorta, and then the rats were euthanized by removing circulating blood via aortic puncture using a sterile disposable syringe. At each time point post-MRI scanning, approximately 6–8 mL of blood was collected from 6 rats in IRI group. As this procedure was terminal, blood samples from the 6 rats in Sham group were collected after the final MRI scan. Following collection, the blood samples were centrifuged (3500 r/min, 10 min). The supernatant was analyzed by automatic biochemical analyzer for BUN and interleukin-6 (IL-6). Similarly, blood was collected from 6 rats each in CON, COM, IRS, and IRT groups. Blood collection time points and processing methods were as described above. The following samples were analyzed by automatic biochemical analyzer: BUN, Scr, and KIM-1 (KIM-1 ELISA kit from Novel Biotechnology, Winnipeg, Manitoba).

Histology

After each blood collection, death was confirmed by the cessation of cardiac and respiratory activity. The rats’ left kidneys were isolated and removed. Each kidney was then sectioned into two halves along its longitudinal (coronal) axis. One half was stored in 4% paraformaldehyde solution (BioSharp, Hefei, Anhui). The samples were dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned into 4 μm for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Solarbio, Beijing, Beijing). Renal pathomorphological characteristics were analyzed under a microscope.

Immunohistochemistry

At the same time points as H&E staining, CON, COM, IRS, and IRT groups in Phase 2 underwent immunohistochemistry for cluster of differentiation 31 (CD31) and alpha smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) (Primary antibodies: anti-α-SMA from Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, 1:500; anti-CD31 from Abcam, Cambridge, MA, 1:2000). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for CD31 and α-SMA was performed to evaluate changes in renal microvascular density and the extent of renal interstitial fibrosis, respectively. Paraffin sections were processed for antigen repair and were stained after the addition of primary and secondary antibodies (DAB Horseradish Peroxidase Color Development Kit from Beyotime, Shanghai, Shanghai). The integrated optical density (IOD) was measured in at least three fields for each sample under light microscope, and analyzed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 analysis software (Version X; Media Cybernetics, Silver Spring, MD). The mean density, derived by dividing the IOD by the corresponding positively stained area, represents the intensity of positive signals per unit area and was used as a measure of CD31 and α-SMA expression levels.

ATP analysis

Renal adenosine triphosphate (ATP) concentration was measured in the CON, COM, IRS, and IRT groups. For this purpose, the remaining half of each kidney was sectioned into small pieces. Samples of approximately 0.05 g were combined with 0.45 mL of extraction solution and homogenized on ice to obtain a tissue homogenate. The homogenate was subsequently centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was carefully collected. ATP concentration in the supernatant was measured using a chemiluminescence assay kit (Elabscience, Wuhan, China).

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) and GraphPad prism (version 10, GraphPad Software, Boston, MA) were used for statistical analysis and results plotting. All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD), p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to determine the interobserver agreement for each parameter (including R2*, RBF, D, D*, f, and ADC values). ICC < 0.20, 0.21 < ICC < 0.40, 0.41 < ICC < 0.60, 0.61 < ICC < 0.80, 0.81 < ICC < 1.00 indicating poor, fair, moderate, good and excellent agreement [19]. Temporal trends of MRI parameters were compared using repeated-measures ANOVA, followed by the Tamhane’s T2 for non-homogeneous variance or the least significant difference (LSD) for homogeneous variance for multiple comparisons. Independent-sample t-test was used to compare the blood biochemical indices (BUN, IL-6) between IRI and Sham groups. One-way ANOVA was employed to analyze intergroup comparisons of both biochemical indices (BUN, Scr, and KIM-1) and MRI results of different groups in CON, COM, IRS, and IRT groups. ATP content was analyzed by two-way ANOVA. To assess the utility of MRI in reflecting injury and repair during the AKD phase, Pearson correlation analyses were specifically conducted on day 14 data to examine the relationships between MRI parameters and blood biochemical indices (BUN, Scr) in Sham and IRI groups, and IHC markers (CD31, α-SMA expression) in CON, COM, IRS, and IRT groups.

Blood biochemical analysis

Following model establishment, Scr, BUN, and KIM-1 levels increased from day 1 in IRS group. These trends were consistent with MRI parameter changes. And the levels in IRT group were significantly lower than those in IRS group on day 1. In IRT group, although BUN and KIM-1 levels were elevated from day 5, they remained lower than those in IRS group (Table S7). BUN and Scr levels in IRS and CON groups, and in IRS and COM groups were statistically significant at day 1 only. However, for KIM-1, statistically significant differences were found: between IRS and IRT groups on day 1 (p = 0.03); between IRS and COM groups on day 5 and 14 (p < 0.05); and between IRS and CON groups at all time points (day 1, 5, and 14) (p < 0.05) (Figure S5).

Histologic analysis

H&E staining showed no obvious abnormalities in glomerulus and renal tubule in Sham, CON, and COM groups at all time points. In IRI and IRS groups, flattening, swelling, vacuolar degeneration, dilatation and detachment of renal tubular epithelial cells were seen, along with inflammatory infiltrates in renal interstitium. All changes progressively worsened over time. The above pathologic changes were all less severe in IRT group than in IRS groups throughout the observation period (based on a consolidated assessment of the results presented in Fig. 2A, Figure S6, and Figure S7A).

Discussion

In this study, we employed three MRI techniques, BOLD, ASL, and IVIM to systematically evaluate the changes in renal function and structure during the progression from IRI-AKI to AKD and MT treatment in a rat model.

In the first phase of our work, we systematically assessed left renal alterations following IRI induction in a rat model using the above MRI techniques. Post-IRI induction, we observed a coordinated deterioration in renal oxygenation and perfusion, evidenced by increased R2* values (indicative of tissue hypoxia) and decreased RBF, D, f, D*, and ADC values (reflecting impaired microcirculation and restricted water diffusion) [19–21]. Notably, compared with CO, statistically significant differences in R2* values at all time points were observed in Sham and IRI groups within both the outer and inner medulla. CO exhibited the most pronounced reduction in RBF values post-IRI induction. This regional disparity can be attributed to the heterogeneous renal blood supply, with approximately 80% perfusing the cortex and only about 15% reaching the outer medulla [22]. Consequently, the outer medulla demonstrates high sensitivity to hypoxia, and long-term monitoring of medullary R2* values could serve as a sensitive biomarker for tracking renal injury progression. Our results suggest that compared to the ADC and D* parameters, the f and D parameters demonstrate statistically significant differences between Sham and IRI groups at more time points in evaluating the progression of IRI-AKI to AKD. This may be due to the decrease in D value caused by cellular edema and impeded diffusion of water molecules [23, 24]. In contrast, the water molecule diffusion state reflected by ADC value includes not only the pure water molecule diffusion component of the tissue itself, but also the blood perfusion and tubular flow within kidney [25], the magnitude of b-value selected during scanning [26], and microperfusion of tissue capillaries [27]. D* values are correlated with capillary segment length [28], whereas IRI predominantly reduces capillary density, which is strongly correlated with f value [21]. Lower f values predict longer recovery times [29].

Most MRI parameters (R2*, RBF, D, and f) did not return to baseline by day 14. This is corroborated by histopathological results. In IRI group at day 14, renal interstitial edema, endothelial cell damage, and endothelial cell swelling were the most severe. This result suggests that although tissue hypoxia demonstrates partial restoration after warm IRI induction, complete recovery to baseline may not be attainable. Localized irreversible renal damage may occur [30]. In this study, IL-6 showed stronger correlations with multiple MRI parameters than BUN. This association suggests that the dynamic changes in MRI parameters correlate with IL-6 levels, collectively supporting their relevance in characterizing the pathological progression from IRI-AKI to AKD.

Given the central role of mitochondrial dysfunction in IRI pathogenesis [31], we next evaluated MT as a therapeutic intervention. MT treatment effectively attenuated IRI-induced damage, as evidenced by serology, histology, and MRI. KIM-1 levels were significantly lower in the IRT group from day 1, and maintained their discriminatory capability at later time points. Multiparametric MRI revealed that MT intervention reversed the pathological trends observed in IRS group, with R2*, RBF, D, and f values recovering toward baseline by day 14. Histologically, this functional recovery corresponded to reduced α-SMA deposition (indicating less fibrosis), increased CD31 expression (suggesting better-preserved vasculature), and restored ATP content in the IRT group. Multivariate correlation analysis reinforced the biological plausibility of our imaging findings, demonstrating significant associations between these histological markers (CD31, α-SMA) and key MRI parameters (R2*, RBF, D, and f). Notably, ADC values showed no meaningful correlation. Therefore, to summarize two phases of the work, ADC and D* values may not be as sensitive as other MRI parameters in detecting renal changes post IRI-induction and therapeutic intervention. We propose that the observed multiparametric MRI changes reflect a sustained hypoxic and metabolic disturbance triggered by IRI, which directly impairs tubular function, leading to subsequent elevations in functional biomarkers. The mitochondria-targeted antioxidant MT demonstrated significant renoprotective, anti-fibrotic, and pro-angiogenic effects in our model, highlighting its therapeutic potential.

This study has several limitations. First, the exclusive use of male rats precludes the assessment of known sex differences in IRI. While the chosen experimental model (45-min ischemia with a 14-day observation) was suitable for capturing the acute-to-subacute (AKI to AKD) transition, it is insufficient to evaluate chronic outcomes such as fibrosis; the selected MT dose also requires further optimization. Second, MRI parameters were acquired under anesthesia, which may influence absolute values, and some parameters exhibited wide ICC confidence intervals. Neither did the MRI parameters demonstrate a direct quantitative correlation with histopathological scores at corresponding time points, nor were their diagnostic thresholds validated in an independent cohort. Third, IHC staining exhibits inherent heterogeneity. Future studies should incorporate both sexes, optimize dosing, extend follow-up beyond 4 weeks to assess fibrosis and use techniques like whole-slide scanning or multiplex immunofluorescence for spatial validation to ideally validate key findings.

In conclusion, multiparametric MRI (BOLD, ASL, and IVIM) provides a non-invasive tool for monitoring the progression of IRI-AKI to AKD and for evaluating the efficacy of renoprotective therapies such as MT. The mitochondria-targeted antioxidant MT has both prophylactic and therapeutic potential against IRI in rats through its anti-injury and anti-fibrotic effects. MT may represent a promising therapeutic agent for clinical IRI management.
