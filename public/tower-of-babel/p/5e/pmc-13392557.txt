Surface charge engineering shields extracellular vesicles from plasma protein corona to enable systemic extrahepatic targeting
Authors: Taotao Tang, Mengyun Wang, Xiaowei Xu, Jin Wang, Yan Wang, Fangzhou Wang, Deping Wu, Yilin Zhang, Shuangshuang Liu, Lingting Kong, Zhiqing Chen, Feng Chen, Yi Wen, Bin Wang, Junjia Wang, Bicheng Liu, Linli Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe plasma protein corona (PC) critically influences the in vivo fate of nanomedicines, yet its composition and impact on extracellular vesicles (EVs) remain poorly defined. Using a biomimetic circulation system, we characterized PC formation and modulation on two clinically relevant EV types: mesenchymal stromal cell-derived EVs (MSC-EVs) and HEK293F-derived EVs (293F-EVs). Under dynamic flow, both EV types acquired stable coronas, resulting in increased particle size and decreased surface charge. Proteomic profiling revealed a shared corona signature enriched in immunoglobulins, complements, and other plasma components. Functionally, PC formation enhanced macrophage uptake and triggered inflammatory activation, primarily via interactions between corona-bound immunoglobulins or complement C3 and their respective receptors. To disrupt this process, we developed a charge-shielding strategy using positively charged chitosan oligosaccharide (COS) to inhibit PC assembly. COS coating effectively neutralized EV surface charge and reduced opsonin adsorption and non-specific macrophage clearance, thereby reshaping EV biodistribution—limiting hepatic sequestration and enhancing delivery to extrahepatic organs. In a murine sepsis model, COS-modified MSC-EVs further improved renal and pulmonary outcomes and markedly increased survival. Collectively, these findings elucidate the molecular architecture and immunological impact of the EV-associated plasma PC and introduce a promising anti-corona strategy for engineering stealthier and more effective EV-based nanotherapeutics.Keywords: Extracellular vesicles, Protein corona, Chitosan oligosaccharide, Surface charge, Mesenchymal stem cell

Introduction

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as versatile platforms in precision medicine [1,2]. Among them, mesenchymal stromal cell-derived EVs (MSC-EVs) inherit the regenerative properties of their parent cells, making them attractive candidates for cell-free therapy [3,4]. Meanwhile, EVs derived from HEK293F cells (293F-EVs) have been widely engineered to deliver small molecules, proteins, and nucleic acids, offering new strategies for targeted therapy [5,6]. These EV-based nanomedicines are steadily advancing from preclinical development to clinical translation. However, following systemic administration, EVs are exposed to plasma proteins, circulating immune cells, and hemodynamic forces. How these factors influence EV clearance, biodistribution, and therapeutic bioavailability remains incompletely understood.

Upon entering circulation, nanomedicines rapidly acquire a plasma protein corona (PC) that shapes their pharmacokinetics, bioavailability, and immune recognition [[7], [8], [9]]. Albumin-rich coronas may prolong circulation time [10], whereas those laden with complement proteins or immunoglobulins often trigger rapid clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) [11,12]. Although PC formation is well-characterized in synthetic nanoparticles, its behavior on EVs remains poorly defined [13,14]. Notably, Liam-Or et al. reported that MSC-EVs from 3D cultures develop albumin-rich coronas that enhance non-phagocytic liver cell uptake [15], whereas other studies show that plasma PC-coated EVs can provoke immune activation [16,17]. These findings underscore the compositional and functional variability of EV coronas. However, most existing studies focus on tumor- or immune cell-derived EVs and use static incubation with serum—conditions that poorly mimic the dynamic circulatory environment. Consequently, the PC landscape and its functional impacts on clinically translatable EVs, such as MSC-EVs and 293F-EVs, remain largely uncharacterized.

Plasma PC formation profoundly impacts nanomedicine performance, with non-specific MPS clearance representing a persistent translational barrier. This process promotes hepatic sequestration and limits delivery to extrahepatic tissues [18]. Consequently, reducing MPS-mediated clearance and enhancing extrahepatic biodistribution have become increasingly important for the development of nanomedicines. To address this challenge, efforts have focused on modulating PC assembly, which is primarily governed by electrostatic interactions, hydrophobic forces, hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals interactions [19]. Nanoparticles with high surface charge or strong hydrophobicity are particularly prone to protein adsorption [19,20]. Accordingly, surface modifications with hydrophilic or charge-neutral materials, such as polyethylene glycol [21] and zwitterionic polymers [22], have proven effective in reducing PC formation on synthetic nanoparticles. However, clinically validated strategies to prevent MPS-mediated clearance, particularly hepatic sequestration, remain limited. Importantly, the applicability of these strategies to EVs is unexplored. Although EVs are intrinsically hydrophilic, their negative surface charge may promote electrostatic adsorption of plasma proteins. This highlights the potential of surface charge modulation to reduce PC formation, though developing safe and effective approaches for EV charge regulation remains a major challenge.

In this study, we established a biomimetic circulation system to model human hemodynamics and investigate plasma PC formation on GMP-compatible MSC-EVs and 293F-EVs. PC assembly altered EV surface properties and enriched them with opsonins, promoting macrophage uptake and inflammation—key barriers to therapeutic efficacy. To address this, we developed a chitosan oligosaccharide (COS)-based surface charge engineering strategy that inhibited protein adsorption, evaded immune clearance, and redirected EV biodistribution, significantly boosting MSC-EV efficacy against sepsis. Our work uncovers the PC as a critical regulator of EV fate and establishes COS functionalization as a promising strategy for engineering more targetable and effective EV therapeutics.

Materials and methods

Human umbilical cord-derived MSC culture

MSCs were isolated from human umbilical cords as previously described [23]. Umbilical cords were obtained from healthy donors at Zhongda Hospital, Southeast University, under protocols approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2023ZDSYLL118-P01). The cords were aseptically processed by removing blood vessels and the amniotic membrane, then mincing Wharton's jelly into 1–3 mm3 fragments. Fragments were washed twice with PBS and cultured in GMP-grade mesenchymal stem cell serum-free medium (NC0107, YOCON) at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Once umbilical cord MSCs had migrated and expanded to passage 3, their surface markers and trilineage differentiation potential were verified. Qualified cells were then subjected to 3D culture. Briefly, 0.3 g of microcarriers (Cytodex 1, Cytiva) was suspended in 30 mL PBS and incubated overnight at room temperature. The following day, PBS was replaced with 75 mL of GMP-grade serum-free medium (NC0107, YOCON). The microcarrier suspension was transferred to an impeller-equipped flask (R009-05-01, CytoNiche), and 1 × 107 MSCs were seeded and gently mixed. Cultures were maintained in a bioreactor (W01-200, CytoNiche) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. On day 1, the bioreactor operated in intermittent agitation mode (30 rpm for 3 min, followed by 0 rpm for 30 min, repeated 30 times). From day 2 onward, continuous stirring was applied at 40 rpm. Starting on day 3, culture supernatants were collected every 2−3 days for EV isolation, with an equal volume of fresh medium replenished after each collection. Cultures were maintained until day 14.

Expi293F cell culture

Human embryonic kidney Expi293F suspension cells (A14527CN, Gibco) were seeded at a density of 0.3−0.5 × 106 cells/mL in disposable Erlenmeyer flasks and cultured in chemically defined, serum-free Expi293 Expression Medium (A1435101, Gibco). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in an environment of 8% CO2 and 90% humidity, with shaking at 100 rpm. After 3−4 days of continuous culture, when the cell density reached 3−5 × 106 cells/mL, the culture was terminated and the supernatant was harvested for EV isolation.

EV isolation and purification

MSC and 293F supernatants were processed using a standardized EV purification protocol. Supernatants were first subjected to sequential centrifugation at 2000×g for 20 min and 10,000×g for 30 min to remove cells, debris, and large vesicles. The resulting supernatants were then ultracentrifuged at 100,000×g for 2 h using a 45-Ti rotor in an Optima XPN-100 ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter) to pellet small EVs. The EV pellets were resuspended in PBS and further purified using the Exosupur kit (Echo-9101A-S10, Echo Biotech) based on size exclusion chromatography (SEC). High-purity EV-enriched fractions were pooled and stored at −80 °C for downstream experiments.

Production of EV-depleted blood plasma (EVDP)

Whole blood was obtained from healthy donors in accordance with ethical guidelines approved by the Ethics Committee for Clinical Research of Zhongda Hospital, Southeast University (Approval No. 2024ZDSYLL249-P01). EV-depleted plasma (EVDP) was prepared as previously described [16,24]. Plasma was isolated by centrifuging the whole blood twice at 2500×g for 15 min at room temperature. The resulting plasma was immediately diluted 1:10−1:20 in ice-cold PBS and centrifuged at 10,000×g for 40 min to pellet large EVs. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22-μm pore filter and subsequently ultracentrifuged at 167,000×g for 16 h at 4 °C to remove residual small EVs. The final supernatant was concentrated to its original volume using a 3-kDa molecular weight cutoff filter (UFC9003, Millipore). The EVDP was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C.

Dynamic incubation in a biomimetic circulation system

A digital peristaltic pump (KrosFlo KR1, REPLIGEN) equipped with a silicone tube (6 mm inner diameter, 300 mm length) was used to establish a biomimetic dynamic circulation system, enabling EVs to interact with EVDP under flow conditions. The RPM was adjusted to achieve a physiologically relevant flow velocity, approximating human abdominal aortic flow (≈40 cm/s at 37 °C) [25]. A mixture of 1 mL EV solution (1 × 1011 particles/mL) and 1 mL EVDP was introduced into the tubing using a fine insulin syringe. After 30 min of circulation, the EV:EVDP mixture was collected, and EVs were isolated by ultracentrifugation and SEC as previously described. EVs bearing plasma PC were subsequently quantified by NTA, and equal particle numbers were used in all downstream experiments. To ensure reproducibility, EVs from independent batches and EVDP from different donors were used.

COS modification

COS (C2849, TCI Chemicals) was dissolved in PBS to generate a concentration gradient (0−200 μg/mL). EV solution (5 × 1010 particles/mL) was added dropwise to the COS solution at a final volume ratio of approximately 1:1.5 (EV:COS), followed by thorough mixing. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h on a rotary mixer. COS-modified EVs were then purified using 100-kDa centrifugal filters (UFC510096, Millipore) and stored at −80 °C for immediate use.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

Particle size, concentration, and zeta potential were measured using nanoparticle tracking analysis (ZetaView PMX 110, Particle Metrix). EV samples were diluted in 0.22 μm-filtered PBS to achieve an optimal particle density of 40−200 particles per frame. The instrument was calibrated with 100 nm polystyrene beads and operated at ambient temperature (20−25 °C). Triplicate measurements were acquired at 11 preprogrammed positions using ZetaView software (v8.05.16) with standardized settings: sensitivity 85, shutter speed 100, frame rate 30 fps, and minimum brightness 20. Data were analyzed using Brownian motion-based algorithms in batch mode. Zeta potential was measured after diluting EVs in ultrapure deionized water (conductivity: 50−200 μS/cm). Values were calculated using the Smoluchowski approximation based on three sequential measurements at 25 °C.

Raman spectroscopy

The chemical composition of EVs was analyzed using a confocal Raman microscope (Alpha300R, WITec GmbH) equipped with a piezoelectric stage (UHTS 300), a 100× air objective lens (Zeiss EC EPIPLAN, N.A. = 0.9), and a green solid-state excitation laser (λ = 532 nm, 20 mW). Raman signals were collected using an imaging spectrograph (Newton, Andor Technology) with a 600 groove/mm grating and a thermoelectrically cooled charged-coupled detector, optically coupled to the objective via a 10 μm single-mode silica fiber-optic cable. The laser excitation spot size was approximately 350 nm, and spectra were acquired over a wavenumber range of 0−3600 cm−1. Laser intensity and integration time (5−8 s) were kept constant across all sample scans. Approximately 60 spectra were collected per sample.

Proteomic analysis

Mass spectrometry was conducted at Echo Biotech Co., Ltd. to profile differential proteins in EVs before and after plasma PC formation, aiming to elucidate corona composition. A total of 5 μg of EV protein extract was processed using an integrated chromatographic column that enabled sequential protein loading, reduction, alkylation, enzymatic digestion, and desalting. Proteins were first enriched on a mixed SCX/SAX magnetic bead layer, then eluted with high-salt buffer to a C18 reverse-phase membrane for desalting and lyophilization. Peptides were reconstituted in 0.1% (v/v) formic acid and analyzed using a nano-flow UHPLC system (EASY-nLC 1200, Thermo Scientific) coupled to a Q Exactive HFX mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Full MS scans were acquired over an m/z range of 400–1250 at 120,000 resolution, and MS/MS scans were collected at 30,000 resolution. Data-independent acquisition (DIA) was performed with an 18 m/z isolation window and an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 1E6. Significantly altered proteins were identified using dual thresholds: |log2FC|≥ 0.58 and adjusted P ≤ 0.05. Plasma PC components were annotated by cross-referencing with the Human Protein Atlas (v22.1, www.proteinatlas.org) and the PRoteomics IDEntifications (PRIDE) database (accession: PXD046562), prioritizing proteins classified as “plasma-enriched” in both sources.

Super-resolution microscopy imaging

EVs with plasma PC were captured on ethanol-immersed coverslips (10812, Ibidi) using streptavidin (1 μg/mL) and biotin-conjugated anti-CD63 (1 μg/mL) (143918, BioLegend). A total of >1010 EVs were captured and fixed with acetone. Subsequently, the samples were incubated with primary antibodies—anti-CD63-CF568 (EV Profiler Kit2, ONI), anti-IGHG1 (CSB-PA009510, Cusabio), anti-C3 (ab200999, Abcam), anti-APOA1 (sc376818, Santa Cruz), anti-FGA (sc398806, Santa Cruz), and anti-A2M (sc390544, Santa Cruz)—for 1 h, followed by incubation with corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Negative controls included incubations without EVs or with isotype antibodies. After staining, coverslips were washed, incubated with imaging buffer, and imaged immediately. Imaging was performed under dSTORM mode using a Nanoimager system (ONI, Oxford) with Nanoimager™ Software (v1.19). The light program was set to 50% intensity at 488 nm or 561 nm and 30% intensity at 640 nm, with 1000 frames acquired per channel. For each sample, five random fields were imaged and analyzed using the EV Profiling App on the ONI online platform CODI (https://alto.codi.bio/).

Labeling of EVs

EVs with or without PC formation were labeled with 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindodicarbocyanine perchlorate (DID) dye (C1039, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, DID labeling was performed under identical conditions across all groups, including EV input (1 × 1011 particles/mL), dye concentration (5 μM), incubation time (2 h), and temperature (room temperature), to ensure comparability. After labeling, excess unbound dye was removed using an Exosupur kit (Echo-9101A-S10, Echo Biotech). The purified DID-labeled EVs were then analyzed by nano-flow cytometry to determine the percentage of DiD-positive EVs and the mean fluorescence intensity, thereby confirming labeling efficiency and consistency across groups.

In vitro evaluation of the immunomodulatory effects of EVs on macrophages

RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded in 12-well plates and, upon reaching 70–80% confluence, were washed with PBS and serum-starved for 6 h. The macrophages were then incubated with EVs either with or without a plasma PC (1 × 108 particles) for 12 h. Following treatment, culture supernatants were collected for ELISA measurement of IL-6 (KE10007, Proteintech) and TNF-α (MTA00B-1, R&D Systems) levels according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were subsequently harvested for flow cytometric analysis of macrophage polarization using CD86-allophycocyanin (APC) (558703, BD Biosciences) and CD206-PE (141706, Biolegend) as markers of M1 and M2 phenotypes, respectively, and for qPCR analysis of pro- and anti-inflammatory gene expression.

Flow cytometric analysis of macrophage uptake of EVs

DID-labeling RAW264.7 macrophages were incubated with DiD-labeled EVs (2 × 109 particles) for 6 h. Following incubation, cells were trypsinized and washed twice with PBS. Samples were analyzed using a Guava® easyCyte™ HT flow cytometer (Luminex) with excitation at 640 nm and emission detection at 670 nm. Data were processed using FlowJo software (v10.8.1). To investigate the role of opsonin-mediated uptake, RAW264.7 cells were pre-treated with either a C3R inhibitor (20 μM) (SB290157, MedChemExpress) or an FcR-blocking antibody (1 μg/mL) (E-AB-F0997A, Elabscience) at 37 °C for 30 min prior to EV exposure.

Nano-flow cytometry (nanoFCM)

A total of 50 μL of EVs, containing approximately 1 × 109 particles, was incubated with 2 μL of primary antibodies against C3 (ab200999, Abcam), IGHG1 (CSB-PA009510, Cusabio), APOA1 (sc376818, Santa Cruz), FGA (sc398806, Santa Cruz), and A2M (sc390544, Santa Cruz) for 1 h at 25 °C. After incubation, samples were washed with PBS and stained with the corresponding Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibodies at a final concentration of 0.2 μg/μL for 1 h at 25 °C. Unstained controls (EVs without antibody) were included in parallel to assess background fluorescence. Data acquisition was performed using nano-flow cytometry (Apogee A60 Micro-Plus, Apogee Flow Systems). Prior to analysis, a two-step calibration was conducted using Apogee 1524 microspheres for optical alignment and Apogee 1527 particles for performance tracking. System cleanliness was confirmed with pre-analysis controls using sheath fluid and PBS. For data analysis, only particles with diameters between 80 and 100 nm defined based on standard microsphere calibration were included. To ensure statistical robustness, a minimum of 500,000 EV events was recorded per sample.

Microscale thermophoresis

The effect of COS modification on the binding affinity of C3 and IGHG1 to EVs was evaluated using microscale thermophoresis (Monolith NT.115, NanoTemper Technologies). Unmodified and COS-modified EVs were first labeled with DiD, and unbound dye was removed via size-exclusion chromatography. DiD-labeled EVs (1 × 1011 particles/mL) were then mixed in equal volumes with serial dilutions of C3 (APA861Hu01, Cloud-Clone) or IGHG1 (HY-P70796, MedChemExpress) to assess binding interactions. The mixtures were loaded into MST capillaries and analyzed at 20% MST power. Binding was determined based on changes in the thermophoretic movement of DiD-labeled EVs upon complex formation with the proteins. Data were analyzed using MO Affinity Analysis Software (version 2.3).

Cecal ligation and puncture-induced sepsis and therapeutic experiments

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with standard guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Southeast University (Approval No. 20220225010). Male C57BL/6 mice (8–10 weeks old, 20–22 g) were obtained from the National Model Animal Center of Nanjing University. Sepsis was induced using the cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) model. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with 2.5% tribromoethanol and subjected to a midline laparotomy. The cecum was ligated 1 cm from the tip using 5-0 silk sutures, punctured twice through approximately 50% of its width with a 25-gauge needle, and gently compressed to ensure fecal extrusion. Sham-operated mice underwent laparotomy without cecal ligation or puncture. Postoperative care included a subcutaneous injection of 1 mL 0.9% saline and maintenance on a 37 °C heating pad until full recovery.

Unmodified or COS-modified MSC-EVs (2 × 109 particles) or vehicle were administered via tail vein injection at 0 and 24 h following CLP surgery. Mice were euthanized 48 h post-surgery, and blood and organs were collected for subsequent analyses. For blood biochemical and inflammatory marker assessments, serum levels of creatinine (C011-2-1), blood urea nitrogen (C013-2-1), alanine aminotransferase (C009-2-1), aspartate aminotransferase (C010-2-1), lactate dehydrogenase (A020-1-1), lactic acid (A019-2-1), and myeloperoxidase (A044-1-1) were measured using commercial kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute. C-reactive protein (DY5105, R&D Systems) and interleukin-6 (DY406, R&D Systems) were quantified using ELISA kits from R&D Systems, following the manufacturers’ instructions. For histological analysis, lungs and kidneys were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Lung sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), while kidney sections were stained with periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) for morphological evaluation.

Macrophage clearance

Phagocytosis of EVs by circulating monocytes was evaluated by flow cytometry. A murine sepsis model was induced by cecal ligation and puncture, followed by tail vein injection of DiD-labeled EVs (1 × 1010 particles). Two hours post-injection, 100 μL of blood was collected from the tail vein. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated using Lymphocyte Separation Medium (LTS1077, TBD Science) and stained with FITC-conjugated anti-CD11b antibody (557396, BD Pharmingen) for 30 min in the dark. After washing with PBS, samples were analyzed on a Guava® easyCyte™ HT flow cytometer (Luminex). CD11b+ monocytes were gated, and the percentage of DiD-positive cells within this population was quantified to assess EV uptake.

In vivo distribution of EVs

To evaluate the in vivo tissue distribution of EVs, DiD-labeled EVs (1 × 1010 particles) were administered via tail vein injection 24 h after CLP surgery. Mice were euthanized 10 h post-injection, and major organs, including the liver, heart, lungs, spleen, and kidneys, were harvested for imaging using the IVIS Spectrum system (PerkinElmer) under the following settings: FOV 14 cm, medium binning, f1, 1 s exposure with an excitation/emission filter of 640 nm/680 nm. Fluorescence quantification was performed using Living Image software (version 4.7.3). Regions of interest (ROIs) were drawn for each organ, and radiant efficiency was calculated as [(p/s/cm2/sr)/(μW/cm2)]. Background fluorescence was measured and subtracted by setting up a background measurement at time of data acquisition.

Immunofluorescence staining

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded lung and kidney sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval by boiling in EDTA buffer (pH 8.0) for 10 min. After blocking with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against F4/80 (GB113373-100, Servicebio) and GSDMD (ab219800, Abcam), followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Images were acquired using an FV3000 confocal microscope, and F4/80+ cells per field and GSDMD+ area were quantified based on three sections from five mice per group.

Western blotting

EV protein lysates were prepared following standard protocols, and protein concentrations were measured using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (KGB2101, KeyGEN BioTECH). Equal amounts of EVs (1 × 109 particles) were separated by SDS-PAGE on 4–20% Bis-Tris gels (ET10420Gel, ACE Biotechnology) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (ISEQ00010, Millipore) using a rapid transfer system (L00686C, GenScript). Membranes were blocked with QuickBlock™ Rapid Blocking Buffer (P30500, New Cell & Molecular Biotech) for 15 min at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing with Tris-buffered saline containing Tween 20, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam) for 90 min at room temperature. Signals were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (WBULS0500, Merck Millipore). Primary antibodies used were anti-Alix (ab275377, Abcam), anti-CD9 (ab236630, Abcam), anti-CD81 (sc166029, Santa Cruz), anti-Calnexin (WL03062, Wanlei), anti-IGHG1 (CSB-PA009510, Cusabio), anti-C3 (ab200999, Abcam), anti-APOA1 (sc376818, Santa Cruz), anti-FGA (sc398806, Santa Cruz), and anti-A2M (sc390544, Santa Cruz).

For silver staining, equal amounts of EVs were separated by SDS-PAGE and visualized using a commercial silver staining kit (P0017S, Beyotime Biotechnology). Gels were fixed overnight in a solution of ethanol/acetic acid/ultrapure water (50:10:40, v/v), followed by sequential treatments with 30% ethanol for 10 min, ultrapure water for 10 min, sensitizer for 2 min, and freshly prepared silver solution for 10 min. The staining reaction was stopped using stop solution for 10 min, after which the gels were rinsed with ultrapure water for 2–5 min and subsequently imaged.

Quantitative real-time PCR assay

Total RNA was extracted using FreeZol Reagent (R711, Vazyme) following cell lysis, chloroform-mediated phase separation, and isopropanol precipitation. The RNA pellets were washed with 75% ethanol prepared in DEPC-treated water, air-dried, and resuspended in nuclease-free water. RNA purity (A260/280 > 1.8) and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Reverse transcription was carried out using HiScript III RT SuperMix (R323-01, Vazyme), and quantitative PCR was conducted with ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q331-02, Vazyme) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex system (Applied Biosystems). Gene expression levels were normalized to β-actin and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primers used are listed in Supplemental Table 3.

Biosafety evaluation of COS-engineered EVs

COS-modified MSC-EVs, COS-modified 293F-EVs (1 × 1010 particles), or vehicle control were administered to mice via tail vein injection every other day for 2 weeks. Body weight was monitored throughout the study at 2-day intervals. At the end of the treatment period, serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and serum creatinine (sCr) were measured to assess hepatic and renal function. Major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, were harvested for histopathological evaluation.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between two groups were analyzed using a two-tailed Student's t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Unless otherwise specified, all results represent at least three independent biological replicates. Sample sizes for proteomic analyses are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. For in vitro experiments, each replicate was performed using EVs derived from an independent batch and distinct plasma sample.

Results

Characterization of plasma protein corona formation on MSC-EVs and 293F-EVs

MSC-EVs and 293F-EVs were produced under conditions compatible with GMP standard and validated according to ISEV criteria (Fig. S1) [26]. To induce plasma PC formation, both EV types from independent batches were dynamically incubated with EV-depleted plasma from distinct healthy donors using a biomimetic circulation system, followed by comprehensive characterization (Fig. 1A).

(D, E) Percentage distribution of particle size ranges for MSC-EVs (D) and 293F-EVs (E), corresponding to panels B and C (n = 3 biological replicates). (F, G) Changes in zeta potential of MSC-EVs (F) and 293F-EVs (G) induced by PC formation, assessed by nanoparticle tracking analysis (n = 5 biological replicates). (H, I) Per-particle protein content of MSC-EVs (H) and 293F-EVs (I) before and after PC formation, determined by normalizing total protein to particle number (n = 3 biological replicates). (J) Representative silver-stained SDS-PAGE gels and densitometric analysis comparing protein profiles of MSC-EVs and 293F-EVs with or without PC formation (n = 3 biological replicates). (K, L) Raman spectra (solid lines) with 5% standard deviation (shaded area) of MSC-EVs (K) and 293F-EVs (L) before and after PC formation. Intensities of protein-associated Raman bands at 1257, 2901, and 2940 cm−1 were quantitatively compared (n = 3 biological replicates). Data are presented as means ± SD. As shown in Fig. 1B and C, PC formation (PC+) significantly increased the median diameter of MSC-EVs (from 129.5 ± 2.6 nm to 140 ± 1.6 nm) and 293F-EVs (from 131.3 ± 1.8 nm to 149.5 ± 4.3 nm). The proportion of particles <150 nm decreased for MSC-EVs (from 67% to 56.7%) and 293F-EVs (from 59% to 49%), while those >150 nm increased correspondingly (Fig. 1D and E), indicating notable size enlargement due to protein adsorption. Zeta potential analysis revealed a significant reduction in surface negativity, with MSC-EVs shifting from −35.3 ± 7.5 mV to −22.7 ± 14.8 mV, and 293F-EVs from −32.4 ± 8.7 mV to −13.0 ± 4.5 mV, suggesting electrostatic interactions between EVs and plasma proteins (Fig. 1F and G). Protein quantification further confirmed increased protein loading, with ∼3.1-fold and ∼5-fold increases per MSC-EV and 293F-EV, respectively (Fig. 1H and I). Gel electrophoresis demonstrated multiple new bands in corona-coated EVs, primarily within the 55-250 kDa and 25-45 kDa ranges, closely resembling the banding pattern of EV-depleted plasma (EVDP) (Fig. 1J). Furthermore, Raman spectroscopy was used to assess EV compositional changes. While the overall fingerprint spectra remained largely unchanged, protein-specific peaks at 1257 cm−1, 2901 cm−1, and 2940 cm−1 were markedly enhanced following PC formation, supporting the increased plasma protein adsorption (Fig. 1K and L). Collectively, these results demonstrate that dynamic plasma exposure induces substantial PC formation on EVs, leading to increased particle size, higher protein content, and reduced surface charge.

Plasma protein corona profiling of MSC-EVs and 293F-EVs

To gain deeper insight into plasma PC composition, we profiled MSC-EVs and 293F-EVs after plasma exposure using direct-DIA mass spectrometry. Compared to naïve EVs (unexposed to plasma), 335 and 433 upregulated proteins were identified on MSC-EVs and 293F-EVs, respectively (Fig. 2A). Cross-referencing with the Human Blood Atlas revealed 208 plasma-derived proteins on MSC-EVs and 263 on 293F-EVs (Fig. 2B and Tables S1 and S2). The top 10 corona components on MSC-EVs were apolipoprotein (APO) A1, haptoglobin (HP), immunoglobulin heavy constant γ 1 (IGHG1), α-2-macroglobulin (A2M), APOA2, IGHA1, C3, fibrinogen α chain (FGA), APOH, and serum amyloid A4 (SAA4) (Table S1), while those on 293F-EVs included albumin, IGHG1, IGHG2, A2M, HP, APOA1, IGHM, APOA2, C3, and IGHA1 (Table S2). The two EV types exhibited broadly similar high-abundance plasma protein profiles, with 50% overlap among the top 50 proteins.

To investigate shared patterns of PC formation, we focused on the 82 overlapping plasma proteins found on both EV types, with expression levels detailed in Fig. 2C. Bioinformatic categorization of these corona proteins by molecular function revealed key classes: immunoglobulins (21%), complement proteins (9%), lipoproteins (7%), coagulation-related proteins (13%), acute phase proteins (7%), and tissue leakage proteins (6%) (Fig. 2D). Within each functional category, both EV types displayed the presence of multiple representative protein species (Fig. 2E). To validate the presence of representative high-abundance proteins, we selected IGHG1, C3, ApoA1, FGA, and A2M for further analysis. Western blotting confirmed that these proteins were nearly undetectable in naïve EVs but became substantially enriched following PC formation (Fig. 2F). Single-molecule super-resolution imaging revealed their diffuse distribution around EVs (Fig. 2G). These findings demonstrate that MSC-EVs and 293F-EVs acquire a core set of plasma-derived corona proteins enriched in immunoglobulins and complement components, providing insight into the compositional features of protein corona formation on EV surfaces.

Plasma protein corona formation triggers macrophage uptake and immune responses

Given that opsonins, such as immunoglobulins, complement, and tissue leakage proteins, constitute over 30% of the shared plasma proteins in MSC-EV and 293F-EV coronas, their potential role in EV clearance and immunogenicity warrants attention. GO enrichment analysis of the 82 shared plasma proteins revealed significant enrichment in processes related to complement activation, humoral immune response, and their regulation (Fig. 3A), suggesting that corona formation may enhance EV-mediated immune responses. To test this, we assessed the immunomodulatory effects of MSC-EVs and 293F-EVs bearing plasma PC on epithelial, endothelial, and immune cells. The presence of a PC had minimal impacts on AML12 hepatocytes and human umbilical vein endothelial cells. In contrast, macrophages exhibited marked upregulation of pro-inflammatory factors, including IL-6, TNF-α, CCL2, and CCL5, accompanied by decreased expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 at the mRNA level (Fig. 3B). Consistently, both EV types with PC significantly increased IL-6 and TNF-α secretion (Fig. 3C and D) and promoted macrophage polarization toward a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype (Fig. 3E and F). Since opsonins are known to enhance nanoparticle phagocytosis [11,12], we next assessed whether corona formation affected EV uptake by macrophages. Flow cytometry revealed that corona-coated MSC-EVs and 293F-EVs were taken up more efficiently by macrophages. This enhanced uptake was significantly inhibited by blocking complement C3 and Fc receptors (Fig. 3G and H), implicating complement fragments and immunoglobulins in receptor-mediated phagocytosis. Overall, these results demonstrate that plasma PC formation promotes macrophage uptake of EVs and triggers their activation.

Chitosan oligosaccharide modification protects MSC-EVs and 293F-EVs from plasma protein corona

Plasma PC formation is largely driven by electrostatic interactions, suggesting that surface charge modulation could offer a viable strategy to control PC assembly. Chitosan oligosaccharide (COS), a naturally cationic polysaccharide with excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and low toxicity, has already been approved by the FDA for biomedical use [27]. Its positive charge has been exploited to modify nanomaterials, enhancing electrostatic interactions with negatively charged cell surfaces or mucus [28,29]. We therefore hypothesized that COS could serve as an effective surface charge modulator: by neutralizing the intrinsic negative charge of EVs, COS may reduce nonspecific plasma protein adsorption and ultimately inhibit PC formation (Fig. 4A).

To determine the optimal COS concentration for EV surface modification, 293F-EVs were incubated with increasing COS doses, and zeta potential was measured. COS induced a dose-dependent increase in surface potential, with a sharp rise from −40.6 ± 3.1 mV to −14.0 ± 0.3 mV at 50 μg/mL, indicating effective charge neutralization. At higher concentrations, the zeta potential plateaued, suggesting saturation of binding sites on the EV surface (Fig. S2A). Correspondingly, BCA assays showed a dose-dependent decrease in plasma protein adsorption, peaking at 75 μg/mL (Fig. S2B). Gel electrophoresis confirmed reduced protein binding, with weaker bands at ∼10, 25, 55, and 70 kDa in the 75 μg/mL COS-treated group compared to unmodified EVs (Fig. S2C). Furthermore, 75 μg/mL COS preserved EV morphology, particle size, and marker protein expression, while maintaining stable zeta potential for up to 48 h after modification (Fig. S3). These findings establish 75 μg/mL as the optimal COS concentration for EV surface modification in subsequent experiments.

Next, we systematically evaluated the inhibitory effect of 75 μg/mL COS modification on plasma PC formation on EVs. Zeta potential analysis showed that plasma protein adsorption significantly reduced the negative surface charge of MSC-EVs and 293F-EVs, causing shifts of 15.8 ± 2.3 mV and 19.2 ± 1.0 mV, respectively. In contrast, COS-modified EVs maintained stable zeta potentials, with shifts under 1 mV, indicating effective prevention of plasma protein adsorption and preservation of surface charge stability (Fig. 4B and C). NTA revealed that COS modification mitigated PC-induced size increases, significantly lowering the median particle size of both EV types (Fig. 4D and E). It also increased the proportion of small vesicles (<200 nm) from 68.5% to 90.4% in MSC-EVs and from 57.9% to 79% in 293F-EVs (Fig. 4F and G). BCA assays confirmed a significant reduction in protein loading per EV following plasma incubation (Fig. 4H and I). Thus, COS modification effectively inhibits plasma protein adsorption.

Focusing on abundant plasma corona proteins IGHG1, C3, ApoA1, FGA, and A2M, we evaluated the effect of COS modification on their adsorption to EV surfaces. After plasma incubation, these proteins significantly increased on unmodified (COS−) EVs but were markedly suppressed by COS treatment (COS+) (Fig. 4J and K). Nano-flow cytometry further confirmed higher positive rates and fluorescence intensities in COS− EVs compared to naïve MSC- and 293F-EVs, while COS modification effectively reduced their binding (Fig. 4L and M). To quantitatively assess binding affinity, microscale thermophoresis measured interactions between IGHG1, C3, and 293F-EVs, revealing that COS increased the dissociation constant of IGHG1 from 2.588 μM to 21.854 μM (Fig. 4N), and of C3 from 0.64 μM to 16.338 μM (Fig. 4O), indicating reduced binding affinity. These results establish a COS-based anti-corona strategy that effectively suppresses nonspecific plasma protein adsorption by modulating EV surface charge.

Chitosan oligosaccharide modified EVs evade macrophage clearance and enhance extrahepatic bioavailability

Given the potent pro-phagocytic and pro-inflammatory effects of the plasma PC, we explored whether COS modification could counteract these effects. Flow cytometry showed that PC formation markedly enhanced macrophage uptake of both MSC-EVs and 293F-EVs, whereas COS modification substantially reduced it (Fig. 5A and B). Consistent with the above findings, PC formation markedly induced macrophage activation, as evidenced by upregulated expression of pro-inflammatory genes (IL-6, TNF-α, CCL-2, and CCL-5 mRNA) (Fig. 5C and D), increased secretion of IL-6 and TNF-α (Fig. 5E and F), and promotion of M1 polarization (Fig. 5G and H), whereas these effects were attenuated by COS modification. Notably, COS treatment after PC formation failed to suppress cytokine induction (Fig. S4), indicating that at the tested concentrations, COS lacks direct anti-inflammatory activity and instead acts primarily by preventing PC formation.

The MPS, particularly in the liver, is the main route of clearance for intravenously administered EVs. Leveraging COS's inhibitory effect on PC-mediated macrophage uptake, we hypothesized that COS modification could mitigate nonspecific MPS clearance. In healthy mice, MSC-EVs primarily accumulated in the liver and lungs, but COS modification significantly reduced their uptake in the liver, lungs, and spleen (Fig. S5A). Similarly, 293F-EVs showed high hepatic and splenic accumulation, which was markedly decreased by COS treatment, especially in the liver (Fig. S5B). We then assessed this effect in a cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced sepsis model. COS modification significantly reduced EV uptake by CD11b+ monocytes in peripheral blood (Fig. 5I) and by liver macrophages (Fig. S5C). Consistent with these findings, COS modification decreased hepatic accumulation while enhancing MSC-EV distribution to the lungs, brain, heart, and kidneys, and 293F-EV distribution to the heart and kidneys (Fig. 5J and K). Collectively, these data demonstrate that COS modification disrupts PC formation, thereby reducing nonspecific MPS clearance—especially in the liver—and enhancing EV bioavailability in extrahepatic organs.

COS-engineered MSC-EVs boost protection against sepsis-induced lung and kidney injury

Given that COS modification enhanced MSC-EV accumulation in extrahepatic organs, particularly the lungs and kidneys, we next sought to determine whether this altered biodistribution could translate into therapeutic benefit. Sepsis is characterized by systemic inflammation and multi-organ dysfunction, with the lungs and kidneys being among the frequently affected organs [30,31]. We therefore evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of COS-engineered MSC-EVs in a CLP-induced sepsis model. Mice received intravenous injections of unmodified (COS−) or COS-modified (COS+) MSC-EVs at 0 and 24 h post-surgery and were sacrificed at 48 h. Survival improved from 40% (CLP group) to 53.3% with COS− MSC-EVs and further to 73.3% with COS+ MSC-EVs (Fig. 6A). Serum ALT and AST levels indicated comparable alleviation of liver injury by both MSC-EVs (Fig. 6B and C), suggesting that reduced hepatic accumulation in the COS+ group did not compromise liver protection. Importantly, while COS− MSC-EVs reduced sCr without statistical significance, COS-modified MSC-EVs significantly lowered sCr and reduced the incidence of acute kidney injury (AKI) to 10% (Fig. 6D). COS modification also led to greater and more consistent reductions in serum lactate (LAC) (Fig. 6E), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) (Fig. 6F), and inflammatory markers including C-reactive protein (CRP) (Fig. 6G), IL-6 (Fig. 6H), and myeloperoxidase (MPO) (Fig. 6I).

Histological analysis revealed that COS modification enhances MSC-EV protection against lung and kidney injury—two major target organs in sepsis. In CLP mice, lung tissues showed alveolar septal thickening and inflammatory infiltration, which were alleviated by MSC-EVs and further reduced by COS-modified EVs (Fig. 6J). Similarly, MSC-EVs partially improved CLP-induced renal damage—characterized by tubular injury and brush border loss—while COS-modified EVs preserved tissue integrity and reduced inflammation more effectively (Fig. 6J). Renal injury markers kidney injury molecular-1 (Kim-1) and neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) were elevated in CLP mice, decreased by MSC-EVs, and further suppressed by COS-modified EVs (Fig. 6K). Immunofluorescence showed reduced macrophage infiltration (Fig. 6L and M) and GSDMD expression (Fig. 6N and O) in both lungs and kidneys following COS+ MSC-EVs treatment, indicating stronger anti-inflammatory and anti-pyroptotic effects. Overall, COS modification improved survival and mitigated sepsis-induced organ damage.

Next, the in vivo biocompatibility of COS-modified EVs was assessed in healthy mice following intravenous administration every other day for 14 days, with no significant differences in body weight observed among the groups (Fig. S6A). Serum ALT, AST, BUN, and sCr levels remained comparable across all groups (Fig. S6B), and no apparent histopathological abnormalities were detected in major organs (Fig. S6C). These findings indicate that COS modification does not noticeably affect the in vivo safety of EVs.

Discussion

Nanomedicines delivered via systemic circulation are strongly influenced by plasma PC formation, which can profoundly affect their circulation kinetics, biodistribution, and immunogenicity [8,9,20,32]. Systematic evaluation of PC's impact on nanomedicine behavior is essential to accelerate preclinical translation. In this study, we engineered a biomimetic dynamic circulation system to demonstrate that two promising EV types—MSC-EVs and 293F-EVs—form distinct coronas with unique plasma protein profiles that shape their immune clearance. Building on these findings, we developed a novel COS-based anti-corona strategy that effectively reduces MPS clearance, enhances EV bioavailability in extrahepatic organs, and significantly boosts the therapeutic efficacy of MSC-EVs.

Accurately modeling plasma PC formation is essential for understanding its impact on nanocarrier properties. However, most EV studies rely on static incubation conditions [16,17], which fails to replicate the hemodynamic circulation. Insights from synthetic nanoparticles have shown that PC formed under static versus dynamic conditions differ in structure and composition [25], highlighting the importance of dynamic modeling. To better simulate in vivo conditions, we established a biomimetic dynamic circulation platform allowing EVs to interact with human plasma under flow (40 cm/s). Using this system, we demonstrated that both MSC-EVs and 293F-EVs formed plasma PC, leading to ∼20 nm increases in particle size—consistent with the hydrated layer thickness observed in PC-coated erythrocyte EVs [33]. PC formation also reduced EV surface negative charge, likely due to electrostatic interactions between positively charged plasma proteins (e.g., albumin, fibrinogen) and negatively charged EV surface components (e.g., glycoproteins, phospholipids) [13,34]. It is worth noting that changes in EV surface charge upon PC formation are not fully consistent across studies [17]. These discrepancies likely arise from differences in corona composition, determined by protein sources and formation conditions, as well as intrinsic EV properties, including baseline surface charge. These findings demonstrate that dynamic incubation alters EV physicochemical properties and suggest that modulating surface charge may offer a strategy to control PC formation.

The plasma PC critically dictates the in vivo behavior and functionality of nanomedicines, making it essential to define its composition to understand functional outcomes. In synthetic nanoparticles, the PC has been well characterized and is primarily composed of immunoglobulins, complement proteins, acute phase proteins, and coagulation [11,25,32]. In contrast, data on EV-associated PCs remain limited. Dietz et al. reported that PC on HCT116-derived EVs is enriched in IGHM, IGHK, C3, and C4 [17]. Tóth et al. identified nine common plasma proteins, including ApoA1, ApoB, ApoC3, ApoE, C3, C4B, FGA, IGHG2 and IGHG4, shared across THP1-derived EVs, viruses, and liposomes [16]. Using a dynamic plasma circulation model, we demonstrated that MSC-EVs and 293F-EVs formed PCs encompassing nearly all major functional classes of plasma proteins, with immunoglobulins being the most abundant. Our and prior observations suggest a conserved PC profile across EVs and nanoparticles, indicating that high-abundance protein adsorption may occur independently of material type or EV origin. Of note, opsonins constitute ∼30% of the PC on both EV types, potentially evoking EV immunogenicity and immune recognition. This supports prior reports linking EV-associated PCs to increased macrophage uptake and dendritic cell activation [16,17]. We further found that while PC-coated EVs elicited minimal inflammatory effects on epithelial and endothelial cells, they robustly activated macrophages, with Fc and C3 receptors playing key roles in uptake. Similarly, Bussin et al. revealed that nanoparticle corona proteins interact with FcγRI and SR-A on hepatic macrophages, promoting nonspecific capture [18]. Therefore, plasma PC is a master regulator in EV recognition and clearance, highlighting corona modulation as a potent strategy for optimizing EV delivery systems.

To overcome the challenges posed by the plasma PC, synthetic nanoparticles have explored various surface modification strategies to minimize protein adsorption [20,35,36]. Coatings with polyethylene glycol (PEG) [20], zwitterionic polymers [21], or glycosylated polyhydroxy polymer [37] have been shown to inhibit protein binding and prolong circulation time. However, PEG can elicit anti-PEG antibodies [38], compromising long-term safety and efficacy, while some zwitterionic polymers may exhibit cytotoxicity. These limitations underscore the need for safer, more biocompatible alternatives. Building on the electrostatic nature of PC formation, this study is the first to propose and validate a surface charge-shielding strategy for EVs using COS—a naturally cationic, biocompatible, low molecular weight derivative of chitosan. Although chitosan has been widely applied in biomedical fields—from drug and gene delivery to tissue engineering—and used as a nanoparticle surface modifier with potential as a stealth coating [39,40], its derivative COS's role in plasma PC inhibition has not been reported. Here, we demonstrated that COS effectively neutralizes the negative surface charge of both MSC-EVs and 293F-EVs, significantly reducing the adsorption of plasma proteins, particularly immunoglobulins and complement proteins. As PC enriched in these opsonins is known to promote MPS-mediated clearance and hepatic accumulation [41], COS modification offers a promising strategy to counteract this effect. COS coating markedly reduced macrophage uptake, decreased liver sequestration, and enhanced EV distribution to extrahepatic organs such as the kidney, heart, and lungs. Importantly, in a sepsis model, COS-modified MSC-EVs provided superior protection to lung and kidney and improved survival compared to unmodified EVs. These findings not only uncover the feasibility of COS-based PC modulation but also demonstrate that engineering EV surface properties to optimize bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy.

An important limitation of this study is that PC formation was evaluated using plasma from healthy donors. Disease-associated alterations in plasma protein composition may influence corona formation and EV behavior, warranting validation under pathological conditions. In addition, our analysis focused on the shared subset of corona proteins between MSC-EVs and 293F-EVs, which does not fully capture EV type-specific protein populations, highlighting the need for more comprehensive characterization of EV-specific corona profiles. Finally, EVs may acquire additional biomolecular coronas depending on their production and biological environment, and the interplay among these multilayered coronas remains to be systematically investigated.

This study deciphers the plasma PC landscapes of two clinically relevant EV types under physiomimetic circulation, uncovering its pivotal role in driving immune clearance. To overcome this barrier to EV efficacy, we pioneered a COS-based surface charge modulation strategy that effectively suppresses PC formation, minimizes MPS sequestration, and redirects EVs for systemic extrahepatic delivery (Fig. 7). Together, our findings establish COS-shield engineering as a biocompatible and broadly applicable platform for optimizing the targeting capability and therapeutic potential of EV-based nanomedicines.
