Clinical Outcomes and Disease Management for Patients with Paroxysmal Nocturnal Haemoglobinuria in China: Results from a Real-World Study
Authors: Rong Fu, Hui Liu, Pin-Tzu Su, Xuefei Liu, Karl-Johan Myrén, Francesca Gatta, Lucy Earl, Yasmin Taylor, Alice Simons, Elmira Lechat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParoxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria (PNH) is a chronic haematological disorder caused by uncontrolled complement activation, leading to intravascular haemolysis, indicated by increased lactate dehydrogenase levels (LDH). Standard-of-care complement factor 5 inhibitors have only recently been approved in China. Using real-world data, this study aimed to understand treatment pattern and burden of disease, including thrombosis risk, for Chinese patients with PNH.MethodsData were from the Adelphi Real World PNH Disease Specific Programme (DSP)™, a cross-sectional retrospective survey of haematologists/haematologist-oncologists and patients with PNH, conducted July–November 2022. Physicians reported demographic and clinical data on one to ten consecutively consulted patients with PNH aged ≥ 16 years. Patients were stratified by LDH level [cutoff of 1.5 times upper level of normal (ULN, 250 U/L)] and compared using bivariate analysis.ResultsOut of 113 patients, 49.5% had LDH levels ≥1.5 ULN (high-LDH), these had greater physician-perceived disease severity (p<0.001). Anticoagulation treatments (39.8%), prednisone (31.0%), and cyclosporine (27.4%) were most prescribed. More high-LDH patients received rivaroxaban (p=0.002) or vitamin B12 supplements (p=0.008). Overall, the most common symptoms were fatigue and anaemia (92.9% and 74.3%, respectively). Haemolytic crises were reported for 39.3% of all patients, thrombotic events were more common in high-LDH patients (50.0%, compared with 23.2%, p=0.006). Patients reported moderate fatigue levels in the FACIT-Fatigue scale.ConclusionsPatients were mainly treated with anticoagulants, prednisone or cyclosporine, and had frequent thrombotic events and haemolytic crises. This demonstrates the current management of PNH in China, focusing on mitigating anaemia and managing haemolytic crises rather than addressing the underlying chronic complement-mediated haemolysis and associated morbidities.

Key Points

This cross-sectional study, conducted prior to the introduction of complement inhibitors in China, is the first real-world Chinese study on the disease burden and treatment of patients with PNH.Treatment in this cohort was largely supportive (i.e. steroids) and focussed on managing haemolytic crises.Under supporting care, patients continued to have a large symptom burden, alongside frequent haemolytic crises and symptom exacerbations.

Introduction

Paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria (PNH) is a rare, chronic, life-threatening disease affecting the complement cascade—a critical part of the normal innate immune response [1]. PNH is caused by somatic mutations in the X-linked phosphatidylinositol glycan anchor biosynthesis class A gene [2]. The resultant complete or partial loss of glycosylphosphatidil-anchored proteins, such as complement regulatory proteins CD55 and CD59, makes erythrocytes sensitive to complement-mediated lysis [2].

In PNH, uncontrolled terminal complement activation affects the normal functioning of red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets, leading to the most severe consequences of PNH, such as intravascular haemolysis (IVH), thrombotic events (TE), renal impairment and pulmonary hypertension [3]. Thrombosis is the leading cause of death in patients with uncontrolled PNH, occurring in 16–46% of patients, and can be fatal at the first event [4–7].

Elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) indicates high disease activity and is a key biomarker for IVH that is associated with an increased risk of thrombosis [3, 8]. Patients who have LDH levels greater than 1.5 times the upper limit of normal (ULN, specified to be 250 U/L for this study) have been found to be at greater risk of thrombotic events and death, as well as having a poorer quality of life (QoL) [4]. Reducing LDH levels to <1.5 × ULN is a recognised goal of therapy [3, 8, 9].

Prior to the introduction of targeted complement factor 5 (C5) inhibition therapies, patients were managed through supportive care only, and 35% of patients with PNH died within 5 years of diagnosis [5, 10–15]. However, targeted C5 inhibitors eculizumab and ravulizumab have transformed the natural history of PNH by improving the survival rate to near-normal levels, similar to that seen in the general population [14]. A complete and sustained terminal complement inhibition is important to prevent breakthrough IVH and limits the expansion of red blood cell PNH clone populations, which is associated with a higher risk of thrombosis in patients with PNH [3, 16–18].

Although data are limited, evidence suggests both the prevalence and presentation of PNH may differ between Western and Asian populations. Prevalence is higher in Asian countries [1], and in a comparative study, both biochemical parameters and symptomatology were quantitatively different between Asian and non-Asian patients [9]. Furthermore, eculizumab only gained approval for use in PNH in China in 2022 [19], and was not reimbursed until 2023. Given the evidence suggesting differences in disease course between Asian patients and Western patients and the lack of approved disease-modifying drugs until recently, it is likely the disease burden and treatment patterns for Chinese patients are different as well. However, data on this are currently limited.

There is a lack of data from China on the clinical burden of disease in patients with PNH who are managed with supportive care. Therefore, our study aimed to characterise demographic and clinical details and the overall disease management of patients with PNH in China, who were managed for their disease before the availability of eculizumab, to increase the awareness around the PNH disease burden and clinical outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

Data were derived from the Adelphi Real World PNH Disease Specific Programme (DSP)™, a retrospective, cross-sectional survey of haematologists and haematologist-oncologists and their patients with PNH, conducted in routine clinical practice in China between July and November 2022, prior to the approval of eculizumab in China. The DSP methodology and generalisability have been previously described [20–22].

Physicians were selected from publicly available lists of healthcare professionals by local fieldwork agencies after being confirmed as eligible for inclusion. Recruitment took place in a geographically spread manner. Physicians were compensated for participating in the DSP according to fair market rates consistent with the time involved.

Physicians completed a survey on their practice setting and workload, and were then instructed to complete an online patient report form to return demographic and clinical data for their next one to ten eligible consulting patients with PNH. Physicians used both patients’ medical records and their own clinical judgment to complete the report forms. Patients were then requested to complete a voluntary survey containing validated patient-reported outcome measures on fatigue and quality of life (QoL).

Eligibility Criteria

In order to be included in the study, physicians were required to be a haematologist or haematologist-oncologist and be treating at least one patient with PNH at time of survey. Patients were required to have a physician confirmed diagnosis of PNH and be over the age of 16 years.

Study Measures

Physicians returned information on their specialism, practice setting, experience with clinical trials and workload of patients with PNH. Physician-reported patient demographics included age, sex, ethnicity, body mass index (BMI) and employment status.

Physician-reported clinical characteristics included time since first symptoms, time since diagnosis, time on current treatment, physician-perceived severity, symptoms at diagnosis and survey completion, concomitant conditions, treatment prescribed at time of survey and number of thrombotic events and haemolytic crises during the 12 months prior to survey. Physicians used their own clinical judgement to define a thrombotic event or haemolytic crisis, reflecting everyday clinical practice.

The most recent LDH levels, haemoglobin levels, reticulocyte counts and PNH clone sizes were also reported by physicians. Physicians also returned information on healthcare resource utilisation, including frequency and type of hospitalisation and number of blood transfusions.

Patient-Reported Outcome Measures

Patients who consented to taking part in the study completed a number of standardised patient-reported outcome measures. In order to quantify the impact of fatigue, patients completed the Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy Fatigue (FACIT-Fatigue) measure, which is a 13-item tool quantifying the level of fatigue and impact on patients’ daily life, scored from 0 (maximum fatigue) to 52 (no fatigue) [23].

The impact of PNH on QoL was quantified using the EQ-5D 5L and visual analogue scale (VAS) measures [24, 25], using a China-specific value set [26]. The EQ-5D 5L and VAS are scored from 0 to 1 and 0 to 100, respectively, with higher scores indicating a better QoL. Work and general activity impairment was assessed using the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment Questionnaire (WPAI), with impairment scored from 0 (no impairment) to 100 (complete impairment) in four domains: work time missed due to PNH (absenteeism), impairment while working due to PNH (presenteeism), overall work impairment and overall activity impairment [27].

Statistical Analysis

Patients were stratified on the basis of their LDH level into groups with a LDH level of < 1.5 x ULN and those with LDH ≥ 1.5 x ULN. ULN was defined as 250 U/L for the purpose of this study, with subgroups assigned on the basis of the physicians answering a binary question: whether the patient was above or below this threshold. Categorical data are shown as frequency and proportions with 95% confidence intervals (CI) as needed, continuous variables as means with standard deviations (SD) and/or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Missing data were not imputed, and group sizes are reported per variable. LDH level groups were compared using bivariate analyses, including t-tests for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for categorical variables. In order to reduce the chance of type 1 errors due to multiple comparisons, p-values of less than 0.01 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analysis was performed using Stata/MP (version 17.0; StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA).

Ethical Considerations

Patients provided informed consent to take part in the survey using a checkbox. Data were collected in such a way that patients and physicians could not be identified directly. The PNH DSP was submitted to the Pearl Institutional Review Board (study protocol number no. 21-ADRW-127/AG9119), and ethical approval was granted. In the absence of specific Chinese regulations, data collection was undertaken in line with European Pharmaceutical Marketing Research Association guidelines [28] and each survey was performed in full accordance with relevant legislation at the time of data collection, including the US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act 1996 [29].

Patient Demographics

Physicians reported data on 113 patients with PNH, of whom 56 (49.6%) had LDH levels greater than 1.5 x ULN. Demographics of patients with PNH are shown in Table 2. The median (IQR) age of patients was 38.0 (30.0–45.0) years. The majority of patients were male (62.8%) and Han Chinese (98.2%). Patients with LDH ≥1.5 x ULN had a significantly lower BMI (21.6 [20.5–22.6] versus 23.4 [21.4–25.5] kg/m2, p<0.001) and were less likely to be in the higher weight categories (p<0.001). The majority of patients worked full-time or part-time, regardless of LDH level. Overall, 28.3% of patients were on long-term sick leave, retired or unemployed owing to their PNH.

Clinical Characteristics

Patients’ clinical characteristics are shown in Table 3. There was no difference between LDH groups in median (IQR) time since patients first noticed PNH symptoms (2.2 [1.0–3.0] years), time since diagnosis (1.8 [0.9–2.5] years) or time on current treatment (0.9 [0.4–1.4] years). The most commonly used tests as part of the diagnostic process were flow cytometry (83.2%), complete blood counts (82.3%) and LDH testing (77.9%). There was a significant difference in physician-perceived PNH severity (p<0.001), with 80.7% of patients with LDH<1.5 x ULN having mild disease, compared with 46.4% of those with LDH≥1.5 x ULN. Mean LDH levels for those <1.5 x ULN were 213.4 ± 95.3, compared with 532.8 ± 111.1 for those ≥1.5 x ULN (p<0.001), although exact LDH values were only available for a subset of patients. Reticulocyte counts, haemoglobin levels and PNH clone sizes were not significantly different between LDH groups. The median (IQR) number of comorbidities was 1.0 (0.0–2.0), and the most commonly recorded comorbidities were bone marrow disorders other than PNH, aplastic anaemia and hypoplastic anaemia in both patient groups.

Disease Management

The treatments patients were prescribed for their PNH at time of data collection are shown in Fig. 2. Overall, the most common treatments prescribed were anticoagulants (39.8%), prednisone (31.0%) and cyclosporine (27.4%), with no significant differences between LDH groups. Significantly more patients with LDH ≥ 1.5 x ULN received rivaroxaban (37.1% compared with 8.8%, p = 0.002) and vitamin B12 supplements (28.6% compared with 8.8%, p = 0.008).

Clinical Outcomes

Overall, 39.3% of patients recorded a haemolytic crisis in the 12 months prior to data collection (Fig. 3a), with no significant difference between LDH groups. A mean number of 1.3 ± 0.4 crises was recorded in the 12 months prior to data collection by those who reported a crisis (Fig. 3b), with no significant difference between groups. A thrombotic event was recorded for 36.6% of the overall patient population (Fig. 3c), but significantly more patients with LDH ≥1.5 x ULN experienced a thrombotic event than those with LDH <1.5 x ULN (50.0% compared with 23.2%, p=0.006). In those who had a thrombotic event, the mean number of events was 1.1 ± 0.3, with no significant between-group differences.

Healthcare Resource Utilisation

Healthcare resource utilisation of patients with PNH is reported in Table 4. In the overall cohort, the median (IQR) number of hospitalisations in the 12 months prior to data collection was 1.0 (0.0–2.0), with no significant effect of LDH level. The most commonly reported reason for the most recent hospitalisation across the cohort was severe fatigue (45.7%), followed by infection (37.1%). In the majority of cases, the most recent hospitalisation was not an emergency admission but did involve an overnight stay. The median (IQR) number of nights in hospital for the most recent hospitalisation was 6.0 (5.0–8.0) with no significant between-group differences. In all cases, the most recent hospital stay did not involve time spent in an intensive care unit. The median (IQR) number of PNH-related blood transfusions in the 12 months prior to survey was 1.0 (0.0–2.0), with the majority of patients receiving either no transfusions (49.5%) or one to five transfusions (43.9%).

Patient Quality of Life

Patient reported outcome measures on fatigue and quality of life are reported in Table 5. The median (IQR) FACIT-Fatigue score recorded by the overall patient cohort was 29.0 (24.0–36.0), with no significant differences between patient groups. Overall quality of life was not significantly different between patient groups, with an overall median (IQR) EQ-5D 5L score of 0.89 (0.74–0.95) and an EQ-5D VAS score of 75.0 (60.0–80.0). Patients missed 10.8% (0.0–28.6%) of work time owing to their PNH, as recorded in the WPAI, with no significant group differences. Impairment at work was 45.0% (20.0–70.0%), leading to an overall work impairment of 53.6% (30.0–75.0%), with no significant between-group differences. Overall activity impairment, including those who did not work, was 50.0% (30.0–70.0%).

Discussion

This retrospective and cross-sectional study provides crucial insights into the real-world clinical characteristics and outcomes of patients with PNH in China before the introduction of complement inhibitor therapies.

Results from this study align with existing research indicating that higher LDH levels are associated with adverse outcomes, with our findings demonstrating that patients with LDH levels ≥1.5 x ULN exhibited nearly a twofold higher likelihood of thrombotic events and greater physician-perceived disease severity compared with those with LDH levels <1.5 x ULN. Interestingly, both patient groups experienced thrombotic event rates higher than those reported in the large International PNH Registry observational study, encompassing 1610 patients, primarily from Europe and North America [4]. This is in contrast with an earlier comparison of Asian and non-Asian patients, which found lower rates of thrombosis in Asian patients [30]. However, differences between definitions of thrombosis and the window for analysis make direct comparisons of thrombosis rates between studies methodologically challenging.

Terminal complement control is critical in preventing IVH and thrombosis in PNH. Our study, conducted prior to the availability of the complement inhibitor therapies in China, shows anticoagulants, corticosteroids and immunosuppressants were the most commonly prescribed treatments, regardless of LDH level. This is in line with a previous report on treatment use in China [31] and with use in the USA [32], despite these treatments being partially supplanted by the use of complement-targeted biologics in the latter market. Patients with LDH levels ≥1.5 x ULN more frequently received rivaroxaban and vitamin B12 supplementation than those with LDH levels <1.5 x ULN, highlighting more severe disease states in patients with elevated LDH.

More than one-third of our patient sample (39.3%) experienced a haemolytic crisis in the 12 months prior to the survey. The chronic complement-mediated haemolysis characteristic of PNH can lead to abdominal pain, pulmonary hypertension, chest pain, dyspnoea, fatigue, anaemia and progressive morbidity [33–35]. Although patients may not manifest severe symptoms initially, PNH-related complications tend to worsen over time, resulting in increased morbidity and mortality [7, 11].

In our patient population, fatigue, anaemia and haemoglobinuria were commonly reported symptoms at diagnosis, with fatigue and anaemia persisting in 92.9% and 74.3% of patients, respectively. Recent data from a Korean PNH registry demonstrated a significantly higher risk of thrombosis in patients with elevated LDH (≥1.5 ULN) at diagnosis [35]. In addition, the combination of elevated LDH with clinical symptoms of abdominal pain, chest pain, dyspnoea or haemoglobinuria was associated with an even greater risk of thrombosis than elevated LDH or clinical symptoms alone [9].

Fatigue is a commonly reported PNH-associated symptom and has been reported to be disabling to patients [4, 36]. Clinical studies have emphasised that fatigue in patients with PNH stems from intravascular haemolysis and is independent of anaemia severity. Blood transfusions, although necessary to manage anaemia related to bone marrow dysfunction or haemolysis, do not prevent chronic haemolysis [37, 38].

In our cohort, half of the patients required at least one blood transfusion, and the mean FACIT-Fatigue score was notably worse than that reported in a cross-sectional survey in adult patients with PNH treated with C5 inhibitors in Europe [39]. Although FACIT-Fatigue population norms are not available for China, mean scores recorded in our cohort are over 14 points lower than population norms recorded for Germany [40]. Given the clinically important difference in the FACIT-Fatigue scale in PNH has been estimated at 5 points [41], this further highlights the need to improve the disease management of patients with PNH in China.

Our study also sheds light on the substantial impact of PNH on work impairment, with patients experiencing significant overall work impairment and missing almost 20% of work time because of their PNH. In our study, we found that over two-thirds of our patient cohort underwent at least one hospitalization in the 12 months leading up to data collection, the majority of which involved stays spanning multiple days. These results highlight the high economic burden associated with PNH in the Chinese healthcare landscape.

This study has some limitations, including self-reported data by physicians and patients; however, physicians had the possibility to extract the data from the medical records, minimizing recall bias. In addition, potential bias in patient selection was mitigated by instructing physicians to provide data for a consecutive series of eligible patients. Physicians used their own clinical judgement to define a thrombotic event or haemolytic crisis, rather than use a study-specific provided definition. Although this reflects everyday clinical practice, this may complicate interpretation and comparisons with other studies, which may have used more rigid definitions. Although significant differences were found between patients on the basis of LDH level, it is possible that other factors, such as age and treatment selection, also affect patient outcomes, and may confound the results in this study.

Conclusions

Our study highlights the current supportive and non-targeted management of PNH in China, focusing on mitigating anaemia and managing haemolytic crises rather than addressing the underlying chronic complement-mediated haemolysis and its associated morbidities. A paradigm shift in PNH management in China, incorporating C5 inhibitor targeted therapy, is imperative to alleviate the disease-related burdens and achieve sustained complement control, preventing breakthrough intravascular haemolysis, thrombosis and organ damage.
