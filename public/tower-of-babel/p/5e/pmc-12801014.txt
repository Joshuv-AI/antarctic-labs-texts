Modified Mason-Allen Suture Bridge with Double-Locking Loop Technique for Rotator Cuff Repair
Authors: Yijun Zhang, Chengjie Yuan, Xiangyu Du, Jian Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractManaging rotator cuff (RTC) tears poses unique challenges for sports medicine surgeons. Although various suture techniques have been explored, many approaches fail due to several limitations. We describe a method of suture bridge technique that incorporates a double-locking loop for medial row fixation, utilizing a modified Mason-Allen (mMA) stitch in place of the conventional horizontal mattress configuration. This modification enhances tissue grip and improves the initial stability of tendon-to-bone fixation. This technique is particularly suited for patients with bursal-side partial-thickness tears or degenerative small- to medium-sized RTC injuries.

Achieving secure tendon-to-bone fixation is a crucial factor influencing rotator cuff (RTC) healing and surgical success.1 Various suture techniques have been developed to enhance fixation strength, including single- and double-row, as well as suture bridge (SB) methods. Recent advancements have introduced locking suture configurations: Double-lock stitch,2 simple cow hitch stitch,3 double-row rip-stop technique,4 double-layer lasso loop,5 and “Chinese knot” stitch.6 These innovations aim to improve biomechanical stability and optimize tendon repair outcomes.

For RTC tears accompanied by tendon degeneration or poor tissue quality, some studies suggest that locking loop sutures can enhance repair stability and reduce the risk of retear.7 However, the biomechanical properties of these sutures vary depending on the technique used.8 Despite their advantages, locking sutures often demand greater surgical expertise and extended operative times. Consequently, there is a growing need for simplified locking methods that minimize arthroscopic steps while maintaining reliability.

The SB has been widely adopted due to its ability to enhance biomechanical stability in RTC repairs. Advances in surgical instrumentation and technique have further refined its application, including the development of optimized anchor designs for both medial and lateral row fixation.9 For medial row fixation, the conventional horizontal mattress stitch—commonly used in standard SB constructs—may compromise repair integrity in the setting of poor tendon quality. Additionally, excessive tension from a horizontal mattress suture can lead to tendon strangulation and necrosis in the medial row. Alternative suture configurations have been explored to address these limitations.10,11 Among these, the modified Mason-Allen (mMA) stitch has demonstrated superior tissue-holding strength while minimizing the risk of strangulation compared to other techniques.11

To enhance biomechanical stability in RTC repair, we introduce a modified SB technique for medial row fixation, replacing the conventional horizontal mattress stitch with an mMA stitch with a double-locking loop technique. This approach offers improved tissue-holding capacity while mitigating the risk of medial-row tendon strangulation. The technique is arthroscopically feasible, utilizes two suture anchors, and provides increased fixation strength to the reattached tendon. It is particularly suitable for patients with bursal-side partial-thickness tears or degenerative small-to-medium-sized RTC injuries (Video 1).

Surgical Technique

This mMA stitch with a double-locking loop has a short learning curve for an experienced arthroscopic surgeon. It needs only 2 anchors and conventional instrumentation for robust tendon reattachment.

Patient Positioning

The procedure is performed under combined brachial plexus block and general anesthesia. Patient positioning follows surgeon preference; in this study, the patient is placed in the lateral decubitus position on a standard operating table with pelvic stabilization. All bony prominences are padded with sponge cushions to prevent pressure injuries, and the nonoperative arm is positioned at 90° of flexion. The knees are maintained in slight flexion with neutral head alignment. Shoulder range of motion and capsular adhesions are assessed under anesthesia. The operative arm is secured in a foam traction sleeve with 3 kg of longitudinal traction applied via a standard traction apparatus. The torso is reclined 30° posteriorly, positioning the operative arm at 70° of abduction and 20° of flexion. Bony landmarks of the shoulder joint are subsequently marked.

Arthroscopic Portal Placement

Four standard portals are used for the SB repair: posterior and posterolateral portals served as diagnostic portals for the 30° 4.0-mm arthroscope (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI), while anterior and anterolateral portals functioned as working portals for instrumentation (Fig 1). The posterior portal incision is made 2 cm inferior and 1 cm medial to the posterolateral acromion corner. Following spinal needle guidance, the anterior portal is established at the midpoint between the coracoid process and the anterior acromion corner, and the anterolateral portal is 1 cm posterior and 2 cm lateral to the anterolateral acromion corner. The posterolateral portal is established under direct arthroscopic visualization. A dedicated lateral portal is made for anchor placement (Fig 1).

Evaluation of the Glenohumeral Joint

The glenohumeral joint is initially inspected via the posterior portal. Then, an anterior portal is made if the long head of the biceps is torn or inflamed. The decision to repair or cut off the long head of the biceps depends on the patient’s age and the clinical examination results. If capsular adhesion is detected during the inspection, it must be released. Concurrently, any other pathological abnormalities within the joint should be addressed.

Subacromial Decompression and RTC Repair Preparation

The subacromial space is visualized through the posterior portal, with the posterolateral portal established for instrumentation. An arthroscopic shaver (Stryker) is used to debride the inflamed subacromial bursa, while a radio frequency ablator (DePuy Synthes, Warsaw, IN) is used to control intraoperative bleeding. Visualization is then directed toward the acromion, and acromioplasty is performed as clinically indicated.

Through the posterolateral portal, the anterior acromial angle is assessed to evaluate RTC tear morphology, size, and tissue quality. The tear is released to determine the feasibility of tension-free reduction, followed by preparation of the tendon footprint using an arthroscopic shaver to expose bleeding cancellous bone at the greater tuberosity. Debridement continued until an adequate bleeding bone surface is achieved to optimize biological healing (Fig 2).

mMA Stitch with Double-Locking Technique for RTC Repair (with Video Illustration)

The medial row fixation sequence is performed as follows: The posterior or posterolateral portal serves as the viewing portal, while the anterior and anterolateral portals function as working portals (step-by-step cannula placement video available). A dedicated lateral portal is established for anchor insertion. Drilling is performed at a 45° angle relative to the humeral head, followed by implantation of a 2.5-mm all-suture anchor (JoinFix Plus, Rejoin, China) into the medial row (Fig 3). Using a suture hook (DePuy Synthes), a PDS shuttle suture is passed through a white limb through the RTC tear from subacromial to articular side at the medial edge. The midportion of this limb is then retrieved to the bursal side, forming a loop (Fig 4). Two additional white limb passages are executed: one 10 mm anterior and another one 10 mm posterior to the initial point, establishing an isosceles triangular configuration (Fig 5). The free ends of both white limbs are threaded through the loop and tensioned to create double-locking loops, securing the mMA stitch (Fig 6). Subsequently, 2 green suture limbs are passed through the tear at positions 10 mm medial to the white limb sites (anterior and posterior) (Fig 7). All four limbs are secured using a knotless anchor (Delta, Shanghai, China) implanted into the lateral row of the greater tuberosity (Fig 8).

Postoperative Rehabilitation

Following surgical completion, the arm is immobilized in a sling for 6 weeks postoperatively, with the shoulder maintained in neutral rotation and slight abduction, while the forearm remains adducted to the side. This position minimizes tension on the repaired RTC. Passive range-of-motion exercises and closed kinetic chain activities commences at 2 weeks postoperatively. The rehabilitation protocol progresses to include both passive- and active-assisted motion during the 6-week immobilization period. Resistive strengthening exercises are initiated at 12 weeks postoperatively.

Discussion

The increasing prevalence of RTC tears in patients with joint degeneration and osteoporosis presents significant clinical challenges. Nonsurgical management is associated with progressive fatty infiltration and reduced acromiohumeral distance, leading to functional impairment and pain exacerbation.12 While advances in arthroscopic technology have yielded multiple suture techniques, arthroscopic RTC repair remains limited by relatively high revision rates.13

To address this, surgical innovations have focused on anatomical footprint restoration. This principle drove the adoption of double-row repair, which expands the tendon-bone contact area at the humeral head footprint.14 Recently, the SB repair technique has gained prominence.15 Some studies have shown that the SB repair has better biomechanical properties compared to double-row repair.16 Moreover, it can reconstruct the RTC footprint more effectively than double-row repair.16

Multiple biomechanical refinements have been developed to optimize tissue fixation in SB constructs. Toussaint and colleagues devised the “modified lasso-loop stitch” for medial row repair.9 Nonetheless, this technique is restricted to small tears due to premature failure at the weakest tendon–-suture interface point. Biomechanical evaluation demonstrates that the lasso–loop suture exhibits significantly lower ultimate failure strength compared to both the mMA stitch and conventional horizontal mattress suture in standard SB configurations.11

This article describes a SB approach using the double-locking loop technique, which replaces the mMA stitch for medial row fixation. In this technique, the double-locking loop is passed through the bursal side, and the three points form an isosceles triangle. It has several advantages, including simplicity in operation and reliability. Locking sutures have been reported to enhance the repair success rate of torn RTCs, especially when the RTC shows no obvious degeneration or poor quality.5,17,18 Nevertheless, the reported locking stitch techniques often increase the duration and complexity of the procedure, which restricts their practical application. Our double-locking loop technique can be carried out with conventional shoulder suture tools. The technique demonstrates technical simplicity with minimal proficiency requirements, making it accessible to surgeons across all experience levels. Its reduced technical demand is particularly advantageous for surgeons developing arthroscopic knot-tying proficiency. In this technique, the double-locking loop stitches are used to lock the RTC fibers obliquely. Since it is a knotless method, the surface of the RTC remains smooth, thereby reducing the stitch stimulating response. Intriguingly, when the MMA stitch is combined with the double-locking technique, a SB is formed on the bursal side. The evenly distributed pressure on the RTC and the enlarged tendon-bone contact area are likely to facilitate the healing of the RTC. While biomechanical validation of this specific construct remains unstudied, the mMA SB technique has been clinically adopted for RTC repairs due to its capacity for biomechanically secure medial row fixation and optimal footprint coverage. To conclude, we have applied the mMA stitch in combination with the double-locking technique for small- to medium-sized RTC tears with degeneration or poor tissue quality. This surgical approach is straightforward and convenient, yielding satisfactory clinical outcomes. The technique is composed of double-locking loops, which offer enhanced tissue-gripping strength. This characteristic potentially reduces the risk of retear and contributes to more favorable clinical outcomes. Nonetheless, future studies should compare the biomechanical and clinical effects of this technique with those of the SB technique and other locking suture methods. This will help us gain a more comprehensive understanding of its performance and efficacy in treating RTC tears.
