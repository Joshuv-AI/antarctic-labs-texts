Neonatal Refractory Seizures and Hyperammonemia in a Neonate With ALDH7A1 Deficiency
Authors: Maryam Saeedi, Kayvan Mirnia, Sahar Sedighzadeh, Razieh Sangsari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPyridoxine‐dependent epilepsy (PDE) is a rare, autosomal recessive neurometabolic disorder characterized by intractable seizures responsive to pyridoxine. We present the case of an 11‐day‐old female neonate with a history of refractory multifocal seizures beginning on day three of life, accompanied by hepatomegaly, metabolic acidosis, elevated serum ammonia and lactate, and abnormal liver function tests. Despite multiple antiepileptic and metabolic treatments, seizures persisted, and the infant developed progressive metabolic disturbances. Genetic analysis revealed a homozygous pathogenic variant in the ALDH7A1 gene, confirming the diagnosis of PDE. The patient's condition deteriorated despite intensive management, culminating in cardiorespiratory arrest and death. This case underscores the importance of early recognition of PDE, especially in consanguineous populations, and highlights the critical need for prompt pyridoxine therapy to prevent fatal outcomes.

Introduction

Pyridoxine‐dependent epilepsy (PDE), also known as antiquitin deficiency, is a rare, inherited metabolic disorder characterized by mutations in the ALDH7A1 gene, characterized by seizures that are responsive to treatment. Mutations in ALDH7A1 lead to a deficiency or complete absence of the ALDH7A1 enzyme. The ALDH7A1 enzyme plays a crucial role in the metabolism of the amino acid lysine. Specifically, it catalyzes the final step in the conversion of lysine to saccharopine, a crucial step in lysine degradation. This enzyme deficiency causes a buildup of α‐aminoadipic semialdehyde (AASA), an intermediate in lysine metabolism. AASA is neurotoxic, and the accumulation of AASA is believed to be the primary cause of the seizures associated with PDE [1]. The exact mechanisms by which AASA causes seizures are not fully understood, but it is likely related to its effects on neurotransmitter systems, energy metabolism, and possibly oxidative stress within neurons. Diagnosis is based on clinical presentation (refractory seizures often responsive to pyridoxine), family history, and genetic testing to confirm the mutations in the ALDH7A1 gene. Measuring AASA levels in body fluids (e.g., urine) can support the diagnosis. The primary treatment for PDE is lifelong supplementation with high doses of pyridoxine [2]. With early diagnosis and treatment with pyridoxine, the prognosis for PDE is generally good, with many individuals achieving significant seizure control and normal or near‐normal development [3]. However, the severity of the disorder and the response to treatment can vary widely, and some individuals experience persistent seizures or developmental delays despite treatment [4]. We report a case of a 14‐day‐old neonate who presented with refractory seizures and hyperammonemia and ultimately died. Whole‐exome sequencing (WES) revealed a homozygous likely pathogenic variant in the ALDH7A1 gene (ALDH7A1:c.1434_1435del), confirming a diagnosis of PDE. This case highlights the need for early suspicion and prompt treatment in neonates with unexplained seizures and metabolic derangements.

Case History

An 11‐day‐old female infant, born at 38 weeks gestation to consanguineous parents with a birth weight of 2835 g, was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) at Children's Medical Center, Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran, with intractable seizures. The neonate was delivered vaginally with normal Apgar scores. The parents are first cousins. Seizures began on day three of life, presenting as multifocal clonic seizures refractory to treatment with phenobarbital and levetiracetam. The infant exhibited hepatomegaly, poor feeding, lethargy, irritability, and muscle rigidity. Laboratory investigations revealed elevated serum ammonia levels (328 μg/dL; normal < 190 μg/dL) and elevated serum lactate levels (60 mg/dL; normal < 20 mg/dL). Liver function tests were abnormal, showing prolonged prothrombin time (PT) (25.4 s; normal up to 15 s) and elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT) (80 IU/L; normal up to 40 IU/L). Serum amino acid levels were unremarkable. Initial metabolic screening, including acylcarnitine profile and organic acid analysis, was unremarkable. Treatment: The infant's seizures proved refractory to treatment with phenobarbital and levetiracetam. Due to this lack of seizure control, pyridoxine (100 mg), folinic acid (5 mg), and a midazolam drip (5 μg/kg/min) were administered. Differential diagnosis: Hyperammonemia and acidosis raised suspicion of an inborn error of metabolism. Outcome: Despite treatment with sodium benzoate and phenylbutyrate, elevated ammonia levels persisted, necessitating dialysis. The infant experienced cardiorespiratory arrest during dialysis and died. Postmortem WES revealed a homozygous pathogenic variant in the ALDH7A1 gene (NM_001182: exon16: c.1434_1435del: p.C478Wfs*13, rs770492145), consistent with pyridoxine‐dependent epilepsy. Parental testing confirmed the autosomal recessive inheritance pattern.

Discussion

PDE, caused by mutations in the ALDH7A1 gene, is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by seizures, often refractory to conventional treatment [5, 6]. ALDH7A1 is crucial in the catabolism of lysine. In neonates with ALDH7A1 deficiency, AASA, an intermediate in lysine metabolism, accumulates. The excess AASA is shunted into alternative metabolic pathways, leading to the production of toxic compounds that impair the urea cycle. This indirectly inhibits the body's ability to efficiently convert ammonia into urea for excretion. The urea cycle is the primary mechanism for removing ammonia from the bloodstream; its impairment leads to elevated ammonia levels. The severity of hyperammonemia can vary depending on the extent of the enzyme deficiency and the individual's metabolic capacity [7]. Additionally, the direct neurotoxic effects of AASA and its metabolites directly damage or disrupt neuronal function, leading to hyperexcitability and seizures [8]. The metabolic disturbances caused by ALDH7A1 deficiency can affect energy production within brain cells. Mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired glucose metabolism, or other energy deficits can leave neurons vulnerable to seizures. The altered neuronal environment caused by AASA accumulation could affect the function of GABA receptors [9]. These receptors are crucial for mediating the inhibitory effects of GABA. If receptor function is compromised, the inhibitory effect of GABA is weakened, leading to increased neuronal excitability and a greater risk of seizures [10]. The primary treatment for PDE is lifelong supplementation with high doses of pyridoxine [2]. With early diagnosis and treatment, the prognosis for PDE is generally good [3]. While pyridoxine is typically effective in treating PDE, a neonate might be refractory to a single 100 mg dose. Several factors could contribute: Severity of enzyme deficiency: The degree of ALDH7A1 enzyme deficiency varies. A more severe deficiency may require higher doses, up to 200 mg or more frequent pyridoxine administration, at least 3 to 5days before concluding that it is not effective [11]. Our patient did not respond to 100 mg of pyridoxine, perhaps due to a delay in starting the drug or the need for higher or longer pyridoxine treatment. This case illustrates the challenges in the rapid diagnosis of PDE in neonates. The initial metabolic screening was non‐diagnostic, highlighting the need for a high index of suspicion in cases of refractory neonatal seizures and hyperammonemia. A trial of pyridoxine is recommended in neonates with seizures unresponsive to second‐line anticonvulsant drugs [11]. While pyridoxine is the cornerstone of treatment, its efficacy can be limited depending on the severity of the underlying genetic defect and the delay in diagnosis. The present patient harbors a homozygous frameshift mutation in the ALDH7A1 gene (NM_001182.5: c.1434_1435del, p.Cys478Trpfs*13) located on exon 16 of 18 within ALDH protein domain. This mutation results in the production of a truncated protein.

Conclusion

This case underscores the importance of considering PDE in the differential diagnosis of neonates presenting with refractory seizures and hyperammonemia. Early diagnosis and treatment with pyridoxine are crucial for optimal outcomes, although the prognosis remains challenging in severe cases. Further research is needed to better understand the genotype–phenotype correlation.
