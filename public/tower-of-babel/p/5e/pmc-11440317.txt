Highly sensitive and miniaturized microcone-curved resonant photoacoustic cavity for trace gas detection
Authors: Zhongke Zhao, Wenjun Ni, Chunyong Yang, Sixiang Ran, Bingze He, Ruiming Wu, Ping Lu, Perry Ping Shum
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This paper proposes a novel microcone-curved resonant photoacoustic cell (MCR-PAC) for highly sensitive trace gas detection. The MCR-PAC features with microcone-curved resonant region and cylindrical buffer chamber, which dominates the photoacoustic signal amplification. By introducing the hyperbolic eccentricity as a new optimization dimension, the quality factor of the MCR-PAC is remarkably strengthened to enhance the acoustic pressure amplitude. At an eccentricity value of 5, the volume of the photoacoustic resonant cavity is approximately 0.23 cm3. Targeting trace acetylene, the system achieves a minimum detection limit of 1.41 ppb with an integration time of 290 s, corresponding normalized noise equivalent absorption coefficient is 1.88×10−9 W·cm−1·Hz−1/2. Compared to the traditional T-type PAC, the overall performance of MCR-PAC has been enhanced nearly fourfold. With its compact millimeter-scale dimensions and high sensitivity, the MCR-PAC demonstrates extensive potential for application in environmental monitoring and breath diagnostics.

Introduction

Among the various gas detection technologies, optical gas sensing technology offers advantages such as high sensitivity, rapid response, and non-destructive detection. It finds wide application in areas like breath analysis, toxic gas leak detection, environmental air monitoring, and energy exploration [1], [2], [3], [4], [5]. Within optical gas sensing, laser spectroscopy technology can be classified into tunable diode laser absorption spectroscopy (TDLAS) [6], Raman spectroscopy (RS) [7], and photoacoustic [8], [9]/photothermal [10] spectroscopy (PAS/PTS). These techniques distinguish between different gases by analyzing molecular fingerprints present in the absorption spectrum of the target gas. In particular, photoacoustic spectroscopy (PAS) technology employs modulated light to induce the heating of gas molecules, leading to localized expansion, contraction, and the generation of acoustic waves within a photoacoustic cell (PAC). These waves are subsequently detected using a microphone [11], [12], [13], [14]. This detection method is independent of the optical path length and does not necessitate the use of costly optical detectors [14], [15], contributing to the downsizing of PAS detection systems and reducing setup expenses. These characteristics make PAS technology appealing for crafting miniature gas sensors suitable for space-constrained environments and remote sensing applications [16].

The performance of the photoacoustic cell (PAC) and microphone significantly influences the detection sensitivity of a PAS system. In recent years, the PAC has garnered attention as a pivotal component of PAS detection systems. Depending on the operational mode, the PAC can be categorized into resonant and non-resonant types. Among these, the resonant photoacoustic cell, renowned for its high sensitivity and large acoustic pressure amplitude, is extensively utilized in trace gas detection. Resonant photoacoustic cells are predominantly classified into H-type and T-type cells. The H-type resonant photoacoustic cell (HR-PAC) typically comprises two buffer chambers and a resonant cavity. Due to its straightforward processing and favorable symmetry, it is commonly employed in trace gas detection. In 2020, Li et al. introduced hyperbolic curves into the resonant cavity to optimize the HR-PAC's performance, thereby enhancing the cavity's quality factor [17]. In 2023, Luo et al. amalgamated a Helmholtz resonant cavity with an HR-PAC, diminishing cavity noise and enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio [18]. Additionally, in the same year, Lu et al. proposed an HR-PAC with three resonant cavities, facilitating the simultaneous detection of multiple gases [19]. Nonetheless, structural constraints result in the larger volume of the HR-PAC, leading to prolonged gas diffusion times and slower response rates in gas detection applications. Conversely, the T-type resonant photoacoustic cell (TR-PAC), predominantly comprising a buffer chamber and a resonant cavity, offers the advantage of a smaller volume and enhanced performance. In 2019, Gong et al. systematically investigated the semi-open TR-PAC, achieving a minimum detection limit of 0.7 ppb for trace C2H2 [20]. The TR-PAC necessitates robust photoacoustic signals and larger cavity constants. In 2022, Xiao et al. proposed a highly sensitive all-optical differential PAS sensor, incorporating two optical fiber microphones and a dual-channel differential TR-PAC, achieving a detection limit of 4.87 ppb for CH4 [21]. Consequently, the TR-PAC is better suited for compact, highly sensitive sensing units.

As the transition from early use of larger capacitor microphones [22] to smaller quartz tuning forks occurred [23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30], followed by the adoption of cantilever Fabry-Perot (F-P) cavities [31], [32], [33], [34], [35] and thin-film F-P cavity optical fiber acoustic sensors [36], [37], research has begun on combining optical fiber acoustic sensors or MEMS microphones with compact TR-PAC micro-PAS gas sensors. For instance, in 2023, Ma et al. cleverly merged a miniature one-dimensional axial resonant PAC with a quartz tuning fork, employing secondary amplification for the excitation and collection of acoustic signals, enhancing the sensitivity of the entire system and achieving a detection limit of 14.93 ppm for methane gas [38]. In 2024, Gong et al. designed a miniature cylindrical-spherical resonant PAC, integrating a cantilever beam optical microphone with a special demodulation algorithm, which not only reduced the size of the sensing unit but also improved the detection limit of the system, achieving a detection limit of 101 ppb for methane [39]. These works leverage the resonant amplification effect of the PAC while enhancing the sensitivity of the microphone. Through dual-stage amplification, the detection limit is raised to a higher level. PAS systems consistently maintain a balance between miniaturization and sensitivity. Although optical microphones have improved the detection limit of the system, they have also significantly increased system costs, raised the system's demodulation complexity, made the system more intricate, and decreased system stability.

This work introduces a microcone-curve resonant photoacoustic cell (MCR-PAC) in which a distributed feedback laser (DFB) serves as the excitation source. The laser interacts with gas molecules within the PAC, stimulating acoustic signals that are subsequently amplified within the resonant cavity. Sensitive detection of these signals is achieved using an electrical microphone, with gas concentration information carried by the secondary harmonic signal demodulated through a lock-in amplifier (LIA). This design realizes a compact and efficient MCR-PAC, establishing a straightforward and cost-effective photoacoustic spectroscopy (PAS) system for trace gas detection. The MCR-PAC incorporates a hyperbolic eccentricity on the inner surface of the resonant cavity to optimize PAC performance. Experimental results show a minimum detection limit (MDL) of 1.41ppb for acetylene (C₂H₂) gas with an integration time (IT) of 290 s. Additionally, the system achieves a normalized noise equivalent absorption (NNEA) coefficient of 1.88×10−9 W·cm−1·Hz−1/2, indicating high sensitivity and low noise levels in the system. By employing numerical simulation and experimental validation, the efficacy of MCR-PAC is assessed relative to the conventional T-type PAC. MCR-PAC exhibited superior sensitivity and a higher quality factor, leading to an almost fourfold enhancement in the minimum detection threshold. This advancement sets a new trajectory for miniature, simplified, affordable, robust, and highly sensitive PAS trace gas detection technology.

MCR-PAC structure and simulation analysis

Fig. 1(a) delineates a schematic diagram illustrating the operational principle of the MCR-PAC. It comprises a microcone-curved resonant cavity, a cylindrical buffer cavity, a fiber optic collimator, an air inlet, and an air outlet. In this configuration, a distributed feedback (DFB) laser functions as the pump laser accessing the PAC via the fiber optic collimator, inducing periodic thermal expansion and collision of the analyzed gas with the PAC walls. This interaction produces a sound pressure signal. When the DFB modulation frequency aligns with the PAC's resonant frequency, the acoustic signal undergoes amplification due to resonance effects. What sets the MCR-PAC apart from conventional T-type cylindrical resonant PACs is the conversion of the cylindrical resonator into a three-dimensional rotating conical resonator formed by the hyperbolic curve's eccentricity.Fig. 1(a) The 3D schematic diagram of the working principle of the MCR-PAC; (b) Axisymmetric 2D of photoacoustic cell.Fig. 1

The propagation and resonance amplification of the photoacoustic signal within the PAC are primarily governed by the wave equation. Given that thermal and viscosity losses significantly impact the actual signal propagation, this study employs the thermal viscosity acoustic interface of COMSOL Multiphysics 5.6, a multi-physics simulation software, to model the experimental conditions accurately. This study systematically refines the resonant cavity's structural parameters using a scanning approach, highlighting the pivotal role of eccentricity (e) in MCR-PAC performance. Initial scans ranging from 2 to 7 in eccentricity, with a constant cavity length (L) of 15 mm and end radius (a) of 1.0 mm, identified e = 4 as maximizing acoustic pressure amplitude, as shown in Fig. 2(a). Fine-tuning from 3.6 to 5.0 in increments of 0.2 showed a slight performance decrease as eccentricity increased from 4 to 5. Balancing practical manufacturing with device miniaturization and performance, an eccentricity of 5.0 is selected. The optimal acoustic resonant modal analysis, as depicted in Table 1 and simulated through parameter modeling, illustrates the distribution of the initial four acoustic modes along with their frequency responses curve, visually represented in Fig. 3. In MCR-PAC, the irregular microcone-curved structure results in non-doubling of the 2nd resonant frequency compared to the 1st, while the 3rd is double the 1st, and the 4th is double the 2nd, as depicted in Fig. 3(a)(b)(c)(d). Thus, adjacent odd and even-order resonance frequencies are in a multiple relationship in MCR-PAC [16]. For efficient sound pressure signal measurements, it is imperative for the sound pressure to be predominantly concentrated at the end of the resonant cavity, with the peak value centrally located in that region. Minimizing the sound pressure signal within the end section of the cylindrical buffer cavity is crucial for attenuating the impact of gas inlet and outlet on the MCR-PAC. Among the presented figures, only Fig. 3(a) and (b) adhere to these stipulations, with the 1st and 2nd resonant modes meeting the specified criteria. Particularly noteworthy is the first resonant mode depicted in Fig. 3(e), which exhibits the highest amplitude of excited sound pressure at the end of the resonant cavity, the lowest amplitude at the end of the cylindrical buffer cavity, and the lowest resonant frequency. These characteristics render it especially advantageous for practical engineering applications. Consequently, this study chooses to utilize the 1st resonant mode with a resonant frequency of f = 7730 Hz. Placing a microphone at the end of the resonant cavity facilitates the detection of the peak acoustic pressure signal. Experimental setup

In this research, acetylene (C2H2) is chosen as the analyte to demonstrate the capabilities of MCR-PAC performance. In order to further validate the advantages of introducing hyperbolic eccentricity into the resonant cavity of MCR-PAC, a traditional T-type PAC is also designed and fabricated in this study. The dimensions of the T-type PAC are consistent with the simulated dimensions, and apart from not introducing eccentricity, the other structural parameters are the same as those in Table 1. The experimental setup depicted in Fig. 5 is meticulously refined to enhance results, with a particular focus on elevating the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) through the utilization of wavelength modulation spectroscopy (WMS) and the implementation of second harmonic detection techniques. A near-infrared continuous wave distributed feedback (DFB) fiber-coupled diode laser, operating at 1532.83 nm, is employed as the pump light source to excite the absorption peak of acetylene molecules at the corresponding wavelength, as outlined in the HITRAN database [40]. The laser's wavelength is actively regulated by adjusting temperature and current parameters using an integrated laser driver system (Optics Valley Interconnect: ORLD-1500).Fig. 5Schematic diagram of photoacoustic spectrum gas detection system based on the MCP-RAC and electrical microphone.Fig. 5

Schematic diagram of photoacoustic spectrum gas detection system based on the MCP-RAC and electrical microphone.

Initially, a low-frequency sawtooth wave and a high-frequency sinusoidal wave signal are introduced to the laser driver of the pump light source via a signal generator (Siglent: SDG2122X) to enable periodic modulation. This configuration induces a symmetric periodic scanning of the pump light. Simultaneously, the high-frequency sinusoidal wave serves as the reference signal and is directed to the lock-in amplifier (LIA) (National Instrument Quantum: OE-1022). The pump light passes through a fiber optic isolator to eliminate system interference arising from laser fluctuations due to Rayleigh backscattering, after which it is further amplified in power by an erbium-doped fiber amplifier (EDFA) (Accelink: EDFA-BA-22). The EDFA not only amplifies the laser power at 1532.83 nm but also boosts the output power of the entire C-band. Subsequently, the pump light is transmitted through a narrow-band tunable filter (TF) with a center wavelength of 1532 nm (±1.5 nm) to prevent the simultaneous excitation of multiple absorption peaks of C2H2 molecules. By precisely fixing the pump light on the absorption peak of C2H2 molecules, a cyclical expansion and contraction of the gas volume occur through periodic modulation with a low-frequency sawtooth wave, driven by the thermal relaxation process. As a result, collisions with the walls of the PAC generate acoustic pressure signals that also carry high-frequency sinusoidal waves. The excited acoustic pressure signals are then intensified by the resonance cavity. An electrical microphone (BSWA TECH, MPA201) is strategically positioned to capture the sound wave signals at the resonance cavity's terminus. These detected sound signals are fed into the LIA, where the high-frequency sinusoidal wave is combined with the reference sinusoidal wave to demodulate the low-frequency sawtooth wave signal. By setting the modulation frequency of the sinusoidal wave to half the resonance frequency of the PAC, the LIA operates in the second harmonic (2 f) mode to accurately decipher the alternating current (AC) component of the output signal.

This approach enables the precise calculation of the second harmonic signal with reduced environmental interference, facilitating the extraction of concentration data related to C2H2 gas. All experimental procedures are conducted under ambient atmospheric pressure and standard room temperature conditions. Data acquisition and processing are carried out using a personal computer (PC) for thorough analysis and interpretation.

Experimental results

Performance evaluation of the MCR-PAC

In a temperature-controlled laboratory environment, a 7505 ppm acetylene (C2H2) mixture gas with pure nitrogen (N2) as the carrier gas is introduced into a sealed chamber, freely diffusing into the MCR-PAC and T-type PAC. The center wavelength of the pump laser is fixed at the absorption line peak of C2H2, and the two PACs are set in the same modulation depth of the PAS system. By varying the modulation frequency of the sinusoidal signal and recording the corresponding peak signals of the second harmonic, frequency response curves of the two PACs are plotted, as shown in Fig. 6(a). The frequency response curves of the two PACs effectively adhere to the Lorentzian distribution. When the modulation frequency of the sinusoidal signal is 4095 Hz, the T-type PAC obtains the maximum second harmonic signal peak, indicating a resonant frequency of 8190 Hz. Similarly, when the modulation frequency of the sinusoidal signal is 4617 Hz, the MCR-PAC achieves the maximum second harmonic signal peak, corresponding to a resonant frequency of 9234 Hz. Analysis of Fig. 6(a) reveals that compared to the T-type PAC, the MCR-PAC exhibits a higher resonant frequency, approximately 1.5 times greater acoustic pressure response amplitude, and a quality factor Q approximately 2.5 times higher.Fig. 6Performance evaluation of MCR-PAC and T-type PAC: (a) Frequency response curves of the two PACs at the same modulation depth; (b) Second harmonic signal of 0.101 ppm C2H2 gas at resonance frequency for the two PACs; (c) Second harmonic signal response of MCR-PAC to C2H2 gas at different concentrations; (d) Linear fitting of the peak of the second harmonic signals response of the two PACs to C2H2 gas at different concentrations.Fig. 6

Performance evaluation of MCR-PAC and T-type PAC: (a) Frequency response curves of the two PACs at the same modulation depth; (b) Second harmonic signal of 0.101 ppm C2H2 gas at resonance frequency for the two PACs; (c) Second harmonic signal response of MCR-PAC to C2H2 gas at different concentrations; (d) Linear fitting of the peak of the second harmonic signals response of the two PACs to C2H2 gas at different concentrations.

The empirically obtained Q value of two PACs, manufactured with the same machining precision (±0.1 mm), deviates significantly from the theoretical optimal value. In simulations, the MCR-PAC demonstrates an approximately 2.7-fold increase in Q value compared to the T-type PAC, while experimentally, this enhancement is around 2.5 times. The discrepancy arises from the heightened machining precision demand for the microcone-curved region of the MCR-PAC in contrast to the conventional T-type PAC. Consequently, with identical machining precision, the performance decline of the MCR-PAC is more notable, resulting in a marginally lower experimental Q value improvement than the simulated outcome. Besides errors arising from non-fixed spatial couplings between the PAC, microphone, and collimator. Thus, in subsequent experiments, the modulation frequencies of the pump laser on the T-type PAC and MCR-PAC are set at 4095 Hz and 4617 Hz, respectively.

To assess the performance of MCR-PAC and T-type PAC gas detection systems, N2 gas mixed with varying concentrations of C2H2 is introduced into the PAC through free diffusion. Standardized gas mixtures of C2H2 at concentrations of 100.4 ppm, 9.51 ppm, 1.00 ppm, and 0.101 ppm are custom-made for this study. Each C2H2 concentration underwent an hour of free diffusion before detection, with a 30-minute purification stage using pure N2 before each gas change to prevent cross-contamination between different concentrations and ensure the standardization of each gas concentration. By scanning the laser wavelength from 1532.70 nm to 1532.96 nm, detection of the second harmonic signal of 0.101 ppm C2H2 is achieved. As experimental results in Fig. 6(b) clearly show that the second harmonic peak of MCR-PAC is higher, with lower noise levels, resulting in a higher signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). This demonstrates the advantages of the micro-cone resonator cavity. Fig. 6(c) presents the second harmonic signals of various C2H2 concentrations detected by MCR-PAC, showing a significant consistency in signal distribution. The peak values of the second harmonic signals detected by the two PAC sensors are extracted, and high linearity is demonstrated through linear fitting, as shown in Fig. 6(d). The R2 values are all greater than 0.999, indicating sensitivities of 0.881 μV/ppm and 0.639 μV/ppm for C2H2 gas detection by the two PACs, respectively. It can be concluded that MCR-PAC exhibits higher sensitivity to C2H2 gas detection compared to T-type PAC, showcasing superior sensitivity and linearity of MCR-PAC.

Analysis of experimental results

The simulation comparison in Fig. 4 and the experimental comparison in Fig. 6 highlight the performance advantages of MCR-PAC, characterized by high quality factor, significantly higher sound pressure amplitude, and lower noise interference. These attributes play a critical role in enhancing the detection threshold within the PAS gas detection system. To evaluate the background noise of the system, pure N2 is introduced into both PACs without changing any other conditions. The 2 f signal generated by the system is continuously monitored for over 8000 s, resulting in standard deviations (1σ) of 29.3 nV and 19.9 nV for T-type PAC and MCR-PAC respectively, as shown in Fig. 7(a) and (c). The detection sensitivities of the two PACs to C2H2 gas are 0.639 μV/ppm and 0.881 μV/ppm respectively, as shown in Fig. 6(d). The detection limit of the T-type PAC system for C2H2 gas is 45.79 ppb, while the detection limit of the MCR-PAC system is 22.58 ppb. According to the experimental results, the detection limit of the MCR-PAC is twice that of the T-type PAC.Fig. 7Noise analysis of the T-type PAC and the MCR-PAC trace gas detection system: (a) The T-type PAC’s background noise of the system with pure N2; (b) The calculated result of the Allan deviation of the T-type PAC’s noise; (c) The MCR-PAC’s background noise of the system with pure N2; (b) The calculated result of the Allan deviation of the MCR-PAC’s noise.Fig. 7

Allan deviation analysis is performed to evaluate the detection limit and long-term stability of the sensor, as shown in Fig. 7(b) and (d), revealing that the minimum detection limit (MDL) of the T-type PAC system at an integration time (IT) of 298 s is 5.52 ppb, whereas the MDL of the MCR-PAC system at IT of 290 s is 1.41 ppb. Based on theoretical limitations, the MDL of the MCR-PAC is nearly four times that of the T-type PAC. Under the same experimental configuration of the two PACs, with EDFA output power, absorption wavelength, and temperature set at 150 mW, 1532.83 nm, and 23°C respectively, the normalized noise equivalent absorption (NNEA) coefficient of the T-type PAC is calculated to be 7.38×10 −9W·cm−1·Hz−1/2, whereas the NNEA value of the MCR-PAC is determined to be 1.88×10 −9W·cm−1·Hz−1/2. This comprehensive analysis highlights the higher sensitivity, lower detection limit, and effective operation at low noise levels of the MCR-PAC system compared to the T-type PAC.

Performance comparison and discussion

Despite some inherent limitations in MCR-PAC gas detection systems, such as susceptibility to external noise interference due to the lack of a fixed coupling mechanism between the microphone, PAC, and fiber optical collimator, as well as inadequate precision in PAC fabrication. While these challenges can be mitigated by adopting miniature thin-film microphones or fiber-optic acoustic sensors [41], it undoubtedly increases system complexity and costs.

Notwithstanding these limitations, the system demonstrates superior performance across diverse parameters, positioning the MCR-PAC as a new technology in the realm of miniature photoacoustic trace gas detection.

Conclusions

This study introduces a novel high performance microcone-curved resonant photoacoustic cell (MCR-PAC) for trace gas detection utilizing PAS. The MCP-PAC comprises a microcone-curved resonant cavity and a cylindrical buffer chamber. By incorporating a hyperbolic eccentricity e as a new optimization dimension on the inner wall of the resonant cavity, enhancements in acoustic pressure amplitude and quality factor of the MCR-PAC are achieved. Utilizing numerical simulation and experimental comparison, the performance of MCR-PAC is analyzed against traditional T-type PAC. MCR-PAC demonstrated higher sensitivity and a greater quality factor, resulting in a nearly fourfold improvement in the minimum detection limit. An electrical microphone is chosen for detecting the photoacoustic signal. With an eccentricity of e = 5, the volume of the resonant cavity stands at approximately 0.23 cm3. When targeting acetylene (C2H2) as the analyte gas, the MCP-PAC achieves a minimum detection limit is 1.41ppb, with an integration time of 290 s, and the NNEA coefficient is 1.88×10−9 W·cm−1·Hz−1/2. The MCR-PAC demonstrates superior performance over traditional resonant and non-resonant PAC systems, offering a new solution for high sensitivity miniaturized trace gas detection through miniaturization PAC. Moreover, by increasing the output power of the EDFA, integrating cutting-edge optical frequency comb sources, or leveraging fiber optical microphone technologies, the performance of the MCR-PAC in measuring gas molecule concentrations can be further enhanced.
