Cross-protective immunity elicited by heterologous DNA prime/subunit or adenovirus boost regimens against bovine viral diarrhea virus
Authors: Mingguo Xu, Xueyu Tao, Hengyun Gao, Hao Guo, Xiaoxiao Gu, Yuting Zhang, Jing Yuan, Honghuan Li, Yueli Wang, Jihai Yi, Jinliang Sheng, Ningning Yang, Huan Zhang, Chuangfu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bovine viral diarrhea (BVD) poses a substantial economic threat to the cattle industry, necessitating vaccines with broad cross-protective immunity. Heterologous prime-boost immunization represents a promising strategy to broaden immune protection by engaging complementary immunological pathways across vaccine platforms. Here, we evaluated heterologous prime-boost regimens combining recombinant subunit, adenovirus-vectored, and DNA vaccines, all targeting the E2 glycoproteins of BVDV-1 (I E2) and BVDV-2 (II E2; I E2-II E2), in a BALB/c mouse model. The DNA/subunit and DNA/adenovirus regimens elicited potent cellular and humoral immunity. Crucially, DNA vaccine priming preferentially skewed the immune response toward a Th1-biased phenotype, optimizing the overall immune profile. Our findings confirm that heterologous prime-boost strategies can synergistically enhance the cross-protective efficacy of BVD vaccines, providing a solid foundation for future development, pending further validation in cattle.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05438-3.

Background

Bovine viral diarrhea virus (BVDV), a member of the Pestivirus genus (Flaviviridae), is the causative agent of BVD, a globally distributed disease that primarily affects cattle but also infects a broad range of other species [1, 2]. This wide host range, coupled with the generation of persistently infected (PI) individuals that continuously shed the virus, underpins its significant economic impact on the livestock industry [3, 4]. PI animals lead to the contamination of biological products such as serum, embryos, and semen. The use of contaminated bovine serum in pharmaceutical cell culture systems constitutes a major biosecurity threat, potentially compromising the production of both veterinary and human vaccines [5]. Due to these far-reaching implications, BVD is classified as a Category C disease by the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) and regulated under China's Animal Epidemic Prevention Law [6].

BVDV is primarily represented by three genotypes of clinical importance: BVDV-1 (Pestivirus A), BVDV-2 (Pestivirus B), and BVDV-3 (Pestivirus H/HoBi-like) [7, 8]. These genotypes are further divided into numerous subtypes: BVDV-1 into at least 23 (1a–1w), BVDV-2 into four (2a–2d), and BVDV-3 into several identified in regions including Brazil, Thailand, and Italy [9, 10]. BVDV-1 is the most prevalent genotype globally, while BVDV-2 is associated with heightened pathogenicity [11, 12]. Based on cytopathic effects (CPE) in cell culture, BVDV strains are categorized as cytopathic (CP) or non-cytopathic (NCP) biotypes, both of which are pathogenic but differ in disease manifestation and severity.

Prime-boost immunization enhances immune responses by administering either the same (homologous) or different (heterologous) vaccine platforms at distinct intervals [13, 14]. Evolving from its empirical origins in the 1950s as "multi-dose immunization", the strategy has been refined through immunological insights [15]. Combining distinct platforms—such as inactivated with live-attenuated or subunit with viral vector vaccines—has proven particularly effective. Recent innovations, including nucleic acid and virus-like particle vaccines, have further expanded the toolkit for heterologous prime-boost regimens. This approach engages diverse immune pathways, yields more robust protection, and reduces the risk of immune tolerance associated with homologous boosting, thereby sustaining response magnitude [16]. The continuous refinement of these strategies has established prime-boost immunization as a cornerstone of global disease control [15, 17, 18].

Conventional inactivated and live attenuated vaccines for BVDV are limited by suboptimal immunogenicity, safety concerns, and, critically, their inability to differentiate infected from vaccinated animals, which severely hinders effective disease surveillance and eradication programs. This limitation underscores the pressing need for novel DIVA-compliant strategies. DIVA (Differentiating Infected from Vaccinated Animals) vaccines offer a core advantage by enabling serological distinction between natural infection and vaccine-induced immune responses [19]. Typical DIVA strategies include marker vaccines, subunit vaccines, and genetically engineered vaccines that delete or replace specific non-structural proteins, ensuring precise differentiation [20, 21]. These platforms are indispensable not only for enhancing the flexibility and safety of control campaigns but also for providing irrefutable evidence for disease-free status certification, a key requirement for international animal trade [22].

Therefore, this study aimed to establish a foundation for developing DIVA-compliant vaccines by evaluating candidate vaccine platforms that are inherently compatible with DIVA principles. We assessed the immunogenicity of four heterologous prime-boost regimens in BALB/c mice, using recombinant subunit, adenovirus-vectored, and DNA vaccines co-expressing the E2 glycoproteins from BVDV genotypes I and II (I E2 + II E2). Our findings provide valuable insights for future bovine trials and next-generation BVDV vaccine development.

Results

Heterologous prime-boost immunization enhances IFN-γ secretion

Heterologous prime-boost immunization significantly enhanced antigen-specific IFN-γ secretion. On days 28 and 42, the number of IFN-γ-secreting spots per 1 × 106 lymphocytes were significantly higher in all heterologous regimen groups compared to the PBS and commercial vaccine groups (p < 0.0001; Fig. 1A, B). Furthermore, IFN-γ secretion increased time-dependently, with the DNA/rAdV and rAdV/DNA groups showing a significant boost by day 42 (p < 0.05; Fig. 1C).

Heterologous regimens elicit robust and balanced antibody responses

All heterologous regimens elicited robust BVDV-specific IgG, IgG1, and IgG2a antibody responses (Fig. 2A-C). Temporal analysis of the IgG2a/IgG1 ratio revealed distinct immune polarization dynamics (Fig. 2D). The DNA-immunized groups (DNA/Protein, DNA/rAdV, and rAdV/DNA) exhibited a Th1-biased response (ratio > 1) until day 21, followed by a transition to a balanced Th1/Th2 profile with ratios stabilizing near 1 from day 28 onward. In contrast, the Protein/rAdV group showed a Th2 bias (ratio < 1) at days 14, 21, and 28, while maintaining a balanced profile at other time points. The commercial vaccine exhibited a mixed pattern.

Potent neutralizing antibody responses induced by heterologous immunization

Neutralizing antibody titers against the BVDV-1 strain were significantly higher in the DNA/protein, DNA/rAdV, and protein/rAdV groups than in the commercial vaccine group at 28 days post-immunization (p < 0.05). By day 42, all heterologous regimens elicited significantly stronger neutralizing antibody responses compared to the commercial vaccine (p < 0.0001; Fig. 3A). For the BVDV-2 strain, the DNA/protein group exhibited a significantly higher neutralizing antibody titer than the commercial vaccine at day 28 (p < 0.0001). This significant advantage was maintained at day 42 by both the DNA/protein and DNA/rAdV groups (p < 0.05; Fig. 3B).

Heterologous immunization reduces viral load following BVDV infection

Heterologous immunization conferred significant protection against BVDV-1 challenge. The DNA/Protein regimen significantly reduced viral loads across all tissues examined (p < 0.01; Fig. 4A–G). The DNA/rAdV and commercial vaccine groups showed significant reductions in all tissues except the small intestine, while the Protein/rAdV group was effective in all tissues except the kidneys. The rAdV/DNA group showed efficacy in all tissues except the kidneys and small intestine. A heatmap summarizing the viral load data across all tissues (Fig. 4H) clearly illustrates these differences. Heterologous immunization also provided similar protection against BVDV-2 infection (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Vaccines preserve tissue architecture post-challenge

Histopathological examination on day 14 post-challenge showed well-preserved duodenal and splenic architecture in all vaccinated groups (Fig. 5). In contrast, the PBS control group exhibited mild pathologies in the duodenum, including inflammatory cell infiltration in the lamina propria and mild submucosal congestion, as well as slight congestion in the spleen (indicated by black arrows).

Heterologous strategies shape a superior cytokine profile

Serum cytokine analysis revealed that heterologous regimens did not significantly alter immunosuppressive cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β1 compared to the commercial vaccine (Fig. 6A, B). For pro-inflammatory cytokines, the DNA/Protein and DNA/rAdV groups induced significantly higher TNF-α (Th1) levels than both control groups at all time points (p < 0.001, Fig. 6C). By day 35, TNF-α levels in the Protein/rAdV and rAdV/DNA groups fell below the commercial vaccine level (p < 0.05, Fig. 6C). All vaccines elevated IL-6 (Th2) by day 35, with the DNA/Protein and DNA/rAdV groups inducing a markedly stronger response (p < 0.05, Fig. 6D). The DNA/Protein and DNA/rAdV groups also initiated significantly higher IL-1β levels at day 21, an effect that persisted for the DNA/rAdV and rAdV/DNA groups at day 35 (p < 0.05, Fig. 6E).

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that heterologous regimens, particularly those initiated with a DNA vaccine, elicit broad and robust immunity against BVDV compared to a conventional inactivated vaccine. By synergistically combining distinct vaccine platforms, these strategies potently activated both cellular and humoral immunity, established a favorable cytokine milieu, and ultimately conferred superior protection in a mouse challenge model, underscoring their potential as a next-generation vaccine strategy.

Our findings affirm the value of the heterologous prime-boost approach for enhancing vaccine efficacy. All heterologous regimens, especially the DNA-primed ones, induced a significantly stronger antigen-specific IFN-γ response than the commercial vaccine, indicating a potent Th1-skewed cellular immunity that was further amplified post-boost. On the humoral front, these strategies elicited high titers of BVDV-specific IgG, its subclasses, and, crucially, virus-neutralizing antibodies. The consistent dominance of the DNA/Protein and DNA/rAdV regimens in neutralizing potency suggests that DNA priming might uniquely optimize the expansion of high-fidelity B-cell clones.

This enhanced immunogenicity directly translated into effective in vivo protection. Heterologous immunization, particularly the DNA/Protein strategy, significantly reduced viral loads across all examined tissues following challenge and mitigated BVDV-induced pathological damage. These results extend the principles observed in other models, such as Malherbe et al.'s [23] work on HIV, confirming the broad applicability of DNA-primed heterologous strategies in achieving multi-tissue viral control.

We acknowledge certain limitations in the present study. First, homologous prime-boost controls for our novel vaccine candidates were not included, as the primary objective was to establish proof-of-concept for heterologous combinations against a commercial benchmark. Future studies should include homologous controls to definitively quantify the benefit of heterologous administration. Second, while the BALB/c mouse model is widely accepted for initial BVDV vaccine screening based on demonstrated susceptibility and pathological responses [24, 25], we recognize that mice are not the natural host and do not fully recapitulate clinical disease in cattle. Therefore, the protection observed—significant reduction in viral loads—serves as a surrogate marker for vaccine efficacy, and validation in cattle remains the essential next step.

Mechanistic insights revealed that the enhanced immunogenicity of DNA-primed heterologous regimens, compared to the commercial vaccine, stems from a qualitative enhancement of the immune response, not the induction of immunosuppression. While the levels of regulatory cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β1 remained comparable across groups, DNA-primed strategies fostered a more robust pro-inflammatory and effector cytokine environment—characterized by elevated TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β [26, 27]. This suggests that priming with DNA more effectively engages innate immunity, thereby establishing a superior foundation for a potent and balanced adaptive response.

Future studies aiming to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms—such as the involvement of innate pathways (e.g., TLR or STING signaling) and the activation status of antigen-presenting cells (e.g., MHC-II, CD80/CD86 expression)—will be critical for understanding the mechanistic basis of this dominant effect.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our data demonstrate that DNA-primed heterologous regimens represent a promising strategy for next-generation BVDV vaccine development. These regimens induced robust humoral and cellular immune responses and conferred significant protection against viral challenge in a mouse model, as evidenced by reduced viral loads and mitigated pathological damage. The sequential presentation of antigen through different platforms may contribute to the enhanced immunogenicity observed. Future work will focus on validating these findings in cattle, incorporating homologous controls to formally quantify the benefit of heterologous administration, and elucidating the precise mechanisms by which DNA priming orchestrates innate and adaptive immunity to achieve sustained protection, thereby accelerating progress toward BVD eradication.

Methods

Animals

Ninety female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old, mean weight 20 ± 5 g) were purchased from the Animal Experiment Center of Xinjiang Medical University. Mice were housed under a 12 h light-dark cycle at 20 °C and 55% relative humidity with ad libitum access to food and water.

Cell and virus culture

Madin–Darby bovine kidney (MDBK) cells were obtained from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Lines (Shanghai, China). Cells were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. BVDV-1 and BVDV-2 strains, identified and maintained by the Zoonotic Diseases Laboratory at Shihezi University, were propagated in MDBK cells. The 50% tissue culture infective dose (TCID50) was determined via the Reed-Muench method [28].

Vaccine preparation

The I E2 + II E2 DNA vaccine is a plasmid DNA construct (pVAX1 backbone) that encodes a fusion protein comprising the E2 glycoproteins from both BVDV-1 and BVDV-2 (I E2 + II E2). This vaccine was adjuvanted with Quil-A (InvivoGen, France). The I E2 + II E2 recombinant protein vaccine consists of the same I E2 + II E2 fusion protein expressed in E. coli and purified, adjuvanted with Freund's adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The rAdV-I E2 + II E2 vaccine is a replication-defective human adenovirus serotype 5 (Ad5) vector expressing the I E2 + II E2 fusion antigen. All vaccines were constructed and validated by the Zoonotic Disease Laboratory of Shihezi University.

Vaccination and sample collection

Mice (n = 15 per group) were immunized twice at a 21-day interval. The four experimental groups received the following prime-boost regimens: (1) DNA/Protein, (2) DNA/rAdV, (3) rAdV/DNA, (4) Protein/rAdV. A PBS control group and a commercial inactivated vaccine (TECON, China) control group served as negative and positive controls, respectively. The specific vaccine doses for each group are detailed in Table 1. Blood samples were collected at designated time points for serum isolation (Fig. 7). DNA and adenovirus vaccines were administered subcutaneously, while the subunit vaccine was delivered intraperitoneally.

Enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISpot) assay

Spleen lymphocytes were isolated from mice (n = 3 per group per time point) on days 28 and 42 post-immunization [29]. A total of 1 × 106 cells were seeded into IFN-γ pre-coated ELISpot plates (Mabtech, Sweden) and stimulated with I E2 + II E2 protein (10 µg), ConA (10 µg; Biosharp, China) and PBS served as positive and negative controls, respectively. After 30 h of incubation, IFN-γ spot-forming cells (SFCs) were quantified.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

Serum levels of BVDV-specific IgG, IgG1, and IgG2a were measured by ELISA [29, 30]. 96-well plates were coated with I E2 + II E2 protein antigen or BVDV whole virus. After blocking, serially diluted mouse serum was added, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG, IgG1, or IgG2a secondary antibodies (Proteintech, China). The reaction was developed with TMB substrate, stopped with sulfuric acid, and absorbance was read at 450 nm.

Virus neutralization test

Heat-inactivated serum samples from days 14, 28, and 42 were serially diluted two-fold and mixed with an equal volume of 100 TCID50 BVDV-1 or BVDV-2. The mixture was incubated with MDBK cells for 1 h, replaced with maintenance medium, and observed for CPE for 5–7 days. The 50% neutralizing antibody titer was calculated using the Reed-Muench method [28].

Challenge experiment and viral load quantification

On day 42 after immunization, mice were challenged intraperitoneally with 100 µL of 1.68 × 10⁵ TCID₅₀ of either BVDV-1 or BVDV-2. A total of 4 mice from each vaccination group were challenged per virus (n = 4 per virus). On day 14 post-challenge, blood samples were collected from 3 randomly selected mice per group (n = 3) via the retro-orbital venous plexus using glass capillary tubes and transferred into tubes containing EDTA. After blood collection, mice were immediately euthanized by cervical dislocation, and major tissues, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and small intestine, were harvested.

Total RNA was extracted from homogenized tissue samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed by spectrophotometry (A260/A280 ratio). Viral loads were quantified by reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) targeting the highly conserved 5'untranslated region (5'UTR) of the BVDV genome, as previously described [29, 30]. Briefly, a standard curve was generated using serial ten-fold dilutions of a plasmid containing the BVDV-5'UTR gene, with a linear range from 10² to 10⁸ copies/reaction. The detection limit of the assay was 10² copies per reaction. Samples with cycle threshold (Ct) values above 40, corresponding to copy numbers below this threshold, were considered negative.

Histopathological analysis

Duodenum and spleen tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned (4 μm), and stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E). Pathological changes were examined under a microscope and digitized for analysis.

Cytokine measurement

Serum concentrations of IL-10, TGF-β1 (Novus Biologicals, USA), TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β (R&D Systems, USA) were measured on days 21 and 35 using commercial ELISA kits, according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., USA) using one- or two-way ANOVA, as appropriate, with a p-value < 0.05 considered significant. Viral loads and serum cytokine levels were determined from standard curves generated in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, USA). All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data are presented as means ± standard deviations (SDs).
