Enhancing the Sensitivity of Mass Spectrometry Imaging through Spatial Signal Averaging
Authors: Yury N. Desyaterik, Mary Peace McRae, Kurt Hauser, Austin M. Jones, Konstantin O. Nagornov, Anton N. Kozhinov, Yury O. Tsybin, Elias P. Rosen, Angela D.M. Kashuba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mass spectrometry imaging (MSI) enables spatially resolved molecular analysis, but automatic data reduction imposed by instrument software, such as in Orbitrap systems, can severely limit the detection of low-abundance ions. When these ions are targeted, the resulting sparse detection hinders spatial interpretation and compromises quantitative accuracy. Using an external high-performance data acquisition system to capture full-length transients, we demonstrate that adjacent-pixel averaging of full-profile (unreduced) Orbitrap data markedly improves the detection frequency of low-abundance peaks without affecting the detection of highly abundant species in biological tissue samples. Pixel averaging increased the coverage of low-abundance ions from less than 10% to over 90% in mouse brain and nonhuman primate vaginal tract tissues. Quantitative accuracy was also enhanced, as shown by improved agreement between observed and theoretical isotopic ratios, and between MSI-derived and liquid chromatography (LC)–MS/MS measured total analyte concentrations in adjacent tissue sections. These findings establish pixel averaging as a simple yet powerful strategy to extend MSI sensitivity and improve quantitative biochemical mapping in complex tissues, while remaining fully compatible with other sensitivity-enhancing techniques.

Introduction

Mass spectrometry imaging (MSI) has emerged as a transformative tool in life sciences, mapping the spatial distribution of biomolecules directly from tissue. Unlike conventional liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) or gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) hyphenated MS approaches, which sacrifice spatial context, MSI enables localization of molecular species within their native environment. Applications of MSI span a wide range of research areas, including studies of protein aging in cataractous lenses, differentiation of melanoma from benign lesions, and quantitative imaging of the dystrophin-glycoprotein complex. MSI has also advanced understanding of microbial communities at the host–pathogen interface, − cancer metabolism, cardiomyocyte decline, and protein glycation in diabetic nephropathy. , Collectively, these examples underscore MSI’s growing role as a cornerstone technology for biomedical research.

Despite this promise, MSI continues to face fundamental sensitivity limitations. All MSI modalities rely on the sequential acquisition of chemical information from discrete sampling locations. As pixel size decreases to achieve cellular or even subcellular resolution, − the sampled volume shrinks in proportion to the square of the pixel size, leading to fewer analyte molecules available for detection. Consequently, improvements in spatial resolution come at the cost of sensitivity. In targeted analyses, this problem hinders the detection of trace analytes; in untargeted analyses, it reduces the molecular coverage and dynamic range.

The magnitude of this challenge is illustrated by direct comparisons between MSI and conventional bulk MS. In a recent study of glioma-bearing mouse brains, liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) detected 459 metabolites with high reproducibility, while MSI identified only a small fraction of these using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI) (16–66 annotations at 10% false discovery rate (FDR)) or desorption electrospray ionization (DESI) (3–13 annotations per sample). Analysis of the Metaspace2020.org repository further highlights the gap: across 323 MALDI MSI datasets of mouse brain tissue, the maximum number of annotations in a single dataset was 282 at 10% FDR, with an average of ∼60, compared to 1,547 metabolites detected in the same tissue by LC–MS/MS, of which 1,214 were detected exclusively by LC–MS/MS as unique m/z (molecular formulas). Beyond metabolites, similar issues arise for small molecule xenobiotics. In a study of six antiretroviral compounds distributed in primate spleen, only one exhibited coverage across more than 10% of the tissue area, and no correlations were observed with LC–MS/MS measurements from adjacent sections. Such sparse ion maps, where annotated signals appear only in a small fraction of pixels, are rarely published (for example), as they offer limited insight into the spatial distribution of ions, which is the core purpose of MSI. Yet, despite their limited utility, these sparse maps account for a substantial fraction of annotated analytes even at a 10% FDR, as evidenced by datasets on Metaspace2020.org. These observations emphasize that sensitivity is not merely a technical nuisance but a fundamental barrier to broader adoption of MSI in quantitative and drug discovery applications.

A wide range of strategies have been explored to address the MSI sensitivity. One set of approaches focuses on improving the desorption and ionization efficiency. For MALDI, this includes extensive optimization of matrix chemistry, use of immunohistochemistry tags for selective protein detection, and surface-assisted laser desorption/ionization (SALDI) employing inorganic nanoparticles , or structured substrates. , The MALDI-2 method, which applies a second laser pulse to intercept the desorption plume, has significantly enhanced sensitivity for neutral lipids, phospholipids, sterols, and pharmaceutical compounds. − Another set of strategies improves ion transfer efficiency by reducing pressure in atmospheric-pressure ion sources, permitting wider apertures between the ionization region and the mass spectrometer. , Although subatmospheric MALDI has demonstrated gains in sensitivity, it comes at the expense of higher cost and reduced throughput. A more targeted approach is to use narrow m/z detection windows to increase sensitivity for specific analytes, as demonstrated for morphine imaging with Orbitrap mass spectrometry.

While these innovations have advanced MSI, they share two common limitations: (1) improvements are often analyte- or instrument-specific, and (2) broad, uniform sensitivity gains across molecular classes remain elusive. What is lacking is a generalizable, ionization-independent approach to enhancing sensitivity without compromising the untargeted nature of MSI.

Signal averaging provides an opportunity. By combining mass spectra from adjacent pixels, the signal-to-noise ratio can be increased in proportion to the averaging window size, increasing the likelihood of detecting low-abundance species. This concept is broadly compatible with every MSI modality, such as MALDI, DESI, and MALDESI, and can be implemented alongside other sensitivity-enhancing strategies. To date, signal averaging in MSI has been applied only in limited contexts, such as pixel-to-pixel smoothing in MALDI imaging, but it has not been systematically developed for sensitivity enhancement.

A major reason for this gap is technical. Orbitrap instruments, which are widely used in high-resolution MSI, apply real-time spectral thresholding (data reduction) to facilitate the data transfer, storage, and processing. While this suppresses noise and reduces the file size, it also discards weak analyte signals, precluding effective averaging. Furthermore, Orbitraps natively support averaging of consecutive mass spectra prior to data reduction (via microscans) but lack the capability for selective averaging across adjacent pixels. Averaging only consecutive spectra along the acquisition path distorts the two-dimensional ion maps, introducing spatial bias. Moreover, when this option is enabled, the Orbitrap averages all detected peaks equally, thereby reducing spatial resolution globally, even though only near-threshold peaks require enhancement.

Recent developments provide a path for the future. External high-performance data acquisition systems (DAQs), such as the FTMS Booster (Spectroswiss), enable direct capture of complete transient signals from the Orbitrap preamplifier. − This capability allows for flexible postacquisition processing and custom signal averaging, retaining weak ion signals that would otherwise be lost during in-instrument thresholding.

Here, we employ such an external data acquisition system with an Orbitrap mass spectrometer to investigate how averaging of unreduced data, which can be selectively applied to ions of interest, can enhance sensitivity in MSI. Specifically, we examine how reducing detection thresholds and applying averaging of low-intensity ion signals affect the ion map coverage, molecular depth, and quantification. By addressing sensitivity limitations through a hardware-agnostic and ionization-independent approach, this work offers a broadly applicable framework for improving the MSI performance across diverse molecular classes and experimental conditions.

Materials and Methods

Animal Studies

Mouse Study

An animal study administering morphine, abacavir, dolutegravir, and lamivudine was conducted on adult female mice under the approval of the Virginia Commonwealth University IACUC. Details of animal dosing and tissue harvesting have been reported previously. Antiretrovirals were administered orally by suspending powdered drug from a tablet in saline, with doses calculated by allometric scaling (abacavir, 2.5 mg/day; dolutegravir, 0.2 mg/day; lamivudine, 1.2 mg/day). Morphine salt pentahydrate was dissolved directly into the antiretroviral solution at a concentration of 2.0 mg/day. Animals were dosed to steady state for 5 days. One brain tissue sample was used for this investigation.

Nonhuman Primate Study

A nonhuman primate (NHP) study was carried out at the California National Primate Research Center in collaboration with the University of California, Davis, and was approved by the UC Davis Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol 18345). Details of antiretroviral dosing, infection, and tissue collection have been described previously. Vaginal tissues analyzed in this study were obtained from animals dosed to steady state (10 days) with emtricitabine (FTC, 30 mg/kg), tenofovir (TFV, 16 mg/kg), and maraviroc (MVC, 150 mg/kg) administered twice daily, together with atazanavir (ATV, 270 mg/kg) twice daily. Four tissues from 4 animals were used for this investigation.

MS Analysis

Sample Preparation

Snap-frozen tissue samples, stored at −80 °C, were sectioned at 10 μm thickness using a clinical cryostat (Leica CM1950, Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Germany), thaw-mounted onto microscope slides, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Calibration of TFV in vaginal tissue was performed by spotting 100 nL of standards (0.0–8.0 μg/mL) onto blank vaginal tissue sections from undosed NHPs. Consecutive slices of NHP vaginal tissue samples were analyzed for TFV content by LC–MS/MS.

IR-MALDESI MSI and LC–MS/MS

Methanol (HPLC grade), acetonitrile (HPLC grade), water (HPLC grade), and formic acid (Optima) were obtained from Fisher Scientific (Hampton, NH).

LC–MS/MS methods for tissue homogenate samples were detailed previously. , The limit of quantification for TFV was 0.01 ng/slice.

Chemical standards and acquisition diagnostics were evaluated by direct electrospray injection into an Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Q Exactive Plus, Bremen, Germany). The electrospray solvent was a (50:50, v/v) mixture of water and methanol containing 0.2% formic acid. Analyses were conducted in positive ion mode at a resolving power of 140,000 (m/z 200). Mass accuracy (<1 ppm) was confirmed through weekly calibration with the manufacturer’s recommended solution. The automatic gain control (AGC) function was disabled to replicate trapping conditions of infrared (IR)-MALDESI MSI, and the ion injection time was fixed at 11 ms. The number of microscans was set to 1.

The IR-MALDESI MSI procedure has been described previously. − In brief, tissue sections were mounted on a thermally controlled stage (TE Technology, Inc., Traverse City, MI) inside the source chamber. Dry nitrogen was introduced to reduce humidity, and samples were cooled to −9 °C. The source was then briefly opened to ambient air to increase relative humidity to ∼50%, permitting an ice film to form on the tissue surface. After roughly 8 min of ice growth, the chamber was closed, and low-flow nitrogen was reintroduced to maintain ∼18% relative humidity during analysis, stabilizing ice thickness.

An IR optical parametric oscillator (OPO) laser (Opotek, Carlsbad, CA) tuned to 2.94 μm was used for sample ablation. The resulting plume was ionized by a perpendicular electrospray and introduced into the Orbitrap Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer. Laser firing and Orbitrap acquisition were synchronized, with ion injection time fixed at 11 ms to ensure consistent ion transfer. The stage was translated in 100 μm increments. Laser, stage, and mass spectrometer control were automated by a microcontroller and custom software.

External Data Acquisition

An external high-performance data acquisition system (FTMS Booster X2, Spectroswiss, Lausanne, Switzerland) was connected to both signal outputs of the Orbitrap preamplifier, enabling the recording of full transients. The internal Orbitrap electronics remained unmodified and operated in parallel, allowing direct comparison between native and externally acquired datasets.

Data Processing

Raw Orbitrap data (enhanced Fourier transform, or eFT mass spectra recorded in a reduced-profile mode) were converted to .mzXML files using MSConvert and visualized as ion distribution maps with MSiReader. Transients recorded by the external DAQ system were converted to absorption-mode Fourier transform (aFT) mass spectra using Mozaic MSI software (Spectroswiss, Lausanne, Switzerland). Spectral processing was performed using the default Mozaic apodization (semikaiser) and zero-filling settings (at least 2).

A procedure to generate N x × N y running-average ion maps was implemented in Mozaic. Averaging with an N × N square kernel (N x = N y = N) involves calculating an average mass spectrum (the average intensity) within a square of size N. For example, when N = 2, the average is computed over four adjacent pixels. For pixels located close to the image boundary, where the full square N × N of pixels might be unavailable, pixel averaging was performed with a maximum possible square size less than N.

A reference noise-thresholding level was adjusted manually, at which ion peaks that remain after noise-thresholding of the full-profile aFT mass spectra matched the corresponding ion peaks in Orbitrap reduced-profile mass spectra. We found that this level could be lowered by up to a factor of 2 in the aFT mass spectra before noise began to appear in off-tissue regions. After applying the N × N averaging method to the aFT mass spectra, the averaged mass spectra were noise-thresholded at reduced levels by a factor of N w.r.t. the noise levels in mass spectra prior to averaging.

Pearson correlation coefficients and descriptive statistics (mean and standard deviation) for quantitative analyses were computed by using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation).

Results and Discussion

Detection Frequency of Low-Abundance Signals: Theoretical Background

To assess how the detection threshold impacts the quality of an ion map, we first considered the ion detection frequency of an analyte uniformly distributed within a sample. Because the number of analyte ions (Ni) accumulated in the Orbitrap mass spectrometer from a single laser shot is independent of a shot at an adjacent position, this accumulation follows Poisson statistics. When the Poisson distribution has a mean value of Ni > 10, it is well approximated by a normal distribution with a mean of Ni and a standard deviation of √Ni. An example of such a distribution for an arbitrary ion with Ni = 30 is shown in Figure A. This number of ions corresponds to the estimated Orbitrap detection threshold based on noise levels of approximately 3000 ions (charges) per second across the acquisition mass range in our typical MSI experiments using a fixed ion injection time of 11 ms. We assume all ions are singly charged in this consideration, which corresponds to the typical metabolite MALDI/MALDESI MSI. If the Orbitrap detection threshold for an arbitrary ion were 40 ions (charges) instead of 30, only 2.1% of pixels in the resulting image would exhibit signal intensities above this threshold, and the remaining 97.9% would be thresholded to zero, resulting in a highly sparse ion map. Conversely, lowering the detection threshold to 20 ions increases the fraction of pixels with detectable signal to 97.9%. Thus, a 2-fold reduction in the signal threshold in this scenario results in an approximately 46-fold improvement in ion map detection frequency, increasing from 2.1% to 97.9%. While the theoretical framework assumes singly charged ions for simplicity, for an ion with charge state m, the signal variance decreases by a factor of 1/√m, such that threshold reduction yields a greater increase in detection frequency for low-to-moderate charge states (e.g., doubly charged ions common in IR-MALDESI), whereas this benefit vanishes once the charge state exceeds the effective detection threshold, since such ions are already detected without averaging.

(a) Statistical distribution of analyte ions in the trap from a single laser shot, with a median value of 30, and the fraction of pixels above threshold as a function of threshold level. (b) Rank-ordered plot of peak intensities from a single murine brain spectrum, illustrating how the number of detected peaks depends on the threshold.

Having established this theoretical framework, we next examined how sensitivity affects the number of MS peaks detected, a key metric for information depth in MSI metabolomics. , A rank-ordered plot of ion signal abundances from a single IR-MALDESI spectrum of mouse brain tissue (Figure B) shows that most peaks fall within the lowest order of magnitude of nearly a four-order dynamic range. This distribution can be approximated by a reciprocal function, indicating that lowering the detection threshold by a factor of N results in an approximately N-fold increase in the number of detected peaks, thereby enhancing the informational depth of the analysis. To assess reproducibility, we determined this exponent across five additional spectra from the same sample, yielding an average value of −1.125 with a standard deviation of 0.0112. To evaluate whether this trend extends to other tissue types, the analysis was repeated for vaginal tissue samples, resulting in an average exponent of −1.082 with a standard deviation of 0.0312 across six spectra.

Full-Profile Data and Signal Averaging

As the theoretical considerations suggest, detection frequencies for peaks with mean values close to the Orbitrap noise threshold are frequently low due to thresholding. To address this limitation, we recorded the full-profile (unreduced) mass spectra using an external data acquisition system that retains signals below the instrument threshold. This approach allowed us to adjust the signal threshold level more flexibly.

We first evaluated the impact of signal averaging on the detection frequencies of both the targeted and endogenous low-abundance peaks. This analysis included three low-intensity ions from a single mouse brain sample and four ions from a nonhuman primate vaginal tract sample. The mouse brain tissue analyzed was dosed with morphine and abacavir. Principal ion heat maps for morphine, abacavir, and an endogenous compound at m/z 287.2729 (identified putatively as a cholesterol fragment (CF)) , are shown in Figure A. The assignment is supported by its identical spatial distribution to the principal cholesterol ion at m/z 369.352, as well as the presence of multiple reported cholesterol fragment ions (m/z 233.2264, 245.2264, 273.2577, and 299.2734) exhibiting the same spatial localization.

The morphine ion map provides an example of low coverage (detection frequency ∼ 15%). Although the monoisotopic ions (M) of abacavir and CF exhibit high detection frequency, their isotopologues ([M+1] abacavir and [M+2] CF) were detected significantly less frequently (Figure B). An added benefit of examining sensitivity enhancement of these isotopologues is that the spatial distribution of the monoisotopic peak serves as an inherent reference.

Using full-profile data, we adjusted the signal threshold level such that the number of pixels with a detectable morphine signal matched the Orbitrap output. Interestingly, the same threshold level was found for the other two small peaks. We then found that lowering the threshold by half of the Orbitrap level increased the detection frequency substantially without introducing noise into the off-tissue region. This 2-fold reduction improved detection frequency by approximately 1.5-fold for [M+1] abacavir and nearly 5-fold for [M+2] CF (Table ).

Next, we investigated whether spatial averaging could further increase coverage. Full-profile data were averaged using N × N square templates. Because averaging of n full-profile spectra increases the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) by √n, and n = N2, the SNR increases by N. This improvement allows the threshold level to be reduced by a factor of N. We tested values of N ranging from 2 to 5. The resulting ion heat maps (Figure C) show almost complete tissue coverage for all three small peaks. Quantitatively, detection frequency increased 13-fold for the isotopologue ion, from 6% to 82.8%. For morphine and abacavir isotopologues, practically complete coverage was achieved with a 5 × 5 averaging template (Table ).

To test generality, we applied the method to vaginal tissue dosed with four antiretroviral medications. For two analytes, MVC and ATV, sensitivity was sufficient for >50% detection frequency, so we used their M+2 and M+1 isotopologues as weak-signal benchmarks. Averaging by 5 × 5 templates increased detection frequency 4–6-fold, from <20% to >80–90% for three analytes. FTC, with only 3.6% detection in Orbitrap data, improved to 62% (∼30-fold increase). While averaging is expected to reduce resolving power, we observed only a minimal effect, with 5 × 5 template averaging resulting in an approximately 6% decrease for low-intensity peaks.

Turbo Booster acquisition increases file size approximately 10–30× relative to standard Orbitrap data but enables flexible reuse and reprocessing of unthresholded time-domain datasets without reacquisition. A more significant trade-off associated with averaging is a reduction in spatial resolution, which is discussed later.

Implications for Spatial Analysis

The improved ion map coverage has important implications for downstream analyses. Usually, it involves either identifying colocalized ions by calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient between ion distributions or reducing data complexity through non-negative matrix factorization. High ion map coverage enables these analyses to be performed with greater accuracy. To illustrate this, we calculated correlations between the spatial distributions of abacavir and CF principal ions and their isotopologues. In theory, these correlations should approach 1. In practice, Orbitrap data yielded Pearson coefficients <0.5, which improved to ∼0.6 after threshold reduction and exceeded 0.93 following 5 × 5 averaging (Table ).

Impact on Quantification

While improved coverage and spatial correlation are valuable for qualitative MSI, accurate quantification is equally critical, particularly in drug distribution and pharmacological studies where dose–response relationships must be established. However, quantification in MSI is strongly affected by the thresholding effect inherent to Orbitrap data processing. Because the instrument discards signals below the noise threshold, a substantial fraction of low-abundance ions is excluded from the mass spectra, leading to systematic underestimation of true ion intensities.

The initial evaluation of improved accuracy of quantitative MSI through signal averaging involved an assessment of experimental isotopic ratios of abacavir and CF isotopologues. Theoretical isotopic distributions can be predicted with high precision, making them ideal internal checks of quantitative accuracy. In the Orbitrap-reduced-profile data, the measured M/M+1 ratio for abacavir was 5.44, nearly 3-fold lower than the theoretical value of 15.16 (Table ). Likewise, the Orbitrap-derived M/M+2 ratio for CF was underestimated by approximately 5-fold. These discrepancies clearly demonstrate that thresholding does not merely reduce map coverage but also systematically distorts abundance ratios, undermining the reliability of MSI quantification, for example, in fluxomic studies.

Quantification Benchmarking against LC–MS/MS

Another way to evaluate the impact of averaging on quantification is to directly compare MSI-derived measurements with those obtained using the “gold standard” validated LC–MS/MS assays. To this end, we quantified TFV in NHP female vaginal tract tissues from 3 animals using both Orbitrap reduced-profile data and FTMS Booster’s full-profile data with averaging and compared the results against replicate LC–MS/MS measurements from consecutive tissue slices. TFV penetration to NHP vaginal tract tissues is relatively low compared to other suspected viral reservoir tissues, so the combination of drug target and matrix represents a challenging combination for quantitative analysis.

For the full-profile dataset, quantification was performed by extracting the intensity of the TFV peak from an averaged mass spectrum generated by summing mass spectra across all tissue pixels. Tissue pixels were identified using the ion map of an endogenous compound at m/z 296.0554, ensuring that only biologically relevant regions contributed to the averaged signal. Representative ion maps are shown in Figure , which compares TFV distributions obtained from Orbitrap reduced-profile data, from an 8 × 8 averaged full-profile dataset, and from the endogenous compound marker.

As illustrated in Figure , the frequency of TFV detection increased dramatically, from only a few percent in the reduced-profile data to over 90% after 8 × 8 averaging, demonstrating that averaging effectively rescues signals otherwise lost due to thresholding. An interesting exception was observed in the region outlined in green, where TFV remained below the detection limit even after averaging. This feature was consistently observed across consecutive slices. Preliminary MS evidence suggests that this region is biochemically distinct from the surrounding tissue, with several peaks either enriched or depleted relative to adjacent areas. Notably, one of the enriched peaks corresponds to a cholecalciferol marker, suggesting that this region represents adipose tissue, as cholecalciferol is known to accumulate in fat. Importantly, this localized lack of TFV penetration exemplifies the type of pharmacologically relevant spatial information that MSI is designed to reveal. The fact that such insight can be obtained from ions with initially very low detection frequencies highlights the power of the averaging approach.

The results of the quantitative comparison are summarized in Table , which presents the total TFV abundances per tissue slice from three animals. As expected, Orbitrap reduced-profile data substantially underestimated TFV content, with values corresponding to only 6–15% of those obtained by LC–MS. In contrast, averaging of the full-profile data restored agreement with LC–MS measurements to within 20–25%, demonstrating that spatial averaging not only increases detection frequency but also corrects for underestimation of total analyte abundance. These findings underscore the utility of averaging-based full-profile acquisition for quantitative MSI, bringing its performance considerably closer to that of LC–MS.

Strategies to Mitigate Resolution Loss from Averaging

Spatial averaging of adjacent pixels substantially improves detection of ions near the instrument’s threshold, increasing coverage even for analytes initially detected in fewer than 10% of pixels. These gains enhance downstream analyses such as spatial correlation, principal component analysis, and quantitative assessment of analyte localization. The approach preserves the integrity of strong peaks and is compatible with other sensitivity-enhancing methods, including MALDI-2 and subatmospheric pressure MALDI. The primary drawback is reduced spatial resolution, but several strategies can mitigate this limitation. Image fusion techniques, , adapted from satellite imaging, can combine low-resolution averaged ion maps with high-resolution optical or strong-peak maps, effectively restoring spatial detail lost during averaging. Deblurring approaches, such as the Richardson–Lucy deconvolution algorithm, − also offer promise; because the averaging kernel is precisely known, resolution can be restored while controlling noise amplification. Machine learning methods, including random forest regression − and convolutional neural networks, can further enhance resolution by learning spatial patterns from strong peaks and applying them to weak analyte maps. Ultimately, no single method is universally optimal; adaptive pipelines that integrate multiple restoration strategies may provide the most robust balance between sensitivity and spatial fidelity in MSI data. Restoration of spatial resolution using these approaches will be demonstrated in follow-up publications.

The averaging approach, in its current implementation, relies on the property that the mean of detector noise approaches zero when averaging transients or absorption-mode Fourier transform spectra. This assumption holds true for Fourier transform–based instruments, such as Orbitrap and FT-ICR mass spectrometers. To assess the broader applicability of the proposed method, we analyzed publicly available imaging datasets from the METASPACE (metaspace2020.eu) repository. We quantified the proportion of Fourier transform (FT)-based mass spectrometry instruments used in submissions from 2023 through January 2026, the point at which timsTOF data began appearing regularly. Across the two largest relevant datasets, FT instruments accounted for 73.3% of human lung samples (N = 1,593) and 72% of kidney samples across all organisms (N = 1,908), underscoring the widespread relevance of this approach.

Other instruments, such as time-of-flight (TOF) systems, can also benefit from averaging; however, their implementation requires careful determination of the noise threshold, a challenge that these instruments face irrespective of averaging.

Some limitations of the present work include the relatively small number of tissues and drugs evaluated. We plan to address this by expanding the range of samples in our future studies. The most significant constraint, however, was the restricted size of the averaging template, which we limited due to the degradation of spatial resolution. In future work, we aim to develop methods for restoring spatial resolution, as discussed earlier, and to systematically examine how far sensitivity can be improved without compromising the spatial detail. We will also explore to what extent additional sensitivity gains are possible when a controlled reduction in the spatial resolution is acceptable.

Conclusions

In this work, we demonstrate that applying adjacent-pixel averaging to full-profile Orbitrap mass spectrometry data dramatically improves the detection frequency of weak signals in imaging mass spectrometry (MSI). Even for ions with an initial detection coverage below 10%, averaging strategies produced nearly complete ion maps, enabling more reliable visualization of spatial distributions. The Pearson correlation coefficient between isotopologues improved from values lower than 0.5 to more than 0.9, and quantification values that were 10 times smaller were restored within roughly 25% of LC–MS measurements. Importantly, these improvements were achieved without affecting the detection of abundant ions, and the approach remained fully compatible with other sensitivity-enhancing techniques.

While spatial averaging inevitably reduces spatial resolution, we outline several computational strategies such as image fusion, deconvolution-based deblurring, and machine-learning-driven reconstruction that can restore or enhance resolution after averaging. These complementary strategies, many of which are well-established in medical imaging and remote sensing, hold considerable promise for extending the utility of MSI in quantitative and spatially resolved metabolomics.

Overall, our findings demonstrate that the MSI detection sensitivity can be extended well beyond the limits imposed by Orbitrap signal thresholding. By combining averaging-based sensitivity enhancement with computational resolution-restoration techniques, MSI can deliver both quantitative accuracy and spatial detail, reinforcing its value as a powerful tool for mapping the biochemical complexity in biological tissues.

The method described in this work has been implemented in the multivendor MSI software Mozaic (Spectroswiss) and is readily applicable to Orbitrap and other MSI instruments. This implementation demonstrates the practical maturity and accessibility of the methodology beyond that of a single laboratory environment.
