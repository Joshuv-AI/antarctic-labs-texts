Association between C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio and the risk of overall survival in advanced non-small cell lung cancer patients with anlotinib treatment: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jiaxin Liu, Jinzhan Chen, Jianying Liu, Xiong Xiao, Haiyan Chen, Yijiao Xu, Zhisheng Chen, Hongni Jiang, Lin Tong, Congyi Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR) reflects systemic inflammation and malnutrition, but its association with survival in anlotinib-treated advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains unexplored.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 417 patients with stage IIIB-IV NSCLC treated with anlotinib at Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University and Zhongshan Hospital (Xiamen), Fudan University. Baseline CAR was calculated as serum C-reactive protein (mg/L) divided by albumin (g/L). Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from treatment initiation to death or last follow-up. Cox proportional hazards models, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, and two-piecewise linear regression model were applied to evaluate the association between CAR and OS. Subgroup analyses were further performed for robustness.ResultsAmong 417 patients (mean age 62.2 ± 10.33 years), 216 deaths (51.8%) occurred during follow-up. The median CAR was 0.34 (0.08–1.09). In multivariable Cox models, elevated CAR was independently associated with worse OS (HR 1.10, 95% CI 1.01–1.21, p = 0.031). RCS analysis revealed a nonlinear association between CAR and OS (p for nonlinearity < 0.001). The inflection point was 2.12: below this threshold, higher CAR significantly increased mortality risk (HR 1.92, 95% CI 1.54–2.40, p < 0.0001), whereas above 2.12, CAR showed a non-significant trend toward lower risk (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.68–1.00, p = 0.050). Subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent associations across most clinical strata.ConclusionBaseline CAR is a strong and independent prognostic marker for OS in advanced NSCLC patients treated with anlotinib. Its simplicity and availability make it a valuable tool for clinical risk stratification and treatment planning.Keywords: Lung cancer, Anlotinib, Albumin, C-reactive protein, Overall survival

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the most deadly cancer globally, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) comprising approximately 85% of cases [1, 2]. Despite significant therapeutic advances, including targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors, the median overall survival for advanced-stage patients remains stagnant, and inter-individual variability in treatment response persists. Thus, the need for widely-accessible and clinically reliable prognostic biomarkers is more urgent than ever.

Systemic inflammation and host nutritional status are increasingly recognized as critical determinants of tumor progression and survival [3, 4]. The C‑reactive protein (CRP) to albumin ratio (CAR) combines systemic inflammatory burden with nutritional reserve, offering a composite index that reflects host-tumor interactions in a simple laboratory assay. Its prognostic value has been demonstrated across multiple solid tumors, such as hepatocellular carcinoma [5], colorectal cancer [6], pancreatic cancer [7], and esophageal carcinoma [8]. In NSCLC, a meta-analysis incorporating over 3,300 patients confirmed that elevated preoperative CAR is significantly associated with poor survival and advanced clinicopathological features [9].

Anlotinib, a multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitor suppressing VEGF/PDGF/FGF signaling pathways, is established as a standard treatment for refractory advanced NSCLC in China [10, 11]. However, significant heterogeneity exists in individual responses, and predictive biomarkers remain lacking. Given the interplay between systemic inflammation, angiogenesis, and tumor behavior, CAR emerges as a plausible and clinically accessible marker for prognosis in this therapeutic context.

Despite the potential, existing research has not yet clarified CAR’s prognostic utility in NSCLC patients treated with anlotinib. To fill this gap, we performed a retrospective analysis of 417 advanced NSCLC patients treated with anlotinib at Fudan University-affiliated Zhongshan Hospital (Shanghai) and its Xiamen branch. Our objectives were to: (1) assess the association between baseline CAR and overall survival (OS); (2) explore potential non‑linear relationships using log‑likelihood ratio testing; and (3) confirm the robustness of our findings through sensitivity analyses.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a retrospective cohort study designed to examine the association between the CAR and OS in patients with advanced NSCLC undergoing anlotinib therapy. We screened medical records of patients admitted to Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (from June 2018 to September 2023) and Zhongshan Hospital (Xiamen), Fudan University (from April 2019 to February 2025). Eligible participants met the following inclusion criteria: (1) pathologically confirmed diagnosis of NSCLC by either histology or cytology; (2) treatment with anlotinib (administered at 12 mg, 10 mg, or 8 mg once daily, two weeks on followed by one week off per cycle). Patients were excluded if they (1) lacked complete baseline laboratory data-serum CRP and/or albumin-obtained within 7 days before anlotinib initiation; (2) did not complete at least one treatment cycle of anlotinib; (3) were not at clinical stage IIIB or IV according to the 8th edition of the IASLC TNM classification; or (4) had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) score greater than 2.

Data collection and variable definition

Demographic data (age, sex, smoking history), clinical characteristics (histologic subtype, stage, ECOG PS), metastatic sites, comorbidities, prior treatments, and molecular markers (EGFR, ALK status) were extracted from the institutional electronic medical records (EMR) or verified via follow-up calls. CAR was calculated as serum CRP (mg/L) divided by serum albumin (g/L) [12, 13]. Laboratory values were obtained from the closest measurement within 7 days before the start of anlotinib. Never smokers were defined as individuals with no history of smoking. The primary endpoint was OS, defined as the interval from the date of anlotinib initiation to death from any cause or last known follow-up [10, 14]. Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. The cutoff data for patients’ follow-up was February 28, 2025.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized according to CAR tertiles. Continuous variables were described as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), depending on distribution normality. Categorical variables were reported as counts with percentages. Intergroup comparisons were performed using the chi-square test for categorical variables, one-way ANOVA for normally distributed continuous variables, and the Kruskal–Wallis test for skewed variables.

To investigate the association between CAR and OS, we employed univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models. Three models were constructed: an unadjusted model, a model adjusted for demographic variables (age, gender, smoking status), and a fully adjusted model incorporating clinical covariates known to impact prognosis (e.g., ECOG PS, metastasis sites, gene mutation status, prior treatments). Results were expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model covariates were selected based on prior literature and biological relevance [12, 15]. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to confirm the robustness of findings. CAR was also analyzed as a categorical variable by tertile grouping. Subgroup analyses were performed using stratified linear regression models. Interaction terms between subgroup variables were incorporated to assess effect modification, and subsequent verification was conducted using likelihood ratio tests. OS among CAR tertiles was assessed using the Kaplan–Meier method and log-rank tests. To assess potential non-linear relationships between CAR and OS, we applied restricted cubic spline regression and generalized additive model (GAM). If non-linearity was detected, a two-piecewise linear regression model was used to identify the inflection point where the CAR-OS association changed. The optimal model was determined by comparing the log-likelihoods using a likelihood ratio test. Because EMR data sources did not reliably capture the initial anlotinib dose and the presence of some mutations (e.g., ROS1 and BRAF V600E), we could not directly adjust for relevant variables. We explored the potential for unmeasured confounding between CAR and OS by calculating E-values. The E-value quantifies the required magnitude of an unmeasured confounder that could negate the observed association between CAR and OS [16]. A p-value < 0.05 indicated a significant difference between the two models, warranting the use of the two-piecewise approach.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2; The R Foundation, https://www.R-project.org) and EmpowerStats (X&Y Solutions, Inc., Boston, MA; http://www.empowerstats.com). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The enrollment of participants

Between June 2018 and September 2023, a total of 425 patients at Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, and between April 2019 and February 2025, an additional 98 patients at Zhongshan Hospital Xiamen Branch were treated with anlotinib for NSCLC. After applying predefined exclusion criteria, 106 patients were excluded: 55 due to missing baseline data (26 in Shanghai, 29 in Xiamen), 25 for receiving less than one full cycle of anlotinib treatment (23 in Shanghai, 2 in Xiamen), 5 for not meeting the minimum clinical staging of IIIB or IV (3 in Shanghai, 2 in Xiamen), and 21 for an ECOG PS > 2 (18 in Shanghai, 3 in Xiamen). Ultimately, 417 eligible patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the study participants enrollment

Baseline characteristics

A total of 417 patients with advanced NSCLC treated with anlotinib were included and stratified into tertiles based on baseline CAR. The median age was 62.21 years, and 35.25% were female. Across increasing CAR tertiles, there was a trend toward older age, a higher proportion of male and smoking patients, and a gradual shift in pathological subtypes, with adenocarcinoma more prevalent in lower CAR groups and squamous cell carcinoma more frequent in higher CAR groups. EGFR mutation rates decreased across tertiles, while baseline albumin declined and CRP levels rose significantly. Other clinical features, including tumor stage, ECOG PS, prior treatments, and metastasis burden, were largely balanced between groups (Table 1).

Univariate analysis of overall survival

In the univariate Cox regression analysis of OS, several clinical and laboratory variables demonstrated significant associations with patient outcomes. A higher ECOG PS score > 1 was strongly associated with worse OS (HR, 2.82; 95% CI, 1.77–4.49; p < 0.0001). Similarly, patients with three or more metastatic sites (HR, 1.46; 95% CI, 1.08–1.96; p = 0.0134) and those who had received ≥ 3 prior lines of treatment (HR, 1.37; 95% CI, 1.05–1.79; p = 0.0222) were at increased risk of mortality. Anlotinib monotherapy, as opposed to combination regimens, was also associated with inferior survival outcomes (HR, 1.57; 95% CI, 1.19–2.07; p = 0.0015). Biochemical parameters were notably predictive: higher levels of CAR were significantly correlated with reduced OS (HR, 1.15; 95% CI, 1.07–1.24; p = 0.0003), as were elevated CRP (HR, 1.00; 95% CI, 1.00–1.01; p = 0.0009) and lower albumin levels (HR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.91–0.95; p < 0.0001) (Table 2).

Association between CAR and overall survival in multivariable models

Multivariable Cox regression confirmed that higher CAR was independently associated with worse OS in patients receiving anlotinib. In the crude model, each unit increase in CAR corresponded to a 15% higher risk of death (HR, 1.15; 95% CI, 1.07–1.24; p = 0.0003). This association remained significant in the minimally adjusted model (HR, 1.15; 95% CI, 1.06–1.24; p = 0.0006) and persisted after full adjustment for demographic and clinical covariates (HR, 1.10; 95% CI, 1.01–1.21; p = 0.0313). Categorical analysis by tertiles showed a significant dose–response relationship: compared with the lowest tertile, the middle and highest CAR groups had increased mortality risks (HR: 1.47 and 2.02, respectively; p for trend < 0.0001) (Table 3).

Kaplan–Meier analysis of overall survival based on CAR stratification

To further characterize the prognostic implications of baseline CAR levels, we stratified the cohort into tertiles and performed Kaplan–Meier survival analysis (Fig. 2). Median OS declined progressively with increasing CAR: patients in the low CAR group exhibited a median OS of 25.96 months, compared to 14.78 months in the middle CAR group and 11.89 months in the high CAR group. These survival differences were statistically significant (log-rank test p < 0.0001).

Nonlinear association between CAR and overall survival

To further elucidate the relationship between the CAR and OS, we employed a two-piecewise linear regression model with a restricted cubic spline. The model revealed a significant nonlinear association between CAR and OS (log-likelihood ratio test p < 0.001), indicating a departure from simple linearity. As illustrated in the smoothing spline curve (Fig. 3), the risk of death increased sharply with rising CAR values up to a threshold of 2.12. Specifically, in patients with CAR ≤ 2.12 (n = 372), each unit increase in CAR was associated with a nearly twofold increase in mortality risk (HR, 1.92; 95% CI, 1.54–2.40; p < 0.0001). However, above this inflection point (CAR > 2.12; n = 45), the association plateaued and exhibited a slight inverse trend (HR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.68–1.00; p = 0.0501), suggesting a saturation effect (Table 4).

Sensitivity analysis

Subgroup analyses demonstrated that the association between elevated CAR and poorer OS was generally consistent across most strata (Fig. 4), with no significant interactions observed (all p for interaction > 0.05), except for previous targeted therapy (p = 0.0444) and previous immunotherapy (p = 0.0198). Notably, the prognostic impact of CAR was more pronounced in patients without prior targeted therapy and those with prior immunotherapy. We generated an E-value to assess the sensitivity to unmeasured confounding. The E-value for the multivariable estimate (HR = 1.10) was 1.43, and for the segmented effect below the inflection (HR = 1.92) was 3.25, indicating significant robustness to unmeasured confounders such as initial dose, other gene status, etc.

Discussion

This study is the first to evaluate the prognostic utility of the CAR in patients with advanced NSCLC receiving anlotinib therapy. Our findings demonstrate a robust and independent association between elevated baseline CAR and reduced OS. In multivariable Cox regression analysis, each one-unit increase in CAR was associated with a 15% higher risk of mortality (HR: 1.15, 95% CI: 1.08–1.23, p < 0.001). Kaplan–Meier survival curves further substantiated this relationship, revealing a markedly longer median OS among patients with lower CAR values. Beyond the observed linear association, GAM and two-piecewise linear regression identified a significant non-linear relationship between CAR and mortality. Specifically, an inflection point was observed at CAR = 2.12: below this threshold, OS declined steeply with increasing CAR (HR = 1.92, p < 0.001), whereas above the threshold, the survival curve plateaued, and the association lost statistical significance (HR = 0.82, p = 0.0501). These results indicate that when CAR is below 2.12, each unit increase corresponds to a striking 92% elevation in mortality risk. Notably, the “non-significant protective trend” above CAR = 2.12 should not be interpreted as a causal benefit of higher CAR. First, the sample size in this upper segment is small (n = 45), yielding a wide confidence interval that borders the null (upper CI ≈1.00), which is compatible with a chance finding. Second, biological “saturation/plateau” is plausible: at very high inflammatory-nutritional burden, incremental increases in CAR may add little prognostic information because outcomes are already dominated by severe systemic inflammation, cachexia, or intercurrent illness [17].

CRP is an acute-phase reactant predominantly synthesized by hepatocytes in response to inflammatory stimuli. By activating the complement cascade and modulating innate immune responses [18], CRP serves not only as a sensitive indicator of systemic inflammation but also as a clinically valuable prognostic marker across diverse malignancies, including pancreatic [19], urothelial [20], and hepatocellular carcinomas [21]. Albumin, another major liver-derived plasma protein, is essential for maintaining oncotic pressure, regulating fluid distribution, and enabling the transport of various endogenous and exogenous molecules [22]. In oncology, elevated serum CRP levels have consistently been associated with poorer survival outcomes in patients with lung cancer [23–25], while hypoalbuminemia has been recognized as an adverse prognostic factor in NSCLC [26]. The CAR, which integrates inflammatory status with nutritional reserve, offers a more comprehensive reflection of host–tumor interactions than either marker alone [27]. Accumulating evidence across multiple cancer types has established the CAR as an independent prognostic biomarker [28].

Our findings are consistent with previous reports. In a retrospective analysis of 436 patients with advanced NSCLC, Ni et al. demonstrated that patients in the low-CAR group had significantly longer OS than those in the high-CAR group, identifying CAR as an independent predictor of OS in this population [15]. Similarly, Yang et al. prospectively evaluated CRP, albumin, and CAR as prognostic indicators for NSCLC, reporting that elevated CAR was strongly associated with increased mortality risk, and that CAR outperformed CRP and Albmin alone in prognostic discrimination [29]. While most prior studies have described a linear association between CAR and OS, none have examined potential non-linear patterns. In the present study, we applied GAM and two-piecewise linear regression, fitting regression splines to nonparametric smoothed data, to explore the shape of the CAR-OS relationship in greater depth. This analysis revealed a distinct threshold effect within the apparent linear trend. Using a recursive algorithm, we identified an inflection point at CAR = 2.12. Below this threshold, each one-unit increase in CAR was associated with a striking 92% higher risk of death, whereas above this point, the association weakened and lost statistical significance. These findings suggest that elevated CAR values beyond this limit confer only marginal additional prognostic information. Clinically, this underscores the importance of identifying patients with mildly to moderately elevated CAR, in whom timely interventions (dietary consultation, exercise/rehabilitation, infection workup when appropriate) may still meaningfully alter the disease trajectory.

Subgroup analyses confirmed the robustness of CAR’s prognostic value across diverse patient subsets, including age, sex, tumor stage, smoking status, comorbidities, and genetic status. Prior studies have shown that anlotinib exhibits antitumor activity across diverse molecular contexts [30]. Consistent with this, our results indicate that the association between CAR and OS is not modified by molecular subtype. It is necessary in the future to conduct prospective cohort studies with comprehensive genetic typing to verify the prognostic value of CAR within specific genomic subgroups. Interestingly, interaction testing revealed that CAR’s impact was more pronounced in patients without prior targeted therapy (p-interaction = 0.0444) and in those with a history of immunotherapy (p-interaction = 0.0198). This may reflect the interplay between inflammatory burden and therapeutic responsiveness. In the immunotherapy context, high CAR may indicate an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment less conducive to checkpoint blockade efficacy. Conversely, targeted therapy outcomes may be more closely tied to molecular alterations than to systemic host factors, potentially attenuating CAR’s predictive influence in those populations.

Our findings carry notable clinical implications. First, CAR is an inexpensive and widely accessible biomarker derived from routine blood tests. Its strong discriminatory capacity for prognostic stratification offers direct and practical value for risk assessment in advanced non-small cell lung cancer. Second, the non-linear curve implies that timely recognition and nutritional/inflammatory modulation may be most beneficial in the early to moderate elevation range.

Despite these strengths, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the retrospective design inherently introduces the potential for selection bias and residual confounding (for example, specific information about gene subtypes). Although multiple covariates were adjusted for, unmeasured factors may still have influenced the observed associations. We used E-value sensitivity analyses to quantify the potential effects of unmeasured confounders and found that unmeasured confounders were unlikely to explain the observed associations. Second, CAR was assessed only at baseline; longitudinal measurements during treatment could yield dynamic prognostic insights and should be investigated in future prospective studies. Third, although our cohort was derived from two tertiary research centers, external validation in more diverse populations is essential to confirm the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusions

Baseline CAR is a robust, non-linear and independent prognostic marker in anlotinib-treated advanced NSCLC, with a steep risk gradient below an inflection near CAR ≈ 2.12 and a plateau at higher levels. These features support CAR for baseline risk stratification-particularly to flag patients with mild-to-moderate elevations for early inflammatory/nutritional optimization and closer follow-up. Prospective multi-center studies should validate the threshold, assess serial/dynamic CAR, and test CAR-guided care pathways to determine impact on outcomes.
