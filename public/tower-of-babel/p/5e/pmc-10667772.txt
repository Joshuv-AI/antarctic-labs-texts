A unique case of anterior cruciate ligament rupture coexisting with asymptomatic tear of discoid medial meniscus: A case report

Published: 2023

Introduction

The knee consisted of various structures in order to perform its function. Among these structures are the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and the meniscus. Both structures play their role in maintaining the knee stability and proprioception, with menisci also taking part in load distribution [1].

ACL is one of the most commonly injured structures in the knee. It is estimated that 200,000 individuals in the United States suffered from ACL rupture each year [2]. Around 40 % to 60 % of these patients also suffered from a concomitant injury to the meniscus [3,4]. However, such cases involving a discoid meniscus, especially discoid medial meniscus is a very rare occurrence. Previous literatures of discoid medial meniscus without ACL rupture used various methods ranging from debridement and saucerization; to partial meniscectomy, and meniscoplasty [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. In the presence of ACL rupture, meniscectomy in conjunction with ACL reconstruction is still the most commonly performed method [9]. We present our case of discoid medial meniscus tear and ACL rupture treated with partial meniscectomy and ACL reconstruction in accordance to SCARCE guideline [10].

Presentation of case

A young adult 30 years old male visited our orthopaedic clinic with chief complain of persisting instability in the left knee after badminton injury two months prior. This is especially disturbing for the patient as he climbs up and down the stairs and hindering him from playing any sport, especially badminton. The patient did not seek for any professional medical treatment immediately after the injury. Upon physical examination, Lachman and anterior drawer test showed positive result followed by Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) of the knee revealed ACL rupture and double PCL sign indicating the presence not only a tear in the anterior cruciate ligament, but also, a tear in the medial meniscus (Fig. 1). There were neither a history of locked knee, nor a tender joint line and there was no sign of meniscus involvement (McMurray, Apley, and Thessaly) in the physical examination. Regarding pain, the patient only complained of slight discomfort with VAS of 3/10. The calculation of The Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) was 66.

Coronal and sagittal slice of the left knee MRI showing sign of ACL rupture and double PCL sign. Double PCL sign occurs as the displaced fragment of the bucket handled tear of medial meniscus lies in front and parallel to the PCL. Double PCL sign has been considered as a reliable sign of bucket handle tear of the medial meniscus [11].

We had planned for arthroscopic ACL reconstruction procedure. As we performed diagnostic arthroscopy, beside the previously known ACL rupture, we also tried to identify the type of meniscus tear presented. Eventually, we identified the presence of full-thickness long-standing bucket-handle tear of the discoid-shaped medial meniscus (Fig. 2). We, therefore, perform partial meniscectomy by resected the torn-part while maintaining the peripheral rim for about 6 mm, able to achieve a stable peripheral semilunar rim of meniscus. Afterwards a thorough examination of the meniscus condition was conducted before proceeding to ACL reconstruction (Fig. 3).

Bucket-handled tear of the discoid medial meniscus.

After resection of the torn discoid medial meniscus.

Arthroscopic ACL reconstruction was then performed using hamstring graft (7 mm diameter and 9 cm in length), a 20 cm endobutton, and 9 mm bioscrew (Fig. 4). Afterwards, we conducted impingement test in both mid-extension and full-extension position (Fig. 5). After completion, both Lachman and anterior drawer test were performed showing negative results. There were no intra- and post-operative complications.

After ACL reconstruction.

Anterior impingement test in full-extension position.

For the first three weeks post-operative, the patient was encouraged to perform range-of-motion exercise from 0 to 90° and non-weight-bearing walking aided with a posterior long knee brace. Gradual progression to full range of motion and partial weight bearing were closely monitored. Exactly four weeks after the surgery, patient was allowed to walk with full weight bearing. Rehabilitation program also included strength, proprioception, and coordination training that resulted in return to preinjury activities six months after the procedure. One year after the procedure, there was no further complains of instability, locking-knee, and pain with 0 VAS score and KOOS 92.

Discussion

The menisci are semilunar fibrocartilaginous discs located within the knee [11]. They serve to distribute forces equally across the joint surface, stabilize the contact between femur and tibia, aid in joint proprioception and lubrication [12].

Discoid meniscus of the knee is a dysmorphic meniscus that widely covers the articular surface of the tibial plateau due to its abnormally wide and thick shape [7,13]. Due to the increase in meniscal thickness, the stress affecting the knee is distributed differently than the one with normal meniscus [14]. Yet, the difference between discoid meniscus and normal meniscus does not exist merely in its gross anatomy, but also to its ultrastructure [15]. The collagen fiber within the discoid meniscus is more disorganized compared to normal meniscus [13,14]. Both of these differences, also take part in making discoid meniscus to have an inferior mechanical property and more susceptible to injury, in comparison to normal meniscus [16].

Discoid medial meniscus was first described in 1941 by Cave and Staples [17]. In the early 21st century, there were only fewer than 70 cases that had been reported in English literature (Pinar). In 1982 Dickason et al. [18] performed a retrospective study by examining 14,731 menisci and it was found that among 8040 medial menisci, only 10 were discoid menisci making the incidence of discoid medial meniscus was at 0.12 % compared to 1.5 % of discoid lateral meniscus. Among these discoid menisci, only one had bilateral involvement making the incidence to be even at 0.012 %. Another study performed described that the incidence of discoid meniscus between 0.4 % and 5 % in Caucasian population [19] in comparison to 9.1 % to 16.6 % in Asian population [20]. An epidemiological study by Randhawa et al. [21] found that 56.6 % patients with discoid meniscus were female with the median age of diagnosis at 29 years old, significantly younger than normal patients presented to the clinic for meniscal problems (p < 0.001).

Most of patients with discoid medial meniscus tear will frequently present with medial knee pain, iterative effusion, and locking in flexion. The first two symptoms are more likely to be present due to the tear in the meniscus, while the locking may be due to the discoid-shaped meniscus [8].

The discoid medial meniscus can be diagnosed from MRI examination of the knee. The presence of continuity between the anterior horn and posterior horn of the meniscus in three or more consecutive, 5-mm thick, sagittal images is pathognomonic for discoid meniscus. This diagnosis is further confirmed with an abnormally thick meniscus that may extend to the intercondylar groove seen in the coronal view [[22], [23], [24]]. MRI examination can also reveal some abnormalities such as: meniscal cyst formation, widened medial joint space, proximal mediotibial collapse, squared femoral condyle, anomalous ACL attachment, and discoid lateral meniscus of the same knee [25].

Currently, there is no widely accepted classification of discoid medial meniscus. Nevertheless, based on the anterior horn insertion, Flouzat-Lachaniette et al. [8] described three groups, which are normal, deficient insertion of the anterior horn onto the tibia with continuity of the anterior horn and anterior intermeniscal ligament over the ACL, and anterior horn in continuity with ACL.

An asymptomatic discoid meniscus should be treated conservatively [5]. Whereas a torn discoid medial meniscus should be managed with surgery due to a greater stress subjected on it from screw home mechanism, its less mobility, and transfer of weight-bearing to the medial side of the knee making the patient more prone to discomfort, giving way, locking, and pain [7]. The type of surgery mostly depends on tear pattern, size, location, and the quality of the remnant meniscal tissue [26]. Meniscectomy is a procedure to remove the meniscus, whether totally, subtotally, or partially. Total meniscectomy was the reference treatment in the past. However, its association with early medial compartment osteoarthritis is causing it to be opted out [8]. Partial meniscectomy or known as saucerization involves excision of the central anomalous discoid meniscus and preservation of the stable peripheral with its size depending on the degree of torn meniscus [5,7]. Its goal is to restore a semilunar meniscus, therefore, distribute femorotibial stress more harmoniously [8]. Meniscectomy showed a satisfactory short and medium-term pain control and restoration of everyday function [8]. Long term result, however, is still poorly characterized due to its rarity [25]. Meniscus repair could be the option of treatment, if the tear is located in the peripheral vascular zone [2]. Yet, there is a risk of repair failure for a complex tear and bucket-handle tear requiring reoperation, especially in the short term [27]. Currently, meniscectomy is the most performed method of surgery to treat meniscus tear coexisting with ACL rupture with 63 % of medial side tear treated with meniscectomy, followed by repair by 27 %, and left in situ with just 9% [9]. In our case, the bucket-handle type injury of the discoid medial meniscus was managed by partial meniscectomy. This procedure was performed prior to ACL reconstruction in order to gain a better visualization of the joint. Previous literatures regarding simultaneous surgery of meniscus and ACL did not include any case of discoid medial meniscus tear [9,27].

Conclusion

Discoid medial meniscus is a very rare condition in the knee, especially presenting with ACL rupture. A thorough diagnostic arthroscopic examination is a must prior to any arthroscopic procedure of the knee. Partial meniscectomy by complete removal of the torn discoid meniscus provides good access and prevents impingement for the ACL reconstruction procedure while reducing the risk of graft failure and promoting better functional outcome.
