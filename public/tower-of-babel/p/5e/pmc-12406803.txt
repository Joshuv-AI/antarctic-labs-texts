Mediating role of health locus of control in supportive care needs and health promoting behaviours among patients with tuberculosis in China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Chang Gao, Fanling Li, Wenxuan Ding, Ying Zhang, Jingjun Zhang, Xiaomei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo explore the mediating role of health locus of control in the relationship between supportive care needs and health promoting behaviours among patients with pulmonary tuberculosis.DesignA cross-sectional study.SettingThis survey was conducted in three tuberculosis-designated hospitals in Xi’an, China, between March and May 2020.ParticipantsA total of 294 participants completed the questionnaires.Outcome measuresThe participants completed a self-designed socio-demographic questionnaire, the Multidimensional Health Locus of Control scale, the Supportive Care Needs Scale for Patients with Tuberculosis and the Health Promoting Lifestyle Profile II. SPSS V.26.0 and Mplus were used for data analysis.ResultsThe patients’ supportive care needs had a negative association with their health promoting behaviours (r=−0.58, p<0.001). The supportive care needs were negatively related to internal health locus of control (r=−0.43, p<0.001), and positively related to chance health locus of control (r=0.44, p<0.001) and powerful others health locus of control (r=0.20, p<0.001). Health-promoting behaviours had a significantly positive correlation with internal health locus of control (r=0.49, p<0.001) and a negative correlation with chance health locus of control (r=−0.36, p<0.001). Both internal health locus of control and chance health locus of control acted as mediators between supportive care needs and health promoting behaviours, while powerful others health locus of control did not.ConclusionHealth locus of control mediated the relationship between supportive care needs and health promoting behaviours. A high level of internal health locus of control and a low level of chance health locus of control may help to improve health-promoting behaviours. Given the cross-sectional design of this study, future experimental interventions targeting health locus of control are needed to establish causal relationships with supportive care needs and health promoting behaviours.

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) is a preventable and usually curable disease. Yet in 2022, TB was the second leading cause of death from a single infectious agent worldwide, with 7.5 million people newly diagnosed with TB, making it the highest number since the mid-1990s.1 China is one of 30 high TB burden countries, with TB cases accounting for 7.1% of the world.1 TB remains a major global health problem, especially in countries with a low Socio-demographic Index.2

Health promotion is ‘the process of enabling people to increase control over, and to improve their health’.3 Health promoting behaviours (HPB) are crucial for maintaining and improving health.4 They ultimately aim to achieve positive health outcomes for the individual and, when integrated into a healthy lifestyle, lead to improved functioning and quality of life.5 Based on Pender’s Health Promotion Model, HPB is conceptualised in six dimensions: health responsibility, physical activity, nutrition, interpersonal relations, spiritual growth and stress management.5 TB is a disease that is closely related to behavioural and environmental factors, such as smoking, alcohol abuse and dependency, malnutrition, crowding and indoor air pollution.6 Engaging in HPB, such as adhering to medication regimens, eating a balanced diet, avoiding alcohol and tobacco and practicing good hygiene, can greatly improve treatment outcomes and prevent the development of drug-resistant TB strains.710 By prioritising HPB, patients with TB can recover more effectively and enhance their overall well-being. However, little is known about the underlying patterns of interactions behind HPB in patients with TB and the most effective ways to intervene.

Patients with TB usually suffer not only from fatigue, weight loss and night sweats,11 but also from depression, anxiety, isolation and a sense of hopelessness.12 In addition, they encounter challenges such as medication side effects, adherence to treatment and risk of drug resistance.13 All of these create a wide range of unmet supportive care needs (SCN).14 SCN refer to the various assistance and services required by an individual to cope with the impacts of disease diagnosis, treatment, relief and deterioration, encompassing physical, emotional, social, psychological, spiritual, informational and practical needs.15 Moreover, patient-centred care, which is the first pillar of the WHO’s End TB Strategy, calls for the diverse and unique needs of each patient.16 The initial step in implementing health promotion is to assess the patient’s needs for health promoting activities. Understanding patients’ needs, especially their SCN, enables better health promotion interventions, which in turn lead to a higher quality of life.17

Health locus of control (HLC) refers to people’ beliefs that their health is or is not determined by their behaviours.18 People attribute their health outcomes to either internal or external sources. People with a higher internal HLC believe that their behaviour determines their health, whereas people with a stronger external HLC believe that their health is controlled by chance, or other people. These differences arise from people’s belief about the causes and consequences of the events they have experienced. HLC is significantly related to patients’ needs. A higher level of internal HLC and a lower level of external HLC were associated with fewer unmet needs.19 Researchers also identified that patients with cancer with a higher level of external HLC were more likely to use information sources and have additional information needs. In contrast, no association was found between internal HLC and information needs.20

HLC also plays a crucial role to understand or predict patients’ HPB. There are generally three types of HLC: internal (IHLC), chance (CHLC) and powerful others (PHLC).18 IHLC refers to the belief that health outcomes are primarily determined by one’s own behaviours. In the context of TB, patients with high IHLC are more likely to take responsibility for disease management, adhere to medication and adopt infection control measures. CHLC represents the belief that health is largely governed by fate, luck or other uncontrollable forces. Among patients with TB, a strong CHLC may lead to fatalism and reduced engagement in treatment, potentially compromising outcomes. PHLC reflects the belief that health is significantly influenced by powerful others, particularly healthcare professionals or family members. Patients with TB who have high PHLC may rely heavily on doctors or nurses for guidance and may demonstrate good adherence when strong support from healthcare providers is present.18 Previous research has found that HPB was positively associated with IHLC and PHLC, while CHLC had negative or no relationship with HPB.21 22 In addition, perceived control of health is also considered as a cognitive-perceptual factor to predict health behaviours in the original Health Promotion Model.5

To date, there have been limited studies examining the mediating factors between needs of patients with TB and HPB. Even though the HLC has been investigated and found to be associated with both HPB and patient needs, the role of HLC in the relationship remains unknown. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between HLC, SCN and HPB, especially to determine whether HLC and SCN are associated with HPB and whether HLC mediates the relationship between SCN and HPB. Materials and methods

Participants and procedure

A cross-sectional study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement.23 This study employed a convenience sampling method. We first chose three TB-designated hospitals in Xi’an, which were willing to collaborate and had the operational capacity during the COVID-19 pandemic. They were located in different administrative districts of Xi’an, and were considered representative of the 17 TB-designated hospitals in the city. Then, eligible patients were consecutively recruited from these participating hospitals from March to May 2020. They were approached during their clinical visits or inpatient treatment. Patients were included if they were: (1) diagnosed with pulmonary TB; (2) aged 18 years or above; (3) currently receiving anti-TB treatment; (4) able to read, write and communicate. Patients were excluded if they had severe complications or comorbidities, cognitive impairment or mental disorders or other conditions that might affect their participation.

According to the study by Weston and Gore,24 structural equation modelling requires at least 200 participants. The sample size for the linear multiple regression analysis was calculated using G*Power V.3.1.9.7. Based on the effect size (f2) of 0.04 (small to medium), a power (1-β) of 0.80, an alpha level (α) of 0.05 and three predictors, the minimum required sample size was 277. A total of 300 questionnaires were distributed to eligible participants; however, 6 were not completed. Consequently, 294 patients with TB were included in the final analysis.

With the help of the head nurses of the departments at the three TB-designated hospitals, we trained six clinical nurses in the data collection process. The questionnaires were launched on Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn/), an online platform for data collection and management. The trained nurses approached the potential participants face-to-face and explained the aim, method and procedure of this study. After obtaining verbal consent from the patients, the trained nurses asked the patients to scan the QR code and carefully read the instructions about the detailed information of this survey. Then, if the patients agreed to proceed, they would give digital informed consent and complete the questionnaire on site. The research team checked the collected questionnaires for accuracy and integrity. All data were properly stored both online and offline.

Patients and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Measures

Socio-demographic and disease-related variables

Socio-demographic variables, such as age, gender, marital status, monthly family income, education level, place of residence, employment status and medical insurance, were collected. Disease-related variables included treatment duration and treatment mode. Among them, age, education level, place of residence, employment status and medical insurance were considered covariates as they were found to be influential factors in HPB and HLC in preliminary analysis (online supplemental material 1).

Health locus of control

HLC was measured using Form A of the Multidimensional Health Locus of Control (MHLC) scale.18 The MHLC consists of three 6-item dimensions: IHLC, CHLC and PHLC. Each item was rated on a 6-point Likert, with 1 indicating strongly disagree and 6 indicating strongly agree. The total score ranges from 6 to 36. The dimension with the highest score is taken as the centre of HLC. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of the Chinese version of the MHLC are 0.80 for the IHLC, 0.75 for the CHLC and 0.83 for the PHLC.25

Supportive care needs

Supportive Care Needs Scale for Patients with Tuberculosis (SCN-TB) was used to assess patients’ need level over the past week.26 The development of the SCN-TB was guided by the Supportive Care Needs Framework,15 which has been widely used to evaluate the unmet SCN of patients with different diseases, such as cancer, HIV and stroke.26 The SCN-TB contains five domains of needs: physical, psycho-emotional, informational, social and practical. Each domain has five items, with a total of 25 items. Items are rated on a 5-point response scale (1=no need, not applicable; 2=no need, satisfied; 3=low need; 4=moderate need; 5=high need). The total score ranges from 25 to 125, with higher scores indicating more unmet SCN of patients with TB.

Health promoting behaviours

The Health Promoting Lifestyle Profile II (HPLP-II) was developed by Walker and colleagues based on Pender’s Health Promotion Model to evaluate HPB.27 HPLP-II consists of 52 items in 6 domains, namely self-actualisation (9 items), health responsibility (9 items), exercise (8 items), nutrition (9 items), interpersonal support (9 items) and stress management (8 items). It is a 4-point Likert scale (1=never; 2=sometimes; 3=often; 4=routinely), with a total score ranging from 52 to 208. The higher scores indicate better individual HPB. The Chinese version of HPLP-II had good reliability and validity, and has been applied in various populations.2830

Statistical analyses

We used SPSS V.26.0 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA) and Mplus software31 to analyse the data. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Harman’s single-factor test was employed to check the data for common method bias. Descriptive analyses were conducted to outline the socio-demographic profile of the patients. Pearson correlation tests were used to examine the relationship between HLC, SCN and HPB. To test the mediation effect, a bias-corrected non-parametric percentile bootstrap method was used. Repeated sampling was performed 5000 times32 to obtain the bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The presence of a mediating effect was determined if the 95% CI did not include value zero. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Mediation analysis

After controlling for age, medical insurance, education level, place of residence and employment status, the parallel mediation analysis evaluated the roles of IHLC, CHLC and PHLC in the relationship between SCN and HPB. The goodness-of-fit indices of the mediation model suggested a well-fitting model (χ2/df=1.68, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA)=0.04, Comparative Fit Index (CFI)=0.96, Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI)=0.93, Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR)=0.05). Figure 2 and table 3 showed the effect of the SCN on HPB through IHLC, CHLC and PHLC. The total effect and direct effect of SCN on HPB were statistically significant (β=−0.573, 95% CI (−0.780 to –0.388), p<0.001; β=−0.407, 95% CI (−0.636 to –0.205), p<0.001). The total indirect effect accounted for 28.97% of the total effect (β=−0.166, 95% CI (−0.259 to –0.079), p<0.001). Results indicated SCN was associated with reduction in IHLC (β=−0.436, p<0.001). In turn, patients with a higher IHLC reported better HPB (β=0.282, p<0.001). IHLC played a mediating role in SCN on HPB (β=−0.123, 95% CI (−0.191 to –0.068), p<0.001). SCN was positively related to CHLC (β=0.441, p<0.001). However, individuals with a higher CHLC reported worse HPB (β=−0.115, p=0.024). CHLC mediated the relationship between SCN and HPB (β=−0.051, 95% CI (−0.103 to –0.007), p=0.036). Patients with more SCN were inclined to have a higher PHLC. PHLC was not significantly associated with HPB (β=0.038, p=0.370). This result revealed that PHLC was not a mediator (β=0.008, 95% CI (−0.007 to –0.030), p=0.401). Furthermore, the mediation effect of IHLC was stronger than that of CHLC.

Discussion

This study investigated a parallel mediating model to examine the relationship between SCN, HLC and HPB. The results indicated that SCN scores were negatively related to HPB. Both IHLC and CHLC acted as mediators between SCN and HPB, while PHLC did not.

The current findings showed that patients with TB who had high levels of SCN performed more poorly on the HPB. High levels of SCN suggested that patients lacked sufficient resource and support to cope with and adapt to their current disease situation. Such unmet needs are often accompanied by psychological distress, including depression and anxiety, which can undermine patients’ self-efficacy in managing their condition and reduce their motivation to engage in HPB.3436 In addition, patients with high levels of SCN often experience inadequate support from family, friends or healthcare providers.35 Social support is a critical facilitator of healthy behaviours—for example, through shared physical activity or medication reminders—and its absence can further hinder patients’ adherence to HPB.36 Moreover, higher SCN levels are commonly linked to poorer quality of life,37 which can serve as a significant barrier to initiating and maintaining HPB.17

Patients with TB who had lower SCN levels were more likely to have stronger IHLC, weaker PHLC and CHLC, which was consistent with a previous study of patients with asthma.19 However, the unmet needs of patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD) were positively related to IHLC, as well as PHLC which included doctor HLC and other people HLC.38 As PD progresses, the patients gradually lose independence in daily activities and control over the disease, which undermines their IHLC. When patients with PD report more unmet needs, it means that they are actively coping with their disease and regaining a sense of control, an indication of an increase in IHLC. In comparison, patients with TB or asthma who had higher IHLC would improve their health status through their own actions and efforts, resulting in lower needs. This finding indicated that patients’ health beliefs may be influenced and shaped by their experience of illness. Moreover, these beliefs about health subsequently influenced their needs. For patients with strong IHLC, adequate information and support should be provided to guide their health behaviours. Conversely, for patients with strong external HLC, healthcare professionals should be more involved in the disease management and help to build their confidence in controlling the disease.

Moreover, HPB was positively related to IHLC and negatively associated with CHLC. PHLC had a positive but not significant correlation with HPB. Berglund39 and colleagues reported that IHLC was positively related to self-rated health, while CHLC and PHLC were negatively related to self-rated health. Another multicentre study showed both PHLC and IHLC positively predicted the health behaviours of organ transplant recipients, while CHLC had a positive but not significant relationship with health behaviours.40 Therefore, IHLC were believed to be a positive predictor of health outcomes, which was also consistent with theoretical assumption.41 As to external HLC, it may have either positive or negative effects. In our study, patients with TB had a higher CHLC, which may be related to the complex process and unpredictable prognosis of the disease. This led to a strong negative stress response in the patients, resulting in a low level of their HPB.42 Additionally, the fact that PHLC was not significantly correlated with HPB may be due to insufficient mobilisation of individual initiative. Further research could explore the conditions under which external HLC has a positive effect, and use this information to guide the development of HLC-related interventions.

The results showed that IHLC and CHLC partially mediated the relationship between SCN and HPB of patients with TB. HLC is a personality trait that can be changed through life experience, education and therapy.43 A randomized controlled trial found that an educational intervention reduced CHLC and improved IHLC, leading to improved mental health and prevention of postpartum depression in mothers in Iran.44 Also, the behavioural therapy was found to reduce PHLC, and increase IHLC among patients with panic disorder and/or agoraphobia.43 Therefore, healthcare providers should pay more attention to health beliefs of patients with TB and select appropriate methods to provide support and guidance, ultimately facilitating the implementation of HPB. Finally, although the mediating effect of PHLC was not significant in this study, the sign reversal phenomenon suggested that PHLC may have a suppression effect on the relationship between SCN and HPB. Future studies should thoroughly explore the role of healthcare professionals in the formation of patients’ health behaviours and identify the optimal intervention timing in doctor/nurse–patient interactions.

Implications for practice

The findings suggested SCN had a negative influence on HPB through IHLC and CHLC. Therefore, the following recommendations are proposed to improve HPB. First, a high level of SCN was associated with poor HPB among patients with TB. Healthcare providers should identify patients’ unmet needs in a timely manner and provide support and guidance to create conditions for the implementation of HPB. Second, HLC is a modifiable psychological trait and a predictor of HPB in patients with TB. On the one hand, interventions targeting HLC could improve IHLC and other health-related outcomes.45 On the other hand, adopting appropriate strategies for patients with different types of HLC may promote the progress in the implementation of HPB. Finally, whether PHLC has a positive relationship with health-related outcomes is still unknown and controversial. Further research is needed to find out the underlying mechanism.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, participants were recruited from three TB-designated hospitals in Xi’an, using a convenience sample. Although the current sample size met the requirements for statistical analysis, the representativeness of the sample was limited, which affects the generalisability of our findings. In the future, multicentre studies should be conducted to validate these results. Second, we cannot determine causal relationships between variables due to the nature of a cross-sectional study. Cohort studies should be considered for future research. Third, due to the anonymous design and lack of follow-up mechanisms, we were unable to collect reasons for questionnaire non-completion. This limited our ability to assess potential barriers to participation. Future studies should consider capturing this information.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that HLC mediated the relationship between SCN and HPB among patients with TB. IHLC and CHLC acted as mediators between SCN and HPB, while PHLC did not. This study offered a new insight into understanding the relationship between needs and health behaviours by introducing the concept of HLC. Considering that HLC was a modifiable variable, future interventions targeting HLC could be implemented to fulfil the unmet needs and promote health behaviours of patients with TB.
