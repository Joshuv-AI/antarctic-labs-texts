Introduction

Takotsubo cardiomyopathy (TC) is a clinical syndrome defined by transient and reversible left ventricular systolic dysfunction of the myocardium, which mimics acute myocardial infarction but occurs in the absence of angiographic evidence of obstructive coronary artery disease[1].

TC is significantly more prevalent in women compared to men and predominantly affects older adults. While its exact pathophysiology remains uncertain, excessive catecholamine release, often triggered by severe emotional or physical stress, is believed to play a key role in its development[2]. The most common presenting symptoms of TC are acute substernal chest pain, dyspnea, and syncope, also the diagnosis of TC is based on seven diagnostic criteria which include morphological characteristics, ECG alterations, cardiac biomarkers, and reversibility of the myocardial dysfunction[3].

We describe a case of a 27-year-old woman who had TC with tonic-clonic seizures as the initial presentation. She had no prior medical conditions other than irritable bowel syndrome. This study adheres to the TITAN 2025 Guidelines on AI declaration and usage[4].

Case presentation

A 27-year-old woman presented to the emergency department after a 2-minute generalized tonic-clonic seizure, which resolved spontaneously. It was associated with tongue biting, fecal and urinary incontinence, followed by 1 hour of confusion. She reported recent emotional stress from a divorce and lives alone. Her medical history includes irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and an eyelid lift cosmetic procedure. She denies alcohol, tobacco, or drug use, and has no history of seizures, coronary artery disease, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, or regular medication use.

Her blood pressure was 70/40 mmHg, pulse 90/min and weak, with peripheral oxygen saturation of 93% on room air. Physical examination was unremarkable except for bilateral positive Babinski’s reflex. Routine labs, including CBC (reference ranges; hemoglobin: 12.0–15.5 g/dL white blood cell: 4000–11 000 cells/µL, platelet: 150 000–450 000 cells/µL), metabolic panel (reference ranges; glucose: 70–100 mg/dL, sodium: 135–145 mEq/L, potassium: 3.5–5.0 mEq/L, blood urea nitrogen: 7–20 mg/dL, creatinine: 0.6–1.3 mg/dL), and thyroid function tests (reference ranges: thyroid stimulating hormone: 0.4–4.0 mIU/L, free thyroxine: 0.8–1.8 ng/dL), were normal. Upon regaining consciousness, the patient reported new psychiatric trauma and severe retrosternal chest pain preceding the seizure. The pain, described as squeezing and radiating to the left shoulder, neck, and jaw, lasted 2 hours and was accompanied by diaphoresis, pallor, nausea, and a single episode of vomiting.

ECG showed ST elevation in the anterior leads (V1–V6) (Fig. 1), and echocardiography revealed apical hypokinesia, reduced systolic function, and the classic ballooning appearance of the left ventricle with an EF of 43% (Fig. 2). Troponin T was significantly elevated at 2 ng/ml (reference range: <0.014 ng/mL), and chest X-ray findings were normal.Figure 1.Typical ST-segment elevation in the anterior leads (V1–V6). A cardiac consultation was conducted for suspected STEMI, and the patient was started on antiplatelet therapy and heparin, then admitted to the ICU. A brain CT excluded intracranial hemorrhage. Due to the unavailability of coronary angiography, the patient received streptokinase. By the second day, she showed clinical improvement with a normal ECG and echocardiogram. Coronary angiography later confirmed normal coronary arteries and a diagnosis of TC.

She remained stable without any complications and was discharged home on day 6. At a two-week follow-up, she continued to be asymptomatic with no recurrence of cardiovascular events.

Discussion

TC has an incidence of 15–30 cases per 100 000 annually in the United States and Europe. It predominantly affects postmenopausal women, with approximately 90% of cases occurring in women, and a mean age of 66–68 years across major registries[5]. Emotional or physical stress can trigger stress cardiomyopathy. However, in some cases, the condition arises unexpectedly without prior warning. This case most commonly affects postmenopausal women[5]. In contrast to this, our case involved a 27-year-old patient.

According to the study of Medina et al, TC is associated with other risk factors, including diabetes mellitus, asthma exacerbations, cannabis use disorder, and certain neurological events like subarachnoid hemorrhage and ischemic stroke[5]. None of these were found in our patient, except for the presence of irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and an emotional trigger, as she had experienced a recent divorce.

The clinical features of TC are consistent across various studies and typically involve the sudden onset of ischemic-like chest pain or dyspnea, transient regional wall motion abnormalities in the apical and mid-ventricular segments, mild elevations in cardiac enzymes and biomarkers, and ST-segment changes on electrocardiography, approximately 33.3% of 15 patients, were found to have seizures associated with the condition[3,6].

As a pathological mechanism, some studies suggest a potential role for catecholamine release induced by emotional stress[1], which may impact central nervous system activity and contribute to the occurrence of seizures, although no definitive relationship between seizures and TC has been established.

In our case, the patient initially presented with a seizure followed by chest pain. ECG revealed an ST-segment elevation, and echocardiography demonstrated heart hypokinesia along with the characteristic ballooning of the left ventricle. Additionally, troponin T levels were significantly elevated at 2 ng/ml.

As the diagnostic criteria require the absence of intracranial hemorrhage, to rule out cerebral hemorrhage or other anomalies, a brain CT scan was conducted to our patient; the results were unremarkable[7].

Since 98.8% of 2944 patients who received an invasive evaluation due to positive troponin test results, were diagnosed with either NSTEMI or STEMI whereas 1.2% were diagnosed with TC, coronary angiography revealed normal coronary arteries excluding other diagnosis and confirming of TC[8].

The aim of treatment is to provide supportive care to maintain life and reduce complications until full recovery, which typically occurs within a few weeks as reported in Medina et al study, so the patient was initiated on antiplatelet medication and therapeutic anticoagulation with heparin and received a therapeutic dose of streptokinase[5].

Our patient was still asymptomatic and had no recurrence of cardiovascular problems after 2-week follow-up, although patients experienced significant death and complication rates after the acute phase, with a 5.6% annual death rate and a 1.7% annual rate of stroke or transient ischemic attack[9].

Conclusion

This case underscores the atypical presentation of TC in a young patient, emphasizing the importance of recognizing the potential link between seizures and the condition, especially when triggered by emotional stress.
