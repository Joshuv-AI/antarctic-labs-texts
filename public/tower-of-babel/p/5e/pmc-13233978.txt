Deuterated perovskite for room temperature spin device
Authors: Xueli Yang, Haidi Liu, Yuexing Xia, Mingchao Shao, Yu Zhang, Ankang Guo, Wenkang Shi, Han Zhao, Rui Zhang, Kun Zheng, Lidan Guo, Ke Meng, Shunhua Hu, Yongli Yan, Xinfeng Liu, Yunqi Liu, Chuang Zhang, Xiangnan Sun, Yunlong Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Organic-inorganic hybrid perovskites (OIHPs) offer a promising alternative, combining strong spin-orbit coupling, high carrier mobility, and tunable optoelectronic properties. However, their potential for spintronic applications has been constrained by rapid spin relaxation, often attributed solely to the inorganic sublattice. Here, we demonstrate room-temperature spin transport in hybrid perovskites enabled by isotope engineering. Substituting hydrogen with deuterium in methylammonium lead iodide effectively suppresses hyperfine interactions (HFI), leading to a 2.6-fold increase in spin lifetime. As a result, CD₃ND₃PbI₃ exhibits a magnetocurrent (MC) ratio of 17.5% at room temperature, whereas conventional CH₃NH₃PbI₃ spin-valve devices show negligible MC response. A spin photovoltaic effect is also observed under ambient conditions, revealing a coupling between optical excitation and spin-polarized transport, and pointing toward new opportunities for light-addressable spintronic functionality. These findings not only revise the fundamental understanding of spin relaxation in hybrid materials, but also establish isotope engineering as a powerful strategy to access room-temperature spin functionality.

Introduction

Spintronics is a transformative field in post-Moore’s law information technology, offering advantages such as low power consumption, high-speed operation, and non-volatility, which enable more efficient data storage and processing techniques1–4. However, achieving stable spin polarization and efficient spin transport at room temperatures remains a considerable challenge5–9. Among emerging materials, OIHPs have garnered significant interest in spintronics due to their strong spin-orbit coupling (SOC), minimal defect states, and solution-processability10–13. Compared to conventional semiconductors, perovskites exhibit extended spin diffusion lengths and superior spin manipulation, positioning them as promising candidates for next-generation spintronic applications, such as spin optoelectronics and quantum computing14–18. Nonetheless, the rapid spin relaxation inherent in these materials restricts their practical implementation in devices. Achieving precise control over spin dynamics and realizing efficient spin transport at room temperature in perovskites is essential to advancing this field.

A prevailing assumption in the field is that spin transport in hybrid perovskites is governed primarily by the inorganic Pb–I sublattice19, with organic cations having a minimal effect on carrier transport as well as spin dynamics. This perspective arises from the strong spin-orbit coupling (SOC) of Pb atoms, which is believed to dominate spin relaxation through Elliott-Yafet and D’yakonov-Perel’ mechanisms20–24. The role of organic cations, such as HFI of hydrogen, has been largely overlooked25–27. In fact, magnetic interactions can mediate long-range spin coupling28,29, indicating that the organic cation sublattice may still influence spin dynamics, even though it does not directly participate in carrier transport. This oversight has hindered progress in understanding the complete spectrum of spin relaxation mechanisms in OIHPs, leaving the contribution of the organic sublattice to spin dynamics largely unexplored.

In this work, we demonstrate that efficient spin transport in hybrid perovskites can be realized at room temperature by addressing a previously overlooked relaxation pathway—HFI associated with the organic sublattice. By selectively substituting hydrogen with deuterium in methylammonium lead iodide, we suppress HFI, as revealed by transient spectroscopic measurements that show a clear extension of the longitudinal spin lifetime under ambient conditions. Furthermore, spin valve devices incorporating deuterated perovskites exhibit enhanced magnetoresistance signals, confirming improved spin transport performance at the device level. This approach reveals that organic cations, long regarded as structurally passive, play an active role in governing spin dynamics, and it offers a practical route to overcome the long-standing limitation of short spin lifetimes in these materials. Our findings emphasize the underestimated role of the nuclear spin environment and underscore the need for a holistic understanding of spin relaxation that includes both inorganic and organic contributions, which is crucial for advancing hybrid perovskite spintronics beyond that based on traditional inorganic and organic semiconductor materials.

Results

Spin valve device based on deuterated perovskite films

The modulation of spin transport dynamics by organic cations in OIHPs remains an open question. They not only influence the structural and electronic properties of hybrid perovskites but also may play a crucial role in spin relaxation mechanisms. Hyperfine interaction (HFI), in particular, represents a key source of spin decoherence, as it couples spin-polarized carriers and nuclear spins, leading to spin relaxation. A potential approach to suppressing HFI is deuteration, where hydrogen in the organic cation is replaced with deuterium30,31. Deuterium has a significantly smaller nuclear magnetic moment (μD = 0.8574μN) than hydrogen (μH = 2.7928μN), where μN is the nuclear magneton, and a different nuclear spin quantum number (ID = 1, IH = 1/2)32. As a result, the hyperfine coupling strength is expected to decrease upon deuteration. The hyperfine coupling strength in hydrogen is approximately 6.5 times greater than that in deuterium. This theoretical prediction is further supported by a comparison of their Larmor frequencies, which are directly proportional to the respective gyromagnetic ratios. Under the same magnetic field, hydrogen exhibits a Larmor frequency of 42.58 MHz, while deuterium exhibits 6.54 MHz, again yielding a ratio of approximately 6.5. This quantitative agreement reinforces the hypothesis that deuteration effectively suppresses HFI and may mitigate nuclear spin-induced spin relaxation (Fig.1a). In OIHPs, charge carriers primarily transport through the inorganic Pb–I framework and do not directly traverse the organic components35. However, due to the long-range nature of HFI—which can extend beyond 20Å36, much greater than the ~3 Å distance between the organic cations (e.g., MA and FA) and the Pb–I lattice37—carriers can still couple with hydrogen nuclei in the organic moieties. This implies that even though the transport path is confined to the inorganic sublattice, spin relaxation may still be significantly affected by the nuclear spins of organic cations. Importantly, such coupling does not require carriers to be statically localized on the organic sublattice. For mobile carriers, the nuclear-spin environment can be viewed as a fluctuating perturbation that is sampled during transport and scattering, which can contribute to spin dephasing/relaxation even when charge transport occurs in the inorganic backbone. To investigate this effect, we focus on methylammonium lead iodide, a prototypical hybrid perovskite where the organic cation plays a dominant role in modulating HFI. By replacing hydrogen with deuterium in the methylammonium cation, we examine whether the predicted suppression of HFI leads to measurable changes in spin relaxation dynamics. We first synthesized single crystals of CD₃ND₃PbI₃ and confirmed their crystal structure, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. UV-visible absorption and photoluminescence spectra of the perovskite thin films (Supplementary Fig. 2) revealed no significant changes upon deuteration, indicating that the substitution does not noticeably alter the electronic structure of the material. This observation is further supported by subsequent X-ray diffraction and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy data (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). However, the corresponding Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra exhibited pronounced differences. Under identical preparation and measurement conditions, the characteristic absorption peaks associated with C–H (2920 cm-1), N–H (3134, 3176, 1577, and 1465 cm-1), and CH₃–NH₃+ (1246 and 906 cm-1) were significantly weakened after deuteration, with partial shifts to lower frequencies leading to the appearance of new absorption peaks at 2600, 1142, and 760 cm-1, which are assigned to CH₃–NH₃+ (Supplementary Fig. 5). These results indicate that the molecular backbone structure and vibrational degrees of freedom remain unchanged after deuteration. The changes in the FT-IR spectra can be attributed to the suppression of vibrational frequencies and the lowering of molecular vibrational energy induced by deuteration39. This suppression of vibrational motion is further reflected in the photoluminescence behavior of the film: after deuteration, its lifetime increased from 128 ns to 133 ns (Supplementary Fig. 6), most likely as a result of reduced high-frequency vibrations, which in turn lowers the probability of non-radiative transitions. Collectively, these characterizations confirm that while deuteration induces clear vibrational modifications, the perovskite system remains structurally and optically unchanged, thus providing a reliable basis for subsequent investigation of its spin transport behavior.

To evaluate the influence of deuterated perovskites on spin dynamics, we employed a spin valve architecture, as illustrated in Fig. 1b. Co and NiFe were selected as ferromagnetic electrodes due to their high Curie temperatures ( > 800°C), enabling stable spin polarization and reliable operation at room temperature. However, the thermal and kinetic energy involved in electrode deposition can damage the spin transport layer, compromising interfacial integrity and spin transport efficiency. To mitigate this, we adopted a low-temperature deposition process to preserve the interface quality40. A 20 nm Co bottom electrode was first deposited on a SiO₂ substrate, serving as the spin injector. Given its high chemical reactivity, Co tends to hybridize with the adjacent perovskite layer, leading to undesirable interfacial effects. To prevent this, a thin aluminum oxide (AlOx) barrier was introduced prior to perovskite deposition, effectively isolating the ferromagnet and maintaining interfacial integrity41. The perovskite layer was then formed by spin-coating and annealing the precursor solution. Using atomic force microscopy, the film roughness was measured to be approximately 1.3 nm, indicating a very smooth surface. This observation was further corroborated by scanning electron microscopy analysis (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). Meanwhile, we found that both the contact angle and surface energy of the film showed no significant changes after deuteration (Supplementary Table 1), which supports the consistent fabrication of the top electrode. Finally, a 12 nm NiFe top electrode was deposited as the spin detector (see Methods and Supplementary Fig. 9 for details).

The spinterface is central to spintronic device performance, governing spin injection, transport, and detection. In spin valves, a well-defined interface minimizes spin relaxation and interfacial scattering, thereby enhancing spin transport. To investigate the interface quality in our device, we conducted cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis. The images show sharp, vertically resolved layer boundaries, indicating excellent structural integrity. Notably, no interlayer diffusion or fusion is observed between the NiFe electrode and the OIHP layer (Fig. 1c). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) maps further confirm that each element remains confined within its designated layer, with no evidence of interfacial mixing (Fig. 1d). These findings highlight the effectiveness of our low-temperature deposition strategy in preserving clean interfaces, a prerequisite for achieving room-temperature spin transport in perovskite-based spintronic devices. Furthermore, both ferromagnetic electrodes demonstrated well-defined anisotropic magnetoresistance signals (Supplementary Fig. 10a,b), confirming their magnetic integrity and enabling reliable evaluation of spin transport phenomena in the subsequent device measurements.

We next investigated the magnetic response of the spin valve under a 10 mV bias. The deuterated perovskite thin film exhibited a pronounced magnetocurrent response, with the device current reversibly switching between a high-conductance state under parallel magnetization and a low-conductance state under antiparallel alignment. Upon rapid modulation of the magnetic field, the deuterated spin valve demonstrated fast and stable magnetocurrent switching over 6,454 cycles. The standard deviations (σ) of the current in the parallel and antiparallel states were approximately 0.0048 and 0.0059 of their respective mean values, with 98.6% and 98.2% of the output data points falling within the ±3σ range (Fig. 1e). According to the definition of magnetocurrent:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${MC}=\left({I}_{P}-{I}_{{AP}}\right)/{I}_{{AP}}\times 100\%\,$$\end{document}MC=IP−IAP/IAP×100%where IP and IAP represent the currents under parallel and antiparallel magnetization, respectively5,42, the MC ratio of the deuterated perovskite device was determined to be 17.5% at room temperature (Fig. 1f). Such a substantial magnetocurrent signal under ambient conditions is rarely observed in perovskite-based systems. Based on devices with varying film thicknesses, the spin diffusion length of the deuterated perovskite was extracted to be 78 nm at room temperature (Supplementary Fig. 10c). In contrast, the spin valve using non-deuterated perovskite thin film exhibited a negligibly smaller MC < 1% (Fig. 1g), which was insufficient to support reliable cycling or thickness-dependent measurements under the same conditions.

The long-term stability of spintronic performance is critical for practical applications. We evaluated the room-temperature durability of deuterated and non-deuterated perovskite spin valves by monitoring their MC signals over extended storage times. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 10d, the MC signal of the non-deuterated device rapidly decayed, with a reduction of ~50% within 100 days and nearly complete loss ( ~ 1%) after 400 days. Remarkably, the deuterated perovskite device exhibited exceptional stability, retaining over 93% of its MC value after 100 days and approximately 75% after 400 days. This improvement is attributed to the suppression of hydrogen-related degradation pathways, as the heavier deuterium atoms reduce the vibrational frequency of hydrogen bonds, thereby slowing hydrogen migration and associated decomposition reactions. These results reveal the key role of deuteration in enhancing spin transport and stability in OIHPs, establishing a basis for spintronic device integration. Their robust room-temperature performance suggests OIHPs may serve as versatile platforms bridging photovoltaic, spintronic and quantum functionalities.

Room-temperature spin lifetime

To explore the role of organic cations in spin relaxation, we investigate how deuteration of the organic ligand influences spin dynamics in hybrid perovskites. Using circularly polarized transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy, we track the evolution of spin-polarized photogenerated carriers and reveal the impact of deuteration on longitudinal spin relaxation time43–45. The experiments employ a pump-probe configuration, where a left-circularly polarized pump pulse (σ⁻) selectively excites the sample, generating spin-polarized carriers. The evolution of these spin-polarized carriers is monitored via a left-circularly polarized probe pulse (σ⁻), tracking the photobleaching (PB) signal, as shown in the inset of Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 11. Carrier mobility

To investigate the impact of deuterated organic cations on carrier transport properties, we employed transient reflectivity microscopy to probe the carrier dynamics in the material. This technique enables high spatial and temporal resolution optical detection of photoexcited carrier density and diffusion behaviour by monitoring changes in reflectivity (ΔR/R) induced by excited states, providing a reliable approach to characterize charge transport49. The corresponding experimental setup is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 12. By systematically varying the delay between pump and probe pulses, we acquired two-dimensional reflectivity maps at different time delays for CD₃ND₃PbI₃, as shown in Fig. 3a. The corresponding data for CH₃NH₃PbI₃ are provided in Supplementary Fig. 13. To visualize the spatial characteristics of carrier diffusion, the reflectivity distribution at 10 ps was fitted with a two-dimensional Gaussian function and rendered as a three-dimensional pseudocolour map (Fig. 3b). Subsequently, one-dimensional spatial profiles of the normalized ΔR/R signal were extracted from the central region of each time-delayed map to construct Fig. 3c. Room-temperature spin photovoltaic effect

OIHPs have a high absorption coefficient, allowing even ultra-thin layers to effectively absorb photons and generate carriers. Moreover, their excellent charge separation and transport properties facilitate the rapid migration of carriers52,53. An interplay between electrically injected spin-polarized carriers and photogenerated non-polarized carriers is central to unlocking room-temperature spin functionalities in perovskite materials (Fig. 4a). Under illumination with 4 mW cm⁻² white light and in the absence of an external bias, the perovskite layer absorbs photons and generates non–spin-polarized carriers. These carriers drift toward the ferromagnetic electrodes under the built-in potential, giving rise to a photocurrent (Supplementary Fig. 15a). Under these conditions, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 15b, the device produces a measurable photocurrent. When an external bias is applied under illumination, the spin-polarized current injected from the ferromagnetic electrode flows in the opposite direction to the non–spin-polarized photocurrent, leading to mutual cancellation (Supplementary Fig. 15c). Because the spin valve employs ferromagnetic electrodes, sweeping the magnetic field switches the device between parallel and antiparallel configurations due to the different coercive fields of the electrodes. As shown in Fig. 4b, under a fixed external bias of 10 mV, the photocurrent generated by the photovoltaic effect flows in the opposite direction to the current injected by the external bias. As the light intensity increases, the total device current crosses zero and shifts toward the negative direction. Furthermore, under specific light intensities, the spin valve exhibits magnetocurrent switching between low and high states as the external magnetic field is swept across zero (Supplementary Fig. 16). By maintaining a constant illumination intensity, the current versus magnetic field (I–B) characteristics can be tuned by adjusting the external bias (Fig. 4c). As the applied bias is gradually increased from 0 mV to 40 mV, the device current transitions from negative to positive, while ΔIout between the parallel and antiparallel states steadily increases. The impact of light intensity and applied bias on ΔIout is summarized in Fig. 4d. This result unambiguously demonstrates that the applied bias governs the number of spin-polarized carriers injected from the ferromagnetic electrode, which fundamentally determines the variation in ΔIout. Although photogenerated non-spin-polarized carriers do not directly affect spin-polarized transport, they effectively modulate the device current and consequently influence the MC magnitude. Notably, when the device current is tuned near zero, the MC value approaches infinity (Fig. 4e). Moreover, under specific light intensity and applied bias conditions, the output current of the device can be tuned close to zero. In this regime, the MC signal output during the external magnetic field sweep originates purely from the spin-polarized current (Fig. 4f). Under these conditions, the background operating current of the device is entirely suppressed, presenting a promising approach for the realization of low-power spintronic devices operating at room temperature.

Results and discussion

In summary, we find that HFI represents a non-negligible and previously underappreciated factor in spin relaxation processes within OIHPs. By replacing hydrogen with deuterium in CH₃NH₃PbI₃, we suppress HFI and extend the spin lifetime from 4.7 ps to 12.4 ps while maintaining high carrier mobility of 51.1 cm²/V·s, resulting in a robust room-temperature magnetocurrent of 17.5%. Furthermore, the observed room-temperature spin photovoltaic effects reveal a strong interplay between optical excitation and spin dynamics in hybrid perovskites. The findings reveal that organic cations, long overlooked in studies of spin dynamics, play a significant role in hybrid perovskites. Their measurable impact on spin transport offers new insights into spin relaxation mechanisms and provides a foundation for tuning spintronic properties in perovskite-based devices.

Methods

Preparation of CD₃ND₃PbI3

All starting materials were purchased from Energy Chemical. CD₃ND₂ was obtained by dissolving anhydrous methylamine gas in D₂O. Lead(II) acetate trihydrate was dissolved in D₂O three times to promote H/D exchange and then dried under vacuum. Deuterated hydroiodic acid (DI, 57 wt% in water) was synthesized by reacting 50 wt% hypophosphorous acid–d₃ (in D₂O) with potassium iodide (KI) at 170°C. The resulting DI vapor was condensed and collected.

For crystal growth54, 1 M solutions of PbI₂ and CD₃ND₃I were prepared separately in γ-butyrolactone (GBL) and heated to 60°C. The solutions were filtered using a 0.2μm PTFE syringe filter, and 2 mL of the filtered solution was transferred to a vial and placed in a 110°C oil bath. Crystals began to appear after ~20 min. The crystals were collected, washed three times with deuterated diethyl ether, and vacuum-dried at room temperature. The synthesized CD₃ND₃PbI₃ was characterized by ¹³C nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, performed on a Bruker Avance 600 MHz NMR spectrometer at 25°C using deuterated DMSO as the solvent. The methyl carbon peak of CD₃ND₃PbI₃ appears around 23.8ppm. The multiple splittings of this peak indicate the successful synthesis of the deuterated perovskite CD₃ND₃PbI₃ (Supplementary Fig. 17). The unit cell parameters obtained from single-crystal analysis are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Preparation of perovskite films

The precursor solution was prepared by dissolving methylammonium iodide (MAI) and lead iodide (PbI₂) in a 1:1 molar ratio. The mixture was stirred in N,N-dimethylformamide at 60 °C for 12 hours. The resulting solution was then spin-coated onto glass substrates and subsequently annealed at 120 °C for 25 minutes under a nitrogen atmosphere.

Device fabrication and measurements

Prior to fabrication, the substrates were sequentially cleaned in deionized water, isopropanol, and acetone using ultrasonic treatment for a total duration of one hour. After cleaning, the substrates were dried with a nitrogen flow. Cobalt (Co) bottom electrodes were deposited by high-vacuum electron beam evaporation through a shadow mask at a deposition rate of approximately 1 Å/s. Subsequently, a thin aluminum layer ( ~ 1.7 nm) was thermally evaporated at 0.3 Å/s, followed by immediate air plasma oxidation to form a tunneling AlOx layer. Perovskite precursor solutions were spin-coated onto the substrates inside a nitrogen-filled glovebox to achieve films of varying thicknesses. On top of the perovskite layer, a 12 nm NiFe top electrode was deposited using a low-temperature process to minimize damage to the underlying organic layer. The deposition was performed at a rate of 0.7 Å/s with a shadow mask to define the device area, which was approximately 0.2 mm². The thickness of the intermediate layer was determined by spectroscopic ellipsometry (SE-VE-L, Wuhan Eoptics Technology Co., Ltd).

Magnetocurrent measurements were conducted using a vacuum magnetic probe station (CRX-EM-HF, Lake Shore Cryotronics Co., Ltd.) employing a standard four-probe configuration. Cross-sectional high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) samples were prepared using a focused ion beam (FIB) system (FEI Strata DB235). A 100 nm thick Al protective layer was deposited on top of the devices prior to FIB milling. High-resolution TEM images were acquired using an FEI G2 F30 microscope operated at an accelerating voltage of 300 kV.

Transient absorption spectroscopy with circular polarization

The transient absorption measurement is based on the femtosecond laser (ORPHEUS-HP, Light Conversion) and the transient absorption spectrometer (HARPIA-TA, Light Conversion). An ultrafast pump laser beam is converted into right or left circularly polarized light by a Berek compensator. A white light probe beam is converted into left circularly polarized light using a linear polarizer and a quarter wave plate.

Transient reflection microscopy

Ultrafast transient reflection microscopy was performed using an experimental setup previously constructed by our collaborators. In this setup, a portion of the 800 nm femtosecond laser output was frequency-doubled to 400 nm via a β-Barium borate (BBO) crystal to serve as the pump beam. The probe beam was generated by an optical parametric amplifier (OPA), providing tunable output for selective excitation and detection. The two beams were spatially overlapped on the sample through a custom-built microscope platform, enabling diffraction-limited spatial resolution. Temporal delay between pump and probe pulses was introduced using a motorized delay stage, allowing for time-resolved mapping of the transient optical response.
