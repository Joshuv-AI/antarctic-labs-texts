The effects of informal digital learning, intrinsic motivation, and grit on online learning effort regulation and English achievement
Authors: Sultan Hammad Alshammari, Oqab Alrashidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The integration of digital technologies has transformed language learning and provided informal avenues that complement traditional English as a Foreign Language (EFL) instruction. This study investigates the direct effects of Informal Digital Learning of English (IDLE), intrinsic motivation, and grit on Online Learning Effort Regulation (OLER) and English achievement among 218 Saudi EFL students from the University of Ha’il. Using a quantitative approach, data were collected through online questionnaires. Confirmatory factor analysis was employed to develop and validate the measurement model, while structural equation modeling using AMOS was used to test the hypothesized relationships. The results indicated that IDLE and intrinsic motivation had significant positive effects on OLER, while grit did not significantly influence OLER. For English achievement, grit had a significant positive effect, while IDLE and intrinsic motivation had insignificant effects. These findings highlight the differential roles of digital learning and psychological factors in enhancing effort regulation versus actual language performance. The study offers valuable insights for policymakers, educators, and curriculum designers to develop strategies and interventions that improve learner engagement, persistence, and language achievement in digital EFL environments.

Introduction

The development and implementation of digital technologies have disrupted pedagogies, including those within English as a Foreign Language (EFL) environment, to supplement classroom-based learning with digital informal learning alternatives1–3. Digital informal learning has become an effective method for language learning outside of formal learning contexts. However, there are numerous factors that can be connected in relation to psychological characteristics, including grit and intrinsic motivation—which have yet to be investigated in this regard—and how they can relate to their online learning effort regulation and success in English4. Grit has been shown to include effort persistence and interest consistency, which can predict learning outcomes (i.e., academic achievement) in challenging areas of learning, such as second language learning5. Grit can also be connected with intrinsic motivation, which can drive continued involvement and encourage confidence and enjoyment with the task, thus contributing to above-average self-regulating learning6 and signifying the potential for increasing intrinsic motivation. There is mixed evidence regarding the structure of grit and its relationship with language learning outcomes7. Intrinsic motivation is also one of the main factors in retaining effort in online learning environments, extending beyond the immediate effect of the externalized incentive3,8. Previous studies have examined one or more of these constructs, but few have explored how they operate together with informal digital learning to influence effort regulation and English achievement. Despite the rapid expansion of digital learning in Saudi higher education, limited empirical research has examined how informal digital learning, intrinsic motivation, and grit jointly influence students’ effort regulation and English achievement. This study addresses this gap by focusing specifically on Saudi EFL learners, thereby situating the theoretical constructs within a contextualized digital learning environment.

While considerable literature exists in Western EFL contexts, little research has been conducted on the general digital learning behaviors and psychological engagement of Saudi higher education students. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, no research thus far has investigated informal digital learning, intrinsic motivation, and grit in a single comprehensive model and has predicted the online learning effort regulation and English achievement of Saudi EFL students. Investigating this gap is important to establish a more comprehensive picture of how the three factors come together to influence learners’ self-regulation behaviors and language learning success. Given the identified research gaps, it is important to explore existing evidence regarding informal digital learning, intrinsic motivation, and grit in EFL contexts. The literature review will detail how the constructs have been conceptualized, measured, and associated with effort regulation and English achievement in previous studies. It will also synthesize these studies to provide a starting point for the current study’s conceptual model, as well as the theoretical and practical justification for studying their interaction effect in the Saudi higher education context.

Literature review

Informal digital learning of English (IDLE)

IDLE is a self-directed language learning activity that takes place beyond the boundaries of formal instruction, often facilitated by digital tools and platforms, such as YouTube, Duolingo, MOOCs, or online community forums9. IDLE does not involve formal instruction or intervention, as learners identify the pacing, content, and mode of engagement, which signifies overall autonomy and motivation10. There is a burgeoning amount of research on IDLE, indicating that IDLE has a positive effect on language acquisition through access to authentic input in English (listening and reading), opportunities to practice the language (active use), and immediate feedback (especially through vocabulary)11. In addition, IDLE can support intercultural competence and a willingness to communicate in a second language through the learner’s exposure to cross-cultural perspectives and interactions with global communities12,13. Although advantageous, IDLE only occurs if learners conceptualize their support, intrinsic motivation, and psychological features (e.g., grit), which are correlated with language outcomes. Studies have shown improvements in language outcomes as a result of informal learning. However, there is a need for studies to show the relationships in how IDLE plays its role in rational motivation, personality traits, self-regulated tasks (activities), and academic achievements3,14. This suggests ongoing research in developing integrative studies, especially studies on non-Western higher education, to find connections between models and constructs.

Intrinsic motivation

Intrinsic motivation, which is based on self-determination theory15,16, represents the motivation toward learning for satisfaction and interest rather than external rewards. In EFL situations, intrinsically oriented learners are more likely to continue learning languages, interact deeply with materials, and frequently participate in informal digital learning17,18. Evidence shows that intrinsic motivation nurtures SRL behaviors, such as goal setting, time management, and exertion regulation, which are necessary in online and informal learning environments14,19,20. While intrinsic motivation has been extensively examined in prior research, few studies have investigated its combined influence with grit and informal digital learning on online learning effort regulation and English achievement, particularly among non-Western EFL learners. To provide a comprehensive motivational foundation, it is worth noting that motivation also includes extrinsic forms and more recent categories such as active versus passive motivation; however, intrinsic motivation remains the central focus of the present study due to its strong theoretical and empirical relevance to autonomous digital learning.

Grit

Grit is commonly understood as a personal disposition that involves sustained perseverance and enduring interest in long-term goals, and it is typically described through two core components: perseverance of effort and consistency of interest21–25. While grit has received considerable attention in psychology and educational research, its role in foreign language learning has not been examined to the same extent and remains open to debate. Existing evidence indicates that the relationship between grit and learning outcomes is complex rather than uniform, particularly in language learning contexts that demand long-term effort, involve changes in motivation, and require learners to cope with repeated challenges.

Empirical research across different educational contexts has revealed mixed findings regarding the relationship between grit and academic achievement. While some studies have identified positive associations between overall grit and indicators such as grade point average or learning outcomes (e.g21,26–28), others have reported weak, non-significant, or even negative relationships after accounting for prior achievement or contextual factors (e.g29–31.). Meta-analytic evidence further suggests that, on average, grit is only weakly related to academic performance, and that this relationship may vary depending on factors such as task difficulty, learning context, and individual differences32. Taken together, these findings call into question the universality of grit as a predictor of achievement and highlight the importance of interpreting its effects within specific educational contexts.

A growing body of research suggests that the two dimensions of grit do not operate in the same way across learning contexts. Evidence from multiple studies indicates that perseverance of effort is more associated with academic and language-related outcomes than consistency of interest, which often shows weaker or less stable relationships32–35. In some cases, consistency of interest has been found to be unrelated, or even negatively related to achievement, implying that maintaining fixed interests may be less adaptive in learning environments that demand flexibility, adjustment, and sustained problem-solving31. These contrasting patterns help explain why grit may be linked to long-term achievement, yet fail to account for short-term effort regulation or context-specific learning behaviors.

Beyond its direct effects, research suggests that grit may influence language achievement indirectly through motivational, affective, and self-regulatory processes. Studies have shown that higher levels of grit are associated with foreign language enjoyment, willingness to communicate, motivational intensity, and persistence, all of which support continued engagement in language learning36–38. However, when these closely related variables are considered together in predictive models, the direct effect of grit often becomes weaker or non-significant. This pattern points to substantial conceptual and empirical overlap between grit, motivation, and conscientiousness39,40. From this perspective, grit may be better understood not as an isolated causal factor, but as a more distal personal characteristic that contributes to language achievement through its interaction with other psychological processes.

Conceptual critiques have also raised questions about the distinctiveness of grit as a higher-order construct, pointing to its substantial overlap with related attributes such as perseverance, conscientiousness, intrinsic motivation, and self-regulated learning32. In language learning contexts, where sustained effort, emotional resilience, and motivational regulation already play central roles, these overlaps may help explain the inconsistent patterns reported across studies. As a result, scholars have increasingly argued for examining grit within broader, integrated models that incorporate motivational and self-regulatory variables, rather than treating it as an isolated predictor of learning outcomes41.

Taken together, existing research suggests that grit plays a complex and context-dependent role in language learning. Its influence appears to vary depending on its dimensional focus, the way it is measured, and its interaction with motivational and self-regulatory processes. These ongoing theoretical and empirical debates highlight the importance of examining grit alongside intrinsic motivation and informal digital learning within an integrated framework, particularly in under-researched non-Western EFL contexts. Such an approach allows for a better understanding of how perseverance supports both effort regulation and language achievement in digital learning environments.

Online learning effort regulation (OLER)

Effort generally refers to the amount of effort learners invest in the learning process42. In language learning, it has been suggested that students who put in consistent effort are often more successful in completing tasks and improving their language skills43. In online learning contexts, OLER reflects learners’ ability to plan, monitor, and sustain their efforts in digital environments. It includes behaviors such as working hard to learn English in online settings, seeking help when facing difficulties, ignoring distractions, and maintaining regular study routines throughout their online learning. Motivated learners spend more time studying and practicing the language whenever they can44. The interaction between intrinsic motivation and grit is particularly important for OLER, as intrinsic motivation and grit can provide the internal motivation that leads to engagement and stamina without outside pressure to succeed. However, how intrinsic motivation and grit interact to create the appropriate OLER for informal digital learning settings requires more analysis, particularly with a focus on EFL students. Aside from OLER being worthy of attention, only a limited number of studies have investigated OLER in informal digital learning, specifically the effects of intrinsic motivation and grit on OLER and, consequently, English achievement for EFL learners. This is especially concerning in the non-Western context, specifically Saudi higher education, as learners engaged in self-regulatory practice in an online context are not examined or well known.

English achievement

English achievement, which is generally assessed through measures of proficiency, grades, or skill-specific assessments, is the highest level of performance resulting from students’ learning efforts. Research has shown that student factors, such as intrinsic motivation, grit, and informal digital learning, positively affect English achievement10,16,25, but it is unclear how they work together to predict more English achievement outcomes specifically based on the learning environment, such as online learning. This study proposes an overall model that includes these constructs to show the integrated effects on English achievement in a technological educational context. The purpose of this study is to fill this void in the literature by creating and testing a comprehensive model to study the links of informal digital learning of English, intrinsic motivation, and grit to online learning effort regulation and English achievement for EFL students in higher education.

While there is a growing interest in IDLE, intrinsic motivation, and grit, there are still some gaps to fill. First, empirical research in this area has mainly investigated Western contexts, with limited attention to the learning behaviors and cultural background of Saudi higher education students. Second, we have limited knowledge of research that integrates IDLE, intrinsic motivation, and grit into one comprehensive model to study the effects of online learning effort regulation and English achievement. Third, little is known about the effects of these factors on students’ learning self-regulation and English achievement. To address these gaps, this study investigates the integration of IDLE, intrinsic motivation, and two components of grit to study the combined effects of these constructs on online learning effort regulation and English achievement for EFL students in Saudi higher education. This will provide an overall perspective of students’ engagement and achievement in digital learning and provide meaningful theoretical and practical considerations of ways to provide better English language instruction in higher education. In addition, Saudi Arabia’s cultural environment, educational practices, and societal expectations that emphasize perseverance and academic responsibility play a central role in influencing learners’ motivational and digital learning behaviors. These social cultural characteristics make the Saudi context particularly suitable to examine how IDLE, intrinsic motivation, and grit interact within a non-Western educational system.

Hypotheses

The literature reviewed has shown that IDLE, intrinsic motivation, and grit are interrelated constructs that influence learners’ self-regulation and academic performance. These empirical findings, collectively, inform the current study’s hypotheses by indicating that IDLE, intrinsic motivation, and grit each have theoretical and empirical justification as predictors of OLER and English achievement. The proposed relationships are theoretically grounded in Self-Determination Theory45, which posits that intrinsic motivation predicts sustained engagement and learning behaviors. Self-Regulated Learning models46 further highlight the central role of autonomous regulation in online learning environments. Grit theory22 also suggests that perseverance and consistency of effort contribute to long-term academic outcomes. Within digital learning contexts, IDLE reflects autonomous, self-initiated learning behaviors that are closely aligned with SRL processes. Together, these complementary frameworks provide a coherent theoretical basis for the hypothesized relationships among IDLE, intrinsic motivation, grit, OLER, and English achievement. In particular, the study examines the following hypotheses:

H1. IDLE has a positive direct effect on OLER.

H2. IDLE has a positive direct effect on English achievement.

H3. Intrinsic motivation has a positive direct effect on OLER.

H4. Intrinsic motivation has a positive direct effect on English achievement.

H5. Grit has a positive direct effect on OLER.

H6. Grit has a positive direct effect on English achievement.

Methodology

Research design

This study employed a quantitative, cross-sectional, correlational, and predictive research design to examine the relationships among IDLE, intrinsic motivation, grit, OLER, and English achievement among Saudi EFL university students. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was adopted as the primary analytical framework, as it allows for the simultaneous examination of multiple latent constructs and their direct relationships within a theoretically grounded model. A survey-based data collection method was used to obtain self-reported data from participants at a single point in time. The use of a cross-sectional design enabled the identification of associative and predictive relationships among psychological and behavioral constructs, rather than causal inferences. This design is particularly appropriate for investigating complex motivational and self-regulatory processes in authentic educational settings, where multiple variables interact dynamically.

Participants

The sample population for this study consisted of 218 Saudi EFL students from the University of Ha’il, selected using convenience sampling based on the students’ previous experiences with online English and/or informal English. Although a convenience sampling strategy was used, the sample included students from seven different colleges, which provided considerable diversity in academic backgrounds. This diversity is valuable for examining how psychological and digital learning variables influence effort regulation and achievement across multiple disciplines. Demographic information, such as gender, college, and EFL online courses taken, was collected to describe the sample. The sample of 218 was adequate for analysis using structural equation modeling in AMOS. It was also sufficient according to the theoretical model used for yielding such effects and establishing the level of statistical power available for these analyses47–49.

Instruments

Data were collected using a structured online survey, which contained the validated instruments for each variable of the study. IDLE was measured using a 13-item scale adapted from Lee and colleagues12,50. Intrinsic motivation was measured using a 10-item scale adapted from Goldman et al.51, and modified in this study to fit the online EFL context. Grit was measured using a nine-item scale adopted from Teimouri et al.52. OLER was measured using four items adapted from Gardner53 and slightly modified to fit the online EFL learning context. English achievement was measured using course grades and standardized English proficiency test scores, both of which were converted to a unified scale to ensure comparability across participants. Aside from English achievement, all scales were in a five-point Likert scale format, with higher scores indicating greater levels of variables. Additionally, all instruments were translated into Arabic following a translation–back-translation procedures54. Three experts in the field reviewed the translations for content and cultural appropriateness. Minor adjustments were made to ensure clarity and context relevance. To ensure face and content validity, the survey instruments were evaluated by a panel of three experts in educational technology and EFL learning. Each expert reviewed the items for clarity, relevance to the constructs, alignment with the theoretical definitions, and suitability for the EFL context. Their feedback resulted in minor rewording of several items to improve clarity and contextual relevance. In addition, a preliminary pilot test was conducted with a group of 30 university students who were not included in the main sample. Participants were asked to comment on item clarity and comprehension. Based on their responses, two items were refined to avoid ambiguity and better match participants’ linguistic familiarity. These procedures ensured the instruments’ face and content validity before full-scale data collection.

Data collection procedure

Data collection was conducted using an online survey over a four-week period. Students were provided with an informed consent form outlining the purpose and voluntary nature of the study and assuring them that their responses would remain anonymous. Follow-up reminders were sent to encourage a higher response rate. Ethical considerations were maintained throughout the study. The confidentiality and anonymity of the participants were strictly protected, and informed consent was obtained prior to participation. Ethical approval for this research was granted by the Research Ethics Committee at the University of Ha’il, Saudi Arabia (Approval No. H-2025-925). All methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant institutional guidelines and regulations. To reduce potential social desirability and recall biases, all surveys were anonymous, no identifying information was collected, and participants were informed that there were no right or wrong answers.

Data analysis

The data collected were analyzed using SPSS and AMOS. The online questionnaires were distributed to the students during the first semester of 2025. The analysis included descriptive statistics to summarize the demographic data and variable distributions, reliability and validity analyses using Cronbach’s alpha and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), and structural equation modeling (SEM) to assess the direct effects of IDLE, intrinsic motivation, and grit on OLER and English achievement. The study hypotheses were tested using path coefficients, standard errors, and significance levels (p <.05).

Results

Descriptive analysis

The study sample consisted of 218 Saudi EFL students from the University of Ha’il. Regarding gender distribution, the majority were male students (56%), while female students accounted for 44%, indicating a slightly higher representation of males in the sample. In terms of college affiliation, the students were drawn from a variety of disciplines, with the largest proportion from the College of Computer Science and Engineering (79, 36.2%), followed by the Colleges of Arts (41, 18.8%), Business Administration (22, 10.1%), Medicine (24, 11.0%), Public Health and Health Informatics (21, 9.6%), Education (18, 8.3%), and Science (13, 6.0%), indicating a diverse academic representation across the university. Regarding online EFL course experience, nearly half of the participants (102, 46.8%) reported taking one online English course, 95 students (43.6%) took two courses, and a smaller portion (21, 9.6%) took more than two courses. This suggests that most of the participants had moderate exposure to online English learning, which is relevant when interpreting their online learning effort regulation and achievement.

Overall, the sample reflects a balanced gender distribution, a diverse college representation, and varied levels of experience with online EFL courses, providing a suitable context for examining the effects of informal digital learning, intrinsic motivation, and grit on OLER and English achievement. Measurement model evaluation

Following the two-step SEM approach, the measurement model was first evaluated to establish reliability and validity, before examining the structural model and testing the proposed hypotheses.

CFA

CFA was conducted to evaluate the measurement model and ensure that the constructs were validly measured by their observed indicators. After deleting items with low factor loadings (< 0.70) (i.e., IDLE1, IDLE7, IM1, G2, IDLE5, IDLE6, IDLE2, OLER4, and IDLE3), all remaining items loaded significantly on their respective constructs, meeting the recommended threshold and providing evidence of convergent validity. Despite item deletion based on weak loadings (< 0.60), the retained items continued to adequately represent the original conceptual domains of the constructs. For IDLE, the remaining items capture core behaviors related to informal digital engagement, including digital content consumption, online language practice, and autonomous use of digital resources. The intrinsic motivation items reflect learners’ enjoyment, interest, and internal drive toward English learning. The OLER items reflect students’ effort and hard work in online learning environments. L2 grit is preserved through both perseverance of effort and consistency of interest, representing the two established subdimensions in grit research. Therefore, item reduction improved psychometric quality without compromising content validity or conceptual coverage.

The model fit indicated that the measurement model achieved an acceptable-to-good fit: the chi-square/df value was below 3; CFI, TLI, and IFI exceeded 0.90; and RMSEA fell below 0.08. These indices were selected because they are among the most widely recommended in SEM literature and allow for a robust evaluation of absolute, incremental, and parsimonious model fit. Altogether, these results confirm that the constructs demonstrated adequate construct validity55,56. Figure 1 illustrates the CFA output.

Discriminant Validity

The findings of the discriminant validity check presented in Table 4 indicate that, for most construct pairs, the square roots of the AVE values (on-diagonal) are greater than the inter-construct correlations (off-diagonal). For example, the value of OLER’s (AVE) square root (0.726) is higher than its correlations with IDLE (0.547), L2 grit (0.565), and English achievement (0.267). A similar pattern is observed for the remaining constructs, indicating that most constructs share more variance with their own indicators than with other constructs. This satisfies the Fornell–Larcker criterion, thus demonstrating acceptable discriminant validity of the measurement model56,58.

One exception is the relationship between OLER and intrinsic motivation, where the inter-construct correlation (r =.883) is slightly higher than the square roots of their AVE values (0.726 and 0.785, respectively). This suggests a strong association between these two motivational/readiness constructs; however, the correlation remains below the redundancy threshold of 0.9059. Given their distinct theoretical definitions and differential relationships with other variables in the model, OLER and intrinsic motivation were retained as separate constructs, with this high correlation acknowledged as a limitation in the interpretation of the results.

Assessment of SEM assumptions

Before conducting the SEM, key statistical assumptions were examined. Normality was assessed using skewness and kurtosis statistics, and all values were within the acceptable range of ± 2, indicating adequate univariate normality59. Furthermore, the potential outliers were inspected using standardized z-scores and Mahalanobis distance. No cases exceeded the recommended cutoff (p <.001), and therefore no observations were removed. In addition, Multicollinearity was examined by inspecting the inter-construct correlations in the measurement model. None of the correlations exceeded the theoretical redundancy threshold of 0.9059, indicating the absence of problematic multicollinearity among latent variables. Although the correlation between intrinsic motivation and OLER was relatively high (r =.883), the square roots of their AVE values were greater than their inter-construct correlations, satisfying the Fornell–Larcker criterion. Therefore, the constructs remained theoretically and empirically distinct56,58.

Standardized estimates

Standardized estimates are crucial in SEM, as they allow the assessment of regression paths, factor loadings, and explained variance (R²)60. They enable a comparison of the effects across constructs, confirm indicator reliability when loadings exceed 0.70, and show how much variance in each endogenous construct is explained by its predictors. These metrics collectively demonstrate the model’s reliability and explanatory power55,56. Figure 2 presents the standardized estimate output.

Unstandardized estimates

Unstandardized estimates play a critical role in SEM, as they are the basis for calculating the CR and for testing the research hypotheses through regression analysis. Unlike standardized coefficients, unstandardized estimates are scale dependent and represent the actual parameter values, which some scholars refer to as the “true beta.” These coefficients are particularly important for hypothesis testing because they capture the raw direct effect of one construct on another, thereby providing the statistical foundation for validating the proposed structural relationships55,56. Regression weights and hypothesis testing results

As shown in Table 5, the findings provide partial support for the proposed model. IDLE significantly enhances OLER (β = 0.163, p =.012), thus confirming H1 and suggesting that informal digital learning can strengthen students’ ability to regulate their online learning efforts. However, its effect on English achievement is not significant (β = 0.011, p =.906), thereby offering no support for H2. Intrinsic motivation plays a key role, strongly predicting OLER (β = 1.047, p <.001) and supporting H3. However, it does not translate into improved English achievement, as the direct path is insignificant (β = 0.046, p =.699), leaving H4 unsupported. Contrary to expectations, L2 grit does not significantly predict OLER (β = −0.167, p =.106), thus rejecting H5. Nevertheless, it has a direct positive effect on English achievement (β = 0.453, p =.003), providing support for H6. Overall, the results highlight the central role of IDLE and intrinsic motivation in shaping effort regulation, while L2 grit appears to be more directly related to academic achievement than to self-regulation processes.

Discussion

The findings of this study provide partial support for the proposed structural model that examines the effects of IDLE, intrinsic motivation, and L2 grit on OLER and English achievement among higher education students. The significant path from IDLE to OLER (β = 0.163, p =.012) confirms that students who engage more frequently in informal digital English learning activities, such as watching videos, using mobile applications, or interacting on social media, are better able to regulate their online learning efforts. This result is in accordance with previous studies indicating that IDLE promotes learner autonomy and self-regulation through authentic, technology-mediated experiences62. This engagement often leads to a more enjoyable learning experience, which fosters greater persistence and dedication in language acquisition3,50,63. Such informal engagement appears to cultivate habits of persistence and strategic learning that extend to formal academic contexts, consistent with evidence showing a positive and strengthening longitudinal relationship between informal digital learning of English and online self-regulated learning64.

However, IDLE did not significantly influence English achievement (β = 0.011, p =.906), suggesting that while informal learning enhances motivation and self-regulatory behaviors, it may not directly translate into measurable academic performance. This indicates that the effects of IDLE on achievement are likely indirect and operate through motivational and self-regulatory mechanisms rather than immediate performance outcomes, as supported by longitudinal evidence linking IDLE to growth in online self-regulated learning64. A possible explanation for the non-significant direct effect of IDLE on achievement is that informal digital activities may enhance motivation and self-regulation but may not align directly with formal exam content or grading criteria. IDLE may instead exert an indirect influence through OLER or other affective variables.

Intrinsic motivation demonstrated a strong positive relationship with OLER (β = 1.047, p <.001), reinforcing the central role of internal drive in sustaining effort and persistence in online environments. This supports previous research emphasizing that intrinsically motivated learners are more likely to engage in goal setting, time management, and metacognitive regulation, leading to more effective online learning activities and improved academic outcomes14,19,20. Conversely, a lack of intrinsic motivation can hinder learners’ ability to engage in self-regulated activities, potentially leading to disengagement and poorer academic performance65. Nevertheless, intrinsic motivation’s non-significant effect on English achievement (β = 0.046, p =.699) indicates that motivation alone may not directly predict academic outcomes unless accompanied by effective learning strategies—a relationship often observed in online learning literature. This finding suggests a complex interplay in which intrinsic motivation serves as a foundational component for effort regulation, but its translation into tangible achievement may require the mediation of other cognitive and behavioral processes.

Interestingly, L2 grit did not significantly predict OLER (β = –0.167, p =.106), contrary to expectations. Although grit reflects perseverance and sustained effort toward long-term goals, this finding suggests that its influence on L2 learning outcomes may be indirect when other motivational and regulatory variables are considered simultaneously. This pattern aligns with recent evidence showing that grit, particularly consistency of interest, may lose its predictive power for L2 achievement once other motivational and emotional factors are included, with perseverance of effort exerting mainly indirect effects39. The lack of a significant association between L2 grit and OLER further indicates that, in online EFL contexts, short-term effort regulation may depend more on situational and cognitive factors, such as self-efficacy, learning strategies, or learner autonomy. In contrast, L2 grit made a significant positive contribution to English achievement (β = 0.453, p =.003), supporting earlier findings that highlight grit’s role in sustaining long-term goal pursuit and academic achievement in EFL contexts66. From an SRL perspective, OLER reflects short-term, context-specific regulation skills, whereas grit is a broader, long-term trait. This distinction helps explain why grit was linked to achievement but did not predict students’ immediate online effort regulation. Furthermore, the perseverance of effort component or grit rather than consistency of interest is shown to be more helpful in predicting language achievement and enjoyment39,67. Given the ongoing debate about grit’s dimensionality and construct validity in L2 contexts, the findings should be interpreted cautiously. Some scholars argue that grit overlaps substantially with perseverance constructs or motivational effort, which may explain its mixed predictive patterns.

Implications

The results of this study have important practical implications for English language education in higher education settings. In practical applications, EFL teachers and those who provide curriculum material need to promote and encourage students’ involvement in more informal kinds of digital activities, such as viewing English videos, discussing things online, and gaining experience in using learning apps as supplementary resources to formal learning. In addition, teachers can integrate IDLE into the curriculum through structured digital tasks, carefully selected English media playlists, reflective journals documenting digital engagement, and the use of gamified applications such as Quizlet or Duolingo as supplemental assignments. Practically, these implications can be implemented through simple classroom strategies. For example, teachers can assign short digital tasks such as watching selected English videos or practicing vocabulary on Quizlet, followed by brief in-class reflections. Students may keep a digital learning log documenting their informal engagement (e.g., apps used, online discussions, or podcasts), which teachers can review weekly. To foster grit, instructors can introduce gradual goal-setting activities (e.g., increasing speaking time each week) and provide constructive feedback that normalizes setbacks and encourages persistence. Teachers can also incorporate growth-mindset reflections, and resilience-training tasks that encourage students to persist through difficulties, thereby fostering grit in the classroom5,68. Finally, to foster intrinsic motivation among students, institutions and teachers can develop learning environments that support students’ autonomy, provide constructive and timely feedback, and offer online activities that relate to students’ personal interests and needs.

Limitations

Despite providing important insights, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. First, the study employed a cross-sectional design, which was chosen for feasibility and to examine associations among multiple constructs within a single time frame. However, cross-sectional data do not allow for causal interpretations. Future longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to track changes in motivation, grit, and online learning behaviors over time. Second, self-reports of the data were employed, which could be vulnerable to social desirability bias and biases derived from the participants’ own assessments of their behaviors. Third, because the sample was drawn from a single university, the findings may not generalize to all Saudi EFL learners. Future studies should include multi-institutional samples to enhance external validity. Fourth, the study focuses on a select set of psychological and digital learning variables, which, while theoretically justified, do not fully capture the multifaceted nature of online language learning. Factors such as self-efficacy, teaching presence, digital literacy, L2 anxiety, and foreign language enjoyment may also significantly influence learners’ effort regulation and achievement. A broad model that incorporates these constructs could yield a holistic understanding of how affective, cognitive, and contextual variables interact. Future research should therefore employ multi-factor or mixed-method designs to examine these broader dynamics. Fifth, the main emphasis of the investigation was the use of SEM in the quantitative analysis, which provides important statistical insight but a limited semi-qualitative understanding of the students’ learning process. Future qualitative studies (e.g., interviews or reflective diaries) could provide deep insights into how students experience digital learning environments and how these environments can be optimized to support intrinsic motivation and grit. Lastly, these findings should be understood within the sociocultural context of Saudi Arabia, where norms of society, expectations of families, and religious values play an important role in influencing learners’ motivation, perseverance, and digital learning behaviors. As a result, the findings may not fully apply to other contexts, and therefore more cross-cultural research is needed to examine how these relationships might differ across educational settings.

Conclusion

This study examined the impacts of IDLE, intrinsic motivation, and grit on OLER and English achievement in a Saudi EFL context. The findings showed that IDLE and intrinsic motivation play important roles in enhancing students’ OLER, which suggest that informal digital engagement and internally driven goals encourage learners to invest great effort in their online learning. In contrast, grit did not predict OLER but it was a significant predictor of English achievement, which highlights its importance in sustaining long-term academic performance. Meanwhile, IDLE and intrinsic motivation did not directly influence EFL achievement. Overall, the results highlight the distinct contributions of digital learning behaviors and psychological traits in influencing students’ online learning effort regulation and language achievement. The study provides valuable insights for educators and policymakers seeking to design interventions that promote successful language learning in digital EFL environments.
