Numerical investigation of the composite mechanical behavior of grouted rock members with rough fractures
Authors: Ermeng Zhang, Yiming Li, Mengmeng Liu, Lang Liu, Qifeng Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIncomplete grout filling in natural rough fractures is common in grouting practices and alters the mechanical behavior of rock masses. This study numerically simulates slurry diffusion in fractures with different joint roughness coefficients (JRC). Rock models with varying roughness and filling degrees are tested under uniaxial compression and constant normal stiffness (CNS) direct shear. Results show that uniaxial compressive strength depends weakly on filling level but strongly on roughness. Higher roughness intensifies stress concentration, accelerates grout damage, and reduces stress transfer, lowering compressive strength. Incomplete filling only induces local stress behind the grout front, slightly reducing bearing capacity. Under CNS direct shear, peak shear strength increases with both filling level and roughness, with filling showing a more linear effect. Residual shear strength is mainly controlled by roughness and can exceed peak strength at high roughness due to asperity interlocking and grout dilation. These findings highlight the key role of fracture roughness in partially grouted rock masses and aid strength evaluation in real grouting conditions.Keywords: Grout-infilled rock, Partial filling, Joint roughness coefficient (JRC), Uniaxial compressive strength, Shear strengthSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Pregrouting is routinely adopted in underground mining and tunneling to increase rock mass integrity and control groundwater inflow, especially where excavations intersect fractured, water-bearing strata or aquifer-connected discontinuity systems1–3. In such settings, pregrouting is commonly regarded as a prerequisite for safe construction and for mitigating water-/mud-inrush hazards4,5. Its field performance is governed not only by grout rheology, injection strategy and onsite control criteria but also—often predominantly—by the inherent geometry of discontinuities that dictates both grout transport and posttreatment load transfer6,7.

A persistent practical challenge is that grout rarely forms a uniform, fully filled layer within natural fractures. Laboratory and field-oriented studies consistently reveal heterogeneous penetration in rough-walled discontinuities, manifesting as partially filled zones, bypassed regions and irregular grout fronts even under sufficient grout take. Such partial filling is promoted by spatially variable hydraulic apertures, tortuous flow paths and local constrictions associated with roughness, which can trigger preferential channels and flow segregation during propagation8,9. Consequently, the treated rock is better represented as a composite system consisting of intact rock, an irregular grout body and residual voids, rather than an idealized joint fully replaced by a homogeneous infill10. Because these morphological features directly define the contact conditions and stress paths across the discontinuity, they are expected to exert first-order control on the mechanical response of grouted rock masses.

From a mechanical perspective, joint roughness governs shear behavior through interlocking, dilation and evolving contact conditions and has been widely embedded in joint strength formulations (e.g., JRC/JCS-type frameworks) for clean joints and extended—although less consistently—to filled joints11,12. Under shear, particularly when dilation is constrained (e.g., constant normal stiffness), roughness-sensitive constitutive descriptions are essential for capturing mobilization and postpeak evolution13,14. Under compression, however, roughness can introduce pronounced stress heterogeneity and local damage initiation at asperities, complicating load transfer across a discontinuity that contains infill material of contrasting stiffness and strength15,16. Despite this recognition, the combined effects of roughness and incomplete grout filling on both the compressive and shear performance of grouted rock remain insufficiently quantified with morphology-consistent evidence.

Two limitations are especially relevant for engineering evaluation. First, many grouting-reinforcement studies idealize the infill as a continuous, uniform layer, which cannot represent irregular grout fronts and bypass voids commonly formed in rough fractures, thereby weakening the linkage between “diffusion outcome (morphology)” and “mechanical outcome (strength and failure localization)”17,18. Second, while the shear behavior of grouted or filled joints has received sustained attention, the behavior of partially filled, rough-fractured rock under uniaxial or low-confinement loading—conditions prevalent around excavations and mining-induced stress redistribution—has been comparatively less examined, even though it is directly tied to near-field stability assessment19,20. Recent numerical studies have successfully applied coupled continuum discrete methods to simulate grouted rock bolting in deep soft roadways, demonstrating the feasibility of our FLAC3D PFC3D coupling approach for evaluating grout rock composite behavior21.

To bridge these gaps, this study systematically investigates the strength characteristics of grout-infilled rock under various degrees of filling (1/3, 1/2, 2/3, and full) and joint roughness (JRC 1, 5, and 10). We first employ computational fluid dynamics (Fluent) to simulate slurry flow and obtain realistic grout morphologies within rough-walled fractures. A coupled continuous-discrete numerical approach is subsequently utilized, where the rock matrix is modeled as a deformable continuum in FLAC3D and the grout body is represented by a bonded particle assembly in PFC3D. This coupled model enables high-fidelity simulations of both uniaxial compression and fixed-boundary shear tests. By analyzing stress‒strain responses, crack evolution, and stress field distributions, we elucidate the mechanisms through which filling completeness and joint roughness interact to control the peak and residual strengths of grout-infilled rock. These findings provide a more robust basis for evaluating the effectiveness of grouting reinforcements under field conditions.

Methodology

Research workflow and technical approach

(1) Foundation of rough fracture analysis. Joint roughness, a critical factor influencing both fluid flow and shear strength, requires quantitative characterization. This study employs the joint roughness coefficient (JRC) proposed by Barton22, which quantifies roughness by comparing profiles against ten standard references. The natural states of both un-grouted and grouted fractured rock masses (Fig. 1A) provide the foundational context for the investigation.

Research technology roadmap.

(2) Simulation of the slurry diffusion process. A three-dimensional rough-walled fracture model, which is representative of field-scale discontinuities, was constructed (Fig. 1B). Three characteristic JRC profiles (JRC:1, 5, and 10) were selected. The JRC:1 profile (within the range of 0–2) was simplified as a straight line to serve as a smooth control case. The model was built by drafting two-dimensional JRC profiles in CAD, creating a planar fracture with a constant aperture of 4 mm by offsetting one profile, and extruding it vertically by 100 mm to form the final three-dimensional model (100 mm × 100 mm × 4 mm). An aperture of 4 mm was selected on the basis of the criterion established by Phien-wej et al.23, where an infill-thickness-to-asperity-height ratio greater than 2 ensures that the joint strength is governed primarily by the properties of the infill material.

(3) Construction of mechanical test models. The grout morphology within the fracture at four key time steps was processed to extract the coordinates of its boundary and flow front via GETDATA software. These coordinates were imported into CAD to delineate the mesh boundaries, which were subsequently imported into FLAC3D. Using an extrusion command, a penetrated fracture model—specifically, fractures with JRC of 1, 5, and 10 and a constant 4 mm aperture—was established. The model was then trimmed according to the flow front coordinates to finalize the geometry of the grout body. A cubic rock matrix model with a side length of 100 mm, containing the penetrated fracture, was constructed in FLAC3D around the generated grout body. Finally, unconfined compressive strength tests and constant normal stiffness (CNS) shear tests were performed on this coupled model, as illustrated in Fig. 1(C) and (D).

Coupling scheme and experimental program

Coupling scheme

A hybrid continuous-discrete numerical approach was employed in this study. The rock matrix was modeled as a deformable continuum via the finite difference method in FLAC3D, whereas the grout body was represented by an assembly of bonded particles via the discrete element method in PFC3D. The two domains were coupled at each computational time step through a TCP/IP socket connection24, utilizing a wall-zone coupling method (edge-to-edge coupling)25.

The coupling interface is discretized into triangular facets, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Forces and moments arising from contacts between discrete particles and these facets are transferred to the nodes of the continuum mesh. A key consideration in this wall-zone coupling scheme is the generation of a moment M when the contact point C between a particle and a facet does not coincide with the facet’s centroid CP. Experimental scheme

The experimental program was designed to analyze the evolution of plastic zones and stress distributions in rock masses at different grout-filling stages systematically. Thirteen distinct numerical samples were constructed and tested. This set comprised twelve grout-infilled models, encompassing all combinations of three joint roughness levels (JRC : 1, 5, and 10) and four filling degrees (1/3, 1/2, 2/3, and full). An additional intact rock sample was included as a benchmark to validate the numerical parameters and the coupling scheme. A clear naming convention was adopted for the infilled models (e.g., “J5-1/2” denotes a specimen with JRC:5 and 50% filling).

The geometry of the grout infill for each model was derived directly from the simulated slurry diffusion process. To establish a consistent basis for comparison, four observation stages were defined at a fracture height of Z = 50 mm. These stages correspond to grout-front positions at 33, 50, 67, and 100 mm along the flow direction (X-axis), representing filling degrees of 1/3, 1/2, 2/3, and full fracture length, respectively. The simulated times required for the grout front to reach these positions are denoted as t₁ through t₄. Following the workflow outlined in Fig. 1(C), the precise grout morphology captured at each time step (t₁–t₄) was used to define the infill geometry within the corresponding mechanical model.

To maintain consistency across all the simulations, the grout was modeled with fixed properties on the basis of a characterized silicate-cement mixture from prior work26, featuring a water‒solid ratio of 1.5:1, a dynamic viscosity of 11.82 cP, and a density of 1260 kg/m³.

Boundary conditions and model parameters

Slurry diffusion simulation

The boundary conditions were applied as follows: the top, bottom, front, and rear surfaces were set as stationary no-slip walls; the left surface was defined as a velocity inlet (with a velocity v = 1 mm/s and a slurry volume fraction of 100%); and the right surface was set as a pressure outlet. The total cell counts for the JRC:1, 5, and 10 fracture models were 532,919, 558,265, and 555,969, respectively.

Uniaxial compression test

A loading plate friction coefficient of 0.5 was set. The axial loading velocity was 2 mm/s. The calculation stopped after the residual strength stabilized.

Direct shear test

A relatively low shear velocity of 0.02 m/s was used in this experiment. To prevent numerical errors from arising from the excessive overlap of joint surfaces27, the simulation was terminated when the shear strain reached 2%.

Model validation using an intact rock sample

Before the mechanical response of the grout-filled samples was analyzed, an intact rock model without fractures was tested under uniaxial compression as a benchmark (Fig. 3). The intact sample exhibited a typical stress–strain response of brittle rock, including a linear elastic stage, stable crack initiation, peak strength, and postpeak strain softening. The simulated uniaxial compressive strength reaches approximately 32.5 MPa, and the failure mode is characterized by the formation of two intersecting macroscopic shear bands, producing an X-shaped damage pattern in the cross section.

Numerical results of the intact rock sample under uniaxial compression.

The stress field and plastic zone distributions of the intact sample are spatially symmetric and consistent with classical theoretical expectations for homogeneous rock under uniaxial loading31. These results confirm the suitability of the adopted constitutive parameters, mesh resolution, and numerical coupling scheme and provide a reliable reference state for evaluating the strength degradation and failure pattern induced by fracture roughness and grout infilling in subsequent analyses.

Results

Grout diffusion and morphology characteristics under varying roughnesses

Figure 4 shows the simulated evolution of grout morphology in rough-walled fractures with JRC values of 1, 5, and 10 at four representative stages (t₁–t₄), corresponding to filling degrees of 1/3, 1/2, 2/3, and full penetration length, respectively.

Results of slurry diffusion within fractures with different JRCs.

For smooth fracture (JRC : 1), the grout front advances in a stable and nearly planar manner throughout the diffusion process. At all stages, the filled region exhibited a uniform slab-like geometry with a well-defined and continuous front, indicating a relatively homogeneous flow field along the fracture aperture. As the roughness increases to JRC : 5, the grout front becomes progressively nonplanar. Localized curvature and moderate fingering appear, and the advance of the grout shows spatial variability along the fracture plane. Although the overall propagation remains continuous, the filled region displays noticeable undulations that reflect the underlying fracture surface geometry.

In highly rough fractures (JRC : 10), grout diffusion is strongly heterogeneous. The grout front is highly irregular and fragmented and is characterized by pronounced fingering, preferential flow paths, and bypass regions. At intermediate filling stages, isolated grout tongues extend far ahead of the average front position, whereas other regions remain unfilled. Upon full penetration, the final grout body closely conforms to the asperity geometry of the fracture walls, resulting in a markedly nonuniform infill morphology.

Overall, the diffusion results demonstrate a systematic transition from uniform, front-dominated filling in smooth fractures to highly anisotropic, morphology-controlled filling in rough fractures. The spatial distribution and continuity of the grout body are increasingly governed by fracture roughness as the JRC increases.

Stress‒strain curves.

Figure 5 presents the uniaxial stress–strain curves of the grout-infilled rock samples, and Fig. 6 shows the corresponding stress–strain responses of the grout body. Relative to the intact benchmark response (UCS = 32.5 MPa, Fig. 3), all grout-infilled samples clearly exhibit a reduction in peak compressive stress and a modified postpeak evolution.

Stress‒strain curves of the grouted rock mass.

Stress‒strain curves of the grouted body.

(1) Effect of the degree of filling: A greater degree of filling slightly increased the peak strength and elastic modulus of the composite sample but did not fundamentally alter the rock-like mechanical response. The postpeak strength and its evolution were similar across different filling degrees for the same JRC. The positive correlation between the postpeak strength and filling degree became less distinct as the JRC increased.

(2) Effect of Joint Roughness: Roughness exerted a dominant control over UCS. Increasing the JRC led to a significant reduction in the peak strength. The strength difference between fully filled and minimally filled samples was smaller at higher JRCs.

(3) Apparent grout strength: Although the microparameters for the grout were constant, its apparent maximum response within the composite was influenced by the JRC and degree of filling. In smooth joints (JRC:1), the grout quickly reached its strength limit and subsequently contributed mainly to the residual strength. In rough joints, stress redistribution due to asperity interlocking led to a smoother grout stress‒strain process and a rightward shift in apparent strength.

Stress distribution analysis.

Stress distribution analysis.

The cross-sectional stress distribution within the modified rock mass (Fig. 7) reveals distinct stress concentration features induced by grouting, which can be categorized into two primary types on the basis of their genesis and characteristics.

Stress field distribution of the cross section (Z = 50 mm).

Type-I stress concentration: Incomplete filling. This concentration arises primarily from the presence of unfilled cavities within the fracture. The injected grout acts as a stress-transfer medium between the two fracture walls. In regions where the fracture is partially filled (PF), the grout body directly transmits compressive stress, leading to a noticeable stress concentration at its periphery, particularly near the interface between the PF and fully filled (FF) zones. At this interface, the discontinuity in stiffness and load-transfer capacity between the filled and unfilled sections creates a marked stress gradient.

Type-II Stress Concentration: Asperity-Induced. This type is directly governed by joint roughness. The geometrical protrusions (asperities) of the fracture walls act as inherent stress concentrators. Under loading, these asperities experience significant tensile stress at their tips due to the distributed force from the surrounding grout and rock, leading to localized high-stress zones. This phenomenon is a key factor in reducing the overall strength of the composite element, aligning with the observed decline in peak strength in the complete stress‒strain curves.

The analysis demonstrated that grout infusion fundamentally altered the stress field. In unfilled fractures, the boundary is typically open with minimal confining stress (near-zero minor principal stress), resulting in a large deviatoric stress and predisposing the rock to plastic deformation. Grout filling provides a medium for stress transmission, changing this condition. However, the effectiveness of this transmission differs between the FF and PF zones because of their distinct stiffness and deformation characteristics, leading to the aforementioned Type-I concentration at their boundary.

Under identical boundary conditions, the geometric features of rough fractures—namely, the morphology of asperities (controlling Type II) and the spatial distribution of grout (controlling Type I)—are the dominant factors governing the stress field distribution within the composite unit. While the grout enhances stress transfer compared with an open fracture, the introduced heterogeneities (both in filling and wall geometry) create new, complex stress concentrations that critically influence the mechanical response.

Plastic zone differentiation and evolution.

Plastic zone differentiation and evolution.

The transition from diffuse yielding to localized shear banding observed in our models is consistent with recent multiscale studies on brittle rock fracture, which link microcrack initiation to macroscopic failure under compression32. The evolution of plastic zones reveals a transition from material-driven failure to structure-driven failure, which is governed jointly by joint roughness and the degree of filling. An analysis of cross-sections A-A′ and B-B′ in Fig. 8 reveals that the grout alters the boundary conditions of the rock matrix, redistributing stress and controlling yield morphology.

Grout‒rock mass combined element plastic differentiation distribution.

Under smooth joints (JRC:1), yielding is minimal and diffuse, with isolated points in the rock unrelated to grout geometry. As the roughness increases (JRC:5), shear bands form along the grout‒rock interface, indicating structural control. At JRC:10, the plasticity expands further into both the rock matrix and confined grout pockets, with the yield pattern precisely mapping the asperity–grout architecture.

The degree of filling simultaneously governs the spatial organization of yielding. Low fill levels produce a diffuse double “X” pattern across the fracture, transitioning to a consolidated single “X” morphology upon complete filling. This shift correlates with stronger mechanical integration and higher peak strength. Thus, while higher roughness consistently promotes more extensive yielding, complete filling is essential to unify the failure pattern and maximize composite strength.

Shear resistance of grouted rough fractures

Shear stress‒strain curves and peak strength

It is necessary to clarify the “peak strength” and “residual strength” in the subsequent presentation. The “peak strength” is the value of the shear stress at the initial stress drop, and the “residual strength” is a variable value, which is the minimum value to which the shear stress drops and its subsequent values.

Peak strength.

Peak strength.

The peak strength is positively correlated with both the filling level and the joint roughness. Compared with the roughness, the filling level has a more linear effect on the strength. The peak strength increases significantly when the roughness increases from JRC 1 to JRC 5 at greater filling levels; however, this is not the case when the roughness increases from JRC 5 to JRC 10. In the experiments with a roughness of JRC1, the peak strength varied from 0.4 MPa to 1.1 MPa, an increase of nearly two times. It is reasonable to believe that the peak strength at a roughness of JRC 1 is linearly related to the filling level. In the two sets of experiments for JRC 5 and JRC 10, the peak strength increases from 1.3 MPa to 4.7 MPa and 0.67 MPa to 5.4 MPa, respectively, an increase of more than three times, indicating that roughness plays an important role in shear strength.

Shear localization: crack orientation, stress concentration, and mode transition

The shear failure characteristics evolve systematically with increasing joint roughness, as directly quantified from the crack patterns, stress distributions, and plastic zone morphologies presented in Figs. 10, 11 and 12.

Under smooth joint conditions (JRC:1), failure is predominantly an interfacial process. This is evidenced by a crack rosette in which more than 95% of the cracks are aligned within 10° of the shear direction, accompanied by a uniform band of elevated shear stress localized strictly at the upper grout‒matrix interface. The resulting plastic deformation in the rock matrix is minimal and discrete, occupying less than 15% of the cross-sectional area, and bears no correlation to the infill geometry.

As the roughness increases to JRC:5, the failure mechanism transitions from interfacial slip to a structurally controlled process involving the surrounding rock mass. The crack orientation diversifies significantly, with only 65–70% of the cracks remaining near the primary shear axis. This shift coincides with the fragmentation of the stress field into intense, localized concentrations at asperity tips, where values reach 2–3 times the interfacial average. Consequently, a continuous shear band forms within the rock matrix, expanding the plastic zone to cover approximately 40% of the cross-section and mirroring the undulating contour of the grouted interface.

This structure-dominated failure intensifies under high roughness (JRC:10). The crack orientation shows the greatest diversity, with less than 60% aligned with the shear direction. The stress becomes intensely focused on fewer dominant asperities. The most pronounced change is the extensive growth of the plastic zone, which now exceeds 60% of the matrix cross-section. Its morphology exhibits a precise, one-to-one correlation with the fracture geometry, extending into the rock in direct proportion to the asperity height and spacing. This provides direct visual evidence that the failure path is physically dictated by the composite interaction between the rock’s structural anatomy and the grout infill.

Residual strength and failure mechanism

Roughness drastically increased the residual shear strength. Analysis of crack patterns, matrix plastic zones, and stress fields (Figs. 10, 11 and 12) elucidated the mechanisms:

For the cases with lower roughness (JRC:1), the cracks were primarily parallel (dip angle ~ 180°) to the shear direction and were located at the upper grout‒matrix interface. The shear strength depended on the grout cohesion. An increased filling level linearly increased the shear contact area. In contrast, under higher roughness conditions (JRC: 5 & 10), cracks were also concentrated at the interface but with more varied orientations. Joint asperities induce multidirectional shear stresses and increase the normal stress between grout particles, increasing their shear resistance (proportional to the normal stress). The maximum shear stress was concentrated at the asperity tips at the interface. The plastic zones in the rock matrix aligned with the filling extent and asperity locations, indicating that the increase in shear strength inside the grout (due to asperity-induced confinement) could exceed the shear strength of the rock matrix itself. As shear proceeded, continued asperity interlocking and grout dilation further increased the residual strength. Higher asperities (JRC:10) promoted a wider plastic zone and greater strength enhancement.

Discussion

The numerical results reveal a complex, nonlinear interaction between joint roughness (JRC) and the degree of filling, which dictates the strength and failure mode of grout-infilled rock. In contrast to a simple material-addition model, the system’s behavior transitions between two fundamental regimes depending on the loading conditions: one where the strength is compromised by roughness (compression) and another where it is enhanced by it (shear). The following discussion synthesizes the stress and plasticity analyses to elucidate this duality.

The dual roles of asperities

The central finding of this study is the contrasting influence of fracture roughness (JRC) on compressive strength versus CNS shear strength. Similar “opposite trends” have been reported in jointed or weakly filled samples, where roughness can intensify local stress heterogeneity under compression yet promote dilation-related resistance under constrained shear, indicating that asperities do not play a single, monotonic mechanical role across loading paths33.

Under uniaxial compression, asperities act as intrinsic stress concentrators and decoupling agents. The geometric interlock of rough surfaces creates localized high-stress zones at asperity tips and along the irregular grout‒rock interface (Type II concentration in Fig. 7), precipitating premature damage in both the grout and the adjacent rock (consistent with stress-focusing and premature cracking observed in weak-filled rough-joint compression tests)34. This process disrupts efficient, transverse stress transfer, causing the composite to behave as an assembly of loosely coupled columns. Consequently, the system’s strength is dictated by the weakest asperity contact, explaining the profound reduction in UCS with increasing JRC and the marginal role of the degree of filling.

Conversely, under CNS shear, the same asperities become load-transfer mediators and confinement generators. Beyond passive geometric interlocking, the key mechanism is dilation under shear and its suppression by the constant-normal-stiffness boundary: dilation attempts to open the joint, but the boundary reaction increases the mobilized normal stress, especially at contacting asperities. Numerical CNS studies consistently show that rougher joints generate stronger dilation and higher normal-stress feedback under stiffness-controlled boundaries, thereby increasing peak and residual shear resistance35. When grout is present, this asperity-induced normal-stress amplification directly increases the frictional resistance of the grout-bearing interface/assembly in a Coulomb-type manner, and recent grouted-joint modeling similarly reports that increasing roughness can substantially increase both peak and residual shear strength under normal-loading constraints9. In this regime, the degree of filling contributes approximately proportionally by scaling the effective area/extent of grout participation and asperity contact available to mobilize confinement and shear transfer, which is consistent with the results of interface-focused shear tests on postgrouted rock, where the strength evolves with the stress state and interface conditions36.

The evolution of the plastic zones provides visual confirmation of this mechanistic shift (Figs. 8, 10, 11 and 12). The progression from discrete, nonaligned yielding in smoother joints to extensive, interface-mimicking shear bands in rougher joints reflects a transition from an interface-governed response (controlled by bond and local stress concentrations) to a structure-governed response where the asperity–grout assemblage acts as the dominant load-bearing skeleton under CNS shear.

From infill-dominated to structure-dominated behavior

Many existing joint-strength treatments implicitly assume a continuous, homogeneous infill layer and therefore struggle to represent partially filled rough fractures, where grout “morphology” (spatial distribution) can be as important as grout “quantity” (injected volume). Recent diffusion studies have consistently shown that grout propagation in fractured media is typically nonuniform, featuring partial sealing, preferential pathways, and bypassed/eroded regions—especially under flowing-water conditions and network heterogeneity—so that partial filling is a frequent outcome rather than an anomaly4,37. To link such diffusion-controlled morphologies with postgrouting mechanics, a transitional conceptual model is proposed bounded by two behavioral end-members, which is consistent with recent analytical/DEM studies that distinguish regimes dominated by the grout layer versus regimes governed by an asperity-supported joint structure38.

(1) Infill-dominated regime. This regime is approached in smooth or very low-JRC fractures where structural interlocking is limited. The grout body acts as the primary load-bearing element, and the strength can be approximated from the grout properties, which are modified by the grout–rock bond/interface strength and the prevailing normal stress. Interface tests on postgrouted rock further indicate that apparent shear parameters vary with confinement and environmental conditions, supporting an interface/bond-controlled response when asperity effects are weak39.

(2) Structure-dominated regime. This regime governs rough fractures, particularly those under partial filling. The asperity–grout assemblage dictates the response: asperity contact chains and local confinement create a highly nonuniform stress field; confined grout may crush progressively; and asperity engagement/breakage controls shear-band development. CNS studies have shown that the stiffness-controlled normal reaction strongly couples with dilation, so roughness-driven dilation elevates the normal stress and reshapes localization, especially when grout fronts are irregular and contacts are discontinuous34,35. Grouted-joint numerical investigations similarly demonstrated that roughness can markedly increase shear resistance under constrained normal conditions through asperity-induced confinement and interlocking9.

Critically, partial filling inherently shifts the system toward the structure-dominated regime. Unfilled cavities introduce asymmetric stiffness and stress paths, making failure highly sensitive to heterogeneous grout-front morphology and its interaction with asperity contacts rather than to the volumetric filling ratio alone. This interpretation is consistent with diffusion evidence of persistent partial sealing and strong spatial heterogeneity in grout distribution, implying that “where the grout solidifies” becomes a first-order determinant of postgrouting mechanical behavior in rough discontinuities37,40.

Limitations and future perspectives

This study investigates the composite mechanical behavior of grouted rock members containing rough fractures, while several limitations remain. Direct experimental verification of partially filled single fractures under uniaxial compression and shear loading is absent, which also demonstrates the novelty of this research. Furthermore, the model fails to incorporate multiple physicochemical and mechanical mechanisms that play vital roles in practical grouting engineering.

Cement particle filtration occurs at narrow fracture apertures and leads to spatially graded grout properties. Narrow rough fracture surfaces act as filters, particularly in highly permeable fractures. The assumption of homogeneous grout material ignores this phenomenon, which may weaken the overall composite strength. In addition, time-dependent viscosity variation caused by cement hydration and fatigue degradation induced by cyclic loading are not taken into account. Under cyclic loading, the Type‑I stress concentration at the grout front and Type‑II concentration at asperity tips would become preferential sites for fatigue crack initiation. The involved mechanisms include local cyclic plasticity, fretting fatigue at the grout‑rock interface, crack propagation from stress raisers, and progressive asperity degradation. We hypothesize that incomplete filling would significantly reduce fatigue life compared to fully filled or intact rock, and that higher JRC would amplify this effect. A systematic investigation of fatigue behavior under cyclic loading is beyond the scope of this monotonic‑loading study, but is a priority for our next‑stage research.

Future research should explore the coupling interaction among stress, fracture evolution and grouting characteristics to reveal mechanical responses within deformable fractures. The aforementioned mechanisms including particle filtration, time-varying rheology and cyclic loading effect shall be integrated to adapt to diverse grout materials and injection conditions. Direct experimental validation via transparent fracture models and in-situ X-ray CT scanning remains a top priority. Discrete JRC profiles can be replaced by continuous roughness indicators such as fractal dimension and three-dimensional surface characterization to optimize quantitative correlations.

Despite the existing limitations, this study delivers straightforward and practical engineering implications. In-situ roughness parameters obtained from borehole imaging, profile measurement and fractal analysis can guide grout selection and injection design, replacing empirical methods and reducing prediction errors. For rough fractures with high JRC values, low-viscosity grouts combined with moderately elevated injection pressure can effectively improve diffusion performance. Medium and high-viscosity grouts are recommended for smooth fractures to avoid excessive diffusion and material waste. For vertical fractures, staged or variable-pressure injection strategies based on gravity-induced asymmetric distribution can mitigate vertical migration discrepancy. The proposed engineering suggestions are applicable to annular cementing of oil exploration wells, underground working face stabilization and geological slope reinforcement of collapsible fractured rock masses.

Conclusion

This study combined CFD-derived grout morphologies with a coupled DEM–FDM mechanical framework to study how fracture roughness (JRC) and the degree of filling jointly govern the strength and failure characteristics of grout-infilled rock under uniaxial compression and constant normal stiffness (CNS) shear. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) Within the investigated range, fracture roughness is the primary factor controlling the strength “sign reversal” between compression and shear. Under uniaxial compression, increasing JRC promotes pronounced stress localization at asperity tips and along the grout–rock interface, which accelerates damage initiation and reduces effective load transfer across the discontinuity. Under CNS shear, roughness enhances asperity interlocking and dilation-induced normal-stress amplification, thereby increasing both peak and residual shear resistance.

(2) Partial filling introduces a morphology-sensitive mechanical response that cannot be captured by homogeneous-infill idealizations. The unfilled cavity and the irregular grout front generate asymmetric stress paths and discontinuous contact chains, making failure more dependent on the spatial configuration of grout bridges and asperity contacts than on the volumetric filling ratio alone.

(3) A transitional conceptual framework is supported by numerical evidence: the response shifts from an infill-dominated regime (smooth/low-JRC, interface/bond-controlled) to a structure-dominated regime (rough joints, especially with partial filling), where the composite asperity–grout assemblage governs localization, progressive crushing, and failure evolution.
