From Ophthalmoplegia to Multilevel Atrioventricular Block
Authors: Anagh Astavans, Andreas S. Barth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Kearns-Sayre syndrome (KSS) is often recognized for its debilitating ocular phenotype, but its most consequential and potentially lethal complications lie hidden within the cardiac conduction system. In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Amaro et al1 describe a 45-year-old male patient who initially presented with worsening ophthalmologic symptoms, including strabismus, diplopia, and external ophthalmoplegia; the diagnosis of KSS was supported by characteristic clinical features and muscle biopsy findings. He was subsequently referred to cardiology for exertional dyspnea and fatigue, at which point bradycardia, intermittent junctional rhythm, and first- and second-degree atrioventricular (AV) block were detected on electrocardiography (ECG) and 24-hour Holter monitoring, ultimately necessitating early dual-chamber pacemaker implantation. This case emphasizes the importance of heightened vigilance among electrophysiologists and serves as a poignant reminder that conduction disease in mitochondrial disorders may advance quietly and erratically, placing patients at risk of sudden cardiac death (SCD) even before advanced block is firmly established.

KSS is a rare mitochondrial cytopathy affecting roughly 1 to 3 in 100,000 people and classically presenting before age 20 years.2 Neurologic and endocrine symptoms may accompany its characteristic progressive external ophthalmoplegia and pigmentary retinopathy.3 Yet these features, although visually striking, are not what determine prognosis. Cardiac complications, including dilated cardiomyopathy, sinus node dysfunction, and particularly infra-Hisian AV block leading to Stokes-Adams syncope, are common and remain the principal driver of morbidity in this unique condition.4,5 As such, longitudinal cardiac surveillance is central to KSS care.5

Expecting the Unexpected

The key clinical challenge in KSS lies not in recognizing advanced AV block but anticipating its evolution. Subtle signs of cardiac conduction system disease may progress to high-grade AV block over a short interval without clear warning. Typically, ocular symptoms precede the development cardiac conduction disease.6 Even as ECG abnormalities emerge, nonspecific symptoms such as exertional dyspnea and fatigue may be subtle and misattributed to noncardiac causes including mitochondrial myopathy, as illustrated by Amaro et al.1 Moreover, disease expression is heterogeneous; classic bifascicular patterns are not universally present, and multilevel conduction delay may develop unpredictably.7 In this setting, an electrophysiologist must navigate a clinical “gray zone,” weighing continued monitoring against preemptive device therapy, recognizing that the window for safe observation can close abruptly.

Despite current guidelines advising the use of permanent pacing once advanced AV block is documented, rare mitochondrial syndromes such as KSS test how strictly such recommendations should be applied.8 For KSS, current guidelines recommend pacemaker implantation for patients who demonstrate AV conduction abnormalities, particularly if progressive, including fascicular block.6 This recommendation is based on observational data indicating that conduction disease in KSS can quickly evolve from early intraventricular delay to heart block,7 suggesting that waiting for high-grade criteria may miss an opportunity for timely intervention. Reports also describe patients who underwent early prophylactic pacemaker implantation and progressed rapidly to complete heart block, underscoring the rapidly progressive nature of cardiac conduction disease in KSS.9 Beyond conventional ECG findings, no other clinical features or biomarkers have been identified that enhance risk stratification in affected individuals.6 Recent population-level data from Wiseman et al10 demonstrate that KSS patients experience a high incidence of both conduction disease and ventricular arrhythmias, yet device therapy is often underused or suboptimally applied, highlighting real-world gaps in pacing adequacy and risk mitigation. Taken together, these observations and the report by Amaro et al1 illustrate that in KSS, early recognition and judicious, surveillance-driven device selection, rather than rigid application of pacemaker criteria derived from nonmuscular dystrophy populations, are often critical to prevent sudden cardiac deterioration. For patients at particularly high risk, including those with left ventricular (LV) dysfunction, documented ventricular arrhythmias, or extensive LV scar on cardiac magnetic resonance, it may be appropriate to consider implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) placement rather than pacemaker therapy alone.10 Cardiac magnetic resonance may be particularly helpful to refine risk assessment in KSS, as a higher burden of late gadolinium enhancement has been associated with arrhythmic risk and adverse outcomes in neuromuscular disorders, potentially guiding the choice between therapies.7

Choosing the Right Device in KSS

Beyond the decision of whether to implant a cardiac device, the optimal choice of device and pacing strategy warrants thoughtful deliberation. Patients with high-grade AV block and progressive conduction disease can be anticipated to require substantial right ventricular (RV) pacing. Although conventional dual-chamber pacemakers are effective for rhythm management, traditional RV apical pacing can induce ventricular dyssynchrony, predisposing to LV function decline.11 In contrast, the BLOCK-HF trial demonstrated that in patients with AV block and LV ejection fraction ≤50%, biventricular pacing (cardiac resynchronization therapy) reduced the risk of mortality, urgent heart failure care, and adverse LV remodeling compared with conventional RV pacing.12 Given documented myocardial involvement in KSS, including dilated cardiomyopathy,5,13 cardiac resynchronization therapy may be preferable to standard dual-chamber pacing in this complex population.

A further alternative is conduction system pacing such as left bundle branch area pacing, a rapidly emerging technique with favorable clinical outcomes.14 Its efficiency, however, depends on the level of block. Pacing the left bundle area is most effective when the block is primarily at the AV node,15 but if conduction disease is infra-Hisian, as can occur in KSS, the reliability of left bundle branch area pacing in maintaining optimal LV-RV synchrony may be influenced as the disease progresses. Accordingly, appropriate device selection in KSS requires assessment of conduction block location (typically infra-Hisian), expected pacing burden, and risk for cardiomyopathy, in addition to traditional considerations for SCD, which require insertion of an ICD.

More broadly, this case exemplifies a recurring dilemma in metabolic cardiomyopathies: balancing clinical uncertainty against the possibility of catastrophic events. In disorders where conduction disease or myocardial dysfunction can advance rapidly, relying solely on pacing guidelines may offer false reassurance while leaving patients vulnerable to preventable morbidity. For a cardiac electrophysiologist, the implications extend beyond KSS, requiring individualized risk stratification, careful cardiac device choice, and a lower threshold for early action in select high-risk patients. In rare but potentially unstable cardiac conditions, early and decisive patient-centered clinical judgment can be as consequential as the intervention itself.
