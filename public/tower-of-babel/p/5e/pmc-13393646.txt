Integrative machine learning and multi-omics identify a centromere gene signature and validate B3GALT4 as a tumor suppressor in osteosarcoma
Authors: Wenchao Yao, LinPing Zhang, Zhen Jiang, Tao Bao, Junhua Ma, Jiangbo Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCentromere-associated genes are linked to genomic instability and tumor progression, yet their significance and immunological roles in osteosarcoma (OS) remain undefined.Methods:We developed a centromere-associated prognostic gene model (CPGM) by applying machine learning across independent cohorts, including TARGET-OS and GEO databases. The Meta-OS and GEO datasets were integrated to form a comprehensive Meta-OS cohort for further analysis. Model performance was evaluated using Harrell's concordance index (C-index). Then, the optimal model was selected and validated using Kaplan-Meier plotter, time-dependent ROC (tROC), Cox regression, and nomogram construction. Functional enrichment and immune infiltration analyses were performed to characterize the tumor immune microenvironment. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was conducted to assess cellular heterogeneity and intercellular communication. Finally, the function of Beta-1,3-galactosyltransferase 4 (B3GALT4) was validated using in vitro assays to evaluate its effects on OS cell proliferation and migration.Results:The CPGM demonstrated consistent prognostic performance across independent datasets, achieving a maximum C-index at 0.733. The tROC analysis showed strong predictive accuracy, with area under of curve values of 0.857, 0.806, and 0.783 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively. Univariate and multivariate Cox analyses confirmed CPGM as an independent prognostic factor (p = 0.002). Meanwhile, high CPGM scores were associated with poor survival, higher tumor purity, reduced immune infiltration, and suppressed antitumor immune responses. Enrichment analyses indicated significant involvement of immune-related pathways. scRNA-seq analysis revealed that high-CPGM cells were enriched in early developmental trajectories and exhibited enhanced intercellular signaling. Among the model genes, B3GALT4 was consistently downregulated in OS tissues and cell lines, and low expression correlated with poor prognosis. Overexpression of B3GALT4 significantly inhibited the proliferation, migration and invasion of OS cells.Conclusion:This study establishes a robust centromere-associated prognostic model linked to immune dysregulation in OS and identifies B3GALT4 as a potential tumor suppressor and therapeutic target.

Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS) is the most common primary malignant bone tumor in children and adolescents, predominantly arising in the metaphysis of long bones [1]. Its aggressive biological behavior contributes to high morbidity and mortality. In recent years, advances in treatments have increased the 5-year survival rate for patients with localized OS from approximately 20% to 70% [2]. However, the prognosis remains poor for patients with distant metastases, whose 5-year survival rate is below 20%. Notably, more than 20% of patients present with metastases at initial diagnosis [3]. OS is a highly heterogeneous malignancy with distinct molecular subtypes, thereby complicating the development of precision therapeutic strategies [4]. Elucidating the molecular mechanisms driving metastasis and identifying novel biomarkers for early detection and therapeutic intervention are therefore critical for improving patient outcomes [5]. Current classification systems have limited accuracy in predicting individualized survival, highlighting the urgent need for robust molecular models and reliable tools to guide personalized management of OS patients.

The centromere is a specialized chromatin structure that ensures faithful chromosome segregation by serving as the assembly platform for the kinetochore complex during mitosis and meiosis [6], [7]. It is composed of repetitive DNA sequences and conserved centromere proteins (CENPs), which together maintain chromosomal stability during cell division [7], [8]. Dysregulation of CENPs perturbs centromere–kinetochore architecture through aberrant protein localization, resulting in chromosome segregation defects and sustained genomic instability that contributes to tumor development [9], [10], [11]. Although centromere dysfunction has been broadly implicated in cancer development, its functional and prognostic relevance in OS remains insufficiently defined.

Recent advances in high-throughput and scRNA-seq technologies have enabled comprehensive characterization of tumor heterogeneity at unprecedented resolution, providing new insights for molecular classification and prognostic modeling in OS [12], [13]. The integration of large-scale transcriptomic data into risk stratification models has improved prognostic assessment and facilitated the identification of potential therapeutic targets [14], [15], [16], [17]. In breast cancer, centromere-associated gene signatures have been reported to improve molecular stratification and prognostic prediction [8]. However, no study has systematically integrated centromere-associated genes with multi-omics and single-cell analysis to develop a robust prognostic model in OS.

In this study, we established a centromere-associated prognostic gene model (CPGM) using machine learning (ML) approaches to systematically evaluate the prognostic relevance of centromere-related genes (CRGs) in OS. We further characterized the immunological landscape associated with different CPGM risk groups and explored their cellular heterogeneity using scRNA-seq analysis. In addition, we identified B3GALT4 as a potential tumor-suppressor. Collectively, this work provides novel insights into centromere-associated molecular signatures and highlights potential therapeutic targets for OS.

Methods

Data collection

The TARGET-OS data, GSE21257, GSE16091, GSE33382, GSE36001 and GSE42352 were collected and used in our previous study. The TARGET-OS, GSE21257 and GSE16091 datasets comprised 85, 53 and 34 patients, respectively, each with complete transcriptomic data and corresponding prognostic information. An integrated GEO-OS cohort was constructed by combining the GSE21257 and GSE16091 datasets. And then, The GEO-OS, GSE33382, and TARGET-OS datasets were integrated to generate a comprehensive Meta-OS cohort. Data preprocessing was carried out consistently with methods described in our previously published studies [12]. Briefly, gene expression data were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus using the “GEOquery” package. The datasets were subsequently integrated, and batch effects were corrected using the “ComBat” algorithm in the “sva” package. The GSE42352 dataset included 3 osteoblasts (OBs), 12 mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and 19 OS cell lines (OSs), while the GSE36001 dataset comprised 6 osteoblasts/bone tissue samples (OBs) and 19 OSs [18].

Construction of CPGM

The CPGM model was developed using an ML-based integrative framework that evaluated 101 distinct algorithmic combinations according to previous studies [12], [19]. Ten classical ML algorithms were incorporated, including penalized regression methods (Enet, LASSO, and Ridge regression), boosting-based approaches (GBM and CoxBoost), kernel-based survival modeling (Survival-SVM), dimension-reduction–based Cox models (plsRcox and SuperPC), random survival forest (RSF), and stepwise Cox regression (StepCox) [19]. Following the established framework, these algorithms were used either independently or sequentially as feature-selection and model-construction procedures, generating a total of 101 eligible algorithmic combinations. CRGs were retrieved from the GeneCards database, and genes with a correlation coefficient greater than 0.15 were included in the analysis; the complete gene list is provided in Sup List 1 [8]. First, the univariate-COX (uni-COX) analysis was performed in the TARGET-OS and GEO-OS cohorts to identify centromere-related prognostic genes (CPGs) respectively. Genes with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant, and overlapping CPGs between the two cohorts were selected for model construction. Subsequently, 101 ML-based algorithmic combinations were applied to construct prognostic models using the TARGET cohort as the training set, with validation performed in the GEO-OS cohort and further assessment conducted in the independent GSE33382 dataset. Feature selection and model development were performed within the training cohort to minimize potential information leakage. Hyperparameter tuning was conducted using cross-validation-based optimization procedures. Specifically, a fixed random seed (seed = 1) was applied throughout all analyses to ensure reproducibility. Ten-fold cross-validation was used for model training and parameter optimization. For penalized regression models, including LASSO and Ridge regression, optimal regularization parameters (λ) were determined using the built-in cross-validation procedures implemented in the corresponding R packages (e.g., glmnet). For CoxBoost and GBM models, model-specific hyperparameters were optimized through internal cross-validation to identify optimal parameter settings. Harrell's concordance index (C-index) was applied as the primary metric for evaluating model performance [20]. A parsimonious model exhibiting the highest average C-index across evaluations was selected as the CPGM model for downstream analyses.

Evaluation of the prognostic predictive ability of the CPGM

Furthermore, CPGM risk scores were calculated for all patients in the TARGET-OS, GSE33382, and GEO-OS cohorts using the established CPGM model. The predictive accuracy of the CPGM model for patient prognosis was subsequently assessed using tROC curves. In addition, the optimal cutoff value of the CPGM score was determined using the “surv_cutpoint” package, based on which patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups. Kaplan–Meier (K-M) plotters were then generated to compare survival outcomes between the two groups.

Clinical stratification analysis of the CPGM model

Uni-COX analysis was applied to examine the associations between prognosis and different variables, including the CPGM score, clinical characteristics, and cohort sources. Patients in different cohorts were stratified into subgroups based on age (≥18 vs. <18 years), sex (male vs. female), metastatic status (M0 vs. M1), Huvos grades (I/II and III/IV), and primary tumor site (axial, lower limb and upper limb). Differences in CPGM scores were analyzed across patient subgroups stratified by clinical features. The predictive performance of the CPGM score for Huvos grading was assessed using tROC analysis.

Independent prognostic analysis and nomogram establishment

To further assess the independent prognostic value of the CPGM model, multivariate Cox regression (multi-COX) analysis was performed. Then, a nomogram for individualized prediction was constructed by integrating the CPGM score with clinical variables. The predictive performance of the nomogram was comprehensively evaluated using tROC curves, conventional ROC curves, calibration plot and decision curve analysis (DCA). The calibration plot was drawn using the “rms” and “regplot” packages, and the DCA curve was conducted using the “ggDCA” function.

Differential analysis and functional enrichment between high- and low-CPGM groups

The differentially expressional genes (DEGs) between high- and low-CPGM groups in TARGET-OS dataset were identified using “limma” function. Genes with a p value <0.05 and a ∣fold change∣ greater than 1.2 were considered differentially expressed. Subsequently, the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Gene Ontology (GO), Gene Set Enrichment KEGG (GSE-KEGG), GSE-GO and GSE-pathway analyses were performed for functional analyses, and adjusted p values were used to determine statistical significance in enrichment analyses.

Immune cell infiltration and immune checkpoint analysis

Tumor immune microenvironment (TME) characteristics were assessed based on gene expression profiles of mesenchymal and immune cells. Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was applied to estimate the relative abundance of diverse immune cell populations in individual patients, while the Estimation of STromal and Immune cells in MAlignant Tumours using Expression data (ESTIMATE) algorithm was employed to compute immune- and stromal-related scores. Comparisons between high- and low-CPGM score groups in the TARGET-OS cohort were performed to evaluate differences in immune cell infiltration and TME composition. Additionally, the expression levels of key immune checkpoint genes were statistically compared using Student's t-test. All visualization of results was conducted with the “ggplot2” R package.

scRNA-seq data downloading and preprocessing

The GSE162454 dataset, including the scRNA-seq data from 6 OS tissues, was acquired from the GEO website and preprocessed using Seurat v5.0 [21]. Genes detected in fewer than three cells or with fewer than 200 expressed features were excluded prior to analysis to ensure data quality. Cells with fewer than 300 or more than 4500 detected genes, as well as those with mitochondrial gene content exceeding 10%, were removed to ensure data quality. Ultimately, a total of 29,278 cells were included in the subsequent analysis. Then, gene expression data were normalized using the “NormalizeData” function in Seurat, with the default “LogNormalize” method. The top 2000 highly variable genes were subsequently identified for principal component analysis (PCA). Batch effects across different samples were corrected using the “harmony” algorithm. Cell clustering was performed using the “FindNeighbors” and “FindClusters” functions, and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) was employed for visualization. The optimal clustering resolution was determined to be 0.1 based on the “clustree” function. Cell-type annotation was carried out using previously established marker genes [22]. To quantify pathway activity at the single-cell level, CPGM scores were calculated using the “AUCell” package, and cells were stratified into high- and low-CPGM groups according to the median score. The relative proportions of different cell types between these two groups were further analyzed. Pseudotime trajectories of OS cells were inferred using “monocle 2.0” to explore their developmental progression. Finally, intercellular communication between the high- and low-CPGM groups was investigated using the “CellChat” package.

Cell culture

The hFOB1.19, MNNG/HOS, U2OS and 143B cell lines were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The MG63 and HEK293T cell lines was purchased from Procell (Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Lentiviral packaging and production

Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK293T cells using a standard transient transfection protocol. pLV3-CMV-B3GALT4(human)-3 × FLAG-Puro, psPAX2 and PMD2.G were purchased from MiaoLingBio (Wuhan, China). Briefly, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with the lentiviral expression plasmid and the packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G using polyethylenimine (PEI), according to the manufacturer's instructions. The culture medium was replaced 6 h after transfection. Viral supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 h post-transfection, centrifuged to remove cell debris, and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter. The viral stocks were either used immediately or stored at −80 °C for subsequent experiments.

GENERATION of stable cell lines

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates and infected with 400 μL of the collected lentiviral supernatant. After 48 h of transduction, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium, followed by puromycin selection for 7 days. Ultimately, stable HOS and 143B cell lines with B3GALT4 overexpression were successfully established.

Cell proliferation assays

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay and the plate colony formation assay. The detailed experimental procedures were performed in accordance with previously published methods [12].

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Transwell chambers were used to assess cell migration and invasion abilities. For invasion assays, the upper chambers were precoated with Matrigel, whereas migration assays were performed without Matrigel coating. The lower chambers were filled with culture medium containing 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cells were resuspended in serum-free medium at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL, and 200 μL of the cell suspension was added to the upper chamber. After incubation for 24 h, the chambers were collected, and cells remaining on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed. The migrated or invaded cells on the lower surface were fixed with paraformaldehyde for 15 min and subsequently stained with crystal violet. Images were captured under EVOS M7000, and quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Wound-healing assay

Cells were seeded into six-well plates and cultured until reaching approximately 90–100% confluence. A sterile 200 μL pipette tip was used to create a linear scratch across the cell monolayer. The detached cells were gently removed by washing with PBS, and serum-free medium was added to minimize the influence of cell proliferation. Images of the wound area were captured at 0 h and at 24 h using an inverted microscope. The wound width was measured using ImageJ software.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The concentration and purity of RNA were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Subsequently, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Cat. No #RR036A, Takara, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using Hieff UNICON® Universal Blue qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Cat. No #11184ES08, Yeasen Biotechnology, China) on Roche LightCycler® 96 PCR system. The PCR cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. 18S RNA was used as the internal control for normalization. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. The primers used in this study were as follows: B3GALT4 forward 5′- CGCTATTCTTGCTGGGAGAG-3′ and reverse 5′- GGGTCTTTAGGGTGAGGTTG-3′; 18s forward 5′- GCAATTATTCCCCATGAACG-3′ and reverse 5′- GGGACTTAATCAACGCAAGC-3′. All reactions were carried out in triplicate.

Western blot (WB) analysis

Total cellular proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS–PAGE and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature and washed with PBST, followed by incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The primary antibodies used were anti-β-actin (Cat. #66009–1-Ig, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) and anti-B3GALT4 (Cat. #13490–1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). After washing with PBST three times, the membranes were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. After additional PBST washes, protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system.

Results

Development and validation of CPGM

The construction process of the CPGM model was shown in Fig. 1. The GSE21257 and GSE16091 datasets were integrated and batch effects were removed using the previously described method [12]. Uni-Cox regression analysis was conducted in the TARGET-OS and GEO-OS cohorts to identify CPGs. Overlapping genes between the two cohorts were identified using a Venn diagram, resulting in 10 shared CPGs (Fig. 2A), which were subsequently applied to the development of 101 ML-based models. A total of 101 ML models were constructed and validated across the TARGET-OS, GEO-OS, and GSE33382 cohorts. Among them, the RSF and stepCOX + RSF models exhibited the highest average C-index values (0.733, Fig. 2B). Given the balance between robust performance and model simplicity, the RSF model was chosen for further model development. Genes with a variable importance value exceeding 0.02 were selected to establish the final CPGM based on the RSF model (Fig. 2B). Risk scores were then calculated for all samples using the CPGM, and its predictive performance was assessed across various datasets. Patients in different cohorts were divided into high- and low-CPGM groups using the “surv_cutpoint” function (Sup Fig. 1A). The K-M plotters demonstrated that patients in the high-CPGM group exhibited significantly worse prognosis than those in the low-CPGM group across the Meta-OS (p < 0.0001), TARGET-OS (p < 0.0001), GEO-OS (p = 0.0016), GSE33382 (p < 0.00096), GSE21257 (p = 0.001), and GSE16091 (p = 0.063) datasets (Fig. 2D-F and sup Fig. 1B-D). In addition, tROC analysis showed that the AUCs of the Meta-OS cohort at 1, 3, and 5 years were 0.857, 0.806, and 0.783, respectively (Fig. 2G). In the TARGET-OS cohort, the corresponding AUCs were 0.938, 0.988, and 0.983 (Fig. 2H), while in the GSE33382 cohort, the AUCs were 0.847, 0.690, and 0.642 (Fig. 2I). For the GEO-OS cohort, the 1-, 3-, and 5-year AUCs were 0.773, 0.696, and 0.650 (Sup Fig. 1E). The tROC analyses were also performed in the GSE16091 and GSE21257 cohorts, showing consistent predictive performance (Sup Fig. 2F-G). Clinical stratification analysis of the CPGM

Subsequently, uni-Cox regression analysis was performed in the Meta-OS cohort to evaluate the associations between clinical characteristics, CPGM scores and patient prognosis. The results indicated that the CPGM score was significantly associated with patient prognosis (Fig. 3A). In addition, Huvos grades, age, metastasis status, and primary tumor site were also correlated with prognosis (Fig. 3A), which was consistent with the results of previous analyses [12]. Subsequently, we conducted a stratified analysis of patients. The results showed that in the Meta-OS and TARGET-OS datasets, CPGM scores in patients with Huvos grade III/IV were significantly lower than those in patients with Huvos grade I/II (Fig. 3B). In the GSE33382 and GEO-OS datasets, CPGM scores tended to be lower in patients with Huvos grade III/IV than in those with grade I/II, although the observed differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, across all four datasets, patients with metastasis consistently exhibited higher CPGM scores than those without metastasis (Fig. 3C). Moreover, no statistically significant differences in CPGM scores were observed among patients stratified by age, sex or primary tumor site (Sup Fig. 2A-C). Given the established prognostic value of Huvos grading in OS patients, tROC curve analysis was performed to assess the ability of the CPGM model to discriminate Huvos grades. Independent prognostic evaluation and nomogram model construction

Subsequently, multi-Cox regression analysis was performed to further investigate the associations between CPGM score, age, metastatic status, primary tumor location and patient prognosis (Fig. 4A). The results indicated that CPGM score, metastatic status and Huvos grades were independent prognostic factors for OS patients (Fig. 4A). To further enhance predictive accuracy, a nomogram was constructed by integrating the CPGM model with relevant clinical variables (Fig. 4B). The predictive performance of the nomogram, the CPGM model alone, and individual clinical characteristics was then evaluated using ROC curve analysis. Notably, the AUC values of the nomogram for 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival were 0.925, 0.895, and 0.937, respectively (Fig. 4D). Functional enrichment and immune analyses

To further explore the regulatory roles of CRGs in OS, DEGs between the high-CPGM and low-CPGM groups in the TARGET-OS dataset were identified by “limma” package. A total of 1099 differentially expressed genes were identified, including 491 upregulated and 608 downregulated genes (Fig. 5A). Subsequent GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that these genes were predominantly involved in immune-related biological processes and pathways (Sup Fig. 3A-B). Furthermore, GSEA based on GO terms, KEGG pathways, and Reactome pathways highlighted several key immune regulatory pathways, including positive regulation of immune system processes, immune system processes, Th17 cell differentiation, and the T cell receptor signaling pathway (Fig. 5B-C and Sup Fig. 3C). Based on these findings, we further conducted comprehensive immunological analyses comparing the high- and low-CPGM groups. The results indicated that patients in the high CPGM group exhibited significantly higher tumor purity than those in the low CPGM group, whereas the immune score, stromal score, and ESTIMATE score were markedly lower in the high CPGM group (Fig. 5D). Moreover, tumor purity showed a gradual increase with rising CPGM scores, while the immune score, stromal score, and ESTIMATE score exhibited a corresponding decreasing trend as CPGM score increased (Fig. 5E). To further characterize the immune landscape, we evaluated the infiltration status of immune cells in both groups. Notably, the high CPGM group demonstrated a significant reduction in tumor-killing immune cells, including activated B cells, activated CD4+ T cells, and activated CD8+ T cells (Fig. 5F). In addition, significant differences were observed in the expression of multiple immune-related factors between the two groups, such as CD274 (PD-L1), CTLA4, and LAG3 (Sup Fig. 3D). CPGM-based scRNA-seq data analysis

The scRNA-seq data (GSE162454), including six OS patients, were utilized to explore the role of CRGs in OS. Following batch effect correction, PCA, UMAP, and t-SNE analyses indicated a marked reduction in batch effects among individual samples (Sup Fig. 4A). The optimal clustering resolution was determined to be 0.1 using the “clustree” function (Sup Fig. 4B). Based on this resolution, the cells were divided into 12 distinct clusters (Fig. 6A). Subsequently, cell type annotation was performed according to previously reported marker genes (Fig. 6B). The cell populations were annotated as myeloid cells, OS cells, NK/T cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), plasma cells, endothelial cells, B cells, and osteoclasts (Fig. 6C). The CPGM score was calculated for each cell in the single-cell transcriptomic dataset based on the genes included in the CPGM model, and the distribution of cellular CPGM scores was visualized using t-SNE plots (Fig. 6D). Cells were subsequently stratified into high- and low-CPGM groups according to the median CPGM score. Comparative analysis of cellular composition revealed that the high-CPGM group contained a higher proportion of OS cells and a lower proportion of NK/T cells (Fig. 6E). To further explore the dynamic characteristics of OS cells, all OS cells were extracted for pseudotime trajectory analysis. The results demonstrated that the CPGM score was elevated at the early stages of OS cell developmental trajectories, indicating a close association between CPGM score and the initiation and progression of OS cells (Fig. 6F). In addition, intercellular communication was systematically analyzed between the two groups. Overall signaling intensity was significantly greater in the high-CPGM group than in the low-CPGM group (Fig. 6G). Notably, enhanced signaling interactions were observed between OS cells and myeloid cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), and endothelial cells in the high-CPGM group (Fig. 6H). Identifying key centromere gene in OS

To further investigate key centromere-associated genes in OS, we analyzed the 7 CPGs included in the CPGM model using the TARGET-OS dataset. The results showed that TERT, MYC, RPL7, MTHFD2 and RPL10A were significantly upregulated in the high-CPGM group, whereas B3GALT4 was significantly downregulated (Fig. 7A). Single-cell analysis revealed that MYC was predominantly expressed in OS cells, while B3GALT4 exhibited consistently low expression across OS tissues (Fig. 7B). In addition, TERT showed low expression at the single-cell level, which was inconsistent with the bulk transcriptomic data, whereas RPL7, MTHFD2, and RPL10A were highly expressed but lacked clear cell-type specificity (Fig. 7A-B). Given that TERT and MYC have been well characterized in OS [23], [24], we focused on B3GALT4, which showed the most significant statistical association (lowest p value) in our analysis but remains poorly understood. Prognostic analyses using the TARGET-OS, GSE21257, and GSE33382 cohorts consistently demonstrated that low B3GALT4 expression was associated with unfavorable prognosis in OS patients (Fig. 7C). In addition, B3GALT4 expression was markedly lower in OS cells than in normal osteoblasts (Fig. 7D). Consistently, experimental validation showed that both mRNA and protein levels of B3GALT4 were significantly reduced in OS cell lines compared with the osteoblastic cell line hFOB1.19 (Fig. 7E-F). Overexpression of B3GALT4 inhibited proliferation, invasion and migration of OS cells

Subsequently, MNNG/HOS and 143B osteosarcoma cell lines, which exhibited relatively low endogenous B3GALT4 expression, were selected for functional investigations. Stable cell lines with enforced B3GALT4 overexpression were successfully established, and the overexpression efficiency was confirmed by WB analysis (Fig. 8A). Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay, which demonstrated that ectopic B3GALT4 expression significantly attenuated the growth rate of both 143B and MNNG/HOS cells compared with the control group (Fig. 8 B). Consistently, colony formation assays revealed that B3GALT4-overexpressing cells formed significantly fewer colonies with reduced colony size, indicating impaired clonogenic capacity (Fig. 8 C). Furthermore, Transwell migration and invasion assays showed that B3GALT4 overexpression markedly suppressed the migratory and invasive potential of 143B and MNNG/HOS cells (Fig. 8 D). In agreement with these findings, wound-healing assays demonstrated a significantly decreased wound closure rate in the B3GALT4-overexpressing group relative to the negative control group (Fig. 8 E). Discussion

Over the past four decades, advances in multimodal therapy have improved survival for patients with localized OS; however, outcomes for those with advanced or metastatic disease remain poor [25]. The pronounced molecular heterogeneity of OS limits the therapeutic efficacy and represents a major barrier to precision oncology. A critical unmet clinical need is the absence of robust and reliable molecular stratification systems that are capable of guiding individualized treatment decisions. Increasing evidence suggests that centromere abnormalities contribute to tumor initiation and progression, and centromere-associated genes have emerged as potential prognostic biomarkers in various malignancies [11], [26]. Nevertheless, their functional and clinical relevance in OS had not been systematically characterized.

In our study, we developed a CPGM by integrating multiple independent datasets and systematically evaluating 101 ML algorithms. The seven-gene-based RSF model was selected for its superior average C-index (0.733) and parsimonious structure. CPGM demonstrated robust predictive performance for overall survival and Huvos grades, that higher scores were consistently associated with poor prognosis and lower Huvos grades. Stratified analyses further revealed higher CPGM scores in patients with distant metastasis and lower scores in those with higher Huvos grades. To enhance clinical applicability, we established a nomogram incorporating CPGM, which exhibited strong concordance between predicted and observed survival outcomes. scRNA-seq analysis revealed that OS cells displayed higher CPGM scores at early developmental states, which progressively decreased along maturation trajectories, suggesting a potential role of CRGs in early tumor progression. Collectively, these findings highlight the prognostic value of centromere-associated gene signatures and their potential utility in risk stratification and personalized treatment of OS.

We also investigated the TME in the high-CPGM and low-CPGM groups. The TME is a highly heterogeneous and complex ecosystem composed of cellular components, including stromal cells, endothelial cells, and diverse immune cells, as well as non-cellular elements [27]. Immune cells encompass tumor-associated macrophages, tumor-associated neutrophils, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, mast cells, T cells, B cells, natural killer cells, and dendritic cells, while non-immune cells such as MSCs and circulating tumor cells interact with immune components to establish an immunosuppressive network [28]. Accumulating evidence highlights the critical role of TME remodeling in OS tumorigenesis, progression, metastasis, and drug resistance, as well as its impact on patient prognosis [29]. In this context, low immune cell infiltration is indicative of weakened antitumor immunity, facilitating tumor immune evasion [28]. Emerging studies have demonstrated that CRGs can modulate the TME. For instance, GSG2 suppresses antitumor immune responses and promotes endometrial cancer progression via PD-1/PD-L1 regulation [30]. Similarly, centromere proteins have been implicated in immune evasion across multiple malignancies [31], [32], [33]. Consistent with these observations, our analysis revealed that patients in the high-CPGM group exhibited reduced immune cell infiltration and increased tumor purity. Specifically, key cytotoxic populations, including activated CD4+ T cells, activated CD8+ T cells, effector memory CD8+ T cells, and natural killer T cells, were significantly diminished in high-CPGM group. In addition, multiple immune checkpoint genes displayed differential expression between the high- and low-CPGM groups, further supporting the presence of an immunosuppressive landscape associated with elevated centromere gene activity. Nevertheless, the underlying mechanisms linking elevated centromere gene activity to immune suppression and immune evasion in OS warrant further experimental investigation.

Apart from establishing a CPGM for stratifying high-risk OS patients, we also identified B3GALT4 as a potential therapeutic target for OS. B3GALT4 is a key glycosyltransferase involved in the biosynthesis of ganglioside GD2 and has been implicated in the regulation of multiple malignancies [34], [35], [36]. Previous studies have shown that B3GALT4 overexpression suppressed cell migration in neuroblastoma and enhanced CXCL9 and CXCL10 expression, thereby facilitating CD8+ T-cell recruitment [36]. In OS, although prior evidence suggested a possible association between B3GALT4 expression and patient prognosis, its precise biological function remains insufficiently defined [37]. By integrating scRNA-seq and bulk transcriptomic analyses, we demonstrated that B3GALT4 was significantly downregulated in OS cells. Consistently, its expression was markedly higher in the normal osteoblastic cell line hFOB1.19 than in OS cell lines. Functional assays further revealed that ectopic overexpression of B3GALT4 in 143B and HOS cells significantly inhibited tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, supporting a tumor-suppressive role in OS. Nevertheless, considering its reported involvement in immune regulation in other tumor types, the impact of B3GALT4 on immune infiltration and the tumor immune microenvironment in osteosarcoma warrants further investigation.

Conclusion

In this study, we established a centromere-associated prognostic gene model (CPGM) for OS by integrating multi-omics data and ML approaches. The model demonstrated stable prognostic performance and effectively stratified patients by survival risk. Our analyses further suggested that elevated CPGM scores were associated with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and distinct cellular states, highlighting a potential link between centromere-related genes and tumor progression.

In addition, we identified B3GALT4 as a potential tumor suppressor and showed that its overexpression inhibited OS cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro. These findings provided both predictive and biological insights that may support future therapeutic strategies.

This work was strengthened by multi-cohort integration, systematic model evaluation, and combined computational and experimental validation. However, several limitations should be noted, including the reliance on retrospective datasets, potential residual batch effects, and the lack of in vivo and mechanistic validation. Further prospective studies are required to confirm the clinical utility of the model and to clarify the biological role of B3GALT4 and centromere-associated pathways in OS.
