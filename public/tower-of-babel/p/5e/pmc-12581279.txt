Visual rehabilitation of a fighter pilot by utilizing a combination of contact lenses: a case report
Authors: Nir Erdinest, Yossi Eshel, Nadav Shemesh, Shoham Kubovsky, Moodi Kupperman, Shani Morad, David Landau, Itay Lavy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPenetrating keratoplasty is often necessary when corneal scars impact the visual axis. Post-surgical irregular astigmatism resulting from corneal deformity may necessitate the use of rigid gas permeable or scleral contact lenses to enhance visual quality. However, lens fitting becomes challenging when irregularity involves the peripheral cornea. Pinhole contact lenses are used to improve visual acuity and reduce glare in patients with iris transillumination defects.Case presentationThis case report describes the successful design and fitting of a quadrant asymmetric peripheral scleral contact lens combined with a pinhole contact lens (a piggyback design) in a 50-year-old Ashkenazi Jewish male of Israeli origin, a fighter pilot with prior iatrogenic iris damage and mild intraocular lens subluxation who underwent a penetrating keratoplasty. Following the quadrant asymmetric scleral lens fitting, the patient’s best-corrected visual acuity in the left eye dramatically improved. In photopic conditions, his distance visual acuity improved from 1.0 logMAR to 0.0 logMAR, and near visual acuity improved from Jaeger 13 to Jaeger 1 with +2.50 D reading spectacles worn over the scleral and pinhole contact lenses. Similar improvements were observed in mesopic conditions, with distance visual acuity improving from 1.3 logMAR to 0.04 logMAR and near visual acuity improving from Jaeger 13 to Jaeger 2.0. In addition, the patient’s 10% low-contrast distance visual acuity in the left eye improved from 1.5 logMAR to 0.22 logMAR. The low-contrast visual acuity (10%) in the left eye improved from 1.5 logMAR to 0.7 logMAR, and the patient’s Quality of Vision questionnaire score improved from 78 to 15. Moreover, the contrast sensitivity measurements across various spatial frequencies were better with the scleral and pinhole lens combination than with spectacles. Furthermore, the patient had mild ptosis that improved after 12 hours of contact lens wear. The lenses were well tolerated for 12 hours of daily wear, with stable visual acuity and no signs of ocular surface damage over a 1-year follow-up period. Patient data were collected after written informed consent.ConclusionThis case highlights the potential of using a quadrant asymmetric peripheral scleral contact lens combined with a pinhole contact lens to address complex corneal irregularities and glares associated with iris irregularities and intraocular lens dislocation to improve patient outcomes.Keywords: Visual rehabilitation, Iris defect, Quadrant asymmetric scleral contact lenses, Pinhole contact lens

Introduction

Penetrating keratoplasty (PKP) is often necessary when corneal scars impact the visual axis. Post-surgical irregular astigmatism resulting from corneal deformity may eventually necessitate the use of rigid gas permeable (RGP) contact lenses or, in more extreme cases, scleral contact lenses to improve visual quality [1, 2]. In cases where irregularity involves the peripheral cornea, fitting a contact lens can be exceptionally challenging owing to the resulting asymmetry, which may extend into the sclera, making lens stabilization challenging [1, 2]. In these cases, asymmetric design may offer a beneficial solution.

Iris damage, whether caused by surgery or trauma (blunt or penetrating), can result in iris defects, iridodialysis (detachment of the iris from its root at the anterior chamber angle), and corectopia (displacement of the pupil from its normal, central position). These conditions might have significant functional and aesthetic implications [1, 3]. From a functional standpoint, patients may experience glare, monocular diplopia, and decreased visual acuity. Iatrogenic iris defects can negatively affect a patient’s self-esteem, potentially leading to psychosocial distress. In the present case, the patient experienced frustration and reduced self-confidence following the ocular trauma and visual decline. He declined to consider surgery again and expressed apprehension about undergoing another procedure, further reinforcing the need for a nonsurgical visual rehabilitation approach.

Several surgical techniques can address iris damage, each with its own advantages and considerations. A more conservative treatment option is the use of a pinhole contact lens that limits and directs the light entering the eye, thus reducing symptoms such as glare and monocular diplopia [4, 5]. Intraocular lens (IOL) subluxation following cataract surgery might also cause decreased vision, monocular diplopia, and glare. Addressing this condition may also necessitate conservative treatments, surgical procedures, or a combination of both [6].

This case report presents for the first time a successful application of a piggyback design, combining a quadrant asymmetric peripheral scleral lens on top of a pinhole contact lens, to manage complex corneal irregularities, iris defect, and mild IOL subluxation.

Case presentation

This was a retrospective case, in which patient information was extracted from the electronic medical records after written informed consent had been obtained.

Patient information

The patient is a 50-year-old Ashkenazi Jewish male of Israeli origin, a fighter pilot and instructor in the Israeli Air Force of Jewish ethnicity. He had previously experienced a retinal detachment in the left eye secondary to posterior vitreous detachment and underwent a traumatic phacoemulsification and vitrectomy with gas injection 2 years prior to presenting to our clinic. Phacoemulsification was performed through a temporal clear corneal incision of approximately 2.8 mm, using the stop-and-chop technique. A nasal side-port incision was created to facilitate bimanual manipulation.

During the surgery, a posterior lens capsule rupture occurred, necessitating IOL implantation in the sulcus. In addition, iatrogenic damage to the iris resulted in a sectoral iris defect. The digital slit lamp CSO Elite SL 9900 (CSO, Costruzione Strumenti Oftalmici, Firenze, Italy) was used to identify the sectoral iris defect between the 10- and 11-o’clock positions. The extent of the iris defect was quantitatively assessed using digital image analysis with the Image Pro-Plus, version 7.0, system (Media Cybernetics, Silver Spring, USA). High-resolution images were obtained via both a digital slit-lamp camera and a three-dimensional (3D) anterior segment model generated by the Pentacam HR Scheimpflug tomography system (Oculus Optikgeräte GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). The area of the intact iris was delineated, and the region corresponding to the defect was segmented. The calculated defect area represented approximately 9.8% of the total iris surface. Slit-lamp examination revealed a sectoral iris defect located between the 10- and 11-o’clock positions (Fig. 1a–c).

During the comprehensive medical history assessment, the patient revealed that he had maintained exceptionally sharp visual acuity prior to the case. Following the traumatic event and subsequent visual impairment, he experienced psychological distress characterized by anxiety and frustration. The patient reported a notable decline in self-confidence, particularly impacting his professional identity as a fighter pilot. The patient expressed considerable frustration regarding his inability to continue flying duties, which represented not only a professional setback but also a fundamental disruption to his personal identity and career trajectory. When queried about his social interactions and relationships, the patient was notably reserved. He neither disclosed whether family members, close friends, or colleagues had commented on or noticed the visible change in his ocular appearance, nor did he elaborate on how the aesthetic alteration may have influenced his interpersonal relationships or social functioning.

A total of 3 days postoperatively, the patient developed herpetic keratitis, causing significant central stromal scarring involving the visual axis. Penetrating keratoplasty (PKP) was performed 8 months later. After the surgery, the patient experienced postcorneal transplant irregular astigmatism (Fig. 1d and e), resulting in a far visual acuity with spectacle correction of 1.0 logMAR without pinhole and 0.5 logMAR with pinhole. Near vision was limited to Jaeger 13, and the patient experienced significant disabling glares and monocular diplopia. An ophthalmic examination revealed a transparent corneal graft with an iris defect extending half an hour from the pupil toward the iris periphery at 10 o’clock and a mild inferior dislocation of the intraocular lens (IOL) located in the sulcus. This resulted in some light passing through the peripheral portion and edge of the optic, while other portions bypassed the IOL entirely. This can lead to scattered light and reduced visual quality. Lambda measurement has reached a visual acuity of 0.3 logMAR, suggesting potential for improvement with additional interventions. Initial refraction was +3.25/−3.00 × 45, and intraocular pressure (IOP) was 9 mmHg. Mild macular puckering in both eyes without distortion of the foveal contour was also noted.

Data indicating the condition of the left eye’s cornea

Several Pentacam key indices were reviewed in assessing the left eye’s cornea condition: pachymetry at the center of the pupil was 535 µm and at the thinnest point of the cornea 521 µm. Maximum keratometry (KMax) at the center of the pupil was documented to be 49.0D. The inferior–superior (I–S) asymmetry value was −3.66, suggesting a certain degree of corneal irregularity. The KISA% index a composite measure incorporating keratometry, inferior–superior, and astigmatism was determined to be 38.135. Polar coordinates for spot locations within an 8-mm zone were identified at 0.82 mm and 1.39 mm as the thinnest location on the cornea, as shown in Fig. 1d. The Scheimpflug image in the horizontal section demonstrated the iris defect (shown in Fig. 1f). The visual outcome measures were high-contrast (100%) and low-contrast (10%) distance visual acuity on the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) chart with logMAR on Chart2020 system (Shemesh Health Solutions, Johannesburg, South Africa). Near visual acuity was assessed using the Rosenbaum Pocket Vision Screening Card, while contrast sensitivity was evaluated using the Functional Acuity Contrast Test (FACT) chart (StereoOptical, Chicago, IL, USA). Distance visual acuity was measured at 6 m with a calibrated projector, and near visual acuity was evaluated at 33 cm. Light intensity measurements were performed using the TSI 7525 IAQ-Calc Indoor Air Quality Meter (TSI Incorporated, Shoreview, Minnesota, USA).

In addition, the patient’s subjective perception of visual quality was evaluated using the Quality of Vision questionnaire [7].

Pertinent corneal data for contact lens fitting in the left eye

In the prescription of contact lenses for the left eye, a few significant corneal parameters were taken. The horizontal visible iris diameter (HVID) was calculated to be 12.3 mm, providing essential information for lens sizing. Keratometry readings showed K1 (flat) and K2 (steep) values of 45.0 D at a 7.50-mm ring and 47.9 D at a 7.04-mm ring, respectively. The mean keratometry value, Km, was 46.4 D at a ring of 7.27 mm. Steep axis was recorded at 109. The corneal asphericity coefficient (Q value), which is very important in determining the shape and eccentricity of the cornea, was −2.32. The patient’s astigmatism, another important parameter for contact lens selection, was 2.9 diopters. Further analysis of the corneal elevation data revealed an apical elevation of +19 microns and an inferior elevation of −66 microns at 2.5 mm from the center, which are critical measures in the assessment of the corneal topography and contact lens-fitting process.

Contact lens fitting

Several corneal rigid gas permeable (RGP) and limbal RGP fitting designs had been tried, including Rose K2 (Menicon Co. Ltd., Nagoya, Japan) and Rose K2 IC (Menicon Co. Ltd., Nagoya, Japan), respectively, but discomfort complaints were frequent, and monocular diplopia persisted. While hybrid contact lenses (Duette, SynergEyes, Inc., Carlsbad, CA, USA) and soft toric (Biofinity® toric, Comfilcon A, CooperVision Southampton, UK) improved comfort for the patient, monocular diplopia persisted. Given this, adopting a combined approach integrating scleral and pinhole contact lenses became a possible alternative approach. Close review of the past contact lens-fitting experiences in this patient demonstrated striking differences. The patient’s visual acuity fluctuated, ranging from 0.4 logMAR with soft toric contact lenses alone to an optimum of 0.0 logMAR when utilizing a combination of scleral and pinhole contact lens. Monocular diplopia was common among the use of various types of contact lenses. However, this was notably absent in the use of a pinhole contact lens alone and in combination with a scleral contact lens (Table 1).

Fitting scleral contact lenses with quadrant asymmetric periphery design

Owing to the irregular structure of the cornea and limbus, a gap between the landing zone of the lens and the sclera was noted upon lens insertion. An asymmetric peripheral lens design allowed the lens to vault the distorted structures, so the lens could land acceptably on the sclera, ensuring comfort and rotational stability. Because of the asymmetrical peripheral design in scleral lenses, the practitioner is able to independently adjust the sagittal height of each quadrant which is an effective solution in cases of corneal asymmetry. Specifically, the scleral lens was designed with a 150-micron reduction in the peripheral zone of the vertical quadrant, compared with the standard vault. This adjustment helped to minimize the gap between the lens and the cornea while still providing adequate clearance over the scar area.

The final scleral lens had a diameter of 16.5 mm, a sagittal height of 4,350 microns, and an elevated limbal area with an additional 200 microns over the entire 360-degree circumference. In addition, the central clearance zone increased by 50 microns around the full 360-degree area. The vaulting of 300 microns combined with the asymmetric peripheral design allowed the lens to conform closely to the irregular ocular surface while avoiding any areas of excessive pressure or edge lift.

Fitting pinhole lenses and scleral contact lenses and clinical outcomes

Considering the fitting of a combined scleral and pinhole contact lens system, several factors are considered. In the process of fitting a scleral lens, the lens design must be carefully considered to ensure adequate oxygen permeability and an appropriate geometric configuration. The scleral lens should be customized to avoid direct contact with the pinhole soft contact lens or the limbus and the initial scleral landing zone.

As for the pinhole soft contact lens, optimal centration is crucial to ensure the small aperture is properly aligned with the patient’s visual axis. Precise positioning of the pinhole on the cornea is essential to maximize the visual benefits and minimize any issues like off-axis positioning or visual distortions. Proper centration of the pinhole contact lens helps to improve visual clarity; reduce aberrations, glare, and halos; and provide a stable, comfortable wearing experience for the patient.

Prior to scleral lens fitting, an endothelial cell count is performed and should be above the acceptable threshold of 1000 cells/mm2 for scleral lens wear [8–10]. Specialized scleral profilometry devices can assist in identifying the scleral structures and designing more asymmetric peripheral lenses.

A pinhole, hand-painted soft contact lens (IP40, No. 7 Contact Lens Laboratory Ltd., Hastings, UK) made from raw material of Contaflex Filcon II 3 (Contamac Ltd., Saffron Walden, UK) with 77% water content and oxygen permeability in diffusion coefficient over solubility coefficient (Dk) is 45 × 10−11 (Fig. 2a). The parameters of the contact lens are as follows: a base curve of 7.6 mm, a diameter of 13.00 mm, a thickness of 0.12 mm, a visible iris diameter of 12.50 mm, a clear iris diameter of 3 mm (pinhole aperture), and a power of 0.00 D (plano).

A scleral lens with a diameter of 16.5 mm, the Onefit MED, was selected for the patient (Blanchard Contact Lenses Inc., San Ramon, CA, USA), which is made from Optimum Infinite® that contains Tisilfocon A raw material (Contamac, Safron Waldon, UK) with oxygen permeability of 180 Dk and flexural modulus of 1341 MPa to insure very high oxygen permeability (due to presence of a pinhole contact lens) and resistance to flexure, respectively (Fig. 2b).

Contact lens fitting was evaluated via slit lamp, and optical coherence tomography (OCT; Moptim Mocean 4000 plus, Shenzhen Slton Technology Co. Ltd., China) was utilized to assess the vaulting. Optical coherence tomography analysis demonstrated a minimum acceptable corneal clearance of 429 microns at the apical region after 12 hours of contact lens wear (Fig. 2c).

The scleral contact lens exhibited excellent centration. Following the quadratic scleral lens fitting, the patient’s best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) in his left eye dramatically improved from 1.0 logMAR at distance and Jaeger 13 for near to 0.0 logMAR and Jaeger 1 with +2.50-D reading spectacles worn over the scleral contact lens and pinhole contact lens in photopic (536 lux measured under exam). At mesopic conditions (62 lux measured under exam ), BCVA in his left eye improved from 1.3 logMAR at distance and Jaeger 13 for near to 0.04 logMAR and Jaeger 2 compared with spectacles worn over the scleral contact lens and pinhole contact lens. At 10% low-contrast (10%) distance, the patient’s BCVA in his left eye improved from 1.5 logMAR to 0.22 logMAR at distance and in photopic (536 lux measured under exam) conditions, compared with spectacles worn over the scleral contact lens and pinhole contact lens.

In addition, the Quality of Vision questionnaire improved from 78 to 15 score while spectacles were worn over the scleral contact lens and pinhole contact lens. The contrast sensitivity measurements for the best-corrected visual acuity with spectacle lenses and the combined scleral and pinhole contact lenses were evaluated across five spatial frequencies (1.5, 3, 6, 12, and 18 cycles per degree [CPD]). The contrast sensitivity values were 25, 40, 23, 8, and 4 for the spectacle lenses, and 50, 57, 64, 15, and 6 for the combination of pinhole and scleral contact lens, respectively.

Margin reflex distance (MRD1)

Margin reflex distance (MRD1) was measured without contact lens and with soft pinhole contact lens with the scleral lens after 12 hours of wear utilizing Image Pro-Plus Software (Image Pro-Plus 6.0; Media Cybernetics, Silver Spring, MD, USA). Before contact lens insertion, the left eye exhibited an MRD1 of 2.9 mm, considered mild ptosis (normal range: 3–5 mm). After the insertion of the scleral and pinhole contact lenses and 12 hours of continuous wear, MRD1 increased to 6 mm, representing a 3.1-mm improvement and resolution of ptosis (Fig. 2d and e).

Combination of pinhole and scleral—aftercare

The patient tolerated the quadrant asymmetric peripheral well, which he had been wearing for 12 hours daily, refreshing the lens every 4 hours with nonpreserved saline without any reported issues or complications. The patient also disinfected his lens using a one-step hydrogen peroxide solution on a daily basis. At follow-up at 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year, visual acuity was stable. The fit of the lens over this time period demonstrated acceptable corneal vaulting, without applying pressure to the scar or having excessive bearing on other ocular surface tissues. Assessment of ocular health including both fluorescein and lissamine green showed no limbal or bulbar staining; tarsal giant papillary conjunctivitis was not evident, and no discharges or debris were found on the lens surface.

Discussion

Iris damage, often resulting from ocular trauma (blunt or penetrating), can lead to functional and aesthetic challenges. Possible functional consequences include glare and monocular diplopia, while aesthetic disfigurement can have considerable effects on self-perception and psychosocial well-being. Mild forms do not need intervention, but the severe and symptomatic forms may necessitate it. The presence of irregular astigmatism due to corneal deformity—a common sequela of PKP—can be variably improved with the use of rigid gas-permeable or scleral contact lenses [11, 12]. Severe peripheral corneal irregularity often requires the use of quadrant-asymmetric scleral lenses [13]. These are prescribed almost only after standard contact lenses have been unhelpful and surgical options are not advised or contraindicated [9, 11]. Dislocation of the IOL may also be associated with blurred vision, halo, and glare, among other visual disturbances, and may necessitate different types of corrections [9, 11].

Since irregular astigmatism can occur concurrently with iris damage in penetrating or perforating ocular traumas and surgeries [14], there is an urge for innovative treatment strategies that would address such complex challenges and broaden our therapeutic options.

This case report represents, to the best of our knowledge, the first published successful application of a customized scleral contact lens in conjunction with a pinhole soft contact lens for the visual rehabilitation of a 50-year-old male fighter pilot suffering from debilitating monocular diplopia and poor vision due to significant irregular corneal astigmatism subsequent to PKP surgery, inferior subluxation of an IOL, and an iris defect causing significant light scatter. The patient demonstrated a clear and definitive unwillingness to pursue further surgical interventions, decisively rejecting such options on the basis of previous experiences. With surgical solutions entirely excluded, the patient sought a noninvasive approach to enhance visual acuity, ultimately opting for an optical strategy involving scleral contact lenses in combination with a piggyback pinhole contact lens. A wide range of contact lens solutions were explored, including soft back toric, rigid gas permeable (RGP), limbal RGP, piggyback systems, and hybrid contact lenses. Scleral contact lenses and pinhole contact lenses were also tried as one-lens options; however, symptoms did not improve, and we further explored unconventional solutions. A soft pinhole lens in conjunction with an asymmetric periphery scleral contact lens was a promising option, which could still be considered experimental. The pinhole lens effectively diminished the scattered light and eliminated out-of-focus rays, while the scleral lens fitted to the irregular cornea provided astigmatic correction. This innovative solution proved very effective, immediately resolving the complaints of the patient and improving vision to logMAR 0.0 during the initial fitting. These results were maintained up to a year following the initial fitting of the lenses, without any associated complications. The contrast sensitivity was higher with the combined scleral and pinhole contact lenses compared with spectacle lenses across the tested spatial frequencies. These findings demonstrate the effectiveness of combining scleral contact lenses with pinhole optics in restoring functional vision in patients with complex corneal irregularities. In addition, the patient reported a significant improvement in their overall visual function and quality of life, as evidenced by the increased contrast sensitivity measurements with the combined scleral and pinhole contact lens system compared with spectacle lenses alone.

Our conservative approach managed to avoid complicated surgeries such as iris suturing or artificial iris transplantation and IOL fixation, each carrying a different set of complications and, like other intraocular surgeries, may result in intraocular infections leading to irreversible loss of vision [15]. Additional advantages of this approach are that it is relatively cheap (which might be available in resource-poor countries) while showing promising visual outcomes. Of note, this approach also improved the patient’s ptosis, adding to their improved aesthetics and thus possibly raising self-esteem.

Although this approach has several benefits, it also has several shortcomings. The pinhole contact lenses may result in a reduced amount of light entering the eye, hence interfering in settings of dim light. Patients are also susceptible to other complications associated with contact lenses: corneal hypoxia, especially due to the use of two contact lenses at one time, erosion, and corneal infection. This technique also requires professional skills for both optometrists and ophthalmologists as fitting of these lenses might be challenging.

Looking ahead, although based on a single case, this dual-lens approach may warrant further exploration and could offer potential for future standardization through the development of structured fitting protocols, clinician training programs, and defined patient selection criteria. While the customization required for quadrant-asymmetric scleral and pinhole contact lenses may initially limit its use to specialized centers, the adaptation of simplified designs and the establishment of collaborative fitting frameworks could enhance accessibility. Further research is necessary to assess the technique’s long-term visual outcomes, safety, and wearer compliance. Comparative studies with control groups evaluating this approach will provide valuable insights into its relative efficacy and feasibility. Ultimately, large-scale prospective trials in diverse populations will be essential to validate its reproducibility, optimize outcomes, and define its role within the broader spectrum of visual rehabilitation strategies.

Conclusion

This case report shows successful visual rehabilitation using combined pinhole contact lenses with customized quadrant-asymmetric scleral contact lenses in a patient with corneal irregular astigmatism, iris damage, and IOL dislocation. The use of such special lenses resulted in improved visual acuity, an enhanced quality of life, and spared the need for complex ocular surgeries.
