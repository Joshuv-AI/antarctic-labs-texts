Population-specific heterogeneity in ontogeny of the broadly-conserved blood transcriptional program during the first week of life
Authors: Bhavjinder K Dhillon, Travis M Blimkie, Olubukola T Idoko, Rebecca Ford, Asimenia Angelidou, Rym Ben-Othman, Sebastiano Montante, Nelly Amenyogbe, Tida Dibassey, Joann Diray-Arce, Reza Falsafi, Abdulazeez Imam, Kerry McEnaney, Oludare A Odumade, Al Ozonoff, William S Pomat, Peter C Richmond, Kinga K Smolen, Oghenebrume Wariri, EPIC-HIPC Consortium, Ofer Levy, Anita H J van den Biggelaar, Beate Kampmann, Tobias R Kollmann, Amy H Y Lee, Robert E W Hancock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrevious research has shown that immune development during the first week of life, i.e. ontogeny, is progressive, consistent and robust, involving large numbers of differentially expressed genes at each sampled time point. To obtain more granular detail about conserved and population-specific ontogeny, the influence of day of life on blood gene expression and cell type composition was examined in two distinct neonatal populations, from The Gambia and Papua New Guinea, employing block randomization strategies to minimize batch effects. This enabled more detailed conclusions about ontogenic program differences between the two cohorts. Population-specific ontogeny revealed mechanistic insights likely to contribute to inherent population-based heterogeneity in the efficiency of neonatal immune responses, including cell cycle, kinesins, and DAP12 signaling in Papua New Guinea, and antigen presentation, clathrin-mediated endocytosis and alpha-defensins in The Gambia. Differences between populations interconnect using protein:protein interaction networks of population-specific genes and pathinkR-based pathway networks and heat maps, thus fitting the concept of pathway/network remodeling. In addition to the population-specific changes, there is a profound core ontogenic gene-expression program involving ~18% of all expressed genes with remarkable 88-96% conservation at each day of life, revealing new contributors to this shared early-life ontogenic program.Subject terms: Gene expression, Gene regulatory networks, Immunology

Introduction

Neonates have heightened susceptibility to infections early in life, and developing public-health interventions targeted at reducing infant morbidity and mortality, including vaccines, should be informed by an understanding of the distinct neonatal immune development1,2. Our previous study3 on the early life ontogeny trajectory revealed that altered neutrophil activity, interferon signaling, and complement cascade are components of major ontogeny changes that constitute a profound, rapidly-changing developmental trajectory during the first week of life. Importantly, despite their dynamic nature, these developmental changes were robust and regulated rather than stochastic in nature3. Therefore, the neonatal immune system cannot be considered, per se, to be immature or deficient, but rather demonstrates important and distinct phenotypic characteristics when compared to the immune system of adults4–7. It has been described as tolerogenic, with more innate features that likely contribute to the slow adaptation towards developing effective long-term immune memory4. Variations of these trajectories are likely important and could be used to predict susceptibility and response to infection throughout the first year of life8,9, but we lack data.

In this study, we explored the details of early gene expression and distinct population-specific molecular mechanisms that define neonatal immune development during the first week of life. Immune ontogeny can differ between populations due to genetics, environmental factors including nutrition and season, or exposure to pathogens10; therefore, studying distinct populations has the potential to reveal additional insights. Building on the previously-established trajectory of immune ontogeny3, we enrolled a cohort of 90 infants from two geographically- and genetically- distinct neonatal populations (The Gambia and Papua New Guinea). Peripheral blood samples were analyzed simultaneously, employing a paired design and block randomization for head-to-head comparison of samples derived from the two sites to minimize batch effects that can confound comparisons. This enabled more granular and detailed analysis of the common and population-specific threads of immune ontogenetic development. A deeper understanding of this critical period of immune development can expand our understanding of how early life transcriptional trajectories may associate with health and disease, including infection, as well as outcomes of interventions such as immunization.

Results

Age was the principal driver of transcriptomic variation in neonates during the first week of life

A reference developmental trajectory of neonatal immune ontogeny was previously established that outlined central roles of interferon-driven signaling, complement cascade and granulocyte function3. Ontogeny was the primary driver of transcriptomic variation in the current neonatal cohorts, whereby samples clustered by day of life (DOL) (Fig. 1A). The number of significant DE genes increased over the first week of life in the combined cohorts of all participants from both populations (Table 1; Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Data 1, 2), and separately for GAM and PNG (Fig. 1B), rising from <100 at DOL1 to ~1700 by DOL7 (Table 1; Supplementary Data 3, 4), with more down-regulated genes at each DOL (Fig. 1C) consistent with previous reports3. Overall, examining a combined cohort larger than our prior study3, many DE genes were commonly changed in both populations (Fig. 1D) and demonstrated fold-changes that were strongly correlated (ranging from 0.88–0.96, i.e. 88–96%, at each DOL) (Fig. 1E). Gene expression was tightly, and to a large extent coordinately, regulated across these diverse populations and represented common core developmental changes and novel pathways (Fig. 2; Supplementary Data 3, 5, 6). This is discussed in more detail below.

However, critically, the study participant population (GAM or PNG) was also observed as a significant source of variation across multiple principal components, in addition to DOL, and more so than any effect of sex (Supplementary Fig. 4). Comparing the overall ontogeny to the population-specific ontogeny (Fig. 2C; Supplementary Data 3, 5, 6) revealed both common and distinct significantly-enriched pathways suggesting population-specific heterogeneity during the first week of life. Indeed, population-specific genes were identified at all sampled DOL (Fig. 1D). This insight of population-specific heterogeneity enabled a strategic block randomization design, which was further analyzed.

Population-specific heterogeneity in ontogeny

Population-specific DE genes and pathways were revealed across the first week of life (select pathways highlighted in Fig. 2C). Use of a paired-design strategy in which each infant was compared to themselves at birth, largely eliminated sources of variation such as differences in genetics and epigenetics, but might be less comprehensive in accounting for other external factors such as environment, nutrition, microbial load, and exposure to pathogens, which could vary somewhat over the first week of life in any given infant. Importantly, the use of this paired-design by indexing each neonate’s DOL1/3/7 sample to DOL0, and employing both overall ontogeny modeling (indicated by All in Fig. 2C) and population-specific modeling (indicated by GAM and PNG in Fig. 2C) was key to detecting large numbers of both shared and population-specific changes at the gene expression level. A less stringent fold-change cut-off yielded additional pathways of interest (Supplementary Fig. 5). [NB. A direct comparison of GAM and PNG using an unpaired approach only revealed modest differences (Supplementary Fig. 6; Supplementary Data 7, 8). Using a paired approach revealed high-level population-specific differences at the pathway level related to cell cycle, alpha defensins, antigen processing, and various metabolism-related pathways (Fig. 2C; see Supplementary Fig. 7 for a comprehensive comparison of all enriched pathways).

A likely explanation for these population-specific changes is that in a specific population of neonates, the contribution of particular genes and their products was greater than in the other population, a phenomenon sometimes referred to as pathway rewiring (i.e., the interacting proteins in the pathway changes)11. To visualize population-specific pathway rewiring, the variable genes were mapped to protein:protein interaction (PPI) Networks, comprising DE genes (nodes/circles) joined by lines (known and curated direct, metabolic or regulatory interactions), to reveal biological snapshots of conglomerates of interconnected pathways. Differences in these interconnected pathways were visualized as combined networks using, e.g., DyNet12, which enables pathway synchronization. The combined DyNet networks created from population-specific DE genes at DOL1 (Fig. 3A) and DOL3 (Fig. 3B) were highly connected and intermingled (NB DOL7 networks were too complex for simple visualization using this method). Such differences are consistent with pathway and network rewiring.

Consistent with rewiring of certain immune pathways in the two cohorts, substantial differences in immune gene expression networks were also evident between GAM and PNG when visualized as separate function-based PPI networks (Supplementary Fig. 8) of population-specific genes. Networks are characterized by connectivity, and key to this are hub proteins that are highly connected to other proteins and are considered key biological intermediates that receive and transmit signals from interconnected proteins. The population-specific DOL1 genes for GAM (Supplementary Fig. 8A) and PNG (Supplementary Fig. 8B) formed well-connected minimum order networks that contained some common hub proteins (bolded in Table 2), such as matrix metalloproteinase MMP9, immune checkpoint regulator CEACAM1, and haptoglobin HP, indicating that overall common biological processes were involved. There were also population-specific hubs (plain-type in Table 2), including genes like complement protein C1QA, master regulator SP1, and the neutrophil elastase gene ELANE (GAM) and energy metabolism enzyme creatinine kinase CKB and histone HIST1H3H (PNG), consistent with pathway or network rewiring. Zero-order networks built from DOL3 DE genes for GAM (Supplementary Fig. 8C) and PNG (Supplementary Fig. 8D), indicated both shared (e.g., crucial immune response regulator STAT1 and ubiquitin-like modifier ISG15) and many unique hubs, but overall showed differences in overall network structure and in the identity of key hub genes (Table 2). Zero-order networks for DOL7 DE genes were substantially denser and again showed both shared and population-specific hubs (Supplementary Fig. 8E, F). Intriguingly, three population-specific hubs were present amongst the top 10 hubs at both DOL3 and DOL 7, including GABARAPL2, MAP1LC3B (GAM) and K1AA0101 (PNG), while others went from population-specific to common (CUL4A, FOS, HSPA1B), likely reflecting a compensatory mechanism to overcome population-specific deficits. Generally speaking, the described hubs (Table 2) were not highly altered in expression (<two-fold) and tended to be downregulated, indicating a regulatory role in the networks.

To further visualize differences at DOL3 and 7, pathway networks using pathlinkR based on shared constituent genes were constructed and allowed interpretation of differences in higher-level processes (Supplementary Fig. 9), further supporting pathway remodeling. For example, at DOL3, antigen processing, α-defensins, interleukin signaling, and autophagy differed specifically in GAM (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Fig. 9A and Supplementary Data 3). Differences in antigen processing and autophagy were also revealed in the expression of the constituent genes in these pathways, reflecting both temporal and absolute changes in expression levels Fig. 4A, B). The antigen processing pathway could influence the efficiency and subsets of antigens that would be presented13, and the observation of population-specific changes in the antigen processing pathway may influence the efficiency of immune responses. Alpha defensins DEFA3 and DEFA4, agents of innate immunity, were both significantly down-regulated at DOL3 in GAM (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Figs. 5, 7, 9E); it is possible this could reflect copy number variation14 and/or cell composition changes, which can affect expression levels of α-defensins.

In contrast, in PNG at DOL3, there was a striking and unique up-regulation of cell cycle and replication pathways (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Figs. 5, 7, 9B/F). For example, BRIP1 expression peaked at DOL3 with a 2.3-fold change from DOL0 (adj p-value 3.65E-06). In addition, up-regulated kinesins were also a notable component of the PNG-specific ontogeny. For example, there was a unique up-regulation of various kinesins involved in intracellular transport (KIF11, KIF18A, KIF2C, KIF20A in PNG), which could also be linked to cell replication, or even possibly the efficiency of antigen uptake in the cell15. There was also a unique up-regulation in PNG in the expression of the ubiquitin ligase UBE2C (2.3 fold-change, adj p-value 3.23E-06), which targets or recruits substrates for degradation by the proteasome during cell cycle progression and division16.

When comparing population-specific ontogeny at DOL7, decreased expression was observed in clathrin-mediated endocytosis and activation of gene expression by SREBF in GAM, while up-regulated DAP12 signaling was unique to PNG (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Figs. 5, 6, 9C/D). These underlying functional differences in critical pathways of cell cycle, antigen processing and alpha-defensins may have a meaningful impact on the overall capacity, efficiency, and specificity of downstream immune responses between populations. Furthermore, while interferon pathway genes were overall substantially conserved, there were still robust differences in some genes in this pathway (Fig. 4C, D, Supplementary Fig. 10).

Population differences in peripheral blood cell-type composition

Overall trends in myeloid cell-types measured by flow cytometry showed changes in minor cell populations (Supplementary Fig. 4), which were consistent with expected distributions in the early neonatal period17–20. A head-to-head comparison of cell types at each DOL between GAM and PNG neonates revealed significant differences (selected cell types in Fig. 3C, Supplementary Data 9 and Supplementary Fig. 11). In general, neutrophil proportions decreased over the first week of life, while CD3+ T-cells remained steady with no observed significant differences. At birth (DOL0), PNG neonates had significantly lower numbers of CD11b-CD16-immature neutrophils (adj p-value 2.97E-05), CD56-CD16+ NK cells (adj p-value 8.82E-03), plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC) (adj p-value 8.15E-03), myeloid dendritic cells (adj p-value 8.82E-03) and basophils (adj p-value 8.15E-03), CD64+ cells were significantly lower in PNG at DOL1 (adj p-value 8.82E-3) and DOL3 (adjusted p-value 2.34E-02). Monocytes (HLADR+CD14+, classical and non-classical) were also lower in PNG at DOL0 to 3 (adj p-value 1.72E-02, 1.91E-02, 9.29E-03, respectively). Although less significant, γδ−T cells were also lower in the PNG population at birth (adj p-value 1.49E-02). The B cell compartment also had modest differences, most significant at DOL3 (adj p-value 1.91E-02). At DOL7, the most significant differences between GAM and PNG were observed in the non-classical monocytes (adj p-value 1.91E-02), γδ-T cells (adj p-value 1.9E-02), B cells (adj p-value 2.63E-02) and HLADR+CD14− cells (2.63E-02). Many of these changes were accompanied, as expected, by changes in specific cell markers. While the proportions of altered cell types were insufficient to explain modest changes in gene expression in the entire population, some of these predicted cell-type compositional differences were aligned with DE gene differences and should be considered when interpreting the population-specific ontogeny differences in blood transcriptome ontogeny. For example, differences in antigen processing (monocyte, T-cell and B-cell mediated) and defensins (neutrophil-mediated) could relate in part to cell composition differences.

Core innate immune ontogeny in first week of life

As mentioned above, we observed a tremendous (88–96%) conservation of the ontogenic gene expression program (Fig. 1E), involving up to ~18% of all measured genes. Our robust head-to-head study enabled nuanced probing of this highly-conserved ontogenic program (Fig. 1B–E; Table 1; Supplementary Data 1–6). Proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines play an important role during labor and delivery21–23, but tight postnatal regulation of inflammation is important, given the consequent abrupt exposure to a large number of microbes. It was observed that gene expression changes consistent with control of key proinflammatory mechanisms were evident across the first week of life. As described above, neutrophils drastically increased in numbers within the first 6–12 h of life, serving as the first line of defense, and then gradually declined by 72 h18. Genes enriched for the neutrophil degranulation pathway, a process by which neutrophils release granule contents including antimicrobial proteins and peptides, ROS, and/or cytokines24, had peak expression levels immediately after birth (DOL0) and showed progressively decreasing expression at all subsequent timepoints (Fig. 2C). The interleukin (IL)-1 and Toll-like receptor (TLR)-4 pathways, well-known regulators of proinflammatory innate immunity25, also closely followed this pattern of expression (i.e. peak at DOL0/1 and significantly down-regulation by DOL3/7, Fig. 2C). The IL-4/IL-13 signaling pathway (including e.g. MMP9, MAOA, FOXO3 and BCL2L1), a signature of TH2 immune responses, was also down-regulated. S100 alarmin proteins such as S100P and S100A8/9 were also consistently down-regulated (Supplementary Data 3-4) over the first week of life. In contrast, interferon (IFN)-α/β/ɣ signaling (heatmap of fold-changes of genes in IFN-ɣ pathway in Supplementary Fig. 10; PPI network in Fig. 4C) and ISG15 antiviral pathways were all up-regulated significantly during the first week of life, consistent with prior observations3. Enrichment of MSigDB hallmark gene sets also revealed similar trends in down-regulation of the inflammatory response, TNF-α signaling, PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling, heme metabolism, etc., while there was up-regulation of IFN responses (Supplementary Fig. 12). Overall, pro-inflammatory processes were robustly regulated in both GAM and PNG neonates after birth.

Important signaling receptor pathways that interface between the innate and adaptive immune response were also up-regulated, including “immunoregulatory interactions between a lymphoid and non-lymphoid cell” (e.g., SIGLEC-1, SIGLEC-8), “chemokine receptors bind chemokines”, “eicosanoid ligand-binding receptors”, and “DAP12 signaling”26–28. The changes in chemokine receptors revealed that CXCL10 and CXCR3 were primarily up-regulated at DOL3, an interaction known to up-regulate the production of IFN-γ by TH1 cells29,30, which was consistent with the timing of up-regulation of the IFN-γ pathway. However, at DOL7, the up-regulation of these receptors was not as high as at DOL3, and there was increased expression of CCR4 (preferentially expressed in TH2 cells in vitro)31. These differences at DOL3 and DOL7 could indicate a critical shift or bias in developing a balanced T-cell response, and therefore, changes related to T-cell development were further examined.

Robust T-cell mediated immune ontogeny in first week of life

Across the first week of life, changes were observed in the expression levels of several markers, regulators and key immune checkpoints during the process of T-cell development into effector cells and activation. CD3+ T cells were consistently detected across the first week of life (Supplementary Fig. 11). Accordingly, consistent changes in the expression of various regulators of T cell signaling and activation, including NFATC2 (transcription factor important in T-cell activation and lineage selection) and LAG-3 (activation marker and inhibitory receptor that limits T-cell activation). Master regulators of specific T helper cell populations, Treg, TH1 and TH2 cell types (regulated by FOXP3, TBX21, and GATA3, respectively), also showed increased expression at DOL7, while there was decreased expression of the T follicular helper (TFH) cell regulator BCL-6 (Supplementary Fig. 13), consistent with known trends of T-cell ontogeny in neonates6. A marker for TH17 cells (RORγT) was not detected in these data, indicating it likely did not contribute significantly. Mitochondrial fatty acid beta oxidation was an enriched pathway consistently observed and might be important in committed T-cell differentiation and fate, especially during the development of memory cells32. Overall, T-cell activation and proliferation during ontogeny followed robust timing and could be evaluated further.

Sex and the ontogeny trajectory

A sex-disaggregated analysis was attempted to identify sex-specific differences in ontogeny trajectory within each location (Supplementary Fig. 14A–F; Supplementary Data 10, 11). This revealed that there are more shared DE genes between males and females than unique genes at both locations and days of life, except in PNG at day of life 7 (Supplementary Fig. 14G). However, this is likely due to the small sample size in this group (n = 4), which resulted in lower numbers of DE genes detected overall at this timepoint in PNG specifically (Supplementary Fig. 14E, F). Any detailed interpretation of these data should be taken with caution because of the small sample sizes, and future studies should recruit larger numbers of participants to carefully elucidate the impacts of sex on ontogeny.

Discussion

We herein report for the first time a head-to-head comparison of population-specific ontogeny of the blood transcriptome over the first 7 days of human life, revealing differences in cell cycle, antigen presentation, and α-defensins representing population-specific heterogeneity superimposed on core ontogeny development. Our carefully-crafted study design, where samples were collected from infants from two distinct geographic populations (GAM and PNG) and simultaneously processed/analyzed, allowed a direct comparison of similarities and differences in immune development between these distinct neonatal populations to reveal detailed mechanistic insights. It was shown that neonatal immune ontogeny follows a robust developmental pathway with unique characteristics, including central roles for IFN signaling, complement cascade and neutrophil activity3. Recent studies have suggested that the strong innate-defense mechanisms in neonates are kept in check by a tolerogenic adaptive system4. This may offer protective effects upon exposure to a multitude of diverse microbes during the establishment of a microbiome. Immune ontogeny was explored in this study through changes in gene expression and cell populations detected in the blood across the first week of life through both population-agnostic and population-specific modeling.

The timing and regulation of key immunological developmental milestones were consistently observed in the two distinct neonatal populations, supporting previous reports of a high degree of convergence to a shared ontogeny trajectory in early life3,20. However, population-specific ontogeny was also revealed, likely influenced by differences in genetics/epigenetics and other external factors. This included antigen processing, cell cycle, kinesins and clathrin-mediated endocytosis, DAP12 signaling, in additional to several metabolism pathways. Previous studies have suggested differences in these pathways, including population-specific differences in e.g., innate cytokine responses, distinct TLR signaling, and further investigation of these pathways is important for understanding heterogeneity in neonatal responses to infection vaccination33–36. We propose here that such shifts in the key genes used for such pathways reflect pathway remodeling that could shape the magnitude, quality, and effectiveness of response against the endogenous challenges that each set of infants would face. PPI networks revealed shared hubs at each DOL, suggesting overall common biological processes (e.g. CEACAM1 at DOL1; STAT1 and ISG15 at DOL3; UBE2D1, FOS, and MAPK1 at DOL7). However, pathway rewiring was also evident with distinct population-specific hubs (e.g. GABARAPL2, MAP1LC3B in GAM and K1AA0101 in PNG).

Changes in cell-types in the blood were also measured using flow cytometry and were broadly consistent with, but expanded previous data3,37. Generally, neutrophil populations decreased over time, and CD3+ T cells remained a steady and significant portion of cells/µl in peripheral blood. PNG neonates had lower levels of many measured cell types at each given DOL (immature neutrophils, basophils, pDCs, mDCs, CD56-CD16+ NK cells, monocytes). This might in part explain some of the clustering observed in the principal component analysis (Fig. 1A), whereby PNG DOL1 samples clustered more closely with all DOL0 samples, indicating that the gene expression data might have been influenced by the predominant neutrophil population. Observed PNG-specific ontogeny could be related to differences in microbial load at birth, since the PNG study area is highly endemic for respiratory and intestinal pathogens38. However, this did not affect the timing in PNG neonates of induction of important immune checkpoints, transcriptional regulators, and activation markers at DOL3/7.

The conserved ontogeny trajectory revealed details of an anticipated surge of potentially-protective proinflammatory activity immediately following birth (DOL0)39,40, but this was tightly regulated, stabilizing across the first week of life. Major proinflammatory genes and pathways that were significantly down-regulated in the days after birth included neutrophil degranulation, IL-1 signaling, TLR-4 cascade and S100 alarmin proteins. This effect was found to be coupled with innate immune regulation involving a significant increase in both type I and II interferon signaling pathways. A strong innate immune response is important during this critical time period in conjunction with the control of proinflammatory pathways. Consistent with previous reports, the complement cascade was a prominent component of early immune development3, and was enriched in GAM at DOL1 but not PNG. Previous data3 has indicated that complement pathway changes occurred at the protein, rather than the mRNA, expression level and may explain some of these differences.

Initiation and enhancement of adaptive immunity were evident through antigen processing and presentation, and interactions between lymphoid and non-lymphoid cells. Consistent with previous data34–36, but providing a mechanistic underlay, this marks an important developmental shift enabling the neonatal immune system to respond to the sudden exposure to foreign microbes by increasing its capacity to present foreign antigens, with likely implications for presentation of vaccine antigens and the optimal timing of administration of different vaccines. Interestingly, several defining ontogeny changes observed at DOL7 differed from those observed at DOL3, including mechanistic features of T-cell activation and regulation. Previous studies have shown important patterns of T cell development in early life41,42, and this study supports observations of the proliferation of effector T cells in the hours after birth42. Chemokine receptors such as CXCL10 and CXCR3 indicated a potential role of TH1 cells at DOL3, which may also be involved in the up-regulation of the IFN-γ signaling pathway at this time. CCR4, an important chemokine preferentially expressed by TH2 cells, became more significant at DOL7, suggesting a potentially increasing role of the TH2-mediated response later in the first week of life. Further exploration of these shifts in bias in T helper cell responses would be invaluable in understanding neonatal immune development. However, LAG-3, FOXP3, and TBX21 and other important activation markers of T-cells were significantly up-regulated in both populations at DOL7 and might represent an important ontogenic shift with the potential to modulate the adaptive immune response and could be related to early life exposures to microbes38,42–44. Lower expression of such immune markers and checkpoints (especially those with potent immunomodulatory abilities) at DOL1 and DOL3 may prove to be critical in determining optimal scheduling of neonatal immunizations in early life.

Expression of genes in autophagy pathways was observed to be high at DOL0 and had differences in down-regulation between populations at DOL3/7 (Fig. 4B). Autophagy is a critical process after birth since, when the transplacental nutrient supply is abruptly stopped, neonates enter a starvation period, prompting degradation of self-proteins in neonatal tissues for maintenance of energy homeostasis45. The observed changes in transcriptome were likely coordinated with breastfeeding practices and changes in milk production or other external factors impacting neonatal nutrition during this time period.

The current study features multiple strengths, including longitudinal design, block randomization for rigorous head-to-head comparison of the blood transcriptome between geographically distinct cohorts derived from low-income countries, and robust sample and data management. Importantly, the findings from this study could be used to inform a better understanding of vulnerabilities in high-risk neonates that may deviate from typical developmental patterns while considering population-specific heterogeneity (measuring the influence of a combination of genetic, microbial exposures, environmental and other factors in healthy neonates).

This study also had some limitations, including little data regarding the maternal environment before birth, such as maternal infection, immunity, and vaccination status. These are likely to substantially affect the neonatal immune response since fetal-neonatal immunity is a continuum. To address this, future studies could include maternal sampling and the collection of granular clinical/environmental data. Perinatal factors, such as the stress of delivery and sudden exposure to the external environment, could contribute to these observed changes. However, all babies were delivered vaginally without any major medically-observed complications, and any such changes would likely be obscured by our use of aggregate data from multiple infants and by the paired design. Furthermore, DOL0 samples were collected between a range of 0−24 h after birth due to constraints in sample collection. Nevertheless, hourly changes were not observed to contribute significantly to gene expression by PCA (Supplementary Fig. 15), likely due to the sample size. A sex-disaggregated analysis was also attempted, but we caution against any detailed interpretations due to the small sample size of some comparison groups (n = 4); nevertheless, the study overall had similar numbers of male and female neonates.

Overall, our study reveals for the first time that there are differences between distinct geographic populations superimposed on robust ontogenetic peripheral blood transcriptional trajectories across the first week of human life, including cell cycle, antigen presentation, alpha defensins, and DAP12 signaling. Future studies could define the potential associations of such differences with clinical outcomes, including health, disease and outcomes of interventions such as immunization.

Methods

Study design

Distinct cohorts of neonates from The Gambia (GAM) in West Africa and Papua New Guinea (PNG) in Australasia were enrolled based on an established clinical study protocol outlining ethical considerations, informed consent, detailed inclusion/exclusion criteria, and sample size calculations46. The study is registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT03246230) and was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki, International Conference Harmonization Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP), and local ethics guidelines46. Neonates with a gestational age of at least 37 weeks and spontaneous vaginal delivery were included. Sex of the neonate was assigned at birth. Mothers and neonates were healthy, and no antibiotics or special care beyond local standards were required at the time of enrollment or biosampling. All studied neonates in this analysis were randomized to a delayed vaccination group, whereby the administration of the hepatitis B (HepB), Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG), and polio vaccines were delayed until after blood draws for to a maximum of 7 days after birth. At each location, 45 neonates were enrolled (total = 90; summary demographics in Table 3) and randomly assigned to 3 groups using a computer-generated block randomization strategy to determine gene expression differences (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Sample collection

Two mL of blood were drawn from each neonate into a sodium heparinized tube (BD VacutainerR, Cat. No. 368884) shortly after birth (Visit 1; denoted as Day of Life/DOL0), and at the follow-up visit (Visit 2; either DOL1, 3, or 7). From each blood draw, 200 µL was pipetted into a tube containing 552 µL of PaxGene fluid (BD Biosciences, cat. #762165) to preserve RNA and tubes were stored at −80 °C prior to shipment to the University of British Columbia for processing. For flow cytometry samples, EDTA was added to 225 µL of fresh blood within 4 h of collection. Cells were stained with fixable visibility dye prior to lysis and storage at −80 °C in Smart Tube reagents (Smart Tube Inc., San Carlos, California) prior to shipment. All blood samples were assigned to groups by block randomization to balance potential major confounding factors: each block contained two samples per participant, samples from all 4 DOL, and samples from both populations.

RNA-Seq and data processing

Poly-adenylated RNA was isolated using the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA), and cDNA libraries were constructed with the KAPA Stranded RNA-Seq library preparation kit (Roche, Basil, Switzerland). Sequencing was done in three batches on an Illumina HiSeqX (BC Genome Sciences Center), with a read length of 150 bp. Sequence quality was checked using FastQC v0.11.8 and MultiQC v1.847,48. Alignment to the Ensembl human reference genome GRCh38 v98 was done using STAR v2.7.3a49. Aligned reads were then counted using the “count” function from HTSeq v0.11.250. Highly-expressed globin genes and genes with very low counts that may skew data normalization were removed prior to analysis. After processing, the median library size was 7 million(M) reads (ranging from 2.9 M to 53.1 M).

Flow cytometry

Upon thawing, flow cytometry samples were washed in staining buffer (PBSAN; 0.5% BSA, 0.1% sodium azide) and stained at room temperature in PBSAN with a cocktail of markers to determine the frequency of target immune cell populations17. High-throughput flow cytometry data were collected using an LSRII flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), an automated gating strategy (Supplementary Fig. 2), including determination of the cell counts/µL of blood by counting beads (Life Technology, USA) for all gated populations using FlowJo software (version 9.9, Becton, Dickinson and Company). The counts of cells/µL were adjusted based on bead counts and normalized by an additional factor of 200 for computational scaling. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to identify cell types with statistically different numbers of cells/µL of gated populations in the myeloid panel (with Benjamini-Hochberg correction).

Statistical analysis

All data was analyzed using the R statistical programming software v4.5.2. DESeq2 v1.48.1 was used to test for differentially expressed (DE) genes between different days of life (DOL)51. A number of different DESeq2 models were evaluated. To minimize potential clinical, demographic and genetic differences, analyses were run using a paired design wherein each neonate’s follow-up samples (DOL1/3/7) were indexed to their own corresponding DOL0 sample. Two such paired models were evaluated: (i) overall ontogeny using all samples without specifying population, and (ii) population-specific ontogeny with population as an additional term in the design formula. A third model using an unpaired approach was also evaluated, wherein (iii) all samples within a given DOL were compared between populations (GAM vs PNG). Sequencing batch was also included as a covariate in all design formulas to control for potential batch effects. A two-sided Wald statistical test (with Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons) was used to identify DE genes, which were considered significant with an additional stringency filter of adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05 and fold-change (FC) ≥ 2 (equivalent to log2FC of 1). A more relaxed fold-change cut-off of 1.5 was also evaluated. Sex-specific DE genes were also identified by running an additional fourth DESeq2 model (iv) separated by sex, but small sample sizes precluded interpretation of the data.

Pathway enrichment was performed using the R package pathlinkR v1.7.3 (Sigora method to reduce duplication by assigning pathways using weighted pairs of genes that uniquely occur together in a single pathway was combined with a one-sided hypergeometric test with Bonferroni correction for multiple testing)52 DE gene lists were split into up- or down-regulated prior to testing for enriched pathways, and results were considered significantly enriched if the adjusted p-value was <0.001. Pathway networks were drawn using pathlinkR, which contains a module for linking pathways based on shared constituent genes, whereby each enriched pathway is represented as a node (circle), with edges (lines) connecting pathways that shared a significant number of constituent genes. The degree of connectivity between pathways was determined using the Jaccard Index, with a threshold of 0.8 used to define pathway pairs as being linked. The same procedure was repeated using enrichR v3.0 to test for significantly enriched terms from MSigDB’s Hallmark collection, with the same threshold used to determine significance53,54. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using NetworkAnalyst and literature-curated PPI data from InnateDB55–57. Complete DE gene lists were provided as the input to generate these networks, which were either a minimum order for DOL1 or zero order for DOL3. To further enable comparison of population-specific genes, DyNet12 was employed since it enables the drawing of joint interwoven networks.

Data management

Data management, governance and quality assurance for this study employed the EPIC-HIPC Data Management & Analysis Core58. Deidentified quality-assured data were deposited as described in the Data Availability statements. Data QA/QC criterion were defined and assessed specifically for each data modality (RNA-seq and flow cytometry). In addition, any samples where the reported sex conflicted with the gene expression clustering observed by principal component analysis were excluded.
