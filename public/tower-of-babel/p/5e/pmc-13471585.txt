Retinol-guided liposomal platform for targeted pirfenidone delivery in hepatic fibrosis
Authors: Jiachen Chen, Zihao Tao, Chenyu Qiu, Rui Wu, Zihao Huang, Xinyu Jiang, Pingyi Zhu, Yifan Wang, Longfa Kou, Xiujun Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiver fibrosis is a major contributor to global mortality due to its progressive disruption of hepatic architecture and function, and it remains a critical unmet medical need. Hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) are considered a key therapeutic target owing to their central role in fibrosis progression, primarily mediated through the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 signaling pathway. Pirfenidone (PFD), a clinically approved broad-spectrum antifibrotic agent, shows potential for repurposing in liver fibrosis therapy. However, its clinical translation is limited by poor aqueous solubility and a lack of cellular targeting specificity. To address these limitations, we developed a vitamin A–conjugated liposomal delivery system (P@GB-Lipo-VA) to enhance the liver-specific accumulation of PFD. In vitro studies demonstrated that P@GB-Lipo-VA significantly increased cellular uptake in TGF-β1–stimulated LX-2 cells and reduced oxidative stress. In a bile duct ligation (BDL)–induced mouse model of liver fibrosis, P@GB-Lipo-VA effectively alleviated collagen deposition, improved liver function, and suppressed activation of the TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway. These findings support P@GB-Lipo-VA as a promising targeted nanotherapeutic platform for enhancing the efficacy and safety of PFD in the treatment of liver fibrosis.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04453-8.Keywords: Liver fibrosis, Hepatic stellate cells, Pirfenidone, TGF-β1/Smad pathway, Vitamin A–modified liposomes

Introduction

Liver fibrosis is a chronic hepatic condition triggered by diverse etiological factors and pathologically characterized by an aberrant wound-healing response to persistent inflammation [1, 2]. A hallmark of this process is the excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) components, most notably type I collagen (Collagen I) [3–5] and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) [6, 7]. This fibrogenic environment is further exacerbated by the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and inflammatory mediators, which drive oxidative stress and perpetuate fibrotic progression [8]. A central player in liver fibrosis is the hepatic stellate cell (HSC), whose activation is primarily induced by the profibrogenic cytokine transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β1) [9–11]. Once activated, HSCs not only promote fibroblast proliferation through paracrine signaling but also sustain a self-amplifying loop of fibrosis via autocrine TGF-β1 secretion [12]. The downstream signaling of TGF-β1 is mediated by Smad proteins, which regulate gene expression upon phosphorylation [13, 14]. Under conditions of elevated oxidative stress, TGF-β1-induced Smad phosphorylation intensifies fibrotic signaling [14–16]. Therefore, targeting the TGF-β1/Smad axis to inhibit HSC activation and ECM deposition represents a promising antifibrotic strategy [17].

Currently, clinical management of liver fibrosis primarily aims to slow disease progression and manage complications such as cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma. However, direct therapeutic targeting of HSCs remains elusive [18]. Pirfenidone (PFD), a broad-spectrum antifibrotic drug approved by the U.S. FDA, has shown the capacity to attenuate TGF-β1–induced fibrotic signaling [19, 20]. It reduces collagen production, suppresses inflammatory infiltration, and alleviates oxidative stress [21]. Yet, its clinical utility is compromised by poor water solubility and limited organ specificity, leading to off-target toxicity [22].

Nanotechnology-based liver-targeted drug delivery provides a viable approach to overcoming these limitations by improving drug solubility, enhancing hepatic accumulation, and reducing systemic side effects [23–29]. For example, Cheng et al. developed a folate receptor-mediated nanoemulsion that increased PFD accumulation in HSCs by 3.8-fold [30]. Other inhalable nanoformulations, such as PFD@FPNs-CAT [31] and PFD-tFNA [32], have further improved bioavailability and reduced dosing frequency. Vitamin A (VA) and its derivatives are primarily stored in HSCs, making them a natural target for VA-mediated delivery. In circulation, VA binds to retinol-binding protein (RBP), forming a complex that is specifically recognized by receptors such as retinol binding protein receptor 2 (RBPR2) and the signaling receptor/transporter STRA6, which are highly expressed on the surface of HSCs [33, 34]. This facilitates selective VA uptake and storage, providing a basis for designing HSC-targeted drug delivery systems [35]. Several VA-functionalized nanocarriers, including liposomes and polymeric micelles, have shown enhanced drug accumulation and antifibrotic efficacy via this targeting mechanism [36, 37].

In this study, we designed and constructed a dual-stage liver-targeted nanotherapeutic system, termed P@GB-Lipo-VA. This system comprises a bilirubin-chitosan conjugate (GCBR), synthesized via amidation, which co-assembles with PFD into core nanoparticles. These were subsequently encapsulated into VA-functionalized liposomes via thin-film hydration, utilizing a targeting ligand (DSPE-PEG2000-VA) to exploit VA–RBP–HSC receptor interactions. The P@GB-Lipo-VA system offers several key advantages: (i) Bilirubin provides intrinsic antioxidant properties, acting synergistically with PFD to inhibit TGF-β1/Smad signaling; (ii) Chitosan enhances cellular uptake due to its cationic nature; (iii) VA-mediated active targeting significantly improves liver-specific delivery efficiency by 2- to 3-fold. These combined features facilitate ROS scavenging, anti-inflammatory effects, and inhibition of fibrotic signaling (Scheme 1). We hypothesize that this co-delivery platform will enhance the bioavailability and targeting precision of PFD and bilirubin, thereby producing synergistic therapeutic outcomes. Both in vitro and in vivo studies confirmed enhanced drug accumulation in liver tissue, reduced oxidative stress, and suppressed fibrotic markers. Collectively, our findings support the potential of P@GB-Lipo-VA as a translationally promising nanoplatform for targeted therapy of liver fibrosis and related chronic hepatic diseases.

Synthesis of chitosan-bilirubin (GCBR) and distearoyl phosphoethanolamine- polyethylene glycol- vitamin A (DSPE-PEG2000-VA) conjugate

GCBR was synthesized through an amide formation reaction using a slightly modified method [38]. Briefly, hydroxyethyl deacetylated chitosan (GC), bilirubin (BR), N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), and N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N’-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC) were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at a mass ratio of 47.4:22.5:8:13.5 (m/m/m/m). They were stirred for 24 h to ensure complete mixing. Then, the mixed solution was dialyzed using a 3500 MW dialysis bag for 24 h. The resulting solution was pre-frozen at −80 °C and subsequently freeze-dried to obtain GC-BR powder.

DSPE-PEG2000-VA was synthesized through an esterification reaction [39]. Dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP) and dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC) were used as catalysts for the esterification reaction. Briefly, DSPE-PEG2000-COOH, DMAP, DCC, and Vitamin A were weighed and dissolved in DMSO in a mass ratio of 10:6:10:10 (m/m/m/m). They were stirred for 48 h to ensure complete mixing. The solution was then subjected to dialysis (500–1000 MW) in ultrapure water under light conditions for 48 h. Pure water was replaced every 12 h. The mixture was frozen and dried to obtain DSPE-PEG2000-VA.

The successful synthesis of the GCBR and DSPE-PEG2000-VA conjugates was verified using a nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) instrument (AVANCE III, Rheinstetten, Germany), Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (NOTED, China), and ultraviolet (UV-Vis) absorption spectroscopy (MAPADA, China).

Preparation of P@GB, P@GB-Lipo and P@GB-Lipo-VA

P@GB nanoparticles were obtained through the reverse solvent method [40]. In simple terms, 10 mg of GCBR and 5 mg of pirfenidone (PFD) were prepared as a mixture solution in DMSO. Subsequently, the mixed solution was added to the PBS solution in a volume ratio of 1:10 (v/v) under stirring conditions (200 rpm) and reacted for 10 min. Subsequently, the product pirfenidone-loaded GCBR (P@GB) nanoparticles were obtained through dialysis and freeze-drying.

P@GB liposomes (P@GB-Lipo) and P@GB-Lipo-VA targeted liposomes (P@GB-Lipo-VA) were prepared by the film hydration method [41]. Briefly, an appropriate amount of PC, CH, PG, and DSPE-PEG2000-VA was dissolved in 1.5 mL of anhydrous ethanol. The non-targeted liposomes use DSPE-PEG2000 instead of DSPE-PEG2000-VA. The organic solvent was evaporated using the rotary evaporator method in the water bath at 50 °C, and then the prepared P@GB nanosolution was added for hydration for 1 h to obtain the initial emulsion. After sonicating at 100 W for 5 min (3 s on/3 s off) in an ice-water bath, the resulting suspension could be stored at 4 °C for subsequent use. When coumarin 6 or DiR was used as a fluorescent marker, C6@GB-Lipo-VA or DiR@GB-Lipo-VA were prepared by replacing pirfenidone following the protocol previously described.

In vitro targeting capability study

Coumarin 6 (C6) was used as a molecular probe and was incorporated during the preparation of the C6@GB-Lipo and C6@GB-Lipo-VA. LX-2 cells were seeded into a 12-well plate and incubated overnight to allow adherence. (2) Following activating with TGF-β1 (5 ng/mL) for 24 h, C6@GB-Lipo and C6@GB-Lipo-VA incubated with the induced LX-2 cells for 1 h, 2 h and 4 h. ROS scavenging ability

LX-2 cells were seeded into a 12-well plate at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well. After culturing for 24 h, the cells were further treated with TGF-β1 (5 ng/mL) for 24 h as the stimulation group. Subsequently, PFD, P@GB, P@GB-Lipo, and P@GB-Lipo-VA (100 µg/mL for PFD) were used to co-treat the cells for 24 h, respectively. Subsequently, the culture medium was aspirated, and cells were washed three times with PBS. The DCFH-DA (2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate) stock solution was diluted 1:1000 in serum-free medium, and 1 mL of the probe-containing medium was added to each well for 20 min at 37 °C. Following incubation, the medium was removed, and cells were washed three times with PBS. Fluorescence images were immediately captured via confocal microscopy, and fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ.

In vitro anti-liver fibrosis study

LX-2 cells were inoculated into 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. The cells were further treated with TGF-β1 (5 ng/mL) for 24 h as the stimulation group. Subsequently, PFD, P@GB, P@GB-Lipo, and P@GB-Lipo-VA (100 µg/mL for PFD) were treated with TGF-β1-induced cells for 24 h. The expression levels of related proteins such as TGF-β1, α-SMA, collagen Ⅰ, and p-Smad3 were determined by immunofluorescence. After the medium was replaced with fresh medium, cells were further fixed with ice-cold methanol for 30 min. Subsequently, PBS containing 0.25% Triton X-100 was used to permeabilize cells for 10 min. After treating with a 1% BSA blocking buffer for 0.5 h, the cells were then incubated with primary antibodies (1:100 dilution) in 1% BSA for 12 h at 4°C. After washing with PBS, the cells were incubated in the dark with DyLight 594 antirabbit secondary antibody (Abbkine, E032420-01) in 1% BSA for 1 h at 37 °C. Nuclei were stained with DAPI for 15 min and then captured via confocal microscopy. The expression levels of p-Smad2, p-Smad3, Smad2/3, and TGF-β1 antibodies were detected by western blotting with GAPDH as an internal control. After cell treatment, proteins were extracted using the RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. The total protein was determined using the BCA protein assay kit. The protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence imaging system and quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

Animal models

Animals were housed in a pathogen-free facility under a 12 h light/dark cycle at 20–25 °C. Liver fibrosis was induced in mice by bile duct ligation (BDL) under inhalation anesthesia. Briefly, C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized using an animal gas anesthesia system and placed in a supine position on a heated operating table. Anesthesia was maintained with 1% isoflurane in oxygen (0.8 L/min). After removal of the abdominal hair and routine disinfection, a midline laparotomy was performed to expose the hepatic hilum. The left medial liver lobe was gently elevated using a sterile cotton swab, and the intestines were carefully retracted inferiorly to expose the connection between the liver and the duodenum. The common bile duct was identified and carefully separated from the adjacent hepatic artery and portal vein using micro-forceps. Two 6 − 0 sterile silk ligatures were placed around the common bile duct, one at the proximal end and the other at the distal end, with sufficient spacing to avoid involving the pancreatic duct. The bile duct was then transected between the two ligatures. The abdominal cavity was rinsed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the organs were returned to their original positions. Finally, the peritoneum/muscle layer and skin were closed separately with interrupted sutures.

In vivo liver targeting

To assess the liver-targeting properties of nanoformulations, DiL fluorescent probes were encapsulated within liposomes to obtain DiL-labeled GB-Lipo (DiL@GB-Lipo) and DiL-labeled GB-Lipo-VA (DiL@GB-Lipo-VA). The fibrosis in mice was induced by BDL, and all procedures were performed under anesthesia. After surgery for 7 days, the BDL mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 3), and were intravenously administered with DiL@GB-Lipo and DiL@GB-Lipo-VA at a DiL dose of 10 µg/kg. After post-injection, in vivo fluorescence images were captured at 4 h using the IVIS Spectrum (PerkinElmer, America). Subsequently, the mice were sacrificed, and the fluorescence intensity of the harvested major organs was analyzed and quantified via ex vivo imaging.

In vivo antihepatic fibrosis

C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5 per group): sham control, BDL model, free PFD, and P@GB-Lipo-VA. Starting on day 1 after BDL surgery, mice in the treatment groups received tail vein injections once every 2 days for 2 weeks at a dose equivalent to 1 mg/kg PFD. Mice in the sham control and model groups received the same volume of PBS. After two weeks, the mice were sacrificed to harvest blood samples and key tissues, including heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys. The serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were measured using commercial ALT and AST assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). The key tissues were fixed with 10% formalin solution for embedding, sectioning, and staining. The others were stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. To evaluate the antifibrotic effects, tissue proteins were extracted and analyzed by Western blotting for α-SMA, collagen I, p-Smad3, Smad2/3, p-Smad2, and TGF-β1 protein expression levels. Immunohistochemical analyses were also performed to visualize the degree of fibrosis with corresponding fibrosis indicators, including α-SMA, collagen I, p-Smad3, and TGF-β1. All images were captured using a microscope, and conducted semi-quantitative analysis of the positively stained areas in each microscopic field was conducted.

The paraffin-embedded liver tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E), Sirius red stain, Oil Red O staining, and Masson’s Trichrome staining, respectively. Liver tissue pathological scoring was conducted according to the Suzuki standard, and the positive area was analyzed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed by t-test or one-way ANOVA analysis test with Tukey’s posttest. Results were expressed as mean ± SD. All data, statistics, charts, and analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Result

Synthesis and characterization of chitosan–bilirubin conjugates (GC-BR)

Chitosan–bilirubin (GC-BR) conjugates were successfully synthesized as the foundational building block for subsequent drug-loaded nanocarrier assembly. The chemical structure of GC-BR was confirmed through Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR), and ultraviolet-visible (UV/Vis) spectroscopy. As shown in Figure S1A, the synthetic route and chemical structures of GC, BR, and GC-BR were illustrated. In the FTIR spectrum (Figure S1B), a prominent absorption band appeared near 1680 cm⁻¹, corresponding to the amide bond formed between the carboxyl groups of BR and the primary amines of chitosan. The 1H NMR spectrum (Figure S1C) of BR exhibited characteristic proton signals at 10.6 ppm, 10.0 ppm, and 5.6 ppm, corresponding to the carboxyl, amine, and vinyl groups, respectively. In contrast, chitosan displayed a signal around 3.5 ppm due to its amine protons. The 1H NMR spectrum of the GC-BR conjugate revealed distinct peaks from both BR and chitosan, confirming the successful conjugation. UV/Vis analysis (Figure S1D) demonstrated a broad absorption peak around 450 nm for BR, attributed to its poor solubility and the strong solvation effect in DMSO. Upon conjugation with chitosan, the GC-BR exhibited a sharper, more defined peak at the same wavelength, indicating improved solubility and dispersion in DMSO, and confirming the formation of the conjugate.

Synthesis and characterization of DSPE-PEG₂₀₀₀-VA

To achieve targeted delivery to hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), a vitamin A-modified polymer, DSPE-PEG₂₀₀₀-VA, was synthesized and characterized via multiple analytical techniques (Figure S2A). FTIR spectra (Figure S2B) showed the attenuation of the carbonyl (C = O) stretch at 1750 cm⁻¹ in DSPE-PEG₂₀₀₀-VA compared to its precursor, DSPE-PEG₂₀₀₀-COOH, along with the emergence of C = C stretching vibrations indicative of esterification. A distinct band in the 1000–1300 cm⁻¹ range further confirmed the successful introduction of VA through ester bond formation. 1H NMR analysis (Figure S2C) showed characteristic signals at 1.25 ppm from the methylene protons of DSPE-PEG₂₀₀₀ and at 1.75 ppm from the VA moiety, confirming the chemical conjugation. UV/Vis spectra (Figure S2D) revealed no significant absorption in DSPE-PEG₂₀₀₀-COOH, whereas both VA and DSPE-PEG₂₀₀₀-VA exhibited a sharp absorption peak around 290 nm, further validating successful VA incorporation. Together, these results demonstrate the successful synthesis and structural integrity of both GC-BR and DSPE-PEG₂₀₀₀-VA conjugates.

Preparation and Characterization of P@GB, P@GB-Lipo, and P@GB-Lipo-VA

Self-assembled nanoparticles (P@GB) were fabricated from the GC-BR conjugate using an anti-solvent precipitation method. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) revealed a uniform particle size of 152.6 ± 1.5 nm (Fig. 1A), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed a spherical morphology (Fig. 1D). Subsequently, two liposomal formulations were developed using the thin-film hydration method: non-targeted P@GB-Lipo and VA-targeted P@GB-Lipo-VA. DLS analysis showed hydrodynamic diameters of 169.9 ± 8.9 nm and 197.6 ± 11.2 nm for P@GB-Lipo and P@GB-Lipo-VA, respectively (Fig. 1B and C). The increase in particle size upon VA functionalization indicated successful surface modification. TEM images further validated the typical liposomal bilayer structure for both formulations (Fig. 1E and F). Surface zeta potential measurements revealed a charge shift from +13.9 ± 0.6 mV (P@GB) to −23.6 ± 0.9 mV (P@GB-Lipo-VA), suggesting increased colloidal stability and reduced recognition by the reticuloendothelial system (RES), which is favorable for liver-targeted delivery. UV/Vis spectroscopy was employed to confirm drug encapsulation. The characteristic absorption peak of free PFD at 328 nm exhibited a hypsochromic shift to 311 nm in the nanoparticle and liposomal formulations (Fig. 1G), indicating successful encapsulation within the carrier matrices. Furthermore, the drug loading efficiency (DE) of P@GB-Lipo-VA reached 15.64 ± 0.12%. The colloidal stability of P@GB, P@GB-Lipo, and P@GB-Lipo-VA was assessed by monitoring particle size changes at 37 °C over time. P@GB displayed significant size increase by day 2, especially in PBS containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), indicating poor stability under physiological conditions. In contrast, both liposomal formulations maintained consistent particle sizes over 24 h (Figure S3A & S3B), demonstrating enhanced serum stability. Drug release profiles showed that P@GB released PFD rapidly, akin to free PFD. In contrast, P@GB-Lipo and P@GB-Lipo-VA exhibited sustained and delayed release behavior (Fig. 1H), aligning with the requirements of controlled intravenous administration. Lastly, hemocompatibility was evaluated by hemolysis assay. P@GB-Lipo-VA induced negligible hemolytic activity (Fig. 1I), confirming excellent blood compatibility and minimal erythrocyte membrane disruption.

These properties justify their selection for further in vivo targeting and antifibrotic efficacy evaluation.

Cellular uptake and targeted distribution of P@GB-Lipo-VA

Vitamin A (VA) exhibits selective affinity for retinol-binding protein (RBP) receptors, which are overexpressed on hepatic stellate cell (HSC) membranes, enabling targeted delivery to these fibrogenic cells. To evaluate this targeting capability, liposomes with or without VA modification were labeled with Coumarin 6 (C6), and designated as C6@GB-Lipo and C6@GB-Lipo-VA, respectively (Fig. 2A). Laser scanning confocal microscopy revealed time-dependent internalization of both formulations in LX-2 cells (Fig. 2C). Quantitative fluorescence analysis showed that intracellular fluorescence intensity in the C6@GB-Lipo-VA group was 1.8-fold higher than that of the non-targeted C6@GB-Lipo. Importantly, under TGF-β1 stimulation, this difference was further amplified to 3.8-fold (P < 0.0001), indicating enhanced receptor-mediated uptake under fibrogenic conditions (Fig. 2B). These findings were corroborated by flow cytometry, which confirmed significantly increased cellular uptake of VA-modified liposomes (Fig. 2D).

To verify the involvement of VA–RBP interactions, LX-2 cells were pretreated with free VA at a final concentration of 40 µg/mL. This concentration was confirmed to be non-cytotoxic by CCK-8 assay (Figure S4) prior to incubation with P@GB-Lipo-VA. As shown in Fig. 2E, cellular uptake of P@GB-Lipo-VA was significantly diminished following free VA pretreatment, suggesting competitive binding at the RBP receptor. These results indicate that VA modification enhances LX-2 cell uptake via receptor-specific interaction. To further assess whether the enhanced uptake of P@GB-Lipo-VA extended beyond activated HSCs, we performed an additional cellular uptake study using C6@GB-Lipo and C6@GB-Lipo-VA in AML12 hepatocytes (Figure S5). Notably, no significant difference in cellular uptake was observed between C6@GB-Lipo and C6@GB-Lipo-VA in normal AML12 cells. In contrast, VA modification significantly enhanced uptake in activated LX-2 cells, supporting that the uptake advantage conferred by VA decoration was more pronounced in activated HSCs than in hepatocytes and was consistent with a retinol receptor-related targeting mechanism.

To further elucidate the endocytosis mechanism, pharmacological inhibitors were employed. LX-2 cells were co-incubated with P@GB-Lipo-VA and either dimethylamiloride (DMA, macropinocytosis inhibitor), chlorpromazine (CPZ, clathrin-mediated endocytosis inhibitor), or nystatin (caveolae-mediated endocytosis inhibitor). Both confocal imaging and flow cytometry demonstrated that cellular uptake was markedly reduced in the presence of nystatin (Fig. 2G), while DMA and CPZ also exerted inhibitory effects. These results suggest that P@GB-Lipo-VA is primarily internalized via caveolae-mediated endocytosis. Additionally, uptake was substantially suppressed at 4 °C (Fig. 2H), confirming an energy-dependent internalization process.

Given the observed enhancement in cellular uptake in vitro, we next evaluated liver-targeting efficiency in vivo. Mice were intravenously injected with DiL-labeled liposomes, and major organs were harvested 4 h post-injection. In vivo imaging showed strong hepatic accumulation of DiL@GB-Lipo-VA (Fig. 2I), with fluorescence intensity approximately 1.5-fold higher than that of the non-targeted DiL@GB-Lipo group (P < 0.01, Fig. 2J). Ex vivo imaging of excised organs further confirmed selective hepatic distribution, with minimal off-target accumulation in other tissues (Fig. 2K). Quantitative analysis revealed a statistically significant increase in liver signal for the VA-modified group compared to the control (Fig. 2L). Collectively, these results validate the targeting efficiency of VA-functionalized liposomes and support their potential for fibrosis-specific hepatic drug delivery.

ROS scavenging and antifibrotic effects of P@GB-Lipo-VA in vitro

The antifibrotic potential of P@GB-Lipo-VA was evaluated in vitro using a TGF-β1-induced activated LX-2 cell model. Excessive ROS are known to play a central role in promoting hepatic fibrosis. To assess ROS levels, intracellular oxidative stress was quantified using the DCFH-DA probe. Following TGF-β1 stimulation, a substantial increase in green fluorescence (indicative of ROS generation) was observed (Fig. 3A). Treatment with free PFD partially suppressed this fluorescence, while both P@GB-Lipo and P@GB-Lipo-VA achieved more pronounced reductions, demonstrating their potent ROS-scavenging capabilities (Fig. 3D). Given the dual antioxidant and targeting features of P@GB-Lipo-VA, we hypothesized that this formulation may also reduce ROS-induced TGF-β1 expression. As shown in Fig. 3B and C, both free PFD and P@GB-Lipo decreased TGF-β1-associated fluorescence, with the most significant reduction observed in the P@GB-Lipo-VA group. These findings support the superior anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic potential of the VA-modified system.

To further elucidate the underlying mechanism, we analyzed the TGF-β1/Smad signaling axis—a key pathway involved in fibrosis progression. Western blot results revealed that P@GB-Lipo-VA significantly downregulated the phosphorylation levels of Smad2 and Smad3 compared to free PFD (Fig. 3E). Quantitative analysis of p-Smad/Smad and TGF-β1/GAPDH ratios (Figs. 3F–H) confirmed that P@GB-Lipo-VA effectively inhibited activation of the profibrotic TGF-β1 pathway. Key markers of HSC activation, including α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and type I collagen, were further evaluated by immunofluorescence. TGF-β1 stimulation substantially increased α-SMA expression, while this effect was notably suppressed by P@GB-Lipo-VA (Fig. 3I and J). Similarly, collagen I levels were significantly elevated following TGF-β1 treatment, but reduced upon treatment with PFD and P@GB-Lipo, with P@GB-Lipo-VA showing the most pronounced inhibitory effect (Fig. 3K). Quantification of fluorescence intensities further confirmed these findings (Fig. 3L).

In summary, P@GB-Lipo-VA effectively attenuates fibrogenic responses by scavenging intracellular ROS, downregulating TGF-β1 signaling, and inhibiting ECM marker expression. These results highlight its therapeutic potential as a targeted nanocarrier for hepatic fibrosis intervention.

P@GB-Lipo-VA protects against liver injury and fibrosis in vivo

The bile duct ligation (BDL) model is a well-established method for inducing liver fibrosis through bile acid accumulation, persistent inflammation, and chronic hepatocellular damage. As shown in Fig. 4A, this model was employed to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of P@GB-Lipo-VA (equivalent to 1 mg/kg of PFD). Treatments—including PBS, free PFD, and P@GB-Lipo-VA—were administered intravenously three times per week for two weeks following BDL surgery. A sham-operated group served as the healthy control. To assess liver function, serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were measured. As expected, BDL induced significant elevations in both markers, indicative of hepatic injury. Treatment with free PFD partially attenuated these increases, while P@GB-Lipo-VA restored ALT and AST levels nearly to baseline, comparable with those of the control group (Fig. 4B and C).

Histopathological analysis of liver tissues was performed using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson’s trichrome, and Sirius red staining to evaluate hepatic architecture and collagen deposition (Fig. 4D). H&E staining of the BDL group revealed extensive hepatocellular damage, including disorganized hepatic cords, inflammatory cell infiltration, and pericellular fibrosis—all of which were substantially alleviated by P@GB-Lipo-VA. Liver injury scores were quantified using Suzuki’s histological criteria, which assess congestion, vacuolization, and necrosis. Both PFD and P@GB-Lipo-VA significantly reduced injury scores compared to the BDL group (Fig. 4E). Sirius red staining revealed prominent collagen accumulation in fibrotic livers, with P@GB-Lipo-VA treatment leading to a 68.56% reduction in collagen-positive area, indicating robust antifibrotic activity (Fig. 4F). Similarly, Masson’s trichrome staining showed widespread extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition in the BDL group, which was markedly reduced by both treatment groups. Semi-quantitative analysis confirmed that P@GB-Lipo-VA reduced fibrotic area to 53.64%, reflecting a partial yet substantial antifibrotic effect (Fig. 4G).

To assess systemic biosafety, H&E staining was performed on major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney). No histopathological abnormalities were observed across treatment groups (Figure S6), suggesting favorable systemic tolerance. No obvious histopathological abnormalities were observed in the major organs examined, suggesting acceptable biosafety of P@GB-Lipo-VA under the tested conditions.

In summary, P@GB-Lipo-VA significantly attenuated hepatic injury and fibrosis in the BDL model, demonstrating both therapeutic efficacy and excellent biocompatibility in vivo.

Mechanistic Insights into P@GB-Lipo-VA–Mediated Antifibrotic Effects

To further elucidate the antifibrotic mechanism of P@GB-Lipo-VA in vivo, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed on liver tissue sections (Fig. 5A). Excessive deposition of collagen I, a principal component of the ECM, is a hallmark of liver fibrosis. IHC results revealed that collagen I accumulation was markedly reduced by P@GB-Lipo-VA, to a greater extent than free PFD (Fig. 5B), highlighting the contribution of VA-mediated targeted delivery. Another key fibrosis marker, α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), was strongly expressed in the BDL group, indicative of activated HSCs. Treatment with P@GB-Lipo-VA significantly suppressed α-SMA expression (Fig. 5C). Semi-quantitative analysis confirmed a clear reduction in both collagen I and α-SMA levels, indicating effective attenuation of fibrosis progression. Moreover, TGF-β1 expression was dramatically elevated—6.75-fold higher in fibrotic livers than in healthy controls (Fig. 5D), underscoring the involvement of the TGF-β1/Smad pathway in disease pathogenesis.

To validate this mechanism, Western blot analysis was conducted on liver tissues (Fig. 5E). The BDL model group exhibited significantly upregulated levels of TGF-β1 and downstream phosphorylated Smad2 (p-Smad2) and Smad3 (p-Smad3). Treatment with either PFD or P@GB-Lipo-VA markedly reduced the expression of these proteins. Specifically, P@GB-Lipo-VA decreased TGF-β1, p-Smad2, and p-Smad3 expression by 66.67%, 36.43%, and 56.34%, respectively (Fig. 5F and H), supporting suppression of the canonical fibrotic signaling axis. In addition, Western blot analysis showed substantial reductions in collagen I and α-SMA protein levels by 38.45% and 63.60%, respectively, following P@GB-Lipo-VA treatment (Fig. 5I and J). These molecular changes are consistent with the histological improvements observed and provide mechanistic evidence of P@GB-Lipo-VA’s antifibrotic action.

Together, these data demonstrate that P@GB-Lipo-VA exerts its therapeutic effects by selectively targeting activated HSCs, scavenging ROS, and inhibiting the TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway. This leads to downregulation of fibrotic markers and attenuation of ECM accumulation, ultimately mitigating liver fibrosis in vivo.

Discussion

Liver fibrosis is a key pathological process underlying the progression from chronic liver injury to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, making timely intervention essential to prevent end-stage liver disease [42]. This process is marked by excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition that disrupts hepatic architecture and impairs liver function. Among the diverse cellular mediators, hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) are considered the central effector cells in fibrogenesis [43]. Upon injury, quiescent HSCs undergo transdifferentiation into activated myofibroblast-like cells, secreting large amounts of ECM components—particularly collagen I and fibronectin—leading to fibrotic scar formation [44]. HSC activation is orchestrated by a complex interplay of profibrotic cytokines and signaling pathways [45]. Among these, transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) plays a pivotal role, promoting HSC transdifferentiation and ECM production through engagement of TGF-β1 RI/II receptors and downstream Smad2/3 phosphorylation [46]. In our study, TGF-β1 stimulation of LX-2 cells induced phenotypic transformation into activated myofibroblasts, as evidenced by elevated α-SMA and collagen I expression (Fig. 3J and K). Moreover, activated HSCs produce TGF-β1 in an autocrine manner, perpetuating their own activation and driving continuous fibrotic progression. This process is further exacerbated by oxidative stress and inflammatory cytokines, which stimulate ROS production (Fig. 3A), initiating a cascade of hepatocellular damage and profibrotic signaling. Given the centrality of HSCs in fibrogenesis, reversing or modulating their activation remains a primary strategy in antifibrotic drug development [47].

Pirfenidone (PFD), a pyridone-derived small-molecule drug approved by the FDA and EMA for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) treatment [48], exhibits anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic properties. It inhibits TGF-β1/Smad signaling, thereby reducing fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition [49]. Our findings corroborate this mechanism. In vitro, PFD effectively suppressed TGF-β1–induced expression of α-SMA and collagen I in LX-2 cells, along with a moderate reduction in ROS levels, indicating an additional antioxidant effect [50]. However, PFD’s clinical application is limited by poor aqueous solubility, low bioavailability, and lack of liver targeting [51]. Efforts to reformulate PFD using nanotechnology—such as lipid-based nanocapsules [52] or nanostructured carriers [52]—aim to improve its pharmacokinetic profile and hepatic accumulation. In this context, we developed a vitamin A-functionalized liposomal nanoplatform (P@GB-Lipo-VA) that employs retinol receptor-mediated targeting to enhance hepatic delivery of PFD. The PFD-loaded GC-BR nanoparticles (P@GB) were initially prepared via self-assembly, but their positive surface charge led to rapid clearance by the reticuloendothelial system (RES) and poor serum stability [53]. To overcome these limitations, we encapsulated P@GB into a DSPE-PEG2000-VA–modified liposomal shell, which conferred negative surface charge, improved stability, and favorable sustained-release behavior (Fig. 1G). HSC-targeted strategies often exploit receptors such as the M6P/IGF-II receptor [54], integrin αvβ3 [55], CD44 [56], and retinol-binding protein receptors (RBPRs) [35, 57].

HSCs are the primary vitamin A-storing cells in the liver, actively uptaking retinol via RBPRs. Thus, vitamin A-modified lipids can serve as targeting ligands by binding circulating RBP and mediating selective uptake into activated HSCs. Using a thin-film hydration approach, we successfully constructed P@GB-Lipo-VA, as confirmed by DLS, TEM, and UV spectroscopy. The resulting formulation demonstrated excellent serum stability and minimal hemolytic activity (Fig. 1I), suggesting good hemocompatibility for intravenous use. The PEG linker mitigated hepatic clearance and nonspecific serum protein interactions, further enhancing delivery efficiency [58]. In the quiescent liver, HSCs remain dormant, but under chronic injury, they become activated and contribute to ECM deposition [59]. To assess targeting efficacy, we performed in vitro uptake studies, which showed significantly enhanced cellular internalization of C6@GB-Lipo-VA in TGF-β1–stimulated LX-2 cells compared to non-targeted liposomes (Fig. 2C). Moreover, free VA pretreatment significantly reduced uptake, confirming the involvement of RBPR-mediated endocytosis (Fig. 2E). Although enhanced uptake of VA-modified liposomes was confirmed in activated LX-2 cells and not observed in AML12 hepatocytes, the present study did not systematically evaluate uptake in all major hepatic cell populations, such as Kupffer cells and liver sinusoidal endothelial cells. Therefore, the in vivo cell-type selectivity of this system within the liver warrants further investigation. In addition, intravenous administration was selected in this study because it enables systemic circulation of the nanocarrier and allows evaluation of liver accumulation and targeting performance under physiologically relevant conditions. In contrast, alternative routes such as oral administration may be limited by poor bioavailability, whereas local delivery is less suitable for the diffuse nature of liver fibrosis.

Oxidative stress is a major driver of fibrosis, triggering HSC activation and ECM overproduction [60]. P@GB-Lipo-VA demonstrated superior ROS-scavenging ability (Fig. 3A-D), likely due to the synergistic effects of PFD and bilirubin, the latter being a potent endogenous antioxidant. The GCBR core enhanced redox balance and reduced ROS-induced signaling. Bilirubin’s antifibrotic effects have been reported in various models of fibrotic disease [61]. Notably, ROS suppression by P@GB-Lipo-VA disrupted the TGF-β1/Smad pathway (Figs. 3E-H and 5E-H), which governs HSC activation and ECM synthesis. α-SMA and collagen I serve as key fibrosis markers and indicators of myofibroblast transformation and pathological ECM accumulation [62, 63]. Our study showed that P@GB-Lipo-VA significantly reduced both markers, thereby alleviating fibrotic remodeling.

In vivo fluorescence imaging confirmed preferential hepatic accumulation of DiL@GB-Lipo-VA in fibrotic mice (Fig. 2K), supporting enhanced targeting capability. Additionally, systemic safety was demonstrated via histological analysis of major organs, which showed no adverse effects following treatment (Figure S4). In the BDL mouse model of liver fibrosis, P@GB-Lipo-VA treatment led to histological improvement, including reduced collagen deposition and restoration of lobular architecture (Fig. 4D). Mechanistic studies revealed a marked downregulation of TGF-β1, p-Smad2, and p-Smad3 in treated livers (Fig. 5E), aligning with PFD’s known mechanism of suppressing fibrogenic signaling. The inhibition of the TGF-β1/Smad axis was accompanied by attenuated HSC activation and reduced collagen production, as evidenced by the decreased expression of α-SMA and collagen I (Figs. 3I–L and 5B–C and I–J). These findings suggest that integrating targeting, antioxidative, and antifibrotic functions within a single nanoplatform contributes to the observed histological and functional improvement.

This study provides compelling evidence for the therapeutic promise of VA-functionalized liposomes in targeted antifibrotic therapy. By combining PFD’s pharmacological activity with bilirubin’s redox regulation, the P@GB-Lipo-VA platform effectively addresses both the structural (ECM accumulation) and biochemical (oxidative stress) contributors to liver fibrosis. This system not only preserved hepatic architecture and function but also achieved substantial reductions in fibrotic markers and serum liver injury indicators (e.g., ALT, AST). These findings establish a robust mechanistic and translational rationale for advancing P@GB-Lipo-VA toward clinical development as a precision nanotherapeutic for hepatic fibrosis.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed a targeted nanodelivery system, P@GB-Lipo-VA, designed to enhance the antifibrotic efficacy of pirfenidone (PFD) by leveraging the overexpression of retinol-binding protein receptors on hepatic stellate cells (HSCs). This dual-functional strategy integrates antioxidative PFD@GB nanoparticles into vitamin A–modified liposomes, enabling selective uptake by activated HSCs via caveolae-mediated endocytosis. Our results demonstrate that P@GB-Lipo-VA significantly increased hepatic accumulation of PFD in fibrotic mice following intravenous administration, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off-target toxicity. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that P@GB-Lipo-VA exhibits potent hepatoprotective activity, effectively scavenging intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and attenuating extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition through inhibition of the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 signaling pathway. Taken together, this work presents a promising nanotherapeutic approach for the treatment of liver fibrosis by combining precise HSC-targeted delivery with redox regulation and antifibrotic signaling suppression. These findings provide a solid foundation for future clinical translation of VA-functionalized liposomal systems in fibrotic liver disease management.
