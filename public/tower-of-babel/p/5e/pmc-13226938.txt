Stiff-yet-tough glassy hydrogels for tendon rupture repair
Authors: Du Wang, Qianjiang Liu, Jingzhou Wang, Jinyuan Zhang, Jiaxing Feng, Zifan Wang, Yu Xue, Yongqiang Zhang, Han Wu, Shixian Lv, Yang Jiao, Hui Li, Jian Sun, Si Chen, Bohang Wu, Ji Liu, Dan Xing, Zehuan Huang, Jianhao Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Natural load-bearing tissues such as tendon exhibit both high stiffness and toughness owing to their densely packed, hierarchical architectures. However, most synthetic hydrogels suffer from biomechanical mismatch and lack of biocompatibility, which restrict their utility in tendon-related therapy. In this work, we report a set of glassy, poly(vinyl alcohol) hydrogels, stiffened and toughened through salting-out with a super-kosmotropic salt, sodium hexametaphosphate. The highly-charged anion displays an exceptional B-coefficient of 1.408 L mol−1 with remarkable kosmotropic effect, which not only promotes the formation of crystalline domains but also immobilizes free water molecules within gel networks. The resultant materials are endowed with substantially enhanced stiffness, toughness and fracture energy of 1061.8 MPa, 548.5 MJ m−3, and 272.5 kJ m−2, respectively, matching those of native tendons. Utilizing these hydrogels as biomechanical scaffolds in a tendon-ruptured rat model demonstrate high efficacy in both morphological and functional recovery. Salting-out biopolymers with super-kosmotropic agents enlightens the design and construction of stiff-yet-tough biomaterials, holding great promise for biomedical applications in regenerative and reparative therapies of natural load-bearing tissues.

Introduction

Natural load-bearing tissues, such as tendons, feature an exceptional combination of high strength, stiffness and toughness, which arises from their densely packed, hierarchical architectures spanning from molecular to mesoscopic scales [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Previous studies have reported that human tendons from young donors possess a Young's modulus up to 600 MPa and an ultimate tensile strength over 60 MPa [[6], [7], [8]]. In contrast, conventional hydrogels typically consist of loosely-crosslinked and homogeneous networks with low polymer content, resulting in their limited load-bearing capacity and poor energy dissipation under stress. Such weak and fragile structures hardly withstand high applied loads or impacts, leading to a pronounced biomechanical mismatch with native tendons. Due to the lack of tendon-like biomechanical properties, it remains a major challenge to simultaneously stiffen and toughen hydrogels to satisfy the requirements in regenerative and reparative therapies of tendon tissues.

Glassy gels have recently emerged as a new class of functional soft materials, which possess high stiffness and strength, comparable to glassy polymers, as well as gel-like extensibility and toughness. Two major approaches have been devised to prepare glassy gels: one is to co-polymerize two monomers with distinct solubility to form microphase-separated domains within gel networks [9], and the other is to introduce solvent-mediated crosslinks between polymer chains to soften glassy polymers [10]. However, these synthetic polymeric systems (e.g., polyacrylamide, polyacrylic acid) are rarely used as biomedical implants or devices in tissue regenerative therapy, on account of their limited biosafety. Therefore, exploiting biocompatible polymeric systems to access stiff-yet-tough, glassy hydrogels with enhanced mechanical properties matching natural load-bearing tissues, is critical for their biomedical applications.

Owing to high biocompatibility and commercial availability, poly(vinyl alcohol) - PVA is one of the synthetic polymers widely-used in biomedical engineering [[11], [12], [13]]. Numerous strategies have been developed to construct mechanically robust PVA hydrogels through incorporation of phase-separated architectures, such as ice templating [[14], [15], [16]], mechanical training [[17], [18], [19], [20]], controlled crystallization [21,22], and salting-out [23,24]. Synergistic combination of freeze-thaw and salting-out treatments has recently been shown effective in reinforcing strength and toughness of PVA hydrogels [24,25]. Repeated freeze-thaw cycles induce abundant nuclei of crystallization within gel networks, while subsequent salting-out drives hydrophilic PVA chains to aggregate and form densely-packed crystalline domains, which is known as the Hofmeister effect [26]. Highly-hydrated kosmotropic salts are essential (e.g., sodium citrate [23] and sodium sulfate [24]) for boosting the formation of high-density crystalline domains. However, due to high amount of free water remained in the networks, most reported PVA hydrogels still fall short of tendon-mimetic stiffness, strength and toughness, a major limitation for tendon replacement and repair.

Here, we develop a set of tendon-like, glassy PVA hydrogels through freeze-thaw and salting-out treatment with a super-kosmotropic, metabolizable salt (sodium hexametaphosphate [27], SHMP), Fig. 1A–C. Highly charged hexametaphosphate anion possess extremely-strong hydration capability, which is evidenced by the large B-coefficient (1.408 L mol−1) upon Jones-Dole analysis, distinct from all the other kosmotropic salts available in Hofmeister series (Fig. S1, Supporting Information). On this basis, such super-kosmotropic anions can cluster and stabilize water molecules through anion-enhanced hydrogen bonds, Fig. 1C, thus immobilizing water molecules and hardening the polymer-scarce phase between crystalline PVA domains. In this way, a new set of biocompatible, glassy hydrogels become accessible and exhibit an exceptional combination of high stiffness (>1000 MPa), strength (>75 MPa), toughness (>540 MJ m−3), and fracture energy (>270 kJ m−2), Fig. 1D, thus enabling their biomedical application in tendon-related therapy, e.g., tendon rupture repair.Fig. 1Design of biocompatible glassy hydrogels used for tendon rupture repair. Result and discussion

Stiffness and toughness

First of all, 10 wt% PVA solutions were subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles followed by 24 h immersion in SHMP solutions to construct PVA-SHMPx hydrogels (where x denotes the SHMP concentration). Increasing working temperatures for SHMP dissolution and subsequent salting-out allowed us to access a wide range of SHMP concentrations from 0.5 M to 3.1 M, beneficial for incorporating high amount of SHMP into hydrogel networks. Tensile tests were then conducted on as-prepared, dumbbell-shaped specimens by uniaxially stretching them until fracture for evaluation of their strength, stiffness, and toughness, Fig. 2A and Fig. S2 (Supporting Information). Elevating SHMP concentrations from 0.5 M to 3.1 M substantially strengthen PVA hydrogels with fracture strengths spanning from 15 to 75 MPa, Fig. 2B. Among them, PVA-SHMP3.1 hydrogels exhibited the highest Young's modulus of 1061.8 MPa, accessing the typical glassy region at GPa values. More importantly, such stiffness reinforcement does not sacrifice the toughness of PVA-SHMP hydrogels (ranging from 210.9 to 548.5 MJ m−3), likely arising from their unique network structures. To further interrogate their fracture-resistance, another set of notched specimens were prepared and stretched until the crack tip began to propagate, Fig. 2C and Fig. S9 (Supporting Information). The fracture energy Γ = HW(λc) was then calculated upon the Rivlin-Thomas method, where H represents the specimen height between fixtures and W(λc) is the strain energy density in material region far ahead of the crack tip [28]. To contextualize our findings, we compared them to other analogous materials known to display high strength, stiffness and toughness, Fig. 2E, F and 2G. The values for fracture strength, Young's modulus and fracture energy of PVA-SHMP hydrogels exceed many reported stiff and tough materials, comparable to native tendon/ligament and their commercial grafts. Crucially, PVA-SHMP hydrogels escape from a long-standing stiffness-toughness conflict between Young's modulus and fracture energy (Γ ∼ E−1/2), described by Lake-Thomas relation [29,30]. Such exceptional synergy of stiffness and toughness likely arises from the incorporation of super-kosmotropic SHMP that facilitates the formation of high-density crystalline domains in the polymer-rich phase and concurrently immobilize free water molecules in the polymer-scarce phase. This allows for the formation of glassy network structures, as evidenced by dynamic mechanical analysis, in which a typical glassy transition above room temperature was observed for PVA-SHMP2.5 hydrogels (Fig. S12, Supporting Information). Since the glass transition temperature exceeds room temperature, the PVA chains are frozen under ambient conditions, confirming that these hydrogels exist in a glassy state rather than a rubbery or viscoelastic solid state.

To demonstrate high load-bearing capability of our materials, a large piece of PVA-SHMP2.5 specimen with 28 (L) × 5 (W) × 2 (T) cm (where L is length, W is width and T is thickness) was prepared for a van-towing test, Fig. 2H and Video S1 (Supporting Information). Impressively, this rectangular specimen (35 g) successfully towed a four-wheel, 2100-kg van (>60,000 times of its own weight), overcoming substantial static and rolling frictions. No fracture or irreversible deformation was observed, highlighting remarkable mechanical robustness of the stiff-yet-tough PVA-SHMP hydrogels. Moreover, compared with other kosmotropic salts such as Na2SO4, (NaPO3)3, and Na3PO4, SHMP consistently imparted substantial mechanical enhancement under identical ionic strengths (Fig. S16, Supporting Information). General applicability of this SHMP-mediated salting-out approach was further extended to many other commonly-used biodegradable polymers including gelatin, carrageenan, and alginate, in which their resultant hydrogels all display substantial enhancement in fracture strength by 20-600 folds and Young's modulus by 80-800 folds (Fig. S17, Supporting Information).

Structure-property relationship

To unveil inherent relationship between superior mechanical performance and microscopic structures within PVA-SHMP hydrogels, a series of wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) and small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) experiments were carried out, Fig. 3A and B. The obtained WAXS profiles displayed three diffraction peaks at 2θ = 11.2°, 19.0°, and 22.4°, corresponding to typical reflection planes of semi-crystalline PVA. As SHMP concentrations increased from 0.5 to 3.1 M, the average size of these domains (D) was calculated from the full width at half maximum of the peak at 2θ = 19.0° upon the Scherrer's equation (Fig. 3C, Fig. S8, Supporting Information), which remained almost unchanged at D = 6.1 nm, identical to previous reports [21]. More importantly, the average distance between adjacent crystalline domains (L) was derived from the SAXS peak position upon the Bragg equation (Fig. 3B), which decreased from 11.4 to 7.1 nm suggesting enhanced crystallinity upon elevated SHMP concentrations. To further quantify the structural conformation, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) experiments were performed to measure crystallinities of both dried and swollen PVA-SHMP hydrogels, Fig. 3D [31,32]. To investigate the influence of free water molecules on mechanical properties, low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) was further employed to estimate the contents of free and bound water molecules as well as soft and hard PVA segments within PVA-SHMP hydrogels [33,34]. Three sets of relaxation peaks were observed, Fig. 3E, corresponding to the hydrogen atoms locating at different regions within the network structures. The assignment of these relaxation peaks was independently validated through progressive control experiments (Fig. S7, Supporting Information). The relaxation peak between 3 ms and 30 ms arises from free water molecules remained in the networks, while the neighboring one between 0.3 ms and 3 ms represents bound water molecules and soft PVA segments in the polymer-scarce phase. More importantly, an unusual peak appeared 0.01-0.04 ms, likely originating from hard PVA segments in the polymer-rich phase.

Integration of the obtained signal intensity further revealed that the amount of free water molecules within the networks gradually decreases with the elevated SHMP concentrations from 0.5 M to 3.1 M and the increased weight ratio of SHMP to residual water, Fig. 3F and Fig. S6 (Supporting Information). This is likely on account of the super-kosmotropic SHMP salts remained in the polymer-scarce phase that greatly limit the motion of water molecules to strengthen the obtained materials, thus resulting in reinforced stiffness for PVA-SHMP hydrogels without sacrificing their toughness. Meanwhile, the amounts of bound water molecules, soft and hard PVA segments all display substantial increase until reaching a maximum transition at 3.1 M, in which extremely-high amount of SHMP even reduced the bound water content in the polymer-scarce phase. Overall, above data indicate that densely packed PVA chains within semi-crystalline regions and reduced amount of free water through incorporation of SHMP may synergistically lead to exceptional mechanical performance of glassy PVA materials.

One intriguing advantage of our reported systems is to impart glassy PVA materials with self-healing capability through salt-welding [35,36], despite their high mechanical strength. Two salt-welding configurations (side-by-side and end-to-end) of PVA-SHMP2.5 hydrogels were designed and investigated by tensile tests, Fig. 3G. In the side-by-side salt-welding mode, the PVA-SHMP2.5 specimens were cut and reattached along the crack with an overlapped length of 4 mm, followed by 24 h immersion in SHMP solutions to facilitate self-healing and welding the cut surfaces. The welded PVA specimen exhibits high recovery efficiency up to 79.2 ± 4.8 % and 70.3 ± 4.7 % in Young's modulus and fracture strength, respectively. In contrast, the end-to-end configuration showed relatively low recovery efficiency with only 40.0 ± 4.4 % in Young's modulus and 15.0 ± 2.3 % in fracture strength. Such excellent recovery performance of the side-by-side mode is likely attributed to relatively large contact area that promotes the in situ formation of enormous crystalline domains at the cut interface, thereby leading to enhanced recovery of material stiffness and strength [36,37].

Conventional polymeric hydrogels and elastomers with high mechanical stiffness often face challenges in damage repair, which restricts their post-healing mechanical performance. To contextualize our findings, a comprehensive comparison for post-healing strength and Young's modulus of our materials with those reported hydrogels and elastomers is shown in Fig. 3H. Among them, PVA-SHMP2.5 hydrogels achieve a record-high post-healing strength while maintaining a high Young's modulus above 60 MPa. Such convenient salt-welding approach further enables the construction of stable-yet-robust architectures. For instance, a group of leaf-shaped soft specimens were firstly assembled and then welded through salting-out in SHMP solutions, resulting in into a self-supporting, flower-like structure, Fig. 3I. Similarly, a cross-shaped ribbon with a thickness of 1 mm were folded and locked into a hollow cube, capable of supporting a weight of 1000 g, Fig. S15 (Supporting Information) and Video S3 (Supporting Information). Overall, above demonstrations highlight great potentials of glassy PVA-SHMP hydrogels for the design of functional hierarchical soft materials.

Biomechanical cues guiding tendon healing

To demonstrate utility of biocompatible PVA-SHMP hydrogels, we employed these stiff-yet-tough glassy materials in biomedical applications that require high load-bearing capacity, such as tendon rupture repair, which are not feasible with existing soft or degradable synthetic hydrogels. Biomechanical properties of a matrix, such as stiffness and toughness, provide crucial physical cues that regulate cell behavior and guide functional tissue regeneration [[38], [39], [40]]. Creating a microenvironment capable of offering biomechanically compatible support throughout entire repair period is essential for load-bearing tendon tissues [[41], [42], [43], [44]]. Repairing Achilles tendon defects is particularly challenging, as it requires the neotissue to withstand high tensile stress while undergoing organized collagen remodeling. A critical unmet clinical need exists for scaffolds that can provide long-term, tendon-like mechanical support (Young's modulus >60 MPa) in vivo and subsequently degrade in a timely manner after tissue maturation.

To address this problem, stiff-yet-tough PVA-SHMP2.5 materials were employed as a regenerative scaffold denoted as stiff hydrogel, which possess high Young's modulus (64.2 MPa) and fracture energy (272.5 kJ m−2), comparable to biomechanical properties of native tendons. In vitro tests confirmed the biocompatibility of these glassy hydrogels (Fig. S20, Supporting Information). More importantly, such stiff hydrogels maintain structural integrity within the first 4 weeks that escape from rapid degradation, while degrade over approximately two months in vivo, aligning with the critical timeframe for functional tendon regeneration. Such degradation behavior provides a stable platform for mechanical stress conduction and cell growth during tendon repair (Fig. S23, Supporting Information). The stiff hydrogel was subcutaneously implanted in rats for one week, in which the explanted hydrogel retained 75.0% of its original fracture strength and 65.2% of its pristine Young's modulus (Fig. S25, Supporting Information). On this basis, we evaluated the PVA-SHMP2.5 hydrogels as a biomechanical implant (stiff hydrogel group) for Achilles tendon repair in a rat model. As comparison, PVA-SHMP0.5 hydrogels was used as soft hydrogel group alongside a blank control without any scaffolds.

Surgical procedures that preserved the transected Achilles tendon sheath are illustrated in Fig. 4A. After 8-week post-implantation, the stiff hydrogel group demonstrated improved defect repair with no scaffold residue, on account of their efficient biodegradation. The blank control group formed irregular scar tissue, while the soft hydrogel group showed tissue continuity but lacked dense collagen fiber structure. Both control and soft hydrogel groups exhibited dense adhesion, preventing easy blunt dissection between the injury site and surrounding tissue. In contrast, the stiff hydrogel group provided regenerated tissues that possess smooth, dense, tendon-like morphology with clear boundaries between adjacent tissues and no pathological adhesion observed, allowing easy blunt dissection closely resembled with native tendon. The restoration of tendon's microstructure, particularly the reconstruction of aligned, dense collagen fibers, is also crucial for function recovery [45]. In the early repair phase (4 weeks), increased collagen deposition was observed in the defect area of both stiff and soft hydrogel groups (Fig. S27, Supporting Information). In the final repair phase (8 weeks), although collagen content had increased in all repair groups, the fiber morphology differed substantially. To interrogate the regulatory role of stiff hydrogel in tendon regeneration, we systematically investigated the composition and maturity of the neocollagen, Fig. 4C. The spatiotemporal dynamics and ratio of collagen type I (COL1) to collagen type III (COL3) are the key determinants of regenerated tissue function, with a COL1-dominated matrix conferring major mechanical properties and structural stability [46]. Picrosirius Red staining under polarized light revealed that the repair tissue in the stiff hydrogel group exhibited similar morphology to native tendon, evidenced by dense, well-aligned bright yellow fibers throughout the defect, indicating a dominant and functional COL1 matrix. In contrast, repair areas in the control and soft hydrogel groups primarily consisted of disorganized greenish fiber networks, representing typical of scar-like tissue rich in COL3. Dynamic changes in collagen expression further supported these findings. Throughout the repair process, the stiff hydrogel group demonstrated continuous, positive collagen remodeling: COL1 deposition was significantly enhanced and increased over time, while COL3 accumulation was effectively suppressed. This active remodeling steadily shifted the COL1/COL3 ratio towards that of mature tendons (Fig. S28, Supporting Information). In contrast, collagen remodeling plateaued in the later stages for the soft hydrogel group, and the blank control maintained an immature extracellular matrix dominated by COL3.

Collagen regeneration pattern corresponded with dynamic changes in key tendon regulatory factors, Fig. 4D. The stiff hydrogel group showed a sustained positive progression: the expression of tendon maturation marker TNMD continually increased, while the pro-fibrotic driver α-SMA remained suppressed (Figs. S29–S32, Supporting Information). In addition, the early tendon-lineage transcription factor Scx peaked at 4 weeks and decreased by 8 weeks, revealing a temporal progression from early lineage commitment to later functional maturation. In contrast, the repair process in the soft hydrogel group stagnated in the later phases, failing to achieve further maturation. This contrast clearly indicates that the stiff hydrogel not only initiates regeneration but also sustains and advances the process beyond the early stages through its stable mechanical support and stress conduction, leading to substantial functional recovery. This process was supported by a favorable immune microenvironment. The stiff hydrogel group exhibited a continuous decrease in pro-inflammatory CD68+ signals alongside sustained high levels of pro-repair CD206+ signals throughout the repair period. The soft hydrogel group, however, showed features of an aborted repair process, where initially induced CD206+ signals significantly declined later and CD68+ signal clearance was delayed, Fig. 4D, Fig. S33 (Supporting Information). Overall, these results indicate that the stiff hydrogel can guide functional matrix regeneration and concurrently ensure repair quality through establishing and maintaining a beneficial immune milieu [47].

Beneficial regeneration patterns observed at the molecular and tissue levels were studied by macroscopic structural integrity. Micro-CT and Safranin O staining analysis confirmed the absence of heterotopic bone within stiff hydrogel group, which indicated that the stiff scaffold can effectively avoid heterotopic ossification, a common complication in tendon repair, Fig. 4E, Fig. S34 (Supporting Information) [48,49]. Furthermore, T2-weighted MRI images showed a continuous, structurally intact tendon regeneration in the stiff hydrogel group, while defects and structural gaps persist in the other groups. Clearly, the stiff hydrogel group demonstrated the optimal-restored tendon contour and the highest signal uniformity, substantially outperforming both the soft hydrogel and control groups, Fig. 4F. Quantitative MRI analysis based on relative signal intensity (RSI, tendon-to-muscle ratio) showed that the stiff hydrogel group exhibited the lowest RSI among all groups, indicating its high structural compaction and well-organized collagen architecture. Overall, above results from both morphological and structural standpoint elucidate high efficacy of stiff-yet-tough PVA-SHMP2.5 hydrogels for functional tendon regeneration.

At week 4, the stiff hydrogel group already demonstrated significant recovery efficacy across multiple core metrics, Fig. 5E–J. The swing speed, stride length, and intensity parameters of rats in stiff hydrogel group were substantially better than those of the control and soft hydrogel groups, approaching normal levels, and concurrently, its swing time was significantly reduced, indicating improved movement rhythm. In contrast, the control group displayed poor performance across all parameters, which is consistent with persistent pain avoidance and functional limitation. At week 8, the divergence in recovery trends became more pronounced. The stiff hydrogel group achieved significant recovery efficacy, as evidenced by swing speed, stride length, and paw load-bearing intensity that exceed the baseline levels of normal rats (Video S4, Supporting Information). Although limited improvement can be observed for the soft hydrogel group, the degree of recovery for all parameters lagged behind the stiff hydrogel group. The control group only exhibited some spontaneous recovery but with pronounced functional deficits. Altogether, the gait analysis results demonstrate that implantation of the stiff hydrogel effectively and persistently restored Achilles tendon function. The efficacy of the stiff hydrogel in improving locomotion mechanics and reducing pain was substantially greater than that of the soft hydrogel or natural healing, which highlights the importance of biomechanically-matched stiffness and toughness for functional recovery of tendon. Furthermore, tuning of degradation kinetics, evaluation on functional recovery in larger-scale studies, and long-term in vivo mechanical testing in large animal models remain as future directions to push forward the clinical use of our materials.

Conclusion

In this study, we introduced a new set of biocompatible glassy hydrogels prepared through salting-out in the super-kosmotropic salt solution. Sodium hexametaphosphate possesses an exceptional B-coefficient of 1.408 L mol−1 in Hofmeister series, which highly enhance kosmotropic effects during salting-out. This facilitates the formation of dense crystalline domains in the polymer-rich phase and immobilizes free water molecules in the polymer-scarce phase, enabling the formation of glassy soft materials. Compared to conventional analogues, the resultant hydrogels possess remarkable stiffness (>1.0 GPa), strength (>75 MPa), toughness (>540 MJ m−3) and fracture energy (>270 kJ m−2), comparable to those of native tendons. Such stiff-yet-tough materials were then applied as a biomechanical scaffold for use in tendon rupture repair, leading to both morphological regeneration and functionality recovery. Compared to commercial tendon grafts, our hydrogels achieve comparable strength with substantially improved toughness, while the low-cost, scalable fabrication process further supports their translational potential. We envision that this line of research will pave a new path for the design and fabrication of biocompatible glassy materials, holding substantial promise for biomedical applications in regenerative and reparative therapies of natural load-bearing tissues.

Experimental section

Materials. Unless otherwise stated, all the chemicals in this research were used without further purification: poly(vinyl alcohol) (MW: 89000-98000 Da, Sigma Aldrich), sodium hexametaphosphate (97%, Dibo), sodium trimetaphosphate (99%, Dibo), sodium alginate (200 ± 20 mPa s, Aladdin), sodium citrate (98%, Aladdin), potassium carbonate, anhydrous (AR, Xihua), carrageenan (Reagent Grade, Aladdin), gelatin (∼240 g Bloom, Aladdin).

Preparation of PVA solution. 10 wt% PVA solutions were prepared by dissolving PVA powder in deionized water. The mixtures were heated to 95 °C in a water bath and stirred at 200 rpm for up to 4 h to yield transparent and homogeneous solutions. Finally, the PVA solutions were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 90 s to remove the bubbles.

Preparation of salt solution. Salt solutions at different concentrations were prepared by dissolving the salts in deionized water. After heating and stirring for 1 h at certain temperatures, a series of salt solutions was obtained. SHMP solutions with concentrations at 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 M were prepared at 30 °C; the saturated SHMP solutions with concentrations at 2.5, 2.6, 2.8, and 3.1 M were prepared at 30, 40, 50, and 60 °C, respectively.

Fabrication of hydrogel. PVA solution was poured into a Petri dish to form a uniform layer. The solution was then subjected to a freeze-thaw cycle, which involved freezing at −20 °C for 8 h and thawing at 40 °C for 3 h. This cycle was repeated three times to form a physically cross-linked hydrogel. The resulting hydrogel was subsequently immersed in salt solutions of varying concentrations at specified temperatures for designated time intervals. After salting-out treatment, dumbbell-shaped specimens were cut from the hydrogel sheet according to ISO 4661-1 standard, with final dimensions of 10 mm in length, 2 mm in width, and 0.1–0.5 mm in thickness. Similar fabrication procedures were also applied to prepare gelatin, carrageenan, and alginate hydrogels.

Tensile tests. Tensile tests were performed on an MTS Criterion Electromechanical Test System equipped with a 500-N load cell under ambient conditions (room temperature, ∼30% humidity). The hydrogels, after the salting-out treatment, were cut into dumbbell-shaped specimens conforming to the ISO 4661-1 standard. The initial nominal dimensions were 10 mm in gauge length, 2 mm in width, and 0.1–0.5 mm in thickness. The exact thickness of each specimen was measured before testing. A constant crosshead speed of 100 mm min−1 was applied during the tests. Young's modulus was calculated from the initial slope (between 1% and 10% strain) of the stress-strain curves. Fracture energy, representing the energy absorbed per unit volume until fracture, was quantified by the integrated area under the stress-strain curves. Each test was repeated with at least three individual samples.

Pure tear tests. Hydrogels were cut into rectangular specimens with a thickness of 0.1 to 0.3 mm, a height of 40 mm and a width of 10 mm for pure shear tests. The thickness of each specimen was measured with a digital caliper. An initial clamp distance of 2 mm was used for every pair of specimens. For pure shear tests, two identical samples (one notched, one unnotched) were loaded under the sample setup as a pair to obtain the fracture energy. Briefly, for the notched samples, an initial 2-mm-long straight cut was made from the middle of the long edge towards the center of hydrogels. The critical strain (εC) for unstable propagation of the crack was obtained from the strain at maximum stress. The pairing unnotched specimens were subsequently loaded until ε = εC. The value for fracture energy (Γ) was obtained by: Γ=H∫0εCσdε, where H is the initial distance between clamps, σ is the area under the stress-strain curve of the unnotched specimens.

Measurement of water content. Hydrogel samples were dried in an oven at 60 °C for 5 days till totally dried to measure their water contents. The weight of the sample before and after drying were measured as Wswollen and Wdry, and water content (W) can be obtained from the following equation: W = (1–Wdry/Wswollen) × 100%.

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR). Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance was employed to translate structure information into relaxation time (degrees of freedom) of hydrogen atoms. Less constrained hydrogen atoms result in higher relaxation time. LF-NMR tests were performed by nuclear magnetic resonance analyzer (VTMR20-010V-I, Suzhou Niumag Analytical Instrument Corporation, China). PVA-SHMPx hydrogels were added into LF-NMR test tubes (8 mm in diameter). Signals of hydrogen atoms from free water, bound water, soft and hard segments were measured under MagicSE-CPMG (Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill) sequence with 16 scans, 8000 echoes, an echo time of 0.3 ms and a waiting time of 2000 ms, and multicomponent fitting of the original T2 decay curve was performed.

Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES). Phosphorus content in PVA-SHMPx hydrogels was determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) on an Agilent 5110 instrument. PVA-SHMPx hydrogels were digested with 3 mL of high-purity nitric acid (65% HNO3) using a microwave digestion system (CEM Mars 6). The digested solutions were diluted to 10 mL with ultrapure water and filtered through 0.45 μm membranes before analysis. Calibration standards were prepared from certified phosphorus reference solutions in the concentration range of 0.1-10 mg L−1. Measurements were performed at the characteristic phosphorus emission wavelength of 213.618 nm, with three independent replicates for each sample.

SEM characterization. For characterization of micro- and nano-structures of hierarchically aligned hydrogels, all the hydrogel specimens were immersed in DI water for 24 h before freeze-drying using a Labconco FreeZone freeze drier. The freeze-dried hydrogels were cut along the aligned direction to expose the inside and sputtered with gold before carrying out the imaging using a ZEISS Supra 40VP SEM.

Crystallinity content measurement. Differential scanning calorimeter (TA Instruments, DSC Q2500) was used to measure crystallinities of PVA-SHMPx hydrogels at their dry state through freeze-drying. To minimize extra crystallization during freeze-drying, we first used excess chemical cross-linkers (i.e., glutaraldehyde) to fix amorphous PVA polymer chains before freeze-drying. In a typical DSC measurement, a freeze-dried hydrogel sample was placed in a hermetically sealed Tzero pan, and a reference Tzero pan without any samples was also contained. The hydrogel sample was heated up from 20 to 250 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min under N2 environment. The heat-flow curve displayed a narrow peak ranging from 200 to 250 °C, corresponding to the typical melting of the crystalline domains in the sample. Melting enthalpy of crystalline domains per unit mass of the sample (Hcrystalline) can be calculated by integration of endothermic transition from 200 to 250 °C. Therefore, the crystallinity in the dry sample (Xdry) can be estimated as Xdry = Hcrystalline/ Hcrystalline0, where Hcrystalline0 = 138.6 J g−1 is the enthalpy of the fusion of 100 wt% crystalline PVA measured at the equilibrium melting point. The water content of the swollen-state sample was denoted as W, the crystallinity in the swollen sample can be calculated as Xswollen = Xdry (1 − W).

X-ray scattering. Wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) was performed on the Xenocs (GANESHA) instrument with an X-ray of λ = 0.154 nm, operated at 50 kV and 0.6 mA, and the WAXS profiles were collected in the q range of 0.07-2.7 Å−1. The average size of crystalline domains (D) can be estimated using the Scherrer's equation [D=kλ/βcosθ], where dimensionless shape factor (k) is set as 1.0 if the shape of the crystalline domain is approximated as a sphere, λ is the wavelength of x-ray diffraction, θ is the Bragg angle and β is the full width at half maximum of the peak. Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) was carried out at Xenocs (GANESHA) with an X-ray of λ = 0.154 nm. The sample-to-detector distance was 1050 mm, covering a momentum transfer (q) range of 0.007-0.25 Å−1, and the exposure time was set as 300 s. To identify the location of the scattering peak more clearly, we multiplied the scattering intensity (I) with the square of q and plotted as Iq2 vs q. According to the Bragg expression (L=2π/qmax), the average distance between adjacent crystalline domains (L) can be estimated from the q of scattering peak (qmax).

Dynamic Mechanical Analysis (DMA). Dynamic Mechanical properties (i.e., viscoelasticity) of PVA-SHMPx hydrogels were characterized by a dynamic mechanical analyzer (DMA, Q800, TA Instruments). Hydrogel specimens were prepared in rectangular strips with dimensions of 20 mm × 5 mm × 1 mm (length × width × thickness). DMA measurements were conducted in tension mode with a preload force of 0.01 N and an amplitude of 10 μm. Temperature-sweep experiments were performed from −30 to 60 °C at a heating rate of 3 °C min−1 under a constant frequency of 1 Hz. Glass transition temperature (Tg) was determined from the peak position of the tan δ curve.

Biocompatibility. Biocompatibility of PVA-SHMPx hydrogels in vitro was evaluated using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay and live/dead cell staining. Human mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs, Viral Therapy Technologies) were cultured in α-Minimum Essential Medium (α-MEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Following ISO 10993-12,50 material extracts were prepared by incubating sterile hydrogel samples in complete cell culture medium at a ratio of 1 g material per 5 mL medium for 24 h at 37 °C. The resultant extracts were subsequently filtered through 0.22 μm sterile filters (Millipore) prior to biological testing. For the assays, MSCs were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 10000 cells per well in 100 μL of culture medium and allowed to adhere for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with the hydrogel extracts; control groups received fresh culture medium. After a 24 h exposure period, cell viability was assessed. For live/dead staining, cells were incubated with a LIVE/DEAD® Viability/Cytotoxicity Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer's protocol and imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus CKX53). For CCK-8 assay, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by incubation for 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BIO-DL, K3 Plus), with blank corrections applied using medium-only controls. All experiments were performed with at least six replicate wells per condition.

Animal model. All animal experiments were conducted according to the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Six-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats were anesthetized via inhalation of isoflurane. Following the induction of anesthesia, the surgical site on the hind limb was shaved and disinfected. A longitudinal skin incision, approximately 1 cm in length, was made along natural depression between the tibia/fibula and the Achilles tendon. The skin and subcutaneous tissues were carefully dissected to fully expose the Achilles tendon. The midpoint of the Achilles tendon was identified. Using fine ophthalmic forceps or scissors, the tendon tissue was bluntly dissected longitudinally. An outer sheath membrane was punctured to completely separate the midpoint of the tendon from the surrounding tissues. A one-third partial resection was performed at the midpoint of the Achilles tendon to create a tendon defect. The PVA-SHMP hydrogel was trimmed to match the defect size and implanted as an inter-positional graft to replace the resected tendon tissue. The hydrogel was secured in place using 6-0 sutures passing through the residual host tendon and the hydrogel edges. After surgery, the operated hindlimb of each rat was immobilized with an external splint for one week to protect the injury site and reduce excessive tensile loading. The splint was then removed to allow free cage activity. Rats were randomly assigned to three groups: the control group (defect without any implant), the soft hydrogel group (implantation of PVA-SHMP0.5), and the stiff hydrogel group (implantation of PVA-SHMP2.5). Gait analysis was conducted at 4 weeks and 8 weeks post-surgery, after which the rats were euthanized for subsequent histological, immunohistochemical, Micro-CT, MRI, and other analyses. Post-operative analgesia and care were provided according to standard protocols.

Micro-CT. Micro-CT (SkyScan 1176; Bruker, Kontich, Belgium) was used to image the collected rat joints, with 13 μm resolution, 60-kV voltage, 200-μA current, and 300-ms exposure time. The resultant 2D and 3D reconstructed images were analyzed with DataViewer64 and CTvox64.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging. For high-resolution morphological assessment, ex vivo magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was conducted on the harvested rat limbs. Following euthanasia at predetermined time points, the right lower hind limb (distal to the knee joint, encompassing the entire Achilles tendon and calcaneus) was dissected and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h. Prior to imaging, the fixed specimens were rinsed in the phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual fixative. MRI was performed using a high-field preclinical MRI system (Discovery MR750 3.0T). High-resolution three-dimensional (3D) T2-weighted and proton density-weighted sequences were employed. Typical acquisition parameters were: repetition time (TR) = 3200 ms, echo time (TE) = 50-55 ms, field of view (FOV) = 25 mm × 25 mm, matrix size = 256 × 256, slice thickness: 1 mm. Multiple averages were used to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio. The obtained images were analyzed to evaluate the microstructure of regenerated tendon tissues, including tendon continuity, fiber organization, the interface between native tendons as well as regenerated tissues or hydrogel implants, and the presence of any ectopic mineralization or adipose tissue infiltration. The MR images were processed and analyzed by the RadiAnt DICOM Viewer software.

Histology. For histological analysis, tendon tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4-μm thickness using a microtome (Leica Biosystems). Hematoxylin-Eosin (H&E) staining and Masson staining were conducted to perform a histomorphology analysis of tendon regeneration in different phases. Sirius Red staining and Safranin O staining were used to study the collagen reconstruction and proteoglycan deposition in tendons. Samples were treated as described above for de-paraffinization and re-dehydration, then stained according to the standard protocols [50]. The images of H&E, Masson and Safranin O were captured by light microscope (Olympus), and the Sirius Red staining was also observed under polarized light.

Immunohistochemistry and Immunofluorescence. Tendon tissues were immunohistochemistry stained with TNMD (BIOSS), α-SMA (Abcam), CD68 (Servicebio) and CD206 (COST) to evaluate the tendon differentiation and fibrotic response. All the slides were captured by the light microscope (Olympus). Tendon tissues were immunofluorescence stained with COL1 (Proteintech) and COL3 (Proteintech) to evaluate the collagen regeneration. All the slides were captured by an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus). For semi-quantitative analysis, three to five randomly selected non-overlapping high-power fields (200×) within the defect center were analyzed per section, focusing on the tendon parenchyma while excluding blood vessels, residual scaffold material, and surrounding connective tissue. Semi-quantification was performed by two independent observers blinded to the group allocation.

Gait analysis. Gait analysis was conducted using the Catwalk XT system (Noldus, Netherlands) with the standard rat calibration (green light intensity, camera gain, run duration, and run maximum variation) to evaluate gait patterns and motor functionality of rats. Before the test, rats were trained to adapt to the environment and compliant runs at each designated time point (weeks 4 and 8). The Catwalk XT system's core component is an enclosed walkway with a glass plate, over which rats traverse from one side to the other. Green light, entering from the plate's long edge, is internally reflected, escaping only where the animal's paws touch the glass, causing light scattering. High-speed video cameras located beneath the walkway capture these paw prints reflected by green light signals, transforming them into digital images by detecting intensity differences in the light received, which allows for detailed analysis of walking motion patterns. Stride length, swing time, swing speed, mean intensity, max. contact mean intensity, and max. contact max intensity were then comprehensively analyzed. Stride length is the distance between consecutive steps of the same foot. Swing time is the time when the paw is off the ground. Swing speed is calculated as the ratio of stride length to swing time. Mean intensity represents average pressure across the entire paw at the moment of ground contact, whereas max. contact max. intensity and max. contact mean intensity are specifically measured within the maximal contact area of a paw.

Statistical Analysis. Data are presented as means ± standard deviations (S.D). Data from experiments were analyzed with Origin 2024 and GraphPad Prism 8. Homogeneity of variances was verified using the Brown-Forsythe test (Table S5, Supporting Information). Two-way ANOVA with Group and Time as fixed factors was then performed, followed by Tukey's HSD post-hoc test (Table S6, Supporting Information). Statistical significance was set at ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001.
