Association between frailty transitions and chronic lung disease incidence: findings from four national cohort studies
Authors: Shuyuan Li, Xiang Tang, Yong Ge, Deju Zhou, Hongjiang Liu, Teng Sun, Shoujie Feng, Cheng Zhang, Hao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe association between changes in frailty status and the prevalence of chronic lung diseases (CLD) is critical. This study aims to explore the relationship between variations in frailty status and incidence of CLD.MethodsThis study utilized data from four national prospective cohorts: China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA), Health and Retirement Study (HRS) and Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE). Changes in frailty status were assessed using the Rockwood Frailty Index. CLD was defined as self-reported physician-diagnosed chronic lung diseases, including chronic bronchitis, emphysema, cor pulmonale. Cox proportional hazards model was employed to examine the relationship between changes in frailty status and the incidence of CLD.ResultsThe analysis included 38,122 participants from four cohorts. Participants who progressed to prefrail or frail status had a significantly higher risk of developing CLD compared to those who remained robust (CHARLS, HR: 1.85, 95% CI: 1.47–2.32; HRS, HR: 1.84, 95% CI: 1.34–2.51; ELSA, HR: 1.58, 95% CI: 1.08–2.31; SHARE, HR: 1.70, 95% CI: 1.40–2.07). Conversely, individuals who transitioned to robust status demonstrated a lower risk of incident CLD compared to those who remained in prefrail or frail status (CHARLS, HR: 0.59, 95% CI: 0.48–0.73; HRS, HR: 0.71, 95% CI: 0.49–1.01; ELSA, HR: 0.57, 95% CI: 0.36–0.89; SHARE, HR: 0.71, 95% CI: 0.55–0.92).ConclusionsDifferent changes in frailty status are associated with varying risks of developing CLD. Progression of frailty status is associated with the increasing risk of CLD, whereas recovery from frailty is associated with the decreased risk of CLD. Introduction

With the aging global population, frailty, a common geriatric syndrome, is emerging as a significant health burden [1, 2]. This condition is marked by diminished reserves across multiple physiological systems, rendering individuals more vulnerable to stressors [3]. Robust evidence indicates that frailty not only heightens the likelihood of negative health effects but also serves as a reliable predictor for age-related issues, including falls, disability, and all-cause mortality [4, 5]. The rising prevalence of chronic lung diseases (including chronic bronchitis, cor pulmonale and emphysema) among aging populations has further amplified interest in frailty among pulmonologists [6–9]. Understanding the interplay between frailty and chronic lung diseases could provide valuable insights for the prevention and treatment of these conditions in older individuals.

Earlier research has shown that frailty and pre-frailty elevate the risk of developing chronic lung diseases [6, 10]. Unfortunately, these research primarily concentrated on baseline frailty status, neglecting potential changes in frailty over time [6, 10]. Tracking frailty status changes, rather than relying solely on baseline assessments, offers a more nuanced understanding of the biological associations [11]. Furthermore, accumulating evidence indicates that frailty is a modifiable condition, with appropriate interventions shown to reverse its effects [12, 13]. For instance, a study of 42,775 community-dwelling older adults revealed that, over an average follow-up of 3.9 years, 42.8% experienced changes in frailty status, with 13.7% showing improvement and 29.1% experiencing decline. Evaluating the risk of chronic lung diseases in participants who revert from frailty could give critical insights into the importance of incorporating frailty interventions into pulmonary care. Thus, there is an urgent need to explore the relationship between changes in frailty status and the risk of developing chronic lung diseases.

In our study, we leveraged data from four nationally representative prospective cohort: China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA), Health and Retirement Study (HRS) and Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE). We explored the relationship between changes in frailty status and the risk of developing chronic lung diseases. We hypothesized that frailty status and its changes are associated with an elevated risk of developing chronic lung diseases, whereas recovery from frailty is linked to a reduced risk.

Methods

Study selection

CHARLS, SHARE, HRS and ELSA are prospective, nationally representative cohort studies conducted in China, Europe,the UK and the United States, respectively [14–17]. In our study, wave 1 in CHARLS (2011), wave 8 in HRS (2006), wave 2 in ELSA (2004–2005) and wave 1 of SHARE(2004) were considered the baseline. 1 st follow-up survey was considered as Wave 2 in CHARLS (2013), wave 9 in HRS (2008), wave 3 in ELSA (2006–2007) and wave 2 in SHARE(2006). We examined data of both the baseline and 1 st follow-up surveys to estimate frailty status changes. Last surveys were used to track outcomes: Wave 5 in CHARLS (2020), wave 15 in HRS (2020), wave 10 in ELSA (2020–2021) and wave 8 of SHARE(2019). CHARLS obtained approval from the Ethics Review Committee of Peking University, HRS received approval from the University of Michigan, ELSA received approval from London Multi-Centre Research, SHARE received approval from Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from each participant.

Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria (see Fig. 1), 11,973 participants from CHARLS (average age 58.2 years), 7,313 participants from ELSA (average age 65.5 years), 12,105 participants from HRS (average age 66.3 years) and 18,577 participants from SHARE (average age 63.9 years) were included in the analysis of baseline frailty status. For the analysis of frailty status changes, 9,155 participants from CHARLS (average age 58.1 years), 6,017 participants from ELSA (average age 65.0 years), 9,452 participants from HRS (averageage 65.1 years) and 13,498 participants from SHARE (average age 62.8 years) were included based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria.Fig. 1Selection process of the study population. Assessment of frailty

Frailty was assessed using the Frailty Index (FI), determined by the buildup of multiple age-related health deficits [18–20]. In our study, We developed the FI using the standard procedure outlined earlier. Based on CHARLS data, we chosen 29 items for calculating FI, covering diseases, symptoms, disabilities, physical functions, depression, and cognition. For each participant, FI was calculated by dividing the sum of current health deficits by 29. Thus, FI is a continuous variable ranging from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater frailty. As shown in previous studies [1, 21], frailty status is classified into three categories: robust (FI ≤ 0.10), prefrail (0.10 < FI < 0.25), and frail (FI ≥ 0.25). Moreover, we used the total FI and its changes to evaluate changes in the frailty status. The total FI was calculated as the sum of FI from baseline and the second survey, FI change was calculated FI by subtracting the baseline from the second survey.

Covariates

The covariates considered included age, gender, marital status, education level, smoking and drinking status, physical activity level, and body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), grip strength, and health insurance. Marital status was divided into two categories: married or partnered, and other statuses (separated, divorced, single, or widowed). Education was categorized into three levels: less than high school, high school, and college or higher. Smoking status was classified as never smokers or ever smokers. Ever smokers included both individuals who currently smoke and those who have smoked in the past. Drinking status was similarly classified into never drinkers and ever drinkers. Physical activity levels were classified into three categories: vigorous (more than once per week of vigorous activity), moderate (more than once per week of moderate activity), and inactive (all other levels).

Outcome determination and follow-up

The primary outcome of this study was the incidence of new-onset chronic lung disease. Chronic lung disease was identified according to self-reported and doctor-diagnosed chronic lung diseases, such as chronic bronchitis, emphysema, cor pulmonale. For baseline frailty status analysis, follow-up started from Wave 1 in CHARLS (2011), wave 8 in HRS (2006), wave 2 in ELSA (2004–2005) and wave 1 in SHARE(2004). For the analysis of frailty status changes, follow-up began from Wave 2 in CHARLS (2013), wave 9 in HRS (2008), wave 3 in ELSA (2006–2007) and wave 2 in SHARE(2006). The follow-up endpoint was defined as the earliest occurrence of CLD, death, or the censoring date. The censoring date was set as the date of the participant's last survey. The final survey was Wave 5 in CHARLS (2020), wave 15 in HRS (2020), wave 10 in ELSA (2020–2021) and wave 8 in SHARE (2019). Participants with missing data for FI calculation at baseline, those with chronic lung disease at baseline, and those lost to follow-up were excluded. For the analysis of frailty status changes, further exclusions were made based on similar criteria.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables in descriptive statistics were summarized using either the mean with standard deviation (SD) or the median with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages). We used chi-square tests for categorical variables and ANOVA for continuous variables to assess differences in baseline characteristics across the four datasets. The Cox proportional hazards model was employed to examine the association between baseline frailty status and the incidence of chronic lung disease, providing hazard ratios (HR) along with their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Additionally, we also tested the proportional hazards assumption using Schoenfeld residuals. Using robust participants as the reference group, two cox proportional hazards models were fitted. Model 1 was unadjusted, while Model 2 accounted for multiple factors, including age, gender, marital status, education level, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity level, BMI, and systolic blood pressure (SBP). Missing data for covariates were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations. Using a similar approach, we also analyzed the association of frailty status changes, total FI, and FI changes with the incidence of CLD. The total FI and changes in FI were categorized into tertiles, with the lowest tertile used as the reference group. Multivariable adjustments were also applied to models analyzing FI changes [22]. Additionally, trend tests were performed for total FI and FI changes. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (Version 4.1.2). Two-sided P-values were used, with a threshold of P < 0.05 indicating statistical significance. Given the controversy surrounding the frailty index (FI) cut-off values for defining frailty status, two additional commonly used cut-off values were applied for sensitivity analyses [23]. Using cut-off value 1, frailty status was categorized as follows: frail (FI > 0.21), pre-frail (0.10 < FI ≤ 0.21), and robust (FI ≤ 0.10). For cut-off value 2, the classifications were: frail (FI ≥ 0.25), pre-frail (0.08 < FI < 0.25), and robust (FI ≤ 0.08).

Role of funding source

The funder of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed data from four national prospective cohorts to evaluate the relationship between frailty status and the incidence of CLD. Our findings indicate that prefrail and frail participants faced a higher risk of developing new-onset CLD compared to robust individuals. Furthermore, robust individuals who transitioned to a prefrail or frail state exhibited an increased risk of CLD compared to those who maintained their robust status. In contrast, participants who recovered to a robust state demonstrated a lower risk of new-onset CLD than those who remained in frail or prefrail states. Additionally, both a higher total FI and an increase in FI over time were linked to an increased risk of CLD events.

With the rapid expansion of the global older people population, frailty has become an increasingly prevalent condition. Previous research has highlighted a strong association between frailty and the development of chronic lung disease. For instance, Jingge Qu et al., using data from individuals of European ancestry (N: 175,226) in the UK Biobank and TwinGene, employed Mendelian randomization to identify a bidirectional causal relationship between frailty and chronic lung disease [6]. Similarly, Alessandra Marengoni et al. conducted a meta-analysis and confirmed the link between frailty and chronic lung disease [24]. Consistent with these findings, our study observed that prefrail and frail participants faced a higher risk of developing chronic lung disease compared to their robust counterparts.

Basic research indicates that common physiological pathways, including inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and coagulation abnormalities—may underlie the biological link between frailty and CLD [25, 26]. Notably, growing evidence indicates that frailty status is modifiable [27]. Previous research suggests that physical function may be a crucial intervention target for reversing frailty [11]. These trends could also be influenced by various factors, including individual differences in health conditions, lifestyle interventions, and the effectiveness of specific treatment approaches. Further studies, both from real-world data and clinical trials, are necessary to identify the most effective interventions for reversing frailty and to evaluate their efficacy and safety. Compared to a single baseline assessment, examining changes in frailty status provides a more comprehensive understanding of these biological associations.

As far as we are aware, the study is the first to research the link between frailty status changes and the risk of CLD. Our study corroborated the dynamic frailty status and further demonstrated its clinical implications. Previous research has shown that frailty is an important risk factor for the onset of chronic lung disease [10]. Our study results, from a dynamic temporal perspective, confirm this finding. Our findings carry substantial implications. First, incorporating frailty assessment into clinical diagnosis and management of pulmonary diseases could enhance patient care, especially in older populations, is essential. Individuals identified as frail or prefrail should be prioritized as key targets for CLD prevention strategies. This research encompasses four prospective cohorts representing various ethnic groups, each characterized by robust study designs and substantial sample sizes. The consistency of our results across all four cohorts suggests that our findings are broadly applicable. Additionally, healthy individuals should undergo frailty risk factor assessments to facilitate early identification of high-risk individuals and enable timely preventive interventions to slow frailty progression. Importantly, as frailty is a reversible condition, our findings highlight the potential to reduce CLD risk through reversing frailty status. This underscores the need for substantial efforts to develop and implement effective interventions aimed at reversing frailty, thereby mitigating its impact on CLD risk.

Beyond categorical assessments of frailty status, we also examined △FI and total FI as a continuous measure. We chose to use the continuous measure because it offers a new perspective on the relationship between frailty and the incidence of CLD, viewed through the lens of a continuous variable. By summing the individual FI items, we were able to treat frailty as a continuous spectrum, rather than simply categorizing it into discrete stages. This approach allows for a more nuanced understanding of how incremental changes in frailty may influence the risk of developing CLD.A higher total FI and increases in FI were both associated with a greater risk of developing CLD, further reinforcing the importance of monitoring and modifying frailty status to mitigate CLD risk. The results from both approaches were consistent in terms of the direction of association with CLD, but the total FI provided a more comprehensive understanding of the long-term frailty burden, while the △FI emphasized the role of transitions in frailty status.

This study has several limitations. First, due to limitations in the database, similar to previous research, the identification of CLD was based on self-reported physician diagnoses, which may have led to misclassification bias. Additionally, due to data coordination issues, we were unable to perform a combined analysis of the data from the four cohorts. Second, despite adjusting for multiple confounding factors, residual confounding—such as genetic predispositions, the rate of deficit accumulation and dietary influences—cannot be entirely ruled out. Also, our analysis utilizes four datasets derived from countries with large populations and represent a significant portion of the global data. However these countries have high economic development and strong social welfare systems, we acknowledge that this could limit the generalizability of our findings to countries with lower economic levels or different social structures. To address this limitation, we plan to incorporate datasets from countries with lower economic statuses in future studies. Further more, while we have utilized cohort study designs to explore causal relationships to the best of our ability, we fully recognize that observational studies inherently have limitations when it comes to establishing causality and it is also challenging to fully address the distortions caused by COVID-19. To strengthen our causal inferences, we intend to conduct future prospective cohort studies. Finally, in our study, participants lost to follow-up were excluded from the analysis. While this approach helps ensure that only participants with complete data are included, we acknowledge that this may introduce selection bias. Future studies could adopt alternative strategies, such as multiple imputation techniques, to handle missing data and thereby reduce the impact of attrition bias.

Conclusion

Varied trajectories in the changes of frailty status are linked to different risks of developing CLD. Frailty development is linked to an increased risk of CLD, while recovery from frail status is linked to a lower risk. The findings suggest that changes in frailty status can serve as important indicators for predicting the risk of developing CLD, with frailty progression increasing risk and recovery from frailty reducing it.
