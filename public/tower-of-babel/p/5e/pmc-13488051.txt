Sirolimus outperforms alpelisib in models of NRASQ61R-driven vascular overgrowth
Authors: Svatava Merkle, Sara Alharbi, Patricia Pastura, C. Griffin McDaniel, Timothy D. Le Cras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Treatment options for KLA remain limited, underscoring the need for new systemic therapies.•Sirolimus outperformed alpelisib in suppressing abnormal vascular overgrowth in NRASQ61R-driven KLA models.

Introduction

The identification of germ line and somatic pathogenic mutations in patients with vascular anomalies (VA) creates opportunities for targeted medical therapies.1,2 Phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)–mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) and the MAPK pathways are the most commonly hyperactive signaling pathways in VA because of these mutations and are also dysregulated in cancers.3,4 Consequently, drugs used to treat cancers driven by these dysregulated pathways represent potential therapies for patients with VA.

Kaposiform lymphangiomatosis (KLA) is a rare complex lymphatic anomaly with significant mortality and morbidity and limited treatment options.5,6 Pathology of lesions from patients with KLA is characterized by multifocal areas of spindled endothelial cells (EC).6 A somatic mutation in the neuroblastoma rat sarcoma virus Q61R (NRASQ61R) has been identified in patients with KLA.7 Experimental models indicate that this NRASQ61R can drive vascular overgrowth.8, 9, 10

Sirolimus was the first targeted treatment for patients with complex VA, including KLA.2,11 However, sirolimus can have side effects, including immunosuppression,12 and some patients only show partial responses and progressive disease.11 Alpelisib, a selective inhibitor of the p110α catalytic subunit of phosphoinositide 3-kinase α (PIK3CA), is the only US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved targeted therapy for complex VA, specifically for phosphoinositide 3-kinase α (PIK3CA)–related overgrowth spectrum (PROS), with recent studies supporting possible application of this drug to other VA.13 PI3K is upstream of mTOR, the target for sirolimus, and so whether targeting this pathway upstream of mTOR would be more effective is unclear. Human EC engineered to express NRASQ61R show roles for both the PI3K-mTOR and MAPK signaling pathways.8, 9, 10 The goal of this study was to determine whether targeting PI3K with alpelisib would be more effective than blocking mTOR with sirolimus in preclinical models using NRASQ61R EC.

Study design

Information on reagents and kits is provided in supplemental Tables 1 and 2.

Cell culture studies

Human NRASQ61R endothelial progenitor cells (EPC) were generated from cord blood–derived EC transduced with the doxycycline (Dox)-inducible pCW-NRASQ61R vector.8,9 Cells were plated at 100 000 cells per gelatin-coated 6-well plate.10 Dox (2 μg/mL) was added 48 hours before all treatment experiments. Cells were treated with vehicle, no Dox, Dox, or Dox plus sirolimus (14.6pM-3750pM) or alpelisib (1.25μM-5μM). Drug doses were based on dose-response studies (supplemental Figures 2-4) to identify the lowest effective dose. Lysates were collected after 24 hours of treatment for western blot analysis.9

Western blot analysis

Western blots of EPC protein lysates were generated,9 and primary antibodies that detect NRAS p.Q61R, serine 473 phospho-Ak strain transforming protein kinase B (AKT), AKT, phospho-ribosomal protein S6 kinase (S6K), S6K, phospho-extracellular signal-regulated kinase, extracellular signal-regulated kinase, and actin were used. Secondary antibodies, washing, and imaging steps were described previously.9

Cell proliferation and migration assays

Treatment groups included the following: (1) no Dox with vehicle, (2) Dox with vehicle, (3) Dox with sirolimus, and (4) Dox with alpelisib. For proliferation studies, NRASQ61R EPC were plated on a gelatin-coated 96-well plate (2500 cells per well; 14 replicates) in endothelial basal media containing 20% fetal bovine serum, puromycin (0.5 μg/mL), and Hoechst dye (2 drops in 40 mL). Cells were imaged every 2 hours after ≥4 hours to allow for attachment using the Lionheart FX Live Cell Imaging System (Agilent).10 For migration studies, NRASQ61R EPCs were seeded into Ibidi inserts (6000 cells per well; n = 6 per treatment) in gelatin-coated 24-well plates and cultured in endothelial cell growth medium-2 microvascular supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 0.5 μg/mL puromycin for 48 hours. Addition of hydroxyurea, insert removal, imaging, and analysis of gap closure were previously described.10 Gap closure area (%) was calculated as [(initial − time point area) / initial] × 100.

Mouse xenograft model

Animal studies were approved by the Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. We used a previously established xenograft model of NRASQ61R-driven vascular overgrowth.8,10 NRASQ61R EPC were cultured with or without Dox for 48 hours, then resuspended at a concentration of 12.5 × 103 cells/μL (2.5 × 106 cells in 200 μL) Matrigel (Corning) and injected subcutaneously into both flanks of male athymic NU/J mice aged 5 to 6 weeks (The Jackson Laboratory) on Dox or standard diet. From days 4 to 11 after injection, mice received daily intraperitoneal (IP) injections of sirolimus (2 mg/kg), alpelisib (25 mg/kg), or vehicle (dimethyl sulfoxide 10%, polyethylene glycol with an average molecular weight of 300 g/mol 40%, Tween 80 5%, saline 45%) for 8 days (Figure 1A). Histology and immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded xenograft plugs were sectioned (5 μm) and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Immunohistochemistry staining using Ulex europaeus agglutinin I identified human EC, and NRASQ61R staining identified mutant cells.8,19 Whole-plug tile hematoxylin and eosin tile scans were acquired with a Nikon NiE Widefield upright microscope, and the vascular area was quantified using ImageJ software. The percentage of vascular area was calculated relative to the total plug area. Representative images were obtained using a Lionheart Cell Imaging System (Agilent).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. For multiple group comparisons, 1-way analysis of variance followed by a Tukey multiple comparisons test was used. For data involving repeated measures and correlated observations, 2-way analysis of variance was performed, followed by a Dunnett T3 multiple comparisons test. Data were also adjusted for nonsphericity. Statistical analyses and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 10.6.1, and P < .05 was considered significant.

Results and discussion

Sirolimus is a potent inhibitor of S6K phosphorylation, proliferation, and migration of NRASQ61R EPC

Alpelisib potently inhibited p-AKT; however, it suppressed the proliferation and migration of NRASQ61R EPC less effectively than sirolimus

Alpelisib is a PIK3CA inhibitor, with AKT being the direct readout kinase of PI3K. We used p-AKT as a readout of its activity. Treatment of EPC expressing NRASQ61R with alpelisib (1.25 μM-5 μM) blocked p-AKT (serine 473; Figure 2A; supplemental Figure 1). Alpelisib at 10μM reduced proliferation by 55% at 72 hours (Figure 2B-C), and at 5 μM reduced gap closure in the migration assay by 17% at 8 hours (Figure 2D-E).

In summary, alpelisib and sirolimus suppressed phosphorylation of AKT and S6K, respectively, as well as proliferation and migration of Dox-induced NRASQ61R EPC in vitro; however, sirolimus achieved these effects at substantially lower doses.

Sirolimus inhibited the growth of NRASQ61R vascular xenografts at a lower dose compared with alpelisib

Upon Dox induction, the NRASQ61R-expressing human EC formed red blood cell–filled vascular structures (Figure 3A; supplemental Figures 6 and 7), whereas noninduced cells did not. Some luminal blood was lost during processing. Xenograft plug weights and vascular area (%) were 93% and 57% higher, respectively, in Dox-treated mice compared with mice on a standard diet (Figures 1C and 3B). Xenograft plug weights and % vascular areas were significantly reduced after 8 days of sirolimus (2 mg/kg, IP), by 52% and 38%, respectively, compared with reductions of 28% and 19% following alpelisib (25 mg/kg, IP; Figures 1C and 3B). Total vascular area was significantly reduced by sirolimus (74%) compared with alpelisib (40%; supplemental Figure 5). EC in Dox-treated mice expressed NRASQ61R on immunohistochemistry, confirming NRASQ61R induction (supplemental Figure 6). In summary, both alpelisib and sirolimus reduced xenograft plug weights and vascular area in an in vivo model of Dox-induced NRASQ61R EPC; however, sirolimus demonstrated greater efficacy at a substantially lower dose.

Conclusions

We compared 2 PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway inhibitors in human NRASQ61R-expressing EC and found that sirolimus more effectively suppressed cell signaling, proliferation, and migration in vitro at lower doses than alpelisib. Consistently, sirolimus more effectively reduced vascular overgrowth in NRASQ61R xenografts in vivo.

Alpelisib is the only FDA-approved medical therapy for PROS, a complex VA and overgrowth caused by PIK3CA mutations. It has been proposed for repurposing in other VA with PI3K-mTOR pathway activation.13 Studies using PI3K inhibition in RAS-mutant models of VA have yielded conflicting results. PI3K inhibition failed to rescue vascular defects in Kirsten rat sarcoma virus gene (KRAS)G12V zebra fish embryos,22 improved Harvey rat sarcoma virus gene HRASV12-driven defects in vitro, yet worsened brain vascular malformations in the HrasV12 mouse model.23

Limitations of this study should be noted. Findings may not be generalizable to other KLA-associated mutations. Experiments were limited to EPC rather than mature EC subtypes. It remains unclear whether KLA lesions arise from dysregulated lymphatic EC or from NRASQ61R-mutant progenitors that later differentiate into lymphatic EC. Plasma drug levels were not measured; doses were based on previous preclinical studies14,15 and human-equivalent scaling.17

Overall, this study demonstrates that sirolimus is more effective than alpelisib in blocking key pathologic features in preclinical models driven by NRASQ61R EC. These findings suggest that sirolimus may remain the preferred option for patients with NRAS-mutant KLA; however, comparative clinical studies are needed to evaluate the relative efficacy of these drugs.
