Exploring Lifestyle Correlates of Mental Health Among Low-Income Filipino Men in Rural Areas
Authors: Andrew Thomas Reyes, Reimund Serafica, Marysol Cacciata, Erwin William A Leyva, Jennifer Kawi, Lorraine S Evangelista
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe 2017 Mental Health Act in the Philippines sought to enhance integrated care; nevertheless, rural regions continue to lack sufficient resources. This study aimed to examine the mental health of low-income Filipino men aged 18 years and over residing in rural areas of the Philippines and explore the relationship between their mental health status and lifestyle and self-care behaviors. A convenience sample of 426 men completed a cross-sectional survey capturing sociodemographics, depression and anxiety, psychological distress, health-related quality of life, and a range of lifestyle behaviors. Analyses included descriptive statistics, Pearson’s correlations, t-tests, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Among 426 rural Filipino men (mean age = 49.3 ± 17.2 years), moderate psychological distress was observed, with 8.2% meeting criteria for depression and 14.8% for anxiety. Distress correlated positively with both depression (r = .228) and anxiety (r = .332) and inversely with physical (r = −.239) and mental quality of life (r = −.298; all p < .01). Regular exercise and seeing a doctor were linked to lower stress levels, less symptoms of depression, and higher standards of life. On the contrary, using herbs to treat ailments has been linked to more pain and anxiety (all p < .01). The results highlight the importance of integrating community-based mental health promotion with culturally accepted self-care practices. This study provides a foundational framework for increasing mental health care access among Filipino men in rural areas of the Philippines and developing culturally appropriate programs for improving their mental health through culturally accepted self-care practices.Keywords: mental health, psychological distress, depression, anxiety, lifestyle behaviors

Introduction

Mental illnesses are a significant cause of ailment on a global scale, with nearly 80% of those affected residing in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs; Fan et al., 2025; Mudiyanselage et al., 2024). Despite the prevalence of this issue, mental health services in these regions continue to receive inadequate funding, with less than 2% of the national health budget allocated to them (Giebel et al., 2024). In rural areas, the early detection and treatment of common mental health issues are particularly challenging due to the inadequate infrastructure, a scarcity of qualified professionals, and the fractured nature of treatment routes (Matthews et al., 2025).

There may be significant variations in access to mental health services in LMICs between urban and rural areas. In cities, mental health services are found to be more numerous (hospitals, psychiatric clinics, and professionally trained health workers), although they are still limited compared with those in high-income countries (HICs; World Health Organization [WHO], 2018). Conversely, mental health in rural communities is often severely limited with a shortage of specialists, less transportation to health care facilities for care, and fewer community-based mental health programs (Edwards et al., 2023). In addition, such institutional obstacles may in part account for some of the delayed diagnosis and untreated mental health problems among rural populations (Edwards et al., 2023). Furthermore, help-seeking styles in rural contexts are possibly not the same as those of urban areas because of the relative preference that is placed toward informal support systems (i.e., family systems, community leaders, or traditional healers), rather than formal mental health services (Reyes et al., 2023). Cultural beliefs and stigmatization of mental illness may additionally act to prevent individuals from reaching out for professional treatment. Consequently, understanding mental health in rural LMIC settings should focus not only on the availability of services but also on the cultural patterns of coping and more locally available forms of support.

The mental health of men in LMICs has not been extensively studied. Gender norms discouraging men from expressing emotions or seeking assistance reduce the likelihood of discussing their symptoms (Landa-Blanco & Mejía Sánchez, 2025). These norms may be further reinforced in rural, low-density communities, where close-knit social structures and increased social visibility can intensify expectations of emotional restraint and self-reliance, while also amplifying stigma associated with mental health concerns. In contrast, urban environments may provide greater anonymity and exposure to diverse perspectives, which can influence help-seeking behaviors differently (Rathod et al., 2017; Seidler et al., 2016). This complicates the assessment of the prevalence of depression, anxiety, and psychological distress in males. Psychological distress in these populations is often shaped by a combination of socioeconomic and cultural factors, including financial strain, employment instability, limited access to health care, and societal expectations of masculinity that emphasize emotional restraint and self-reliance (Lund et al., 2010; Rathod et al., 2017; Seidler et al., 2016). Without specialized studies, understanding risk factors, coping techniques, and culturally relevant interventions specific to men is challenging (Sunal & Berkout, 2025).

Mental health has not been a high priority on the national health agenda in the Philippines in the past (Tan & Maravilla, 2025). The 2017 Mental Health Act was intended to enhance access to integrated treatments and mitigate stigma; however, rural regions continue to encounter challenges in obtaining the necessary resources (Sales et al., 2025). Similarly, many LMICs allocate about 0.5%–5% of their national health budget to mental health care services, which are significantly disproportionate to the countries’ average burden of mental illness (Freeman, 2022). More specifically, in the Philippines, only about 3% of the Department of Health’s budget has been allocated to mental health (Arevalo et al., 2022). Maglalang et al. (2025) concur that limited research on the mental health of Filipinos contributes to the underestimation of mental health needs in the Philippines, which results in further obscuring the burden of mental illness and consequently deprioritizing increased allocations for mental health services. In addition, other factors affecting the low priority of enhancing mental health care in LMICs include cultural attitudes associating mental illness with personal frailty and stigma toward mental illness, resulting in a high proportion of males in under-resourced areas being reluctant to discuss their symptoms and seek appropriate and timely care (Martinez et al., 2020).

Compared to HICs, LMICs face vastly higher structural barriers to the financing and provision of mental health care services. In addition, HICs spend more on mental health and have more comprehensive mental health systems (including integrated community-based services, wider insurance coverage, and access to mental health specialists) than LMICs (WHO, 2018). On the contrary, although they share a large part of the burden of the global mental health problem, many LMICs invest less than 2% of their overall health expenditure on mental health (Freeman, 2022; Giebel et al., 2024). This imbalance results in significant treatment gaps, and it is estimated that a substantial proportion of clients with a mental disorder in LMICs do not have access to formal mental health programs. Furthermore, inadequate financial investment means that workforce development, community programming, and early intervention efforts are restricted, especially in rural areas (Docrat et al., 2019). These findings also highlight the need to consider mental health determinants for assessment in an LMIC setting, where preventive strategies and culturally relevant approaches are likely to be significantly beneficial to the improvement of psychological health by promoting mental well-being across underserved populations in low-formal-resource settings.

Most such studies on lifestyle patterns are based on HICs, where mental health research has frequently researched the impact of lifestyle and self-care techniques on psychological well-being and the risk of mental health disorders; examples of these are studies on regular physical activity, balanced nutrition, social support, and complementary therapies (Yusuf et al., 2024). Yet these relationships as a whole were not systematically studied among low-income Filipino men living in rural areas. Here, there is the co-occurrence of biomedical and traditional health systems as well as their impact on individuals’ mental health symptom understandings and care-seeking in these settings (Martinez et al., 2020).

To address these gaps, the present study examined the mental health status and its associations with lifestyle and self-care behaviors among low-income Filipino men aged 18 years and older residing in rural communities, also known as barangays (i.e., the most local government/administrative units in the Philippines). Specifically, we sought to describe levels of depression, anxiety, psychological distress, and health-related quality of life; assess the prevalence of key lifestyle practices; and identify the behaviors most strongly linked to better or worse psychological outcomes. Findings are intended to inform the development of culturally congruent, community-based interventions that align with local health beliefs, incorporate existing self-care practices, and leverage community resources such as barangay health systems to strengthen preventive and promotive mental health efforts in low-resource settings in the Philippines (Giebel et al., 2024; Martinez et al., 2020; Reyes et al., 2023).

Method

This cross-sectional study is a component of the Improving Health Equity through Lifestyle Promotion among Filipinos (I-HELP-FILIPINOS) cohort, a larger initiative examining several determinants influencing chronic disease and mental health in disadvantaged Filipino populations (Cacciata et al., 2021; Reyes et al., 2023). The design, sampling strategy, and ethical approvals for the parent study are comprehensively discussed in another publication (Kawi et al., 2024; Serafica et al., 2025). This more focused research aimed to gain a deeper understanding of the emotional well-being and self-care practices of low-income Filipino males living in rural areas of the Philippines.

Community health centers in rural areas of three provinces in Central Luzon and the Visayas collaborated to collect data. In addition, community health workers and barangay leaders collaborated to identify and enroll eligible individuals. Participation was limited to those who were at least 18 years of age, identified as male, resided in a rural barangay, and could provide informed consent. The study comprised 426 men.

The study was conducted in designated private areas of the barangay health clinics (Kawi et al., 2024). This measure was implemented to safeguard the confidentiality of the participants and mitigate response bias. Participants completed a structured questionnaire in either English or Filipino, contingent upon their language proficiency and reading ability (Reyes et al., 2023). Multilingual research assistants were ready to provide assistance and address inquiries as required. The survey required approximately 30–45 min to complete. The survey inquired about various items, including mental health indicators, sociodemographic characteristics, quality of life related to health, and lifestyle behaviors. Age, marital status, degree of education, and yearly household income were all sociodemographic factors (Humer et al., 2025). The selection of measurement instruments was guided by their established psychometric properties, prior use in LMIC settings, and relevance to assessing mental health and well-being in culturally diverse populations (Mughal et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2021). These instruments were considered appropriate for capturing the mental health status and quality of life of rural Filipino males. We employed three tools to assess mental health. The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) has 14 questions, and there are two independent subscales for anxiety and depression. It was used to identify symptoms from the previous week. The HADS has been tested on Filipinos and proved to have good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha was 0.83 for anxiety and 0.79 for depression) and construct validity (De Guzman, 2013).

We utilized the Distress Thermometer, a single-item visual analog scale that ranges from 0 (no distress) to 10 (severe suffering), to measure psychological pain. This instrument was selected due to its brevity and feasibility for use in community-based settings, particularly where participant burden and varying literacy levels may be a concern (Ma et al., 2014). This visual analog scale demonstrated strong convergent validity, indicating superior performance to screening tools for identifying emotional problems (Ascensio Huertas et al., 2024). In this study, we asked participants to rate their level of psychological pain on a scale from “not at all” to “very stressful.” A single-item question was used for this purpose (Ascencio Huertas et al., 2024).

The Short Form-12 Health Survey (SF-12) was used to assess how people’s health impacts their quality of life. It delivers overall scores for both the physical and mental parts. The SF-12 was selected due to its brevity, reliability, and prior use in diverse populations, making it suitable for large-scale community surveys in LMIC contexts (Ohrnberger et al., 2020). People from several Asian populations, including Filipinos, have completed the SF-12 in many previous LMIC research studies (Flores et al., 2018; Mangohig et al., 2026). It is reliable (Cronbach’s alpha >.80) and sensitive to changes in health status (Liao & Gan, 2025; Turner-Bowker & Hogue, 2014).

We used yes/no questions from prior community health surveys to assess lifestyle and self-care practices. Participants were asked whether they exercised frequently, saw a doctor regularly, used herbal treatments when they were sick, prayed when they were ill, visited community health services, ate regular meals, and spent time with family or friends. These behavior queries were selected because they are prevalent in rural Filipino communities and hold significant cultural importance. The selection of these behaviors was based on their cultural significance and their prevalence in rural Filipino communities.

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27 (IBM, 2020). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize sample characteristics, mental health indicators, and lifestyle behaviors. We employed the Pearson correlation coefficients to examine the interrelations among psychological distress, quality of life, and self-care practices. We employed independent t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to explore the disparities in mental health outcomes among various groups. All studies used a p-value of less than 0.05 to demonstrate the statistical significance of the results.

This study was approved by the University of California, Irvine Institutional Review Board and the University of the Philippines Ethics Review Board. Before conducting any interviews, each participant was informed of the purpose of the study, and we ensured that all participants provided written informed consent.

Results

The analytic sample consisted of 426 low-income Filipino men recruited from rural barangay health centers. Participants ranged widely in age, with nearly two-thirds married or cohabiting and about half having an education beyond high school. Although less than half reported annual incomes above the Philippine peso (PHP) 75,000 threshold, the cohort represented a cross-section of rural men who routinely access both biomedical and traditional health services (Table 1).

Mental health assessments indicated that the majority of individuals experienced moderate distress; however, only a limited number fulfilled the clinical criteria for depression or anxiety (Table 1). The scores for physical and mental quality of life predominantly fell within the moderate range, suggesting that this group did not encounter significant issues or consistently elevated levels of well-being (Table 1).

The findings showed varying percentages of self-care and lifestyle habits. Consistent exercise and regular dietary habits were the most frequently cited practices. Fewer than half of the men used herbal remedies, prayer, or visited a local health center when they were ill. Approximately 54.7% of the participants maintained regular visits with their physicians, and about 40.8% used socializing with their families and friends as a self-care/coping behavior (Table 1).

The bivariate correlations in Table 2, although entirely significant, indicate varying patterns of association. For example, psychological distress was positively related to depression and anxiety, while negatively associated with perceptions of physical and mental quality of life. Utilizing routine medical services exhibited a modest yet significant protective effect, correlated with reduced depressive symptoms and improved physical health. Consistent exercise demonstrated the most significant health benefits, as it was associated with reduced perceived stress and improved mental quality of life. Conversely, dependence on herbal self-medication was associated with increased distress and anxiety, highlighting the complexity of traditional practices in this context. In these bivariate analyses, additional behaviors such as praying, visiting health centers, and socializing with friends and family exhibited negligible or no relationships with mental health indicators.

Discussion

The present study examined the mental health, depression, anxiety, quality of life, and lifestyle behaviors of 426 low-income Filipino men residing in rural barangays. Participants reported moderate distress; however, only a limited proportion met the clinical criteria for depression or anxiety. Although participants reported moderate distress, very few met the clinical criteria for depression or anxiety, which further indicated that most participants had subclinical symptoms of depression and anxiety. In addition, our findings from the SF-12 demonstrated mild impairment in physical and mental health functioning. These findings are consistent with studies showing that subthreshold symptoms of depression and anxiety are significantly associated with low quality of life (Domínguez-Martínez et al., 2015; Tang & Thomas, 2020).

In addition, our findings resonate with those of Giebel et al.’s (2024) study on the impact of emotional stoicism as a significant barrier to accessing mental health care in LMICs. Giebel et al. (2024) explained that even with the presence of well-designed programs, the lack of culturally accessible language to disclose and express mental health problems to health professionals and the pervasive cultural stigma against mental disorders remain significant barriers to mental health care uptake. Furthermore, gender norms that prioritize independence reduce the likelihood of males seeking help, thereby obscuring the complete impact of depression and anxiety (Martinez et al., 2020).

Mental illness is often perceived as a personal failure in the Philippines (Aldalaeen et al., 2025; Reyes et al., 2020), with less than 3% of the national health budget allocated to mental health needs (Arevalo et al., 2022). Rural areas face significant challenges in accessing health care due to a shortage of skilled professionals and inadequate infrastructure (Dondonayos et al., 2023). This study addresses a substantial gap in epidemiological research by determining the prevalence and correlates of psychological and emotional distress among Filipino men, a demographic that has received limited attention (Serafica et al., 2025).

Regular exercise and medical consultations are associated with reduced stress levels and improved quality of life (Soong et al., 2025). In addition, results of the study align with findings from rural India and Indonesia, where physical activity and biomedical interactions serve to protect individuals (Singh et al., 2023). However, our findings show that using herbs for self-treatment was associated with increased levels of depression and anxiety. In contrast, herbal remedies were associated with more severe depression and anxiety in this sample. But this finding needs to be understood within a wider cultural framework. In the context of many LMIC settings (e.g., the Philippines), herbal remedies frequently reflect engagement with traditional or indigenous healing systems that include not only plant-based forms of treatment but also spiritual, community-based, and cultural meanings of illness (Martinez et al., 2020; Najibi et al., 2025). Such approaches can function as accessible and culturally relevant modalities of care, even in environments where formal forms of mental health care are scarce (Giebel et al., 2024; Reyes et al., 2023) and can function as part of community-centered health systems in LMICs. Such an association may indicate delayed access to biomedical care, that a greater symptom burden leads to self-treatment, or structural barriers to formal services, rather than the suboptimal nature of traditional practices themselves. Previous literature indicates that integrative models with both biomedical and traditional healing practices show to be more responsive to the needs of the community and a better predictor of engagement with mental health care (Kenda et al., 2022; McIntyre et al., 2015). More research will be necessary to better understand how these practices can be integrated into culturally congruent mental health interventions.

The results have significant implications for public health programming and clinical practice. Incorporating mental health screenings and brief psychosocial interventions into the operations of barangay health centers may facilitate the early identification and treatment of mental health issues (Devassy et al., 2025). Community exercise groups, nutrition counseling, and reminders for regular check-ups can employ established methods to enhance individuals’ mental well-being (Villarreal & Peterson, 2025). In addition, community-based health education programs that combine the use of herbal and complementary medicine with Western biomedical care may help reduce stigma and resistance and improve engagement with mental health care, especially among Filipino male population groups who have strong cultural attitudes and stigmatized values against mental health care (Kenda et al., 2022). Such integrated approaches may include the concurrent use of plant-based remedies, consultation with traditional or spiritual healers, and engagement with formal health care providers for screening, counseling, and pharmacologic treatment, as well as involvement of family and community members in care decisions and the use of community-based or non-specialist providers to support treatment delivery (Rathod et al., 2017). These strategies reflect pluralistic health-seeking behaviors and may enhance the acceptability and accessibility of mental health services in rural communities.

The population of focus in this study, Filipino men living in the Philippines, is disproportionately underrepresented in research on LMICs. This current descriptive study, which explored the mental health status of Filipino men, marks the beginning of exploring how this particular population group can help amplify how cultural norms, such as stigma toward mental health disorders, emotional stoicism, and masculinity, interact with mental health care access, especially in rural areas where resources are already limited. Therefore, we have used research methods that have been validated in Filipino settings (including establishing trust with cultural gatekeepers) to not only ensure cultural relevance and credibility in our findings but also establish a foundational partnership for future research undertaking (Aldalaeen et al., 2025).

Limitations of the Study

Our study has several limitations. First, we used a cross-sectional design, which restricts causal inferences. Second, we employed convenience sampling, which limits the generalizability of our findings. In addition, our surveys were self-report measures, which may have increased the likelihood of social desirability bias. Finally, systemic factors such as school dropout rates, migration, access to mental health services, involvement with the justice system, community infrastructure, substance use, and political and economic instability were not collected in the study. Including these factors in our study could have provided us with broader insights into the mental health of Filipino men.

In light of these limitations, future studies should employ longitudinal designs to monitor the mental health of rural Filipino males over time and to evaluate culturally appropriate therapies that integrate biomedical and traditional approaches (Najibi et al., 2025). Future research should employ both qualitative and quantitative methodologies to readily identify the cultural nuances of mental health-seeking behaviors and ultimately help generate culturally sensitive interventions (Badu et al., 2019).

Conclusions

This study examined the mental health status and its correlates among low-income Filipino males residing in rural barangays of the Philippines, a disproportionately underrepresented population group in epidemiological research among LMICs. Our main findings show that Filipino males in rural areas exhibited moderate levels of psychological distress but did not meet the clinical criteria for depression or anxiety. Results also showed that subclinical symptoms appeared to diminish both physical and mental quality of life. Individuals who engaged in regular exercise and consulted their physician regularly experienced less distress and showed improved health. Conversely, individuals who depended on herbal self-medication experienced greater depression and anxiety. These trends resemble those observed in other rural regions characterized by low to intermediate income levels. These findings highlight the complexity of the interplay between cultural practices, gender norms, and mental health. This study establishes a foundation for culturally relevant, community-oriented interventions that leverage men’s current practices and the existing health systems within their communities. Barangay health centers have the potential to enhance community engagement and bridge the gap between biomedical and indigenous health care practices by providing mental health screenings and short-term psychosocial support, promoting structured fitness and nutrition programs, and fostering respectful discussions about traditional treatments. Future research should focus on longitudinal and interventional designs to provide preliminary evidence on strategies that improve mental health treatment in the communities that are the most neglected in the Philippines. Finally, in conducting research among LMICs where mental health stigma contributes to the lack of mental health resources, culturally relevant research methods must be employed to establish trust with community gatekeepers, build on local assets, and collect culturally pertinent and credible data.
