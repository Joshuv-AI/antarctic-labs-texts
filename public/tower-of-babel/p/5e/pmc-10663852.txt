Transcriptome profile analysis revealed the potential mechanism of LIPUS treatment for Adriamycin-induced chronic kidney disease rat
Authors: Zhiqiang Ouyang, Guodong Zhang, Weipeng Wang, Lishi Shao, Xiaolan Du, Guocheng Li, Na Tan, Xinyan Zhou, Jun Yang, Lin Huang, Chengde Liao
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDeveloping effective therapeutic strategies to delay the progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD) remains a significant challenge. Low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) has demonstrated potential for treating CKD, but the underlying molecular mechanisms are still elusive. This study aimed to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of LIPUS and to elucidate the involved genes and signaling pathways.MethodsThe CKD model was established in rats using Adriamycin (ADR). The bilateral kidneys of CKD rats were continuously stimulated with LIPUS for a period of four weeks. The therapeutic efficacy was defined by renal function and histopathological evaluation. RNA sequencing was employed to profile the transcriptome of rat kidneys in each group. Cluster analysis was utilized to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs), followed by enrichment analysis of their associated pathways using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases.ResultsLIPUS treatment improved ADR-induced renal dysfunction in the CKD group. Renal fibrosis and pathological damages were also alleviated in the ADR + LIPUS group compared to the ADR group. Cluster analysis identified 844 DEGs. GO enrichment analysis revealed enrichment in inflammatory response terms, while KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling and ferroptosis-related pathways.ConclusionContinuous LIPUS treatment improved ADR-induced renal fibrosis and dysfunction. The therapeutic effect of LIPUS was primarily due to its ability to suppress the CKD-related inflammation, which was associated with the modulation of the NF-κB and ferroptosis signaling pathways. These findings provide a new insight into the potential molecular mechanisms of LIPUS in treating CKD. Further research is necessary to confirm these findings and to identify potential therapeutic targets within these pathways.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a progressive and irreversible damage to the renal structure and function, characterized by a persistent decline in glomerular filtration rate (GFR) below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or the presence of kidney damage for more than three months [1]. The main risk factors of CKD include hypertension, diabetes, nephrotic syndrome, autoimmune diseases, and other underlying conditions. Globally, around 10 % of the population suffers from CKD, and this percentage is steadily increasing each year [2,3]. As chronic kidney disease (CKD) advances, the inflammation and renal fibrosis intensify, leading to a gradual decline in renal function and potentially resulting in end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [4]. At this stage, patients rely on hemodialysis or renal transplantation for treatment, which brings a heavy burdens and often leads to poor survival rate and quality of life [5]. Studies have revealed that the mortality rate associated with CKD has increased by 45.1 % over the past 30 years [2]. Although numerous clinical trials have been conducted, few pharmacological therapies show significant clinical benefits [6]. Therefore, it is necessary to develop a safe, effective, and economical treatment method in clinic to prevent the progression of CKD.

Low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) is a physical stimulation technique that transmits low-intensity (<3 W/cm2)and low-frequency (1–3 MHz) sound waves into tissues, generating acoustic pressure waves [7]. This conversion of mechanical signals from the pressure waves to biochemical responses results in subsequent downstream effects [7,8]. Although the intensity of LIPUS is low, it has high penetration capabilities. Compared to traditional treatment methods, LIPUS boasts the advantages of being non-invasive, well-tolerated, and having minimal side effects. More importantly, the application of LIPUS in vitro demonstrates a potential therapeutic approach for CKD. A p recent study provided the first evidence indicating that appropriate LIPUS stimulation could effectively mitigate inflammation and renal fibrosis in experimental hypertension and diabetes-related CKD [9]. Additionally, Lin et al. found that LIPUS treatment could effectively delay the progression of CKD by restoring antioxidant enzymes, attenuating renal failure aging, and decreasing the level of uremic toxin indophenol sulfate [10]. However, the precise mechanism through which LIPUS provides protection against CKD remains largely unexplored.

Adriamycin (ADR), also known as Doxorubicin, belongs to the anthracycline antibiotic family and is widely used as a broad-spectrum anti-tumor drug [11]. However, due to the risk of focal segmental glomerulosclerosis and subsequent development of CKD in patients receiving ADR chemotherapy, the therapeutic application of ADR is limited [12]. The ADR-induced animal model exhibits comparable pathological and clinical characteristics to human nephrotic syndrome, making it a recognized model for studying CKD [13]. In our previous study, we found that LIPUS exerts a beneficial therapeutic effect on the rat model of ADR-induced CKD, characterized by its ability to inhibit the CKD-related inflammation and renal fibrosis [14]. Therefore, in the present study, we aimed to clarify the potential molecular mechanism of LIPUS treatment for ADR-induced CKD, which may provide insights into the development of novel therapies for this debilitating condition.

Materials and methods

Animal model

The experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Kunming Medical University (approval number: Kmmu20220330). All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines to ensure the welfare of animals and minimize their discomfort. Eighteen male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, aged 6–8 weeks, were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Kunming Medical University (SPF, license number SYXK (Dian) k2020-0006). All rats were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility, maintaining a temperature of 22 ± 2 °C and a standard 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. They had access to sterilized water and food ad libitum, and the padding was replaced every three days. After a week of acclimation, the rats were randomly assigned to three equal groups: Control group, ADR group, and ADR + LIPUS group (n = 6 per group). According to Serag's method, rats in the ADR and ADR + LIPUS groups were intravenously injected twice with 4 mg/kg ADR on the 1st and 14th day, using a concentration of 2 mg/ml for the ADR solution [12]. The Control group rats were administered an equal volume of physiological saline. In the sixth week, all rats were humanely euthanized.

LIPUS treatment

The LIPUS therapy equipment is provided by the scientific group of Professor Lin Huang, including a dual-channel function generator (SDG1022X, Siglent, Shenzhen, China), an oscilloscope (TDS2022B, Tektronix, Shanghai, China) and some ultrasound transducers (rated frequency = 1 MHz, Φ = 3 cm, effective area = 7.1 cm2). Prior to LIPUS stimulation, rats were prepared for back hair removal, and the position of bilateral kidneys was marked by ultrasonic imaging. Next, the ultrasonic transducers were placed on the ultrasonic gel pad (Mibo, Mp-A, Shenzhen, China) and aligned with the surface mark of rat kidneys. Throughout the treatment, isoflurane (R510-22-10, RWD, Shenzhen, China) was administered using a gas anesthesia machine (MSSVAP01, MSS, Keighley, England) to keep the rats anesthetized. In the ADR + LIPUS group, the rats’ bilateral kidneys were exposed to LIPUS for 20 min daily, for a total treatment duration of four weeks. The LIPUS treatment parameters were set based on previous studies as follows [9,10]: an intensity of 60 mW/cm2 with a 50 % duty cycle, a pulse repetition rate of 1 kHz, and pulse widths of 500 μs and 500 μs between pulses (pulse space = 1 ms). The Control and ADR groups received the same intervention, minus the LIPUS treatment.

Renal function evaluation

The urine was collected by housing rats in a metabolic cage for 24 h prior to sacrifice. After recording the volume of the urine samples, the supernatant was separated by centrifugation for 20 min. The urine albumin concentration was then determined using a rat albumin enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) quantitative kit (C035-2, Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China), in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. The blood was collected via heart puncture under isoflurane inhalation anesthesia, followed by centrifugation at 3000 × rpm and storage at −20 °C. The serum creatinine (Scr) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels were then measured using a fully automatic biochemical analyzer (Chemray 800, Rayto, Shenzhen, China).

Histopathological evaluation

Kidney samples were fixed in 4 % neutral-buffered formalin for 24 h, followed by dehydration in 7 % sucrose solution for three cycles. The specimens were then embedded in paraffin, sliced into 4 μm thick sections, dewaxed, and rehydrated. The prepared slides were individually stained with hematoxylin eosin (H&E), periodic acid Schiff (PAS), and Masson's trichrome staining. The sections were examined using an optical microscope (Aperio VERSA, Laica, Shanghai, China), with 15 randomly selected fields analyzed per slice. Renal injury scores were determined based on previously established criteria [15]. The fibrotic areas of Masson's trichrome staining were quantified using Image J software (https://imagej.net/) by calculating the average value for each slice.

RNA extraction and sequencing

Kidney samples were cryogenically preserved in liquid nitrogen at −196 °C until RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted from the renal cortex using TRIzol reagent (G3013, Serviocebio, China), following the manufacturer's instructions. The concentration and quality of RNA were assessed using a Nanodrop 2000 instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA), respectively. Each sample was prepared for stranded RNA sequencing library construction using the KCTM Stranded mRNA Library Prep kit (DR08402, Wuhan Seqhealth, China) for Illumina. The PCR products corresponding to 200–500 base pairs were enriched, quantified, and sequenced on a Novaseq 6000 sequencer (Illumina, United States) with the PE150 model.

RNA matrix construction

To ensure the quality of data, raw data underwent several filtering steps. Contaminated reads containing adapters and low-quality reads (Phred quality value < 5, occurring in over 20 % of the read) were removed, along with reads containing more than 3 Ns. Following these filtering criteria, the remaining clean reads were mapped to the silva database to eliminate rRNA, using the bowtie2 software (version 2.5.1, https://bowtie-bio.sourceforge.net/). Subsequently, the clean reads were aligned to the rat reference genome (rn6) utilizing HISAT2 version 2.1.0 [16]. The fragments per kilobase million mapped reads (FPKM) for each gene were calculated, and the read counts for each gene were acquired through the HTSeq software (version 2.0.3, https://htseq.readthedocs.io/). In addition, we conducted a principal component analysis (PCA) on three groups of transcriptome data to test the biological duplication of samples.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and functional enrichment analysis

DEGs analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package (version 1.40.1) within the R software (version 4.3.0, https://cran.r-project.org/) to compare three groups: the Control group vs. the ADR group, and the ADR group vs. the ADR + LIPUS group (n = 5 per group). Firstly, the fold change (FC) of each gene was calculated based on the average FPKM value. Consequently, genes were classified as up-regulated or down-regulated depending on the sign of the log2 (FC) value: positive for up-regulated genes and negative for down-regulated genes. The threshold for significantly DEGs was set at log2 (FC) > |1.5| and adjusted P-value <0.05. DEGs were clustered in a heat map based on the transformed adjusted P-values, where the transformed values were scaled for each gene and clustered using the default complete hierarchical clustering method. Additionally, all genes were visualized in a volcano plot according to their FCs and adjusted P-values.

The DEGs common to both the Control and ADR groups, as well as those shared between the ADR and LIPUS groups, were employed for conducting Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/kegg1.html) enrichment analyses. The GO terms included Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC), and Molecular Function (MF). To identify pathways that exhibited significant enrichment in DEGs compared to the entire genome background, a hypergeometric test was employed. Consequently, the significant enrichment pathways were elucidated based on KEGG. The top 20 most significant GO and KEGG terms were selected and visualized.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

RT-qPCR was performed to validate the mRNA expression levels of the key DEGs. The RNA samples used for RT-qPCR were identical to those employed for RNA sequencing. We made 1 μg of total RNA reverse transcribed through RT Master Mix for qPCR (G3330, Serviocebio, Wuhan, China), and the generated cDNA was used as a template for the PCR. All samples were amplified by PCR three times. Subsequently, the mRNA abundance was determined using the 2 × SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (G3320, Serviocebio, Wuhan, China). The house-keeping gene (β-actin) was employed as an internal reference, and the DEGs were quantified relative to it using the comparative Ct method formula (2−ΔΔCt). The primer sequences for all genes can be found in Table S1.

Western Blotting (WB)

The protein expression levels of the key DEGs were examined by WB. Total proteins of rat kidneys were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (R0010, Solarbio, Beijing, China) in the presence of protease inhibitors (HY–K0010, Med Chem Express, USA), and protein concentration was measured using a BCA protein assay kit (PC0020, Solarbio, Beijing, China). For each protein sample, 30 μg was separated via 10 % SDS-PAGE (PG112, Epizyme, Shanghai, China) and transferred to PVDF membrane (IPVH00010, Millipore, USA). After blocking with 5 % nonfat milk at room temperature for 1 h, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) (1:1000; HUABIO), nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) (1:1000; Affinity), tumor necrosis factor receptor I (TNF-RI) (1:1000; Affinity), heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) (1:1000; Affinity), interleukin−1β (IL-1β) (1:1000; Affinity) and β-actin (1:10000; HUABIO), following incubation with the normal isotype-matched horseradish peroxide-labeled IgG (1:5000; HUABIO) at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, the blot signals were visualized by chemiluminescence reagent (HY–K1005, Med Chem Express, USA) and quantified using Image J software.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism Software (version 8.0.2, https://www.graphpad.com/). The results are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess significant differences, and P-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

LIPUS ameliorated renal function in ADR-induced CKD rats

The renal function injury of ADR-induced CKD model is characterized by the presence of substantial proteinuria, along with elevated levels of Scr and BUN. Initial urine sample analysis revealed a notable decrease in the 24 h urine volume of ADR rats compared to the Control and LIPUS groups (Fig. 1A). Subsequent blood and urinary biochemical tests demonstrated that the ADR rats exhibited a significant increase in 24 h urine total protein (UTP), as well as elevated levels of Scr and BUN, in comparison to the Control group (Fig. 1B–D). These findings confirm the successful induction of CKD in the rats by ADR. LIPUS moderates histopathological changes in ADR-induced CKD rats

H&E staining showed significant morphological alterations in the ADR rats, including glomerular and renal tubular dilatation, tubular epithelial injury, and lumen destruction (Fig. 2A). The histopathological scoring suggested that the degree of nephron damage in the ADR group fell between severe and extensive injury. Nonetheless, this damage was notably mitigated in the ADR + LIPUS group (Fig. 2D). In addition, PAS staining revealed a significant accumulation of glycogen in the dilated glomeruli and renal tubules of the ADR rat kidneys but was also ameliorated by LIPUS treatment (Fig. 2B). Masson staining demonstrated the presence of glomerular and tubulointerstitial fibrosis in the kidneys of ADR group rats. DEGs profiles

Transcriptome analysis revealed 5222 DEGs in the Control group compared to the ADR group. Among which, 2351 genes were up-regulated, while 2871 genes were down-regulated (Fig. 4A and B), indicating their potential involvement in the pathogenesis of ADR-induced CKD. Additionally, a total of 3056 DEGs in the ADR + LIPUS group compared to the ADR group, with 1566 genes up-regulated and 1490 genes down-regulated (Fig. 4C and D), suggesting the close involvement of these genes in the therapeutic effect of LIPUS on ADR-induced CKD. Functional enrichment of DEGs

Based on the above 844 cross-regulation DEGs, GO enrichment analysis was first conducted to identify essential terms associated with the therapeutic effect of LIPUS for ADR-induced CKD. A total of 7783 GO terms were derived for three fields including 6129 GO_BP, 573 GO_CC and 108 GO_MF ontologies. The remarkably enriched GO terms which were most likely to explain the therapeutic effect of LIPUS included response to interleukin−1 (IL-1), regulation of inflammatory response, and acute inflammatory response in the GO_BP catalog (Fig. 5A). In the GO_CC catalog, the enriched terms included the external side of the plasma membrane, extracellular matrix, collagen-containing extracellular matrix, and lipoprotein particle (Fig. 5B). Lastly, the enriched terms in the GO_MF catalog were mainly related to signaling receptor activation, receptor ligand interaction, and cell adhesion molecule binding (Fig. 5C). Discussion

LIPUS primarily performs its biological functions by utilizing mechanical effects, including cavitation, acoustic streaming, and acoustic radiation force [21]. These mechanical stimuli can alter cell volume, cell membrane permeability, and cell activation, thus facilitating the exchange of biomolecules across the cellular membrane system [22]. Currently, LIPUS is regarded as a promising novel method, which can achieve a breakthrough in the clinical dilemma of delaying CKD progression. In this study, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of LIPUS in the ADR-induced CKD model. The findings revealed that LIPUS stimulation effectively ameliorated ADR-induced pathological changes and renal dysfunction when applied at an intensity of 60 mW/cm2 and a duty cycle of 50 % to the bilateral kidneys of rats daily for a period of four weeks. These results are consistent with previous studies [9,10,23]. More importantly, to further elucidate the mechanism of LIPUS in improving CKD, transcriptome sequencing was performed on the kidneys of experimental rats. Through functional enrichment analysis. We found that LIPUS mainly delays CKD progression by inhibiting inflammation. Additionally, the NF-κB and ferroptosis signaling pathways emerged as potential targets for LIPUS treatment. Finally, we validated the mRNA and protein expression levels of some key DEGs in the NF-κB and ferroptosis signaling pathways, thereby providing preliminary evidence in support of our findings.

It Is very important for CKD patients to improve renal function, and renal fibrosis is often the culprit leading to chronic renal dysfunction [24]. Therefore, we initially assessed the effectiveness of LIPUS in delaying renal fibrosis. The results of Masso’'s trichrome staining showed that LIPUS treatment markedly decreased the accumulation of collagen fibers in the glomeruli and renal interstitium of ADR-induced CKD rats. In mechanism, recent studies reveled that fibroblast growth factor 23 (Fgf23) and fibrinogen γ chain (Fgg) can aggravate the progression of renal fibrosis, making them potential targets for CKD treatment [25,26]. Coincidentally, our analysis of DEGs revealed an up-regulation of these two genes in ADR-induced CKD rats, which were subsequently down-regulated upon LIPUS treatment. This suggests that the beneficial impact of LIPUS in delaying the progression of renal fibrosis may be attributed to the regulation of Fgf23 and Fgg genes. With the relief of renal fibrosis after LIPUS treatment, the renal dysfunction of CKD rats has also been significantly improved, which is manifested by an increase of 24 h urine volume and a decrease of blood 24 h UTP, blood Scr, and blood BUN. In addition, the cytochrome P450, subfamily 2, polypeptide 11 (Cyp2c11) gene, known for its vital role in kidney drug metabolism, was down-regulated in the ADR group but up-regulated with LIPUS treatment. A Prior study found a significant negative exponential correlation between renal function and Cyp2c11 gene expression [27], suggesting that Cyp2c11 may serve as a potential target or molecular biomarker for LIPUS to improve renal function in CKD rats.

As is known to all, regardless of the primary cause, inflammation consistently serves as a common pathological hallmark in CKD [28]. Normally, inflammation is a protective response to harmful stimuli, as it helps to remove the underlying injury and promote tissue repair [29,30]. However, if the inflammatory response persists without timely intervention, it could lead to the activation of intrinsic renal cells and infiltration of leukocytes, ultimately leading to the release of profibrotic cytokines and growth factors [29]. As a result, the profibrotic cytokines and growth factors contribute to the development of fibrosis, which progressively damages the glomeruli and interstitial tissues, resulting in the end-stage renal disease [29]. In our study, inflammatory driving factor (NF-κB) and inflammatory factor (IL-1β) were markedly highly expressed in the kidney of rats after two injections of ADR, suggesting that extensive renal interstitial fibrosis and renal dysfunction may be accompanied by severe inflammation in CKD rats. Notably, GO enrichment analysis based on the transcriptional DEGs revealed that the therapeutic effect of LIPUS on CKD could be attributed to its potential ability to inhibit the CKD-related inflammation initially. Specifically, most significantly enriched biological processes in the GO analysis were related to the regulation of inflammatory response and immune response, primarily occurring within the cellular matrix and basement membrane, and primarily driven by cytokines and chemokines. And ultimately, KEGG enrichment analysis demonstrated that the inhibitory effect of LIPUS on the CKD-related inflammation may be achieved through the regulation of the NF-κB signaling pathway.

Accumulating evidence indicates that the NF-κB signaling pathway plays a crucial role in the progression of CKD [31]. NF-κB is an important nuclear transcription factor involved in regulating inflammation, stress, immune, cell proliferation, and apoptotic responses to various stimuli [22]. In the absence of stimulation, NF-κB exists in the cytoplasm as a trimer complexed with its inhibitors, IκB and P65/P50. Upon cell activation by various chemical and mechanical signals, IκB within the NF-κB trimer is phosphorylated and degraded, allowing p65 to translocate from the cytoplasm into the nucleus. This process leads to the activation of the inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 and monocyte chemotactic protein 1 (MCP1) [17,31]. At this time, the inflammatory cytokines attract macrophages, which in turn secrete TNF, leading to the activation of TNFR-1 [17]. TNFR-1 and downstream signaling can trigger apoptosis, which exacerbates inflammation and injury by promoting the nuclear translocation of NF-κB [32]. Several prior studies have demonstrated that suitable LIPUS stimulation can suppress the phosphorylation of IκBα and subsequent nuclear translocation of p65, indicating that LIPUS has the potential to inhibit the cellular inflammatory response triggered by the NF-κB signaling pathway [[33], [34], [35]]. In this study, we observed that LIPUS down-regulated the NF-κB signaling pathway, overall, by regulating the expression of twenty-five genes. Moreover, we investigated the mRNA and protein expression levels of key molecules in the NF-κB signaling pathway, including NF-κB, TNF-R1, and IL-1β, using RT-qPCR and WB experiments. The results were consistent with those of the KEGG pathway analysis. The above findings preliminarily elucidate that LIPUS inhibits inflammation and treats CKD by modulating the NF-κB signaling pathway.

In addition to the NF-κB signaling pathway, the ferroptosis signaling pathway was also among the top 20 KEGG pathways in our enrichment analysis, aiming to elucidate the therapeutic mechanism of LIPUS in ADR-induced CKD rats (Fig. 5D). To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to propose that LIPUS treatment could potentially ameliorate the CKD-related inflammation, renal fibrosis, and dysfunction by regulating ferroptosis signaling pathway. For a long time, the prevention of cell death has been regarded as a critical strategy to suppress inflammation, renal fibrosis, and functional injury [36]. Currently, the prevailing perspective is that the main types of cell death are apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis [37]. Notably, ferroptosis, a newly discovered type of cell death, is characterized by iron accumulation within cells and lipid peroxidation [38,39]. In the present study, pathway analysis revealed that seven genes were repressed, while two genes were activated in the ferroptosis signaling pathway following LIPUS stimulation. Among the DEGs, we confirmed the expression levels of two key genes (ACSL4 and HO-1), to further provided evidence that the CKD-related ferroptosis signaling pathway is regulated by LIPUS treatment. The results showed that compared to the Control group, the mRNA and protein levels of ACSL4 were significantly increased in the ADR group, whereas the level of HO-1 was decreased. However, LIPUS treatment effectively corrected these changes induced by ADR (Fig. 8, Fig. 9). As previously mentioned, ACSL4 plays a vital role in the regulation of ferroptosis. Silencing ACSL4 inhibits ferroptosis, while its overexpression modulates cellular lipid composition and sensitivity to ferroptosis [39,40]. Additionally, HO-1 modulates enzymatic reactions that facilitate the breakdown of heme and enhance the uptake of intracellular iron [41]. Activation of ferritinophagy and/or reduction of HO-1 can increase free iron levels, resulting in the accumulation of lipid peroxides and subsequent ferroptosis [39,41]. All the clues we found strongly indicate that LIPUS stimulation can treat CKD by regulating ferroptosis signaling pathway, but its exact mechanism and relationship with improving inflammation, renal fibrosis and renal dysfunction need further investigation.

The present study has several limitations that warrant think. First, considering the complexity of the original etiology of CKD, other kinds of CKD models should be used to validate our findings. Second, the relatively small sample size in our study may introduce potential sampling bias. Third, there were not experiments to explore the time-effect and intensity-effect relationship of LIPUS treatment for CKD, which will be the main content of our next study. Fourth, our study only identified the potential genes and signaling pathways that may be regulated during LIPUS treatment for CKD, however, the exact mechanisms are still unclear. In addition, the latest view points out there is a series interaction between NF‐κB and ferroptosis signaling pathway [42], which remains to be clarified in the future.

Prospect of using LIPUS to treat CKD. Traditional ultrasound equipment is too expensive and bulky to be used for long-term home treatment. Moreover, the rigid ultrasound probe does not perfectly align with the human body during treatment, resulting in a decrease in the effective area of ultrasound radiation and potentially affecting treatment outcomes. Considering the challenges in translating LIPUS treatment for CKD into clinical practice. We are exploring a new approach based on the latest technology in flexible electronics. The goal is to develop a soft, flexible, and conformable ultrasound excitation device that can replace traditional ultrasound treatment equipment.

Conclusion

This study provides convincing evidence that LIPUS has a significant therapeutic effect on ADR-induced CKD. Fgf23, Fgg, and Cyp2c11 are identified as potential targets of LIPUS for improving renal fibrosis and renal dysfunction. The therapeutic effect of LIPUS should primarily be attributed to the inhibition of inflammation, which is closely associated with the regulation of NF‐κB and ferroptosis signaling pathways. These findings offer novel insights and provide a valuable foundation for further research on the mechanisms and clinical applications of LIPUS in the treatment of CKD.
