Impact of Photon-Counting Detector Computed Tomography on Image Quality and Radiation Dose in Patients With Multiple Myeloma
Authors: Alexander Rau, Jakob Neubauer, Laetitia Taleb, Thomas Stein, Till Schuermann, Stephan Rau, Sebastian Faby, Sina Wenger, Monika Engelhardt, Fabian Bamberg, Jakob Weiss
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveComputed tomography (CT) is an established method for the diagnosis, staging, and treatment of multiple myeloma. Here, we investigated the potential of photon-counting detector computed tomography (PCD-CT) in terms of image quality, diagnostic confidence, and radiation dose compared with energy-integrating detector CT (EID-CT).Materials and MethodsIn this prospective study, patients with known multiple myeloma underwent clinically indicated whole-body PCD-CT. The image quality of PCD-CT was assessed qualitatively by three independent radiologists for overall image quality, edge sharpness, image noise, lesion conspicuity, and diagnostic confidence using a 5-point Likert scale (5 = excellent), and quantitatively for signal homogeneity using the coefficient of variation (CV) of Hounsfield Units (HU) values and modulation transfer function (MTF) via the full width at half maximum (FWHM) in the frequency space. The results were compared with those of the current clinical standard EID-CT protocols as controls. Additionally, the radiation dose (CTDIvol) was determined.ResultsWe enrolled 35 patients with multiple myeloma (mean age 69.8 ± 9.1 years; 18 [51%] males). Qualitative image analysis revealed superior scores (median [interquartile range]) for PCD-CT regarding overall image quality (4.0 [4.0–5.0] vs. 4.0 [3.0–4.0]), edge sharpness (4.0 [4.0–5.0] vs. 4.0 [3.0–4.0]), image noise (4.0 [4.0–4.0] vs. 3.0 [3.0–4.0]), lesion conspicuity (4.0 [4.0–5.0] vs. 4.0 [3.0–4.0]), and diagnostic confidence (4.0 [4.0–5.0] vs. 4.0 [3.0–4.0]) compared with EID-CT (P ≤ 0.004). In quantitative image analyses, PCD-CT compared with EID-CT revealed a substantially lower FWHM (2.89 vs. 25.68 cy/pixel) and a significantly more homogeneous signal (mean CV ± standard deviation [SD], 0.99 ± 0.65 vs. 1.66 ± 0.5; P < 0.001) at a significantly lower radiation dose (mean CTDIvol ± SD, 3.33 ± 0.82 vs. 7.19 ± 3.57 mGy; P < 0.001).ConclusionWhole-body PCD-CT provides significantly higher subjective and objective image quality at significantly reduced radiation doses than the current clinical standard EID-CT protocols, along with readily available multi-spectral data, facilitating the potential for further advanced post-processing.Keywords: Photon-counting detector computed tomography, Multiple myeloma, Radiation dose, Image quality, Spatial resolution

INTRODUCTION

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a proliferative disorder of monoclonal plasma cells, characterized by primary infiltration of the bone marrow and excessive production of abnormal monoclonal immunoglobulins [1]. MM comprises approximately 1% of all malignancies and is the second most common hematologic malignancy after chronic lymphocytic leukemia [2]. Current guidelines rely on a combination of clinical tests (blood tests, urine analysis, bone marrow sampling, and imaging) to establish the final diagnosis and type of plasma cell disorder, determine the disease stage, and detect disease-related end-organ damage [3]. With recent advances in laboratory and imaging techniques, the International Myeloma Working Group updated the diagnostic criteria to identify patients at earlier stages and before (severe) end-organ damage to reduce morbidity and mortality [3].

Imaging plays a crucial role in MM screening, as up to 90% of patients develop bone lesions [4]. Conventional radiography has traditionally been used to screen for osteolytic lesions [5]. Given the higher sensitivity of cross-sectional imaging techniques such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), whole-body low-dose CT has been incorporated in the most recent guidelines as the imaging modality of choice [3]. High image quality at low radiation doses is desirable for an accurate diagnosis, especially for smaller lesions. In addition, reliable assessment of bone marrow infiltration is important to determine the disease extent and activity, and is often limited in conventional CT imaging and MRI [6,7,8].

Over the past decade, there has been a growing body of research on the role of dual-energy (DE) CT in diagnosis and disease monitoring using traditional morphological images combined with advanced DE data postprocessing. For example, Kosmala et al. [9] showed a similar diagnostic performance of DE virtual non-calcium images to MRI in assessing bone marrow infiltration. However, the availability of DE CT systems is limited, and it has been argued that the radiation dose in DE imaging protocols is higher than that in single-energy (SE) protocols [10].

With the clinical introduction of photon-counting detector (PCD)-CT systems in 2021, a novel technology for CT imaging has become available. Given the different detector architectures, PCD-CT enables significantly higher spatial resolution [11,12]. In addition, multi-spectral data that allow for advanced post-processing are intrinsically acquired in each scan at a substantially lower radiation dose owing to the direct conversion of the detected photons [11,12]. Consequently, spectral data can be obtained in a dose-dependent manner with the potential to overcome the current shortcomings of traditional energy-integrating detector (EID)-CT scanners.

Here, we aimed to investigate the impact of PCD-CT in patients with MM with respect to image quality, diagnostic confidence, and radiation dose, compared with conventional SE and DE EID-CT protocols. We hypothesized that PCD-CT would provide significantly superior image quality at reduced radiation doses compared with the currently established standard EID-CT protocols.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics Approval

This prospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (No. 21-2469 Ethics Committee, University of Freiburg), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Patient Cohort

We prospectively enrolled patients with MM diagnosed according to international consensus guidelines [3] who underwent clinically indicated non-contrast-enhanced whole-body PCD-CT in 2021 and 2022 and underwent prior EID-CT of the same scan region serving as the reference standard. Patients were excluded if they were under the age of 18 years, or if imaging data were missing, incomplete, or corrupted. Patient records were screened for myeloma classification and remission status on imaging.

CT Imaging Acquisition and Reconstruction

PCD-CT

All PCD-CT examinations were performed using NAEOTOM Alpha (Siemens Healthineers) in the supine position without a contrast agent. The acquisition parameters were as follows: multi-spectral Quantumplus mode, CARE Dose4D enabled and tube voltage 120 kVp, CARE keV IQ level 65 (corresponding to 59 quality reference mAs), gantry rotation time 0.5 s, pitch 0.6, focal spot 0.8 × 1.2 mm, and collimation of 144 × 0.4 mm.

From the acquired raw data, a standard series was reconstructed in an axial orientation at a monoenergetic level of 70 keV using a dedicated bone kernel (Br60) with a slice thickness of 2 mm, an increment of 2 mm, and quantum iterative reconstruction (QIR; Siemens Healthineers) with a strength level of 2. In addition, the coronal series of the upper and lower extremities were reconstructed using a standard soft tissue kernel (Br40) with a slice thickness of 2 mm and an increment of 2 mm with a QIR strength level of 4 to allow for detailed bone marrow assessment.

EID

Whenever possible, we used DE EID scans for comparison; if not available, SE EID-CT tin-filtered scans were employed. DE EID-CT examinations were performed in 16 patients on a third-generation dual-source CT system (SOMATOM Force, Siemens Healthineers) with a fixed tube voltage of 100 kVp for tube 1 and 150 kVp with tin filtration for tube 2; quality reference mAs were 220 mAs for tube 1 and 110 mAs for tube 2, gantry rotation time of 0.5 s, pitch of 0.6, focal spot of 0.9 × 1.2 mm, and standard collimation of 128 × 0.6 mm for both tubes with z-flying focal spot technology. A whole-body axial series was reconstructed from the acquired data in 2 mm slices with 2 mm increment in the Br59 kernel. In addition, a coronal series of the upper and lower extremities was reconstructed using a soft tissue kernel (Bf40), slice thickness of 2 mm, and 2 mm increment.

SE EID-CT was available for 11 patients on a third-generation dual-source CT system (SOMATOM Force, Siemens Healthineers) with a fixed tube voltage of 150 kVp, 653 quality reference mAs, rotation time of 1.0 s, pitch of 0.6, focal spot of 0.9 × 1.2 mm, and standard collimation of 192 × 0.6 mm with z-flying focal spot technology and reconstructed in 2 mm slices with a 2 mm increment in the Br59 kernel. The coronal series of the upper and lower extremities were also reconstructed with a soft tissue kernel (Bf40), slice thickness of 2 mm, and 2 mm increment. For eight patients, prior SE EID-CT images were acquired on a third-generation Aquilion scanner (Toshiba Medical Systems) using protocol settings to mimic a similar image impression (3D modulation = on; 120 kVp; 320 mAs; pitch = 0.813; focal spot 1.4 × 1.6 mm; reconstructed in 3 mm slices in an FC35 kernel).

Quantitative Image Analysis

Image quality in CT imaging is affected by several factors, including the radiation dose, detector resolution, and image reconstruction algorithms. Since the implementation of iterative image reconstruction algorithms, the use of traditional Fourier-based image quality metrics such as signal-to-noise and contrast-to-noise calculations are limited to objectively measuring image quality due to the nonlinearity of iterative reconstruction methods [13]. To address this but still obtain quantitative measures of the acquired signal for objective comparison of image quality between PCD-CT and EID-CT, we quantified and calculated the modulation transfer function (MTF) in a phantom study and the coefficient of variation (CV) in human participants.

Modulation Transfer Function in a Phantom Study

The MTF measures the spatial resolution capability of a CT system, which can be quantified by calculating the full width at half maximum (FWHM) in the frequency space as an indicator of spatial resolution relative to the pixel size. In addition, the amplitude of the peak at each frequency reflects the contrast sensitivity at that frequency [14].

To measure the MTF and compare the spatial resolution of the PCD-CT system with that of the EID-CT system, we used a standard phantom with a fine tungsten wire (200 µm) (Model No. 10743595, Siemens Healthineers), which was scanned using both CT systems. Axial images were acquired and reconstructed using settings similar to the clinical scans, transferred from the console, and processed using an in-house MATLAB script (MathWorks version R2020b; https://www.mathworks.com) to calculate the MTF from the magnitude of the Fourier transform of the profile response of the sampled wire (line spread function, LSF). Qualitative Image Analysis

The subjective image quality of all PCD-CT and EID-CT images was assessed in a reading session by three radiologists (AR, JW, and JN, with 4, 6, and 11 years of experience in musculoskeletal imaging, respectively) independently, in a random fashion, and blinded to the scanner type and acquisition mode. To ensure a consistent reading among the three radiologists, different regions typically involved in MM [4] were defined and evaluated separately on axial slices, which comprised the skull bone, fourth rib on the right side, 12th thoracic vertebral body, the 3rd lumbar vertebral body, pelvis, medullary cavity in the left upper arm, and a reference lesion per patient, where available. The image quality for each region was assessed in terms of 1) overall image quality, 2) edge sharpness and trabecular bone structure, and 3) subjective image noise on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = non-diagnostic, 2 = poor image quality/severe artifacts, 3 = moderate image quality/artifacts, 4 = fair image quality/minor artifacts, and 5 = excellent image quality/no artifacts). In line with current guidelines [3], reference lesions were defined as osteolytic lesions ≥ 5 mm in size. In addition, as an additional verification step, we only included lesions that were clearly detectable in at least one follow-up study (CT or MRI). The reference lesion was assessed with respect to its conspicuity and the reader's diagnostic confidence in the axial bone and coronal soft tissue reconstructions using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = nondiagnostic; 5 = excellent). All reading sessions were performed using a dedicated workstation (Dell Precision 5820, Dell Inc.; Eizo RX250 and Eizo Med XN51LP, EIZO Corporation; DeepUnity Diagnost 1.2.0.1, Dedalus HealthCare GmbH).

Radiation Dose

All dose calculations were performed using a commercially available dose management and reporting platform (DoseM; INFINITT Europe GmbH). The volume CT dose index (CTDIvol [mGy]) was extracted for the entire cohort and compared between PCD-CT and EID-CT examinations.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the R statistics software (version 3.6.3; R Core Team, https://www.R-project.org). Continuous variables are reported as mean ± SD or median and interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate, while categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. We used the Shapiro-Wilk statistic to test for normal distribution. Patient demographics were compared using the paired sample t-test, chi-square test, or McNemar test, as appropriate. Differences in reading scores were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, based on the average reading scores of the three readers per reading item for PCD-CT vs. EID-CT. The MTF was quantified using FWHM calculations in the frequency space. For CV analysis, a paired-sample test was conducted. To investigate potential differences in EID-CT scan modes (SE vs. DE), we performed subgroup analyses, in which we compared the qualitative and quantitative image parameters between the SE and DE EID-CT scans and the corresponding PCD-CT scans, respectively. An α-level of 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

Whole-body PCD-CT was successfully performed in all 35 patients (mean age 69.8 ± 9.1 years; 18 [51%] males) included in this study. Prior DE EID-CT scans were available for 16 patients (median delay, 303 days), while SE EID-CT scans were available for the remaining 19 patients (median delay, 582 days). There were no significant differences in baseline demographics between patients who underwent DE and SE EID-CT with respect to age, sex, height, weight, and body mass index (all P > 0.05). Clinical data were not available for one patient who was referred for imaging only. The MM remission status (i.e., disease progression vs. remission) on imaging was similar for PCD-CT and EID-CT scans for the entire cohort (P = 0.741) and the DE (P > 0.999) and SE EID-CT subsets (P = 0.63). Reference lesions were identified in of 29/36 patients. We report the diagnostic confidence and lesion conspicuity of four skull lesions, 12 vertebral body lesions, four rib lesions, seven pelvic skeletal lesions, and two medullary cavity lesions of the humerus. Radiation Dose

Radiation dose analyses revealed a significantly lower CTDIvol for PCD-CT compared with EID-CT, with a mean CTDIvol of 3.33 ± 0.82 vs. 7.19 ± 3.57 mGy; P < 0.001 for the entire cohort. Similar results were found in the subanalysis comparing PCD-CT vs. DE EID-CT (3.23 ± 0.81 vs. 9.47 ± 2.71 mGy; P < 0.001) whereas no significant differences were noted between PCD-CT and SE EID-CT (3.38 ± 0.84 vs. 5.16 ± 2.99 mGy; P = 0.114) (Fig. 5).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we investigated the impact of PCD-CT on whole-body imaging in patients with MM with respect to image quality, diagnostic confidence, and radiation dose, compared with conventional EID-CT. We found that PCD-CT allows for significantly improved subjective and objective image quality and superior diagnostic confidence at a substantially reduced radiation dose (54%) compared with the current clinical standard EID-CT imaging protocols.

This has clinical implications, as the most recent practice guidelines for MM recommend whole-body low-dose CT imaging as the modality of choice for diagnosis and follow-up imaging to assess symptomatic disease-related complications (e.g., fractures) and relapse [3]. For decades, the diagnosis of MM has relied on imaging-based assessments of bone lesions detected on conventional radiographs in combination with other signs of end-organ damage (renal dysfunction, anemia, and increased calcium levels) [1]. Recently, the International Myeloma Working Group updated its recommendations on imaging in response to significantly higher sensitivity of cross-sectional imaging techniques, including whole-body low-dose CT, which is the cheapest and most widely available modality [1]. To allow for accurate assessment of small bone lesions, a high spatial resolution at low image noise and radiation doses is desirable [15].

In the current study, PCD-CT yielded significantly superior reading scores with respect to image quality, sharpness/trabecular bone structure, and lesion conspicuity than the current clinical standard EID-CT approach. These findings are supported by the results of the quantitative MTF analysis, which revealed superior resolution for PCD-CT. Ultimately, this translates into improved diagnostic confidence, underpinning the clinical value of the new CT technology. These results are in line with those of recent studies that reported improved assessment of lytic lesions, medullary manifestations, fatty metamorphosis, and pathological fractures using PCD-CT versus EID-CT [15,16,17]. In particular, the recent work of Baffour et al. [15] showed the clear superiority of PCD-CT over EID-CT in a group of 27 patients with myeloma in terms of image noise and spatial resolution, resulting in a better assessment of the bone and medullary cavity. In contrast to our approach, they investigated the ultrahigh-resolution mode, and the radiation dose of PCD-CT was matched to that of EID-CT [15]. Our analysis revealed significantly lower image noise ratings for PCD-CT than for EID-CT. This is especially useful for improving the assessment of extramedullary soft tissue and bone marrow manifestations, which are otherwise prone to underdiagnosis [8].

In addition to the subjective image quality metrics and information regarding spatial resolution, additional quantitative measures of the acquired signals are of interest. In our CV analysis, we found a significantly more homogeneous signal on PCT-CT scans than on EID-CT examinations. While this may improve comparability between sequential scans, it could also prove beneficial for advanced postprocessing techniques [7]. This may be particularly beneficial for improving the assessment of bone marrow infiltration, which is often limited by conventional CT imaging. Currently, consensus guidelines recommend MRI for bone marrow assessment in cases of clinical suspicion of MM but with negative CT findings [3]. However, this is expensive, time-consuming, cumbersome for patients, or not possible because of contraindications [18]. In these cases, DE CT-based assessment of bone marrow infiltration can provide a valuable alternative, as previously described by Kosmala et al. [9], who found a similar diagnostic performance between virtual non-calcium CT images and MRI. Whether PCT-CT has the potential to match or further improve these results is beyond the scope of the current analysis and needs to be evaluated in future studies.

Another key result obtained in this investigation is the possibility of using PCT-CT for dose optimization without compromising image quality. Compared with the current clinical standard EID-CT protocols, we found a dose-saving potential between 35% (SE EID-CT with a tin filter) and 66% (DE EID-CT without a tin filter). As a substantial proportion of patients diagnosed with MM are in their forties and fifties (2% and 10%, respectively) [19], efficient methods to reduce radiation dose are paramount. With dose values in a similar range as tin-filtered SE EID-CT scans but with full multispectral information superior to DE EID-CT, allowing for advanced post-processing, PCT-CT offers new opportunities to improve patient management and reduce the risk of ionization radiation-associated side effects [11,12].

However, some limitations of our study must be considered. The study cohort was small and included EID-CT data from both SE and DE examinations, resembling heterogeneous disease stages. MRI was not available as an independent reference standard to validate the CT findings. Furthermore, raw data could not be obtained for the eight patients who underwent SE EID-CT on the Toshiba Aquilion scanner, and only reconstructions with a 3 mm slice thickness were available. This could introduce a minor bias in the quality assessment. Because the investigations were performed within standard internal clinical procedures, a temporal delay occurred. There was no significant difference in disease stage between the examinations, making relevant bias due to disease dynamics unlikely. We did not observe a statistically significant difference in radiation dose between PCD-CT and SE EID-CT, likely because of the small sample size. In addition, the PCD-CT protocol settings for the current study were optimized in the evaluation period preceding the current analysis to model the clinical standard EID-CT protocol in terms of image impression and not to achieve the lowest radiation dose possible. In addition, this study focused on bone involvement of MM and omitted extraosseous manifestations. Furthermore, despite the independent and blinded reading sessions, biased assessments due to the identification of PCD-CT scans based on superior image quality and sharpness cannot be excluded.

In conclusion, whole-body PCD-CT in patients with MM provides significantly higher subjective and objective image quality at significantly reduced radiation doses than the current clinical standard EID-CT protocols, along with readily available multispectral data, facilitating the potential for further advanced post-processing.
