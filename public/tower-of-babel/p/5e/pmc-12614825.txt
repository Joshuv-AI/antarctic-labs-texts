Potential inhibitory effect of Shigyakusan on gallstone formation in a mice model of cholesterol gallstone Disease
Authors: Go UTSUNOMIYA, Yuta SHINOHARA, Haruki KURIHARA, Yishan LIU, Yusuke ISHIHARA, Minami KOBAYASHI, Mohamed ELBADAWY, Amira ABUGOMAA, Tatsuya USUI, Kazuaki SASAKI
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCholesterol gallstones (CGs) are formed in the biliary system due to cholesterol supersaturation in the bile and delayed bile excretion. The current treatments for CGs involve potentially severe complications, and novel therapeutic approaches are still required. Shigyakusan is a traditional Chinese medicine used for treating CG disease (CGD), but there are no reports on the action or efficacy of Shigyakusan in CGD. The present study was conducted to clarify the inhibitive effects of Shigyakusan on CGD. Mice were fed a normal or lithogenic diet for 8 weeks (to create a mouse model of CGD) and simultaneously treated with vehicle or Shigyakusan. Organs were removed after blood sampling to examine CG formation and perform pathological evaluations. Genetic analysis of the liver, gallbladder, and ileum tissues was also conducted. CGD was induced by feeding mice with a lithogenic diet. Specifically, gallstones were detected, and serum T-CHO and LDL-C were significantly elevated. Administration of Shigyakusan to CGD mice reduced the development of CGs and lowered the serum T-CHO and LDL-C levels. Additionally, Shigyakusan administration increased ABCG5/G8 and ABCB4 mRNA expression in the liver, AQP5 expression in the gallbladder, and ABCG5 expression in the ileum of CGD mice. Shigyakusan administration diminished the synthesis of CGs in mice by lowering serum cholesterol and improving bile flow. The results show that Shigyakusan may be a beneficial option for CGD treatment and prevention.Keywords: cholesterol gallstone, gallstone disease, herbal therapy, Shigyakusan, traditional Chinese medicine

INTRODUCTION

Cholelithiasis is a general term for diseases in which stones form in the biliary system of the gallbladder or bile ducts [36]. Gallstones are classified into several types based on their composition, including the cholesterol and pigment contents, sometimes resulting in mixed stones [27]. Cholesterol gallstones (CGs) contain 50% to 90% cholesterol and are the most common form observed in countries where people consume a Western diet high in protein and fat [24]. In Japan, the prevalence of CGs has been increasing due to the westernization of dietary habits and the aging population [31]. The formation of CGs is influenced by genetic factors, excessive secretion of cholesterol from the liver, and cholesterol supersaturation in the bile. Bile juice contains ~95% water in which several endogenous solids are dissolved, including bile acids, bile salts, bilirubin, phospholipids, cholesterol, and amino acids [4], but insoluble cholesterol is solubilized in bile by mixed micelles composed of bile acids and phospholipids [30]. When the relative cholesterol concentration in bile increases due to various factors, supersaturation occurs, and the supersaturated cholesterol crystallizes with mucin gel and bilirubin in the gallbladder and grows into stones.

Cholesterol metabolism serves a critical role in cholelithiasis. Niemann-Pick C1-Like 1 (NPC1L1) is an essential transporter that mediates exogenous cholesterol uptake by the enterocyte [38]. Dietary cholesterol and cholesterol secreted into the intestine are absorbed by NPC1L1 and transported to the liver via the bloodstream [8]. ATP-binding cassette subfamily G members 5 and 8 (ABCG5 and ABCG8) are cholesterol transporters that directly secrete cholesterol into the bile through their coordinated functions. In the liver, cholesterol is secreted into the bile from hepatocytes via ABCG5/G8 on the hepatocyte-capillary membrane [3]. The secretion of cholesterol also requires the assistance of phospholipids. ABCB4 is a phospholipid transporter involved in the excretion of phospholipids into the bile [23]. Additionally, ABCG5 and ABCG8 are expressed in the intestinal epithelium, suppressing intestinal cholesterol absorption and actively excreting cholesterol into the intestinal lumen [25]. The involvement of these transporters in the liver and intestine is thought to play a role in gallstone formation [16]. Moreover, delayed bile excretion into the duodenum due to reduced gallbladder function is closely related to the formation of CGs [28]. The gallbladder contains water channels, such as AQP1 and AQP8, and a decrease in the expression of these channels due to functional changes in the gallbladder has been reported in a gallstone disease model mice [32].

Cholelithiasis is known to induce various diseases. For example, gallbladder inflammation caused by gallstone formation is considered one of the major risk factors for gallbladder cancer [17]. In addition, several complications, such as acute cholecystitis, acute cholangitis, and biliary pancreatitis, can be life-threatening [20].

Cholecystectomy is recommended as the standard treatment for symptomatic gallstone disease [10]. However, in some cases, surgery may not be advised due to the patient’s overall condition. Furthermore, surgical intervention may lead to complications, such as post-cholecystectomy syndrome, potentially reducing the patient’s quality of life [13]. Regarding medical treatment, oral bile stone dissolution therapy is commonly available, but several limitations persist, including a narrow range of indications, low complete dissolution rates, prolonged treatment duration, and a high recurrence rate.

Therefore, we have considered traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) to identify potential options for novel medical treatments. TCM is frequently used to expel gallstones and prevent them from recurring [6]. Notably, Shigyakusan is a TCM composed of four herbs, namely Saiko, Shakuyaku, Kijitsu, and Kanzo, and is used in cases of cholecystitis and cholelithiasis. However, the actions and effectiveness of Shigyakusan in treating gallstones have not been clarified.

In cholelithiasis research, a common mouse model of gallstones is frequently utilized [33]; the CG disease (CGD) mouse model is created by feeding normal mice a lithogenic diet [32]. This model exhibits hallmark characteristics of cholelithiasis, including the formation of CGs within the gallbladder, thickening and inflammation of the gallbladder wall, and functional changes such as mucus secretion and motility [19].

In this study, the CGD mouse model was used to evaluate the inhibitive effect of Shigyakusan on gallstone formation and examine the underlying mechanism.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

TCM and chemicals

Shigyakusan was provided by Iskra Industry Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan. Additionally, 0.5% methylcellulose and 0.2% Tween 80 were purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan).

Experimental design and animal treatment

AIN93G-milk casein (Oriental Yeast Co., Tokyo, Japan) was used as the normal diet, and a modified AIN93G diet (Oriental Yeast Co.) containing cholesterol and other components was used as the lithogenic diet. Specifically, the CGD mouse model was created by feeding C57L/J mice a lithogenic diet containing 15% cholesterol, 1.25% lipids, and 0.5% cholic acid [26].

Shigyakusan was suspended in 0.5% methylcellulose, 0.2% Tween 80, and sterile distilled water, and 4.8 mg/mouse/day was administered intraperitoneally.

A total of 23 six-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were assigned to four groups: normal diet + vehicle (n=6), normal diet + Shigyakusan (n=6), lithogenic diet + vehicle (n=6), and lithogenic diet + Shigyakusan (n=5). The mice were fed their respective diets and administered either vehicle or Shigyakusan for 8 weeks, and then they were sacrificed under isoflurane anesthesia (Fig. 1). Fasting was performed the day before tissue collection to ensure adequate bile accumulation in the gallbladder. Gallbladder tissue containing bile was carefully excised, ensuring that the Oddi sphincter was not cut, and liver and ileum tissues were also collected. Body and liver weights were measured, and the liver-to-body weight ratio was calculated. The gallstones in the gallbladder were visually inspected, and grading was performed following a previous study (Table 1 ) [2]. The bile in the gallbladder was placed on a microscope slide and photographed using an optical microscope (CKX-53, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Blood was collected from the posterior vena cava, and the serum was separated. The levels of ALT, AST, total cholesterol (T-CHO), triglycerides (TGs), LDL cholesterol (LDL-C), HDL cholesterol (HDL-C), and total bile acid (TBA) were measured at Oriental Yeast Co., Ltd. This experiment was conducted under the approval of the Animal Care and Use Committee and the Ethics Committee at the Tokyo University of Agriculture and Technology (Approval Number: R06-131).

Experimental design used for the administration of a lithogenic diet and Shigyakusan to mice. The mice were fed a lithogenic or normal diet for 8 weeks and simultaneously received intraperitoneal injections of either Shigyakusan (4.8 mg/head/day) or vehicle.

Pathological examination

Gallbladder and liver tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and then embedded in paraffin. The paraffin blocks containing the tissues were sectioned to a thickness of 4 µm using a sliding microtome (REM700, DAIWA KOGYO, Yokohama, Japan). After deparaffinization, the sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Images were captured using an optical microscope (BX-43, Olympus).

Quantitative real-time PCR

The excised tissues were processed by flash freezing in liquid nitrogen and grinding into a powder. RNA was then extracted using the NucleoSpin RNA Kit (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany). Next, the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Kit (Toyobo Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) was used to convert the RNA into cDNA. Real-time PCR was performed using the QuantiTect SYBR I Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Netherlands) and StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). The primers for GAPDH, ABCG5, ABCG8, ABCB4, AQP5, and NPC1L1 (Fasmac Co., Ltd., Atsugi, Japan) were used (Table 2).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± SEM. Statistical comparisons between the two groups were performed using a t-test with Bonferroni correction. ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test, was applied to analyze the difference among all groups. P-values of <0.05 were considered significant.

RESULTS

Effect of Shigyakusan on gallstone formation

In the groups fed a normal diet, no gallstone formation was observed by visual inspection or optical microscopy. In the groups fed a lithogenic diet, gallstone formation was observed by eye, in addition to optical microscopy (Fig. 2A, 2B). A grading evaluation was conducted (Table 1), as previously described [19]. A lower CG grade was observed in the Shigyakusan group than in the vehicle group, among those fed a lithogenic diet (Fig. 2C, 2D).

Effect of Shigyakusan on blood parameters

In blood biochemical tests, a significant increase in T-CHO and LDL-C was observed in the group fed the lithogenic diet compared with those in the group fed the normal diet. Shigyakusan administration significantly suppressed these increases in the lithogenic diet group, compared with vehicle administration (Fig. 3A, 3B). For HDL-C, the lithogenic diet group showed significantly lower levels than the normal diet group, and Shigyakusan administration further reduced the levels in both diet groups, compared with the vehicle treatment (Fig. 3C). TG levels were significantly decreased in the lithogenic diet group compared with those in the normal diet group. In the normal diet group, Shigyakusan treatment significantly increased the TG levels, whereas no significant difference was observed in the lithogenic diet group following Shigyakusan treatment (Fig. 3D). Regarding TBA, Shigyakusan treatment significantly increased the levels in the lithogenic diet groups (Fig. 3E). For AST, a significant increase was observed in the normal diet group following Shigyakusan treatment, but no significant difference was observed in the lithogenic diet group (Fig. 3F). No significant differences were observed in regard to ALT (Fig. 3G).

Effect of Shigyakusan on liver-to-body weight ratio

The liver-to-body weight ratio in the lithogenic diet group with vehicle treatment was significantly increased compared with that in the normal diet group with vehicle treatment. Shigyakusan treatment markedly lowered the liver-to-body weight ratio in the lithogenic diet group but not in the vehicle-treated group (Table 3, Fig. 4A).

In the lithogenic diet group, significant macrovesicular steatosis was observed in the liver tissue by HE staining, regardless of the treatment (Fig. 4B). No changes were observed in the gallbladder tissue upon HE staining among the groups (Data not shown).

Gene analysis of the liver revealed that Shigyakusan treatment significantly increased the expression of ABCG5, ABCG8, and ABCB4 in the lithogenic diet group, but no significant change was observed in the normal diet group. Gene analysis of the gallbladder showed that Shigyakusan treatment significantly increased the expression of AQP5 in the lithogenic diet group, and after Shigyakusan treatment, the expression of AQP5 was significantly higher in the lithogenic diet group than in the normal diet group (Fig. 5B).

DISCUSSION

CGs are commonly observed in Western countries and have been increasing in Japan [31]. The primary treatment for gallstone disease is surgical cholecystectomy, although complications remain a concern. Furthermore, medical treatments have a high recurrence rate and limited indications. In this context, we focused on TCMs to identify novel therapeutic approaches. This study aimed to determine the effects of Shigyakusan on gallstone formation and its underlying mechanisms using a CGD model mouse. Intraperitoneal administration was selected as the route of administration in order to ensure a consistent dosage and safe administration [35]. Regarding the dosage, although it was not related to gallstones, we referred to a previous study on Shigyakusan [22] and conducted preliminary experiments using various dose levels. We selected the highest dose that did not result in adverse effects as the administration dose for the present study. CGD is a pathological condition in which cholesterol crystals or aggregates of crystals form in the gallbladder, leading to gallstone formation. In this study, mice fed a lithogenic diet for 8 weeks exhibited cholesterol-like crystals in the gallbladder, indicating the successful development of CGs. In the lithogenic diet group, the CGs in the Shigyakusan-treated mice were lower grade than those in the vehicle-treated mice, suggesting that Shigyakusan suppressed gallstone formation.

Blood tests showed that the lithogenic diet caused significant increases in T-CHO, LDL-C, and TBA, whereas TG and HDL-C significantly decreased. The increase in T-CHO and LDL-C, as well as the decrease in HDL-C, following the administration of the lithogenic diet, were consistent with a previous study [11]. This increase in T-CHO and LDL-C was significantly suppressed by Shigyakusan administration, suggesting that Shigyakusan can suppress the rise in blood cholesterol levels. Cholesterol in bile is predominantly derived from dietary intake and peripheral tissue uptake into the liver [9]. The increase in blood cholesterol due to the lithogenic diet may cause increased cholesterol uptake in the liver, leading to excess cholesterol in the bile; supersaturation of the cholesterol then promotes gallstone formation. Shigyakusan appears to act by suppressing this cholesterol increase in the blood, thereby preventing bile cholesterol supersaturation and inhibiting gallstone formation. Additionally, a formulation containing Saiko, Shakuyaku, and Kijitsu, namely Daishakuhoto, was shown to suppress the increase in blood cholesterol and inhibit gallstone formation [1], and Kanzo, a component of Shigyakusan, has been reported to lower blood cholesterol levels [12].

The liver-to-body weight ratio reflects the extent of hepatomegaly; a previous study [34] has shown that a lithogenic diet induces pathological changes in the liver, such as hepatomegaly and hepatic lipid accumulation. The lithogenic diet also increased the liver-to-body weight ratio in the present study. Moreover, previous research [39] has demonstrated that Shigyakusan administration ameliorates typical hepatic lesions, such as hepatomegaly and hepatic lipid accumulation, induced by a lithogenic diet. Although no significant difference was observed in the present study, Shigyakusan administration slightly suppressed the increase in the liver-to-body weight ratio. Shigyakusan may have alleviated the extent of the hepatomegaly induced by the lithogenic diet. However, histological examination of the liver revealed significant macrovesicular steatosis caused by the lithogenic diet, and no significant changes were observed with Shigyakusan administration. These results indicate that Shigyakusan does not completely suppress liver fat degeneration, but it may exert a protective effect against pathological changes in the liver.

Cholesterol absorbed from the diet is transported by the bloodstream to the liver, where it is metabolized, and some of it is secreted into bile [9]. NPC1L1 is involved in cholesterol absorption from the small intestine [8], whereas ABCG5 and ABCG8 are transporters that regulate the secretion of cholesterol into bile and inhibit cholesterol absorption in the intestine [3, 25]. Increased expression of these genes has been observed in the liver of mice fed a high-cholesterol diet and in humans with gallstone disease [14, 38]. In the present study, however, no significant increase in expression was observed in the liver of the CGD mouse model. The previous study [38] was conducted with a 2-week lithogenic diet feeding regimen in mice. In this experiment, the diet was continued for 8 weeks, and it is possible that the biological response to the lithogenic diet alone had diminished over time. For example, a previous study that investigated gallstone formation and gallbladder changes with a lithogenic diet found that after 2 weeks of feeding, thickening of the gallbladder mucosa was observed, but after 8 weeks, this thickening was no longer present [5]. Notably, another study [37] showed that the administration of compounds that increase the expression of ABCG5/G8 in the liver can reduce blood and liver cholesterol levels and inhibit gallstone formation. This is consistent with our findings showing that Shigyakusan administration significantly increased the expression of ABCG5 and ABCG8 in the lithogenic diet group, compared with vehicle administration. Furthermore, Shigyakusan increased the expression of ABCG5/8 in the liver only when mice were fed a lithogenic diet. While further investigation is needed, it is possible that in our study, when excessive lipids were consumed through the lithogenic diet, Shigyakusan might have enhanced the increase in ABCG5/8 mRNA expression, thereby exerting a regulatory effect on biological functions. For example, there are studies on other traditional Chinese medicines that suggest a diuretic effect, where it is reported that while these medicines do not show diuretic effects in a dehydrated state, they exhibit diuretic effects when there is excess water in the body [21]. Meanwhile, Shigyakusan likely increased ABCG5 expression in the intestine. Ultimately, Shigyakusan might promote cholesterol excretion into the lumen and thereby contributes to the reduction of blood and liver cholesterol levels, effectively suppressing the formation of gallstones.

In this study, we focused on ABCG5/8 and conducted investigations primarily on the aspects related to the suppression of cholesterol absorption. However, it is also possible that Shigyakusan may have other effects beyond cholesterol absorption. For example, a previous study on Kanzo, one of the ingredients of Shigyakusan, reported that when rats fed a high-fat diet were treated with Kanzo, there was a significant decrease in hepatic cholesterol levels and serum lipoprotein cholesterol [15]. This was accompanied by a reduction in the activity of Hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA synthase (HMGS), an enzyme involved in cholesterol synthesis, and an increase in the activity of CYP7A1, which is involved in converting cholesterol to bile acids. We aim to explore such mechanisms beyond cholesterol absorption in future work.

Phospholipids are thought to protect bile duct epithelial cells from the toxic effects of bile acids and solubilize excess cholesterol by forming mixed micelles. ABCB4 is involved in the secretion of phospholipids from liver cells into the bile [23]. ABCB4-knockout mice, which do not secrete phospholipids into the bile, develop portal inflammation and cholangiopathy, leading to the progression of CG formation [18]. In the present study, Shigyakusan administration increased ABCB4 expression in the liver, and thus, Shigyakusan may enhance phospholipid secretion and cholesterol solubilization, mitigating cholesterol supersaturation in the bile.

AQPs are membrane proteins that facilitate the permeability of water, and they are expressed in various tissues, including the liver and gallbladder epithelial cells [15]. The AQPs present in gallbladder epithelial cells play a crucial role in the reabsorption of water from the bile. When the expression of AQPs is reduced, the reabsorption of water in the gallbladder is impaired, hindering the ability to concentrate bile. Consequently, the concentrations of cholesterol and bilirubin decrease, and the bile acids and phospholipids necessary to dissolve these components are insufficiently supplied [26]. As a result, undissolved cholesterol and bilirubin may crystallize, creating an environment conducive to CG formation. The suppression of gallbladder AQP expression and a reduction in gallbladder concentration capacity were previously observed in a CGD mouse model [32]. Herein, we found that Shigyakusan increased the expression of AQP5 in the gallbladder, which likely inhibited the gallbladder concentration ability, thereby reducing the formation of gallstones. Notably, AQP5 expression was significantly elevated only in mice receiving both a lithogenic diet and Shigyakusan treatment. This suggests that Shigyakusan may have enhanced the biological response to the high-cholesterol diet via AQP5 expression.

Research on TCM is limited, and studies on gallstone formation and gene expression related to cholesterol metabolism are particularly scarce. In the future, we plan to comprehensively investigate the effects of Shigyakusan and the chemical compounds contained in its constituent herbs on gene expression. In this experiment, there is also limitation due to the use of intraperitoneal injection. We plan to consider oral administration in future studies.

Additionally, in this study, we focused on TCM, particularly Shigyakusan, as a potential alternative in light of the challenges associated with existing gallstone treatments. In addition to TCM, it has been reported that cholesterol biosynthesis inhibitors, Tu 2208 and Ro 48-8071 [7], and NPC1L1 inhibitors, diosgenin and ezetimibe [29] effectively prevent cholesterol cholelithiasis in the similar cholesterol gallstone model mice. Since Shigyakusan may have a different mechanism of action compared to these pharmacological agents, it could potentially serve as a new option for gallstone treatment, possibly in combination with these drugs. Additionally, unlike Tu2208 or Ro 48-8071, Shigyakusan has a proven track record of use in Japan and is recognized as a pharmaceutical, making it easily accessible and practical to use. These may represent advantages of Shigyaku-san. On the other hand, the effectiveness of Shigyakusan in treating cholelithiasis has not been demonstrated, and this study did not assess its ability to dissolve gallstones. This may be considered a limitation of Shigyakusan. We believe that further studies that demonstrate a clear therapeutic effect of Shigyakusan are needed.

In conclusion, this study showed that Shigyakusan, when administered alongside a high-cholesterol diet, increased cholesterol secretion from the liver into the bile while enhancing phospholipid secretion, thus increasing the soluble cholesterol in the bile and preventing supersaturation. Furthermore, Shigyakusan increased ABCG5 expression in the ileum, which helped suppress cholesterol absorption and promoted the excretion of cholesterol into the intestinal lumen, contributing to the suppression of blood cholesterol levels. Finally, Shigyakusan promoted water reabsorption in the bile by increasing AQP expression in the gallbladder, preventing the decrease in the bile concentrating ability. Shigyakusan appears to inhibit gallstone formation through these mechanisms and may be a beneficial adjunctive or preventive treatment for CGD.
