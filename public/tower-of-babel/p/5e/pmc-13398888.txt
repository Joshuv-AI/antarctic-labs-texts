Post‐Translational Control of TaFT1 by WAPO1 Ubiquitination Shapes Spike Architecture and Yield in Wheat
Authors: Yujie Jiang, Xiongtao Li, Yiqing Wang, Haoran Wang, Zhaoyan Chen, Chaoqun Dong, Xiangqing Liu, Xincheng Wang, Sufang Li, Weilong Guo, Yingyin Yao, Mingming Xin, Xingguo Ye, Zhongfu Ni, Qixin Sun, Jie Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe florigen protein TaFT1 coordinately regulates heading time and spikelet number per spike (SNS), serving as a key yield determinant in wheat. However, how its stability is post‐translationally controlled in the shoot apical meristem remains unclear. Here, we identify the F‐box protein WHEAT ORTHOLOG OF APO1 (WAPO1), allelic to a major SNS quantitative trait locus (QSns.cau‐7A), as a direct ubiquitin E3 ligase targeting TaFT1 for degradation. A crucial missense mutation (C47F) in the F‐box domain of WAPO1 has a significant impact on the SNS. The elite allele WAPO‐A1b (from large‐spike germplasm AS420, encoding 47F) exhibits stronger binding affinity and ubiquitination activity toward TaFT1 compared with the allele WAPO‐A1f (from cultivar Lunxuan987, encoding 47C). Enhanced degradation of TaFT1 by WAPO‐A1b in the shoot apical meristem impairs the TaFT1–TaFDL transcriptional complex, thereby downregulating the floral identity gene VRN1/WAP1 and increasing SNS without delaying heading. Notably, the favourable WAPO‐A1b allele has been positively selected in modern breeding, and its ectopic activation significantly boosts grain yield in field trials. Our work elucidates a post‐translational pathway that fine‐tunes spike architecture and highlights WAPO‐A1b as a valuable genetic target for high‐yield wheat breeding.

Introduction

Wheat ( Triticum aestivum L.) stands as one of the world's most vital cereal crops, contributing approximately 20% to global grain production and serving as a primary food source for billions of people (Ding et al. 2022). With the global population continuing to expand, sustained increases in wheat yield remain an urgent agricultural priority (Glover et al. 2010; Jones and Engleson 2010). Grain yield in wheat is determined by several key components: spike number per unit area, grain number per spike (GNS), and thousand‐grain weight. Among these, GNS are established early in reproductive development and are highly heritable, coupled with the fact that the floret fertility determining GNS is highly sensitive to heat stress during the critical pre‐flowering period, makes GNS a promising target for genetic improvement (Liu et al. 2022; Zhang, Gizaw, et al. 2018; Zhao et al. 2023).

GNS is governed by two principal components: spikelet number per spike (SNS) and floret fertility. Recent advances have identified numerous genetic regulators of spike development, which can be broadly categorized into three hierarchical classes (Luo et al. 2023). The first class comprises genes involved in sensing environmental cues, such as Photoperiod‐1 (Ppd‐1), Vernalization 1 (VRN1, also called WAP1), TaFT1 (a florigen gene and homologue of Arabidopsis thaliana FLOWERING LOCUS T), also known as VRN3, and TaFDL2 (Boden et al. 2015; Li and Dubcovsky 2008; Li et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2025; Zhou et al. 2025). These genes coordinate the timing of the floral transition, thereby influencing the duration of spikelet initiation and final SNS. The second class includes genes that directly regulate spikelet meristem identity and initiation, such as VRN1, AGAMOUS‐LIKE6 (TaAGL6), FRIZZY PANICLE (WFZP), DUO‐B1, SQUAMOSA PROMOTER BINDING PROTEIN‐LIKE 3/17 (TaSPL3/17), and key microRNAs like miR156 and miR172 through modifying their target genes AP2 and SPL (Debernardi et al. 2022; Du et al. 2021; Li et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2017, 2023; Shen and Wang 2022; Wang et al. 2022). The third class consists of transcription factors that modulate floret development and fertility, including SHORT VEGETATIVE PHASE (SVP) MADS‐box gene TaVRT2, the AP2/ERF transcription factor gene Q, and the homeodomain leucine zipper gene Grain Number Increase 1 (GNI1) (Greenwood et al. 2017; Luo et al. 2024; Sakuma et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2025).

Within this regulatory network, TaFT1 serves as a central integrator of vernalization and photoperiod signals, synchronizing heading date with the determination of SNS. TaFT1 expression is activated by the Ppd‐1–CO1/2 module in response to inductive photoperiods and is derepressed upon vernalization, promoting the expression of VRN1/WAP1 to drive the vegetative‐to‐reproductive transition (Shaw et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2002). Notably, mutations in TaFT1 often lead to delayed heading and increased SNS, as observed in a frameshift mutation in TaFT‐D1 (Chen et al. 2022) and deletion of TaFT‐B1 (Finnegan et al. 2018), suggesting a trade‐off between heading date and SNS. However, it is important to distinguish between heading date (the time of spike emergence from the flag leaf sheath) and inflorescence development (including spikelet initiation and determinacy). Although heading date and inflorescence development are temporally coupled in many genetic backgrounds, they are separable traits. In Arabidopsis, the FT protein acts as a mobile florigen, moving from leaves to the shoot apical meristem (SAM) where it interacts with the bZIP transcription factor FD to activate downstream targets (Corbesier et al. 2007). This mechanism is largely conserved in wheat, where TaFT1 interacts with TaFDL2 (Li and Dubcovsky 2008; Zhang et al. 2025). However, while transcriptional regulation of TaFT1 is well‐studied, how TaFT1 protein activity is post‐translationally modulated within the SAM—and whether such modulation contributes to the uncoupling of heading date from SNS—remains poorly understood.

Parallel to the heading date pathway, quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping has identified WAPO1 on chromosome 7AL as a major regulator of SNS. WAPO1, the ortholog of rice ABERRANT PANICLE ORGANIZATION1 (APO1), promotes spikelet meristem identity and determinacy. Functional alleles of WAPO1 enhance SNS by prolonging meristem activity, whereas loss‐of‐function alleles reduce spikelet number (Kuzay et al. 2022, 2019; Paraiso et al. 2024). Phylogenetically, WAPO1 belongs to a conserved protein family that includes rice ( Oryza sativa L.) APO1 and Arabidopsis UNUSUAL FLORAL ORGANS (UFO), which often exhibit genetic interactions with transcription factors such as LEAFY/APO2 to regulate inflorescence architecture.

Structurally, WAPO1 contains an F‐box domain, its function as a component of the SCF complex, suggesting a potential role in the ubiquitin‐proteasome system (Kuzay et al. 2019). The ubiquitin‐proteasome system is a key regulatory mechanism for targeted protein degradation, involving E1 (ubiquitin‐activating), E2 (ubiquitin‐conjugating), and E3 (ubiquitin‐ligating) enzymes. E3 ligases, particularly the SCF (SKP1–CUL1–F‐box) complex, determine substrate specificity via highly variable F‐box proteins (Dibus et al. 2024; Feldman et al. 1997; Skowyra et al. 1997). In plants, F‐box proteins regulate diverse processes, including hormone signalling, development, and heading date. The presence of an F‐box domain in WAPO1 raises the intriguing possibility that it may function as a substrate recognition component within an SCF complex, potentially targeting key developmental regulators for degradation (Kuzay et al. 2022, 2019).

In this study, we identified a major, environmentally stable quantitative trait locus (QTL) for SNS, QSns.cau‐7A in wheat, using a recombinant line (RIL) population derived from AS420 (large‐spike line) × Lunxuan987 (LX987, modern cultivar). Through map‐based cloning, we confirmed WAPO‐A1 as the causal gene and identified a functional G‐to‐T (G140T) polymorphism causing a C47F substitution in the F‐box domain as the key determinant of phenotypic variation. We further demonstrate that WAPO1 acts as an F‐box protein that physically interacts with TaFT1, promoting its ubiquitination and degradation in an allele‐specific manner. This post‐translational regulation attenuates TaFT1–TaFDL complex formation and suppresses WAP1 expression in the SAM, thereby increasing SNS without delaying heading. Field evaluations confirmed that the AS420‐derived WAPO‐A1b allele significantly improves grain yield, highlighting its potential for breeding high‐yield wheat varieties.

Results

Map‐Based Cloning of QSns.Cau‐7A Reveals WAPO‐A1 as the SNS‐Regulating Gene

To identify QTLs regulating SNS in wheat, we developed a RIL population consisting of 237 F6 individuals derived from a cross between the high‐SNS germplasm AS420 (27.7 ± 1.2) and the cultivated variety LX987 (20.3 ± 1.2). In our previous work, we performed QTL mapping by constructing a genetic linkage map (Jiang et al. 2025). QTL mapping revealed a major, environmentally stable locus, QSns.cau‐7A, on chromosome 7AL, accounting for 7.33% of the phenotypic variation in SNS (Figure 1A; Table S1). The QTL was initially delimited to a 79.3–86.6 cM genetic interval, corresponding to a 652.3–675.5 Mb physical region in the Chinese Spring (CS) reference genome (IWGSC RefSeq v1.0/2.1).

To further refine the locus, we developed heterogeneous inbred families using flanking markers and identified six critical recombinants. Fine‐mapping with 1395 segregating progenies narrowed the interval to a 225.4 kb region (markers 7A‐3892 to 7A‐2314), which harboured seven annotated genes, including the well‐defined WAPO‐A1 (TraesCS7A02G481600, hereafter WAPO1) (Figures S1 and S2; Table S2). This finding aligns with prior studies implicating WAPO1 as the causal gene for SNS QTLs on 7AL (Kuzay et al. 2019; Muqaddasi et al. 2019; Voss‐Fels et al. 2019), strongly supporting WAPO1 as the candidate gene underlying QSns.cau‐7A.

Allelic Variation in WAPO1 Associates With SNS and Pleiotropic Effects

Resequencing of WAPO1 in the parental lines revealed two distinct haplotypes: LX987 allele (WAPO‐A1f) carries a 115‐bp promoter deletion and multiple SNPs relative to AS420 allele (WAPO‐A1b); WAPO‐A1b exhibits a G‐to‐T transversion (G140T) in the coding region, resulting in a cysteine‐to‐phenylalanine substitution at position 47 (C47F) within the conserved F‐box motif (Figures S3 and S4; Table S3). This allele same as previously reported (Figure 1B) (Kuzay et al. 2019; Muqaddasi et al. 2019; Voss‐Fels et al. 2019).

Previous studies proposed that the 115‐bp promoter indel in WAPO1 might regulate its expression, while the C47F substitution could alter protein function, collectively affecting SNS (Kuzay et al. 2022). To test this, we analysed WAPO1 expression in young spikes of parental lines and near‐isogenic lines (NILs). No significant expression differences were detected (Figure S5), suggesting that the promoter polymorphism may not underlie the QSns.cau‐7A effect; further verification is needed through relevant genetic materials. We therefore focused on the functional consequences of the C47F substitution. Based on this SNP, we developed the corresponding marker, named WAPO1‐marker (Table S9). Strikingly, RILs segregated by the G140T polymorphism (encoding C47F) exhibited significant differences in SNS across all environments (Figure 1C), confirming its tight association with QSns.cau‐7A. The G140T variant also consistently influenced plant height and spike length (Figure 1C), demonstrating pleiotropic effects of this QTL on agronomic traits.

WAPO1 Physically Interacts With TaFT1 With Allele‐Specific Affinity

Given that the G140T variant results in the C47F amino acid substitution within the F‐box domain of WAPO‐A1 (hereafter referred to as WAPO1), we hypothesized that this mutation might affect WAPO1's function as an F‐box protein. We found that the C47F amino acid substitution did not affect the subcellular localization of the protein (Figure S6). To investigate this possibility and identify potential protein partners of WAPO1, we conducted a comprehensive yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) screening assay using WAPO‐A1b (the AS420‐derived protein form) as bait to screen a cDNA library prepared from young wheat spike tissues. This screening identified several potential interacting proteins, including the 26S proteasome regulatory subunit, histone acetyltransferase, and DEAD/DEAH box helicase (Table S4). Most notably, the screening revealed an interaction between WAPO‐A1b and TaFT1, a well‐documented key regulator that plays crucial roles in both controlling heading date and influencing SNS in wheat (Chen et al. 2022; Yan et al. 2006). This WAPO‐A1b‐TaFT1 interaction was validated through three complementary approaches: bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) revealed nuclear‐localized interaction between WAPO‐A1b and TaFT1 (Figure 2A); split‐luciferase (LUC) complementation imaging (LCI) confirmed their physical association (Figure 2B); and co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) demonstrated in vivo complex formation (Figure 2C).

We further examined whether the natural C47F variation affects this WAPO1‐TaFT1 association. Indeed, comparative LCI assays showed significantly stronger WAPO‐A1b‐TaFT1 interaction signals compared to WAPO‐A1f‐TaFT1 (Figure 2D). This differential affinity was corroborated by Co‐IP experiments, where WAPO‐A1b‐GFP pulled down substantially more TaFT1‐Flag proteins than WAPO‐A1f‐GFP (Figure 2E), indicating that the C47F substitution influences its interaction with TaFT1, with WAPO‐A1b exhibiting stronger binding affinity than WAPO‐A1f. Together, we conclude that WAPO1 physically interacts with TaFT1 in the nucleus with allele‐specific (WAPO‐A1b and WAPO‐A1f) binding affinity.

WAPO1 Promotes TaFT1 Degradation in an Allele‐Specific Manner

As an F‐box protein, WAPO1 likely functions as a component of the SCF (SKP1‐CUL1‐F‐box protein) E3 ubiquitin ligase complex to mediate substrate ubiquitination, thereby regulating the stability of target proteins (Chen et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2025). Given that WAPO‐A1b exhibits stronger interaction with TaFT1 than WAPO‐A1f (as shown above), we focused on WAPO‐A1b for subsequent functional analyses. To test whether WAPO1 regulates TaFT1 protein stability, we performed LUC activity assays using recombinant TaFT1‐LUC as a reporter. Results showed that co‐expression of WAPO‐A1b‐GFP with TaFT1‐LUC in Nicotiana benthamiana resulted in significantly lower LUC activity compared to control samples expressing GFP and FT‐LUC (Figure 2F), indicating that WAPO‐A1b reduces FT protein stability. This finding was further confirmed by protein degradation assays, where WAPO‐A1b‐GFP co‐expression led to a marked decrease in TaFT1‐MYC protein accumulation relative to the Flag‐expressing empty vector control (Figure 2G). Notably, this protein reduction was attenuated by the addition of MG132, a proteasome inhibitor (Figure 2G).

To further validate the role of WAPO1 in mediating TaFT1 protein degradation in wheat, we generated two types of genetically modified materials, that is, WAPO‐A1b‐overexpressing (OE‐WAPO‐A1b) and WAPO1‐knockout mutant (wapo1) lines (Figure S7). First, recombinant GST‐TaFT1 protein expressed in E. coli was incubated with protein extracts from either OE‐WAPO‐A1b or wapo1, and its degradation was monitored over time. GST‐TaFT1 was degraded more rapidly in OE‐WAPO‐A1b extracts compared with wapo1 extracts, indicating enhanced stability of TaFT1 in the absence of WAPO1 (Figure S8A). We next quantified in vivo TaFT1 protein accumulation in these lines using an anti‐Hd3a antibody that cross‐reacts with wheat TaFT1. Compared with wild‐type Fielder (carrying the WAPO‐A1b allele) plants, TaFT1 levels were markedly altered in both transgenic lines. In OE‐WAPO‐A1b plants, TaFT1 accumulation was drastically reduced to approximately 10% of wild‐type levels (Figure 3A). Conversely, wapo1 knockout mutants exhibited significantly elevated TaFT1 protein accumulation, reaching about 4.8‐fold higher than in wild‐type plants (Figure 3B).

To gain spatial resolution of these effects at the subcellular level, we conducted immunogold labeling experiments followed by electron microscopy analysis. The results showed that TaFT1 proteins were distributed throughout the cell in all genotypes examined. However, quantitative analysis of gold particle distribution revealed significantly higher TaFT1 signals in both nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments of wapo1 mutants compared to wild‐type plants (Figure 3C–F). In contrast, OE‐WAPO‐A1b plants showed the lowest levels of TaFT1 labeling. These in vivo observations provide evidence that WAPO1 plays a crucial role in facilitating TaFT1 protein degradation in wheat cells.

Based on the observed differences in binding affinity between WAPO‐A1b and WAPO‐A1f with TaFT1 (Figure 2D,E), we investigated whether these allelic variants between WAPO‐A1b and WAPO‐A1f may confer different activities in facilitating TaFT1 protein degradation. As expected, degradation assays revealed that while both WAPO‐A1b and WAPO‐A1f promoted TaFT1 turnover, WAPO‐A1b exhibited significantly stronger activity (Figure 2H). This functional difference was further corroborated by LUC reporter assays, where WAPO‐A1b‐GFP expression reduced TaFT1‐LUC activity by 43.83% compared to the 26.01% reduction mediated by WAPO‐A1f‐GFP (Figure 2I). The consistent correlation between binding affinity (Figure 2D,E) and degradation efficiency (Figure 2H,I) strongly suggests that the C47F substitution in WAPO‐A1b enhances its ability to target TaFT1 for proteasomal degradation. These findings establish a direct link between the natural C47F variation and the functional divergence between the two WAPO1 alleles in regulating TaFT1 protein stability.

WAPO1 Mediate TaFT1 Ubiquitination for Degradation

As a member of the F‐box protein family that facilitates TaFT1 degradation, we sought to determine whether WAPO1 functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase to mediate ubiquitination of TaFT1 protein. To address this question, we first established an in vitro ubiquitination assay system to evaluate the self‐ubiquitination activity of recombinant MBP‐WAPO‐A1b protein. Subsequent immunoblot analysis revealed the formation of high‐molecular‐weight ubiquitinated species of MBP‐WAPO‐A1b (Figure 4A). Importantly, parallel control reactions systematically lacking each essential component (E1, E2, or MBP‐WAPO1AS) failed to produce any detectable ubiquitination signal, confirming that the observed ubiquitination was specifically dependent on WAPO‐A1b and the complete ubiquitination machinery. These results support that WAPO‐A1b possesses intrinsic E3 ubiquitin ligase activity in vitro.

To examine whether WAPO1 can mediate ubiquitination of TaFT1, we performed an in vitro ubiquitination assay using purified components. E. coli ‐expressed and affinity‐purified MBP‐WAPO‐A1b was incubated with GST‐TaFT1 fusion protein in the presence of E1, E2, HA‐Ub and ATP. Immunoblot analysis with anti‐HA antibody demonstrated robust ubiquitination of GST‐TaFT1 only when all reaction components were present (Figure 4B; Figure S8B). The specificity of this reaction was confirmed by control experiments showing that omission of either E1, E2 or replacement of MBP‐WAPO‐A1b with MBP alone completely abolished TaFT1 ubiquitination.

To extend these in vitro findings to a plant system, we employed a transient expression assay in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves, and co‐expressed WAPO‐A1b‐mCherry with TaFT1‐GFP, followed by treatment with the 26S proteasome inhibitor MG132 (50 μM for 6 h). Immunoprecipitation of TaFT1‐GFP followed by immunoblotting with anti‐ubiquitin antibody showed significantly enhanced ubiquitination signals in the presence of WAPO‐A1b‐mCherry compared to the mCherry control (Figure 4C). Together, these biochemical and in planta analyses establish that WAPO‐A1b functions as a bona fide E3 ubiquitin ligase that specifically mediates ubiquitination of TaFT1 protein.

WAPO1 Mediates TaFT1 Ubiquitination at the C41 and K44 Sites

To identify the ubiquitination sites on TaFT1 targeted by WAPO1, we combined computational prediction with experimental validation. Initial bioinformatics analysis using MusiteDeep (https://www.musite.net/) predicted Lysine 44 (K44) as a potential ubiquitination site (Figure S9A). We further conducted immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry (IP‐MS) of TaFT1‐GFP transiently expressed in N. benthamiana, which identified an additional ubiquitination site at Cysteine 41 (C41) (Figure S9B). To functionally characterize these sites, we generated a series of TaFT1 mutants by substituting C41 and/or K44 with arginine (R), including single mutants (GST‐TaFT1C41R and GST‐TaFT1K44R) and a double mutant (GST‐TaFT1C41R K44R). In vitro ubiquitination assays revealed that both single mutants showed significantly reduced polyubiquitination levels (42.65% and 37% reduction, respectively) compared to wild‐type GST‐TaFT1 when incubated with MBP‐WAPO‐A1b. Notably, the double mutant exhibited the most dramatic effect, with approximately 50% reduction in ubiquitination (Figure 4D). These findings were corroborated by the in vivo experiments where transient expression in N. benthamiana demonstrated that GFP‐TaFT1 underwent robust ubiquitination in the presence of WAPO‐A1b‐mCherry, while the single mutants (GFP‐TaFT1C41R and GFP‐TaFT1K44R) showed intermediate ubiquitination levels, and the double mutant (GFP‐TaFT1C41R K44R) displayed minimal ubiquitination (Figure 4E).

Protein stability assays provided further evidence for the functional importance of these ubiquitination sites. The results showed that both the single and double mutants of GFP‐TaFT1 (GFP‐TaFT1C41R, GFP‐TaFT1K44R, and GFP‐TaFT1C41R K44R) exhibited higher protein accumulation levels than the wild‐type, with the double mutant form showing the highest accumulation level (Figure 4F), demonstrating that both C41 and K44 serve as authentic ubiquitination sites for WAPO1‐mediated modification, functioning in an additive manner to regulate TaFT1 protein stability. The identification of these specific modification sites provides crucial molecular insights into the mechanism by which WAPO1 controls TaFT1 protein turnover and SNS regulation.

WAPO‐A1b Attenuates VRN1/WAP1 Expression by Impairing TaFT1–TaFDL Complex Formation

Previous research has shown that TaFT1 interacts with the bZIP transcription factor FDL (wheat homologue of Arabidopsis FLOWERING LOCUS D) to form a complex that upregulates floral meristem identity genes such as VRN1/WAP1. This activation accelerates heading and reduces spikelet number (SNS) (Li et al. 2025; Taoka et al. 2011; Yan et al. 2003). We hypothesized that WAPO1‐mediated degradation of TaFT1 could disrupt TaFT1–TaFDL complex formation, thereby suppressing VRN1/WAP1 activation. To test this hypothesis, we first examined whether WAPO1AS affects the TaFT1–TaFDL interaction using luciferase complementation imaging (LCI). Co‐expression of WAPO‐A1b‐GFP markedly reduced luminescence signals generated by cLUC‐TaFT1 and TaFDL‐nLUC interaction compared to the GFP control (Figure 5A), indicating a substantial weakening of the protein–protein interaction.

We next assessed the transcriptional consequences of this disruption using a VRN1/WAP1 pro :LUC reporter construct driven by the native promoter of VRN1/WAP1. As expected, co‐expression of TaFT1‐GFP and TaFDL‐Flag strongly induced LUC activity, confirming TaFT1/TaFDL‐dependent transactivation of VRN1/WAP1 expression (Figure 5B). However, simultaneous expression of WAPO‐A1b‐BFP with TaFT1‐GFP and TaFDL‐Flag significantly suppressed LUC activity to baseline or lower levels (Figure 5B). These transient assays collectively demonstrate that WAPO1‐mediated TaFT1 degradation impairs TaFT1–TaFDL complex formation and subsequent VRN1/WAP1 transactivation.

To determine the physiological relevance of these findings, we analysed VRN1/WAP1 mRNA accumulation in young spike primordia using in situ hybridization. Two genetic backgrounds–AS420 and LX987–and a pair of near‐isogenic lines (NILs) contrasting in WAPO1 alleles (NIL‐A: WAPO‐A1b; NIL‐B: WAPO‐A1f) were examined. Strong VRN1/WAP1 signals were detected in spike tissues of LX987 and NIL‐B, whereas significantly weaker signals were observed in AS420 and NIL‐A1b (Figure 5C,D). These in vivo results confirm that the WAPO‐A1b allele suppresses VRN1/WAP1 expression in developing wheat spikes, likely through enhanced TaFT1 degradation.

Increased WAPO1 Expression Enhances Wheat Grain Yield Under Field Conditions

Given that the WAPO‐A1b allele promotes TaFT1 degradation and increases spikelet number per spike (SNS) without delaying heading date, we evaluated the agronomic and yield performance of WAPO‐A1b overexpression lines (OE‐WAPO‐A1b) under field conditions (Shangzhuang Experimental Station, China Agricultural University, Beijing, China; 40° N, 116° E). Phenotypic analysis showed that all OE‐WAPO‐A1b transgenic plants exhibited significantly higher SNS, longer spike length, more grains per spike, longer grain length, increased grain width, and greater grain weight per spike compared to the wild‐type Fielder. In contrast to the OE‐WAPO‐A1b lines, two mutant lines showed significantly reduced yield performance, including decreased SNS, shorter spike length, fewer grains per spike, lower thousand‐grain weight (TGW), and reduced grain weight per spike (Figure 6E–I; Figure S10). Consistent with these phenotypic differences, OE‐WAPO‐A1b lines produced significantly higher plot grain yield, whereas the wapo1 mutants yielded significantly less than the wild‐type Fielder (Figure 6I). These results demonstrate that the WAPO‐A1b allele effectively improves wheat yield under field conditions and represents a promising genetic resource for wheat high‐yield breeding.

The Use Potential of Natural WAPO‐A1b Allele in Breeding High‐Yield Wheat Variety

To further explore the evolution and distribution of the WAPO‐A1b and WAPO‐A1f alleles, we developed a diagnostic gene marker to distinguish the G140T sequence variation in the coding region of WAPO1. Using this marker, we screened a diversity panel of wheat accessions, including 28 T. urartu and T. monococcum materials (AA), 49 tetraploid wheat varieties (AABB) and 215 bread wheat varieties (AABBDD) (Figure 7A; Table S5). Results showed that WAPO‐A1b allele appeared in the diploid AA genome at the earliest, and then gradually increased its utilization rate in tetraploid and hexaploid wheat, suggesting positive selection of this allele by wheat breeders.

To determine the distribution of the WAPO‐A1b and WAPO‐A1f alleles in relation to the origin of geographic area, a total of 1568 wheat accessions with known origin were used for analysis (http://wheat.cau.edu.cn/WheatUnion/). Interestingly, we found that WAPO‐A1b was more frequent among accessions from Asia. Utilizing the resequencing data of 268 China micro‐core germplasms, WAPO‐A1b was the prevalent allele in the Qinghai‐Tibetan Plateau Spring–Winter Wheat Zone, Southern Autumn‐Sown Spring Wheat Zone, and Northeastern Spring Wheat Zone (Figure 7B,C; Tables S6 and S7) (Li et al. 2022). These results suggest that the WAPO‐A1b allele may be selectively utilized in long‐day areas.

To investigate the implications of our findings on wheat breeding, we analysed the effects of the two alleles in a diversity panel of 339 accessions (Figure S11; Table S8). As expected, the accessions with the WAPO‐A1b allele had more spikelet number and spike length. These results indicated that WAPO1 was conserved in the hexaploid wheat and that the WAPO‐A1b allele was effective for spike‐related traits in different genetic backgrounds; it could be used to enhance grain yield in a diverse array of genetic backgrounds and environments.

In addition, we evaluated the yield traits of the NIL‐A1b (WAPO‐A1b allele) and NIL‐A1f (WAPO‐A1f allele) in field trials, and showed that NIL‐A1b plants had grain weight increases of 11.38% per spike and increases of 4.8% in grain yield per plot compared to the NIL‐A1f plants, thereby further suggesting that the rational utilization of WAPO‐A1b allele will contribute to the improvement of final grain yields in wheat (Figure 7D,E).

Discussion

SNS is a pivotal determinant of GNS and wheat yield potential. While recent studies have highlighted the importance of the ubiquitin‐proteasome pathway in regulating agronomic traits (Hao et al. 2021; Huang et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2021), the specific components and mechanisms governing wheat spike development remain largely unexplored. Here, we demonstrate that the F‐box protein WAPO1 positively regulates SNS by orchestrating ubiquitin‐dependent degradation of TaFT1 in developing spikes. Crucially, we identify a natural amino acid substitution (C47F) within the F‐box domain that enhances WAPO1's binding affinity for TaFT1 and its efficacy in mediating TaFT1 turnover, thereby revealing a previously unknown mechanism for fine‐tuning spike architecture.

Our findings firmly establish WAPO1 as a key quantitative trait locus for SNS, corroborating prior genetic studies (Kuzay et al. 2022, 2019; Muqaddasi et al. 2019). More importantly, we extend these observations by providing direct biochemical evidence that WAPO1 functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, with the C47F natural variation modulating its functional capacity. Different from previous hypotheses implicating promoter polymorphisms as the causal determinant, our data demonstrate that the C47F amino acid substitution in the coding region is also a causal variant underlying the QSns.cau‐7A effect. This allelic variation enhances WAPO1's interaction with TaFT1, leading to more efficient TaFT1 ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation. Given the technical challenges of performing in planta protein stability assays in wheat, at current stage, we cannot formally exclude the possibility that this allele may also modulate the activity of WAPO1 as a component of the SCF complex, potentially altering the ubiquitination rate of its target proteins such as TaFT1 (Zhang, Yin, et al. 2018). The allele‐specific functionality provides a molecular explanation for observed SNS differences and explains why the WAPO1AS (47F) allele has undergone positive selection in wheat breeding, particularly in long‐day environments. Although the C47F variant explains the SNS phenotypic divergence between the parental lines, and our tissue‐ and stage‐specific RT‐PCR and promoter activation assay data collectively support C47F as the causal variant, the potential contribution of promoter region variants to the phenotype requires further functional characterization. It's reasonable to assume that promoter‐driven transcriptional alterations and the altered protein identity conferred by the C47F substitution may function additively to underlie the full genetic effect of QSns.cau‐7A. Further studies using independent allelic separation or gene editing are needed to test these mechanisms.

Cysteine (C) residues represent a well‐documented class of non‐canonical ubiquitination sites in eukaryotes, where ubiquitin is covalently attached via a labile thioester bond between the C‐terminal glycine of ubiquitin and the thiol group of cysteine (Kelsall 2022; Tait et al. 2007). Extensive studies have elucidated the molecular mechanisms and biological functions of cysteine ubiquitination in diverse eukaryotic systems (McClellan et al. 2019). However, the prevalence and functional significance of this non‐canonical modification remain largely unexplored in wheat. In this study, we demonstrate that WAPO1 mediates the ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation of TaFT1, and we identify two putative ubiquitination sites on TaFT1: the canonical lysine residue K44 and the non‐canonical cysteine residue C41. These findings provide novel evidence for the mechanistic dissection of non‐canonical ubiquitination in wheat. Future studies will be required to elucidate the precise molecular mechanism underlying WAPO1‐mediated TaFT1 ubiquitination.

From an evolutionary perspective, WAPO1 belongs to a conserved F‐box protein family with well‐characterized orthologs. In rice, ABERRANT PANICLE ORGANIZATION1 (APO1) regulates panicle architecture, while in Arabidopsis, UNUSUAL FLORAL ORGANS (UFO) controls floral meristem identity. Previous studies showed that UFO physically interacts with LEAFY (LFY), and this interaction is conserved in the rice APO1‐APO2 system. Similarly, wheat WAPO1 interacts with LFY homologues, and loss‐of‐function mutations in both genes cause synergistic floral defects and reduced SNS (Paraiso et al. 2024). However, the molecular mechanism underlying APO1/UFO function has remained a long‐standing enigma. While APO1 has been shown to interact with SKP1 homologues to form putative SCF complexes, no direct biochemical evidence for its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity or substrates has been reported to date (Huang et al. 2021). In Arabidopsis, the prevailing hypothesis that UFO acts as an SCF E3 ligase to ubiquitinate LFY was recently overturned by structural and functional studies demonstrating that UFO primarily functions as a transcriptional cofactor rather than a ubiquitin ligase, with its F‐box domain being largely dispensable for its core role in flower development (Rieu, Turchi, et al. 2023). Our study reveals a mechanism in wheat where natural variation in WAPO1's F‐box domain has been co‐opted to regulate SNS through a previously unrecognized pathway: direct ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of TaFT1, rather than exclusively through the conserved APO1‐APO2/LFY pathway. This finding represents direct biochemical evidence that a member of this conserved F‐box protein family functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase (Rieu, Arnoux‐Courseaux, et al. 2023). We still have the following questions: Does wheat contain a similar LFY like that in rice, which interacts with WAPO1 to jointly regulate the development of the young spike and SNS? Additionally, in rice and Arabidopsis, is there a similar situation where WAPO1 regulates the ubiquitination of TaFT1? These are important topics for further investigation.

Parallel to research on WAPO1, the florigen FT has been extensively studied as a key florigen that integrates flowering signals. Synthesized in leaf vascular tissues, FT protein moves to the shoot apical meristem where it interacts with the bZIP transcription factor FD to form a complex that activates downstream floral identity genes, including VRN1/WAP1, thereby promoting the transition to flowering (Abe et al. 2005; Li and Dubcovsky 2008; Taoka et al. 2011). While this core FT‐FD module is well established in model plants, the post‐translational regulation of FT protein, particularly its stability and turnover dynamics at the SAM during critical developmental windows, represents a crucial but less understood aspect of SNS control. Our study in wheat reveals a sophisticated mechanism whereby WAPO1‐mediated TaFT1 degradation in SAM young spikes attenuates the formation of the TaFT1‐TaFDL transcriptional activation complex, consequently suppressing VRN1/WAP1 expression. The difference in TaFT1 protein abundance between WAPO1 overexpression and knockout young spike meristem suggests that the regulation of TaFT1 is a critical checkpoint that regulates spike development without delaying the heading date, thereby increasing SNS.

Field trials demonstrated that the WAPO‐A1b allele increases SNS, spike length, and grain yield, while loss‐of‐function mutations have opposing effects. However, potential negative effects of the WAPO‐A1b allele—such as alterations in tiller number or trade‐offs between grain number and grain weight—should be further evaluated across different genetic backgrounds (e.g., spring vs. winter wheat). Additional field‐based phenotypic characterization is necessary to address these possibilities. The widespread distribution of WAPO‐A1b across diverse wheat‐growing regions, particularly its enrichment in long‐day environments, underscores its adaptive value. The development of a functional marker for the G140T (C47F) polymorphism will facilitate efficient marker‐assisted selection for this valuable allele.

In conclusion, our work delineates a complete molecular pathway from genetic variation to agricultural trait. As summarized in the model (Figure S12), TaFT1 protein synthesized in leaves moves via the vasculature to young spike primordia, where its abundance is precisely controlled at the post‐translational level. Specifically, WAPO1 directs the ubiquitination of TaFT1 at residues C41 and K44, marking it for recognition and degradation by the 26S proteasome. This turnover relieves TaFT1‐mediated inhibition of the meristem, thereby extending the window for spikelet initiation and differentiation (Figure S12A). Furthermore, we demonstrate how natural allelic variation modulates this regulatory circuit: the WAPO‐A1b allele (from AS420) encodes a protein with strong affinity for TaFT1, promoting efficient ubiquitination and degradation, which enhances the establishment of a differentiation‐permissive environment in spike primordia, leading to higher spikelet number per spike and increased grain yield; in contrast, the WAPO‐A1f allele (from LX987) encodes a WAPO1 variant with weakened TaFT1 binding, resulting in less efficient TaFT1 turnover, prolonged suppression of spikelet initiation, and consequently lower SNS and yield (Figure S12B). Collectively, these findings provide a mechanistic understanding of how ubiquitination‐mediated protein degradation fine‐tunes wheat spike architecture. They also establish WAPO1 as a valuable target for molecular breeding—through marker‐assisted selection or gene editing—to develop high‐yielding wheat varieties.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Field Experiments

A recombinant inbred line (RIL) population was developed by crossing the large‐spike wheat germplasm AS420 with the normal‐spike cultivar Lunxuan 987 (LX987). The RIL population was advanced to the F6 generation and phenotyped for spikelet number per spike (SNS) across five field environments: E1 (Jize, Hebei, China, 2019–2020), E2 (Jize, Hebei, China, 2020–2021), E3 (Xinxiang, Henan, China, 2020–2021), E4 (Linfen, Shanxi, China, 2020–2021), and E5 (Jize, Hebei, China, 2022–2023). This population was used for initial mapping of a quantitative trait locus (QTL) for SNS.

Fine‐mapping of QSns.cau‐7A was performed using heterogeneous inbred families derived from the RILs. Six critical recombinants were identified based on flanking markers, and progeny testing allowed the candidate interval to be delimited to a 225.4 kb region containing WAPO1, a known regulator of SNS. Recombinant progeny carrying the WAPO1 allele from AS420 (WAPO‐A1b) were selected and backcrossed twice to LX987, followed by one generation of self‐pollination. From the BC2F2 population, two near‐isogenic lines (NILs) homozygous for either the AS420 or LX987 allele of WAPO1 were developed: NIL‐A1b (with WAPO‐A1b) and NIL‐A1f (with WAPO‐A1f). These NILs were used to validate the phenotypic effect of QSns.cau‐7A.

Generation of Transgenic and Mutant Lines

To generate WAPO1‐overexpressing (OE‐WAPO1) transgenic plants, the full‐length coding sequence (CDS) of WAPO‐A1b fused with the GFP tag sequence was cloned into the pMWB110 vector to generate Ubi pro ‐WAPO1‐A‐GFP, which was further transformed into the wheat variety Fielder (carrying the WAPO‐A1b allele) via Agrobacterium tumefaciens –mediated gene transformation (Kumar et al. 2019). The PLC41‐Z2 expression vector was kept by our own laboratory.

The wapo1 mutant plants (lines #1 and #2) were generated by CRISPR/Cas9–based gene editing. The sgRNA target sequences for wapo1‐A/B/D gene editing were designed using the web‐based tool E‐CRISPR Design website (http://www.ecrisp.org/E‐CRISP/) based on the exons of target genes. The sgRNA sequences were introduced into the pBUE411 vector (Xing et al. 2014) via T4 DNA ligase (M0202, New England Biolabs). The resulting constructs were transferred into A. tumefaciens strain EHA105 and transformed into wheat cultivar Fielder by A. tumefaciens –mediated transformation (Kumar et al. 2019).

Trait Assessment

Field trials followed a randomized complete block design with three replications. Each block consisted of two 2‐m‐long rows per line, with 30 seeds sown per row.

For OE‐WAPO1 transgenic plants (OE‐WAPO‐A1b‐#1 and OE‐WAPO‐A1b‐#2), wapo1 mutant plants (wapo1‐#1 and wapo1‐#2), and the wild‐type control Fielder, heading date was recorded as the number of days from sowing to the stage when approximately 50% of spikes had fully emerged from the flag leaf sheath in each genotype.

Plant height (PH), spike length (SL), and spikelet number per spike (SNS) were measured for all RILs, NIL lines, OE‐WAPO1 transgenic plants, wapo1 mutant plants, and Fielder. PH was measured from the soil surface to the spike tip (excluding awns). SL was measured from the rachis base to the apex of the terminal spikelet (excluding awns). SNS was recorded as the total number of spikelets per spike. For all RILs, six representative plants were randomly selected for trait evaluation. For NIL lines, OE‐WAPO1 transgenic plants, wapo1 mutant plants, and Fielder, phenotypic data were collected from the main spike of individual plants.

Grain number per spike, grain weight per spike, thousand grain weight were measured and recorded after harvest for NIL lines, OE‐WAPO1 transgenic plants, wapo1 mutant plants, and Fielder. For each trait, data were collected from 10 spikes of main tillers.

Map‐Based Cloning

A map‐based cloning strategy was employed to delineate the genomic region harbouring the candidate gene. From residual heterozygous lines (RHLs), a total of 1395 individual plants between the flanking markers of QSns.cau‐7A were selected for fine‐mapping. By integrating phenotypic and genotypic data from the recombinants, the QTL interval was narrowed to the region between markers 7A‐3892 and 7A‐2314. According to the RefSeq v1.1 annotation (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/refseq/), this region contains only seven high‐confidence genes.

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertion/deletion (InDel) variants between the two parents were identified based on resequencing data (http://wheat.cau.edu.cn/WheatUnion/). Marker development was performed using the online tool (https://junli.netlify.app/apps/design‐primers‐with‐primer3/) to design SNP and InDel markers. All primers used for map‐based cloning are provided in Table S9.

Total RNA Extraction and Real Time‐PCR (RT‐PCR)

Young spikes were collected for total RNA extraction using TRIzol Reagent (15 596 018, Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA was synthesized from the extracted RNA using HiScript II Q Select RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) (R223‐01, Vazyme Biotech) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR) was performed with Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q712‐02, Vazyme Biotech) on a real‐time PCR system. Each sample was analyzed with at least three biological replicates, and gene expression levels were normalized to the wheat Actin gene. All primers used for qRT‐PCR are listed in Table S9.

In Situ Hybridization

Young spikes were fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde. Subsequent section pretreatment, hybridization, and immunological detection procedures followed previously established methods (Burgess 1995). Gene‐specific RNA probes targeting VRN1/WAP1 were synthesized and digoxigenin‐labelled. The probe sequences are provided in Table S9.

Firefly Luciferase Complementation Imaging (LCI) Assays

The coding region of WAPO‐A1b and FT was separately cloned into the pCambia1300‐nLUC (nLUC) and pCambia1300‐cLUC (cLUC) vectors. The resulting constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 and co‐infiltrated into leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana. Approximately 48 h after infiltration, the LUC signal within the infiltration region was analysed using NightSHADE LB 985 (Berthold Technologies). The primer sequences are listed in Table S9.

Bimolecular Fluorescence Complementation (BiFC) Assay

The coding sequences of WAPO‐A1b and FT were cloned into pUC57‐cYFP and pUC57‐nYFP vectors, respectively. Plasmid combinations were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 and co‐expressed in N. benthamiana leaves. YFP fluorescence was visualized approximately 48 h after infiltration using a confocal microscope (LSM880; Carl Zeiss, Heidenheim, Germany). All primer sequences are provided in Table S9.

Transient Transcriptional Activation Activity Assay

Transcriptional activation activity was assessed in N. benthamiana leaves as previously described (Liu et al. 2017). For reporter construction, the 2‐kb promoter sequences were cloned into the plant binary vector pGWB35 via Gateway recombination (Invitrogen). Each reporter was co‐expressed with the indicated effector protein in N. benthamiana through Agrobacterium‐mediated transient transformation. LUC activity was measured 48 h after infiltration using the NightSHADE LB 985 system. All primer sequences used are listed in Table S9.

Yeast Two‐Hybrid (Y2H) Assay

The coding sequence for the full‐length WAPO‐A1b was amplified and cloned into the pGBKT7 vector to generate pGBKT7‐WAPO‐A1b, which was used as a bait to screen a yeast cDNA library derived from Fielder wheat spike to identify the candidate WAPO‐A1b‐interacting proteins. The Y2H screening was performed according to the user's manual (PT3024‐1, Clontech Laboratories Inc.). Because pGBKT7‐WAPO‐A1b has autoactivation activity, positive transformants were selected on synthetic dropout medium lacking Trp and Leu (−L/−T), while the screening of interactions was performed on SD medium lacking Trp, Leu, His, and adding 10 mm 3‐AT.

Co‐Immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) Assay

The coding sequences of FT and WAPO‐A1b were cloned into the pSuper1300‐GFP and pCAMBIA1300‐211‐Flag vectors, respectively. The resulting constructs were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 and co‐infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. Total protein was extracted from infiltrated leaf tissue using lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA pH 8.0, 0.1% Triton X‐100, 0.2% NP‐40, 0.6 mM PMSF, 1× protease inhibitor cocktail, and 20 μM MG132). After incubation on ice for 30 min, lysates were centrifuged twice at 13000 rpm for 20 min each. The supernatant was incubated with anti‐GFP beads for 2–4 h at 4 C. Immunoprecipitated samples were separated by SDS‐PAGE and analysed by immunoblotting using anti‐GFP (1:5000; TransGen) and anti‐Flag (1:5000; TransGen) antibodies. All primers used are listed in Table S9.

Protein Subcellular Localization

The coding sequences of WAPO‐A1b and WAPO‐A1f were separately cloned into the pSuper1300‐GFP vector to generate C‐terminal GFP fusion constructs. The resulting plasmids were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 and transiently expressed in leaves of 4‐week‐old N. benthamiana plants. GFP fluorescence was observed 48 h after infiltration using a confocal microscope (LSM880, Zeiss). All primers used are listed in Table S9.

In Vivo Quantification of TaFT1 Protein Levels

Young spikes were collected from Fielder, OE‐WAPO‐A1b transgenic, and wapo1 mutant plants, and total proteins were extracted from these samples. The TaFT1 protein levels were detected in vivo by western blotting using an anti‐Hd3a antibody (1:1000 dilution; catalogue no. A20675, Abclonal).

In Vitro Ubiquitination Assay

The in vitro ubiquitination assay was performed as previously described (Nemoto et al. 2017). Recombinant proteins GST‐FT, GST‐FTC41R, GST‐FTK44R, and GST‐FTC41R K44R were expressed and purified from Escherichia coli strain BL21 (DE3). Each GST‐FT variant, either individually or in combination, was incubated with E1 (UBA1, AT2G30110, 50 ng), E2 (UBCH5a, 23–029, Sigma, 50 ng), MBP‐WAPO‐A1b, and HA‐Ubiquitin (HA‐Ub, 1 μg) in reaction buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 5 mM ATP, 20 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT) at 30°C for 2 h. Reactions were terminated by adding SDS loading buffer, followed by boiling for 5 min. Ubiquitinated proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE and detected by immunoblotting using an anti‐HA antibody (1:2000, ABclonal).

In Vivo Ubiquitination Assay

For in vivo analysis, GFP‐FT, GFP‐FTC41R, GFP‐FTK44R, or GFP‐FTC41R K44R were co‐expressed with WAPO‐A1b–mCherry and Flag–Ubiquitin in N. benthamiana leaf epidermal cells. GFP‐FT proteins were immunoprecipitated using anti‐GFP magnetic agarose beads (gtma‐20, Chromotek), and ubiquitin conjugation was analysed by immunoblotting with an anti‐Flag antibody (1:2000, ABclonal).

Protein Degradation Assay in N. benthamiana

For the protein degradation assay, Agrobacterium strains carrying constructs for TaFT1‐MYC alone, TaFT1‐MYC with WAPO‐A1b‐GFP, or TaFT1‐MYC with WAPO‐A1f‐GFP were co‐infiltrated into leaves of N. benthamiana. At 60 h post‐infiltration, the treated leaves were infiltrated with 100 μM cycloheximide (CHX; Sigma, C7698) to inhibit de novo protein synthesis. After an additional 12‐h incubation, total protein was extracted from the leaf tissues using 2× SDS loading buffer (TRIS/HCl 126 mM, glycerol 20%, SDS 4%, bromphenolblue 0.02%, 2‐mercaptoethanol 5%, pH 6.8). The samples were boiled for 5 min and subjected to western blot analysis using an anti‐MYC antibody (ABclonal, 1:2000 dilution).

Phylogenetic and Genetic Diversity Analyses

The protein sequence of WAPO1 was used as a query to identify orthologous sequences from other plant species via the EnsemblPlants database (http://plants.ensembl.org). Multiple sequence alignment was performed using the MUSCLE algorithm. A phylogenetic tree was then constructed with MEGA11 software using the Neighbour‐Joining method, with statistical support assessed by 1000 bootstrap replicates. Gaps and missing data were handled by complete deletion. A panel of diverse wheat accessions (including landraces and modern cultivars) was used to investigate the haplotype variation at the WAPO1 locus. The detailed information for all accessions is provided in Table S3.

KASP Marker Development and Genotyping

Based on the single nucleotide polymorphism (G/T) at the 140th base of WAPO1 between AS420 and LX987, we developed a Kompetitive Allele‐Specific PCR (KASP) marker following the manufacturer's design guidelines (LGC Genomics) (Rasheed et al. 2016). The genotyping assay was performed in a 5 μL reaction volume containing 2 μL of genomic DNA (50–100 ng), 2 μL of 2× KASP master mix (V4.0, LGC Genomics), and 1 μL of primer mix (12 μM of each allele‐specific primer and 30 μM of the common primer). PCR was conducted on an OMEGA F reader (LGC Genomics) using a 384‐well plate format under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 94°C for 10 min; 10 touchdown cycles of 94°C for 20 s and 61°C–55°C (decreasing by 0.6°C per cycle) for 45 s; followed by 30–40 regular cycles of 94°C for 20 s and 55°C for 45 s. Endpoint fluorescence data were collected and analyzed using KlusterCaller software (LGC Genomics) for allele calling. Sequences of molecular markers developed in the study were listed in Table S9.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three biological replicates. Statistical significance of differences between two groups was determined using a two‐tailed Student's t‐test. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0). A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Detailed numerical data and statistical results are provided in Source data.
