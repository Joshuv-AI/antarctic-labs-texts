Gut microbiota remodeling and sensory-emotional functional disruption in adolescents with bipolar depression
Authors: Anying Tang, Yi Chen, Jiahong Ding, Zhiming Li, Chenyue Xu, Shaohua Hu, Jianbo Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdolescence is the peak period of newly-onset bipolar disorder (BD). Accumulating studies have revealed disturbed gut microbiota can interfere with neurodevelopment in adolescents. In this study, we aimed to characterize the gut microbiota in adolescents with BD and its correlation with brain dysfunction.MethodsThirty unmedicated BD adolescents within depressive episode were recruited and underwent four-week quetiapine treatment. Twenty-five age-, gender-, and BMI-matched healthy controls (HCs) were recruited. Fecal samples were collected from HCs and all BD adolescents before and after treatment and analyzed by metagenomic sequencing. Resting-state cranial functional magnetic images were collected from 21 BD adolescents before treatment. Random forest models were used to evaluate the discriminative power of gut microbiota and neuroimaging data for BD and the predictive power of treatment effect.ResultsAlthough no significant difference was found in alpha-diversity, intra- and inter-group differences in beta-diversity were observed among HCs, pre- and post-treatment patients. Compared to HCs, unmedicated BD adolescents presented a differentiated gut microbial communities, which correlated to the short-chain fatty acids, choline, lipids, vitamins, polyamines, aromatic amino acids metabolic pathways. Four-week quetiapine treatment improved the abundance of specific genus, such as Odoribacter splanchnicus, Oribacterium sinus, Hafnia alvei, Fusobacterium periodonticum, Acidaminococcus interstini and Veillonella rogosae. Neuroimaging analysis revealed sensor-emotional brain regions were associated with BD severity. Finally, random forest models based on gut microbial biomarkers can well distinguish unmedicated BD from HCs (AUC = 91.12%) and predict the treatment effect (AUC = 91.84%). The random forest model integrating gut microbiota and neuroimaging data exhibited a better predictive efficacy than using microbiota data alone.ConclusionThis study first characterized the gut microbiota architecture in adolescent BD. Combining gut microbiota and brain function biomarkers may benefit disease diagnosis and predict treatment outcome. Nonetheless, these findings should be carefully interpreted considering the limitations of a modest sample size and the absence of detailed mechanistic explorations. Trial registration NCT05480150. Introduction

Bipolar disorder (BD) is a severe and recurrent mental illness characterized by mood fluctuations, often leading to significant lifelong functional impairment [1]. Adolescence witnesses the first peak period of risk for the disease onset [2]. Previous studies demonstrated that approximately 50 to 70% of patients suffered from mood swings before the age of 21 years [3]. Compared with adult patients with bipolar depression, depressive episodes in adolescents seem to be more complicated and devastating [4]. Moreover, compared to those with unipolar depression, children and adolescents with bipolar depression are more likely to have comorbid conduct disorder, opposive-defiant disorder, agoraphobia, obsessive–compulsive disorder, and alcohol abuse [5]. Even worse, the predominant symptomatology during the initial stage of disease is depressive rather than (hypo-)manic episodes, thereby leading to a delayed diagnosis of BD by 8 to 10 years from the initial onset [6]. Given the high mortality and disability related to BD, it has become a challenging clinical issue in the young patients [7]. Therefore, more attention is needed to uncover the pathological mechanisms and biological markers in adolescents with BD, thereby developing better diagnostic and intervention strategies.

Adolescence is a critical period for human brain and gut ecosystem development, accompanied by significant hormonal, behavioral and social changes [8]. This period is characterized by neuronal reorganizing and remodeling, including extensive synaptic pruning and myelination [9]. It is also marked by the development and dynamic reorganization of limbic structures, including nucleus accumbens [10]. Additionally, neural development during this period is characterized by significant remodeling of neurotransmitter systems [11]. Increased activity in the mesolimbic dopamine pathway, accompanied by alterations in glutamatergic and GABAergic signaling, is closely associated with enhanced reward sensitivity, heightened risk-taking behaviors, and modifications in motivation regulation [12]. These neurodevelopmental features make adolescents vulnerable to environment stimuli, such as infection, drugs, stress, exercise and dietary deficiencies, and increase vulnerability to mood disorders [13]. As a part of host environment, gut microbiota dynamically evolves across the life span, with a balanced gut ecosystem being essential for maintaining physical and mental well-being [14]. Recently, the role of gut microbiota in shaping neurodevelopment during adolescence has been gradually unraveled [8]. In early life, the developing gut microbiota is disturbed and interferes with neurodevelopment through the immune, neuronal, and endocrine pathways [15], and sometimes lead to adverse mental health outcomes (e.g., mood disorders, autism spectrum disorders, attention deficit/hyperactivity disorders) [16–21].In previous studies, we have revealed disease-specific alterations in the diversity, composition and metabolic pathways of commensal bacteria in BD population [22–25], which distinguish those with bipolar depression from healthy subjects [23]. For instance, multiple studies have reported decreased levels of Faecalibacterium, a bacterial genera that produce short-chain fatty acids (SFCAs) [26], alongside increased microbes such as Lachnoclostridium and Eggerthella [27]. Metagenomic analyses have also consistently identified functional perturbations in microbial pathways involved in short-chain fatty acid biosynthesis, GABA metabolism, tryptophan degradation, and neuroactive immunomodulation [28]. More recent studies suggest that these microbial signatures may correlate with mood episode severity, treatment response, and neuroinflammatory markers [29]. However, due to the particularity of the adolescent stage, there is still a lack of studies focusing on the disrupted gut microbiota and dysregulated brain functioning in adolescents with BD.

In this study, we investigated the remodeling of gut microbial communities and functional pathways using shotgun metagenomic sequencing in adolescents with BD before and after quetiapine treatment, and further analyzed brain dysfunction in unmedicated adolescents with BD. Furthermore, we also explored the possibility of combining gut microbiota and brain functional connectivity as a potential tool for diagnosing BD and predicting treatment outcome. This study helps to unravel the underlying mechanisms of the microbiota-gut-brain regulation in young individuals with BD.

Methods

Participant recruitment, assessment, and enrollment

Adolescents with BD and healthy controls (HCs) were recruited from the outpatient clinic or the Department of Psychiatry at the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. This research received approval from the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital at Zhejiang University School of Medicine, as part of the Chinese Longitudinal and Systematic Study of Bipolar Disorder (CLASS-BD, Clinical Trial registration number: NCT05480150). Written informed consent was secured from all participants and their legal guardians before they were enrolled in the study.

The diagnosis of BD-I, BD-II, and BD not otherwise specified (NOS) was confirmed by two trained psychiatrists, following the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV) and Chinese version of the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (M.I.N.I.) through a structured clinical interview. The severity of depression was assessed using the 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-24) [30] and the Montgomery-Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) [31], while anxiety severity was measured by the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA) [32]. In addition, the Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) [33] was applied to evaluate the severity of mania.

All patients had to meet the following criteria for inclusion: (i) a score of HAMD-24 no less than 14; (ii) either drug-naïve or drug-free for at least 3 months; (iii) absence of other psychiatric comorbidities or pronounced suicidal ideation (suicidal thoughts scored ≤ 2 on MADRS) to ensure suitability for standardized pharmacotherapy in the CLASS-BD study. Exclusion criteria included: (i) chronic or severe cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, or other brain-related diseases (e.g., epilepsy or tumors); (ii) chronic or acute inflammatory and autoimmune disorders; (iii) history of substance abuse, such as alcohol or tobacco; (iv) current pregnancy or breastfeeding; (v) recent use of antibiotics, prebiotics, or probiotics within the last 4 weeks before screening; (vi) contraindications for MRI scans (e.g., metal implants); (vii) failure to provide informed consent. HCs without any DSM-IV psychiatric disorder were recruited from local communities and adhered to the same exclusion criteria. Before enrollment, participants were inquired about their dietary habits to exclude those with specific dietary needs, such as food allergies, intolerances, vegetarian diets, or restrictions based on religious or cultural practices. Additionally, other demographic and clinical profiles, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), age of onset, education level, medication history, and treatment outcomes, was collected (Additional file 1). All patients received 4 weeks of quetiapine treatment, which was gradually titrated up to the maintenance dosage of 200–300 mg per day.

Stool sample collection

Fecal samples were collected from all participating adolescents at the baseline. For the patient group, fecal samples were re-examined after four-week quetiapine treatment. They were instructed to pass their fresh stool into a provided collection bowl and give it to a research assistant. The stool was then portioned with a scoop and transferred into a tube, which was promptly snap-frozen on dry ice and stored at − 80 °C within 30 min. As a control, a blank swab was placed into the fecal preservation solution.

Fecal DNA extraction and metagenomic sequencing

Following the manufacturer’s protocol, DNA was extracted from thawed fecal samples using the OMEGA-soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, USA). Specifically, samples were homogenized in the provided SLX-Mlus Buffer (Omega Bio-Tek, USA) containing a lysozyme solution (20 mg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Mechanical disruption was further achieved by bead-beating using a tissue homogenizer (FastPrep-24, MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA, USA) at 6.0 m/s for 40 s. After centrifugation, the supernatant was mixed with Binding Buffer (Omega Bio-Tek, USA) and loaded onto a DNA Mini Column (Omega Bio-Tek, USA). The column was then washed with DNA Wash Buffer (twice) (Omega Bio-Tek, USA) and SPW Wash Buffer (once) (Omega Bio-Tek, USA) to remove impurities. To remove any RNA contamination, the extracts were treated with DNase-free RNase. DNA quality was assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Negative controls (extraction blanks) were included in each batch to monitor for potential contamination during the DNA extraction process. Finally, DNA was eluted in 50 μL of Elution Buffer (Omega Bio-Tek, USA) and stored at − 80 °C until further processing. The DNA library was prepared according to the instructions from Illumina. Using a previously described workflow [34], we built a paired-end library for each sample with a 350 bp insert size, which was followed by high-throughput sequencing with 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads, utilizing the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq kit (BioScientific, Austin, TX, USA). Paired-end sequencing was conducted using the Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Reads of low quality or those originating from human genomic DNA were filtered out [35]. Human genomic DNA sequences were identified using SOAP2.21 [36] and eliminated if they exhibited over 95% similarity to the reference human genome sequence (hg38) [35]. After processing, a total of 5054.3 million high-quality reads were obtained, with an average of 59.5 million reads per sample. To identify differentially abundant taxa between patient and HC groups, statistical analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction, with a significance threshold set at adjusted p < 0.05.

Construction of species and KO profiles

High-quality reads were aligned to the gut microbiome genome catalog [37] using bwa (default settings). Contig abundance profiling based on sequence data was performed using jgi_summarize_bam_contig_depths with default parameters [38]. Reads were mapped to the genome catalog, and the resulting read counts were used to build a mapping depth or abundance matrix. To account for variations in sequencing depth across samples, we normalized the mapping depth matrix to estimate contig abundances. For species profiling, we used the species assignment of each contig from the initial genome catalog and calculated the median relative abundance of contigs within the same species to determine species abundance. The functional profile for KEGG Orthology (KO) of each species was derived by summing the relative abundances of contigs associated with each KO term.

Image acquisition

Before treatment, BD patients underwent a 20-min MRI session including structural and functional scans on a 3.0 Tesla GE Signa HDxt scanner (GE Healthcare, Waukesha, Wisconsin, USA), equipped with an eight-channel phased array head coil. They were instructed to keep still and awake with their eyes open during the whole session.

Resting-state functional images with the echo-planar imaging sequence were acquired using the following parameters: TR = 1800 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 90 degrees, voxel size = 3.75 × 3.75 × 4 mm3, field of view = 240 × 240 mm2, 28 axial slices per volume, 180 time points/volumes. High-resolution 3D T1-weighted magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition with gradient echo structural images were also collected for anatomical reference, with parameters of TR = 7.05 ms, TE = 2.85 ms, flip angle = 8 degrees, voxel size = 1 mm3 isotropic, field of view = 240 × 240 mm2.

Data preprocessing and denoising

Both structural and functional images were preprocessed with the CONN-19.c toolbox (http://www.nitrc.org/projects/conn, RRID:SCR_009550) [39], based on SPM 12 (Statistical Parametric Mapping 12; https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/) [40].

The default preprocessing pipeline in CONN was applied, where functional scans were realigned to a reference volume and resampled along the phase-encoded direction to correct for susceptibility distortions, adjusting for head motion and any potential deformations caused by field inhomogeneities. Functional slices, acquired in ascending interleaved order, underwent time-shifting and resampling for slice-timing correction. Given that resting-state functional images are particularly susceptible to head motion artifacts, a more conservative approach was used to identify outlier scans affected by excessive head motion. Scans were marked as outliers if the composite subject motion exceeded a threshold of 0.5 mm or if global blood oxygen level dependent (BOLD) signal changes were observed to be above 3 standard deviations, as identified by the Artifact Detection Tools (ART) [39, 41]. These motion artifact thresholds were selected based on established practices in the resting-state fMRI literature to stringently control for micromovements while balancing the retention of usable data volumes. All anatomical and functional images were normalized to the standard Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space, and then segmented into gray matter, white matter and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) classes. Direct normalization was used to map the functional data, which were interpolated to isotropic 2 mm voxels consistent with the MNI average mask, onto the reference structural data. The resulting functional data were smoothed with a Gaussian kernel of 6 mm full width half maximum to boost the BOLD signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).

Although resting-state functional data had been preprocessed, a large amount of noise was still present, which would introduce biases to functional connectivity (FC) results. To mitigate the influence of these noise components, necessary denoising procedures had been applied. CONN’s anatomical component correction strategy computed the confounding effects of noise components from white matter and CSF, subject-motion parameters, and outlier scans or scrubbing. High-pass filtering (> 0.008 Hz) was applied to the resulting functional data. Denoising parameters were adjusted to better control the functional data quality.

Neuroimaging analyses

Seed-based functional connectivity (SBC) analyses were performed utilizing CONN-19.c toolbox [39, 41]. Selected seeds or regions of interest (ROIs) were 106 bilateral areas from FSL Harvard–Oxford maximum likelihood cortical and subcortical atlases [42–45] and 30 bilateral areas from canonical intrinsic functional network regions [41, 46]. Both whole-brain seed-to-voxel and ROI-to-ROI connectivity analyses were carried out to investigate within-group FC patterns (i.e., computed in Fisher-transformed bivariate correlation coefficients Z by CONN) [41] in terms of HAMD scores.

In the seed-to-voxel analysis, a statistical parametric map corresponding to each seed was created based on Gaussian Random Field theory [47] to display voxel-level FC metrics, which were first filtered through an uncorrected p-value threshold at 0.001 to make cluster-level inferences. FC clusters were subsequently assessed using cluster-level false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted p-values [48]. In terms of the ROI-to-ROI analysis, functional network connectivity (FNC) of selected ROIs was investigated [49] using a multivariate parametric general liner model (GLM). FNC results were evaluated with a cluster-level FDR-corrected p < 0.05 threshold, whereas each connection involved in the network was also scrutinized with a post-hoc uncorrected p < 0.05 threshold [41].

Statistical analysis

Multivariate analysis of gut microbiota structure

Multivariate statistical analyses were employed to distinguish BD patients from healthy controls. Species count calculations followed a previously established method [50], identifying all non-zero species present in each sample. To assess alpha diversity, we calculated the Shannon index based on the gene profile of each sample, using the vegan package in R (version 4.2.2). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted on the subject-level RRC matrices using the ade4 package in R [51].

Co-inertia analysis (CIA) analysis

CIA was applied to the gut microbial abundance profiles across groups to examine the relationships among post-, and pre- states.

Hypothesis testing and multiple testing correction

The differential abundance of gut microbial composition and function between BD patients and HCs was evaluated using a two-tailed Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Functional connectivity strength in rs-fMRI was compared between HCs and BD patients via Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). FDR correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg method [52] through the p.adjust function in R, with local FDR values reported in the article.

Multiple regression (performed with GLM)

This regression model investigated the relationship between pre-treatment HAMD scores (assumed to reflect the symptomatic severity in patients) and FC levels, regressing out potential confounders of age and gender. Resulting statistics would be used to implicate whether there were robust FC alterations and networks associated with the severity of bipolar symptoms.

Random forest classification models

Following a previously established method [53], random forest package (version 4.6–12) was used to build models based on species compositional profiles and MRI data between cohorts. The leave-one-out cross-validation method was employed, treating each sample as an independent validation set while the remaining samples served as the training set. This cross-validation approach was consistently applied throughout all stages of the random forest modeling process. Finally, the mean result was used to as the final training results. The ROC curve (R 3.3.0, pROC package) was used to evaluate the random forest classification models, and the ROC efficiency was assessed by area under the curve (AUC).

Gut microbiota diversity changes between BD adolescents and HCs

Our metagenome shotgun sequencing results showed that no significant difference of alpha diversity was found across the three groups (30 pre-treatment patients, 21 post-treatment patients, 25 healthy adolescents) as assessed by the Shannon index (Fig. 1A). Beta diversity showed significant intra- and inter-group differences among HCs, pre- and post-treatment patients (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01) (Fig. 1B). The intra-beta diversity in pre-treatment patients was significantly higher than the HCs group and converged significantly after treatment. The inter-difference between the samples before and after treatment was smaller than their differences with the HCs group. Specific gut bacteria and annotation function distinguished pre-treatment BD adolescents and HCs

Between the HCs and pre-treatment BD adolescents, we differentiated their gut microbiota at the phylum, genus, and species levels (Fig. 2A-C). At the phylum level, that abundance of Euryarchaeota, Candidatus Saccharibacteria, Fusobacteria was increased in the pre-treatment BD group. At the genus level, there were 22 genera of bacteria enriched in the BD group. At the species level, compared to HCs, a total of 25 species were increased and 9 species were decreased in BD. We further annotated functional categories of specific bacteria group using the KEGG database to find the potential pathways associated with bacteria, and the spearman rank correlation heatmap showed gut microbe-related functional divergence between HCs and pre-treatment BD patients. We identified differential bacterial enrichment with altered functions, including acetyl-CoA, choline, lipid metabolism cofactor and vitamin functions and polyamine-related pathways (Fig. 2D, Additional file 3). Additional changes were observed in pathways related to glucose and amino acid metabolism. The ROC curve illustrated the performance of a random forest model in differentiating unmedicated patients and HCs, based on 35 differential species. The model achieved an AUC of 91.12% with a 95% confidence interval (CI) ranging from 81.97 to 100% (Fig. 2E). Specific gut bacteria distinguished pre-treatment and post-treatment BD adolescents

We further explored the pre- and post-treatment gut microbial profiles in BD adolescents. The CIA analysis showed the microbial structure relationship between pre- and post- treatment BD patients (***p < 0.001; RV coefficient:0.76) (Fig. 3A). Specific species with significant abundance changes before and after treatment (e.g., Intestinibacter bartlettii, Clostridiales spp. and Lachnospiraceae spp.) were identified (Additional file 4, A), indicating the impact of 4-week quetiapine treatment on gut microbial composition. The metabolism-related functions of these differential bacteria were also annotated (Additional file 4, B-C). To clarify whether the relative abundance of any species returned to normal levels after treatment, we analyzed the difference of bacterial taxa across HCs, pre-treatment, and post-treatment BD groups (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01) (Fig. 3B). Notable shifts were observed in several taxa, such as Odoribacter splanchnicus, Oribacterium sinus, Hafnia alvei, Fusobacterium periodonticum, Acidaminococcus interstini and Veillonella regosae. The differential metabolism-related functions of these species included Aromatic amino acid metabolism, Cofactor and vitamin biosynthesis, Acetyl-CoA related function, Choline related function, Polyamine biosynthesis and Lipid metabolism (Fig. 3C). The model achieved an AUC of 84.67% (95% CI 73.46–95.87%) when differentiating HCs from pre-treatment patients, and an AUC of 78.56% (95% CI 66.75–90.37%) between pre- and post-treatment groups. Associations of resting-state functional connectivity with symptom severity and gut microbiota

Resting-state cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data was collected from 21 adolescents with BD before treatment. One participant had over 20% of their total scans flagged as invalid, with 133 out of 180 scans meeting the quality control threshold, leading to their exclusion from further analyses. Finally, our neuroimaging analyses included a subset of 20 unmedicated adolescent patients. In regression models controlling for age and gender, resting-state seed-to-voxel analyses, examining all 136 cerebral network and atlas regions at the whole-brain level, reported altered resting-state functional connectivity (RSFC) patterns with different HAMD scores presumably measuring BD severity (Additional file 5).

Consistent with prior work on exploring RSFC in BD [54], reduced levels of FC between ROIs (i.e., precentral [somatosensory cortex] and postcentral [motor cortex] gyrus) involved in the sensorimotor network (SMN) and bilateral insular cortex were found significantly associated with depression severity (Fig. 4A). Another noteworthy seed in this analysis was the fusiform gyrus. Notably, strengthened FC between the orbital frontal cortex and bilateral supracalcarine cortex was detected, and yet most of the functional connections were significantly lower (negative statistics) with increasing HAMD scores. Similarly, changes in RSFC Patterns with BD severity were combined with changes in gut microbial composition to construct a random forest model (Fig. 4D). The results of model showed that the AUC value of the model combining both gut microbiota and MRI data in predicting the treatment effect was 96.3% (95% CI: 88.18–100%), which was 4.46% higher than that of the model using gut microbiota data alone (AUC = 91.84%).

Discussion

Unlike previous studies focusing on adult patients, the present study provided new evidence of altered gut microbiota in adolescents with BD and its change following 4-week quetiapine monotherapy.

Although there was no significant regarding the richness (Shannon index) of gut microbiota in BD adolescents before and after treatment when compared to HCs, PCA analysis showed significant structure changes of gut microbiota in BD adolescents. Several specific gut microbes enriched in pre-treatment adolescents was identified, including Clostridium spiroforme, Ruminococcus gnavus, Bacillius licheniformis, while the abundance of Butyricimonas spp., Faecalibacterium spp., Clostridium spp. decreased. The altered bacterial metabolism-related functional pathways were mainly involved in the SCFAs biosynthesis, choline, lipid metabolism, cofactor and vitamin functions, polyamine-related functions, aromatic amino acid degration and biosynthesis. Our previous report in adult BD patients also revealed gut microbe-related dysregulation in serum metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids, polyamines, B vitamins, kyunolinic acid, and γ-aminobutyric acid [28]. The current findings suggested that BD adolescents shared similar microbe-related metabolic dysfunction with BD adults. Circulating SCFAs (e.g., acetate, propionate, and butyrate) not only associate with blood–brain barrier integrity [55], but also have effects on the brain immune and endocrine systems [56], thereby modulating neuroplasticity and neurotransmitter systems [57]. In addition, vitamin B families have been considered to link to mental well-being [58]. Vitamin B deficiency has been associated with the occurrence of depression and cognitive deficits [59, 60]. In addition, accumulating studies demonstrated that aromatic amino acids metabolism was dysregulated in psychiatric disorders such as depression [61]. Changes in polyamine metabolism (spermidine, spermine, etc.) have been implicated in BD individuals [62, 63]. Notably, dysregulated microbial pathways in lipid metabolism was also observed in unmedicated BD adolescents. In addition to fluctuations in hormone levels (such as sex hormones and cortisol) during the development of puberty [64], BD adolescents often have comorbid with eating and circadian disorders related to their emotional status [65, 66]. These factors along with gut dysbiosis may synergistically may disrupt the lipid metabolism in BD adolescents. Compared with adults, the clinical manifestations of BD adolescents are more complicated and challenging [67]. Targeting the gut microbiota to normalize lipid metabolism could be a potential new avenue for the treatment of BD adolescents.

This study also suggested that four-week quetiapine treatment can remodel gut microbiota, changing the abundance of specific species such as Odoribacter splanchnicus, Oribacterium sinus, Hafnia alvei, Fusobacterium periodonticum, Acidaminococcus interstini, and Veillonella rogosae. Compared to prior studies in adults with BD, our findings in adolescents demonstrate both overlapping and distinct patterns of gut microbial remodeling following a four-week quetiapine treatment. Recently, some research claimed that Hafnia alvei had the ability of glucose-degrading [68] and can reduce food intake and fat mass in obese mice [69, 70]. However, it was also related to lung infections [71] and intestinal inflammation [72]. In a previous study, we found that the abundance of Hafnia alvei also showed increase in adult BD cohort [28]. Notably, our adolescent cohort exhibited more pronounced and species-specific alterations, including a significant increase in Odoribacter splanchnicus, Acidaminococcus, Oribacterium sinuss, Fusobacterium periodonticum and Veillonella rogosae. Odoribacter splanchnicus, known as a producer of SCFAs [73], inhibits the NLRP3-related inflammatory pathway via extracellular vesicles [74] and modulates gut microbiota and intestine inflammation in concert with Bifidobacterium [75]. Its abundance is positively correlated with host serum serotonin levels, suggesting a potential role in mood regulation [76]. The relative abundance of Acidaminococcus was negatively associated with plasma levels of omega-6-derived oxylipins [77], and it was enriched in overweight/obese patients [78]. The Oribacterium sinus, Fusobacterium periodonticum and Veillonella rogosae, which inhabit widely in the human oral cavity [79–81], were also found to be elevated in the gut of untreated BD adolescents, may reflect age-dependent differences in mucosal immunity or gut barrier integrity during development. Veillonella rogosae was positively associated with proinflammatory diets, thus contributing to activated intestinal inflammation and impaired intestinal barrier function [82, 83]. These comparisons highlight that while certain bacterial species both exist in adults and adolescents with BD, the changes in the gut microbiome in BD patients are also influenced by the developmental stage. The altered abundance of gut bacteria in BD adolescents may be related to host inflammatory disturbance, lipid and glucose metabolism disruption. Our findings underscore the need for age-specific interventions targeting the microbiota-gut-brain axis in BD treatment.

Our MRI results suggested that reduced levels of FC between ROIs involved in the SMN, bilateral insular cortex and fusiform gyrus were found significantly associated with BD severity. Moreover, strengthened FC in BD adolescents between the orbital frontal cortex and bilateral supracalcarine cortex was detected with increasing HAMD scores, while the most of the functional connections were significantly lower. In terms of the functionality of regions involved, highlighted ROIs could be categorized into three possible dimensions. Regions of the SMN, including Heschl’s gyrus (a component of the primary auditory cortex), the supracalcarine cortex (SCC, adjacent to the cuneus), the fusiform gyrus, and the lateral occipital cortex, were linked to sensory information processing. Additionally, the orbitofrontal cortex (FOrb) and insular cortex (IC) were noted for their extensive connections to sensory areas and limbic structures [47, 84]. Middle temporal gyrus, amygdala and hippocampus were neural substrates of emotional processing and memory. These connectivity clusters seemed to resonate with each other functionally and portray putative sensory-emotional functional disruptions in the neuronal mechanism of adolescent BD. Previous researches suggested that the visual cortex and the right inferior frontal gyrus subregions respond less in BD patients [85, 86]. And compared to healthy subjects, children and adults with BD exhibit impaired memory for emotional faces and abnormal fusiform activation during encoding, which were correlated with mania severity [87]. Adolescence is a critical stage of brain development, and the neural circuit of the brain is not yet fully mature [88, 89], which characterized in limbic and prefrontal circuits mature at different rates [90]. The SMN, which is usually involved in processing and integrating sensory stimuli, undergoes further development during this period [91]. Meanwhile, the insular cortex, the key regions for assigning emotional value and regulating interoceptive signals was stable at an earlier age [92, 93]. This imbalance in maturation may lead to impaired functional integration among these regions, resulting in region connection changes and mood disorders. In addition, the fluctuations of neurotransmitter system (e.g., the dopamine system) in adolescence are also more apparent [94]. These neurodevelopmental alterations may represent a neural basis for increased vulnerability to psychopathology during adolescence. Combined with social and psychological stress, genetic susceptibility and other reasons, adolescents are more likely to have sensory and emotional brain network dysfunction [88, 95, 96]. However, it is not known whether such emotional and sensory network dysregulation is due to the disease or to underlying abnormalities in neurodevelopment that exist before the onset of disease symptoms.

Finally, we distinguished BD adolescents and HCs by gut microbiota, and a satisfactory classification discrimination effect was achieved (AUC = 91.12%). We also evaluated the predictive treatment effect (AUC = 91.84%) of four-week quetiapine treatment for BD adolescents. Although we previously conducted a similar study in adults with BD [23], the current study constructed the random forest model by combining the gut microbiota and brain function data. The results showed that the combined model predicted treatment outcome with greater accuracy (AUC = 96.3%) than using microbiota data alone, suggesting the advantage of using multi-modal data in depicting the gut-brain axis. Multi-modal analysis integrating gut microbiota, serum metabolome, and brain imaging data can help uncover more precise pathophysiological mechanisms underlying adolescent BD and provide a basis for personalized treatment approaches.

Although this study provides novel insights into the gut-brain axis in adolescent BD, several limitations should be discussed. The small cohort size may limit in detecting subtle microbial shifts and their potential associations with brain network alterations, potentially increasing the risk of Type I/II errors, such as limiting the discovery of microbial changes or exaggerating the predictive power of models. Our study has additional limitations regarding confounding variables. Gut microbial composition exhibits high individual variability influenced by BMI, dietary habits, and geographic factors that were not fully stratified in our analysis. Furthermore, during the treatment phase with quetiapine, we did not systematically monitor medication compliance and dosage titration schedules. The lack of stratification for baseline confounders may have obscured specific microbial signatures, while the absence of treatment monitoring introduces potential bias in interpreting the relationship of gut microbiome and clinical outcomes. Future studies with a larger and multi-center cohort and well-controlled confounding factors are needed. Although brain RSFC and disease severity have been analyzed in pre-treatment BD adolescents, we lack data for post-treatment BD adolescents and HCs, which may lead to the lack of reference system for our analysis and overestimate the strength of the association between brain network changes and the severity of BD. Moreover, the key mechanisms underlying gut-brain axis dysregulation in this study remain unelucidated. Future research should employ microbial metabolomics to identify the roles of critical microbiota-derived metabolites, such as SCFAs or lipopolysaccharide. In addition, this study focused on compositional shifts in the gut microbiota but did not examine microbial interactions, host-microbe dynamics (e.g., mucosal immunity), or intestinal morphological changes. Such investigations, particularly during dysbiosis, would elucidate how microbial imbalances drive structural and functional remodeling of the gut. In the future, longitudinal multi-modal studies with basic experiments are needed to fill this gap, so as to better explore the brain-gut mechanism in adolescent BD.

Overall, our study proved altered gut microbiota in BD adolescents, identified potential microbe-related metabolic pathway alterations and found brain network (SMN, bilateral insula) linked to BD severity. Crucially, specific gut bacterial markers can distinguish BD patients and HCs and predict 4-week quetiapine treatment outcome. A combination of MRI data and gut microbiota showed a better predictive efficacy to distinguish BD adolescents from HCs.

Conclusion

This study reveals disrupted gut-brain interactions in adolescent BD, linked to alterations in microbial metabolic pathways and sensor-emotional neural networks. Short-term quetiapine treatment modulated specific gut bacteria, and combined gut microbiota and brain network biomarkers improved treatment prediction. Future studies should explain how specific microbes regulate brain functional networks underlying the gut-brain axis in adolescent BD and advance the optimization of targeted interventions.
