Optimization of mechanical properties and microstructure characterization of resistance spot welded martensitic stainless steel: in-situ tempering and TLBO approach
Authors: Vinayak Gurav, Divya Shrivastava
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractResistance spot welding (RSW) is widely utilized in the automotive and manufacturing industries for joining high-strength materials such as AISI 420 martensitic stainless steel (MSS). However, MSS spot welds exhibit low fracture toughness due to the formation of a brittle martensitic microstructure in the fusion zone (FZ), impairing weldability. This study investigates the influence of two distinct welding strategies: the weld nugget forming strategy (WNFS) and the weld nugget in-situ tempering strategy (WNTS) to enhance joint performance and mitigate these challenges. A Box-Behnken design of experiments was adopted to systematically vary weld current, weld time, and electrode force on weld quality. Weld quality was assessed using nugget diameter, peak load, and absorption energy. Tensile-shear strength, microhardness profile, and fatigue life were evaluated to characterize mechanical behavior. The microstructural analyses were conducted using optical microscopy (OM), field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM), electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD), and X-ray diffraction (XRD) to characterize the phases and morphological features of the welded regions. Analysis of Variance was conducted to evaluate the significance level of the welding parameters. Furthermore, the Teaching-Learning Based Optimization (TLBO) algorithm was employed to identify the optimal parameter set. Fatigue testing (R = 0, 10 Hz) showed that within the tested range of post-weld tempering durations, the in-situ tempering strategy (WNTS) provided improved fatigue life, with a maximum of 8885 cycles achieved at a tempering time of 0.36 s. However, extending the tempering duration to 0.44 s reduced fatigue resistance due to over-tempering effects, indicating that further work is needed to generalize these findings across different loading frequencies and materials. This work proposes an optimized RSW strategy advancing the weldability of AISI 420 MSS for structuring demanding applications through data-driven optimization.Keywords: RSW, ANOVA, TLBO, In-situ tempering, Peak load, Fatigue life, Microhardness, Microstructure analysisSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

The automotive industry has experienced profound transformations in recent decades, driven by the increasing demand for lighter, safer, and more fuel-efficient vehicles. Weight reduction is one of the effective strategies for enhancing fuel efficiency and reducing greenhouse gas emissions. For instance, a 10% reduction in total vehicle weight could lead to a 4.9% improvement in fuel economy1. However, the safety of the vehicle and occupants should not be compromised. To meet these dual demands, the steel industry has developed high-strength steel (HSS) and advanced high-strength steel (AHSS) with good mechanical properties and unique metallurgical characteristics through innovative processing techniques. Adopting HSS offers the potential to achieve up to 25% in weight savings and 14% in cost reduction by replacing conventional steel2, making it an alternative material in modern vehicle design. HSS has been developed to meet the automotive industry’s evolving demands for manufacturability, durability, efficiency, emissions, safety, and quality, all at a relatively low cost3,4. At the same time, the vehicle structure should have high strength, high energy absorption capacity, and load-carrying capacity in an accident condition would be ideal from a passenger safety point of view. The crashworthiness capacity of the car provides adequate protection to passengers. It solely depends on the structural integrity and mechanical performance of the body in white (BIW) of the vehicle. The BIW of the car has two areas: a crumple zone and a safety cage. A Crumple zone is designed for high fracture toughness to absorb energy through deformation, and the safety cage is intended to protect the occupants by transferring the crash load without buckling in the event of a collision5.

RSW is a widely utilized fusion welding process in automotive manufacturing due to its efficiency, high production rate, easy adoption of automation, and cost-effectiveness6,7. Its importance is highlighted by its extensive application in modern vehicles, where approximately 2000–5000 weld spots are produced on the BIW assembly8,9. The RSW process is inherently complex, involving the interplay of mechanical, thermal, electrical, and metallurgical phenomena. These complexities are magnified when welding HSS and AHSS, materials favored in the automotive industry for their superior strength-to-weight ratio and crashworthiness10. Despite these advantages, the primary challenge in RSW of these materials lies in the phase transformation that occurs during the process. Fast cooling rates inherent to RSW often lead to the formation of martensitic microstructure in the fusion zone (FZ) and heat-affected zone (HAZ)11–13. These microstructures, characterized by their high hardness, high tensile-shear strength (TS), and low fracture toughness14–16, significantly diminish the weld joint energy absorption capacity and dynamic load-bearing ability, thereby compromising the overall quality of the weld joint. Pouranvari and Marashi10 have critically reviewed the fundamental understanding of the microstructure and mechanical properties relationship for AHSS, exhibiting metallurgical behavior such as complex martensite formation in FZ and softening in HAZ. These phenomena significantly influence the mechanical properties and failure modes, necessitating advanced models to predict phase transformation and hardness distribution. They concluded that existing weld sizing criteria are inadequate for AHSS, requiring new guidelines to ensure structural integrity under various loading conditions. Similarly, Hernandez et al.17 reported that, in RSW of dissimilar AHSS, softening within the HAZ weakens the joint, leading to strain localization and making the weld prone to interfacial failure. In another study, Khan et al.18 examined the microstructural features and static mechanical behavior of AHSS welds and proposed a predictive model for hardness distribution in the FZ. They observed that FZ mainly comprises martensite, responsible for high hardness, and the HAZ microstructure comprises undissolved ferrite and dispersed martensite islands, which reduce hardness at HAZ compared to FZ. Martensitic microstructures pose significant challenges, as their brittleness increases susceptibility to crack propagation and reduces energy absorption under dynamic loading conditions. This heterogeneous hardness distribution across the base metal (BM), FZ, and HAZ decreases the fatigue strength of the weld joint due to loss of ductility in critical regions of the weld joint19. To improve weld joint performance through process tuning, Gurav and Srivastava20 employed Jaya, Rao-II, and TLBO metaheuristics algorithms, showing superior optimization of weld nugget size and mechanical responses compared to conventional methods. To address these issues, Post-weld tempering techniques has been explored to improve the mechanical performance of the fusion zone and mitigate these effects10,21,22. The application of a secondary weld cycle after the primary weld current has proven effective in tempering the martensitic structure, thereby enhancing the fracture toughness and overall performance of the weld23. Pouranvari et al.24 examined the effect of introducing a secondary pulse after the initial melting/solidification cycle during resistance spot welding of AISI420 martensitic stainless steel. Their finding revealed that, when the second pulse was applied under optimized conditions, the welds exhibited notable improvement in tensile-shear strength, cross-tensile strength, and associated energy absorption capacity. In a related study, Soomroo et al.25 investigated post-weld tempering in the RSW of DP590 dual-phase steel. They reported that the application of a second pulse led to a marked enhancement in weld performance, with peak load and energy absorption increasing by 62% and 62.3%, respectively, compared with conventional single-pulse welding. These improvements were attributed to the enlargement of the weld nugget and the tempering effect on martensite within the fusion zone (FZ) and heat-affected zone (HAZ). Furthermore second pulse promoted pullout failure (PF), a favorable failure mode associated with higher energy absorption, while reducing the likelihood of brittle interfacial failure (IF). A similar trend was reported for Q&P1180 steel, in which post-weld tempering yielded a 70% rise in cross-tension strength and a 6% increase in tensile-shear strength26. These findings further reinforce the potential of tempering-assisted welding strategies to enhance the mechanical performance of spot welds. In addition, a simulation and machine learning (ML) based approach developed but Gurav and Shrivastava27 accurately predicted weld nugget diameter in HSLA-65 steel, demonstrating strong agreement between simulation data and ML algorithm, highlighting the potential for intelligent RSW control and quality prediction.

In addition to the severe phase transformation of microstructure, another major challenge in RSW is the segregation of alloying elements. The high-temperature gradients and rapid thermal cycles in RSW induce the migration of alloying elements toward the interface of the joined sheets, leading to their segregation at the faying surface. This segregation may sometimes lead to the formation of intermetallic compound layers. The thickness and morphology of these IMC layers are influenced by the type and distribution of the segregated alloying element. While the formation of the IMC layer is more common in the RSW of dissimilar metals such as steel and aluminum, where elements like iron and aluminum react to form IMC28–30, it also poses significant challenges during the RSW of dissimilar materials. The segregation of these alloying elements to the grain boundary during RSW can lead to embrittlement31,32. Choi et al. reported that embrittlement cracks in spot welds reduce mechanical properties33. Furthermore, DiGioovanni et al.34 highlighted that the location of the cracks plays a critical role in the tensile shear performance of the joint. Lee et al.35 studied the effect of boron segregation on the weldability of high-strength steels and reported that higher boron levels decreased energy absorption and increased the tendency for crack formation. Similarly, Betiku et al.36 examined the segregation of phosphorus at the edge of the FZ for press-hardening steel and found it to be detrimental to the weld joint integrity. Betiku et al.36 proposed a novel technique involving in-situ double force combined with post-weld heat treatment (PWHT) to overcome this issue. In the case of double force with the PWHT schedule, the crack trajectory shifted in regions where grain refinement appeared along the fusion zone boundary, offering greater resistance to crack advancement. These advancements highlight the importance of controlling alloying element segregation and IMC layer formation to improve the weld quality in RSW applications. Although several studies have investigated RSW parameters and post-weld heat treatments, very few have explored how in-situ tempering cycles influence the fatigue behavior of martensitic stainless steels. Previous work shows that tempering can significantly alter microstructure24 and mechanical properties37; however, the influence of in-situ tempering cycles on fatigue life in martensitic stainless steels remains unexplored so far. This study addresses this gap by experimentally evaluating how different tempering durations affect weld nugget characteristics and cyclic durability.

The present study aims to optimize RSW parameters for AISI 420 MSS to improve the weld joint physical characteristics and mechanical performance. Two distinct welding strategies were employed: Weld Nugget Forming Strategy (WNFS) and Weld Nugget In-Situ Tempering Strategy (WNTS). A Box-Behnken design-based experimental strategy was adopted to study how weld current, weld time, and electrode pressure influence weld quality. Mechanical characterization, including tensile-shear tests, microhardness, fatigue analysis, and microstructural analysis, was performed to evaluate the effectiveness of these welding strategies. Furthermore, the TLBO algorithm was employed to optimize the sets of parameters to enhance joint performance. The novelty of this study lies in the systematic comparison of WNFS and WNTS for AISI 420 MSS, the identification of an optimal fatigue life window for in-situ tempering, and demonstrating a combined experimental-optimization approach where TLBO links fusion zone softening with measurable improvement in energy absorption and joint strength.

Teaching learning-based optimization algorithm

Originally introduced by Rao et al.41, the TLBO method is inspired by the knowledge transfer process that occurs in a classroom setting between instructors and learners. Unlike many metaheuristic algorithms, TLBO does not rely on algorithm-specific control parameters and functions only with the population size. The optimization progresses through two sequential stages: the teacher phase and the learner phase, as depicted in Fig. 6. In the teacher phase, the decision variables of candidates solutions are probabilistically updated by Eq. (1) to produce an improves solution

Here, X denotes the original solution, corresponds to the best-performing solution in the population, and represents the average value of that variable across all candidates. The teacher factor and the random number (r) are generated within the ranges [1 or 2] and [0, 1] respectively. The updated solution interacts with other population members in the learner phase to seek further improvement. The TLBO algorithm effectively converges on optimized solutions through these iterative processes without requiring additional control parameters.

Mechanical characterization of RSW of AISI 420 MSS

Initial experiments were conducted to determine the optimal ranges of process parameters for weld nugget growth, in accordance with AWS standards. Based on these trials, the factors and levels used in the study were determined (Table 3). The experimental plan was structured using a Box-Behnken design, resulting in 15 welding trials. Earlier research10,42 has established that the likelihood of WND failure in pull-out mode during TS loading is influenced by the balance between fusion zone (FZ) toughness and heat-affected zone (HAZ) shear strength. Since toughness generally decreases with increasing hardness, while yield strength increases with it10,42, the hardness distribution in both FZ and HAZ becomes a critical parameter. These hardness levels are determined by the underlying microstructures, which are strongly affected by heat input. Therefore, assessing the role of the WNTS on nugget size, microhardness, and microstructural evolution is of particular importance in this study.

This is because AISI 420 steel, being a martensitic stainless steel, transforms to austenite upon exposure to welding temperature and rapidly reforms martensite upon cooling. The base metal of AISI 420 steel primarily comprises martensite (dark phase) and chromium carbide (bright phase), as depicted in Fig. 9b. The micrograph of the FZ obtained using FESEM (Fig. 9d) reveals a dendritic microstructure characteristic of localized thermal effects during welding. The detailed microstructural features of the FZ at higher magnification are provided in the FESEM micrograph (Fig. 9g), which predominantly exhibits a martensitic structure with traces of δ-ferrite. It was analyzed that these peaks confirm the dominance of the martensite phase. Similar peak positions for α-Fe and α′ have also been reported by Erdem et al.43 and K and Kacar44. Figure 12 provides the results obtained from the electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) analysis conducted on the spot weld. Figure 12a shows the grain spread of BM, HAZ, and FZ. Figure 12b shows the microstructure phase map of the weld nugget. Figure 12c, d shows the grain spread and microstructure phase map at the interface of BM and HAZ, respectively. Figure 12e shows the inverse pole figure (IPF) map of the BM, HAZ, and FZ of the RSW joint, while Fig. 12f presents the magnified IPF map at the intersection of the BM and HAZ. It is important to note that, as indicated by the phase map analysis (Fig. 12b), both martensite and ferrite are represented with a green color corresponding to body-centered cubic (BCC) crystal structure due to the inability of the EBSD to discern these two phases. For a more detailed exploration of the presence of ferrite within the spot weld, advanced characterization analysis (e.g., TEM) would be necessary to confirm this. However, according to the physical metallurgy of AISI420 stainless steel, it is reported that the weld metal microstructure contains some amount of ferrite45. Owing to the high hardness of the FZ (~ 620 HV shown in Fig. 13), it is likely composed of predominantly martensite with a small amount of ferrite. Notably, the FZ exhibits a significantly higher hardness (~ 620 HV) compared to the BM (~ 150 HV) as shown in Fig. 13, indicating that the FZ primarily consists of martensite. This observation aligns with previous studies13,46, which have confirmed the formation of a martensitic phase in the RSW of AISI 420 steel. However, the inherent brittleness of the FZ promotes crack propagation under static and cyclic loading conditions. Therefore, to enhance the mechanical performance of the spot welds, a systematic approach is required to improve the toughness of the FZ. This can be achieved through in-process tempering of the weld nugget using the post-weld pulse tempering (PWPT) of spot weld under the WNTS strategy.

Thus, the improved mechanical properties observed with the PWTP cannot be attributed to the weld nugget diameter.

Fatigue life of in-situ post-weld heat-treated RSW of AISI 420 steel

The fatigue performance of resistance spot-welded AISI 420 steel with in-situ post-weld heat treatment was evaluated under cyclic loading. All specimens were welded using a primary weld cycle consisting of 7000 A current, 160 ms weld time, and 4.5 kN electrode force, followed by a second current pulse applied as the post-weld heat-treatment cycle, as detailed in Table 9. The weld schedules were varied in terms of tempering time while maintaining constant squeeze time, weld time, cool time, and hold time. Fatigue tests were carried out under load control using a sinusoidal pulsating-tension waveform with a maximum load of 7.5 kN and a minimum load of 0 kN, corresponding to an R-ratio of 0. The frequency used for all specimens in this study was 10 Hz. No run-out criterion was applied, as the objective was to measure the actual number of cycles until crack initiation or failure for different in-situ tempering durations. The test automatically terminated upon the first sharp load drop or displacement increase indicating crack formation. The fatigue life corresponding to each weld schedule is summarized in Table 9. The results indicate a significant improvement in fatigue life with an increase in in-situ tempering time up to 18 cycles. The fatigue life for the first schedule (10 cycles tempering time) was the lowest at 3819 cycles, while an increase to 14 cycles of tempering improved fatigue life to 5037 cycles. The highest fatigue life of 8885 cycles was recorded at 18 cycles of tempering time, demonstrating an optimal balance between stress relaxation and microstructural refinement. However, further increasing the tempering time to 22 cycles resulted in a decline in fatigue life to 5332 cycles (refer Fig. 16a). This reduction suggests that excessive tempering may lead to over-softening of the weld zone, thereby reducing its fatigue resistance. In connection with this observation, the reduction in fatigue life beyond 18 cycles of in-situ tempering is attributed to over-tempering of the martensitic fusion zone, which causes excessive softening and coarsening of microstructure. This prolonged thermal exposure also introduces microstructural heterogeneity in the FZ and HAZ, making these regions more susceptible to early crack initiation under cyclic loading. As a result, fatigue resistance declines once the tempering duration exceeds the threshold of 18 cycles. Furthermore, as observed in Fig. 16b, for a given post-weld heat treatment schedule, the improvement in fatigue life performance cannot be attributed solely to nugget size. The enhancement is primarily influenced by microstructure modification induced by in-situ tempering.

Conclusion

The present study comprehensively investigated the mechanical and microstructural characteristics of RSW of AISI 420 MSS. The key findings are summarized as follows.

Effect of welding parameters on mechanical performance: The WND and failure modes significantly influenced the tensile-shear strength and AE of the welds. The experimental results demonstrated that weld current and weld time dominated WND, PL, and AE, whereas weld force exhibited a relatively lower influence.

Microstructural characterization: FZ exhibited a martensitic microstructure with δ-ferrite traces, leading to high hardness (⁓620 HV), which was significantly greater than that of the base metal (⁓150 HV). The brittleness of the FZ, however, may contribute to crack propagation under loading conditions.

This improvement results from the transformation of the FZ microstructure, shifting from a primarily hard martensitic structure in single pulse welds to a tempered martensitic structure after applying a post-weld pulse. The resulting softening effect enhances the toughness of the FZ and reduces its brittleness.

Future Work: This research offers significant insights into the mechanical behavior, microstructural characteristics, and process optimization of RSW for AISI 420 MSS. Building upon these findings, future studies should focus on the development of advanced tempering techniques aimed at reducing the brittleness of the FZ and improving the fatigue life of welded joints. The application of data-driven methodologies may be further explored to establish robust correlations between welding parameters, microstructural evolution, and mechanical performance, thereby facilitating more reliable prediction and control of weld quality. In addition, detailed failure analysis using high-resolution imaging and microstructural examination could provide a deeper understanding of crack initiation and propagation mechanisms under cyclic loading conditions, ultimately supporting the design of more durable and structurally reliable welds.
