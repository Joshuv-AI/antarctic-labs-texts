Interaction between microvascular invasion and early recurrence of hepatocellular carcinoma in cirrhosis patients with curative liver resection: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yuanming Qiang, Binwei Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the independent effects and potential effect modification by liver function reserve on the prognostic value of MVI for early recurrence in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) arising from cirrhosis following curative liver resection (CLR).MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted, consecutively enrolling 120 patients who underwent CLR with histopathologically confirmed HCC arising from cirrhosis between January 2021 and December 2023. MVI status (positive/negative) was determined based on postoperative pathological reports. Preoperative clinical data were used to assess Child-Pugh class (A/B). The primary endpoint was early tumor recurrence within 24 months post-surgery. A multivariate Cox model incorporating an effect modification between MVI and Child-Pugh class was constructed to evaluate their potential effect modification.ResultsA total of 120 patients were included, of whom 54 (45.0%) were MVI-positive. Early recurrence occurred in 50 patients (41.67%). MVI positivity (HR = 4.00), tumor size > 5 cm, anatomical liver resection, and Edmondson grade III-IV were identified as independent risk factors for early recurrence. A significant effect modification between MVI and Child-Pugh class was observed (interaction HR = 0.11, 95% CI: 0.03–0.40, P = 0.001), which remained stable after bootstrap internal validation. The risk effect of MVI positivity was strongest in patients with Child-Pugh class A, whereas this effect was significantly attenuated in patients with Child-Pugh class B. Based on this interaction, patients were stratified into low-risk, low-intermediate risk, high-intermediate risk, and high-risk groups. The high-risk group exhibited a significantly increased risk of recurrence compared to the low-risk group.ConclusionA risk assessment model integrating MVI status and Child-Pugh class may help stratify patients. The prognostic value of MVI appears to be modified by liver function reserve, with the strongest effect observed in patients with well-preserved liver function (Child-Pugh A). These findings suggest potential value for personalized postoperative management, pending further validation.Keywords: Hepatocellular Carcinoma, Liver Cirrhosis, Microvascular Invasion, Child-Pugh Class, Early Recurrence, Interaction

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) ranks among the most prevalent malignancies, posing a significant threat to human health [1]. For patients with resectable HCC meeting surgical criteria, curative liver resection (CLR) remains the cornerstone modality for achieving potential cure. However, early recurrence within 24 months following surgery constitutes a major obstacle limiting long-term patient survival. Early recurrence frequently signifies more aggressive tumor biology and is closely correlated with adverse prognosis. Consequently, identifying and elucidating the critical risk factors for early recurrence is of paramount importance for improving patient outcomes [2, 3].

Among various pathological prognostic factors, microvascular invasion (MVI) has been firmly established as a potent independent predictor of HCC recurrence and survival after resection [4, 5]. MVI, the presence of tumor cell clusters within vascular spaces lined by endothelial cells, represents a crucial manifestation of aggressive tumor biological behavior and is directly associated with the formation of intrahepatic micrometastases, thereby significantly elevating the risk of postoperative recurrence [6]. Furthermore, the vast majority of HCCs arise in the context of chronic liver disease, with cirrhosis being the most prevalent underlying pathological condition. The severity of cirrhosis, commonly quantified clinically using the Child-Pugh class, not only reflects hepatic functional reserve and influences surgical safety and patient tolerance but also involves persistent inflammation, immune microenvironment dysregulation, and aberrant extracellular matrix deposition. These factors may together create a “soil” conducive to tumor recurrence [7]. Therefore, investigating the prognostic value of MVI specifically within the context of cirrhosis is particularly warranted.

Currently, existing studies have individually established the adverse impact of MVI positivity or cirrhosis (particularly Child-Pugh class B) on postoperative HCC recurrence [8]. However, within the patient population with cirrhosis, whether the prognostic value of MVI is modified by liver function reserve status (as represented by Child-Pugh class), that is, whether their combined effect on early postoperative recurrence is merely additive or involves synergistic or antagonistic mechanisms, remains insufficiently explored and lacks definitive evidence. Elucidating this relationship could advance risk assessment models from univariate analysis towards a binary framework integrating “tumor aggressiveness (MVI)” and “host liver status (Child-Pugh class),” thereby enabling the development of a more refined stratification system for recurrence risk.

To address this gap, the present study aims to investigate, through a single-center retrospective cohort design, the independent and combined interaction effects of MVI and cirrhotic Child-Pugh class on early (≤ 24 months) postoperative recurrence in patients with cirrhosis who underwent CLR for HCC. A key objective is to rigorously examine the potential statistical interaction between these two factors, with the goal of providing more nuanced evidence to inform clinical risk assessment and therapeutic decision-making.

Participants and methods

Study population

Participants were recruited sequentially for this retrospective investigation, who underwent CLR at the Department of Hepatobiliary Surgery of our hospital between January 2021 and December 2023, and who were postoperatively diagnosed with HCC arising from cirrhosis based on histopathological examination.

Inclusion criteria: (1) aged 18 or more than 18 years; (2) preoperative clinical diagnosis and postoperative histopathological confirmation of HCC [9]; (3) histopathological confirmation of underlying cirrhosis [10]; (4) underwent CLR (R0 resection); (5) comprehensive medical histories, biopsy results, and longitudinal follow-up information available.

Exclusion criteria: (1) perioperative mortality (death within 90 days post-surgery); (2) presence of other concurrent malignancies; (3) receipt of prior local or systemic therapies for HCC (e.g., transarterial chemoembolization, ablation, targeted therapy) before surgery; (4) Child-Pugh class C cirrhosis; (5) loss to follow-up or follow-up duration less than 24 months in the absence of a recurrence event.(6) presence of macrovascular invasion (e.g., portal vein tumor thrombosis) confirmed by preoperative imaging.

Based on preliminary results from a pilot study and relevant literature [11], the hazard ratio (HR) for early recurrence between the MVI-positive and MVI-negative groups was estimated to be approximately 2.5. Based on a predefined significance level of 0.05 and a power of 0.80, the minimum recruitment goal was set at 110 participants. This sample size allows for robust Log-rank testing of the primary outcomes. This study ultimately enrolled 120 patients, meeting the statistical requirements.

Patients were categorized into MVI-positive and MVI-negative groups based on postoperative pathological reports. Primary outcome definition was referred to previous literature [9]: The primary outcome was early postoperative recurrence, defined as radiologically confirmed tumor recurrence (intrahepatic or extrahepatic) on contrast-enhanced CT or MRI occurring within 24 months after surgery. For statistical analyses, recurrence-free survival (RFS), calculated as the time interval from the date of surgery to the date of first radiologically confirmed recurrence or the date of last follow-up, and recurrence status were used as survival analysis variables.

Data collection and definitions

Data were independently extracted by two researchers, who were blinded to the patients’ final outcomes, using a pre-designed standardized case report form. Information was retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical record system, the pathology information system, and the picture archiving and communication system. Following extraction, data were cross-checked for consistency; any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third senior researcher.

The collected data encompassed the following categories: (1) Demographic and clinical characteristics: Age, sex, body mass index (BMI), history of diabetes mellitus, smoking history, alcohol consumption history, and cirrhosis etiology (hepatitis B virus-related vs. other). (2) Cirrhosis-related parameters: Child-Pugh class (A or B) and hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) status. (3) Tumor pathological characteristics: Maximum tumor diameter (categorized as > 5 cm [1] or ≤ 5 cm), tumor number (solitary/multiple), Edmondson-Steiner pathological grade (I-II/III-IV), and presence of satellite nodules (yes/no). (4) Surgical characteristics: Surgical procedure (anatomical liver resection/non-anatomical liver resection) and surgical margin width.(5)Laboratory examinations and treatment: alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) level, postoperative antiviral therapy status.

Assessment of recurrence and MVI

Early postoperative recurrence, defined as radiologically confirmed tumor recurrence (intrahepatic or extrahepatic) on contrast-enhanced CT or MRI occurring within 24 months post-surgery, was recorded as “yes” or “no”.

The primary endpoint, RFS, tracked the months elapsed from surgery until the first documented radiological recurrence. For those who did not experience a relapse, data were censored at the time of their final follow-up visit.

MVI was defined according to postoperative pathological reports [12]. MVI positivity was defined as the presence of tumor cell clusters within vascular spaces lined by endothelial cells identified under light microscopy; otherwise, MVI was considered negative.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.5.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation if normally distributed, and comparisons between groups were performed using the independent samples t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For non-normally distributed continuous variables, data were presented as median with interquartile range [M(Q1, Q3)], and comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts with percentages [n(%)] and compared between groups using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. For survival analysis, the primary study endpoint was RFS within 24 months post-surgery. RFS curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups were compared using the Log-rank test. To explore the combined effect of MVI and Child-Pugh class on prognosis, integrated survival curves were plotted encompassing four subgroups: MVI-negative/Child-Pugh A, MVI-negative/Child-Pugh B, MVI-positive/Child-Pugh A, and MVI-positive/Child-Pugh B. For risk factor analysis of recurrence, clinically relevant variables were pre-specified based on biological plausibility and previous literature, including MVI, Child-Pugh class, tumor size, resection type, tumor number, AFP level, Edmondson grade, and satellite nodules. These variables were forced into the multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model to identify independent risk factors for early postoperative recurrence. Results were presented as hazard ratios (HR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). To further examine the potential interaction between MVI and Child-Pugh class, a product interaction term was constructed and incorporated into the multivariate Cox model. Interaction was assessed using both the likelihood ratio test and the Wald test. The goodness-of-fit of each model was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index).

To assess the stability of the interaction model and avoid overfitting, internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations. The 95% confidence intervals for the interaction term were derived using the percentile method.

Results

A total of 120 patients were enrolled in this study, including 54 (45.0%) in the MVI-positive group and 66 (55.0%) in the MVI-negative group. Significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding tumor size and Edmondson grade (P < 0.05). However, no statistically significant differences were found in age, sex, BMI, history of diabetes mellitus, smoking history, alcohol consumption history, cirrhosis etiology, HBsAg status, Child-Pugh class, tumor number, presence of satellite nodules, surgical procedure, or surgical margin (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Univariate Cox regression analysis for early recurrence

Anatomical liver resection, multiple tumors, and BMI showed marginal significance at the P < 0.10 level and were therefore included in subsequent multivariate analysis. Variables such as Child-Pugh class, Edmondson grade, presence of satellite nodules, and surgical margin were not significantly associated with early recurrence.

Interaction analysis

To investigate the potential interaction between MVI and liver function status, we constructed a Cox regression model incorporating an interaction term (Table 4, Fig. 1). The results revealed a statistically significant interaction between MVI and Child-Pugh class (interaction term HR = 0.11, 95% CI: 0.03–0.40, P = 0.001). Bootstrap internal validation with 1,000 resamples confirmed the stability of this interaction effect (bootstrap median HR = 0.096, bias-corrected 95% CI: 0.022–0.387, bootstrap P = 0.002), indicating that the observed effect modification is robust against overfitting. The risk effect of MVI positivity was strongest in patients with Child-Pugh class A (HR = 5.94, P < 0.001), whereas this effect was significantly attenuated in patients with Child-Pugh class B. This finding indicates that the prognostic value of MVI is modulated by liver function status, with the risk effect of MVI being more pronounced in patients with well-compensated liver function.

Interactive Effect of MVI Status and Child-Pugh Class on Early Recurrence Rate in Patients with HCC Arising from Cirrhosis

Recurrence risk stratification based on MVI and Child-Pugh class

Based on the multivariate analysis and interaction analysis results, patients were stratified into four risk groups (Table 5, Figs. 2 and 3): low-risk (MVI-negative/Child-Pugh A, n = 43), low-intermediate risk (MVI-negative/Child-Pugh B, n = 23), high-intermediate risk (MVI-positive/Child-Pugh B, n = 21), and high-risk (MVI-positive/Child-Pugh A, n = 33). The high-risk group exhibited the highest recurrence rate (69.7%) and the lowest 24-month RFS rate (30.3%), with a hazard ratio of 3.28 (95% CI: 1.58–6.81, P = 0.001) compared to the low-risk group. The differences in survival among the four groups were statistically significant (Log-rank test, P < 0.001).

Discussion

Through a systematic analysis of patients undergoing CLR for HCC arising from cirrhosis, this study yielded several key findings. First, MVI was a significant independent risk factor for early postoperative recurrence, with its presence being significantly associated with aggressive tumor pathological characteristics. Second, and innovatively, the prognostic value of MVI is substantially modulated by the patient’s liver function reserve status, demonstrating a clear effect modification by Child-Pugh class on the prognostic value of MVI. Specifically, the predictive power of MVI for early recurrence was most pronounced in patients with well-compensated liver function, whereas this effect was significantly attenuated in patients with decompensated liver function. The risk stratification model constructed based on this interaction enabled more precise discrimination of patient groups with varying recurrence risks.

Independent predictive value of MVI for early recurrence in HCC arising from cirrhosis

The present study demonstrated that MVI is an independent predictor of early postoperative recurrence in patients with HCC arising from cirrhosis, which aligns with the current consensus [13]. As direct morphological evidence of tumor cell invasion into the vascular system, MVI fundamentally signifies the initiation of intrahepatic micrometastasis. The high rate of early postoperative recurrence observed in MVI-positive patients corresponds with the pathological process whereby micrometastatic foci are activated and proliferate within a permissive microenvironment [14]. Furthermore, MVI was significantly associated with aggressive pathological features such as larger tumor size and poorer differentiation, further validating its role as a “composite marker” of aggressive tumor biological behavior [15]. Even within the complex context of cirrhosis, the independent predictive efficacy of MVI remained significant in multivariate analysis, consolidating its central role in prognostic assessment.We acknowledge that MVI was treated as a binary variable (positive vs. negative) in this study, which may oversimplify its pathological heterogeneity. Emerging evidence suggests that features such as the number of MVI foci, the distance from the tumor, and the location (microscopic vs. subtler vascular invasion) may carry different prognostic implications. Due to the limitations of retrospective data collection, we were unable to perform such detailed subclassification. Future prospective studies with standardized pathological reporting of MVI subcharacteristics are warranted to further refine risk stratification.

An unexpected finding in our multivariate model was that anatomical liver resection was associated with an increased risk of early recurrence (HR = 2.04, P = 0.047), which appears to contradict the established surgical principle that anatomical resection confers better oncological outcomes. However, this result should be interpreted with particular caution, as the P value is borderline and the confidence interval (1.01–4.14) only narrowly excludes the null. We attribute this counterintuitive finding predominantly to selection bias inherent in retrospective surgical cohorts: at our center, anatomical resections are preferentially performed for patients with larger, more centrally located, or biologically aggressive tumors, whereas non-anatomical resections are reserved for smaller or peripherally located tumors. This confounding by indication, rather than a true detrimental effect of the surgical technique itself, plausibly explains the observed association. Notably, tumor size > 5 cm—significantly more frequent in the MVI-positive group and independently associated with recurrence—was a major driver of the decision to perform anatomical resection. Therefore, this finding does not challenge the oncological value of anatomical resection when appropriately indicated.

Impact of liver function reserve on recurrence risk in the context of cirrhosis

Liver function reserve, as a critical indicator reflecting the host’s internal microenvironment, exhibits a multifaceted and complex relationship with HCC recurrence [16]. Although cirrhosis is recognized as the primary pathological basis for HCC development and progression, influencing recurrence risk through multiple mechanisms, the direct association between Child-Pugh class and early recurrence did not reach statistical significance in this study. This finding prompts a re-evaluation of the clinical significance of liver function assessment. First, modern hepatobiliary surgery has developed comprehensive perioperative management strategies that effectively preserve liver function, potentially mitigating its direct impact on recurrence risk [17]. Second, the exclusion of patients with Child-Pugh class C, representing the highest-risk cohort, may have attenuated the extreme effects of liver function reserve [18]. Finally, and most importantly, the in-depth analysis in this study revealed that liver function reserve may primarily act as an “effect modifier” rather than a direct risk factor. The influence of liver function reserve on recurrence risk is likely exerted through modulating the effect intensity of other risk factors. In patients with Child-Pugh class B, although the abnormal hepatic microenvironment increases overall recurrence susceptibility, this “background risk” may obscure the independent impact of specific tumor characteristics such as MVI [19]. This mechanism may explain the inconsistent associations between liver function and recurrence risk reported across studies, underscoring the need for more refined assessment models.

Effect modification of MVI by Child-Pugh class

We found significant effect modification between MVI and Child-Pugh class, characterized by a markedly enhanced risk effect of MVI in patients with Child-Pugh class A and a significantly attenuated effect in those with Child-Pugh class B. This result challenges the conventional “single-factor independent prediction” paradigm and reveals a dual regulatory model involving “tumor aggressiveness (MVI)-host liver status (Child-Pugh class),” providing a novel perspective for understanding the complexity of HCC recurrence in the context of cirrhosis. The potential mechanisms underlying this effect modification can be explained from the following three aspects. First, liver function reserve directly reshapes the hepatic microenvironment. In patients with Child-Pugh class A, characterized by well-compensated liver function, low inflammation levels, and intact immune function, micrometastatic foci resulting from MVI become the core driver of recurrence, with the risk effect being fully manifested. In contrast, patients with Child-Pugh class B exhibit severe inflammation, immunosuppression, and extensive parenchymal injury; the microenvironment itself is inherently “pro-carcinogenic,” with recurrence risk primarily dominated by liver failure and cirrhosis progression, thereby masking the additional micrometastatic effect of MVI [20]. Second, Child-Pugh class may indirectly influence the biological behavior of MVI-positive tumors [21]. The more complex pathological background in patients with Child-Pugh class B may exert regulatory effects on tumor invasive potential, attenuating the recurrence-driving role of MVI. Third, clinical decision-making considerations regarding liver function may indirectly modulate the risk impact of MVI [22]. Treatment strategies for patients with Child-Pugh class B emphasize liver function preservation, and related therapeutic adjustments may partially offset the recurrence risk associated with MVI.We acknowledge that residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. Liver function reserve and tumor aggressiveness may be interrelated, and unmeasured factors (e.g., severity of portal hypertension, inflammatory status, or genetic susceptibility) could potentially influence both Child-Pugh class and recurrence risk. Although we have adjusted for major clinicopathological variables, the possibility of unmeasured confounding remains. Nevertheless, the robustness of the interaction was supported by bootstrap internal validation, and the consistent findings across sensitivity analyses strengthen the validity of our observation. Future studies with more comprehensive data collection, including biomarkers of systemic inflammation and fibrosis, are needed to further address this issue.Additionally, the marginal association observed for anatomical liver resection is likely confounded by indication, reflecting preferential use of this procedure in patients with more aggressive tumor profiles rather than a true detrimental effect of the surgical approach.

Clinical applicability of the risk stratification system

Based on the interaction between MVI and Child-Pugh class, this study constructed a risk assessment system encompassing four distinct risk strata. Survival analysis demonstrated differences in recurrence-free survival among the risk strata, with patients in the high-risk group exhibiting particularly prominent recurrence risk and a significantly lower 24-month recurrence-free survival rate compared to the low-risk group. This stratification system offers several clinical benefits. First, it provides a basis for formulating individualized follow-up strategies, with high-risk patients potentially benefiting from more intensive surveillance. Second, it offers a reference framework for the precise application of postoperative adjuvant therapy, identifying high-risk patients as priority candidates for intervention. Third, the system is based on routinely available clinicopathological indicators, rendering it practical and readily implementable. An important observation from this study is the dynamic modulation of prognosis in MVI-positive patients by liver function status. This regulatory effect confers differential clinical significance to MVI positivity across varying liver function backgrounds, further validating the necessity of comprehensively evaluating both tumor characteristics and host status, while also providing novel insights into the mechanisms underlying HCC recurrence.

Limitations and future perspectives

The antiviral treatment rate (approximately 57%) in our cohort was lower than the HBsAg positivity rate (approximately 85%), primarily because we strictly defined ‘antiviral treatment’ as therapy that had been regularly initiated within 1 month post-surgery; some patients experienced delayed initiation due to postoperative recovery or health insurance reimbursement procedures. Although the proportional hazards assumption was not fully satisfied in our Cox model (global Schoenfeld test P < 0.001), the reported hazard ratios can be interpreted as time-averaged effect estimates over the 24-month follow-up period, and the concentration of recurrence events in the early postoperative phase supports the clinical utility of these estimates. More importantly, the core finding of a significant interaction between MVI and Child-Pugh class was confirmed by bootstrap internal validation (1,000 resamples), suggesting that this effect modification is robust and does not critically depend on strict adherence to the proportional hazards assumption. As a single-center retrospective analysis, this study has certain limitations. The relatively limited sample size may affect statistical power and the generalizability of conclusions. Although external validation was not performed due to the single-center design, internal validation using bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) supported the stability of the interaction model, reducing the concern of overfitting. Additionally, the relatively modest sample size of 120 patients, particularly when stratified into four subgroups for interaction analysis (with the smallest subgroup comprising only 21 patients), represents a notable limitation. This reduced statistical power increases the risk of both overfitting and Type II errors, potentially affecting the stability of subgroup-specific estimates. Nevertheless, the significant interaction effect observed despite this limited power, coupled with its stability confirmed by bootstrap internal validation (1,000 resamples), suggests that the effect modification is robust and unlikely to be a spurious finding. These results should be considered hypothesis-generating, and external validation in larger, independent cohorts is essential to confirm the generalizability of our risk stratification model. Additionally, the marginal association observed for anatomical liver resection is likely confounded by indication, reflecting preferential use of this procedure in patients with more aggressive tumor profiles rather than a true detrimental effect of the surgical approach. The follow-up period was primarily focused on the early postoperative phase, necessitating further observation regarding impacts on mid- and long-term recurrence and survival. Selection bias is an inherent limitation of this retrospective surgical cohort. Patients who underwent curative resection were selected based on favorable tumor characteristics and preserved liver function, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to patients with more advanced tumors or poorer liver function. The observed effect modification by Child-Pugh class may be influenced by this selection mechanism, as patients with Child-Pugh B cirrhosis who were deemed eligible for surgery likely represent a healthier subset of this population. Future prospective studies with broader inclusion criteria are warranted to validate our findings. Patients with macrovascular invasion (e.g., portal vein tumor thrombosis) were excluded from this study based on preoperative imaging, as such patients are generally not candidates for curative liver resection. Therefore, our findings apply only to patients without macrovascular invasion, and the prognostic value of MVI in the presence of PVTT remains to be investigated. The study did not incorporate numerous potentially relevant factors influencing recurrence, such as ALBI grade, BCLC stage, extent of hepatic resection (major vs. minor hepatectomy), PIVKA-II (des-gamma-carboxy prothrombin), tumor molecular markers, patient genetic background, or preoperative treatment history. Additionally, the specific biological mechanisms underlying the observed effect modification warrant further in-depth exploration.

Future research directions may include the following: conducting multicenter, large-sample prospective studies to validate the reproducibility and robustness of these findings; integrating multi-omics technologies to deeply investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the interaction between MVI and liver function status; developing comprehensive predictive models integrating clinical, pathological, and molecular features to enhance the accuracy of recurrence risk stratification; exploring individualized adjuvant treatment strategies tailored to patients in different risk strata, particularly intensive interventions for the high-risk population characterized by MVI positivity and well-preserved liver function; and investigating the potential application of novel non-invasive detection technologies (e.g., radiomics, liquid biopsy) in preoperative prediction of MVI status and recurrence risk.

Detailed data on the distribution of intrahepatic versus extrahepatic recurrence and the multiplicity of recurrent lesions were not systematically recorded in this retrospective cohort, precluding further subclassification. In addition, patients with Child-Pugh B cirrhosis may have a higher risk of non-recurrence mortality (e.g., liver failure), which could act as a competing risk that precludes the observation of recurrence. Future prospective studies with standardized follow-up protocols and competing risk analyses are warranted.

Conclusion

This study confirms that in the context of cirrhosis, MVI is an independent risk factor for early recurrence following CLR of HCC; however, its prognostic value is significantly modulated by the patient’s liver function reserve status. The prognostic value of MVI is significantly modified by Child-Pugh class, indicating that both tumor invasiveness and liver function status must be considered concurrently when assessing recurrence risk. The risk stratification model constructed based on this effect modification may provide a foundation for individualized postoperative management and adjuvant treatment decisions. Future research is warranted to deepen mechanistic understanding, optimize risk assessment systems, and explore precision intervention strategies tailored to patients across different risk strata, ultimately improving the overall prognosis of patients with HCC arising from cirrhosis.
