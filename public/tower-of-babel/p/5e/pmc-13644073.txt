Comparison of barium sulphate and iohexol for gastrointestinal transit time in Barn owls (Tyto alba)
Authors: Can Nacar, Kamil Serdar Inal, Elif Bagatir Kurban, Hatice Ozlem Nisbet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of the study is to compare gastrointestinal (GI) transit times and evaluate the adequacy of radiographic opacification of barium sulphate (BaSO4) and iohexol in Barn owls (Tyto alba). Eight healthy juvenile owls were included in a randomized crossover study with a 5-day washout period. BaSO4 (30% w/v) and iohexol (300 mg I/ml, undiluted) were administered in a randomized order to prevent order effects and were administered via orogastric tube at a dosage of 25 ml/kg. Serial radiographs were obtained at specific intervals for up to 300 min. Three independent observers evaluated the images for transit times using a time-blinded protocol.ResultsTotal transit time (esophagus to cloaca) was significantly shorter for iohexol (5.0 ± 0.0 min) compared to BaSO4 (15.0 ± 8.4 min) (p = 0.039). Both agents provided adequate radiopacity for clear anatomic differentiation. No adverse effects or contrast-related complications were observed.ConclusionsLimitations of this study include the small sample size (n = 8) and the use of static radiography rather than fluoroscopy, which precluded real-time motility assessment. Iohexol is a safe and significantly faster alternative to BaSO4 for GI contrast studies in Barn owls. It is particularly advantageous in emergency cases requiring rapid diagnosis or involving suspected GI perforation.

Background

Raptors play a vital role in the ecosystems they inhabit. These animals function at the apex of the food web and have evolved a gastrointestinal (GI) system adapted to a carnivorous diet [1]. Owls (order Strigiformes) are nocturnal birds of prey distributed across nearly all parts of the world. In veterinary medicine, owls are frequently encountered either as educational animals in certain institutions or as patients in rehabilitation units. Despite their prevalence, limited information is available regarding contrast imaging in these raptors. Although gastrointestinal transit has been investigated in some avian species [2], comparative data on the use of barium sulphate and iohexol in Barn owls are lacking, limiting evidence-based selection of contrast agents in this species.

Contrast radiography has become a widely utilised method for evaluating suspected pathological changes in the avian GI system [2–5]. The presence of air sacs in birds provides negative contrast by demarcating the boundaries of coelomic organs, making them highly suitable for radiographic evaluation. In direct and indirect radiography, anaesthesia may be required, depending on the patient’s body condition and temperament. However, it must be considered that the administration of anaesthesia may alter GI motility and vital parameters [3, 4].

Contrast agents used for GI imaging are administered orally, and serial radiographs are taken at various time intervals to obtain data on organ filling and emptying times. Additionally, this technique enables the diagnosis of diverse pathological conditions such as filling defects, masses, foreign bodies, or perforations [2, 6]. It has been reported that contrast agents used in mammals, such as barium sulphate suspension (BaSO4) and iohexol, can also be utilised in birds. While BaSO4 can provide excellent visualisation of anatomical structures and mucosal detail, its use is contraindicated in patients with suspected gastrointestinal perforation [6]. In contrast, iohexol, due to its non-ionic nature, low osmolality, and suitability for intravenous administration, can be safely used in almost any situation where contrast imaging is required, including cases with suspected perforation [7–9].

The aim of this study was to determine and compare the GI transit times of two contrast agents in Barn owls. We hypothesise that due to its low viscosity and osmolarity, iohexol will exhibit a faster gastrointestinal transit time compared to barium sulphate in Barn owls.

Methods

The study material consisted of eight juvenile Barn owls (Tyto alba) (3 females, 5 males; approximately 20 weeks old) with a mean body weight of 320 ± 40 g. These individuals, observed to be unable to fly in their natural habitats, were officially transferred to the Clinic of Wild Animal Diseases, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Ondokuz Mayıs University by the officers of the General Directorate of Nature Conservation and National Parks for medical evaluation and rehabilitation. All diagnostic procedures, including the administration of contrast media, were performed as part of the clinical assessment protocol during the rescue process. Initially, nine individuals were evaluated; however, one owl was excluded from the study due to impaired gastrointestinal transit. All procedures in this study were approved by the Ondokuz Mayıs University Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2024-06). This study was conducted with the permission of the Republic of Türkiye Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry, General Directorate of Nature Conservation and National Parks (Ref. No: E-21264211-288.04-15410951, Date: 14.08.2024) and in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 guidelines for animal research. Sex and age determination were performed according to the criteria described by Blasco-Zumeta and Heinze, 2023 [10]. Only Barn owls without proventricular dilatation, gastrointestinal tract pathology, or orthopedic abnormalities were included in the study. Prior to the study, all owls underwent thorough physical examinations, manual complete blood counts (CBCs), assessment of feeding behaviour, and evaluation of physiological condition, confirming that they were clinically healthy. Upon completion of the study, all owls demonstrated the ability to feed and hunt independently and to perform normal flight maneuvers; consequently, they were released into a suitable habitat in good health.

The owls were randomly assigned to two groups in a crossover design. Four owls received BaSO₄ first, while the remaining four received iohexol. A five-day washout period was observed between administrations to ensure complete elimination of the contrast agents and to prevent potential interactions. The second phase was initiated only after confirming that all individuals had returned to normal physiological parameters and feeding behavior, at which point the contrast agents were swapped between the groups. Barium sulphate and iohexol contrast agents were administered to the owls following a 12-hour fasting period. Each owl was housed individually, at separate time points, in a cardboard box measuring 80 × 80 × 80 cm throughout the clinical assessment and imaging procedures; the boxes were numbered for identification and record-keeping purposes. Radiographic examinations were performed at 75 kV and 1.2 mAs. The contrast agents were prepared in advance, with doses calculated according to the body weight of each owl prior to administration. Baseline survey radiographs of the whole body were obtained in two orthogonal views (ventrodorsal and right lateral) prior to contrast administration. Subsequently, barium sulphate (BaSO₄; R-X 1 g/ml suspension) was prepared as a 30% solution and administered orally at a dose of 25 ml/kg [2] via an orogastric feeding tube, and iohexol (Opaxol 300 mg I/ml, which inherently represents a 30% solution) was administered undiluted at a dose of 25 ml/kg [7] via an orogastric feeding tube. The dose of 25 ml/kg utilised in this study represents only 50% of the maximum theoretical capacity, ensuring adequate contrast distension while minimizing the risk of regurgitation or aspiration [11]. Throughout the administration, the glottic entrance was carefully monitored to prevent aspiration. This time point was designated as 0 min, at which time laterolateral and ventrodorsal radiographs were acquired. Thereafter, orthogonal radiographs were obtained at 5, 15, 30, 60, 120, 180, 240, and 300 min. To eliminate potential sequence effects, a randomised crossover design was employed.

For each gastrointestinal segment, the onset of emptying was defined as the earliest time point at which the contrast medium was visibly identified passing into the subsequent organ [2, 3, 6]. Specifically, entry into the small intestine was recorded at the moment the contrast medium exited the ventriculus, clearly delineating the duodenal loop. Similarly, arrivals at the large intestine and at the cloaca were determined from anatomical landmarks, defined as the first time the contrast reached the rectal region and the first time radiopacity was detected within the cloacal lumen, respectively. Due to the distinct radiographic appearances of the contrast agents, blinding to the treatment group was not feasible. However, to minimise evaluation bias, the review process was blinded to the acquisition times. The time labels on the radiographs were concealed, and reviewers assessed the anatomical distribution of the contrast medium without knowledge of the specific post-administration time points. All radiographs were initially evaluated independently, without prior knowledge of the other observers’ findings, by three veterinary surgeons (PhD), one of whom specializes in avian diagnostic imaging and raptor medicine. Following the independent evaluations, a joint review meeting was held to discuss any discrepancies in the initial transit time recordings. The final transit times were established by unanimous agreement among the three observers.

Statistical analysis

Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Comparisons between the two contrast media in each gastrointestinal segment were conducted using the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Time points for which no measurable data could be obtained were excluded from the analysis. The sample size was determined by constraints imposed by the ethics committee on the use of wildlife rehabilitation patients and by case availability, consistent with a convenience sampling approach. The minimum sample size (six animals in each group) was determined by power analysis (power = 82%; p < 0.05) based on oesophageal emptying times (5 min: 4.7 ± 3.8 vs. 123 min: 0.5 ± 1.2) derived from a similar study [4]. The effect size in our study was calculated to be approximately 1.25. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

In this study, positive-contrast gastrointestinal tract imaging was performed using barium sulphate (BaSO₄) and iohexol in a total of eight Barn owls (Tyto alba), consisting of three females and five males, aged 20 weeks, with a mean body weight of 320 ± 38 g, and the results were compared. As both contrast agents passed rapidly through the oesophagus, no contrast medium was observed in this region on radiographs obtained at 0 min. Transit through the oesophagus was rapid, making optimal imaging of this segment challenging. Apart from this limitation, the proventriculus (in some cases), ventriculus, and intestinal segments could be visualised clearly and in detail. The detailed times and mean values for gastrointestinal transit of BaSO₄ and iohexol are presented in Table 1. The oesophagus-to-cloaca total transit time was significantly shorter in the iohexol group (p = 0.039). Both contrast agents maintained adequate radiographic opacity throughout the study period, with no significant loss of image quality due to dilution or contrast depletion up to 300 min post-administration. During a positive-contrast radiographic examination, the contrast medium could be traced continuously to the cloaca.

The contrast material progressed aborally through the stomach and successive intestinal segments. However, due to caecal physiology and retrograde gastrointestinal peristalsis in birds, accumulation of contrast medium indicating retrograde transport from the colon to the caeca was observed, and the medium subsequently remained there. The residence time of the contrast medium in the caeca differed from that in other intestinal segments. At 300 min, both contrast agents were present in the caeca in all cases (Figs. 1 and 2). In both groups, no residual contrast medium was detected on follow-up radiographs obtained at 24 h. All owls remained clinically healthy throughout the study and after its completion; no adverse effects were observed. Five days after completion of the study, all owls were released into a habitat appropriate for the species.

Discussion

This study compared iohexol (an iodine-based contrast agent) and barium sulphate for GI contrast studies in Barn owls (Tyto alba) using conventional radiography. In Barn owls, iohexol exhibited a significantly faster gastrointestinal transit time than that of barium sulphate and provided adequate radiographic density without impairing image interpretation. The literature on contrast radiographic studies in avian species is limited; nevertheless, the findings of the present study were compared with data reported for other avian species. Previous investigations have demonstrated that, in medium-sized psittacine birds, iohexol is equivalent to barium sulphate in terms of radiographic image quality. One of the advantages of iohexol over barium sulphate is its more rapid transit time through the gastrointestinal tract [7]. As suggested by previous literature [12], this rapid transit represents a potential clinical advantage that may facilitate a more rapid diagnostic process, thereby potentially helping to minimize handling stress in avian patients. Although iohexol allowed partial visualization of the ventriculus in radiographs taken prior to the first minute (0.83 min) in some cases in our study, unlike barium sulphate, the extremely rapid transit observed, particularly in the proximal gastrointestinal segments, complicates capturing detail using conventional radiographic methods. Barium sulphate suspension is one of the most commonly used agents in radiological contrast studies because it provides detailed information on the structure and mucosal surface of the GI tract [7, 13]. However, the use of barium sulphate is contraindicated in patients with suspected GI perforation due to the risk of inducing peritonitis [7, 13, 14]. In this context, iodine-based contrast agents, such as iohexol, are recommended as alternatives in cases where GI perforation is suspected [9, 13]. Iohexol is a non-ionic, iodinated contrast agent with low osmolality (520 mOsm/kg H₂O). Unlike high-osmolality iodinated contrast media, the low osmolality of iohexol does not promote fluid shifts from the circulation into the intestinal lumen, thereby reducing the risk of dehydration or diarrhoea, particularly in small or already dehydrated avian patients [13, 14]. In addition, in the event of aspiration—which may occur due to stress associated with radiographic procedures [7] —iohexol is expected, based on findings in mammals, to result in fewer adverse effects on the lungs and air sacs compared with barium sulphate [7, 14]. Owls (Strigiformes) are a widely distributed group of nocturnal birds of prey, for which information on GI transit times remains limited [2]. Previous studies in Barred owls (Strix varia) have reported ventriculus and small intestinal filling times of approximately 5 min, indicating relatively rapid transit compared with many other avian species [2]. These findings are consistent with the results of the present study. In addition, a median overall GI transit time of 60 min (range: 5–120 min) has been reported in Barred owls [2]. This transit time is shorter than the mean values described for psittacine birds and red-tailed hawks (Buteo jamaicensis) [2]. The observed interspecies differences are likely attributable to variations in metabolic rate and feeding strategies [2]. Unlike most other raptors, owls (Strigiformes) lack a crop. This anatomical distinction necessitates the administration of contrast medium directly into the distal third of the oesophagus or beyond during contrast studies. Previous studies have suggested that, compared with red-tailed hawks in which the contrast medium is administered into the crop, direct administration into the gastrointestinal tract may result in faster overall transit times [1, 2]. Although caeca are generally rudimentary in many raptor species, they are well developed in the order Strigiformes (owls), where they play critical roles in water reabsorption and nitrogen homeostasis. Anatomical analyses have demonstrated that the caeca of owls are relatively large and glandular, in contrast to the small, predominantly lymphoid caeca observed in hawks [1, 15]. This physiological adaptation is considered important for the processing of excess uric acid derived from a high-protein diet and for water conservation [1, 15]. Anatomically, birds of prey, including members of the order Strigiformes, possess gastrointestinal systems optimised for protein-rich diets. In Strigiformes, the well-developed caeca primarily function in water reabsorption and nitrogen homeostasis [1, 16]. To facilitate these processes, retrograde movement of intestinal contents from the rectum into the caeca occurs [15]. Evaluation of the serial radiographs obtained in the present study revealed that after reaching the large intestine, both contrast agents moved back into the caeca via retrograde peristalsis, resulting in increased radiographic conspicuity. This observation is consistent with general digestive physiology and previously reported findings. However, to clearly demonstrate true retrograde peristaltic activity, fluoroscopic imaging would be a more appropriate modality.

In this study, findings from comparing the concentrations of iohexol with those of barium sulphate using conventional radiography highlight the importance of selecting an appropriate contrast agent in clinical practice. Consistent with these findings, previous studies have reported that Barred owls administered barium sulphate and evaluated using fluoroscopy remained clinically healthy, with no adverse effects attributable to contrast administration [2]. Similarly, in a study conducted in medium-sized psittacine birds using iohexol and barium sulphate, no regurgitation or aspiration was observed, appetite remained normal, and no body weight loss was recorded [7]. Our results support this, as the 25 ml/kg dose remains well within the safety margin, considering the estimated 50 ml/kg gastric capacity of healthy raptors [11]. The absence of complications associated with either contrast agent and the successful release of all owls back into the wild suggest that the applied protocols—namely conventional radiography and contrast administration via gavage—can be used safely in this raptor species.

The use of conventional radiography in the present study differs methodologically from that of some studies reported in the literature. While conventional radiography provides only static images of organ position and contrast distribution at discrete time points, fluoroscopy is considered the ideal modality for real-time assessment of GI motility and peristaltic activity [2, 3, 5, 14, 17]. Therefore, the data presented in this study reflect quantitative ‘transit velocity’ rather than qualitative ‘motility patterns’ (e.g., strength of peristaltic contractions), which would require real-time fluoroscopic evaluation to assess. The repeated positioning and manual restraint required for conventional radiography may increase stress levels, which in turn can influence GI motility and potentially confound results [2–4]. In the present study, the absence of anaesthesia or sedation—often required during conventional radiographic procedures—minimised the potential chemical effects on gastrointestinal motility [2, 3]. Nevertheless, stress associated with manual restraint remains a potential limitation, given its possible impact on motility parameters [2]. In raptors, real-time evaluation of GI motility, including ventricular contractions and intestinal peristalsis, is clinically important for the early diagnosis of pathological hypomotility or dysmotility disorders [2, 3]. Although fluoroscopy is considered the gold standard for real-time evaluation of GI motility, including peristaltic waves, conventional radiography remains far more widely available and is more commonly used in clinical practice. This study therefore demonstrates that, for clinicians without access to advanced imaging modalities, iohexol is a safe alternative contrast agent and reliable gastrointestinal transit times can be obtained using conventional radiography.

Both contrast agents, administered as 30% solutions at a dose of 25 ml/kg, were used safely in Barn owls; no complications were observed, and all individuals were released back into the wild in good health. Iohexol demonstrated a statistically significant shorter oesophagus-to-cloaca transit time (p = 0.039). The ability to safely perform GI contrast radiography in owls and to achieve successful post-procedural release without complications underscores the value of this diagnostic approach in raptor rehabilitation medicine.

The limitations of this study include the small sample size (n = 8) due to the challenges of working with wild raptors and the use of conventional radiography instead of fluoroscopy. However, the randomized crossover design minimized bias related to the order of administration. Another limitation of our study is that all birds were evaluated after a standard fasting period; consequently, these findings may not fully reflect transit times in clinical cases in which the gastrointestinal tract is not completely empty. Furthermore, the rapid transit induced by iohexol’s hyperosmolarity may have hindered the capture of instantaneous anatomical detail by conventional radiography in the absence of fluoroscopy or high-speed serial imaging.

Conclusions

In conclusion, based on the significantly faster gastrointestinal transit time observed in this study, and considering its safety profile in suspected GI perforations reported in the literature, iohexol may be preferred over barium sulphate in Barn owl patients in which time is critical or urgent surgical intervention is required [7–9]. Nevertheless, in chronic cases requiring detailed mucosal evaluation, the diagnostic value of barium sulphate should not be overlooked. The total transit time of the contrast medium during contrast-enhanced gastrointestinal imaging in owls may vary according to the patient’s general condition and degree of gastrointestinal fullness. Furthermore, in positive contrast studies utilising iohexol, radiographic intervals should be kept short to ensure the complete visualisation of gastrointestinal segments, given the rapid total transit time. We believe that the physiological values established for the gastrointestinal system will serve as a significant data source for the diagnosis and treatment of various pathological conditions.
