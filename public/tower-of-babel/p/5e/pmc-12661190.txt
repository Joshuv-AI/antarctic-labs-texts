Reconsidering peer-based interventions for loneliness: reflections on the feasibility and future directions of the Home Support Intervention Program
Authors: Schawanya Kaewpitoon Rattanapitoon, Nav La, Nathkapach Kaewpitoon Rattanapitoon
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

We read the recent article by Kousha et al. [1] titled, “Effectiveness of an informal home care support intervention program to reduce loneliness and improve quality of life among lonely community-dwelling older adults: a feasibility study,” published in Korean Journal of Family Medicine, with great interest. The authors deserve recognition for addressing a critical public health issue: the psychosocial isolation of older adults, particularly in low-resource settings.

The Home Support Intervention Program (HoSIP), which leverages peer-to-peer support through both face-to-face and online interactions, represents an innovative and contextually grounded response to the unmet social needs of aging populations. The study’s integration of emotional, informational, instrumental, and affiliative support, underpinned by digital engagement via WhatsApp, is consistent with emerging global trends that leverage digital platforms to enhance informal care delivery among the aging population.

Scientific significance and innovative aspects of the intervention

The design of HoSIP aligns well with models of community-based participatory research, allowing for the co-creation of interventions tailored to local cultural norms. The use of validated instruments (e.g., the UCLA Loneliness Scale and Control, Autonomy, Self‐Realization and Pleasure Scale) supports the methodological rigor of outcome assessments. Moreover, the study offers important longitudinal data points at 3 and 6 months post-intervention, an aspect often missing from similar peer support interventions.

A noteworthy strength lies in the program’s persistence, beyond the active involvement of the research team. Nearly two-thirds of participants continued to support each other after the formal intervention phase, suggesting a degree of sustainability and community ownership.

Methodological considerations and limitations

Despite these strengths, the following methodological limitations require careful consideration:

Lack of randomization and blinding

The quasi-experimental design without randomization and baseline group matching (especially regarding education level and digital literacy) introduces confounding variables. For instance, the intervention group had a significantly higher educational level, which likely influenced their engagement with smartphone-based platforms.

Control group context

The control group was drawn from a separate city (Aliabade Katul) with distinct social contexts. This geographic separation may have introduced biases that were not adequately controlled for.

Selection and attrition biases

The final sample size (intervention: n=36 and control: n=51) was reduced owing to the impact and constraints of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, leading to a potential self-selection bias where more socially inclined or health-conscious individuals remained in the intervention arm.

Short intervention duration and follow-up

Although the follow-up period was extended to 6 months, the intervention itself lasted only 12 weeks. Although feasible, the long-term effects on loneliness, especially without structured reinforcement, remain unclear.

Implications for policy and broader scientific context

Globally, peer-based support interventions have demonstrated success in improving psychosocial outcomes in older adults [2,3]. However, most models are nested within broader geriatric service ecosystems. In contrast, the HoSIP appears to function in the absence of sustained organizational infrastructure or integration with formal health systems.

The sustainable scale-up of such interventions would require policy-level endorsement and partnerships with local health authorities or non-governmental organizations. Programs such as the Campaign to End Loneliness in the UK or Community Networks for Seniors in Singapore exemplify how informal peer networks can be embedded within formal eldercare frameworks.

Moreover, the use of digital tools among older adults is influenced by socioeconomic status, cognitive function, and prior digital exposure [4]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, online interactions often served as temporary surrogates for face-to-face contact, helping to maintain social connectivity during isolation [5]. Therefore, the reliance of HoSIP on WhatsApp, while culturally appropriate and cost-effective, may exclude the most digitally marginalized subgroups, raising concerns regarding equity.

Recommendations for future research

We recommend the following directions for future research: (1) Conduct a fully powered randomized controlled trial with stratified randomization based on key demographic and digital literacy factors. (2) Include objective measures of social engagement (e.g., mobile usage logs and visit frequencies) in addition to self-reported scales. (3) Explore hybrid models that combine peer mentoring with professional oversight to enhance clinical relevance and safety. (4) Investigate cost-effectiveness to inform potential policy adoption.
