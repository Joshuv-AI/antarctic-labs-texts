Case Report: Retained gallstone migrating to the chest wall after cholecystectomy presenting as chronic abscess, sinus tract, and inflammatory pseudotumor
Authors: Weimin Li, Yi Zhao, Wenting Zhou, Zhigang Xiu, Yi Luo, Yubo Kan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGallbladder perforation and gallstone spillage are common during laparoscopic cholecystectomy, occurring in up to 40% and 6%–10% of cases, respectively. While most retained stones remain asymptomatic, 2.3%–8.5% of patients may present with intra-abdominal abscess, granuloma formation, abdominal wall sinus, fistula or a mass mimicking a malignancy (1). We report a unique case of a retained gallstone migrating to the chest wall 2 years after cholecystectomy, forming a chronic abscess, sinus tract, and inflammatory pseudotumor.Case presentationA 61-years-old woman with a history of laparoscopic cholecystectomy 2 years prior presented with a 1-month history of right chest pain and a palpable chest wall mass. Computed tomography (CT) revealed a 2.4 cm × 2.2 cm soft tissue mass adjacent to the right 10th rib with an internal ring-like hyperdensity, focal rib erosion, and pleural thickening. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) showed a nodular T2 hypointense lesion (approximately 2.7 cm) adjacent to the right hemidiaphragm and posterior to the right hepatic lobe, with surrounding inflammatory exudation and ipsilateral chest wall soft tissue swelling, along with a small right pleural effusion and intrahepatic bile duct stones. The chest wall lesion was initially concerning for malignancy. At surgery, a 6 × 5 × 4 cm cystic mass was excised from the right posterolateral chest wall. Inside the cyst, a 3 cm spherical gallstone was found embedded in purulent material, with a sinus tract extending toward the pleura. Histopathology showed chronic suppurative inflammation, abscess formation, multinucleated giant cells, and granulation tissue, consistent with inflammatory pseudotumor secondary to foreign body reaction. The patient recovered uneventfully and remained asymptomatic at 6-months follow-up.ConclusionGallstones spilled during cholecystectomy can migrate to extra-abdominal sites years later, causing chronic chest wall pathology that mimics malignancy. A ring-like hyperdensity on CT and a T2 hypointense nodule on MRI in a post-cholecystectomy patient should raise suspicion for a retained gallstone. Complete surgical excision is curative.

Introduction

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is the gold standard for symptomatic cholelithiasis. Gallbladder perforation during the procedure is common (10%–40%), and gallstone spillage occurs in approximately 6%–10% of cases (1, 2). While most intraperitoneally spilled stones remain asymptomatic, 2.3%–8.5% of patients develop delayed complications, including intra-abdominal abscess, granuloma formation, wound sinus or fistula, and small bowel obstruction (3, 4).

Migration of spilled gallstones beyond the peritoneal cavity is uncommon, but this complication is well recognized among surgeons who perform laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Most reported cases have described stones reaching the pleural cavity, causing cholecystopleural fistula, biliothorax, or empyema (5–7). Nevertheless, to our knowledge, a retained gallstone migrating into the chest wall with chronic abscess, sinus tract formation, and pathological features of an inflammatory pseudotumor has seldom been reported. This case is noteworthy because the stone remained extrapleural, embedded in the chest wall musculature, and produced a mass lesion that radiologically and clinically mimicked a malignant neoplasm. This case highlights the need for heightened clinical suspicion in post-cholecystectomy patients with unexplained chest wall pathology.

Case description

Patient information

The patient was a 61-years-old Chinese woman with an 11-years history of type 2 diabetes mellitus (managed with metformin, acarbose, and glimepiride). Her surgical history was significant for laparoscopic cholecystectomy for cholelithiasis performed 2 years prior at an outside hospital. The operative report from that procedure did not mention gallbladder perforation or stone spillage.

Chief complaint and history of present illness

The patient presented with a 1-month history of right-sided chest wall pain and a palpable mass that had progressively enlarged over the preceding 2 weeks. The pain was localized to the right lower posterolateral chest wall and was described as dull, non-radiating, and worse with lying on the right side. She denied dyspnea, hemoptysis, fever, chills, night sweats, or weight loss. There was no history of thoracoabdominal trauma or any thoracic intervention since her cholecystectomy.

Physical examination

On admission, the patient was afebrile (36.5 °C), with blood pressure 142/57 mmHg, heart rate 87 beats/min, respiratory rate 20 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation 97% on room air. Palpation of the right lower chest wall revealed a firm, fixed, mildly tender subcutaneous mass measuring approximately 4 cm × 3 cm. Overlying skin was intact without erythema, warmth, or fluctuance. No lymphadenopathy was present. Breath sounds were clear bilaterally.

Past medical and surgical history

Type 2 diabetes mellitus for 11 years.

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy (2 years prior).

No known drug allergies.

No smoking or alcohol abuse.

Diagnostic assessment

Preoperative ultrasound of the right chest wall (Figure 1a) revealed a hypoechoic mass deep to the muscle layer. Preoperative imaging included contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the chest (Figure 1b), which demonstrated a 2.4 cm × 2.2 cm irregular soft tissue mass adjacent to the right 10th posterior rib and the right hemidiaphragm. The mass had a ring-like hyperdensity with a hypodense center. There was focal erosion of the right 10th rib, adjacent pleural thickening, and a small pleural effusion. The mass extended posteriorly to the latissimus dorsi muscle (Figure 1c).

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was performed to evaluate the biliary tree and to further characterize the chest wall lesion (Figure 1d). MRCP showed a nodular T2 hypointense lesion measuring approximately 2.7 cm located adjacent to the right hemidiaphragm and posterior to the right hepatic lobe (corresponding to the CT mass). Surrounding the nodule, there were patchy exudative changes (T2 hyperintense) consistent with inflammatory reaction, and the right chest wall soft tissues appeared swollen. The lesion did not show communication with the biliary tract. Additionally, MRCP revealed small stones within the right intrahepatic bile ducts and a possible small stone at the distal common bile duct. No biliary dilatation was present. The gallbladder was absent, with a small fluid signal noted in the gallbladder fossa, suggesting a possible remnant cystic duct or gallbladder stump. A small right pleural effusion was also noted. A right kidney cyst was incidentally found.

Laboratory tests revealed a normal white blood cell count (7.2 × 109/L) and C-reactive protein (8 mg/L). Liver function tests were normal. Tumor markers (CEA, CA19-9) were within normal limits.

The differential diagnosis included retained foreign body (specifically, spilled gallstone) with inflammatory mass, chest wall sarcoma, metastatic disease, lymphoma, tuberculous abscess, actinomycosis, and retained foreign body with inflammatory mass. The ring-like hyperdensity on CT and the T2 hypointense nodule on MRI strongly suggested a calcified structure, and a spilled gallstone was considered the primary diagnostic possibility, despite the prior operative report not documenting spillage, because absence of documentation does not definitively exclude intraoperative gallstone loss.

Intraoperative frozen section was performed to rule out malignancy, and definitive histopathology of the excised specimen confirmed the diagnosis.

Therapeutic intervention

Following a multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussion (Table 1), the patient underwent surgery under general anesthesia with double-lumen endotracheal intubation. A thoracoscopic approach was chosen because preoperative imaging could not definitively exclude malignancy, and the lesion was intimately associated with the diaphragm and ribs, raising concern for pleural or intrapulmonary involvement. The MDT consensus favored thoracoscopic exploration to ensure complete assessment and safe resection. A thoracoscopic approach was first performed: a 2 cm port at the right 5th–6th intercostal space (posterior axillary line) and a 4 cm working port at the right 6th–7th intercostal space (anterior axillary line). Thoracoscopy revealed dense adhesions between the right lower lobe and the diaphragm at the location of the mass but no pleural effusion or studding.

A separate 6 cm transverse incision was made directly over the chest wall mass. Dissection through skin, subcutaneous tissue, and latissimus dorsi muscle exposed a 6 × 5 × 4 cm cystic mass with a thick fibrotic wall (approximately 1 cm thick). The cyst was tightly adherent to the 10th rib, with focal bony depression caused by chronic erosion. The cyst was opened, yielding 10–15 mL of thick, yellowish-purulent material. A 3 cm spherical, dark brown gallstone was found embedded within the purulent matrix. A sinus tract extending from the cyst toward the parietal pleura was identified and carefully dissected; it did not enter the pleural cavity proper (Figure 3).

The cyst, sinus tract, and involved chest wall soft tissues were completely excised en bloc, including the eroded segment of the 10th rib. The pleural cavity was irrigated with dilute iodine solution. A chest tube was placed via the thoracoscopic port, and a closed-suction drain was placed in the chest wall wound.

Intraoperative frozen section of the chest wall mass reported: “Chronic suppurative inflammation with abscess formation, foam cell aggregation, multinucleated giant cell reaction, and granulation tissue proliferation – no malignancy identified, consistent with inflammatory pseudotumor.”

Follow-up and outcomes

The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit for overnight observation. On postoperative day (POD) 1, she was hemodynamically stable, tolerating a regular diet, and ambulating independently. Chest tube output was minimal (50 mL serosanguinous fluid over 24 h). Chest X-ray showed no pneumothorax. The chest tube was removed on POD 2. The chest wall drain was removed on POD 3 when output decreased to <10 mL/24 h.

The patient was discharged home on POD 6 in good condition, on a 3-weeks course of levofloxacin 750 mg daily based on intraoperative culture results (which grew no organisms). At 6-months follow-up, she remained asymptomatic, with complete healing of the surgical wound and no clinical or radiological evidence of recurrence.

Discussion

Strengths and limitations

This case has several strengths. It provides a complete diagnostic and therapeutic journey from presentation to 6-months follow-up, with multimodality imaging (CT, MRCP, ultrasound), intraoperative findings, histopathology, and MDT input. The MRI finding of a T2 hypointense nodule with surrounding inflammatory exudation has not been previously emphasized in similar cases. The main limitation is that it is a single case report, and the incidence of such a complication cannot be determined. Additionally, the prior operative report did not document stone spillage, so the exact migration mechanism of the gallstone cannot be fully clarified. Nevertheless, the trajectory of gallstone migration is likely influenced by its initial intra-abdominal location: a stone situated beneath the diaphragm adjacent to the lung may migrate into the pleural cavity, whereas a stone located near the chest wall may migrate into the thoracic wall, as observed in the present case. Our case also illustrates that in some instances, abscess formation or local inflammation may prompt clinical recognition before the stone migrates beyond the abdominal cavity.

Comparison with the literature

Gallbladder perforation and stone spillage are common during laparoscopic cholecystectomy, with most retained stones remaining asymptomatic (1–4). However, delayed complications occur in 2.3%–8.5% of patients (3, 4). Thoracic complications are rare. Cunningham et al. (5) reported a cholecystopleural fistula with cholelithiasis presenting as a pleural effusion. Lee et al. (6) described a biliopleural fistula after percutaneous transhepatic gallbladder drainage. Chopra et al. (7) reported cholelithoptysis (expectoration of gallstones) and pleural empyema.

Our case is distinct from these reports because:

1. The stone migrated to the chest wall rather than into the pleural space or bronchial tree.

2. It produced an inflammatory pseudotumor – a benign but mass-forming lesion that mimicked malignancy both clinically and radiologically.

3. The CT finding of a ring-like hyperdensity with central hypodensity and adjacent rib erosion is a specific clue that, in a post-cholecystectomy patient, should raise suspicion for a retained gallstone.

4. The MRI appearance – a T2 hypointense nodule (due to calcification) with surrounding T2 hyperintense inflammatory exudation and chest wall soft tissue swelling – further supports the diagnosis and helps differentiate from malignant soft tissue tumors, which typically do not show such marked surrounding inflammatory change without necrosis.

To our knowledge, only one similar case has been reported: Gaster et al. (8) described a chronic cutaneous chest wall fistula due to retained gallstones after laparoscopic cholecystectomy. However, their case did not emphasize the inflammatory pseudotumor pathology or the ring-like hyperdensity sign on CT, nor did it include MRI characterization. Our case adds these novel observations.

Patient perspective

The patient was interviewed after her 6-months follow-up visit. She provided the following perspective (translated from Chinese, edited for clarity and anonymity):

“When I first felt the lump on my chest, I was worried it might be cancer. The doctors also said it was not clear what it was. I was very scared before the surgery. They did an MRI that showed a dark spot near my liver, but still no one was sure. After the operation, they showed me the stone they removed from my chest – it looked exactly like a gallstone. I could not believe it had come from my gallbladder surgery 2 years ago. I am very grateful that it was not cancer. The surgery went well, and I recovered quickly. Now I feel completely normal and have no pain. I hope my story can help other patients who have similar lumps after gallbladder surgery, so they know it might just be a stone and not something serious.” Supplementary material containing the CARE Checklist is available online.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that gallstones spilled during laparoscopic cholecystectomy can migrate to distant extra-abdominal sites, including the chest wall, years after the initial surgery. Such complications can mimic malignant neoplasms on imaging and physical examination. A high index of suspicion is required when evaluating post-cholecystectomy patients with unexplained chest wall pathology. Contrast-enhanced CT with attention to ring-like hyperdense foci and MRI with T2 hypointense nodule characterization can provide critical diagnostic clues. Complete surgical excision of the stone and associated inflammatory tissue is curative. Clinicians should document gallbladder perforation and stone spillage in the operative record to aid future diagnosis.
