When infection meets malignancy: A case report of a rare collision lesion of leiomyosarcoma and amebic cyst in the gluteal region
Authors: Mohammad Rida, Maryam Mansour, Yorgho Maalouf, Sami Slaba, Ismat Ghanem, Antoine Haddad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We report on a 61-year-old male patient who presented with a chronic left buttock mass and pain. Initial MRI revealed a multiloculated mass in the gluteal region. The mass was percutaneously drained after initial microbiology analysis identified it as an isolated amebic cyst. However, the patient showed only partial improvement after 1 month of drainage and conservative management. Subsequently, surgical resection of the mass was performed. Histological examination of the resected tissue revealed an exceptionally rare diagnosis: a collision lesion of a leiomyosarcoma and an amebic cyst.

Introduction

Human amebiasis is a parasitic gastrointestinal infectious disease primarily caused by Entamoeba histolytica. The parasite typically follows an oral-fecal cycle through the ingestion of contaminated food and water, though some cases of infection have been reported without gastrointestinal involvement [1,2]. Clinical features range from an asymptomatic state to severe amoebic colitis, with diarrhea and abdominal pain as the main symptoms. Rarely, E. histolytica can spread outside the gastrointestinal tract to affect the liver via the portal vein system, as well as the lungs, brain, heart, kidneys, skin, and soft tissues, manifesting as abscesses in these organs [3,4]. Cutaneous infection is very rare and has been described in the perianal, perineal, genital, and abdominal wall skin [2]. To our knowledge, only 2 previous cases of infection and abscess formation in the gluteal region have been reported [5,6].

Soft tissue sarcoma (STS) is a rare adult malignancy with numerous subtypes. Leiomyosarcoma (LMS), which arises from smooth muscle, accounts for 10%-20% of all STSs. It most commonly occurs in the retroperitoneum, uterus, and large blood vessels, and less frequently in the extremities [7,8].

Both LMS and amebic abscesses in the soft tissues, particularly in the lower extremities, are rare diseases. Furthermore, no previous case has been reported of these 2 distinct lesions occurring in the same location. We present a case of the collision of a LMS and an amebic abscess in the left buttock region, which presented as a single multiloculated mass and was histologically confirmed after surgical resection.

Case report

A 61-year-old white male patient presented to our radiology department for evaluation of a large left buttock mass and pain. His past medical history is nonsignificant.

History started 8 months ago, when he started complaining of low back and left buttock pain for which NSAIDs were prescribed. He felt partial improvement. Due to increased pain and swelling in the left gluteal region, a hip MRI was done (Fig. 1A-H). It showed a voluminous mass measuring 18.4×11.5×16.6 cm, which is very heterogeneous (“triple sign”?), with protein-rich and/or haemorrhagic components and without fluid-fluid level or a sedimentation level. This well demarcated mass revealed multiple cavities delineated by numerous irregular septations of variable thickness, with a few T2-hyposignal septations, without obvious calcification on Xray (Fig. 2). There was no evidence of an isolated fat lobule other than the displaced inter-muscular fat. Dynamic contrast injection did not delineate any nodular nor linear enhancement within this mass or the various septations. No adjacent iliac bone erosion or involvement was noted nor edema of the adjacent compressed muscles. No large lymph nodes were noted in the pelvis. No high flow vessel enlargement was seen within or adjacent to this mass. The appearance could be suggestive of a highly hemorrhagic lesion with no sedimentation level. The hypothesis of an undifferentiated soft-tissue lesion of lipomyxomatous or synovial origin could not be ruled out, despite the absence of enhancement, hence the decision of biopsy was made for histopathology correlation. Before doing the biopsy, a CT Scan (Fig. 3) was performed to confirm the absence of calcifications. The final histology of the resected presumed isolated amebic cyst was consistent with pleomorphic and myxoid leiomyosarcoma FNCLCC grade 3 (T = 18 cm), with focally tangent surgical resection borders. No Entamoeba histolytica was seen at any stage in the final specimen.

Discussion

Few cases of coexisting parasitic infections and tumors have been reported in the literature. A case of amebic lung abscess presenting simultaneously with lung carcinoma has been reported, demonstrating that the infection delayed the diagnosis of the underlying tumor [9]. This was also the case with our patient. However, a collision of a concomitant amebic abscess with a myxoid leiomyosarcoma in the gluteal region is exceptional and has not been described previously to our knowledge.

Extra-intestinal amebiasis is very rare. It typically occurs secondary to the dissemination of the parasite from the gastrointestinal tract, and uncommonly develops as a primary infection, especially in the skin and soft tissues. Dissemination of E. histolytica can occur via various routes: hematogenous, lymphatic, fistulization to the skin, in particular from the rectum or other genital organs, or even through infection of skin surrounding colostomies or following the drainage of hepatic abscesses. In rare cases, a primary infection can occur after direct contact of wounded skin with contaminated materials, allowing the parasite to disseminate to soft tissues and subcutaneous regions [2,4]. Therefore, infection of the gluteal region may result from 1 of these possibilities. In the 2 previously reported cases of gluteal amebic abscesses, Morton and Soutar proposed lymphatic spread from the rectum as a hypothesis [5], while Sengupta et al. found that a gluteal abscess resulted from a fistula from the cecum [6].

In our case, the patient denied any dysenteric symptoms in the past few months. While this does not completely rule out a prior asymptomatic or paucisymptomatic digestive infection, the probability of systemic dissemination without general signs or involvement of other organs is low. The hypothesis of an infection by fistulization from pelvic organs, especially the rectum, has a very low incidence, as neither the physical exam nor the MRI showed a fistulous tract reaching the mass. The hypothesis of a primary infection is also considered, given the presence of a skin wound in the region of the abscess. However, the absence of any superficial cutaneous lesions calls this possibility into question, as deep amebiasis cutis is often associated with co-existent visceral disease [10], which was not found in our patient. Therefore, the origin of the abscess and the transmission pathway were not definitively proven, particularly since no stool culture was performed due to the absence of gastrointestinal symptoms.

The treatment of a gluteal amoebic abscess is based on the same principles as the treatment for a liver amebic abscess. First-line treatment consists of antibiotics combined with percutaneous drainage under radiological guidance. If no significant improvement is noted, surgical management is considered [11], which was the course of action in our case. The surgery revealed that the amoebic abscess was coexisting with a myxoid leiomyosarcoma, which explains the limited improvement of the lesion in the gluteal region after initial treatment.

Soft tissue myxoid leiomyosarcoma is a rare type of soft tissue malignant tumor. It usually presents on MRI as a lesion with low to intermediate signal intensity on T1-weighted images and high signal intensity on T2-weighted images, which corresponds to the myxoid part of the tumor [12]. The enhancement of such tumors is variable. In our case, however, the MRI showed a heterogeneous signal, revealing that the lesion contained other components associated with the myxomatous one. The coexistence of the amoebic abscess may be an explanation for the described signal, along with the possible presence of necrotic components in the tumor. The simultaneous presence of these 2 lesions can be interpreted as either a superinfection of the tumor by the parasite or as an independent coexistence.

Conclusion

Soft tissue myxoid leiomyosarcoma is a rare adult malignancy, and an isolated extra-digestive gluteal amoebic cyst is an extremely rare entity. The collision of these 2 lesions is an exceptionally rare diagnosis. Given the rarity and complexity of such cases, it is crucial for clinicians to remain vigilant when faced with soft tissue masses. Prompt and thorough investigations, particularly through advanced imaging, are essential for an accurate diagnosis. A multiloculated mass with protein-rich and hemorrhagic components on MRI, especially in the absence of septal enhancement, should raise suspicion for a parasitic infection in the differential diagnosis. Conversely, if a parasitic abscess does not respond to conservative management, it should prompt consideration of an underlying malignancy. Ultimately, surgical excision remains the treatment of choice for managing these challenging and uncommon lesions.
