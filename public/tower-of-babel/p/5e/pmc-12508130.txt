Vapor-induced porosity in graphene/PDMS: a scalable route to high-performance pressure sensors
Authors: Nadeem Tariq Beigh, Nouha Alcheikh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the present paradigm of flexible and wearable technologies, piezoresistive sensors hold immense promise as e-skin in sensing applications. Introducing porosity in these sensors elevates the performance multi-fold. However, fabrication of porous piezoresistive sensors is complicated, energy-intensive and cost-ineffective, negating their pertinent advantages. We present a new method of developing porous, thin films based piezoresistive sensors by utilizing the inherent vaporability of ethanol to introduce controlled porosity in graphene nanoplatelet (GNP)/ polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) nanocomposites. The resulting vapor-channeled (VC) GNP/PDMS is formed without utilizing scaffolds, skeletons, high temperature etching or prolonged chemical processing. The fabrication process for porous nanocomposites is highly repeatable and controllable; the fabricated VC-GNP/PDMS thin films are reliable and show immense promise as flexible/wearable pressure sensors. The VC-GNP/PDMS achieves an achieve exceptional compressibility (up to 68.97% strain) without structural failure, yielding a flexible pressure sensor with an unprecedented linear response (R2 = 0.99) across an ultra-wide dynamic range up to 2.5 MPa and a high sensitivity of 33.2% MPa-1. The engineered porosity and micro-structure synergistically enable a tunable gauge factor, shifting from 0.66 (0-45% strain) to 1.72 (>45% strain). Critically, the sensor exhibits negligible hysteresis (1.08%), remarkable long-term stability over 5 weeks, and rapid response/relaxation (0.3/0.7 s), alongside robust insensitivity to temperature (25-60 °C) and humidity (5-100% RH). This unique fabrication strategy and the resulting high-performance pressure sensor, offering exceptional tunability in sensitivity and range, position it as a leading candidate for next-generation, cost-effective tactile biomechanical sensing.Subject terms: Nanoparticles, Electrical and electronic engineering, Nanoparticles

Introduction

Flexible pressure sensors are a crucial component of smart sensor systems within the paradigm of personalized healthcare1,2, e-skin3,4, soft robotics5,6, smart automation7,8, and sports sciences9. There have been numerous studies on flexible pressure sensors based on piezoelectric10,11, triboelectric12,13, capacitive14,15, and hybrid15,16 transductions developed via thin films17, nanocomposites18, multilayer structures19, multi-dimensional interfaces20,21. However, the inherent capability of piezoresistive pressure sensors to change their electrical resistance with applied force and retain the resistance change until the applied force is maintained makes them a viable choice over the existing pressure sensors22–24. Nonetheless, new pressure sensors with high dynamic range and ultra-sensitivity are essential for measuring a wide range of physiological characteristics in humans, ranging from a whole-body movement to minute micro-circulation dynamics25. The major goal of all the previous studies have been to improve the sensitivity and sensing range by modification of base matrix conductivity26, micro-structure27,28, and morphology29. The addition of nano-dopants in the form of conductive fillers and the mixing of conductive polymers to the base matrix are the most reported studies30,31. It is worth noting that pressure sensitivity and sensing range are strongly correlated to spatial deformations that initiate resistance change via conductive pathways in the nanocomposite32. Even though, solid or liquid doping of the base matrix enhances the mechanical strength and oxidation resistance but on it significantly impairs the much-needed flexibility and stretchability of the nanocomposite33–35.

Ideally, a flexible pressure sensor should have excellent mechanical strength, a moderate elastic modulus, high compressibility and stretchability, a large deformation range, and high conformability to adhere to a variety of surface topologies7,36. One of the most intriguing methods to transform conventional nanocomposites based flexible pressure sensors into a near-ideal sensor is the introduction of porosity37. Porous nanocomposites for pressure sensing applications can be realized with plethora of approaches, including aerogels38, solvent evaporation39, phase separation40, emulsion templating41, 3D molds42, soft/hard templating43, and salt/sugar substitution44. The resultant pores in the nanocomposite matrix improve flexibility, pressure sensitivity, and dynamic sensing range by acting as zero-stiffness dopants and lowering the matrix’s overall modulus45. Recently, He et al. reported a carbon nanotube (CNT)/PDMS foam prepared with a combined method oil-in-water emulsion and dual-solvent ice templating. The sensor showed a high sensitivity of 5.5 kPa−1 in a working range of 0-9.2 kPa46. Qin et al. reported a reduced graphene oxide (rGO)/PI composite prepared with freeze-drying and heat treatment (argon thermal annealing at 300 °C). The resulting sensor has a sensitivity of 0.18 kPa−1 within the working range of 0–1.5 kPa47. A large-area, high-pressure sensor with a sensitivity of 0.26 kPa−1 in the 0–2 kPa range was fabricated by Yao et al. using a single conductive filler graphene (GE) nano-sheet as a conductive coating on the PU sponge48. Other works report the use of expensive, complex, time extensive and energy inefficient methods and materials to achieve higher sensitivity and sensing ranges49–51. In all the reported work on piezoresistive pressure sensors, the deformation of porous nanocomposite leads to compaction around the porous channels and results in surge in the elastic modulus. This phenomenon limits further compression and stabilizes any resistance change as well as the working range to 1000 kPa. The major reason for this limitation is the non-uniform dopant concentration and spacing within the micro-channels around the pores. The conventional mechanisms of porous structure fabrication utilize bake-first approach, wherein the pores are created after the matrix has solidified. The dopant concentration and spacing in such cases is correlated to nanocomposite mixing parameters rather than capillary forces. A high concentration of conductive dopants in the micro-channels can overcome the issues related to channel compaction by enabling large resistance changes near the pressure limits and thereby enhance the pressure sensitivity and sensing ranges.

On this context, we report a novel porous nanocomposite consisting of vapor-channeled polydimethylsiloxane and graphene nanoplatelets (GNPs). The inclusion of ethanol during the nanocomposite mixing process results in nanocomposite bubbling. By optimizing, the bake cycle and using a “parallel bake” approach, the nanocomposite porosity can be tuned to achieve a high-pressure sensitivity and large pressure sensing range for biomechanical and tactile sensing applications. Compared with non-porous nanocomposite, this optimized nanocomposite exhibits a 7-fold enhancement in pressure sensitivity and 2.25-fold improvement in sensing range without using any complex fabrication method or high entropy processing. Furthermore, the VC-GNP/PDMS nanocomposite translates into a proof-of-concept integrated pressure sensor, capable of biomechanical sensing for smart healthcare, sports science, gait assessment. Materials and methods

Experimental setup

The VC-GNP/PDMS, non-porous GNP/PDMS and pristine PDMS nanocomposites were mechanically characterized with a universal testing machine (UTM-X350-20) using a 2000 Kgf load cell. The resistance measurements were carried out by integrating the Keithley 2450 source-meter with the UTM (Fig. S1). The wearable sensor prototypes are integrated SAMD21 Cortex®-M0 + 32 bit low power ARM MCU, equipped with ublox NINA-W102 radio module. The sensor data is acquired in real-time using Microsoft Data Streamer. DHT22 sensor from Adafruit is used as reference temperature and humidity for cross-sensitivity study. The FLIR C5 thermal camera is used to validate the sample temperature for cross-sensitivity study.

Material acquisition

PDMS curing agent and elastomer (SYLGARD™ 184 Silicone Elastomer Kit) is acquired from Dow Corning. Graphene nanoplatelets (5 µm particle size, surface area 50-80m2/gm, xGnP H-5) are acquired from Sigma Aldrich. Ethanol (99.99%) is acquired from ESTS-UAE. The flexible IDEs (150 µm Kapton and 30 µm stiffeners) are fabricated from JLCPCB, China.

Introducing porosity

The underlying principle of creating a porous structure for the proposed porous pressure sensor is based on a simple yet innovative method of vapor-induced pores or channels in the GNP/PDMS nanocomposite (shown in Fig. 3, detailed in experimental section). Our proposed bake cycle leverages a short-duration, high-temperature hot-bake followed by a long-duration, low-temperature open bake. The initial high-temperature curing of the ethanol-mixed GNP/PDMS nanocomposite is critical for initiating rapid bubble nucleation, driven by the supersaturation of ethanol vapor within the PDMS matrix. The rate of nucleation is highly dependent on the ethanol’s partial pressure (dictated by its initial volume and the system temperature) and the local energy barriers within the viscous PDMS52.

The process of micro-channel formation in the GNP/PDMS nanocomposite. a Uncured GNP/PDMS nanocomposite with ethanol; b the start of hot bake cycle results in bubble formation at the bottom; c the bubble formation proceeds with displacement of GNPs to narrow channels; d the open-bake cycle cures the vapor induced channels with a high GNP concentration; e The FEM simulation of non-porous and porous nanocomposites highlighting the stress and displacement gains due to porosity introduction

As these initial bubbles form, their growth is governed by mass transfer of ethanol vapor from the surrounding supersaturated matrix into the bubble, influenced by both temperature-dependent vapor pressure and the viscosity of the curing PDMS. The upward progression of these bubbles, as described, leads to the controlled relocation of uniformly dispersed GNPs, forming micro-channels. Bubble coalescence is minimized and controlled by the increasing viscosity of the partially cured PDMS, which inhibits uncontrolled merging and helps sustain the individual micro-channels, resulting in the desired porous network53. A quantitative understanding of these phenomena could be further explored through physical models such as classical nucleation theory (for bubble nucleation rates)54, diffusion-limited growth models (for bubble expansion)55, and rheological models describing the PDMS viscosity evolution during curing56. These models provide a predictive framework for how ethanol volume precisely dictates initial supersaturation and vapor generation, while specific temperature profiles control the kinetics of nucleation and subsequent growth, ultimately dictating the final pore size distribution and interconnectedness, thereby enhancing the controllability of porosity. A simple 2D Multiphysics FEM simulation is performed in COMSOL Multiphysics 6.2 to validate the enhancement of stress translation and deformation range in the porous nanocomposite over the non-porous nanocomposite (Fig. 3e). The simulation study uses solid mechanics physics to evaluate the displacement and stress ranges, and it is performed on a PDMS substrate. These prevalent enhancements and simple mechanism of introducing the porosity project this research as a new paradigm in the design of porous sensors ranging from piezoresistive, piezoelectric, magnetic, triboelectric, etc.

Controlled porosity and resistance tuning

The porosity of the nanocomposite depends primarily on the initial ethanol volume and the hot bake temperature due to inherent nature of pore formation. In this work we have intuitively used hot bake temperatures of 100 °C and more to assure ethanol bubbling and channel formation. It was observed that lower temperatures resulted in longer saturation time and unreliable channel formations (See Supplementary information, Fig. S2). The saturation time is defined as the maximum time required to complete the bubbling process of the nanocomposite to form the preliminary vapor-induced micro-channels. The process of nanocomposite bubbling can be visualized in the supplementary video. The innovative bake cycle is defined by the hot bake saturation time and temperature. Figure 4a highlights the dependence of nanocomposite (5% GNP/PDMS) saturation time on the initial ethanol at different hot bake temperatures. It is observed that the saturation time increases linearly with ethanol volume for a given hot bake temperature. A lower ethanol volume is bubbled away in a short duration and at high-temperature hot bake steps, and vice versa. Furthermore, a lower ethanol volume generally results in smaller pore sizes and lower pore density, while a higher ethanol volume yields larger pore sizes. An optimum ethanol volume is crucial to achieve a desired pore size and density, as very low or very high ethanol volumes can lead to scattered or uncontrolled pore structures. In this work, three different pore sizes have been achieved by tuning the ethanol volume and hot-bake temperature. The average pore size range of 50 µm to 1 mm (Fig. 4c(i-iv), Fig. 4d(i-iv), and Fig. 4e(i-iv)) is achieved by varying the ethanol volume between 5 ml to 20 ml and hot bake temperature between 100 °C to 140 °C. The relationship between ethanol volume, hot bake temperature, pore size and density are detailed in Figure S3 of the supplementary information. It is worth noting that for a given nanocomposite area and thickness, a higher pore density is correlated to denser micro-channels and in turn results in a lower initial resistance for the pressure sensor. As illustrated in Fig. 4b, the initial resistance for a non-porous 5% GNP/PDMS nanocomposite is ∼37 kΩ and decreases exponentially with increasing pore density (number of per/unit area). However, it is also observed that different pore sizes (Size A= is <500 µm; Size B is >500 µm and <1 mm; Size C is ∼1 mm) affect the initial resistance significantly. For example, for a pore density of 10, size C will form channels with the highest GNP concentration among the three sizes but will be stiffer among the three. Therefore, there is a trade-off between strainability, initial resistance and pore density. This effect of pore sizes can be visualized from the SEM/EDS of the micro-channels illustrated in supplementary information, Fig. S4. The thinner micro-channels are formed due to the squeezing effect of larger pores and hence have more GNP concentration. But higher GNP content also adds to the effective stiffness of the micro-channel. An optimum pore-size results in an optimum GNP concentration, highest deformability and hence a higher sensitivity (Fig. S5(a)). Results and discussions

Mechanical characterization

As discussed in the previous sections, the porous nanocomposite can be tuned for varying pore density and pore size with a simple and low-cost mechanism. As illustrated in Fig. 5a (i-vi), the porous nanocomposite is highly flexible, stretchable, bendable, and twistable. The porous nanocomposite is visibly more compliant than the non-porous nanocomposite and pristine PDMS films of similar thickness. To further evaluate the superior mechanical properties of the porous nanocomposite, we evaluated the VC-GNP/PDMS, non-porous GNP/PDMS and pristine PDMS using a UTM (Universal Testing Machine) with integrated source-meter. Figure 5b highlights the comparative strainability of the three variants. The samples are all 3 mm thick and have an area of 2.25 cm2. The tests were performed at 1 mm/min speed. The mechanical characterization highlights the three important parameters; peak loading, linear range, and hysteresis of the samples under test. It is observed that the peak loading for the three cases varies significantly with microenegineering of the nanocomposite structure. The peak loading and peak %stress varies as follows: ∼412 N and 31% for pristine PDMS, ∼471 N and 50.4% for non-porous GNP/PDMS, and ∼487 N and 68.97% for VC-GNP/PDMS. The linear strain range varies from 0-25%, 0-36%, and 0-45% for pristine PDMS, for non-porous GNP/PDMS, and for VC-GNP/PDMS, respectively. Furthermore, the reverse loading cycle highlights the hysteresis characteristics of the three variants. The bulk or non-porous samples show a more compliant behavior to loading cycles, while the porous sample show a relatively larger hysteresis loop. The superior performance of the porous VC-GNP/PDMS sensor is directly attributable to its engineered micro-structure, which fundamentally enhances mechanical compliance and strategically localizes GNPs for an optimized piezoresistive response under compression. As illustrated in Fig. 5a, the inclusion of micro-channels and micropores significantly reduces the effective stiffness, granting the porous sensor exceptional flexibility and a far greater linear strain range (up to 45% vs. 36% for non-porous). This increased deformability allows for a wider pressure sensing range and robust performance under higher loads. However, increased deformation and strainability adds to the energy loss mechanism in nanocomposites and contribute to the wider hysteresis loops. Furthermore, varying the pore density can also affect the hysteresis loop width. Therefore, there is a clear trade off between linear range or strainability and hysteresis. Nonetheless, the VC-GNP/PDMS sample shows a large strainability, large linear range and higher loading capability. These advantages project the VC-GNP/PDMS as a viable candidate for large-range pressure sensing applications.

Electromechanical characterization

Sensitivity

The electrical characterization of the developed porous and non-porous sensors was performed by integrating the UTM and Keithley source meter. The insulated measuring wires were directly soldered on flexible contacts for the best results. The measurement setup is shown in Fig. S4 of the supplementary information. The non-porous 5% GNP/PDMS and 5% VC-GNP/PDMS have an initial resistance (Ro) of 37 kΩ and 2.5 kΩ, respectively. The lower initial resistance indicates a more interconnected GNP network already present within the vapor-channeled structure. Furthermore, it is observed that the %RRC varies linearly in both variants (Fig. 5c). The resistance change shows a conventional “linear saturation” trend over the applied forcing range. The linear region for the non-porous and VC-GNP/PDMS is 1.1 MPa and 2.5 MPa, respectively. Therefore, the sensitivity of the proposed sensor is calculated as 4.698% MPa−1 and 33.2% MPa−1 for non-porous and VC-GNP/PDMS, respectively. The introduction of porosity with a low-cost, rapid, and simple mechanism allows for a 7-fold enhancement of pressure sensitivity while improving the sensing range from 1.1 MPa to 2.5 MPa. To evaluate the piezoresistive sensitivity, the gauge factor (GF) is calculated for the linear, working range of the non-porous and VC-GNP/PDMS. The value of GF represents a normalized instantaneous change in the nanocomposite resistance to the applied compressive strain. As shown in Fig. 5d, the nonporous GNP/PDMS shows a linear piezoresistive response (GF = 0.13) for compressive strains 0–35%. In contrast, the VC-GNP/PDMS shows two linear regions corresponding to gauge factors of 0.66 under compressive strain 0–45% and 1.72 beyond 45%. This GF shift at 45% strain is primarily linked to a pore deformation and closure mechanism that brings GNPs into closer proximity, thereby increasing conductivity. This mechanism is likely influenced by PDMS matrix deformation and potentially reorientation of GNPs. In the low-to-moderate strain regime (0–45%), the microchannels and micropores undergo gradual deformation, increasing inter-particle contacts and reducing tunneling distances, leading to a GF of 0.66. The transition point at approximately 45% strain signifies an accelerated pore collapse and network optimization, where mechanical stress induces rapid closure of pores, bringing a greater density of GNPs into direct contact or significantly reducing tunneling barriers, resulting in a higher GF of 1.72. Furthermore, the pre-optimized graphene network, combined with the efficient collapse of the micro-channels under compressive strain, drives the dramatic enhancement in piezoresistive sensitivity. This mechanism yields a remarkable 7-fold increase in sensitivity (33.2% MPa−1) for the porous sensor compared to its non-porous counterpart.

Tunneling describes the non-classical phenomenon of electron transport across an insulating potential barrier, such as a polymeric matrix. This effect is prevalent in polymer-based nanocomposites, particularly those incorporating two-dimensional materials like graphene. In these systems, a nanoscale dielectric polymer layer intercalates adjacent graphene platelets. When the inter-platelet separation falls within the tunneling distance, electrons exhibit a finite probability of penetrating the potential barrier, establishing quantum tunneling junctions. This process contributes to the formation of a conductive percolative network within the composite material. The tunneling resistance associated with this phenomenon can be approximated using Simmons’ model, which provides a theoretical framework for quantifying electron transport across thin insulating films57.

The VC-GNP/PDMS sensor response and relaxation time are correlated to its mechanical stiffness, porosity, and hysteresis. Applications

To investigate the potential applications and wearability of the proposed VC-GNP/PDMS based pressure sensor, it was employed to distinguish four different bio-mechanical activities such as elbow bending, finger bending, heel movement and finger tapping. The strain levels at bone joints such as knees and elbows are high and require highly durable and reliable sensor patches. Moreover, the straining results in both transversal compression and lateral stretching of the nanocomposite. The highly compliant structure of the VC-GNP/PDMS enables such bi-axial excitation for an efficient sensing operation. In the first case, the pressure sensor patch was attached to the right elbow and the interface electronics was attached to the foreside of the arm (Fig. 8a). The sensor was driven by 3.3 V DC and the resulting analog sensor data was evaluated in real-time using the integrated Arduino- Microsoft Data Streamer. The %RRC was computed for 5 different bending positions of the arm, corresponding to the varied strain levels at the elbow. The %RRC is stable in the hold positions and shows a sharp response at each stimulus, reaching a maximum value of 25% at position E (Closed Arm).

Figure S8 demonstrates the wearability study of 7 days for wearing the sensor on the biceps (8 consecutive hours each day). It was observed that no visible rash or discomfort appeared. This demonstrates the sensor’s flexibility and ability to integrate seamlessly with natural body movements, which is critical for its potential in sports, robotics, and rehabilitation.

The selection of film thickness for flexible pressure sensors presents critical trade-offs directly impacting sensor performance and suitability for specific applications, a factor we recognize as highly important. While a thinner film, typically favored for applications like finger-touch sensing, generally offers a faster response time and recovery due to its lower mechanical impedance, it often exhibits a more limited dynamic range. Conversely, a thicker film, better suited for high-force applications such as foot insoles or impact sensing, can achieve a wider pressure detection range but may incur slower response times and potentially reduce durability under continuous dynamic loading. Figure S11 highlights dependence on film thickness on peak loading pressure and response time. Therefore, optimizing film thickness is not merely a design choice but a crucial balancing act between desired sensitivity, response time, dynamic range, and long-term mechanical stability, underscoring the need for application-specific material engineering.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this work presents a significant advancement in the field of piezoresistive pressure sensors by introducing a facile, cost-effective, and scalable method for fabricating highly sensitive, porous micro-structured nanocomposites. The innovative vapor-channeling technique applied to PDMS, combined with the incorporation of GNPs, allows for precise control over pore size and distribution, leading to a remarkable 7-fold increase in pressure sensitivity and a 2.25-fold expansion in sensing range compared to non-porous devices. Critically, the sensor maintains negligible hysteresis, exceptional durability exceeding 5000 s of rigorous testing, and robust performance across a broad temperature range (up to 60 °C) and 100% relative humidity, effectively eliminating the influence of environmental factors on pressure readings. The rapid response and relaxation times ∼0.3 s and ∼0.7 s, respectively) further underscore the sensor’s suitability for dynamic pressure measurements. Finally, the successful translation of this technology into a wearable, conformal patch demonstrates its practical applicability for diverse biomechanical activity assessment, spanning a wide pressure range from kPa to MPa. This novel sensor architecture, therefore, holds immense promise for a broad spectrum of applications, from wearable health monitoring and soft robotics to industrial pressure sensing and beyond.

Looking forward, several avenues of research can be explored to further enhance the capabilities of this sensor. Investigating different conductive nanomaterials and polymer matrices could potentially optimize the sensitivity and dynamic range. Exploring advanced microfabrication techniques could lead to even finer control over pore morphology and device miniaturization. Furthermore, integrating the sensor with wireless communication modules and sophisticated signal processing algorithms would enable real-time, remote pressure monitoring. Finally, in-vivo studies could validate the long-term biocompatibility and performance of the wearable patch for continuous physiological monitoring, paving the way for its integration into advanced healthcare applications.
