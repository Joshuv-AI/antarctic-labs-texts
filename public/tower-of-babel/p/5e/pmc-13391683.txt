The Relation Between Aggression and Psychotic-Like Experiences in Help-Seeking Adolescents: The Role of Threat/Control-Override Symptoms
Authors: Samantha L Redman, Steven C Pitts, Jordan DeVylder, Bruce G Link, Lindsay V Healey, Sarah Kopelovich, Vijay A Mittal, Elizabeth Cauffman, Pamela Rakhshan Rouhakhtar, Gloria M Reeves, Jason Schiffman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and HypothesisPsychosis is a heterogeneous phenomenon that emerges across a severity spectrum. Attenuated psychotic symptoms, or psychotic-like experiences (PLEs), reflect potential risk factors for formal psychosis and other clinical outcomes. There has historically been a widespread misconception that individuals who experience symptoms of psychosis are dangerous and prone to aggressive behavior. However, empirical evidence reveals only a modest link between psychosis and aggression. The existing associations are sometimes linked to a set of psychotic symptoms known as threat/control-override (TCO) symptoms that are often amenable to treatment. Despite research linking TCO symptoms and aggressive behavior in formal psychosis samples, studies in populations with PLEs are lacking. In line with the TCO model, we hypothesized that a subset of PLEs representing threat perception and control would have unique positive associations on aggression in the sample.Study DesignThe present study examined the relation between PLEs and aggression in help-seeking adolescents (n = 129). Psychotic-like experiences were assessed by parent/caregiver report (Prime Screen-Revised-Parent/Caregiver Version), and aggression was measured using a parent-rated assessment of adolescent behavior (Behavioral Assessment System for Children, Second Edition).Study ResultsResults supported study hypotheses, revealing small, independent effects of threat perception and control, in addition to a significant combined effect of these variables (TCO) on aggression.ConclusionsFindings support the use of symptom-specific measures of PLEs, including those aligning with the TCO model, to investigate the relation between attenuated psychotic symptomatology and aggression.

Introduction

The Psychosis Spectrum and Aggression

Psychotic-like experiences (PLEs) are similar to symptoms experienced during formal psychotic disorders, but are less impairing, less severe, and more transient.1 Psychotic-like experiences represent a possible premorbid phase on the developmental pathway to formal psychosis,2 with common comorbidities, shared risk factors, and similar functional deficits.3 Approximately 5%-18% of community samples experience PLEs, with 7.4% of those developing a formal psychotic disorder.4 Irrespective of progression, PLEs are linked with negative outcomes including impaired functioning,5 suicidality,6 and hospitalization.7

Psychotic disorders are disproportionately conflated with aggression and violence risk8 despite meta-analyses revealing relatively small effects.9,10 Biased depictions of individuals with psychosis perpetrating aggression in media and entertainment perpetuate this stereotype.11 Scientific literature may also report misleading results on this relation due to methodological flaws and systematic bias.12 The literature documenting a small but consistent relation between aggression and psychosis almost exclusively includes samples with formal psychosis. Some research on the relation between PLEs and aggression in community samples reports null effects,13 while others reveal effects of varying magnitudes (rs = 0.10 to 0.47).14,15 Otherwise, extant literature on PLEs and aggression is limited, restricting the opportunity to address aggressive behavior and related consequences.

Threat/Control-Override Symptoms Along the Psychosis Spectrum

Link and Stueve16 proposed that a subset of psychotic experiences referred to as threat/control-override (TCO) symptoms underlie the association between psychosis and aggression. Threat/control-override symptoms are heightened feelings of threat and/or concerns of external control or intrusion (eg, thought insertion). These experiences, once accepted as real, can elicit “rational” aggressive responses to situations that are perceived as dangerous in the context of one’s delusional beliefs.17 For instance, a person who defines their external experience as threatening may justify an aggressive response. Likewise, an individual who defines their situation through the lens of external commands (eg, mind control, thought insertion) may ultimately override their proscriptions against aggression. These delusional beliefs, when considered as real, are more likely to elicit aggression relative to other psychotic symptoms.

Threat/control-override symptoms have been linked with perpetration of aggression in both nonclinical15,18 and clinical samples,19,20 with effects ranging from small to moderate (rs = 0.10 to 0.25). For instance, in undergraduates, PLEs with threat-related content (eg, believing others wanted to cause them harm) mediated the relation between attenuated psychotic experiences (eg, magical ideation, perceptual abnormalities) and aggression.14 Other nonclinical samples reveal similar associations, including research by Kinoshita et al21 who found an effect of TCO-related PLEs on violence in adolescents, and Nederlof et al.22 who report similar links with trait anger and aggression. Contextualizing this relation will be essential for guiding treatment, given that such experiences (eg, persecutory delusions) are typically most responsive to specific therapeutic techniques like Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Psychosis.23

Current Study

The current study sought to improve our understanding of the effects of PLEs on aggression in a sample of help-seeking adolescents, a group of clinical and conceptual relevance that may provide generalizable insights and is well-suited for guiding future interventions. Applying the TCO model, we explored the effects of PLEs related to heightened threat perception and external control on aggressive behavior. We hypothesized that we would find a positive effect of all types of PLEs on aggression, and more specifically that TCO-specific PLEs would have a unique positive effect on aggression.

Methods

Participants

Adolescents and transitional age youth (TAY) aged 12-25 were referred by local providers and families to the Strive for Wellness clinic, a clinical research program concentrating on early intervention for those experiencing at-risk or early phases of psychosis in central Maryland. Participants were selected for suspected PLEs and/or engagement with current mental health treatment. The analysis sample was restricted to adolescents and TAY (aged 12-21) with active parental involvement in the study (n = 129). Participants provided informed written consent or assent with informed consent from a parent or legal guardian for participants who were under 18 years old.

Procedures

Ethics approval was obtained from the institutional review boards of all participating institutions. After consent, participants and caregivers were administered psychodiagnostic interviews and completed a battery of questionnaires. Interviews were conducted between December 2010 and April 2021 by graduate-level study staff supervised by licensed clinicians. Due to literature supporting the use of parent report for externalizing symptoms24,25 and attenuated psychotic symptoms,26,27 parent/caregiver (PC) reported scores were used for all PLE and aggression variables included in study analyses.

Measures

Demographics

Participants either self-reported demographic information or their caregivers provided it on their behalf. Participants were asked to report their gender as male or female. Ethnicity (Hispanic/Latino or non-Hispanic/Latino) and race (participants selected all that applied) were collected separately; see Table 1 for a descriptive summary of demographic variables.

PRIME Screen-Revised-PC Version

The Prime Screen-Revised-Parent/Caregiver Version (PRIME-PC) was used to measure PLEs. The PRIME-PC,28 adapted from The Prime Screen-Revised (PS-R),29 is a 12-item screening questionnaire devised for parents/caregivers to rate the presence and severity of PLEs in their children in the past year. The measure mirrors the positive symptom section of the gold standard interview for attenuated psychosis (Structured Interview for Psychosis-Risk Syndromes [SIPS]),30 and had a medium correlation (r = 0.41) with clinician-rated SIPS scores.28 Each item asks caregivers to rate the likelihood that their child experienced the symptom on a Likert scale from 0 (Definitely Disagree) to 6 (Definitely Agree). The scale’s total score ranges from 0 to 72, with higher scores representing more PLEs in the respondent’s child.

TCO—Threat and Control PLEs

Threat/control-override symptoms were measured with the PRIME-PC. Although the PRIME-PC does not characterize items as TCO symptoms, individual questions have strong face valid correspondence to TCO constructs. For instance, one item asks the PC respondent to rate the degree to which their child “wonders if people may be planning to hurt them,”29 which demonstrates the TCO model’s threat construct (“How often have you felt that there were people who wished to do you harm?”)16 and operationalized threat in the current study. Likewise, asking respondents to indicate the extent their child feels that “something may be interrupting or controlling their thoughts, feelings, and actions”29 is an example of an item used to operationalize the control component of TCO (“How often have you felt that thoughts were put into to your head that were not your own and/or your mind was dominated by forces beyond your control?”).16 Though there is precedence for treating these variables as a single construct,16,18 some research operationalizes threat and control symptoms as 2 separate but related variables.14,20 We elected to treat threat and control as separate variables, though a test of their joint effects will be conducted.

Non-TCO PLEs

An additional variable was created based on the remaining non-TCO PLE items on the PRIME-PC. Non-TCO PLEs were defined as the mean of the 10 PRIME-PC items that were not used to measure threat and/or control (eg, magical ideation, grandiosity, hallucinations).

Behavioral Assessment System for Children, Second Edition

The Behavioral Assessment System for Children, Second Edition (BASC-2)31 is a multidimensional behavioral assessment used to assess a range of adaptive and clinical facets of youth functioning. The parent rating scale (PRS) portion (150 items) of the BASC-2 was utilized in the current study. The BASC-2 PRS is rated by a PC to assess their adolescent’s (ages 12-21) adaptive skills and behavioral problems. The ratings are made on a 4-point Likert scale, from 0 (Never) to 4 (Always).

The aggression subscale was utilized to operationalize aggression in analyses. Raw item ratings were summed and converted to T scores (mean of 50 and standard deviation of 10). Norms for adolescents (12-21) from the general population were used.31 The aggression subscale has parents/caregivers rate the frequency of several aggressive behaviors, including “threatens to hurt others” and “hits other adolescents,” revealing good internal consistency and high interrater and test–retest reliabilities.31 Interrater reliability in the current sample was excellent (α = 0.90). The BASC-2 PRS aggression subscale is positively correlated (0.67-91) with other widely used functional assessments for adolescents (eg, Achenbach System of Empirically Based Assessment).

Results

Preliminary Analyses

We selected participants with complete data on all measures defined using PRIME-PC and aggression. Of the n = 148 consented participants with an active PC, n = 19 (13%) were removed due to missing data. The analysis sample (n = 129) ranged in age from 12 to 21 (M = 15.10, SD = 2.17), was approximately 52% female (n = 67), and varied in racial/ethnic composition (see Table 1). Distributional properties of the PLEs gave no indication of restriction of range, nor moderate or high non-normality. Measures of aggression were well distributed in the current sample and appeared consistent with the BASC-2’s comprehensive normative data.31

Of the total sample, 77 participants (60%) reported at least one TCO PLE. Among them, 26 (20%) endorsed only TCO PLEs, while 51 (40%) endorsed both TCO and non-TCO PLEs. The remaining 52 participants (40%) reported no TCO PLEs; of these, 29 (23%) endorsed only non-TCO PLEs, and 23 (18%) reported no PLEs at all. Within the sample, n = 56 (43%) were help-seeking controls, n = 71 (55%) were positive for Attenuated Psychosis Syndrome (APS), and n = 2 were missing.

Primary Analyses

It was hypothesized that all PLE measures (total PLEs, threat, control, and non-TCO PLEs), would be positively related to aggression. These hypotheses were supported wherein aggression had roughly small, positive, statistically significant correlations with total PLEs, threat, control, and non-TCO PLEs (rs = 0.24, 0.27, 0.28, and 0.21, respectively; see Table 2). (The presence/absence of positive APS was related to threat (r = 0.17) and non-TCO PLEs (r = .33) with the APS sample reporting more of each measure.) Age and gender (Given the well documented theoretical rationale for considering developmental age32 and gender33 in the study of aggression, these demographic variables were included in regression models.) were included covariates in all primary analyses. Age revealed a significant small, negative correlation with aggression (r = −0.26), indicating that younger participants exhibited more aggression. Gender was not significantly related to aggression.

Race was included in preliminary analyses to address accumulating evidence indicating racial differences in the presentation and measurement of PLEs.34,35 Group racial differences were explored only among participants who identified strictly as Black or White (n = 109), as there were too few participants from other racial groups. Results revealed that race was not related to aggression but only to age (r = −0.25), with Black participants being, on average, younger than White participants. There was also an effect of control symptoms (r = 0.29), with Black participants, on average, rating this item higher than White participants. The magnitude of the bivariate relations between aggression and total PLEs, threat, control, and non-TCO PLEs remained the same in this reduced sample. The presence/absence of any non-TCO PLE was not apparently related to aggression, race, age, or gender (all Ps > .18).

The unique effect of threat and control on aggression while covarying for the other TCO domain, non-TCO PLEs, and covariates was estimated using hierarchical multiple regression. It was hypothesized that threat and control, independently and jointly, would have unique effects on aggression when covarying for the covariates and non-TCO symptoms. In Model 1, aggression was regressed on age, gender, and non-TCO PLEs. In Model 2, threat and control variables were added to the predictors. The combined effect of the threat and control variables on aggression was evaluated using the change in R2 between Models 1 and 2. The unique effect of the threat and control variables was evaluated using their coefficients in Model 2.

In Model 1, the covariates and non-TCO PLEs accounted for a significant amount of variance in aggression, R2 = 0.14, F(3, 125) = 6.58, P < .001. Discussion

Relation Between Aggression and PLEs

This study sought to evaluate the relation between attenuated psychotic symptomatology and aggression to address 2 gaps in the current literature: (1) estimating the relation between aggression and PLEs in the absence of a formal psychosis diagnosis and (2) applying a symptom-specific conceptualization of PLEs to account for their heterogeneous nature. Results confirmed hypothesized positive associations between aggression and total PLEs but showed specificity of these relations for the subset of PLEs characterized by TCO content. Few studies have examined this relation, making direct comparison of results to current literature difficult. However, the significant relation of a small magnitude between aggression and total PLEs appears consistent with reports of slightly elevated risk of aggression among individuals with formal psychosis,10,36 in addition to prior literature revealing a modest link between PLEs and attenuated psychosis experiences with aggression.14,15 These results support the possibility that a modest association exists along the psychosis risk spectrum, providing impetus for further investigation into this relation.

TCO and Aggression in a Population With PLEs

Prior research on the TCO model indicates that specific psychotic symptoms, like a heightened sense of threat and/or thoughts of external control, may help explain the modest association between psychosis and aggression. More specifically, including non-TCO symptoms in operationalizations of psychosis may attenuate the association between psychosis and aggression, as many psychotic symptoms are not uniquely predictive of aggression. These findings have been largely limited to populations with formal psychosis.37,38 Current results support and extend these findings, revealing positive and unique effects of threat and control, both jointly and independently, on aggression among those with PLEs. Further, when threat and control were separated from non-TCO PLEs, non-TCO symptoms no longer significantly predicted aggression. Results are also in line with the literature linking exaggerated experiences of threat and aggressive behavior in nonclinical, community populations.21 In conjunction with prior TCO literature, current findings provide valuable, cautionary evidence against operationalizing psychosis risk as a single construct and suggest that aggressive behavior may instead be modestly associated with a group of specific symptoms that can vary across the continuum of PLEs and attenuated psychotic symptoms.

Age, Gender, and Race Differences

Age and gender were incorporated as covariates in statistical models given their theoretical relevance to aggression. Findings were consistent with prior research, indicating an overall decline in aggressive behavior with increased age.39 While the nonsignificant effect of gender conflicts with the broad literature on aggression, indicating that men may be more likely to engage in aggressive behavior than women,40 current findings are consistent with prior research that fails to find a moderating effect of gender on the relation between aggression and psychosis.41 Race was considered in preliminary analyses to explore possible group differences in PLEs as observed in prior studies.35 There appeared to be significant group differences across race only in the control component of the TCO construct, with Black parents endorsing these PLEs more. While additional regression analysis controlling for race (see Supplementary Material 1) revealed minimal changes in statistical inference or effect sizes, suggesting that race was not predictive of aggression in the current sample, future research should explore possible racial disparities across TCO symptoms.

Limitations

The study’s cross-sectional methodology restricted assessment of the direction and/or progression of the association between PLEs and aggression. Because aggression and PLEs were reported by parents/caregivers, common method variance42 may have influenced findings. While we acknowledge the sole reliance on parent report of PLEs and aggression as a limitation, the use of collateral informants (eg, parents/caregivers, teachers) is thought to offer a more valid account of aggressive behavior in adolescents.43 There is also evidence that parents are often the first to recognize attenuated psychotic symptoms such as PLEs in their children.26 Though ideally in conjunction with self- and/or clinician-reported data, findings highlight the crucial perspective of the PC when studying both aggression and PLEs in adolescent samples.

To meet the essence of the TCO construct, items that aligned with threat and control experiences were extracted from existing measures capturing PLEs, though this approach has not been previously validated. The literature is unclear and inconsistent regarding approaches for defining and validly measuring TCO symptoms, and subsequent studies should consider utilizing validated measures designed specifically for addressing these constructs (eg, PERI).44

The current study failed to consider other pertinent risk factors for adolescent aggression. Substance use has been established as a strong predictor of aggressive behavior,45 and the seminal work of Monahan and colleagues46 has demonstrated it as a factor mediating the link between psychosis and aggression. Despite this, substance use was not included in the study’s statistical models as access to this information was highly limited in the current sample. Given existing findings with formal psychosis (eg, Fazel et al for meta-analysis),9 we recognize the importance of including substance use in investigations on the link between PLEs and aggressive behavior, and encourage this in future research. Given the age range of the participants and the nature of the behavioral outcome, it may have also been valuable to include childhood disorders like attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Unfortunately, the small sample size limited the ability to include covariates like ADHD, but this would be an important consideration for future research. Likewise, the current study was unable to incorporate other potentially important covariates such as cultural and contextual factors (eg, socioeconomic status).

Implications and Future Directions

Existing literature has left a gap in the field’s understanding of how specific types of attenuated psychotic symptomatology, particularly PLEs, are related to aggression. The current results indicate a modest association between parent/guardian-rated PLEs and aggressive behavior among adolescents. In contrast to the existing literature’s broad operationalization of psychosis, results suggest that more nuanced measures, particularly those that consider TCO symptoms separately, may be particularly beneficial to understanding this relation. By failing to consider the heterogeneity of attenuated psychosis spectrum experiences, treating these experiences as a singular construct may oversimplify this relation, perpetuating a negative and distorted stereotype of individuals with psychosis. Findings show that when controlling for TCO symptoms, other PLEs are not related to aggression, suggesting that even in the presence of PLEs, people without these specific PLEs do not show an elevated risk of aggression relative to those with no PLEs at all. This underscores the need for future research to use symptom-specific measures of PLEs and attenuated psychotic experiences, ideally aligned with constructs from the TCO model, to examine their links with aggressive behavior. These efforts may offer insight to develop targeted intervention in populations with PLEs, as well as help reduce stigma that results from the disproportionate association between attenuated psychosis spectrum symptomatology and aggression.
