Beyond the Usual Suspects: Atypical Dural-Based Tumors With Rare Histology and Intracranial Extension
Authors: Hafiza Hifza Bashir, Uzair Ahmed Siddiqui, Manzar Hussain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dural-based lesions are commonly presumed to be meningiomas due to characteristic imaging features. However, rare neoplastic entities may mimic their radiologic appearance, leading to potential diagnostic pitfalls. This report highlights two unusual cases of dural-based tumors with features mimicking meningiomas: metastatic hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and Ewing sarcoma (ES). The first case involved a 52-year-old man presenting with a painless parietal scalp swelling. Imaging revealed a dural-based mass with bone erosion and extracranial extension, initially suspected to be a meningioma. Histopathological evaluation confirmed metastatic HCC. The second case described a 22-year-old man with a large frontoparietal mass exhibiting both intra- and extracranial components. Imaging suggested a meningioma; however, histology and immunohistochemistry confirmed the diagnosis of Ewing sarcoma. Both cases underscore the importance of considering differential diagnoses beyond meningioma for dural-based lesions, particularly when clinical or radiologic findings are atypical. While metastatic HCC to the dura is rare and typically managed with palliative intent, ES requires aggressive multimodal therapy including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. These cases emphasize that dural-based lesions with meningioma-like features may harbor unexpected pathology. A high index of suspicion, coupled with thorough histopathological evaluation, is essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.

INTRODUCTION

Doctors are often taught the maxim, “When you hear hoofbeats, think horses, not zebras,” as a reminder to prioritize common diagnoses. Yet, clinical practice occasionally challenges this principle. While radiological findings may point toward familiar pathologies, atypical presentations demand a broader differential. In this report, we present two cases where lesions radiologically suggestive of meningioma revealed entirely different histopathologies, highlighting the importance of maintaining diagnostic vigilance beyond the expected.

An extra-axial dural-based lesion with avid contrast enhancement and calvarial involvement typically leads to a straightforward diagnosis of meningioma, which makes up to 33%–36% of all brain tumors [12], often referred to by neurosurgeons as a “surgeon’s delight” particularly when occurring over the convexities. The vast majority of extra-axial, dural-based masses are meningiomas and their radiological diagnosis is usually obvious and accurate; however, there are several intra-axial and extra-axial masses that can mimic meningiomas on imaging and pose a diagnostic challenge [3]. Some of these lesions are both rarer and more aggressive than meningiomas, which are usually benign. Distinguishing meningiomas from their mimics is essential, as it significantly impacts both clinical management and prognosis.

Here, we describe two cases of extra-axial dural-based lesions with atypical radiology and rare histological features of Ewing sarcoma and metastatic liver carcinoma in extra axial location.

CASE REPORTS

Case 1

History and presentation

A 52-year-old man with hypertension presented with a progressively enlarging, painless right-sided scalp swelling over the past two months. The patient reported a history of head trauma six months prior, although his current symptoms began only two months ago. The swelling was not associated with any headache, nausea, vomiting, or focal neurological deficits. However, he complained of intermittent episodes of drowsiness lasting approximately 30 minutes.

On physical examination, a well-defined, palpable mass measuring approximately 7×7 cm was identified in the right posterior parietal region of the scalp. The mass was firm in consistency, non-tender on palpation, and showed no signs of discharge or erythema. The overlying skin was intact, with no visible ulceration or other abnormalities (Fig. 1).

Imaging studies

Imaging revealed a lobulated extra-axial mass in the right posterior parietal region, involving the middle third of the superior sagittal sinus and extending across the midline to the left side. The lesion caused significant erosion of the overlying calvarium with extracranial extension, resulting in noticeable scalp bulging (Fig. 2). Internally, the mass displayed areas of calcification and exhibited mild homogeneous contrast enhancement, along with a characteristic dural tail sign, further mimicking the appearance of a meningioma.

Operative details

The patient underwent surgery, during which a scalp flap was raised around the swelling. A firm, moderately vascular, greyish-colored tumor was identified, protruding through the calvarial defect in the right posterior parietal region. The tumor extended intracranially up to the dura but did not invade it. A high-speed craniotomy drill was used to achieve a tumor-free calvarial margin. Cranioplasty was subsequently performed using synthetic bone cement to reconstruct the defect.

Histopathology and final diagnosis

Histopathological analysis revealed atypical features indicative of a metastatic carcinoma of hepatocellular origin. The sections demonstrated a neoplastic lesion with trabecular and pseudoglandular architecture composed of polygonal atypical cells arranged within an intervening sinusoidal network. The tumor cells exhibited clear to eosinophilic cytoplasm, hyperchromatic pleomorphic nuclei, and prominent nucleoli. Additionally, foci of bile pigment were noted, which are characteristic of hepatic origin. Immunohistochemical staining showed focal positivity for cytokeratin (CK) AE1/AE3 and strong positivity for hepatocyte paraffin 1 (Hep Par 1), while EMA was negative, confirming the diagnosis of metastatic hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) (Fig. 3).

Postoperative course

The surgery was uneventful, with no intraoperative or postoperative complications. The patient was discharged on the first postoperative day in stable condition. Follow-up remained unremarkable, with no evidence of local recurrence. The patient was subsequently referred to the oncology services for further evaluation and comprehensive management.

Case 2

History and presentation

A 22-year-old man with no known medical history presented with a progressively enlarging left-sided scalp swelling over the past year. He also experienced intermittent headaches, seizures, and weakness in his right arm during this period. There was no history of trauma or other significant past medical events.

On examination, a firm, non-tender mass measuring approximately 20×25 cm was identified in the left frontoparietal region (Fig. 4). The overlying skin was intact, with no evidence of discoloration, discharge, or ulceration. Neurological evaluation revealed right arm weakness graded as Medical Research Council Scale 4 and a positive pronator drift, raising concerns about intracranial involvement.

Imaging studies

Brain MRI revealed a large, multilobulated, heterogeneous extra-axial lesion located in the left frontoparietal convexity. The lesion exerted a significant mass effect on the surrounding brain parenchyma and caused thinning and erosion of the skull, with extension through the bone into the overlying soft tissues of the scalp. Post-contrast imaging demonstrated heterogeneous enhancement, with a non-enhancing cystic component within the mass (Fig. 5).

Operative details

The patient was scheduled for a left frontoparietal craniotomy for tumor excision. A circular skin incision was made around the elevated region of the scalp, followed by careful elevation of the galeal flap to expose the extracalvarial component of the tumor. The extracalvarial portion was meticulously excised, ensuring complete removal.

A craniotomy was then performed, achieving a negative bone margin to access the intracranial extension of the tumor. Intraoperatively, the tumor was found to invade the dura, necessitating excision of the involved dura for complete resection. Reconstruction of the dural defect was performed using a pericranial graft for duroplasty. The procedure was completed without any intraoperative or postoperative complications.

Histopathology and final diagnosis

Histopathological analysis confirmed the tumor as Ewing sarcoma. The specimen revealed a neoplastic lesion arranged in large sheets and nests infiltrating fibrocollagenous and hyalinized stroma with areas of necrosis. The tumor cells had scant clear cytoplasm and ovoid to rounded nuclei with vesicular chromatin and prominent nucleoli, and few mitotic figures were noted (Fig. 6A and B). The tumor cells show cytoplasmic glycogen positivity (Fig. 6C). Immunohistochemistry showed NKX2.2 positive and FLI-1 positive with Ki-67 index of 8%–10%.

Postoperative course

A postoperative follow-up scan confirmed complete resolution of the tumor. The patient underwent cranioplasty one month later and was subsequently referred to oncology services for further management, including radiation therapy and chemotherapy.

DISCUSSION

Meningiomas are the most common dural-based tumors, constituting approximately 36% of all primary intracranial neoplasms [12]. Their classic imaging features—including extra-axial location, broad dural attachment, homogeneous contrast enhancement, hyperostosis, and occasionally a dural tail—often lead to a presumptive diagnosis, making them relatively straightforward for neurosurgeons to identify preoperatively [45]. However, reliance on these imaging characteristics alone may be misleading, as several uncommon but clinically significant entities can closely mimic meningiomas both radiologically and clinically [56].

In both cases presented in this report, preoperative imaging strongly favored a diagnosis of meningioma. In Case 1, metastatic HCC was not clinically suspected prior to surgery and was diagnosed only after histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation, which subsequently prompted systemic oncologic work-up, directly addressing the diagnostic timeline [789]. In Case 2, the lesion was ultimately diagnosed as a primary dural-based Ewing sarcoma. These diagnoses represent rare meningioma mimics with fundamentally different biological behavior, treatment strategies, and prognoses, underscoring the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis when evaluating dural-based lesions, particularly when clinical or radiological features are atypical [6789].

Non-meningothelial dural-based tumors encompass a heterogeneous group of neoplastic and non-neoplastic conditions. Neoplastic lesions include metastases, solitary fibrous tumor/hemangiopericytoma, lymphoma, plasmacytoma, melanocytic tumors, and mesenchymal neoplasms such as Ewing sarcoma [101112]. Non-neoplastic entities, although less common, include infectious granulomas, neurosarcoidosis, and Rosai–Dorfman disease [10]. Despite their diverse etiologies, many of these lesions share overlapping imaging characteristics with meningiomas, making definitive diagnosis dependent on histopathological examination and immunohistochemistry rather than imaging alone [111213].

From a neuroradiological perspective, differentiating meningiomas from their mimics remains challenging [14]. Certain radiological “red-flag” features may suggest alternative diagnoses, including disproportionate bone destruction rather than hyperostosis, extensive extracranial soft tissue components, marked heterogeneity, cystic or necrotic areas, and rapid interval growth [3614]. Both Ewing sarcoma and dural metastases may present as well-circumscribed, contrast-enhancing extra-axial masses with calvarial erosion and extracranial extension, closely resembling meningiomas on conventional imaging [1415]. Advanced imaging modalities such as diffusion-weighted imaging, MR spectroscopy, perfusion MRI, and PET/CT may provide adjunctive information but are not consistently reliable for definitive preoperative differentiation [16]. Several case series report initial misdiagnosis of primary dural Ewing sarcoma and dural metastases as meningiomas, highlighting the persistent diagnostic gray zone despite advances in neuroimaging [151718].

Primary dural-based Ewing sarcoma is an exceptionally rare entity and often presents as a localized, contrast-enhancing mass mimicking meningioma [1920]. Unlike meningiomas, which are typically benign and often cured with surgical resection alone, Ewing sarcoma is a highly aggressive tumor requiring multimodal treatment, including surgery, systemic chemotherapy, and radiotherapy [2122]. Delayed or incorrect diagnosis may result in postponement of systemic therapy and adversely affect patient outcomes, emphasizing the need for early recognition and accurate pathological confirmation [23].

Similarly, dural metastases from hepatocellular carcinoma are rare but clinically important. Intracranial and skull metastases from HCC are typically associated with advanced systemic disease and poor prognosis, necessitating comprehensive oncologic evaluation in addition to local surgical management [7892425]. In patients without a known history of malignancy, dural metastasis may represent the first manifestation of an underlying systemic cancer, as demonstrated in our first case. Immunohistochemical markers play a crucial role in distinguishing metastatic carcinoma from meningioma and other mimics, particularly in diagnostically challenging cases [26].

From a surgical standpoint, both patients underwent gross total resection without immediate complications. These cases highlight the dual role of surgery—not only as a therapeutic intervention for mass effect and local control, but also as a critical diagnostic tool enabling definitive histopathological classification. Postoperative management diverged substantially based on final diagnosis, with systemic oncologic evaluation and palliative intent for metastatic HCC, and aggressive adjuvant chemoradiation for Ewing sarcoma.

In summary, these cases emphasize the necessity of a systematic and evidence-based approach when evaluating dural-based lesions. While meningiomas remain the most common pathology, awareness of clinical and radiological red flags, coupled with meticulous histopathological and immunohistochemical assessment, is essential to avoid misdiagnosis. Recognition of rare meningioma mimics such as Ewing sarcoma and metastatic carcinoma has significant implications for patient counseling, treatment planning, and prognosis, ultimately contributing to improved clinical outcomes.

In conclusion, these cases emphasize the critical need for neurosurgeons and radiologists to approach dural-based lesions with a thoughtful differential diagnosis. While meningioma remains the most common entity, rare pathologies must be considered, as they directly influence patient counseling, treatment planning, and prognosis.
