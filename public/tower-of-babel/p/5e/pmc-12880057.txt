Serial gait speed measurements over time and dynamic survival prediction in older adults
Authors: Subashan Perera, Xiao Zhang, Charity G. Patterson, Robert M. Boudreau
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA one-time gait speed measurement predicts mortality risk. A framework for updating a clinician’s mortality risk perception with new information from each clinic visit is needed. We used joint modeling of longitudinal and survival data for dynamic prediction of mortality risk.MethodsWe fit sex-stratified joint models to 20-meter (bi)annual longitudinal gait speed measured every 6 months and 14-year survival data from the Health, Aging and Body Composition Study allowing for non-linear fluctuations of gait speed and controlling for important covariates such as age, recent hospitalization, blood pressure, obesity, and comorbidities.ResultsParticipants (N = 3048) were 74 years old with gait speed 1.18 m/s. They were 42% Black, and 52% died over 14 years. Higher gait speed of 0.1 m/s was associated with 23% (95% confidence interval or CI = 20–25%) and 25% (CI = 21–28%) reductions in mortality risk in men and women; and a 0.05 m/s annualized slowing (slope) with 31% (CI = 13–51%) increase in men (all p < 0.05), with findings persisting after covariate adjustment. Distant gait speed history over a year prior contributed little for mortality risk prediction with mean change of only 1–2% in 5-year risk.ConclusionThe two most recent gait speeds appear sufficient to consider for mortality risk in the present initial analysis. More frequent gait speeds need to be considered in mortality risk prediction before definitive conclusions supporting real-world application.

Introduction

Physical performance measures, especially those of lower extremity such as walking speed, have been consistently shown to be associated with future health outcomes such as disability,

hospitalization, institutionalization, and mortality in older adults [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Consequently, many have suggested that gait speed be treated as a vital assessment in clinical care [[9], [10], [11]]. Beyond measures of association, initial metrics of absolute risk such as estimated median survival, and 5- and 10-year probability of mortality and disability have been developed that show their dependence jointly on sex, age and gait speed [12,13]. Moreover, criteria for a meaningful change over time in lower extremity physical performance have been developed [[14], [15], [16], [17]] and shown to be largely invariant with respect to several important individual characteristics [18]. Subsequently, randomized trials in older populations have started using lower extremity physical performance measures as primary or secondary outcomes [[19], [20], [21]].

Beyond a one-time measurement of physical performance, improvements in gait speed over time, even if transient, appear to be associated with better survival and declines with increased mortality risk [22,23]. However, a major remaining gap in knowledge is the lack of an integrated framework to interpret or distill information contained in serially measured lower extremity performance measures collected in clinic visits over time as related to future outcomes. It is not immediately clear which longitudinal measurement(s) of gait speed will be most informative for predicting mortality. Will only the most recent measurement matter as in assessing fracture risk with bone mineral density? Will the change and trajectory matter more than any one point-measurement as in recovery after a stroke or surgery? Or will the cumulating effect of the gait speed profile matter as with exposure to anticholinergic medications and risk of cognitive decline?

Associations between serial longitudinal measures and time-to-event outcomes have traditionally been assessed using extended Cox regression models with a time-dependent predictor [24]. However, such models are not considered appropriate in situations with endogenous serial measurements (e.g., outcome affects the existence of the serial measure) or when longitudinal assessments contain measurement error [25]. A somewhat novel approach is to jointly model the longitudinal data and the survival data which is gaining popularity in other in analytically similar settings such as serially collected CD4 counts and survival in HIV/TB co-infected patients, performance scores and motor diagnosis in Huntington’s disease, cognitive performance and survival, BNP and time to intervention/mortality in aortic stenosis, and red blood cell width and anemia/mortality in heart failure [[26], [27], [28], [29], [30]].

In the present report, we sought to explore which longitudinal gait speed measurement(s) were most important for predicting mortality using up to 10 gait speed measurements collected annually or every other year along with mortality available in Health, Aging and Body Composition study and applying joint modeling of longitudinal and survival data. By restricting analyses to a later subset of longitudinal assessments, we are able to objectively assess the added value of historical trajectory beyond the most recent assessment.

Methods

Parent study and participants

The detailed Health, Aging and Body Composition (Health ABC) study protocol has been published elsewhere [31]. Briefly, the longitudinal cohort enrolled of 3,075 older adults of age 70–79 with no self-reported difficulty in walking a ¼ mile, climbing 10 steps, or performing activities of daily living from Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania and Memphis, Tennessee metropolitan areas during 1997–1998. Clinic, in-home, and/or phone assessments were conducted annually during the follow-up. In the present analysis, we use data from 2,669 participants who had valid data for baseline gait speed.

Measures and outcomes

Gait speed measured over a 20-meter at fast pace was collected at baseline and at usual pace at years 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7 and 9 of the follow-up period (labeled as years 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8 and 10 in the parent study operations, respectively). A standing start short distance (6-meter) usual gait speed commonly used in older adults was also available but less frequently only at baseline, and years 3, 5 and 8 of the follow-up period. As 20-meter usual pace speed was not collected at baseline, we used 6-meter usual pace speed for baseline. Mortality data were gathered via proxy reports, hospital records, obituaries and national death index, and confirmed by death certificates. Time to mortality right censored 5 years after the year 10 assessment or 14 years from baseline for those still alive at that time. Participant characteristics used to describe the sample included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), race, smoking status (current, former, never), history of hospitalization during prior year and important/relevant chronic conditions (cancer, heart disease, diabetes, arthritis).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were stratified by sex because of the well-known differences in mortality risk and physical performance. We used descriptive statistics to summarize the participants’ baseline characteristics. We fitted joint models for simultaneously addressing longitudinal and survival outcomes [25]. Briefly, the ith participant’s survival component of the joint model is given by SitMit,wi=exp-∫0th0sexpγTwi+αmi(s)ds, where mit is the true unobserved value of gait speed at time t, Mit is the history of such information up to time t, wi are covariates, and h0(t) is the baseline hazard function. The longitudinal linear mixed model component for observed gait speeds is yit=xiTtβ+ziTtbi+εit, bi∼N0,D, and εit∼N0,σ2, where xit and zit are design vectors for fixed and random effects. For the linear mixed submodel of the joint model, we used the 20-meter gait speed as the dependent variable, and a B-spline with 4 knots as the functional form with sufficient flexibility to capture longitudinal fluctuations in gait speed. For the survival submodel, a proportional hazards Cox regression model was used with piecewise constant baseline hazard function. In adjusted models, common mortality risk factors age, history of hospitalization, BMI category (normal/overweight/obese), history of cancer, arthritis, diabetes and heart disease, and systolic blood pressure at baseline were included as covariates in the survival submodel. Next, we fitted an extended joint model that allowed the hazard function to vary with the estimated instantaneous slope (i.e., increasing/decreasing trajectory). Using the covariate-adjusted extended model, we examined how the predicted survival curve was influenced by the newly available gait speed measurements over time, and also by the look back period used in prediction. R package jm was used for the main analysis and SAS (SAS® Institute, Inc., Cary North Carolina) for other analyses including data manipulation, summarization and graphics [32].

Results

At baseline, 3,048 participants had a valid gait speed measurement and constituted our analytic sample (Table 1). They were (mean ± standard deviation) 73.6 ± 2.9 years old and had a gait speed of 1.18 ± 0.23 meters/second, consistent with the parent study inclusion criterion of no self-reported mobility difficulty at baseline. Participants were 52% female and 42% Black. Despite functioning at a relatively high level, participants had a range of health issues as indicated by a history of prior hospitalization (15%), being at least overweight (42%) with a substantial proportion falling into obese category (25%), cancer (19%), arthritis (57%), diabetes (15%) and heart disease (22%). Men tended to walk faster at baseline (1.24 vs 1.12 m/s; p < 0.0001) and gait speeds generally declined in the ensuing 9 years. Discussion

We have successfully applied the joint modeling framework to serial measurements of gait speed over time and survival in community-dwelling older adults. Under this framework, we have shown that both gait speed and its trajectory (increasing/decreasing) are associated with mortality risk. We have shown that the model was able to successfully capture fluctuations of gait speed of a person over time, and also the entailing mortality risk fluctuations. Distant gait speed history appears to play little or no role in mortality risk prediction beyond the more recent. Taken together, we infer two most recent gait speed measurements may be sufficient for mortality risk assessment.

Why is gait speed such an important construct to consider? We argue that it is because of its close connection to physical and cognitive frailty which is very meaningful to both older adults and clinicians, despite current debates in measuring frailty objectively. Recent evidence has identified many important clinical correlates and contributors to frailty such as lipid levels, glycemic control and nutrition. Among pre-frail hypertensive older adults, a greater percentage of those hyperglycemic developed frailty than those normoglycemic (40% vs. 12%) in The Montefiore Study [33]. Further, among older adults, triglyceride-to-glucose index was very strongly correlated to both cognitive function and gait speed (r > 0.80), prediabetes elevated the risk of developing frailty, and metformin may play a role in reducing frailty risk [34]. Mendelian randomization studies have shown a potential inverse association between better protein nutritional status and risk of frailty [35]. A recent review speculated that gait speed and cognitive function should be assessed prior to hospital discharge in frail older women after an acute myocardial infarction [36]. In fact, gait speed is one important component of widely used scales such as Fried criteria for assessing frailty [37].

It is important to note the notion that two most recent measurements may be sufficient is a logical inference rather than a direct finding. We found that more distant measurements do not substantially alter five-year mortality risk predicted by the most recent measurement; and that the instantaneous slope of the gait speed trajectory was a significant predictor of mortality risk at least in men. To estimate a slope, for example in the context of a clinician assessing change in gait speed over the previous two years, one needs at least two measurements over time, which led to our inference that the two most recent measurements are sufficient. This is supported by our finding that the slope at the current timepoint is a significant independent predictor at least in men. However, the time between measurements also plays an important role. Our analysis is based on measurements which are one year apart. Long time intervals between measurements will likely capture the long-term slope more accurately, but perhaps less so the instantaneous slope at the most recent measurement.

To the best of our knowledge, ours is the first study to specifically examine the association between serial gait speed measurements and mortality risk using the novel joint modeling framework. However, the methodology has already been applied in other similar contexts involving older adults. Guure et al. have used joint modeling to demonstrate the association between serially measured 3MS scores and mortality risk among cognitively impaired individuals [28]. More recently, Stolz et al. used joint modeling to examine the association between longitudinal fluctuations in an older adult’s intrinsic capacity and risk of negative health outcomes [38]. Intrinsic capacity is a construct proposed by the world health organization as an aggregate measure of physical and mental capacities consisting of vitality, locomotor, sensory, cognitive, and psychological domains [[38], [39], [40], [41]]. In the work of Stolz et al., locomotor domain was assessed using multiple performance measures (balance and chair rise) in addition to a 20-foot gait speed with a turn. The prior studies found significant associations as did ours. Our estimated magnitude of the association, 23–25% mortality risk reduction per 0.1 m/s in gait speed, appear to be somewhat greater than that obtained with cross-sectional one-time gait speed measurements. Study by Studenski et al. of more than 30,000 older adults from 9 cohort studies documented a pooled estimate of 12% risk reduction [12]. The difference could be due to consideration of information from multiple measurements over time, gait speed measurements over shorter distances, or a combination.

In large part, the linear mixed submodel captured the non-linear fluctuations and trends of gait speed measurements, and joint modeling incorporated these estimated patterns into the predicted survival as noted above. However, the model’s reluctance to fully capture an increase in gait speed or a period of stability and its influence deserves comment (e.g., participants B and C in Fig. 1). It is possible for an older adult to increase gait speed over a period of time, as noted in other work [22,23]. Potential reasons include participating in exercise, quitting smoking, cessation of a sedating medication and initiating an activating or mood enhancing medication. In the joint model, each individual’s fitted gait speed trend is essentially a smoothed version where the smoothed fit is one that is moved towards the mean of sufficiently similar smoothed trends (as piece-wise polynomials in our case) within the population. It is akin to using information on other “similar” individuals’ gait speed histories to improve prediction. For instance, smoothing using other individuals’ data as described above tends to down-weight the contribution of an outlier so that the fitted line retains a good fit and is not drawn towards the outlier. On the other hand, in our study, for individuals who are notably different from the typical because they have unusually variable gait speed trends, the finite-dimensional aspect of the smoothing can result in smoothed fits that do not adhere very well to their actual data. Related, the overall secular trend among older adults is a general decline in gait speed over time. Because linear mixed modeling tends to borrow and share information across subjects to optimize the estimating process, we speculate a given person’s improvement or stability in gait speed (and its downstream implications by extension) gets down-weighted by others decline.

Our study had several strengths. The sample size was relatively large and included an approximately 40% African American participants. The large sample size allowed us to stratify the entire analysis by sex, which was imperative considering the fact that women generally walk slower but live longer than men contrary to the general notion of faster walking speed is associated with lower mortality risk. The follow-up period was long, allowing us to consider 8 serial gait speed measurements over time and a subsequent 5-year period for mortality assessment. We employed the relatively novel technique of joint modeling to reach our conclusions. The parent data set was sufficiently rich to allow consideration of known mortality risk factors (age, history of hospitalization, BMI, history of cancer, arthritis, diabetes and heart disease, and systolic blood pressure) as covariates in our models. BMI operationalized as a categorical variable in consideration of its well-known U-shaped associations with mortality in older adults [42].

Our study has many important limitations as well. Basing the sufficiency of the two most recent gait speed measurement on an inference rather than a direct finding, and the reluctance of the model to fully capture the increases in gait speed have already been mentioned. Our longitudinal gait speed measurements were 1–2 years apart. More frequent gait speed assessments could have provided additional information for more nuanced conclusions considering quarterly measurements considered in prior work on transient and persistent changes [22,23]. Limitations regarding gait speed itself as a performance measure for widespread use should also be kept in mind. Primary care provider teams are already extended and may not have time for an additional assessment within the short time allocated for a clinic visit. Many primary care settings may not have a straight unobstructed hallway of sufficient length to measure gait speed allowing for a gradual slowdown after completing the measured walk. In addition, ability to walk well on a straight walkway on an even surface may not be the most appropriate performance measure indicative of real-world mobility function. While other competing performance measures such timed get-up-and-go test does not appear to add quantitatively to gait speed, there may be other useful qualitative elements with prognostic value [43]. The Health ABC study participants were only of races White and Black from metropolitan areas of Pittsburgh, PA and Memphis, TN. Therefore, our findings may not generalize to the wider population of community-dwelling older adults in the United States. In view of the limitations, our work should be considered a first step rather than one arriving at definitive conclusions and recommendations.

Conclusion

Joint modeling is able to capture aspects of the complex association between serial gait speed measurements over time and mortality risk, subject to important limitations. The two most recent gait speeds appear sufficient to consider for mortality risk in the present initial analysis, but the conclusion is based on logical inference rather than a direct result. More frequently measured gait speeds need to be considered in mortality risk prediction before definitive conclusions supporting real-world clinical application.
