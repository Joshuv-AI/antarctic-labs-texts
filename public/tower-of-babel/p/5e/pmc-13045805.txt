Moral Distress of Critical Care Nurses in Japan
Authors: Reiko Matsuda, Keiko Akashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Critical care nurse moral distress seriously affects the quality of care. Although there are quantitative studies in Japan, few have explored Japanese critical care nurses’ (CCNs) experiences, necessitating a contextualized understanding.Objective:To explore Japanese CCNs’ descriptions of the causes of their moral distress.Method:A descriptive qualitative study using semistructured interviews was conducted on 10 CCNs working in intensive care units (ICUs) in Japan’s Tokai region. Participants were recruited via purposive and snowball sampling. Inclusion criteria were at least 3 years of working in the ICU and 5 years’ nursing experience. Data were analyzed using Tesch’s eight-step content analysis.Results:Five categories and 17 subcategories were identified as constraints preventing nurses from acting in accordance with their personal values and ethical standards: (1) difficulty in achieving common understanding between patients/family members in crisis and medical professionals; (2) insufficiencies in the therapy/treatment/care centered on patients and families; (3) physician attitudes that communicate superiority and higher status; (4) work environments that make expressing differences in values difficult; and (5) tension between one’s professional and moral responsibilities.Discussion:This study provides empirical evidence of situations where moral distress arises among Japanese CCNs in ICUs, emphasizing the influence of cultural norms (conformity and harmony). A cross-cutting theme of silence emerged, as nurses often chose not to voice concerns to maintain harmony, contributing to moral distress. These findings highlight the need for strategies supporting ethical dialogue and culturally sensitive interventions.

Critical care nurses (CCNs) work in a high-stress environment where they constantly deal with patients with highly urgent and severe conditions and are often faced with ethical situations. Moral distress, defined as the psychological burden nurses feel when they know the ethically appropriate thing to do but are unable to do it because of some constraint, is a well-known problem in the field.1

CCNs experience moral distress when making decisions about discontinuing a patient’s life support, based on the wishes of the family.2 External constraints (eg, understaffing and lack of team communication) and internal constraints (eg, uncertainty about one’s competence) contribute to moral distress.3 Nurses experience emotional turmoil, such as anger, stress, depression, and guilt, as well as physical symptoms, such as nausea and high blood pressure, in response to situations of high moral distress.4 This impacts nursing quality, leading to job dissatisfaction and a desire to leave the profession.5

Moral distress has recently received considerable research attention in Japan. For example, Epstein et al6 have developed a Japanese version of the Measure of Moral Distress for Health Care Professionals (MMD-HP), and Fujii et al7 have numerically reported the frequency and level of distress for physicians and nurses. A report8 comparing the results of a survey measuring moral distress among CCNs in Japan with the results of an earlier survey6 in the United States found that the top two items found in the surveys—“not respecting the patient’s wishes and following the family’s wishes” and “continuing aggressive treatment”—were the same for both countries. However, significant differences were observed in the subsequent items’ rankings in both countries. These findings may differ across countries because of sociocultural structures, highlighting the need for contextual understanding through qualitative inquiry.9

To address this gap, the present study employed a descriptive qualitative approach to explore Japanese CCNs’ descriptions of how they experience moral distress, focusing particularly on constraints preventing them from acting in accordance with their personal values and ethical principles. That is, the study investigated the following research question: What constraints prevent Japanese CCNs from acting in an ethically appropriate manner based on their personal values and principles?

The findings provide valuable knowledge for the development of targeted interventions and support strategies to alleviate moral distress and enhance CCNs’ physical and mental well-being.

METHODS

Research design

This study adopted a descriptive qualitative study design to present nurses’ narratives in everyday language without generating a theory or engaging in philosophical interpretation. Data were collected through semistructured interviews, allowing the researchers to remain closely tied to participants’ experiences while ensuring minimal inference.10

Setting and participants

The study targeted CCNs working in intensive care units (ICUs) in Japan’s Tokai region. Invitations were sent to the ICU departments of 20 independent general hospitals, each of which operated at least one adult ICU. Nine of these hospitals agreed to participate. The ICUs of participating hospitals ranged in size from 6 to 30 beds, with an average of ~13 beds. Using purposive and snowball sampling,11 10 ICU nurses were recruited from 10 separate and independent general hospital departments. Initially, one ICU nurse was recommended by the head nurse at each of these 9 hospitals. Subsequently, through snowball sampling,11 an additional eligible ICU nurse working at a tenth hospital was introduced by one of the participants. If they wished to, the recommended nurses contacted the principal researcher directly by email to arrange interviews, without needing to inform the head nurse or the recommender of their decision to participate. Sample identification in qualitative research involves selecting the most suitable individuals capable of providing detailed accounts of an event.10 Therefore, we recruited nurses who had worked in an adult ICU for at least 3 years, had at least 5 years of nursing experience, had experienced ethical conflicts, and provided direct patient care during the study period. The first of these criteria was based on Benner,12 who suggested that a minimum of 3 years’ critical care experience indicates a good understanding of clinical situations and experience with ethical conflicts. Those not involved in direct care, such as nurse managers, were excluded. The interviews were conducted in a private room designated by the participant or at their place of work; in each case, the interviews were conducted away from the clinical setting and set up by mutual agreement.

Data collection

One of the researchers conducted semistructured interviews from October 2020 to August 2021. During this period, Japan was experiencing the COVID-19 pandemic; thus, the choice between in-person and web-based interviews was offered to participants. The first author, who conducted the interviews, is an ICU nurse at a university hospital with 10 years’ experience and is currently a university faculty member. The co-author is a graduate faculty member of the Graduate School of Critical Care Nursing with surgical experience. The first author and participants had not met before the interviews. Before each interview, the first author verbally explained the study’s purpose and obtained participants’ consent to proceed with the interview. The interview guide comprised questions based on moral distress according to the literature review on ethical issues and ethical dilemmas among nurses in Japan. During the interviews, the following information was collected: (1) situations in participants’ daily practice wherein they tried to act according to their values and principles but were prevented from doing so, and (2) factors that prevented them from doing so (Table 1). The interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ permission, and field notes were taken. The recorded interviews were transcribed by an external transcription service under a confidentiality agreement. The transcripts were anonymized using a code sheet, and all materials—including field notes—were stored in a locked cabinet.

Data analysis

The researchers used Tesch’s 8-step method for data analysis.13 In the process of data analysis, care was taken to eliminate the researcher’s assumptions and focus solely on the participants’ words. Becoming familiar with the data involved repeatedly reviewing all data, revisiting notes and records to deepen comprehension, and recalling observations to enhance contextual interpretation. The researcher extracted information on the constraints preventing participants from taking appropriate action when facing ethical issues. The information extracted from all the participants was compiled into a list and divided into meaningful groups, and symbolic labels were assigned using terms close to the participants’ words. Annotations regarding ideas and categories were written in the margins, and subcategories were extracted by focusing on similarities in codes. The subcategories were compared in detail with the original data and grouped before categories that expressed the data’s content were extracted. The names of the categories were decided via team discussion. The original text belonging to a specific category was confirmed. The entire list of categories was reviewed and grouped to reduce the number of categories. MAXQDA 2020 was used for data organization. All analytical decisions and coding were conducted manually by the research team. The interviews and analyses were conducted simultaneously during the data collection period. The data analysis showed information redundancy when up to 8 participants were interviewed.10 However, data saturation was determined at 10 participants after confirming that no new information emerged despite further interviews.10 The data analysis was repeated until an agreement was reached between the first author and the co-author. We used “categories” as the main analytical units, in line with Tesch’s flexible use of themes and categories.

Rigor

To improve the study’s rigor, 4 criteria14—credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability—were used. The participants had no personal interests or conflicts of interest with the external researcher-interviewer. Before commencing the interviews, participants were informed that they could speak openly because their answers would not be considered incorrect. Although we did not conduct member checking of the interview content, we ensured the data’s credibility through multiple rounds of coding and discussion among researchers. To meet the transferability requirement, we used purposive sampling to recruit participants with substantial information on the research topic. The criteria for dependability were met by keeping raw data, observation notes, and interview records to check the research process.15 Confirmability was achieved through notes and records showing the process of interpreting interview data.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the appropriate Research Ethics Committee (blinded for review). Before the study, we explained the study outline, the advantages and disadvantages of participation, and the freedom to accept or decline participation. We also assured the participants, directors of participating facilities, and directors of nursing departments of the protection of participants’ privacy and personal information, both verbally and in writing. Subsequently, we obtained consent for the use of the data. Participants gave their consent directly to the researcher.

FINDINGS

Overview of research participants

A total of 10 CCNs participated, each recruited from a different facility (8 women and 2 men). Three had bachelor’s degrees, 2 had master’s degrees, and 5 had graduated from a nursing vocational school. Nursing experience ranged from 5 to 30 years, and critical care experience from 5 to 18 years. All interviews were conducted face-to-face, with an average duration of 92.1 minutes (64 to 143 min). Each participant was interviewed once. Constraints were identified regarding acting according to one’s values/principles when facing ethical issues.

Five categories and 17 subcategories were extracted (Table 2). The categories and subcategories are described below. Although not all subcategories are accompanied by participant quotations, they were extracted from the interview data via a systematic coding process and consensus among the research team members. These subcategories represent recurring patterns and experiences shared by participants during the interviews.

DISCUSSION

This study explored the constraints underlying moral distress among Japanese CCNs. Five major categories and additional findings were identified, reflecting the complex ways in which nurses are prevented from acting according to their values and ethical principles.

Difficulty in achieving a common understanding between patients/family members in crisis and medical professionals

Nurses in this study experienced moral distress when patients and families in crisis could not fully understand treatment, delaying decisions and complicating care. Previous studies also reported unclear patient intentions2 and discrepancies in perceptions between professionals and families.16 Our findings extend such conclusions by showing that nurses sometimes concealed the severity of illness to avoid distressing the family, which in turn hindered open discussion and reinforced misunderstanding.

Insufficiencies in therapy/treatment/care centered on patients and families

Nurses reported distress when invasive procedures were performed on patients with poor prognoses, recognizing them as futile yet being unable to intervene. Previous studies described similar situations, where nurses continued interventions they considered meaningless and felt discouraged.5 These findings highlight how constraints on professional judgment limited advocacy for patient-centered care and deepened moral distress.

Physician attitudes that communicate superiority and higher status

Participants described moral distress when physicians dismissed their input and imposed treatment decisions. Previous studies have shown that nurses felt excluded when physicians’ authoritative attitudes limited dialogue and collaboration.17 Such dynamics reinforced nurses’ sense of powerlessness to raise ethical concerns despite feeling responsible for patient advocacy.

Work environments that make expressing differences in values difficult

Nurses also faced distress when workplace cultures discouraged them from voicing differences in values. One described that care for dying patients was left perfunctory, while another explained that physicians dominated treatment decisions. Previous studies reported that disagreements among staff could stall decision-making and disrupt patient care.18 These findings suggest that unresolved differences within health care teams leave nurses unable to act on their values, thereby heightening moral distress.

Tension between one’s professional and moral responsibilities

Nurses in this study experienced moral distress when their perceived responsibilities—such as informing families or protecting patients’ dignity—did not align with their authorized responsibilities. For example, they thought they should be able to speak openly with patients’ families about prognosis, but felt restricted in doing so. Similarly, they often felt compelled to use restraints to avoid blame in cases of accidents, even though they felt this was inappropriate treatment for the patients. Prior studies have reported similar issues, including cases where health care staff recognized a patient’s severe decline but withheld this information from families, due to institutional constraints.16 In other cases, fear of being held accountable for self-extubation led nurses to use restraints, despite the nurses’ ethical concerns about their use.19 These persistent misalignments between responsibility and authorized duty compromised the nurses’ professional autonomy and intensified their moral distress.

Additional findings

Some nurses in this study reported that experiencing moral distress led them to reflect on their practice and modify their behavior to provide better care. This suggests that moral distress, while burdensome, can also stimulate professional growth. Previous studies have highlighted similar outcomes, showing that moral distress promoted self-awareness and reflection, with nurses describing these experiences as profoundly influential in shaping their ability to focus on patients and families.20 Our findings support this perspective, indicating that moral distress may cause suffering and foster deeper commitment to patient-centered and family-centered care when adequately supported.

Silence as a cross-cutting theme

Across all five categories, one common thread that emerged was silence—often expressed as “not saying” or “not being able to say.” Nurses described holding back their thoughts or concerns to avoid disrupting relationships with physicians or colleagues. While this silence may have helped keep the peace on the surface, it also meant that ethical tensions remained unresolved, contributing to their moral distress.

Such difficulty in speaking up has been reported in prior studies on critical care settings, where hierarchical structures and fear of reprisal can make open discussion difficult.18 Our findings reflect the same dynamics, along with a cultural layer. In many interviews, nurses spoke of choosing silence to maintain a sense of harmony in the workplace—a value deeply rooted in Japanese culture.21

However, Konishi drew a clear line between conformity and true harmony. Conformity involves suppressing one’s real opinions to avoid conflict, while harmony, in its genuine sense, is about working toward mutual understanding and shared goals through respectful dialogue.21 In our study, nurses often chose silence out of obligation or fear—not out of a shared ethical consensus. This suggests that their silence was more about conformity than harmony. At times, participants themselves referred to their silence as a way to preserve harmony, but, looking more closely, it may have been a strategy to endure difficult situations rather than resolve them. As Konishi et al21 noted, for nurses to act ethically and professionally, they must understand the difference between maintaining true harmony and simply conforming. This distinction becomes especially important in ethically complex environments like critical care.

This tendency toward silence does not indicate a lack of ethical awareness or professional weakness among nurses; rather, it reflects a structural response shaped by hierarchical and cultural norms. In critical care settings, cultural norms often make maintaining silence appear the most realistic way to preserve relationships. Understanding this context is essential in explaining why Japanese CCNs experience moral distress under such circumstances. Simultaneously, it is crucial to distinguish genuine harmony from mere conformity. While silence may temporarily ease tension, moving beyond conformity toward mutually constructive communication would better support both professional integrity and patient-centered and family-centered care. Limitations

This study has certain limitations. First, as our study involved interviews with experienced CCNs recruited from only 9 department general hospitals in the Tokai region of Japan, each operating at least one adult ICU, the findings may not reflect the perspectives of nurses from other regions or from those who are less experienced. Second, we do not know whether external events, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, influenced the interviews or whether nurses were affected by current patient care, because some of them were interviewed at work. Finally, not all participants who experience moral distress may view these experiences similarly. Despite such limitations, the findings provide important insights that inform both practice and future research directions.

Recommendations or implications for practice and scope for future research

This study’s findings allow us to recognize moral distress among Japanese CCNs. Identifying the root causes of moral distress will help managers undertake appropriate interventions to address the essential underlying causes that affect nurses in critical care settings. Some nurses continue to work and change the way they practice to act appropriately, reflecting on their experience of moral distress. This topic requires further investigation to understand ways to endure moral distress and the changes that occur over time. Qualitative and quantitative studies are needed to assess CCNs’ moral distress level and investigate its associated factors.

CONCLUSIONS

This study identified 5 categories of constraints and additional findings that describe how Japanese CCNs experience moral distress. These constraints include difficulties in achieving common understanding with patients and families, insufficiencies in the therapy/treatment/care centered on patients and families, physicians’ attitudes that communicate superiority and higher status, work environments that make expressing differences in values difficult, and tension between one’s professional and moral responsibilities. Collectively, they show how moral distress arises when nurses are prevented from acting according to their values and ethical principles. This study contributes to the existing literature by highlighting silence as a cross-cutting theme that connects these constraints. Cultural values emphasizing wa (harmony) and hierarchical respect appear to intensify this silence, amplifying its impact on nurses’ autonomy and professional integrity, which has been described as the core of moral distress.22 These findings suggest the importance of organizational and cultural interventions that enable nurses to voice ethical concerns and support patient-centered and family-centered care. Promoting open ethical dialogue and supportive team communication may help ease nurses’ psychological burden and intention to leave, while improving patient safety and quality of care. Future research using a moral distress scale for Japanese critical care nurses will clarify how moral distress influences care quality, ethical sensitivity, and patient outcomes.
