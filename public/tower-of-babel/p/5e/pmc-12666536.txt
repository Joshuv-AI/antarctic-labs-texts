Neo-sex Chromosomes Track the Mitochondrial Phylogeny and Exhibit an Extensive Added Stratum of Recombination Suppression in Honeyeaters (Aves: Meliphagidae)
Authors: Sophia C M Orzechowski, Doaa Doudin, Foyez Shams, C Jonathan Schmitt, Alex Drew, Chris Wilson, Leo Joseph, Tariq Ezaz, Scott V Edwards
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMounting discoveries of avian neo-sex chromosomes are providing opportunities to understand the extent of variation in fundamental aspects of avian neo-sex chromosome evolution. We integrated cytogenetic data, long-read assemblies, and whole-genome resequencing to test phylogenetic hypotheses of recombination suppression and to elucidate the phylogenetic distribution of neo-sex chromosomes in honeyeaters (Aves: Meliphagidae). We find that neo-sex chromosomes in honeyeaters evolved through a fusion of the long arm of chromosome 5 and the pseudoautosomal region (PAR) of both ancestral Z and W sex chromosomes. Resequencing data from 11 species of honeyeaters and outgroups supports our cytogenetic evidence that these neo-sex chromosomes are derived within honeyeaters. Phylogenetic analyses confirm that all tested honeyeaters share the same breakpoint for a new 17.4 Mb PAR at the end of the neo-sex chromosomes and suggest a single, large expansion of recombination suppression, encompassing 44.6 Mb, is most supported in the newly fused region of the neo-W. We also discovered phylogenetic discordance between the mapping of neo-sex chromosomes on the established nuclear and mitochondrial (mtDNA) phylogenies. We conclude that neo-sex chromosomes arose once in honeyeaters because they form a monophyletic clade on the mtDNA tree, which shares the phylogenetic history of the neo-W through matrilineal coinheritance. Overall, our findings provide new insights on recombination suppression dynamics of avian neo-sex chromosomes and demonstrate the value of comparing nuclear and mtDNA trees to determine the phylogenetic distribution of neo-sex chromosomes, especially in the presence of mitonuclear discordance, which is common across the avian tree of life.

We report on the widespread occurrence of neo-sex chromosomes in birds known as honeyeaters, resulting from fusion of ancestral chromosome 5 with both Z and W sex chromosomes. We inform the ongoing inquiry regarding how recombination suppression progresses on neo-W chromosomes, finding that it has expanded across a large added region in all honeyeaters we tested, rather than piecemeal in specific species or lineages. Our findings also reveal the importance of comparing mitochondrial and nuclear trees when deciphering the evolutionary origin of neo-sex chromosomes.

Introduction

Sex chromosomes are variable across the tree of life in terms of their homology, size, shape, genetic content, and role in sex determination. Some animal groups, such as squamate reptiles and fish, display exceptional rates of sex chromosome turnover (Bachtrog et al. 2014; Beukeboom and Perrin 2014), which can involve transitions between XY (male heterogamety) and ZW (female heterogamety) systems, translocation of sex-determining loci to other chromosomes, evolutionary turnover of the loci involved in sex determination, and formation of neo-sex chromosomes—where an autosome fuses, partially or wholly, to one or both ancestral sex chromosomes (Abbott et al. 2017). The lability of sex chromosome systems, including the formation of neo-sex chromosomes, provides valuable opportunities to study the early stages of sex chromosome evolution, which is difficult to reconstruct for sex chromosomes that are ancient and highly heteromorphic.

The ZW sex chromosomes of birds largely display phylogenetic stability over >140 million years of evolution (Nam and Ellegren 2008; Ellegren 2010; Yazdi and Ellegren 2014); however, numerous recent discoveries of avian neo-sex chromosomes are revealing that this type of sex chromosome turnover in birds is more common than previously thought. Currently, at least four examples are known of neo-sex chromosome formation across different songbird lineages (order Passeriformes; suborder Passeri), and there are yet further nested occurrences at lower taxonomic levels (Pala et al. 2012; Gan et al. 2019; Sigeman et al. 2020; Sigeman 2021; Burley et al. 2023; Shogren et al. 2024). Additionally, at least four examples of neo-sex chromosome systems have been recently discovered in members of four avian clades outside Passeriformes: parrots, ibises, anis, and penguins (Gunski et al. 2017; Kretschmer et al. 2020; Huang et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2024), further indication that in birds the landscape of sex chromosome evolution is more dynamic than previously thought.

Most documented cases of neo-sex chromosomes in birds appear to involve fusion of an autosome to each of the two ancestral sex chromosomes, resulting in enlarged neo-Z and neo-W chromosomes (Sigeman et al. 2021; Huang et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2024). Autosomal regions that fused to the sex chromosome are considered “added,” whereas the regions of the sex chromosomes that fused are considered “ancestral,” following Sigeman et al. (2021). Once fusion occurs, recombination suppression (RS) may evolve and persist between the initially homologous added Z and added W for a variety of reasons, including sexually antagonistic selection or regulatory evolution to counteract degeneration following a nonadaptive inversion causing RS, thereby reinforcing it (Ponnikas et al. 2018; Lenormand and Roze 2022; D. Charlesworth 2023; Jay et al. 2024). Regardless of the underlying cause, RS often leads to degeneration of the female-limited (neo-)W chromosome in birds, divergence between (neo-)Z and (neo-)W chromosomes, and ultimately, heteromorphic sex chromosomes (Charlesworth 2021).

Empirical evidence demonstrates that RS between (neo-)Z and (neo-)W chromosomes can occur gradually or in large, discrete strata (Handley et al. 2004; Olito and Abbott 2023; Jay et al. 2024). The evolutionary processes underlying these alternate patterns have been difficult to test empirically, making indirect inference an important alternative approach. For instance, recent simulations suggest that the length of the stratum subject to RS can provide insight into whether it was an adaptive or nonadaptive loss of recombination, with the fixation of larger strata being more likely to be universally beneficial or capture unlinked loci that would benefit from sex-linkage to resolve sexual antagonism (Olito and Abbott 2023). The cause of the formation of small strata is more difficult to disentangle with simulations. Small strata could result from either small, neutral inversions, or beneficial inversions that, due to physical proximity, capture an allele experiencing sexual antagonism and already in linkage disequilibrium with the ancestral zone of RS (Olito and Abbott 2023). Regardless, it is instructive to map out patterns of RS along neo-sex chromosomes to generate hypotheses regarding underlying causes and to compare to other avian neo-sex chromosome systems.

Neo-sex chromosomes are expected to reflect organismal phylogeny depending on their time of origin and how frequently they arise convergently. Many of the known cases of neo-sex chromosomes in birds involve independent fusions of different autosomes with the ancestral sex chromosomes. However, chromosome 5 (Zebra Finch, Taeniopygia guttata, nomenclature; henceforth Chr 5), alone is now implicated in at least three independent fusion events in songbirds: in honeyeaters, larks, and Australasian robins (Sigeman 2021; Shakya et al. 2022; Burley et al. 2023; Low 2023; Shogren et al. 2024). Although such rearrangements can occur convergently across phylogenies, at narrower taxonomic scales among subfamilies or genera they may serve as “rare genomic changes”: phylogenomic markers made more powerful by virtue of their reduced homoplasy (Rokas and Holland 2000; Edwards 2019).

To determine the phylogenetic distribution of neo-sex chromosomes within a particular lineage, it can be useful to compare how they map onto nuclear and mitochondrial phylogenies. Neo-W and W chromosomes are inherited matrilineally with mtDNA (Moore 1995; Berlin and Ellegren 2001; Kubatko and Degnan 2007; Irwin 2025), which means that they are—with the exception of the PAR—expected to show phylogenetic concordance with mtDNA, but not always with the autosomal genome. Indeed, discordance between the mtDNA and nuclear genomes in birds is now commonplace, often resulting from differing histories of incomplete lineage sorting (ILS) or introgression (Toews and Brelsford 2012; Pavlova et al. 2013; Kimball et al. 2021; DeRaad et al. 2023). Sex chromosomes and mtDNA both possess reduced effective population sizes, which should reduce the likelihood of ILS relative to autosomal loci, although ILS due to deep coalescence of the mtDNA genome can still occur in rapidly speciating groups (DeRaad et al. 2023). In addition, mtDNA, and by extension the (neo-)W, can often preferentially flow across species boundaries compared to nuclear loci due to its matrilineal inheritance, lack of recombination, and the potential for rescue of deleterious mtDNA mutations by introgression (Toews and Brelsford 2012; Hill 2019). On balance, the likelihood of departures from the species tree is lower on sex chromosomes than on autosomes (Sætre et al. 2003; Jiang et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2024; Burbrink et al. 2025) because of reduced meiotic recombination and reduced effective population sizes, but still possible, whereas linkage of the (neo-)W with mtDNA is unlikely to be broken. Ultimately, because the (neo-)W must track the mtDNA genome, which in turn can differ from the autosomal phylogeny, comparing the phylogenetic distribution of neo-sex chromosomes with trees based on mtDNA and nuclear data can be particularly informative when trying to decipher their evolutionary origin.

We turned to honeyeaters (class Aves; order Passeriformes; suborder Passeri; family Meliphagidae), which we recently discovered exhibits genomic signatures of a neo-Z chromosome involving Chr 5 (Burley et al. 2023), to address the following questions regarding the evolution of neo-sex chromosomes in this family. Our first objective was to cytogenetically validate the existence of neo-sex chromosomes in honeyeaters, and our second objective was to determine how widespread neo-sex chromosomes are in this family, and whether they arose from a single origin. Our final objective was to employ phylogenetic hypothesis testing to determine the timing and extent of RS across the added region, to establish whether RS occurred piecemeal in multiple lineages or once in a single common ancestor, potentially resulting in a new, large evolutionary stratum on the neo-W, consistent with adaptive RS and a single origin of these chromosomes (Nam and Ellegren 2008; Olito and Abbott 2023).

To investigate neo-sex chromosome evolution in honeyeaters, we herein employ a “chromosomics' approach (Deakin et al. 2019) by integrating cytogenetic experiments, long-read sequencing, Hi-C data, and whole-genome resequencing. Cytogenetic approaches, including fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), are valuable tools for revealing karyotypic evolution and novel chromosomal rearrangements, especially for chromosomes that are difficult to assemble, like the repeat-rich (neo-)W chromosome (Peona et al. 2021a). Our comprehensive characterization of these novel neo-sex chromosomes in honeyeaters is a fundamental first step toward uncovering the evolutionary forces shaping neo-sex chromosomes and how they may be involved in the process of diversification across the tree of life.

Results

Cytogenetic Validation of Fusion and Structure of Neo-Sex Chromosomes in Honeyeaters

To test our hypothesis regarding the formation of neo-sex chromosomes in honeyeaters, we generated cytogenetic data from three species of honeyeaters: Blue-faced Honeyeater (Entomyzon cyanotis), White-eared Honeyeater (Nesoptilotis leucotis), Noisy Miner (Manorina melanocephala), and two close outgroups: Striated Pardalote (Pardalotus striatus) and Yellow-rumped Thornbill (Acanthiza chrysorrhoa; Table S1). We chose these species because they represent different major clades in the most recent honeyeater phylogenies spanning ∼30 million years of evolution (Marki et al. 2017; Andersen et al. 2019). Previously, we found genomic signatures of sex-linkage across much of Chr 5 and evidence of fusion between Chr 5 and ancestral Z in a draft long-read assembly of Blue-faced Honeyeater (Burley et al. 2023). From the Blue-faced Honeyeater assembly, we selected three regions to design probes to test the fusion hypothesis with FISH. Two probes were designed to map to the ancestral Chr 5; one was expected to map next to the putative fusion breakpoint (green) and the other to the putative autosomal remnant (yellow; Fig. S1). The third probe we designed so that it mapped to the ancestral pseudoautosomal region (henceforth PAR) on both Z and W (red; Fig. S1).

Using these three probes, we found clear evidence of fusion between the ancestral PAR and Chr 5 in a homogametic (male, ZZ) Blue-faced Honeyeater karyotype, confirming our previous work (Fig. 1). In the closely related White-eared Honeyeater, for which we were able to produce a heterogametic (ZW) karyotype, we found clear evidence of fusion between Chr 5 and both ancestral Z and ancestral W (Figs. 1 and 2). In both these honeyeaters, the third probe (yellow) mapped to a microchromosome, apparently a highly truncated version of Chr 5, which we designated Chr 5a (Fig. 1). The third honeyeater we tested, the Noisy Miner, was found not to possess neo-sex chromosomes, because both Chr 5 probes mapped to the same chromosome, and the ancestral PAR probe mapped to different chromosomes (Fig. 1). However, unexpectedly, the Noisy Miner showed evidence of an additional and novel autosome-autosome fusion involving Chr 5, because the two Chr 5 probes mapped to the second largest chromosome in the Noisy Miner karyotype, disrupting the syntenic chromosome order found in many songbirds (Fig. 1, see Discovery of a Novel Chr 5-1A Fusion in Noisy Miner). Our two outgroup species, the Yellow-rumped Thornbill and Striated Pardalote, were also found not to possess neo-sex chromosomes, and displayed the ancestral avian karyotype, whereby both Chr 5 probes map to the fifth largest chromosome (Fig. 1).

We present a schematic diagram of the probe locations and relative size of chromosomes in Fig. 2. Due to the considerable challenge of developing cell cultures for nonmodel wild avian species for which tissues were harvested and preserved in the field, we did not have both heterogametic and homogametic karyotypes for each species we tested. Nevertheless, it was possible to infer the homogametic karyotype for the species for which we only had heterogametic individuals, because all chromosomes are present in heterogametic individuals (Fig. 2). We predicted the heterogametic karyotype for Blue-faced Honeyeater based on its close phylogenetic relationship with the White-eared Honeyeater (Fig. 2; Marki et al. 2017, Andersen et al. 2019). Our cytogenetic data, and the PAR probe in particular, revealed that the ancestral PARs appeared at the termini of the ancestral sex chromosomes for the three tested species lacking neo-sex chromosomes (Fig. 1). In the neo-Z of Blue-faced Honeyeater, the ancestral PAR occurs at the proximal end of the ancestral Z region, which now corresponds to the middle of the neo-Z chromosome because of the fusion with ancestral Chr 5 (Figs. 1 and 2). In summary, the autosome-sex chromosome fusions that we cytogenetically validated in honeyeaters resulted in an ancestral and added region for both enlarged neo-Z and neo-W chromosomes, following the terminology of Sigeman et al. (2021; Fig. S1).

Assemblies and Resequencing Data Refine Neo-Sex Chromosome Structure and Placement of New PAR

We generated new Oxford Nanopore long-read genome assemblies for a honeyeater, the Noisy Miner, and two outgroups: the Striated Pardalote and Yellow-rumped Thornbill. We scaffolded our long-read assemblies of Blue-faced Honeyeater and Noisy Miner into chromosomes using Hi-C chromatin conformation data (Figs. S2 and S3). Our Hi-C contact maps and Hi-C–scaffolded assembly of Blue-faced Honeyeater corroborated our cytogenetic evidence that fusion initially occurred between the long arm of Chr 5, likely in the pericentric region, and the PAR of the ancestral sex chromosomes (Fig. 3a; Figs. S2 and S3). By comparing the Hi-C-scaffolded neo-W and neo-Z chromosomes of Blue-faced Honeyeater through synteny mapping, we also discovered that the neo-W has undergone several rearrangements since the initial Chr 5 fusion event with the ancestral PAR (Fig. 3b). The synteny analysis demonstrates that the ancestral W has become interwoven within the neo-W chromosome through one or multiple inversions and/or intrachromosomal translocations, and the ancestral PAR is now in the middle of one portion of ancestral W sequence (Fig. 3b). This rearrangement suggests that the ancestral PAR of the neo-W is no longer recombining with the ancestral PAR on the neo-Z in Blue-faced Honeyeater, a conclusion supported by our previous genomic evidence that the ancestral PAR is sex-linked in this species (Burley et al. 2023). We note that we cannot know for certain where the ancestral PAR occurs in the neo-W chromosome of White-eared Honeyeater (see Fig. 2) because we do not yet have a Hi-C–scaffolded assembly of this species. However, our cytogenetic data clearly position it within the neo-W, not at the terminus, in White-eared Honeyeater. Therefore, we placed it within one of the ancestral W portions in the schematic for Fig. 2.

We used FindZX (Sigeman et al. 2022), a computational pipeline that scans for genomic signatures of sex chromosomes with resequencing data from both sexes, to identify sex-linked regions and the placement of the new PAR on the neo-sex chromosomes. FindZX corroborated the existence of neo-sex chromosomes in honeyeaters, as demonstrated by an excess of heterozygosity in females when mapping reads from both sexes to a homogametic reference genome of Blue-faced Honeyeater (Fig. 4a). This signature of sex-linkage is a consequence of RS on the sex-limited neo-W chromosome (see Phylogenetic tests of RS of the neo-W). Our FindZX analyses also demonstrated that a 17.4 Mb new PAR occurs at the end of the added region of the neo-sex chromosomes, which possesses no sex-linked differences in heterozygosity, indicating frequent recombination in this region (Fig. 4b). We found that the boundary of the new PAR, as determined by the transition from elevated female heterozygosity to zero heterozygosity differences between sexes, is the same for all tested species of honeyeaters possessing neo-sex chromosomes (Fig. 4b). Although additional, unique chromosomal rearrangements likely exist among these species, we found that the total amount of sex-linked sequence across the neo-sex chromosomes is equivalent for all species tested—from which it is reasonable to infer that the size and terminal location of the new PAR of these neo-sex chromosomes does not vary across these species. Our identification of the new PAR through genomic signatures of sex-linkage is corroborated by phylogenetic tests of RS (see Phylogenetic tests of RS of the neo-W).

Phylogenetic Distribution of Neo-Sex Chromosomes Across Honeyeaters

To extend our understanding of the distribution of neo-sex chromosomes across the honeyeater phylogeny, we scanned nine honeyeater species for genomic signatures of neo-sex chromosomes using the FindZX pipeline, including the three honeyeaters with cytogenetic data, for purposes of consistency and cross validation (Table S1). In addition, we sequenced both sexes of Rufous-throated Honeyeater (Conopophila rufogularis), as well as a male Little Friarbird (Philemon citreogularis) and a female Silver-crowned Friarbird (Philemon argenticeps), which together represent the genus Philemon (Table S2). We also downloaded resequencing data from NCBI for both sexes of Long-billed Honeyeater (Melilestes megarhynchus), Rufous-backed Honeyeater (Ptiloprora guisei), and Smoky Honeyeater (Melipotes fumigatus) (Pujolar et al. 2022; Table S3). As expected, Blue-faced Honeyeater and White-eared Honeyeater had clear signatures of sex-linkage on Chr 5 in the form of elevated heterozygosity in females (Fig. 4a and b). The FindZX analysis showed that the samples of the genus Philemon also possessed the same signatures of neo-sex chromosomes (Figs. 4a and 4b). The other five honeyeaters displayed no signatures of sex-linkage on Chr 5 or any other autosome (Fig. 4a). Although we did not have resequencing data from a female New Holland Honeyeater (Phylidonyris novaehollandiae), we scanned the short read genome assembly (GenBank ID: GCA_041053245.1) of a male from NCBI (Osipova et al. 2025) and by aligning the assembly to Zebra Finch (GenBank ID: GCA_003957565.4) found clear evidence of fusion between Chr 5 and the ancestral Z PAR on contig JACBJL010000044.1, similar to our findings with the initial assembly of Blue-faced Honeyeater (Burley et al. 2023).

We then mapped the above resequencing results onto the honeyeater phylogeny, which has been the focus of multiple previous phylogenetic studies and constructed with a range of loci and methods (Gardner et al. 2010; Joseph et al. 2014; Marki et al. 2017; Andersen et al. 2019). We used the most recent and comprehensive phylogeny for honeyeaters (Andersen et al. 2019) to map the distribution of neo-sex chromosomes across the clade and its outgroups, using the nine honeyeater species that we scanned for neo-sex chromosomes as representatives of major clades on the tree (Table 1). Andersen et al. (2019) sampled densely, at the level of genera, and built their trees from 4,397 nuclear ultraconserved element (UCE) loci with both maximum likelihood and coalescent methods. We therefore refer to their UCE tree as the nuclear species tree to distinguish it from the mtDNA gene tree (Andersen et al. 2019), which represents the evolutionary history of a single non-recombining locus. Mapping presence/absence of neo-sex chromosomes onto the nuclear species tree (Andersen et al. 2019) reveals paraphyly within the neo-sex chromosome clade (Fig. 5a). This paraphyly is not present when we map to trees built mostly or entirely from the mtDNA locus (Gardner et al. 2010; Joseph et al. 2014; Marki et al. 2017; Andersen et al. 2019), all of which suggest a single origin and monophyletic distribution of neo-sex chromosomes (Fig. 5b). Andersen et al. 2019 discussed the marked differences between the nuclear and mtDNA trees and we confirmed the existence of significant mitonuclear discordance by conducting a likelihood ratio test of the nuclear and mtDNA topologies against the UCE alignment of 56 honeyeater species and three outgroups. For computational tractability, we randomly sampled 1,000 loci from the UCE alignment and conducted a test of the topologies using IQTree2 (Minh et al. 2020). We found that the UCE data rejected the mtDNA tree across all metrics in favor of the nuclear species tree topology (Table S4).

Phylogenetic Tests of RS of the Neo-W

To further distinguish between single or multiple origins of neo-sex chromosomes in honeyeaters, we conducted additional phylogenetic tests to determine the genomic position and timing of RS in the honeyeaters with neo-sex chromosomes (Zhang et al. 2022). To conduct these tests, we created alignments with a subset of species, for which reference genomes or resequencing data were also available, representing major clades across the honeyeater phylogeny (Andersen et al. 2019). Clades E and G (designated by Andersen et al. 2019) contain neo-sex chromosomes and clade H does not (Table 1, Fig. 5). We phased W-linked and Z-linked sequences of species with neo-sex chromosomes using custom scripts adapted from Sigeman et al. (2018). We were specifically interested in the timing and extent of RS expansion in the ancestral PAR and added region of the neo-sex chromosomes. Other than the ancestral PAR, we chose not to focus on the timing of RS in the ancestral region because shared RS across multiple evolutionary strata in the ancestral sex chromosomes has been previously established in Passeriformes (Xu et al. 2019a; Sigeman et al. 2021). However, we included 232 coding loci in the ancestral region beyond the ancestral PAR solely to test whether the nuclear or mtDNA tree topology was best supported there. We constructed a set of rooted reference topologies to test different scenarios of RS at coding regions in the ancestral PAR (6 loci) and added W region (164 loci) on the backbones of the nuclear species tree and mtDNA tree, respectively (Fig. S4). To represent different scenarios of RS in the ancestral PAR and added region, the topologies differed in the phylogenetic position of phased W-linked sequences for the relevant species (Fig. S4). We then compared the phylogenetic support for each topology at each coding alignment using a metric of expected likelihood weights (ELWs; Strimmer and Rambaut 2002), which estimates the support for each predefined topology with site-wise log-likelihoods and computes an averaged ELW from bootstrapped replicates of site likelihoods for each topology.

The most frequent and supported signal from the maximum ELWs across all coding alignments across the chromosome indicates that a single origin of RS at the base of the clade containing species with neo-sex chromosomes is the most common scenario overall (Fig. 6). When we include all loci in the added region of RS (after filtering out alignments < 1000 bp), 76% of the loci (111 out of 146) support a single origin of RS based on maximum ELW values. However, for a number of loci, the maximum ELW value is < 0.5 and ELW values at these loci are spread out across multiple topologies indicating low phylogenetic signal. When we subset more strictly to consider loci where the maximum ELW value for the best supported topology is > 0.5, 92.6% of the loci (75 out of 81 total) support a single origin of RS, represented by Trees 5, 7, and 9. We find that the median alignment length for loci with maximum ELW > 0.5 is 2295 bp, whereas loci with maximum ELW < 0.5 have a median length of 1335 bp, a pattern showing, as expected, that alignment length strongly dictates the amount of available phylogenetic signal. With our stricter filter in place, a small proportion of loci (7.4%, or 6 out of 81) display other patterns, represented by Trees 4 (1 locus), 6 (3 loci) and 8 (2 loci). These scattered loci suggest that RS may initially have been incomplete for a subset of loci or proceeded in a mosaic fashion across the chromosome, as found in Sigeman et al. (2021). The degree of uncertainty and spatial clumping of the ELWs varies across the neo-sex chromosomes, increasing around the fusion breakpoint and decreasing towards the distal ends (Fig. S5). We find that, whereas the proximal end, where the fusion event took place, displays a monophyletic origin of the neo-sex chromosomes (Fig. 6) represented by Trees 5 and 7, the distal end of the added region displays a paraphyletic placement of clade H (Table 1) within the neo-sex chromosome clade (Fig. 6 and Fig. S5). This conflicting pattern at the distal end predominates and is strongly supported by the ELWs represented by Tree 9 in the region of RS and Tree 2 in the new PAR.

Trees 1Z and 2Z occur in the ancestral region of RS, thus these trees solely test relative support for the mtDNA (1Z) or species (2Z) tree topology in this region. The fusion point at the ancestral PAR is represented by the dashed red line.

The overall signal of a single event of RS across a large stratum in the added region of the neo-W is shared across species and consistent with the shared pattern of sex differences in heterozygosity across the added region of the neo-sex chromosomes (Fig. 4b). Our phylogenetic tests confirm the absence of RS in the new PAR region, represented by Tree 2, which had high scores of maximum ELW throughout this region (Fig. 6 and Fig. S5). The sharp and invariant transition from strong sex-linked differences in heterozygosity to none in the new PAR for all tested species (Fig. 4b), supports our phylogenetic tests and also strongly suggests a shared history of RS among all honeyeaters with neo-sex chromosomes.

Repetitive Landscape of Neo-Sex Chromosomes in Blue-faced Honeyeater

One of the consequences of RS on (neo-)W chromosomes is that the corresponding decline in the efficacy of selection can facilitate increased fixation of repetitive transposable elements (TEs), which are often deleterious (Bachtrog 2013; Peona et al. 2021b). Analysis with RepeatModeler2 and RepeatMasker showed that 72% of the ancestral W region in Blue-faced Honeyeater consists of TEs (Fig. 7). We found a moderate enrichment of TEs on the added W (22% repeat content) compared to the ancestral W, neo-Z or new PAR (Fig. 7).

Repetitive content and TE divergence landscapes across neo-sex chromosome regions of Blue-faced Honeyeater. The ancestral W region is enriched in repeats (72%), followed by the added W (22%). Both W regions are enriched in LTRs (long terminal repeats) from endogenous retroviral elements. LINEs (long interspersed nuclear elements) are proportionally more common in the ancestral Z, added Z, new PAR, which overall have reduced repeat content compared to the neo-W. The TE divergence landscape for the ancestral W and added W is fairly similar, with a shared peak of recent to moderately diverged LTRs (<10% divergence).

Based on the Hi-C–scaffolded assembly of Blue-faced Honeyeater, we found that the RS region of the added W was ∼7.4 Mb larger than the homologous added Z region, constituting a 20% increase in the size of the added W. This increase in size can be explained by the influx of repetitive elements in the recombination-suppressed added W compared to the homologous region on the added Z (22% vs. 6.4% repetitive, respectively; Figs. 3a and 7). The expansion of TEs is the reason why the stratum of RS encompasses 44.6 Mb on the neo-W whereas the corresponding neo-Z sequence encompasses only 37.2 Mb in Blue-faced Honeyeater (Burley et al. 2023). Including the 17.4 Mb new PAR, the total size of the added Z region is 54.6 Mb, and the total size of the added W region is 62 Mb in Blue-faced Honeyeater. We expect the size of the neo-W to vary somewhat in other honeyeaters due to differential gain and loss of TEs, but we expect the signature of RS relative to the neo-Z to remain the same across species under a single origin of RS as demonstrated by Fig. 4b.

We examined the distribution of TEs across the neo-sex chromosomes in Blue-faced Honeyeater to determine if any particular elements were especially abundant around the fusion breakpoint and therefore potentially involved in the formation of neo-sex chromosomes in honeyeaters. We found that a 2254 bp element classified as a Rolling Circle/Helitron was enriched in the ancestral PAR, adjacent to the fusion breakpoint of the neo-Z and neo-W, and nowhere else on these chromosomes (Fig. S6). Misclassification of this element is likely because Helitrons have not hitherto been found in birds (Weissensteiner and Suh 2019; Li et al. 2024). We therefore analyzed the Blue-faced Honeyeater genome with the software Heliano (Li et al. 2024) to look for intact and full length Helitrons, but found no positive hits. This same helitron-like element was also annotated in the ancestral PAR of Painted Honeyeater, Noisy Miner, and the two outgroups—Yellow-rumped Thornbill and Striated Pardalote—which confirms that it arose prior to the radiation of honeyeaters and is not specific to the clade of honeyeaters with neo-sex chromosomes (Figs. S7 and S8). It was not present at the fusion breakpoint of Chr 5 and Chr 1A in Noisy Miner, a honeyeater without neo-sex chromosomes (Fig. S7), and we found no evidence of it on truncated Chr “5a” of Blue-faced Honeyeater or on any contigs homologous to ancestral Chr 5 in Painted Honeyeater, Noisy Miner, Yellow-rumped Thornbill, or Striated Pardalote.

Discovery of a Novel Chr 5-1A Fusion in Noisy Miner

Beyond confirming the fusion scenario and corroborating the phylogenetic distribution of neo-sex chromosomes, our cytogenetic data also revealed a previously unknown rearrangement involving the macrochromosomes in the Noisy Miner. We found that Chr 5 fused independently to another autosome, Chr 1A (Zebra Finch nomenclature; Fig. 1 and Figs. S9 and S10). We initially noticed that both Chr 5 probes mapped to the same arm of a large metacentric chromosome—the second largest chromosome overall in the Noisy Miner karyotype—which meant that “Chr 5” had essentially doubled in size compared to Zebra Finch or the two closer outgroup species, Striated Pardalote and Yellow-rumped Thornbill (Fig. 1). Upon integrating chromatin conformation (Hi-C) data to scaffold the genome assembly into chromosomes, we obtained a clear signal of fusion between Chr 5 and Chr 1A in the Noisy Miner (Figs. S9 and S10). This likely occurred through a Robertsonian translocation between two acrocentric or telocentric autosomes and does not appear to have resulted in much, if any, loss of sequence, based on synteny analyses with closely related species (Fig. S9). This rearrangement is not shared in another closely related honeyeater, the Helmeted Honeyeater (Lichenostomus melanops cassidix), which also has a chromosome-level assembly built from linkage maps, long-read sequencing, and Hi-C data (Robledo-Ruiz et al. 2022). In the Yellow-rumped Thornbill and Striated Pardalote outgroups, we found that both Chr 5 probes mapped to the 5th largest chromosome, meaning that, at this cytogenetic level of resolution, this syntenic relationship with Chicken and Zebra Finch has not changed. Our cytogenetic data also revealed a pericentric inversion wherein Chr 5 is metacentric in Yellow-rumped Thornbill, but acrocentric or telocentric in Striated Pardalote (Fig. 2 and Fig. S9).

Discussion

We leveraged multiple lines of evidence to test for the presence of neo-sex chromosomes in honeyeaters and relatives; to establish the phylogenetic concordance of these chromosomes with nuclear or mitochondrial phylogenies; and to determine the timing and extent of RS across the added region. Given our initial discovery of a neo-sex chromosome in the Blue-faced Honeyeater (Burley et al. 2023) and an independent discovery involving the same autosome (Chr 5) in Myzomela honeyeaters (Sardell 2016; Shogren et al. 2024), our working hypothesis was that neo-sex chromosomes arose once in honeyeaters. Thus, we expected to delimit a monophyletic clade, either within or encompassing honeyeaters, possessing neo-sex chromosomes, because this scenario is most parsimonious for a phylogenetically constrained chromosomal rearrangement. Our cytogenetic experiments confirmed the existence of neo-Z and neo-W chromosomes in multiple honeyeater species, whereby the long arm of ancestral Chr 5 has fused to both ancestral Z and W sex chromosomes. However, our mapping of multiple neo-sex chromosomes across the honeyeater phylogeny revealed, surprisingly, that a monophyletic distribution of neo-sex chromosomes was only evident on the mtDNA tree, which was known to differ from the nuclear species tree (Marki et al. 2017; Andersen et al. 2019). Our results demonstrate the utility of mtDNA in deciphering the phylogenetic distribution of neo-sex chromosomes because of its linkage with the (neo-)W, regardless of whether the mtDNA tree is discordant with the nuclear species tree. Additionally, our finding of a single, concordantly mapped region of RS across multiple honeyeaters is also consistent with neo-sex chromosomes, and expansion of RS, evolving once within honeyeaters.

Complex Phylogenetic History of Neo-Sex Chromosomes in Honeyeaters

Several scenarios can help explain the pattern in which neo-sex chromosomes exhibit paraphyly on the nuclear species tree for honeyeaters. These include (i) multiple independent origins of neo-sex chromosomes in clade G and clade D + E (Fig. 5), (ii) a loss of neo-sex chromosomes in clade H (Fig. 5), or (iii) tracking of the neo-sex chromosomes with mtDNA, which conflicts with the nuclear tree due to ILS, introgression, and/or hybrid speciation. The likelihood of each of these possibilities varies considerably. Two independent origins of neo-sex chromosomes, one within clade D + E and one within clade G, are possible, especially since Chr 5 appears to have a propensity for rearrangement and fusion, and is plausible under a scenario of TE-mediated rearrangement. If neo-sex chromosomes arose multiple times, we would have likely seen differing patterns of RS in different clades, which we do not. It is also conceivable that different extents of RS could occur in different subclades after a single origin of neo-sex chromosomes, however. This is one reason why cytogenetic evidence is so important—it is the best and most rigorous way to conclusively identify neo-sex chromosomes in subclades where fusion but subsequent RS has not occurred, for example. Ultimately, we did not find differing patterns or varying extents of RS in the honeyeaters we tested, which supports a single origin scenario of both neo-sex chromosome formation and RS in honeyeaters. Finally, a loss of neo-sex chromosomes in clade H is unlikely from the perspective of Dollo's law of irreversibility (Gould 1970), because it involves many coordinated steps. First, both neo-sex chromosomes would have to have fissioned in the same place at the ancestral PAR; second, the truncated Chr “5a” microchromosome would then have to reattach to the long arm fragments of Chr 5 to support our cytogenetic data and the Hi-C–scaffolded assemblies of Noisy Miner and Helmeted Honeyeater.

The third scenario—tracking of the neo-sex chromosomes with the mtDNA tree, in conjunction with phylogenetic discordance between the nuclear and mtDNA tree—is also possible, and, we argue, the most likely. Mitonuclear discordance in honeyeaters is likely a genuine biological signal and not simply phylogenetic uncertainty, given that we showed that the two trees differ significantly. We found that the mtDNA tree topology was significantly excluded when comparing it against the nuclear topology and a randomized subset of 1,000 UCE loci used to build the species tree. In addition, the newest species tree of honeyeaters is unlikely to suffer from a lack of resolution given that over 4,000 nuclear UCE loci from 57 species representing all honeyeater genera were used to build a species tree with maximum likelihood and coalescent methods (Andersen et al. 2019). The mtDNA tree from Andersen et al. (2019) included the same taxa and consisted of all 13 mtDNA coding loci concatenated into a single alignment. The main source of uncertainty in the mtDNA tree would stem from signal saturation due to homoplasy, which could increase uncertainty and non-phylogenetic signal among deep divergences of the mtDNA tree (Philippe et al. 2011), but there was no evidence of phylogenetic uncertainty at the deepest split between clades with and without neo-sex chromosomes in the analyses of Andersen et al. (2019); this node received 100% bootstrap support in all mtDNA analyses (see supplementary files of Andersen et al. 2019). The fact that neo-sex chromosome presence/absence matches relationships in the mtDNA tree in honeyeaters adds certainty to the mtDNA phylogeny, supporting the idea that neo-sex chromosomes arose once and thus can serve as a rare genomic marker that aids in phylogenetic inference—specifically of mtDNA, in this case (Rokas and Holland 2000). It should also be explicitly noted that the (neo-)Z is not matrilineally coinherited with mtDNA, but in this case, mtDNA can reasonably illuminate the evolutionary history of the neo-sex chromosome system in honeyeaters—not just the formation of the neo-W—because of concurrent fusions involving the PAR-end of the ancestral sex chromosomes (see Implications of fusion with the ancestral PAR and formation of a new PAR).

Discordance between nuclear and mtDNA trees has been widely reported in birds at multiple phylogenetic levels, and is not unusual (Toews and Brelsford 2012; Pavlova et al. 2013; Kimball et al. 2021; DeRaad et al. 2023). Introgression between ancestral populations with and without neo-sex chromosomes is one possible explanation for these patterns. The fact that topologies near the fusion point support the monophyly of the mtDNA tree means that this region would have been resistant to introgression, along with the fused neo-sex chromosome karyotype. Meiotic drive, which causes segregation distortion of individual loci and whole chromosomes, is one way that a fused neo-sex chromosome itself could be resistant to introgression, but not resistant to smaller-scale crossover events fueling introgression prior to meiotic chromosome segregation (Yoshida and Kitano 2012; Boman et al. 2024). Alternatively, neo-sex chromosomes could have been subject to ILS when they were in the process of sweeping to fixation in an ancestral population. The latest phylogenetic analyses suggest that internode branches were short in this region of the tree, suggesting rapid speciation events and making ILS another distinct possibility (Andersen et al. 2019) in generating conflict between the nuclear and mtDNA trees. Ultimately, a monophyletic origin of neo-sex chromosomes is supported by the mtDNA tree as well as our phylogenetic analyses of RS and genomic scans of sex-linkage across all tested species of honeyeaters with neo-sex chromosomes. Based on the mapping of presence and absence of neo-sex chromosomes within honeyeaters, our results are suggestive that at least ∼95 species and ∼19 genera of honeyeaters possess neo-sex chromosomes given their phylogenetic placement on the mtDNA tree, but further evaluation of additional honeyeaters will help test this phylogenetic hypothesis.

Progression of RS in Honeyeater Neo-Sex Chromosomes

How sex-limited chromosomes progressively lose recombination is still unclear, with empirical evidence for both gradual and large, discrete losses depending on the study system (Ponnikas et al. 2018; Jay et al. 2024). The signature of RS in the added region encompasses 44.6 Mb of the neo-W (and 37.2 Mb of the neo-Z; see Burley et al. 2023) in Blue-faced Honeyeater and our phylogenetic analyses suggest that this was not a gradual or species-specific pattern of loss, but rather occurred in its entirety at the base of the honeyeater clade containing neo-sex chromosomes. The structural rearrangements of the neo-W are consistent with large-scale suppression of recombination, although a recurring difficulty in sex chromosome studies is proving whether rearrangements such as inversions cause RS or evolve after the fact (Furman et al. 2020). Our phylogenetic results based on the mtDNA tree, which support a monophyletic origin of neo-sex chromosomes, are consistent with the hypothesis that RS occurred in the common ancestor of the clade. Had it not occurred then, the mtDNA tree would likely not display a monophyletic distribution of species with neo-sex chromosomes. It is the suppression of recombination, bringing the added W region in linkage disequilibrium with the ancestral W and mtDNA, that makes the mtDNA tree relevant in understanding the evolutionary history of neo-sex chromosomes in honeyeaters. Regions of low or no recombination across the genome, like mtDNA, can also aid in phylogenetic inference (Li et al. 2019; Burbrink et al. 2025), however it is important to reiterate that the species tree can differ from the evolutionary history of neo-sex chromosomes and mtDNA. Overall, when assessing phylogenetic hypotheses, it is best to consider the signals from Z and W (or X and Y) chromosomes, mtDNA, and autosomes separately (Burbrink et al. 2025). When incorporating neo-sex chromosomes into phylogenetic analyses, it is helpful to further distinguish between ancestral, added, and PAR regions of neo-Z and neo-W chromosomes, and take into account linkage between neo-W and mtDNA.

In the context of phylogenetic analysis of RS in honeyeater neo-sex chromosomes, we find a subset of loci on the added Z that do not support a clade-wide pattern of RS, but these loci do not undermine our conclusions for several reasons. RS can be incomplete, through mechanisms like gene conversion or double crossovers (B. Charlesworth 2023), which can act at smaller scales, even within a large tract of RS caused by an inversion or other mechanisms (Korunes and Noor 2019). In addition, because we created alignments from coding regions ranging from 1,002 to 11,067 bp (median length: 1869bp; Table S5), insufficient phylogenetic signal is likely at play in some cases, muddying the predominant pattern. Ultimately, we suggest the pattern we find in honeyeaters is more consistent with incomplete RS at small scales, which could also lead to a mosaic and nonlinear progression of RS like that found by Sigeman et al. (2021). The predominant signal of a single event of RS occurs throughout the recombination-suppressed added region. Loci that do not support a single event of RS are scattered intermittently throughout. Near the fusion breakpoint there is a cluster of loci supporting Trees 5 and 7, which both represent a single event of RS on a mtDNA tree backbone, differing only in whether RS occurs before or after the split of clade H (Fig. S4). Since ELW support is split between these two similar trees, this explains why we see a decline in the maximum ELW values around the fusion breakpoint (Fig. S5). This pattern indicates conflicting or insufficient phylogenetic signal in the coding loci in the midst of high densities of repeats around the fusion breakpoint. This reduction in signal could arise from any of the usual causes of phylogenetic conflict or uncertainty (Voelker and Edwards 1998; Galtier and Daubin 2008; Smith et al. 2015). However, this does not change our overall finding that the mtDNA tree is best supported right around the fusion breakpoint. Nor does it change our overall finding that the overall clade-wide pattern of RS is consistent for the majority of loci (ranging from 76% to 92.6% with strict filtering), and the extent of RS spans a large stratum (44.6 Mb relative to the neo-W of Blue-faced Honeyeater and 37.2 Mb relative to the neo-Z of Blue-faced Honeyeater; see Burley et al. 2023) in the added region. This pattern mirrors the findings of other avian studies examining progression of RS in the ancestral W, which in other systems has also occurred in discrete evolutionary strata (Zhou et al. 2014; Xu et al. 2019a; Gu et al. 2023).

TEs and the Formation of Neo-Sex Chromosomes

TEs are well-known catalysts of chromosomal rearrangements (Gray 2000), potentially facilitating the translocation of the long arm of Chr 5 to the ancestral PAR of the sex chromosomes regardless of whether the rearrangement was nonadaptive or beneficial. We characterized the repeat content across the entire neo-Z and neo-W and found that a helitron-like element was enriched in the ancestral PAR, and thus next to the fusion breakpoint, but not elsewhere in the neo-sex chromosomes of Blue-faced Honeyeater or in truncated Chr “5a.” However, as indicated by the wide phylogenetic distribution of this helitron-like element in the ancestral PAR of all tested species, which does not coincide with the origin of neo-sex chromosomes in honeyeaters, we conclude it is unlikely this element was the driving force behind neo-sex chromosome formation. In particular, the lack of this helitron-like element in regions homologous to ancestral Chr 5, either at the fusion breakpoint or otherwise, is another indication that it was likely not involved in ectopic recombination leading to neo-sex chromosomes in honeyeaters. It is certainly possible that a transposable element could have facilitated ectopic recombination and the initial fusion of the ancestral PAR and Chr 5, even though it may be difficult to determine a posteriori whether a particular element occurred within both the fusion breakpoint of ancestral Chr 5 and the ancestral PAR in the ancestral population—a necessary prerequisite for illegitimate pairing to occur. More chromosome-level assemblies and fine-scale surveys of TEs, especially including honeyeaters most closely related to the neo-sex chromosome clade, are needed to help test whether a TE-mediated rearrangement led to the formation of neo-sex chromosomes in honeyeaters as well as the novel Chr 5-1A fusion in the Noisy Miner.

Implications of Fusion With the Ancestral PAR and Formation of a New PAR

Which sex chromosome (Z or W) fused first to Chr 5 is equivocal given that fusion occurred on the PAR end, which in heteromorphic sex chromosomes is usually the only region with no sequence divergence between the sex chromosomes due to obligate meiotic recombination in the PAR to ensure proper segregation (Hinch et al. 2014; Bergman and Schierup 2022). The ancestral W fusing first is a reasonable hypothesis, particularly if it were prone to mispairing during meiosis due to a fragile and tiny PAR (Blackmon and Demuth 2015; Blackmon and Brandvain 2017). However, the question of which chromosome fused first is ultimately inconsequential because the added region would have been transferred to the other sex chromosome in a subsequent obligate meiotic crossover on the PAR (Jones and Franklin 2006; Barchi et al. 2008). When this double rearrangement swept to fixation in an ancestral population of honeyeaters, it is reasonable to imagine the same added haplotype would have been repeatedly recombined onto both ancestral sex chromosomes as individuals with fused chromosome(s) mated with those who did not yet possess fused neo-sex chromosomes (Fig. 4). In essence, we propose it was a cascading, concurrent formation of neo-Z and neo-W chromosomes in the ancestor of the neo-sex chromosome clade (Fig. 5).

The evolutionary driver causing the initial autosomal fusion with the ancestral sex chromosomes remains an outstanding question, with possible explanations including meiotic drive (Yoshida and Kitano 2012), TE-mediated mispairing and rearrangement (Gray 2000), rejuvenation of fragile PARs (Blackmon and Demuth 2015; Blackmon and Brandvain 2017), or autosomal genes with sex-related functions driving neo-sex chromosome formation to resolve sexual antagonism (van Doorn and Kirkpatrick 2007; Matsumoto and Kitano 2016). However, simulations show that fusions with the ancestral PAR are selected against when a sexually antagonistic locus is present on the autosome involved in the fusion (Wilhoit et al. 2024). This result occurs because obligate recombination in the PAR during meiosis would cause the fused region to recombine onto the other chromosome. Thus, an autosome with a sexually antagonistic locus would benefit from fusing to the non-PAR end of the sex chromosomes where female beneficial alleles could be permanently linked to the nonrecombining W, for example.

In the songbird group Sylvioidea, fusions to both the PAR and non-PAR end have been documented, depending on the autosome involved (Sigeman 2021). In larks (family Alaudidae), Chr 5 has also fused to the PAR end in the same place as honeyeaters (Burley et al. 2023). In larks, the fused, previously Chr 5, region has continued to freely recombine in some species, resulting in significant expansion of the PAR. For example, in the neo-sex chromosomes of Horned Larks (Eremophila alpestris), the fused region that was previously Chr 5 is freely recombining in the subspecies Eremophila alpestris flava from Sweden (Sigeman et al. 2021), but a portion of it has undergone RS in populations from the southwestern United States (Shakya et al. 2022). Thus, in the context of avian neo-sex chromosomes, RS in the recently fused portion, and thus expansion of regions in linkage with the ancestral W and mtDNA, may not immediately follow fusion to the ancestral PAR, and RS can occur independently in different species. In honeyeaters, however, we find that RS appears to have occurred essentially concurrently with fusion because we find support for a single origin of RS across a large region shared by all honeyeater species with neo-sex chromosomes. The large added stratum of RS included the ancestral PAR and consequently led to PAR turnover and the formation of a 17.4 Mb new PAR at the end of the neo-sex chromosomes. The terminal placement of PARs, as seen in mammals and birds (Otto et al. 2011; Xu et al. 2019b), is likely biologically constrained because an interstitial recombining PAR might cause deleterious recombination of ancestral sex-linked regions flanking it.

Our results bear on whether the new stratum of RS in honeyeater neo-sex chromosomes could be adaptive, as outlined by Olito and Abbott (2023) . The large size of the stratum involved in RS (44.6 Mb relative to the neo-W and 37.2 Mb relative to the neo-Z in Blue-faced Honeyeater) suggests an adaptive suppression of recombination, potentially from universally beneficial breakpoint effects rather than sexual antagonism, given the fact that Chr 5 fused with the ancestral PAR (Olito and Abbott 2023; Wilhoit et al. 2024). Analysis of changes in gene regulation, dosage, and function for the 790 + genes on the added region (ancestral Chr 5), while beyond the scope of this study, could also help decipher the evolutionary drivers and consequences of neo-sex chromosome formation and RS. Either way, our findings suggest that fusion with the ancestral PAR does not preclude large-scale and potentially adaptive RS immediately following, or concurrent with, fusion. Evolutionary forces causing the fusion and subsequent RS might differ, but this is difficult to deconstruct a posteriori, especially given the apparent signature of simultaneous fusion and RS.

In conclusion, our work illuminates the value of “chromosomics” and phylogenetic inference in understanding the evolution of avian neo-sex chromosomes and dissecting phylogenetic incongruence and patterns of RS. In addition, we show that tree inference and deduction is helpful in formulating and testing evolutionary hypotheses. Our characterization of neo-sex chromosomes in honeyeaters highlights the phylogenetic complexities and mounting instances of variable neo-sex chromosome systems that occur across the avian tree of life.

Cytogenetic Experiments

We developed cell cultures from five species within infraorder Meliphagides, including three honeyeaters, one thornbill, and one pardalote. Details on preparation of cell cultures can be found in Supplementary material online. Using the Blue-faced Honeyeater genome, we developed custom probes with Daicel Arbor Biosciences that mapped to either side of the hypothesized fusion point found in Burley et al. (2023; Fig. S1). We developed a probe for the ancestral PAR, which allowed us to identify the ancestral Z and W sex chromosomes in all species (Probe_2; labeled ATTO 594). This was important to test whether fusion involving both ancestral sex chromosomes had occurred. We developed two probes for Chr 5, one that was within the sex-linked region (Probe_1, labeled Alexa 488), and one within the remnant autosomal region (Probe_3; labeled ATTO 647N). We used these custom probes on all species, which are closely related enough—spanning ∼30 MY of evolution—that we expected the probes to map to all regions in all species. Divergence times in millions of years based on Marki et al. (2017) and retrieved from TimeTree 5 (Kumar et al. 2022) are as follows: P. striatus and A. chrysorrhoa (5.9 MYA); E. cyanotis and N. leucotis (14.3 MYA); M. melanocephala and E. cyanotis or N. leucotis (19.3 MYA); all honeyeaters and outgroups A. chrysorrhoa or P. striatus (27.4 MYA). Further details on FISH experiments can be found in Supplementary material online and Table S6. Information on probe locations and densities can be found in Table S7 and the probe sequences can be found in the associated FigShare repository.

De Novo Assembling and Polishing of New Genomes

To construct the genomes of the Noisy Miner (Manorina melanocephala) and two outgroup species, Striated Pardalote (Pardalotus striatus), and Yellow-rumped Thornbill (Acanthiza chrysorrhoa), we assembled promethION long-reads using Flye v. 2.8.1 (Kolmogorov et al. 2019). A manual read separation approach, as employed below for Blue-faced Honeyeater, was not necessary because we were assembling homogametic (ZZ) male genomes to avoid the issue of similar added-Z and added-W reads confusing the haploid assembly algorithm. Information on high molecular weight extractions and sequencing statistics can be found in Tables S8 and S9.

To improve upon the assembly of the Blue-faced Honeyeater genome, focusing particularly on the neo-W, we employed a manual read separation approach, following Burley et al. (2023), to remove Z-linked reads and assemble W-linked reads based on % identity. We merged the genome assembly containing the refined neo-W contigs with the refined assembly of the neo-Z from Burley et al. (2023). See Supplementary material online for details.

To polish our assemblies, we generated whole-genome resequencing data (Illumina 2 × 150 bp reads) from the same individuals used to create the long-read assemblies, aiming for 30 to 40 × coverage per individual. We polished all long-read assemblies with the program NextPolish (Hu et al. 2020). For the Blue-faced Honeyeater, our goal was to correct potential mis-assemblies or errors in the newly refined neo-Z and neo-W contigs with the higher quality Illumina reads. See Supplementary material online for further details.

Hi-C Library Preparation, Sequencing, and Assembly

To create a Hi-C–scaffolded assembly of the Blue-faced Honeyeater and Noisy Miner, we generated Hi-C data from the same individuals that were sequenced on promethION using the Proximo Hi-C Kit (Phase Genomics, Seattle, WA, USA). We generated Hi-C libraries in-house using brain and liver tissues preserved in RNAlater and stored long-term at −80 °C. We shipped the resulting libraries to Phase Genomics to QC and sequence them. In total, 157,666,772 read pairs were generated for Blue-faced Honeyeater, and 124,590,934 read pairs for Noisy Miner. We used the Arima Mapping pipeline to align the Hi-C reads to each genome, following Zhou et al. (2023). We ran YaHS to create contact matrices and scaffold the contigs together into chromosomes (Zhou et al. 2023). The resulting assembly statistics can be found in Table S10 and the resulting contact matrices can be found in Figs. S2 and S3.

Whole-genome Scans for Neo-Sex Chromosomes

We used the snakemake pipeline FindZX (Sigeman et al. 2022) to test for the presence of neo-sex chromosomes in the two outgroup species and nine species of honeyeaters representing major clades in the latest honeyeater phylogeny (Andersen et al. 2019), as detailed in results and Tables S1 and S3. The required input for FindZX is a closely related homogametic reference genome, and whole-genome resequencing data from one or more individuals per sex. FindZX tests for the presence of sex chromosomes and sex-linkage using two metrics that enable detection of both young and old sex chromosome systems. The first metric—sex differences in heterozygosity—is a common signature of young sex chromosome systems that are revealed when males and females are mapped to a homogametic reference genome. The heterogametic sex (e.g. ZW), which typically contains the sex-limited chromosome, will have elevated heterozygosity compared to the homogametic sex (ZZ), due to reads coming from the diverging, sex-limited chromosome (e.g. the W or Y), which can still map to the homogametic reference. The second metric—sex differences in read depth—is a classic genomic signature of old sex chromosome systems (Xu et al. 2019b). Information on the numbers of individuals per sex and species and coverage per sample can be found in Tables S1 and S2, and S3.

The reason we sequenced and compared two closely related species of Friarbirds in the same genus is because the female voucher specimen (MCZ:Orn:336106) was mislisted as a Little Friarbird in the database and corrected to Silver-crowned Friarbird after the manuscript had been analyzed and written (Table S1). A phylogenetic study of the mitochondrial locus ND2, retrieved from TimeTree 5 (Kumar et al. 2022), estimates the divergence between these species at 6.8 mya (Joseph et al. 2014). In Fig. 4, we refer to Silver-crowned Friarbird because it is the female sample that drives the patterns of sex differences in heterozygosity. The use of this voucher specimen, because it is in the same genus as the male Little Friarbird voucher specimen (MCZ:Orn:336117), does not change any of our findings or conclusions based on our phylogenetic analyses of RS and FindZX scans.

Phylogenetic Analyses of RS

To assess whether RS occurred once or multiple times among honeyeaters with neo-sex chromosomes, we employed a phylogenetic approach following Zhang et al. (2022) and Sigeman et al. (2021). Using resequencing data from both sexes mapped to the homogametic version of the Blue-faced Honeyeater genome (all chromosomes minus the neo-W), we phased W and Z gametologs across the neo-sex chromosomes of Blue-faced Honeyeater, White-eared Honeyeater, Little Friarbird, and Silver-crowned Friarbird, using a custom phasing script from (Sigeman et al. 2018). The phasing script takes as input an all sites vcf file containing a male and female sample and outputs W-linked and Z-linked sequences for all coding regions. See Supplementary material online for details. All sites were phased as W-linked and Z-linked by comparing all genotypes in the male and female sample. For example, sites that were homozygous for an alternate allele in the male (1/1) and heterozygous for an alternate in the female (0/1) were coded as the Z being homozygous variant and the W being the reference. This follows from the fact that the Z is fixed for the alternate allele in males, but since females are ZW, the alternate allele comes from the single Z chromosome. The W must therefore possess the reference in order for the female to have a heterozygous genotype of 0/1 when mapping all reads to a homogametic (Z only) genome. The phasing script cycles through all possible genotypes, including ambiguous genotypes, which are flagged and the sequence converted to “N.” The final phased W-linked and Z-linked sequences include invariant reference sites for alignment purposes. For honeyeater species where we only had male resequencing data and no genome assembly, namely Painted Honeyeater (Grantiella picta) and White-throated Honeyeater (Melithreptus albogularis), we employed a modified single sample phasing approach to include the Z-linked or autosomal haplotypes of those species in the alignment. To include outgroup species in our alignment, we used TOGA (Tool to Infer Orthologs from Genome Alignments; Kirilenko et al. (2023)) to gather orthologs from Chr 5 and Z in Noisy Miner, Helmeted Honeyeater, Striated Pardalote, and Yellow-rumped Thornbill for a total of 10 species and 12 sequences (including phased Z and W gametologs for Blue-faced Honeyeater and White-eared Honeyeater, Little Friarbird Z gametologs, and Silver-crowned Friarbird W gametologs) per alignment. We used a hypothesis testing framework to compare different scenarios of RS and monophyly versus paraphyly of the neo-sex chromosome clade. Specifically, using IQTree2 we calculated ELWs of a set of nine reference topologies (Fig. S4) to determine which RS scenario was most likely across 164 coding loci in the added portion of the neo-sex chromosomes and 6 coding loci in the ancestral PAR (Strimmer and Rambaut 2002; Minh et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2022). We also calculated ELWs to compare support for the species tree and mtDNA tree topology across 232 coding loci in the recombination-suppressed ancestral portion of the neo-sex chromosomes. Our question about the timing of RS was focused on the ancestral PAR and the added region, which is why we included loci with phased Z and W gametologs for those regions, but not the ancestral recombination-suppressed region. The names and alignment lengths of the coding loci used in these analyses can be found in Table S5. Further details on the pipeline for phasing gametologs, gathering orthologs, and constructing alignments can be found in Supplementary material online.

TE Annotation

To annotate the repeat content of our Blue-faced Honeyeater and Noisy Miner genomes, we ran RepeatModeler (Flynn et al. 2020) to generate a de novo repeat library of consensus sequences for each species and then ran RepeatMasker (Smit et al. 2013-2015) on each genome to classify and quantify all repetitive content based on each species-specific library.
