Development of an 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography-based visceral pleural invasion prediction index in peripheral subpleural non-small cell lung cancer ≤3 cm
Authors: He Zhang, Bei Lei, Lihua Wang, Xuan He, Shengguo Jia, Gang Huang, Liu Liu, Wenhui Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate prediction of the visceral pleural invasion (VPI) status in stage I peripheral non-small cell lung cancers (NSCLCs) may help patients benefit from surgery and chemotherapy strategies. Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG PET/CT) is a combination of CT and FDG PET that reflects not only the FDG PET-associated metabolic features but also the CT-associated anatomic features. The FDG PET/CT radiomics-based model has demonstrated greater accuracy in the diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment evaluation of tumors compared with those based on CT only, including in NSCLCs. This study aimed to develop an FDG PET/CT-based VPI prediction (VPIP) index for peripheral subpleural NSCLCs ≤3 cm to help design a more rational treatment strategy for small-size NSCLCs.MethodsThis retrospective study included 2,383 patients with peripheral subpleural NSCLCs ≤3 cm between August 2016 and February 2022 diagnosed at Shanghai Chest Hospital. The patients were randomly assigned to the modeling and validation cohorts in a 3:1 ratio. Further, 1,691 PET and 1,691 CT radiomics features of tumors were extracted using the Multi-Modality Radiomics software on the syngo.via workstation. The t-test combined with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression was used for feature selection. The regression coefficients were used to formulate the VPIP index. The predictive performance of the VPIP index in both cohorts was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and compared using the DeLong test.ResultsEventually, 6 PET and 10 CT features were selected to formulate the FDG PET/CT-based VPIP index. The predictive accuracy of the PET/CT-based VPIP index, with a threshold >−1.13 for VPI, was 83.4% and 83.7% in the modeling and validation cohorts, respectively. This accuracy was substantially higher than that based on PET alone (formulated by 15 PET radiomics features) or CT alone (formulated by 12 CT radiomics features) in both the modeling (AUC, 0.926 vs. 0.906 vs. 0.921) and validation cohorts (AUC, 0.918 vs. 0.892 vs. 0.905) (P=0.00–0.01).ConclusionsThe VPIP index formulated in this study based on FDG PET/CT in peripheral subpleural NSCLCs ≤3 cm may help develop more rational surgery strategies for small-size NSCLCs.Keywords: Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG PET/CT), non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), radiomics, visceral pleural invasion (VPI), visceral pleural invasion prediction index (VPIP index)

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for nearly 85% of lung cancer cases. Surgical resection is the preferred modality for early-stage NSCLCs, with stage 1 NSCLCs (T1 size ≤3 cm) benefiting the most from surgical resection (1). Visceral pleural invasion (VPI) is a vital risk factor associated with poor prognosis in patients with lung cancer; the presence of VPI upgrades stage T1 (size ≤3 cm) NSCLCs to stage T2a (size ≤3 cm with VPI) NSCLCs, decreasing the 5-year survival rates from 86% to 62–70% in surgically resected NSCLCs (2). Peripheral NSCLCs are more likely to have VPI compared with central NSCLCs, and adjuvant therapy may be recommended with or without surgery for stage T2a (size ≤3 cm with VPI) NSCLCs to improve patient outcomes (3-5). Certain clinical trials have even recommended sublobar resection in stage I peripheral NSCLCs without VPI to avoid unnecessary loss of pulmonary parenchyma (6-9). However, determining the VPI status in peripheral NSCLCs before surgery is challenging, especially for lesions adhering to the pleura, because anatomic lobectomy with mediastinal nodal staging is the standard treatment for stage I peripheral NSCLCs. Therefore, accurately predicting the VPI status for stage I peripheral NSCLCs might help avoid unnecessary loss of pulmonary parenchyma in those without VPI and allow the administration of chemotherapy in those with VPI.

Computed tomography (CT) is the most commonly used imaging modality for diagnosing and staging lung cancer. Studies have demonstrated that CT can predict VPI in NSCLCs, with prediction accuracies ranging from 62.7% to 72.3% (10-13). 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/CT (FDG PET/CT) is a combination of CT and FDG PET that reflects not only the FDG PET-associated metabolic features but also the CT-associated anatomic features. PET/CT provides more information regarding lesions and is more efficient in cancer preoperative staging than CT alone (14-16). In recent years, the emergence of “radiomics” and the advancements in machine learning have improved the effectiveness of the FDG PET/CT radiomics-based model in tumor analysis by enhancing the diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment evaluation compared with models based on CT only (17,18).

Considering the critical role of preoperative diagnosis of the VPI index in stage I peripheral NSCLCs and the strengths of FDG PET/CT in tumor staging, this study aimed to leverage FDG PET/CT radiomics for predicting the VPI index in such patients, specifically those with tumors ≤3 cm adhering to the pleura. Therefore, a reliable model was developed to predict the VPI index in peripheral subpleural stage I NSCLCs before surgery. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD+AI reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2024-2921/rc).

Methods

Patient selection

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This retrospective study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Shanghai Chest Hospital (approval No. IS24179), and individual consent for this study was waived due to its retrospective nature. The charts of 8,797 patients examined by FDG PET/CT scanning 1–3 weeks before surgery from August 2016 and February 2022 in Shanghai Chest Hospital were retrospectively reviewed to mitigate unavoidable selection bias. Eventually, 2,383 patients with resectable peripheral subpleural NSCLCs ≤3 cm in PL0–PL2 were included in the study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) peripheral lesions adhering to the pleura (with 0-mm distance between lesions and pleura) diagnosed on CT; (II) lesions examined by FDG PET/CT scanning 1–3 weeks before surgery; (III) tumor length (maximum diameter) ≤3 cm; and (IV) lesions pathologically diagnosed as NSCLC. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) pathologic subtypes other than NSCLC; (II) mismatched PET and CT findings due to unstable respiratory movement; (III) no peripheral subpleural NSCLCs; (IV) degree of invasion of the pleura not clearly defined; (V) tumor length >3.0 cm; (VI) lymph node metastasis diagnosed on PET/CT; (VII) distant metastasis diagnosed on PET/CT; (VIII) other metastasis diagnosed using other medical imaging modalities [e.g., CT or nuclear magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)]; and (IX) lesions with PL3 invasion, which upgrades T1 NSCLC to stage T3, causing poor prognosis compared with those with VPI (2). All patients underwent lobectomy following an FDG PET/CT examination. The pleural invasion in this study was classified as VPI based on postoperative pathologic results. Based on the PL staging, the recruited patients were categorized into negative (PL0) and positive (PL1–PL2) groups. The process of patient screening and grouping is depicted in Figure 1.

PET/CT scanning

All the patients were examined under the same scanning conditions using the same device (Biograph mCT-S PET/CT, Siemens, Shanghai, China.). A 64-slice spiral CT was used. FDG was supplied by Shanghai Atom Kexin Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The pH value was ~7.0, and the radiochemical purity was maintained at >95%. All the patients fasted without water for more than 6 h before the scanning, and their plasma glucose level was maintained at <7.8 mg/dL. All patients were injected with FDG at 5 MBq·kg−1±10% of body weight and then allowed to rest for 60 min. The parameters of the CT scan were set as follows: tube voltage = 120 kV and tube current adjusted using the CAREDOSE technology. The CT images were reconstructed to a 512×512 matrix corresponding to a 1-mm pixel size with 2-mm thickness and 0.98-mm in-plane spatial resolution to meet the spatial resolution of PET images and avoid misalignment during image fusion. The anisotropic voxel was 4.07×4.07×3.0 mm3. The PET scan was divided into 5 or 6 beds, and each bed was scanned for approximately 2 min. The CT data were used to attenuate corrected PET images, and Truex + time-of-flight (TOF) was used to reconstruct PET images. After regular PET and CT scans, a 1-mm breath-hold lung CT scan was added. CT images were reconstructed using a conventional algorithm, whereas PET images were reconstructed using an iterative method.

Image preprocessing

The volumes of interest (VOIs) surrounding the tumor profile were first semi-automatically segmented to identify the maximum cross-sectional area of the target lesion on the combined PET/CT images. Patients with mismatched PET and CT data due to respiratory movement were excluded to ensure the accuracy of VOIs. Further, two nuclear radiologists (L.L. and W.X. with more than 10 years of experience), blinded to the pathologic or radiologic information, reviewed the VOIs and manually adjusted them layer by layer along the lesion edges on the CT windows. Any disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Feature extraction

Radiomics feature extraction was performed on the Multi-Modality Radiomics software in the syngo.via workstation (version VB40; Siemens Healthineers, Munich, Germany). The voxel size of 1×1×1 mm3 was used for image isotropic resampling. The bin width was set at 25 Hounsfield units for CT images and 0.1 standard uptake value (SUV) for PET images during feature extraction based on previous studies (19,20). A total of 1,691 PET and 1,691 CT features were extracted, including gray-level dependence matrix (GLDM), neighboring gray tone difference matrix (NGTDM), gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM), gray-level run-length matrix (GLRLM), gray-level size zone matrix (GLSZM), first-order features, shape, and transform-based features (Table 1 and Figure S1). Advanced filters, including Laplacian of Gaussian edge enhancement and wavelet decompositions, were applied to extract additional transform-based features to reveal more details in the images.

Modeling and validation of the VPI prediction (VPIP) indexes

The original datasets were randomly divided into modeling and validation cohorts (training:validation =3:1) (21,22). The differences in clinical parameters between the two groups were negligible (Table 2, P>0.05). As a result, 2,662 radiomics features were identified as VPI-associated parameters in the modeling cohort based on their correlation with VPI using the t-test (P<0.05). Features with low variance among the 2,662 radiomics features were further excluded with variance threshold method. This process identified 711 PET and 709 CT features as remarkably associated with VPI status (variance value >0.05) (Figure S2). Finally, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used to achieve superior predictive performance, maximizing the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) while minimizing overfitting and enhancing the generalization ability of the radiomics model. In this study, the optimal alpha was selected by LASSO with tenfold cross-validation (23). As the lambda changed from 10−5 to 10−1, the number of variables entering into the model reduced and the absolute values of the coefficients of the variables declined to zero (Figures S3-S5). Lambda.min is the value of lambda that achieves the minimum mean cross-validated error. In contrast, lambda.1se is the largest value of lambda for which the cross-validated error remains within 1 standard error of the minimum. The purpose of dimensionality reduction is to strike a balance between accuracy and simplicity, demonstrating that a model with the smallest number of features can still enhance accuracy. To this end, we selected lambda.1se as the most suitable value. As a result, 12 CT (2 GLDM, 1 GLCM, 5 GLRLM, 2 GLSZM, 1 first-order feature, and 1 shape), 15 PET (2 GLDM, 4 GLCM, 2 GLRLM, 2 GLSZM, 2 NGTDM, 2 first-order features, and 1 shape), and 16 PET/CT features (6 PET features comprising 1 GLDM, 2 GLCM, 1 GLSZM, and 2 first-order features and 10 CT features comprising 2 GLDM, 1 GLCM, 4 GLRLM, 2 GLSZM, and 1 first-order feature) (Tables 3-5) were selected to build VPIP indexes (Figures S3-S5). The lambda value was 0.0414029, 0.03077635, and 0.03437342 in the PET/CT, PET, and CT models, respectively (Figures S3-S5). The regression coefficients were determined using a data visualization method and are depicted in Figures 2-4. The definitions of PET-, CT-, and PET/CT-based VPIP indexes are provided in Formulation of VPIP indexes section. The effectiveness of the PET-, CT-, and PET/CT-based VPIP indexes was evaluated and compared in the independent validation cohort using the optimal cutoff values of each index formulated through the modeling cohort.

Statistical analysis

An unpaired-sample 2-tailed t-test was used to determine the differences in FDG PET/CT radiomics features between the two patient groups with varied VPI statuses. The ROC curve analysis was used to assess the most suitable d of the VPIP index to predict VPI in stage I NSCLCs. This threshold value was determined based on the highest Youden index. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify the independent clinical features for predicting VPI. The differences between ROC curves were assessed using the DeLong test by comparing the AUC values between the two cohorts. The decision curve analysis was performed to calculate the net benefit rate of the predictive models. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA) and SPSS 19.0 software (SPSS, IL, USA). Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. *, P value <0.05 and **, P value <0.005 indicated statistically significant differences.

Results

Patient features

Of the 2,383 patients with untreated peripheral subpleural NSCLCs ≤3 cm who underwent R0 resection (Figure 1 and Table 2), 1,000 were men (median age, 63 years; age range, 27–89 years) and 1,383 were women (median age, 61 years; age range, 30–86 years). The most enrolled subtype was lung adenocarcinoma (2,255/2,383, 94.6%), where 1,778/2,383 (74.6%) NSCLCs were with PL0 invasion, 468/2,383 (19.6%) with PL1 invasion, and 137/2,383 (5.7%) with PL2 invasion. Although all patients with NSCLCs were clinically diagnosed to be in stage N0, 62/2,383 (2.6%) and 90/2,383 (3.7%) NSCLCs were postoperatively pathologically diagnosed to be in stage N1 and stage N2, respectively. Further, all enrolled patients were clinically diagnosed to be in stage M0.

Modeling and validation of PET-, CT-, and PET/CT-based VPIP indexes

In the modeling cohort, 15 most useful PET features were selected to formulate the PET-based VPIP index (Figure 2A). Patients with NSCLCs with VPI had a considerably higher PET VPIP index score compared with those with PL0 invasion (Figure 2B). The ROC curve revealed that the predictive sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) with high PET-based VPIP index (>−0.97) were 82.9%, 82.3%, 82.4%, 61.3%, and 93.4%, respectively, with the optimal cutoff value of −0.97 (Figure 2C,2D). In the validation cohort, patients with NSCLCs with VPI had a remarkably higher PET-based VPIP index score compared with those with PL0 invasion (Figure 2E). The predictive sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV, and NPV with PET-based VPIP index >−0.97 were 76.8%, 83.5%, 81.8%, 61.3%, and 91.4%, respectively (Figure 2F,2G), consistent with those observed in the modeling cohort.

In the modeling cohort, the 12 most useful CT features were selected using the LASSO regression model to formulate the CT-based VPIP index (Figure 3A). Patients with NSCLCs with VPI had a substantially higher CT-based VPIP index score compared with those with PL0 invasion (Figure 3B). The ROC curve revealed that the predictive sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV, and NPV with high CT-based VPIP index (>−1.27) were 90.9%, 77.7%, 81.0%, 58.0%, and 96.2%, respectively, with the optimal cutoff value of −1.27 (Figure 3C,3D). In the validation cohort, patients with NSCLCs with VPI had a considerably higher CT-based VPIP index score compared with those with PL0 invasion (Figure 3E). In contrast, the predictive sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV, and NPV with CT-based VPIP index >−1.27 were 81.4%, 79.3%, 79.8%, 57.2%, and 92.6%, respectively (Figure 3F,3G), consistent with those in the modeling cohort.

In the modeling cohort, the six most useful PET and 10 most useful CT features were selected to formulate the PET/CT-based VPIP index (Figure 4A). Patients with NSCLCs with VPI had a remarkably higher PET/CT-based VPIP index score compared with those with PL0 invasion (Figure 4B). The ROC curve revealed that the predictive sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV, and NPV with high CT-based VPIP index (>−1.13) were 88.4%, 81.7%, 83.4%, 62.1%, and 95.4%, respectively, with the optimal cutoff value of −1.13 (Figure 4C,4D). In the validation cohort (Figure 4E), patients with NSCLCs with VPI had substantially higher PET/CT-based VPIP index scores compared with those with PL0 invasion. The predictive sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV, and NPV with PET/CT-based VPIP index >−1.13 were 84.0%, 83.5%, 83.7%, 63.5%, and 93.9%, respectively (Figure 4F,4G), consistent with the values in the modeling cohort.

The efficacy of the aforementioned three VPIP indexes was then compared, with the PET/CT-based VPIP index demonstrating the highest accuracy in predicting VPI compared with PET-based (P<0.001 in the modeling cohort and P=0.001 in the validation cohort) or CT-based (P=0.04 in the modeling cohort and P=0.01 in the validation cohort) VPIP index in both the modeling (AUC of 0.926 vs. 0.906 vs. 0.921) and validation cohorts (AUC of 0.918 vs. 0.892 vs. 0.905) (Figure 5A-5D). In conclusion, we developed three VPIP indexes using PET, CT, and PET/CT radiomics features, among which the PET/CT-based VPIP index was the optimal index for predicting VPI.

Correlation of patient clinicopathologic features with VPI

Certain clinical features are also potential VPI predictors, so we investigated the correlation of age, sex, location, smoking history, and tumor length with VPI in all enrolled patients with NSCLCs in the modeling and validation cohorts, separately. The univariate logistic analysis revealed that age [in years; hazard ratio (HR) =1.01 and P=0.03 in the modeling cohort and HR =1.02 and P=0.04 in the validation cohort], smoking history (smoker vs. nonsmoker: HR =1.64 and P<0.001 in the modeling cohort and HR =1.65 and P=0.01 in the validation cohort), sex (male vs. female: HR =1.49 and P<0.001 in the modeling cohort and HR =1.74 and P=0.003 in the validation cohort), tumor length (in cm; HR =3.03 and P<0.001 in the modeling cohort and HR =2.46 and P<0.001 in the validation cohort), and the PET/CT-based VPIP index (high vs. low: HR =6.99 and P<0.001 in the modeling cohort and HR =6.90 and P<0.001 in the validation cohort) were associated with VPI in both modelings (Table 6) and validation cohorts (Table 7). However, the predictive potential of the aforementioned four features was negligible when adjusting for the PET/CT-based VPIP index. In contrast, the multivariate logistic regression analyses revealed that the PET/CT-based VPIP (high vs. low) index was the only independent predictor for VPI with an HR of 7.02 and 6.88 in the modeling and validation cohorts, respectively, when adjusting for clinical features (P<0.001) (Tables 6,7). In conclusion, we demonstrated the independent predictive potential of the PET/CT-based VPIP index.

Discussion

This study explored the potential of FDG PET/CT radiomics in predicting VPI in patients with peripheral subpleural stage I NSCLCs. We proved the superiority of the combined use of CT and PET radiomics over CT or PET radiomics features alone in predicting VPI. We also developed a VPIP index for VPI in small-size NSCLCs adhering to the pleura with a high predictive accuracy of approximately 83.5%, which is the highest using medical imaging techniques (e.g., CT or FDG PET/CT) in NSCLCs so far. These findings might help clinicians design more rational strategies for treating stage I NSCLCs, especially for those with suspicious VPI occurrence.

Studies have frequently reported the correlation of FDG PET metabolic radiomics features with tumor biologic behavior because of the close association between glucose metabolism and tumorigenesis (24,25). The potential of FDG PET in predicting VPI in lung cancer has also been investigated. Chen et al. revealed that the maximum standard uptake value (SUVmax) of FDG >5.5 helped predict VPI in lung cancer with a sensitivity of 100%, a specificity of 60%, a PPV of 68.18%, and an NPV of 100% in stage I lung adenocarcinomas in 65 patients (26). The present study used the largest sample size (2,383 patients) to develop and validate a PET-based VPIP index in patients with NSCLCs, demonstrating the highest accuracy (81.8%) and specificity (83.5%) in predicting VPI (26,27). This further supported the potential of PET in predicting VPI in peripheral subpleural stage I NSCLCs.

CT is a traditional medical imaging modality based on anatomy, which helps distinguish lung cancer with or without VPI through certain CT morphologic features (e.g., pleural indentation and pleural attachment). However, the accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of studies based on the morphologic features of CT images remained low, which were 31.6–83.7%, 11.0–100%, 27.4–98.0%, 5.5–78.7%, and 84.0–100%, respectively (11,28-31). A few studies using CT-based deep learning radiomics have demonstrated its potential in predicting VPI in lung cancer with higher efficacy than the morphologic features of CT images (11,28-30,32). Recently, a nomogram model based on six CT radiomics features effectively predicted VPI in 659 patients with lung adenocarcinoma with prediction accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of 0.83–0.84, 0.71–0.74, 0.88–0.89, 0.65–0.75, and 0.89–0.90, respectively (33). In this study, we amplified the patient size to 2,383 and enrolled NSCLC subtypes rather than lung adenocarcinoma (e.g., lung squamous cell cancer) for analysis, which is the largest sample size of NSCLCs so far investigating the correlation of CT radiomics with VPI. We combined 12 CT radiomics features and developed a CT-based VPIP index in small-size NSCLCs adhering to the pleura, which exhibited a prediction accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of 79.8%, 81.4%, 79.3%, 57.2%, and 92.6%, respectively, in the validation cohorts.

The combination of PET and CT improved the diagnostic efficacy of tumors, including NSCLCs (34,35). We had previously established several PET/CT combined models and successfully predicted molecular subtypes, lymph node metastasis, or pathologic subtypes of lung cancer (36-39). Cui et al. recently developed an FDG PET/CT radiomics-based VPI predictive model with relatively poor accuracy of lower than 80.0% (and 71.5% in the validation cohort) using 165 patients with solid lung adenocarcinoma (27). However, in this study, we developed a PET/CT-based VPIP index that successfully predicted VPI with a high accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of 83.7%, 84.0%, 83.5%, 63.5%, and 93.9%, respectively, in the validation cohort. Compared with the previously reported PET, CT, or PET/CT-based VPIP model (11,27-29,33) listed in Table 8, the PET/CT-based VPIP model developed in this study demonstrated the highest accuracy, NPV, and AUC.

Owing to the relatively low resolution of PET imaging, the efficacy of PET radiomics features needs to be enhanced and stabilized by combining them with structural radiomics features. For example, Qian et al. revealed that incorporating CT imaging radiomics features enhanced the prediction efficiency of the PET/CT radiomics model for MYCN amplification in pediatric neuroblastoma (40). This explains that the PET/CT-based VPIP index outperformed the PET- or CT-based VPIP index in predicting the VPI status of NSCLCs. Thus, our findings support the increasing potential of PET/CT in the NSCLC diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment evaluation with the rapid advancements in machine learning and radiomics. Our data may also provide a new reference for other researchers to design a biologic phenotype (e.g., tumor treatment-associated molecular subtypes, treatment response, or disease outcomes) evaluation model for various cancer types, including NSCLCs, in the future.

The radiomics features extracted from PET or CT images can be classified into the following categories: GLDM, NGTDM, GLCM, GLRLM, GLSZM, first-order features, shape, and transformed features. Detailed mathematic definitions and feature explanations have been thoroughly described in previous studies (41) (Table 1). The predictive role of CT or PET radiomics features has been reported in several cancer types, including lung cancer (42-45). The CT-based first-order feature skewness (or Δskewness) reported has demonstrated promising potential for detecting the various invasive degrees of lung adenocarcinoma (46). A CT-based large-dependence emphasis-based GLDM could discriminate lung cancer from tuberculosis (43). A CT-based GLCM predicted tumor margins in breast cancer (44). PET/CT-based NGTDM and SUVmax predicted malignancy in lung nodule diagnosis (45). As demonstrated earlier, the PET/CT score was not entirely dependent on the features contained in the PET and CT scores, and it revealed a remarkably strong VPI predictive potential in validation cohorts. This supports the superiority of PET/CT in evaluating lung cancer biologic subtypes over PET or CT alone because of the combination of tumor metabolic and anatomic characteristics.

Certain clinical features (e.g., distance between the nodule and the pleura, sex, and location) are also risk factors for VPI in NSCLCs, with the distance between the nodule and the pleura being identified as the most independent risk factor (47,48). Tumor invasion occurs with time, and the greater the distance between the lesion and the pleura, the less likely the pleural invasion. In this study, the NSCLCs adhered to the pleura with a high risk of VPI. As a result, for these NSCLCs, the correlations among clinical features were unremarkable, supporting the necessity of PET/CT radiomics in predicting VPI in these patients.

Currently, the VPI status of early-stage NSCLCs can only be diagnosed after surgery, preventing clinicians from developing a more rational neoadjuvant or operation planning before surgery. The PET/CT-based VPIP index developed in this study will provide valuable insights into VPI risks in early-stage NSCLC, helping clinicians formulate more rational preoperative or surgical plans. For patients at high risk of VPI with a PET/CT-based VPIP score >−1.13, anatomic lobectomy with mediastinal nodal staging is recommended to achieve radical treatment and reduce the risk of tumor recurrence after surgery. Further, several studies have also recommended adjuvant therapy after surgery for NSCLCs with VPI to improve patient outcomes (3,4,49). Therefore, for small-size peripheral NSCLCs at a high risk of VPI with a PET/CT-based VPIP score >−1.13, adjuvant therapy before surgery may help decrease the risk of postsurgical recurrences of NSCLCs. Conversely, for those at low risk of VPI with a PET/CT-based VPIP score ≤−1.13, sublobar resection may be recommended to avoid unnecessary surgical injury while preserving normal lung tissue and function, as preoperational adjuvant therapy may be unnecessary for their low VPI risk. Hence, our VPIP index may be crucial for designing treatment strategies for small-size peripheral NSCLCs before surgery for not only minimizing unnecessary surgical trauma but also developing more rational adjuvant chemotherapy strategies and therefore improving patient outcomes.

Harmonization and standardization of radiomics methodology are essential for reproducibility. Patients may present altered images after chemotherapy or radiotherapy, such as focal radiotracer uptake and SUV measurements (50). Therefore, we applied rigorous screening to eliminate treatment-related image effects. Large sample sizes will enable researchers to overcome certain heterogeneities inherent in clinical imaging (51). This study enrolled 2,383 patients with NSCLCs, ensuring sufficient data variability coverage. The VOIs were semi-Dally segmented, and further reviewed and corrected by two nuclear radiologists. To further reduce the variability, the images were resampled with a fixed 1×1×1 mm3 voxel size. Image intensities were discretized to enhance intensity range comparability across a cohort. A previous study demonstrated a substantial impact of respiratory status on the reproducibility of CT imaging features repeatedly collected in patients with lung cancer (52). In the future, we will further investigate the influence of respiratory status on the reproducibility of PET/CT radiomics in patients with lung cancer.

Using multiple imaging modalities and fusion techniques to extract complementary information can greatly improve the quality and usefulness of the images obtained (53). In this study, we combined CT images with PET images to build the radiomics model, enabling the extraction of complementary information from various aspects of the tumor. Radiomics frameworks allowed for the extraction of precise quantitative radiomics features (e.g., morphology and intensity profile) where or when included in prediction models, thereby enhancing model interpretability (54). Further, the features extracted from images included first-order statistics, shape and size, and various texture features, further reducing the bias in this study.

There are certain limitations in this study. First, the retrospective nature of this study might have introduced inevitable selection bias. Hence, a prospective design should be adopted in future studies to further improve the accuracy of PET/CT-based VPIP index in small-size NSCLCs. Second, the patient population was homogeneous, as all the data were collected from a single hospital in Shanghai, limiting the representation of diverse patient populations with peripheral subpleural NSCLCs. Moreover, no external validation of the predictive models was conducted using a separate patient cohort from another institution. Given the complex radiomics model and the very high AUCs reported, overfitting is a potential concern. Although a validation cohort helps mitigate this, overfitting should still be acknowledged as a potential limitation. The study sample lacked ethnic and demographic variability, affecting the universality of the model when applied to other patient populations (55). Therefore, attention should be paid when applying this outcome to various cohort studies. Also, external validation with more diverse patient populations from multiple centers is necessary to enhance the robustness and applicability. Nonetheless, this study enrolled the largest NSCLC sample to build a VPIP index. In future research, we will conduct a multicenter, prospective study and enroll NSCLCs larger than 3 cm to develop a more reliable VPIP index prior to lung cancer treatment. We will collaborate with other hospitals to validate their VPIP index externally, strengthening the model’s generalizability, and also use an independent test set in the future to further address overfitting. Additionally, we not only included both PET and CT modalities in the optimal model but also conducted validation and cross-validation to increase the robustness of the predictive model (54,56). We also used clinical features during model development. The images were acquired and reconstructed using the same method. The image of various parameters needs to eliminate the batch effect of data. In the future, more PET/CT images acquired and reconstructed using varied parameters should be analyzed to further validate the generalizability of our radiomics model. This study demonstrated that the multi-modality radiomics model improved prediction performance. With the development of more advanced radiomics approaches, various models established using various methods, such as machine learning and deep learning, should be compared and combined to determine the most suitable modeling approach.

Conclusions

We developed an FDG PET/CT radiomics-based VPIP index to predict pleural invasion in peripheral subpleural NSCLCs ≤3 cm. This approach may help develop more rational surgical strategies for treating patients with peripheral subpleural stage I NSCLCs.
