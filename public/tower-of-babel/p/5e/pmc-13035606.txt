Improved Source Localization of Auditory Evoked Fields using Reciprocal BEM-FMM
Authors: Derek A. Drumm, Guillermo Nuñez Ponasso, Alexander Linke, Sirshendu Ganguly, Abbie Wang, Gregory M. Noetscher, Burkhard Maess, Thomas R. Knösche, Jens Haueisen, Jeffrey David Lewine, Christopher C. Abbott, Sergey N. Makaroff, Zhi-De Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Precise localization of auditory evoked fields (AEFs) from magnetoencephalography (MEG) data is very important for the functional understanding of the auditory cortex in medicine and cognitive neuroscience. The numerical solution of the field equations in the human head using the boundary element method (BEM) is a powerful tool for achieving this. The spatial resolution of the BEM is crucial for the achievable accuracy of localized neural sources. However, in classical BEM (as implemented, e.g., in MNE-Python), very high resolutions are impractical due to the associated prohibitive computational effort. In contrast, our recently introduced reciprocal boundary element fast multipole method (reciprocal BEM-FMM) allows for hitherto unprecedented spatial resolution of forward models. In this work, we employ reciprocal BEM-FMM to construct high-resolution forward models to localize AEFs. Simulated AEFs were generated using a direct BEM-FMM approach on realistic 40-tissue Sim4Life segmentations. Comparative analyses from simulated data demonstrate that high-resolution BEM-FMM forward models yield statistically superior source estimates relative to the 3-layer BEM. We also compare BEM-FMM forward models with source dipole resolution varying from 25,000 to 3,200,000 sources, and find that resolutions above 200,000 sources are sufficient for achieving accurate, high-resolution source estimates. We therefore recommend using the high-resolution reciprocal BEM-FMM to utilize high spatial anatomical precision for the modeling of neural activity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10548-026-01190-x.

Introduction

The objective of source localization (or source estimation, source reconstruction) (Knösche and Haueisen 2022) is to estimate the location of neural activation from non-invasive recordings such as electroencephalography (EEG) or magnetoencephalography (MEG) (Hämäläinen et al. 1993). In both the EEG and MEG forward problem, the quasi-static approximation to Maxwell’s Equations is sufficient (Plonsey 1972; Bosetti et al. 2008). This implies that, upon prescribing a set of possible source locations, known as source space, any activation pattern which is a linear combination of activation in different locations of the source space will have, as sensor measurements, the corresponding linear combination of the measurements of the individual source space locations. Ultimately, this implies that all information relative to activation along the source space can be obtained from the lead-field matrix, which has size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M\times N$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M$$\end{document} is the number of sensors, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N$$\end{document} is the number of sources, and whose (i, j)-th entry is the measurement at sensor i elicited by source j. The calculation of the lead-field matrix given a head model—i.e. a collection of surface or volumetric meshes approximating the head geometry, together with a selection of conductivity values—and a source space is the forward modeling step of source localization.

Once the lead-field matrix is computed, the inverse step follows by producing estimates based on the lead-field matrix itself and no longer involves the forward solvers. The inverse problem has infinitely many solutions (Helmholtz 1853) so, typically, it is solved by means of a minimum norm estimation (MNE), an Occam’s razor approach aiming to give a “best explanation” given the measured data, in this case a solution with the smallest \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L^2$$\end{document} norm.

The most common approach to solve the forward step when using surface meshes is the boundary element method (BEM). When using volumetric meshes, the typical approach is the finite element method (FEM). Both BEM and FEM reduce to the computation of a forward system matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A$$\end{document}, which solves for the measurements \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x$$\end{document} given activation at a location \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b$$\end{document} by solving the linear system \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Ax=b$$\end{document}. The major disadvantage of BEM is that its system matrix is dense, so the solution of the linear system already becomes computationally infeasible when the number of elements in the head model is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}10,000. On the other hand the system matrix of FEM is sparse, which allows models involving millions of surface elements, however many more elements are needed in a volumetric mesh in order to capture fine geometric details.

More recently, the boundary-element fast multipole method (BEM-FMM) has appeared as an alternative to BEM for models involving surface meshes (Makarov et al. 2018, 2020). This method is based on the coupling of the fast multipole method (Greengard and Rokhlin 1987) with the charge-based formulation of the BEM (Barnard et al. 1967; Ponasso 2024). The major advantage of BEM-FMM is that it can compute solutions with surface element meshes involing several million elements, and since less elements are required on surface meshes to capture fine details, the resolution of the models that FEM can compute pales in comparison to BEM-FMM. A recent notable example of an application of BEM-FMM is the largest map to date of extracellular electric field distributions of samples of mouse visual cortex at the microscopic level (Qi et al. 2025).

In Ponasso (2025b) we introduced BEM-FMM to the problem of source localization by combining it with the reciprocal approach based on Lorentz’s reciprocity theorem. This approach is based on the computation of cortical basis functions by means of a fictitious current injected through each MEG sensor coil, similar to forward solutions in transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS). The use BEM-FMM allows us to avoid computing the dense system matrices of the traditional surface-potential BEM (Geselowitz 1967, 1970), and as a result our method enables source localization using multiple high resolution meshes. In Ponasso (2025b) we localized somatosensory evoked fields and obtained good results compared to the standard BEM as implemented by MNE-Python with 3-layer models (Gramfort et al. 2013, 2014).

The objective of this work is to investigate the performance of high-resolution models in contrast to lower resolution models in the context of auditory evoked fields, which are more challenging from a source reconstruction perspective than the somatosensory evoked fields: on the one hand, the binaural auditory stimulation elicits activation on both hemispheres, with two approximately symmetric sources at the far-apart left and right temporal lobes; on the other hand, the head anatomy near the temporal lobes is more complicated than near the parietal lobes, and it is harder to represent accurately using downsampled 3-layer head models. As a result, high-resolution models will be able to provide more accurate source localizations—this was already suggested in the error maps of Ponasso (2024).

Auditory evoked fields (AEFs) are magnetic signals elicited by auditory stimuli. They are localized in the auditory cortex, having components in the Heschl gyri (primary auditory cortex) and in the planum temporale as a secondary area (Pelizzone et al. 1987; Liégeois-Chauvel et al. 1994; Yoshiura et al. 1995; Godey et al. 2001; Henderson et al. 2023). There are three main components of auditory evoked fields: 1) Middle-latency components, e.g. P1m (< 50 ms) which originates in regions medial and lateral to the Heschl gyri; 2) N1m (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}100 ms), consistently localized in the lateral Heschl gyri and planum temporale; and 3) P2m (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}200 ms), which has been found to have origins near the N1m source locations although with much greater interindividual variability (Lütkenhöner and Steinsträter 1998).

The source localization of AEFs is relevant from an applications standpoint as they can be used clinically for presurgical mapping in the temporal lobe epilepsy (Godey et al. 2001), in the identification of functional reorganization in patients with lesions (Nakasato and Yoshimoto 2000), and as a biomarker for language and developmental disorders (Cardy et al. 2008; Park et al. 2022). The sources of AEF are also very important due to their correlations with mental health and psychological disorders (Kim 2021; Fitzgerald 2024; Dib et al. 2024). Given these important clinical contexts, it is essential to establish that BEM-FMM forward models provide accurate source estimates of AEFs.

In this study, we analyze the source localization of auditory evoked fields elicited by binaural auditory stimuli for a cohort of 7 patients with drug-resistant depression, whose data were provided by the authors of the study of Dib et al. (2024). We construct forward models using the standard 3-layer BEM implemented by MNE-Python and using reciprocal BEM-FMM on models of 7 tissues, which we refer to as the BEM and BEM-FMM forward models, respectively. We simulate noiseless N1m peaks of binaural auditory stimulation for each patient using a direct BEM-FMM approach on the Sim4Life 40-tissue segmentation by the IT’IS Foundation (Sim4life 2024, 2025). We compute source localization solutions from simulated data of varying levels of signal-to-noise ratio, and analyze the effects of inverse operator regularization on the accuracy of source estimates. We then compute solutions from experimental N1m peaks and draw comparisons between estimated sources using the BEM and BEM-FMM forward models. Additionally, we compare BEM-FMM forward models of varying dipole resolution, from 25,000 to 3.2 million sources, and on 3- and 4-layer head models.

By inspecting Eq. 5, one can see that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document} serves the role of improving the condition number (the ratio between the maximum and minimum singular values) of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{L}R\tilde{L}^{\textrm{T}}$$\end{document} by nudging the diagonal. Therefore, we may automatically determine a sufficient choice for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document} by iteratively ‘nudging’ the diagonal of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{L}R\tilde{L}^{\textrm{T}}$$\end{document} by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda I$$\end{document} until the condition number is within a tolerable size (e.g. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {cond}(\tilde{L}R\tilde{L}^{\textrm{T}})< 100$$\end{document}). This procedure removes zero eigenvalues from the matrix, and thus guarantees a numerically stable computation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{M}$$\end{document} (Belsley et al. 1980; Dennis and Schnabel 1996). We take this approach of regularization for all inverse solutions in this study unless stated otherwise.

It is worth noting that our approach here is akin to applying Tikhonov regularization (a.k.a. ridge regression) or truncated singular value decomposition (TSVD) to compute the whitened inverse operator \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{M}$$\end{document}. In Tikhonov regularization, one uses a technique such as cross-validation or the L-curve method to determine the optimal choice of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document}. In TSVD, the forward operator dimensions associated to near-zero singular values are projected off. Both of these approaches are concerned with improving the conditioning of the forward operator and have been used for MEG source localization (Golub et al. 1979; Gencer and Williamson 1998; Knösche and Haueisen 2022). The key distinction between these methods and our approach of automatic regularization is that we seek the numerically stable inversion of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{L}R\tilde{L}^{\textrm{T}}$$\end{document} without directly modifying the forward operator.

Auditory Response Data Processing

Data acquisition. Data were provided to us by the authors of Dib et al. (2024). These data were collected from 9 study participants with major depressive disorder. Out of these 9 participants (labeled N01– N09), two of them (N03 and N05) did not have MRI data available, so we restricted our study to the remaining 7. Participants were recruited from the University of New Mexico Mental Health Center to participate in a study focused on the loudness dependence of auditory evoked potentials (LDAEP) in pre- and post- electroconvulsive therapy (ECT). However, for this paper we only analyze the pre-treatment data.

T1-weighted structural MRI images were recorded using the 3-Tesla Siemens Trio MRI scanner at the Mind Research Network of Albuquerque, New Mexico. Raw MEG recordings were collected using the Elekta Neuromag VectorView 306, which possesses 102 magnetometers and 204 gradiometer pairs. Participants were stimulated with binaural auditory tones at a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2\,\hbox {kHz}$$\end{document} frequency, with each tone lasting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$50\,\hbox {ms}$$\end{document}. The stimulation was performed using tones of varying loudness; however, for this paper we only consider the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$95\,\hbox {dB}$$\end{document} tones. Further details of patient data acquisition are described in Dib et al. (2024).

MEG signal pre-processing. MEG data pre-processing steps for the Max Planck Institute pipeline were performed in MNE-Python 1.8.0 (Gramfort et al. 2013). Measurement data were run through a FIR low-pass filter with the upper pass-band edge at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$85\,\hbox {Hz}$$\end{document}, processed with Maxwell filtering—spatiotemporal signal space separation (tSSS), see Taulu and Simola (2006)—and finally high-pass filtered with a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\,\hbox {Hz}$$\end{document} lower pass-band edge. Maxwell filtering was done with the default recommended expansion settings of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l_{in} = 8$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l_{out} = 3$$\end{document}, a spatiotemporal buffer duration of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20\,\hbox {s}$$\end{document}, and a spatiotemporal correlation setting of 0.9. The applicability of the default expansion settings was evaluated for a single patient’s data and found adequate. N1m peaks for each participant were determined at a mean latency of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$105\,\hbox {ms}\pm 20\,\hbox {ms}$$\end{document}.

Reciprocal BEM-FMM forward models of varying dipole resolution. Dipole resolution in BEM models is limited due to the computational constraints of computing large system matrices and computing the lead-field matrix directly. As the reciprocal BEM-FMM method does not posses these constraints, we are better suited to examine the effects of extremely high dipole resolution on localized sources. Therefore, we compute BEM-FMM forward models using resolutions of approximately 25, 50, 100, 200, 800, and 3200 thousand dipoles.

For each BEM-FMM forward model, we take as a source space the SimNIBS WM mesh (which initially possesses approximately 200,000 triangles for each participant) with dipoles normally oriented to the WM. The models of various resolution are achieved by decimating and refining this initial WM mesh. Mesh decimation was performed in MeshLab (Cignoni et al. 2008) using the Quadric Edge Collapse Decimation at varying levels. Mesh refinement was performed using a simple barycentric triangular subdivision routine.

Additionally, on models with 200, 800, and 3200 thousand dipoles, we apply zero, one, or two iterations of Laplacian smoothing (Lalancette 2018) on the dipole orientations, to examine effects of a smoother dipole vector field on these extremely high resolution models. BEM forward models. Because MNE-Python is limited in its capacity to build high resolution forward models, we cannot draw precise comparisons from these forward models with more than approximately 20,000 dipoles. Despite this, it is useful to analyze the differences in localized sources computed from various forward model methodologies.

We computed BEM forward models using MNE-Python with approximately 20,000 dipoles (the upper limit of the software’s capabilities). The source spaces for the BEM models are found via the ico-5 option of MNE-Python, which creates a uniform sampling of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document} 10242 sources (for each hemisphere) on the interface between the FreeSurfer-segmented WM and GM meshes. The resulting LFMs were exported from MNE-Python into our in-house source localization software.

Head model coregistration. Participant head-to-MEG coregistration was performed on the SimNIBS scalp mesh. First, the sensor digitization points were manually transformed such that the anatomical points (left/right preauricular and nasion) were in the approximately correct locations. Then an iterative closest points (ICP) algorithm (Bergström and Edlund 2014) is applied so that the digitization points are better mapped to the scalp. Final small manual adjustments were made to the transformation as needed to ensure visually correct coregistration. The coregistration transformation obtained was then used for each of the BEM and BEM-FMM forward model computations. Errors in the digitization and coregistration can propagate into the localized sources; we assume that these errors are negligible when compared to model errors or signal measurement errors. For detailed analysis of how coregistration errors can affect source localization, please see (Troebinger et al. 2013; Sonntag et al. 2018; Chella et al. 2019).

Generation of Synthetic Data for the Evoked N1m Peak

Generation of noiseless data. We simulated noiseless data for the N1m peak of binaural auditory stimulation. This is similar to the simulation of evoked somatosensory data described in Ponasso (2025b), with the difference that to model activation on the primary auditory cortex we used multiple dipoles distributed over a patch of the region rather than a single dipole. We generate this simulated data in two manners, the first using MNE-Python’s SourceSimulator functionality, and the second using direct BEM-FMM on the 40-tissue Sim4Life head models.

To generate the BEM-FMM simulated data, we manually segmented the Heschl gyri of each participant from their T1-weighed MRI data by selecting nearby points in MRI coordinates. The nearest WM mesh triangles were tagged, and a dipole was placed directly above each of the tagged triangle centers between the WM and GM interfaces. The orientations of each of these dipoles were chosen to be the average normal to the nearest GM triangles. Once the dipoles were placed, we used the method of BEM-FMM with b-refinement described in Wartman et al. (2025), to calculate the electric potential on each tissue interface; see also Ponasso (2024). Once the potential is calculated, we computed the B-field on 64 observation points for each magnetometer coil and each individual coil the gradiometer pairs. With this, the integral of the normal component of the B-field is computed to generate the sensor outputs of the magnetometers, and in the case of gradiometers we oriented the unit normals in opposite ways for each gradiometer coil and divided the integral by the distance separating the coils of each gradiometer pair, cf. Knösche and Haueisen (2022). This process of simulation of the evoked N1m is illustrated in Fig. 1.

The sensor outputs were simulated independently for the dipoles on the Heschl gyri of each hemisphere. Source Reconstruction

All source localization solutions shown in this paper were found using dSPM. For source localization of synthetic N1m peaks, the noise covariance was set to be the identity matrix. For localization using the experimental data, we created a noise covariance matrix from the pre-baseline signals at the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[-200,-5]\,\hbox {ms}$$\end{document} window.

To visualize reconstructed sources from BEM and BEM-FMM forward models in a same mesh, we mapped the BEM source spaces to the SimNIBS headreco mesh using a rotation and translation provided by the ICP algorithm. For each source (i.e. triangle center) on the headreco WM mesh, we computed its source strength by averaging the estimated source strengths of those sources of the BEM source models within a sphere of radius \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10\,\hbox {mm}$$\end{document} from the triangle center.

Model Analysis Using ROC Curves

To analyze model performance, we compute the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for each of the source localization solutions using the simulated data. The ROC curve is a useful statistical tool for evaluating the performance of a binary classification model (Hastie et al. 2009). The ROC curve is computed for source localization by treating the source strengths as binary classification variables across varying activation thresholds (Knösche and Haueisen 2022). Specifically, we define each of the triangle faces denoting the Heschl gyrus found in Section Generation of Synthetic Data for the Evoked N1m Peak as true dipoles, and all other triangle faces as false dipoles. Given the normalized dSPM solution of source localization, consider a reconstructed source or normalized strength \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s$$\end{document} and a threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T$$\end{document} from 0 to 1. If the source lies on the tagged Heschl gyrus, we call the source a true positive (TP) if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s> T$$\end{document}, a false negative (FN) if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s < T$$\end{document}. Similarly, if the source does not lie on the Heschl gyrus, we call the source a false positive (FP) if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s> T$$\end{document}, a true negative (TN) if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s < T$$\end{document}. The ROC curve is defined as the curve plotting the false positive rate, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FP/(FP + TN)$$\end{document}, versus the true positive rate, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$TP/(TP + FN)$$\end{document}, across all thresholds \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T$$\end{document}.

The area under the ROC curve (AUC) can be used as a scalar metric to evaluate the performance of the solutions. In particular, if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$AUC=1$$\end{document}, then source localization perfectly captures the true dipoles tagged on the Heschl gyrus. If \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$AUC\le 0.5$$\end{document}, then the source localization is no better than a random chance solution.

We perform ROC-AUC analysis on the BEM and BEM-FMM models for every participant using the noiseless simulated data described in Section Generation of Synthetic Data for the Evoked N1m Peak. The smoothing that is done on the source estimations from BEM forward models may negatively bias the ROC curves. Thus, when computing ROC curves for these solutions, we only consider the sparse BEM model solution and define the true dipoles as those on the sparse white matter mesh that are within \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10\,\hbox {mm}$$\end{document} of the triangle centers of the tagged Heschl gyrus. The TP, FN, FP, and TN sources are then computed in the same manner as described above.

To assess the validity of the automatic regularization procedure introduced in Section 2.2, we conducted ROC AUC analyses on localized sources derived from noiseless data across varying regularization parameters. Particularly, we vary \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1/\lambda )$$\end{document} from 0.1 to 100, 000, with the increase in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1/\lambda )$$\end{document} corresponding to a reduction in regularization (see Eq. 4). The resulting AUC values were then compared against those obtained using the automatic regularization procedure via box plots for each parameter choice, aggregated across participants. We performed this analysis using the BEM forward models, as well as the BEM-FMM forward models on 20 and 200 thousand sources. In this case, the 20 thousand source model was obtained by selecting the source dipoles on the SimNIBS WM mesh nearest to the 20 thousand dipoles obtained from MNE-Python using k-nearest neighbors, and truncating the 200 thousand dipole LFMs.

Additionally, we evaluate the robustness of the BEM-FMM forward models to noise by performing ROC-AUC analyses with the simulated data across varying SNR levels described in Section Generation of Synthetic Data for the Evoked N1m Peak. Particularly, for each tested SNR, we compute source localizations for each of the 1000 generated noisy signals, repeated for every participant. In each of these scenarios, the automatic regularization method described in Section Inverse Solutions Using Minimum Norm Estimation is employed. This analysis is not applied to the BEM forward models, as the noise robustness of these low resolution, 3-layer models has been well studied (Goldenholz et al. 2009).

Quantifying Solution Focality Via Dispersion

We can quantify the focality of the source estimation solutions by obtaining a measurement of solution dispersion. This is accomplished by constructing a probability density function from the normalized source estimates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_i$$\end{document}. Specifically, we compute \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{s}_i = \alpha s_i$$\end{document} where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} is a constant so that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sum \hat{s}_i = 1$$\end{document}. In this manner, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{s}$$\end{document} is a probability density function with Cartesian coordinates treated as random variables. We then measure the dispersion of the estimated sources by computing the standard deviation of the distribution across X, Y, and Z coordinates. A smaller standard deviation, and thus a smaller dispersion, indicates a more focal solution and vice versa. Of course, this measurement depends on anatomical structure, and therefore cannot be used for precise comparison across participants. But we may make use of this to compare the focality of different solutions on the same participant.

Each white matter is aligned with the Cartesian axis, with the lateral, anterior-posterior, and superior-inferior axes corresponding to the X, Y, and Z axes, respectively. The binaural stimulation of the experimental data is therefore expected to yield symmetric activation across the X direction (i.e. across the left and right hemispheres). It follows that the distribution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{s}$$\end{document} would be bimodal in the X direction. In this case, the standard deviation would misrepresent the focality of the source estimates. Because of this, we construct two distributions in the same manner described above, each corresponding to the left and right hemispheres. Focality of the source estimates can then be measured across each hemisphere separately.

Discussion

Source estimation of simulated data. Noiseless simulated data was recovered more accurately with BEM-FMM forward models over the BEM forward models, as depicted in Fig. 2 and Table 3, with participant averaged AUC yielding a 5 point improvement with magnetometer data, and an 8 point improvement with gradiometer data. In Fig. 3a, we observe that the variability of the AUC values between participants is smaller when using the BEM-FMM forward models. We also note how the automatic regularization procedure yields quite good AUC values across all participants and models. This suggests that the condition number threshold described in Section Inverse Solutions Using Minimum Norm Estimation is indeed sufficient for numerically stable and accurate source localization.

In Fig. 3a, we also see a trend in the recovered AUC as regularization is varied. When the models are over-regularized (i.e. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1/\lambda )$$\end{document} is decreased) we see a reduction in the achieved AUC values, with this reduction being slightly more pronounced in the BEM forward models. As expected for simulated MEG signals with no noise, when regularization is reduced (i.e. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1/\lambda )$$\end{document} is increased), the recovered AUC values increase and stabilize when using the BEM-FMM forward models, with slight improvements when using BEM-FMM forward models of 200,000 sources over those of 20,000 sources. However, perhaps unexpected, we see the opposite trend occur when using the BEM forward models. This may be in part due to the poor conditioning of the BEM forward operators as compared to the BEM-FMM forward operators, as shown in Fig. 3b. We see that the condition numbers of BEM-FMM forward models are almost an order of magnitude smaller than those of the BEM forward models, on average, with the BEM-FMM models of 200,000 sources being slightly better conditioned than the models of 20,000 sources. In Fig. 3a, we do see a slight decrease in the recovered AUC from BEM-FMM forward models when using gradiometer signals, particularly for the models on 20,000 sources. When comparing to the trend from the magnetometer signals, this difference may also be explained by the conditioning of the forward operator in Fig. 3b. Here, it is clear that the condition numbers of the magnetometer forward models are much better than those of the gradiometer forward models, which also holds true for each model type. Note that the conditioning of the inverse operator is likely not the source of this trend in recovered AUC, as depicted by Fig. 3b. We see that, although the inverse operators from the BEM-FMM models are generally much better conditioned than those from the BEM models, there is a trend in the condition number as regularization is decreased, in which each model type seem to follow.

Additionally, we see in Fig. 4 that the BEM-FMM forward models achieve high AUC values across a wide range of regularization. From these plots, we see that the magnetometer forward models recover AUC more consistently than the gradiometer forward models across varying model resolutions. This may again be a result of the generally improved condition number for magnetometer forward models. Further, we see that models with 200,000 sources perform slightly better than the other models. Notably, the AUC recovery of the 3- and 4-tissue models closely match those of the 7-tissue models. This is perhaps expected, as MEG measurements are relatively insensitive to additional anatomical detail in the head model (Lau et al. 2012; Piastra et al. 2020). (We might expect different behavior for EEG signals, where signal propagation is more strongly affected by tissue geometry and conductivity contrasts Lau et al. 2012; Vorwerk et al. 2019.)

The improved performance of the BEM-FMM forward models is additionally seen through the localization of noisy simulated data, as demonstrated by Table 4. Within, we see an average achieved AUC of 0.85 for both the magnetometer and gradiometer signals of highest tested noise (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {SNR}=1.5$$\end{document}), demonstrating the robustness of BEM-FMM forward models for localizing signals within a tolerable amount of noise.

In Fig. 2 (as well as Fig. 4 in the Supplemental Figures), we note a visual improvement in the focality of the recovered solutions using the BEM-FMM forward models, with the localized sources from BEM forward models appearing more spread around the temporal lobe. The estimated sources from the BEM-FMM forward models are distributed almost exclusively along the Heschl gyri, with peaks occurring on one or both of these gyri; whether peaks occur on one or both gyri is mostly explained by head model coregistration to the MEG sensors. The source reconstructions from BEM forward models show instead spurious regions of activation in cortical regions away from the Heschl gyri, and a much less focal distribution; nevertheless the peaks remain located near the Heschl gyri with an identical pattern of peak asymmetry observed in the BEM-FMM reconstructions. The distinction between solutions from the two models likely arises from three factors: first, the improved forward operator conditioning of BEM-FMM forward models over those of BEM; second, differences in dipole resolution between the BEM and BEM-FMM forward models; and third, the more realistic tissue models employed by reciprocal BEM-FMM compared to the simplified tissue models used in MNE-Python.

These results suggest the clear influence of dipole resolution and number of tissues on source localization accuracy. However, more mysterious is the nature of what underpins the conditioning of the forward operators. Figs. 3 and 4 show that the BEM-FMM forward models are much less sensitive to smaller regularization parameters than the BEM forward models. Although this could be in part due to the number of sources and tissue layers, as indicated by Fig. 4, Fig. 3b suggests there may be some additional factor influencing the worse conditioning of the BEM forward operators. One possibility is that the BEM source space created by MNE-Python has less smooth normal vectors than the BEM-FMM source space. This may be unlikely, however, since the source space created in MNE-Python is sampled from the FreeSurfer meshes, which have quite smooth normals. Another possibility is the propagation of ‘hidden’ numerical errors in the creation of the BEM forward operator. Using BEM requires a dense kernel matrix, and there are a variety of approaches that can be taken in its construction (Mosher et al. 1999); we are unaware of the precise details of how the kernel is built in MNE-Python. In contrast, charge based BEM-FMM is a matrix-less method, the parameters of which we use are shown in Table 2. There could be a propagation of numerical errors in both methods, but the errors from BEM may be more pronounced. Exploring the underlying cause of the discrepancy shown in Fig. 3b would be a valuable direction for future investigation.

Given that real experimental data will be more similar to the high-resolution forward models, these results indicate that the BEM-FMM forward models will have better performance than the BEM forward models on experimental data. This is confirmed in the following subsection.

Source estimation of experimental data. The estimated sources recovered by the BEM and BEM-FMM forward models seen in Fig. 7 (as well as Fig. 4 in the Supplemental Figures) show very focal activation peaks on the Heschl gyri across all participants, as expected from a binaural auditory evoked N1m peak. Solutions appear to have similar focality across forward models, with BEM-FMM forward models yielding source estimates which encompass more of the Heschl gyri when compared to those of the BEM models, particularly for participants N01, N04, N06, N08, and N09. This larger coverage of the Heschl gryus is particularly easy to see in participant N01 of Fig. 5, where we see a strong activation spanning the full extent of the left Heshl gyrus, with minor activation on the right Heschl gyrus and left Heschl sulcus. By contrast, source estimates from BEM forward models on participant N07 seem more focal, and occupies a partial extent of the right Heschl gyri, with strong activation in the right Heschl sulcus. These focality observations are justified by the dispersion measurements from Table 5, where within we see that the localized sources from BEM and BEM-FMM forward models achieve similar levels of dispersion across all participants, with the BEM solutions yielding slightly more focal solutions.

In Fig. 8a, we see that the solutions from BEM-FMM forward models are localized in similar regions in both the 25 thousand and 3.2 million dipole models, as expected. We see that the 25 thousand dipole model strongly recovers sources on the medial portion of the Heschl gyrus, whereas the 3.2 million dipole model most strongly yields sources along the full extent of the Heschl gyrus. In Fig. 8b, we see how the solutions from models of varying resolution compare to the most refined and smoothed 3.2 million dipole model depicted in Fig. 8a. We see that solutions from all models are highly correlated with the solution on the reference model, with a correlation greater than 91%. This correlation is greatly increased for models of at least 200,000 dipoles, with a correlation greater than 98%. We also see the relative norm of solutions decrease substantially with increasing dipole resolution, from approximately 35% with the 25,000 dipole model, down to 15% with the 200,000 dipole model. The trend observed here indicates that BEM-FMM forward models with a resolution of 200 800 thousand dipoles can adequately approximate the solutions obtained from extremely high resolution models, at least in the context of evoked auditory signals. Interestingly, no clear trend emerges in relative norm percentage or correlation as a function of Laplacian smoothing steps. This may be due to our restricting dipoles orientations normal to the WM surface; perhaps the inclusion of arbitrarily oriented dipoles may reveal a more systematic trend.

In Fig. 9, we see that the relative percent difference between the 3- and 4-layer models against the 7-tissue model is quite small: smaller than 0.1% for both model types. This is expected, as the addition of the CSF (from 3 to 4 layers) and the eyes and ventricles (4 layer to 7 tissues) would have minimal impact on the localization MEG sources (Ramon et al. 2006; Birot et al. 2014), especially for evoked auditory signals.

In the comparative source reconstruction results of (Ponasso 2025b) between our BEM-FMM forward models and the BEM forward models of MNE-Python, our methods performed only marginally better. However, for the auditory data that we considered here, our method provides a significant improvement in localization accuracy. A possible explanation for this is that the geometry of the head near the central sulcus can be sufficiently approximated by a sphere or by a simplified classical BEM model with low-resolution shells. On the other hand, the temporal lobes cannot be accurately described with simpler models. Another possible explanation for this is that the quality of the evoked data in (Ponasso 2025b) allowed for simpler low resolution models to perform well. Conversely, the experimental auditory data used here was less than ideal—it posed a fair challenge to process and, even with sufficient filtering, it still seemed subject to moderate noise. With this in mind, we find the accurate and focal source localizations obtained from BEM-FMM forward models to be quite impressive.

With these observations, we find that head models with 200,000 source dipoles appear sufficient for localization of AEFs, with Laplacian smoothing used for visualization. Future work could assess the fit of signals from cortical patches of varying size and region, comparing high- and low-resolution models. Another interesting direction of future study is the evaluation of high-resolution multi-layer models for EEG source localization. Since EEG is more sensitive to conductivity changes in the head, this modality may benefit more from highly-realistic models. Preliminary results on EEG source localization employing highly-realistic models can be found in Ponasso (2025a)

Implications of Applying Reciprocal BEM-FMM to Auditory Source Localization. Accurate auditory source localization is critical for both clinical and research applications, as AEFs provide non-invasive markers of auditory cortical function. Validation of source localization through reciprocal BEM-FMM across varying physiological contexts ensures that high-resolution forward models can be applied reliably in translational neuroscience. For instance, in regards to the data studied in this work, analysis of AEFs was critical for determining whether LDAEP serves as a valid biomarker for major depressive disorder. Typically, analysis of loudness dependence was more common as an EEG task; application of MEG source estimation to investigate this phenomenon is relatively recent (Dib et al. 2024). In addition to psychiatric applications, AEF localization has been crucial for auditory cortical response in patients with auditory pathway lesions and neurodevelopmental disorders. AEF localization also plays an important role in pre-surgical mapping of the auditory cortex, particularly within the left temporal lobe, given its close proximity to language-processing regions (Shvarts et al. 2020). By enabling the computation of high-resolution forward models on realistic tissue models, reciprocal BEM-FMM can support further clinical and neurological research through the precise localization of auditory signals. These models could facilitate a more detailed understanding of the functional organization of the auditory cortex, and could extend to mapping across the entire cerebral cortex (Matsubara et al. 2024; Sanchez-Bornot et al. 2024).

Conclusions

Our results indicate that high-resolution BEM-FMM forward models including cortical layers significantly improve the accuracy of source estimates from AEFs compared to those of the 3-layer BEM models utilized by MNE-Python. All of our source estimations using high-resolution models indicate good quality of source localizations, with focal peaks of activation found on the Heschl gyri of each participant. This performance held true across various types of data, both simulated and experimental. Using simulated AEFs from the realistic 40-tissue Sim4Life segmentation and ROC measures, we find statistically superior results to the 3-layer BEM, with over a 5 point increase in achieved AUC from localized sources. Results from experimental data show that BEM-FMM forward models localize sources which cover more of the extent of the Heschl gyri when compared to those of the BEM forward models, while maintaining comparable solution focality. Comparison of BEM-FMM forward models of varying dipole resolution indicate that 200,000 source dipoles is sufficient for obtaining accurate, high-resolution source estimates. The high resolution source space and accurate source estimates achieved by BEM-FMM forward models indicates that our methodology could improve the applications of source localization in clinical settings, particularly in the localization of auditory evoked fields.
