Clinical evaluation of a modified minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion approach in single-level lumbar spine revision: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Qing-Lin Luo, Xiao-Xing Wang, Xin-Zhi Li, Wei Huang, Bo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to evaluate the clinical outcomes and safety of a modified minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion approach incorporating a quadrant channel and medially shifted working corridor in patients undergoing single-level lumbar spine revision surgery. This retrospective cohort included 50 patients who underwent single-level lumbar revision surgery at the Department of Orthopedics, Affiliated Renhe Hospital of China Three Gorges University, between January 2020 and December 2022. All patients had previously undergone percutaneous endoscopic transforaminal discectomy and subsequently developed recurrent lumbar disc herniation at the same spinal segment. Surgical parameters assessed included operative time, intraoperative blood loss, and postoperative drainage volume. Clinical outcomes were measured using the Visual Analog Scale and the Oswestry Disability Index. Interbody fusion was evaluated using thin-slice lumbar computed tomography at the final follow-up. The mean operative duration was 104 ± 12.1 minutes. The average intraoperative blood loss measured 316 ± 12.6 mL, with a postoperative drainage volume of 132 ± 8.3 mL. One patient developed transient ipsilateral nerve root palsy during the first postoperative week, and delayed wound healing was observed in 3 patients. Visual Analog Scale scores decreased significantly, from a preoperative mean of 8.09 ± 0.30 to 2.18 ± 0.23 at 4 weeks postoperatively, and further to 0.95 ± 0.16 at the final follow-up (P < .001). Oswestry Disability Index scores improved from 38.75 ± 1.53 preoperatively to 15.84 ± 0.58 at 4 weeks and 5.57 ± 0.99 at the final follow-up (P < .001). Successful interbody fusion was confirmed in all cases by the final follow-up. The modified minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion technique, employing a quadrant channel and a medially adjusted working corridor, demonstrated satisfactory clinical outcomes and an acceptable complication profile in patients undergoing single-level lumbar revision procedures.

1. Introduction

Advancements in minimally invasive spinal techniques have facilitated the widespread adoption of percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy (PELD) as a standard intervention for managing uncomplicated lumbar disc herniation (LDH). PELD has increasingly supplanted traditional open discectomy due to its advantages, including reduced surgical trauma, decreased intraoperative blood loss, and accelerated postoperative recovery.[1,2] However, a subset of patients experience recurrent or worsening symptoms following PELD. Common etiologies include recurrent herniation of the nucleus pulposus, progressive intervertebral disc degeneration, secondary spinal instability, and development of spinal canal stenosis. In such cases, conservative treatment is frequently insufficient, necessitating revision surgery.[3,4]

Revision lumbar spine procedures are inherently more complex than primary surgeries.[5] Contributing factors include altered anatomical landmarks, postoperative scarring, neovascularization with increased intraoperative bleeding, and dense adhesions involving the dural sac and adjacent nerve roots. These factors heighten the risk of iatrogenic injury, including dural tears and nerve root damage.

Minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (MIS-TLIF), originally described by Foley et al,[6] has been widely adopted in revision lumbar spine surgery and has demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes. This technique utilizes sequential dilation of the paraspinal musculature to access the affected segment via a quadrant channel, thereby preserving the posterior ligamentous complex and avoiding previous surgical incisions. Under direct visualization, resection of the inferior articular process and part of the superior articular process enlarges the intervertebral foramen, facilitating exposure of both the superior exiting and inferior traversing nerve roots. This permits effective removal of pathological disc material, decompression of the ventral dural sac, and lumbar interbody fusion, all with minimal nerve manipulation. Compared with conventional revision methods, MIS-TLIF is associated with reduced intraoperative bleeding and a lower incidence of traction-related neural injuries related to scar tissue dissection (Fig. 1).

Schematic illustration of the standard MIS-TLIF surgical approach. (A) Extent of decompression in the cross-sectional plane. (B) Extent of decompression in the coronal plane. MIS-TLIF = minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion.

Despite these benefits, standard MIS-TLIF typically involves unilateral zygapophysial joint resection without laminectomy, which may be insufficient for decompression in cases with severe central canal stenosis – a frequent comorbidity in recurrent LDH. Additionally, the constrained transforaminal working corridor increases the risk of superior exiting nerve root injury during interbody cage implantation.[7,8] Reports indicate that recurrence rates following PELD may reach up to 10.3%,[9] while the incidence of nerve root injury during revision MIS-TLIF has been reported at 8.7%,[5] underscoring the need for further technique refinement.

To address these limitations, a modified MIS-TLIF technique has been developed. This approach incorporates medialization of the quadrant channel and expanded bony resection. The resection includes the inferior articular process and the lower margin of the lamina of the superior adjacent vertebra, along with the upper margin of the lamina and hypertrophic or fused elements of the superior articular process of the inferior adjacent vertebra (Fig. 2). These modifications enable effective decompression of the central spinal canal, ipsilateral lateral recess, and the nerve root canal. Furthermore, the partial laminectomy provides increased operative space for interbody fusion and facilitates multidirectional placement of instrumentation beyond the intervertebral foramen, thereby reducing the risk of nerve root injury.

The present study aims to evaluate the clinical outcomes and complication profile associated with this modified MIS-TLIF technique in single-level revision surgery among patients presenting with recurrent symptoms following a prior single-level PELD. The results presented herein support the potential clinical utility of this surgical modification.

2. Methods

2.1. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: patients with a history of PELD performed for single-level LDH; patients with imaging-confirmed recurrence of LDH, secondary spinal stenosis, and nerve root compression at the previously operated spinal segment; patients presenting with characteristic clinical symptoms who had received standardized conservative management for a minimum of 3 months without satisfactory symptom relief; and patients who elected to undergo revision surgery.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: patients presenting with a first episode of lumbar spinal disorders, such as LDH, lumbar spondylolisthesis, or lumbar spinal stenosis; patients with concomitant conditions such as lumbar fractures, infections, neoplasms, spinal tuberculosis, or degenerative scoliosis; patients with a history of open lumbar spine surgery or multilevel lumbar procedures; patients with contraindications to surgical interventions; and patients with incomplete follow-up data.

2.2. Study participants

Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 50 patients were retrospectively enrolled. These patients had been admitted to the Department of Orthopedics at the Affiliated Renhe Hospital of China Three Gorges University between January 2020 and December 2022. The study cohort included 22 male and 28 female patients, ranging in age from 42 to 75 years, with a mean age of 52 years. Of the total, 38 patients had previously undergone percutaneous endoscopic transforaminal discectomy, while 12 had received percutaneous endoscopic interlaminar discectomy as the initial surgical intervention. In all cases, recurrence of LDH and symptom exacerbation occurred at the previously operated spinal segment following the initial procedure. Complete preoperative imaging datasets were available for all patients.

Revision surgery was performed in all cases using the modified MIS-TLIF technique, which incorporated a quadrant channel with a medially shifted working corridor. Prior to surgery, detailed information regarding the procedure was provided to each patient and their family members. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Renhe Hospital of China Three Gorges University (Approval Nos. 2022KY28, 2022KY26).

2.3. Surgical method

All patients included in this study initially presented with primary LDH (Fig. 3) and subsequently developed symptom recurrence at the same spinal segment following single-level PELD (Fig. 4). All revision surgeries were performed by a senior spine surgeon with extensive experience in minimally invasive spinal procedures.

Under general anesthesia, patients were positioned prone on a radiolucent spinal operating table. The abdomen was suspended to reduce intra-abdominal pressure, and the hips and knees were slightly flexed to optimize lumbar alignment. C-arm fluoroscopy was utilized to localize the affected intervertebral segment, and the skin projections of the pedicles of the adjacent superior and inferior vertebrae were marked. A longitudinal skin incision was made along the lateral border of the marked pedicles (Fig. 5A), followed by standard disinfection and sterile draping procedures.

Description of the surgical procedure. (A) Preoperative body surface markings indicating the incision site, guided by intraoperative fluoroscopy. (B) Intraoperative image showing decompression of the spinal canal with visible relaxation of the nerve root. (C) Postoperative view of the surgical incision site showing complete wound healing.

The incision was placed on the symptomatic side based on preoperative imaging findings. The skin, subcutaneous tissue, and lumbar fascia were incised sequentially. Blunt dissection was carried out through the natural intermuscular plane between the longissimus and multifidus muscles to expose the zygapophysial joint and the lateral edge of the superior lamina of the cranial adjacent vertebra.

A primary dilator from the quadrant retractor system was introduced to the mid-lateral portion of the superior lamina, followed by sequential dilation to establish a muscle-splitting operative channel. A working quadrant retractor was then positioned (Video S1, available at https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.28877024). The retractor angle was adjusted to approximately 10° to 30° relative to the sagittal plane and extended longitudinally to expose the surgical field, including the joint capsules of the superior and inferior vertebrae, the inferior lateral margin of the superior lamina, and the superior margin of the inferior lamina. The medially shifted working corridor was defined by targeting the anatomical junction between the inferior lateral margin of the superior lamina and the superior articular process of the inferior vertebra, ensuring consistency and reproducibility of the approach.

Under direct visualization, pedicle screw entry points were identified and prepared. Bony resection was performed on the inferior articular process, the inferior lateral portion of the superior lamina, hypertrophic or fused components of the superior articular process, and the superior laminar edge of the inferior vertebra. The ligamentum flavum was excised to decompress the central canal. Lateral recess decompression was then completed, with enlargement of the nerve root canal to expose both the superior exiting and inferior traversing nerve roots.

Partial opening of the intervertebral foramen and spinal canal was achieved. The inferior traversing nerve root and dural sac were gently retracted medially to access the herniated disc material. The disc was removed, and the intervertebral space was curetted to prepare the endplates for graft placement. Decompression was deemed adequate once the spinal canal was visibly expanded and the nerve roots were fully mobilized (Fig. 5B).

In patients presenting with bilateral symptoms, contralateral decompression was performed using the same approach. For asymptomatic contralateral sides, pedicle screws were placed without further decompression.

After appropriate trial sizing, an interbody fusion cage of suitable dimensions was selected. The operative field was irrigated with diluted povidone-iodine followed by saline. Autologous bone harvested during decompression was morselized and packed anteriorly into the intervertebral space. A cage filled with autologous cancellous bone was inserted to complete the interbody fusion.

Four pedicle screws were inserted into the prepared channels. A pre-contoured connecting rod was applied to restore physiological lumbar lordosis and then secured with compression. Intraoperative C-arm fluoroscopy was used to verify the position of the cage and instrumentation.

A drainage tube was placed on the symptomatic side and secured in position. The surgical wound was closed in layers using standard suture techniques (Fig. 5C).

Postoperatively, all patients received intravenous mannitol and dexamethasone sodium phosphate for 2 days to manage perioperative spinal cord and nerve root edema. Prophylactic antibiotics were administered within the first 24 hours following surgery. Patients were instructed to maintain bed rest with axial rotation restrictions and to initiate respiratory physiotherapy and lower extremity isometric exercises to prevent venous thromboembolism.

The drainage tube was removed once output was reduced to <50 mL within 24 hours. Postoperative lumbar spine radiographs in anteroposterior and lateral views were obtained. Patients remained on bed rest for up to 3 months and were advised to gradually resume ambulation while wearing a lumbar orthosis to support spinal stabilization during recovery.

2.4. Observation indicators

The following intraoperative and postoperative parameters were recorded for all patients: operative duration, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative drainage volume, and the incidence of any procedure-related complications. Clinical efficacy was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) to measure pain intensity and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) to evaluate functional impairment. These assessments were performed at 3 distinct time points: preoperatively, at 4 weeks postoperatively, and at the final follow-up visit.

Radiographic evaluations at the final follow-up included standard anteroposterior and lateral lumbar spine radiographs, along with thin-slice computed tomography (TSCT) of the lumbar spine. Interbody fusion status was determined using the Bridwell interbody fusion grading system,[10] which categorizes fusion outcomes as follows:

2.5. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.0.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables with a normal distribution were reported as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s). Changes in VAS and ODI scores across 3 time points – preoperative, 4 weeks postoperative, and final follow-up – were analyzed using repeated measures analysis of variance. Intervention type and time point were treated as independent variables, and their interaction effects were assessed. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple testing. A two-tailed P-value of <.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

3. Results

All patients completed clinical follow-up ranging from 12 to 20 months, with a mean follow-up duration of 16.8 months. The average operative time was 104 ± 12.1 minutes. Mean intraoperative blood loss was 316 ± 12.6 mL, and the average postoperative drainage volume was 132 ± 8.3 mL.

Transient ipsilateral nerve root palsy occurred in 1 patient (2% incidence). This patient developed numbness and mild motor weakness within the L5 dermatome during the first postoperative week, which was attributed to transient nerve root irritation during decompression. Management included intravenous administration of mannitol to reduce edema and dexamethasone sodium phosphate to alleviate inflammation for 3 consecutive days. This was followed by a 1-month course of oral mecobalamin as a neurotrophic agent. Neurological symptoms improved within 1 week and resolved completely within 3 months, without any lasting sensory or motor deficits.

Delayed wound healing was identified in 3 patients (6% incidence), each presenting with localized erythema and serous discharge at the surgical incision site between postoperative days 5 and 7. These cases were not accompanied by systemic signs of infection, such as purulent drainage, fever, or elevated inflammatory markers. Management involved partial suture removal to relieve tension at the incision site, local debridement, and daily sterile dressing changes. All wounds achieved complete healing within 4 to 6 weeks and did not impact interbody fusion or long-term functional recovery.

3.3.1. Interbody fusion status

Solid interbody bony fusion was achieved in all 50 patients. According to the Bridwell interbody fusion grading system, 42 patients (84%) were classified as grade I, indicating definitive fusion with trabecular remodeling, while 8 patients (16%) were classified as grade II, indicating intact grafts with partial incorporation and no radiolucency. No cases of grade III (presence of radiolucency) or grade IV (graft collapse or resorption) were identified. TSCT images revealed trabecular bone bridging between the graft and adjacent vertebral endplates, confirming stable and mature fusion.

3.3.2. Instrumentation position

All pedicle screws remained accurately positioned within the vertebral pedicles, with no evidence of loosening, migration, or mechanical failure. The pre-contoured connecting rods maintained physiological lumbar lordosis, and the interbody cages remained well-seated within the intervertebral spaces without evidence of displacement or collapse.

3.3.3. Decompression efficacy

Postoperative magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated adequate decompression of the central spinal canal, lateral recesses, and bilateral nerve root canals. No residual disc herniation or nerve root compression was observed. These radiological findings correlated with the sustained clinical improvements in VAS and ODI scores.

4. Discussion

4.1. Widespread adoption of PELD and associated complications

LDH is a prevalent degenerative spinal condition, most commonly affecting the L4–L5 and L5–S1 segments, and remains a leading cause of low back pain and radiculopathy. While standard conservative management, including pharmacotherapy, physical therapy, and activity modification, is effective for many individuals with primary LDH, a substantial proportion ultimately require surgical intervention due to persistent or worsening symptoms.[11]

PELD has gained widespread acceptance as a minimally invasive surgical technique for the treatment of LDH. The progression of this method – from the foundational concept of the “safety triangle” introduced by Tumialán et al,[12] to the development of systems such as the Yeung Endoscopic Spine System and the Transforaminal Endoscopic Spine System – has significantly enhanced procedural safety and improved clinical outcomes. Compared with conventional open discectomy, PELD offers several advantages, including reduced soft tissue disruption, shorter operative time, faster postoperative recovery, and greater patient satisfaction.

Despite these benefits, a subset of patients experience symptom recurrence or clinical deterioration following PELD. Recurrence rates of up to 8% have been reported,[9] which may exceed those observed with traditional open discectomy. Several factors contribute to this elevated risk. As a non-fusion, single-channel approach, PELD provides a limited surgical field and restricted access, which can result in incomplete disc removal and inadequate decompression. Additionally, the lack of interbody fusion permits ongoing degeneration of the involved spinal segment, increasing the likelihood of recurrent herniation and symptom recurrence over time.

In cases of recurrent LDH, conservative treatment often fails to produce durable symptom relief. Consequently, revision surgery becomes necessary to achieve complete neural decompression and sustained clinical improvement. Recurrent disc herniation following PELD is thus considered an absolute indication for revision lumbar spine surgery.

4.2. Use of MIS-TLIF in revision lumbar spine surgery and its limitations

Revision lumbar spine surgery presents distinct technical challenges compared with primary procedures. These challenges arise from the presence of dense postoperative scar tissue, adhesions involving the dural sac and nerve roots, and altered or absent anatomical landmarks, such as the zygapophysial joints. These factors increase the risk of intraoperative bleeding and elevate the potential for iatrogenic neural injury during decompression, including complications such as cerebrospinal fluid leakage. The primary goals of revision surgery are to achieve sufficient neural decompression and stable spinal fusion while minimizing the likelihood of neurological damage. Accordingly, the appropriate selection of the surgical approach is critical to optimizing outcomes.

Common surgical approaches for lumbar revision include posterior lumbar interbody fusion, transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion, and MIS-TLIF. MIS-TLIF, first described by Foley et al, in 2003,[7] has gained widespread acceptance in the context of revision spine surgery and has been associated with favorable clinical outcomes.

The MIS-TLIF technique employs a transforaminal approach using a quadrant channel system. Access to the affected spinal segment is established via sequential dilation of the natural intermuscular plane between the longissimus and multifidus muscles, thereby avoiding previously scarred tissue planes. This approach preserves the posterior tension band and minimizes disruption of the paravertebral musculature. Under direct visualization, resection of the inferior articular process and a portion of the superior articular process expands the intervertebral foramen, enabling exposure of both the superior exiting and inferior traversing nerve roots. Pathological disc material is excised with limited nerve manipulation, facilitating indirect decompression of the ventral dural sac and permitting interbody fusion. This method reduces the need for direct dural handling and lowers the risk of traction-related neural injury.

Compared with traditional open revision procedures, MIS-TLIF offers several advantages, including reduced soft tissue trauma, lower intraoperative blood loss, and faster postoperative recovery. The quadrant retractor system, equipped with a cold light source, provides enhanced visualization of the surgical field and eliminates the need for complex hand–eye coordination, as required in endoscopic techniques. These features contribute to a shorter learning curve and broader clinical adoption of MIS-TLIF.[13–15]

Nonetheless, standard MIS-TLIF has limitations regarding its indications. It is best suited for patients with mild lumbar spinal stenosis, low-grade spondylolisthesis, and single-level pathology without extensive intraspinal involvement. In cases involving severe central canal stenosis or high-grade spondylolisthesis, the standard unilateral resection of the hypertrophic zygapophysial joint – typically performed without laminectomy – may not allow for sufficient decompression. Additionally, the constrained transforaminal working corridor increases the risk of superior exiting nerve root injury during interbody cage insertion due to limited operative space.[8,16]

To address these limitations, a modified MIS-TLIF technique has been proposed, involving medialization of the quadrant channel working corridor.[17,18] This modification permits more extensive resection of hypertrophic and adherent articular processes, widening of the intervertebral foramen, and partial removal of the ipsilateral lamina. These changes allow for more thorough decompression of the central spinal canal and improved access to the intervertebral space. As a result, procedural safety is enhanced, and the risk of superior exiting nerve root injury is reduced when compared with the conventional transforaminal approach.

4.3. Unique features and advantages of the modified surgical technique

This study introduces a modified version of MIS-TLIF, which incorporates a quadrant channel with a medially shifted working corridor. Building upon the foundational MIS-TLIF technique, this modified approach integrates several key refinements intended to improve surgical efficiency and anatomical access, particularly in the context of revision lumbar spine surgery. The principal technical innovations and their associated benefits are outlined below.

4.3.1. Medial shift of the working corridor

The quadrant retractor is repositioned medially within the intermuscular plane between the longissimus and multifidus muscles. This adjustment facilitates improved exposure of deep anatomical structures while maintaining the integrity of the paraspinal musculature.

4.3.2. Optimized working corridor placement angle

The orientation of the working corridor is modified to achieve a wider operative field, typically ranging from 10° to 30° relative to the sagittal plane. This medial angulation enhances both visual and instrumental access to the pathological segment.

4.3.3. Expanded surgical exposure

Longitudinal expansion of the quadrant channel permits comprehensive exposure of essential landmarks, including the joint capsules of adjacent vertebrae, the inferior lateral edge of the lamina of the superior vertebra, and the superior edge of the lamina of the inferior vertebra. Notably, this exposure is achieved without disturbing the transverse processes, thereby preserving native bony architecture.

4.3.4. Enhanced decompression capability

The technique allows for resection of the inferior articular process and the inferior lateral lamina of the superior vertebra, as well as the superior laminar edge and hypertrophic or adherent components of the superior articular process of the inferior vertebra. These modifications enable thorough decompression of both the central canal and the ipsilateral lateral recess.

4.4. Indications and contraindications

4.4.1. Indications

The modified MIS-TLIF technique is indicated for patients with recurrent LDH at the same spinal segment following single-level PELD, particularly in cases with imaging-confirmed secondary spinal stenosis. Additional indications include severe central canal stenosis, defined by an anteroposterior diameter of <10 mm, or high-grade lumbar spondylolisthesis (Meyerding grade II–III), where single-level revision surgery is warranted. The procedure is also appropriate for patients who have failed to respond to standardized conservative treatment for at least 3 months and who continue to experience significant low back pain, radicular symptoms, or neurological deficits.

4.4.2. Contraindications

Contraindications to this technique include the presence of multilevel lumbar degenerative disease involving 2 or more motion segments. Additional exclusion criteria comprise lumbar vertebral fractures, spinal infections, neoplastic lesions, spinal tuberculosis, or degenerative scoliosis with a Cobb angle exceeding 20°. A history of prior open lumbar surgery or poor bone mineral density (T-score < −2.5) also contraindicates the procedure. Finally, patients with contraindications to general anesthesia or those deemed unfit for surgery due to comorbid medical conditions should not undergo this intervention.

4.5. Practical considerations

While the modified MIS-TLIF technique offers several clinical advantages, it also introduces technical challenges that must be carefully addressed to ensure procedural safety and efficacy. Precise positioning of the quadrant channel is critical; incorrect placement into the paraspinal muscle mass may result in muscle hemorrhage or necrosis. Additionally, compared with the standard MIS-TLIF approach, the modified technique involves more extensive osseous resection, potentially increased intraoperative blood loss, and a greater degree of dural sac manipulation.

Based on operative experience, the following procedural recommendations are proposed to minimize technique-related risks:

4.5.1. Preoperative planning

Accurate identification of the symptomatic intervertebral segment is essential and should be confirmed intraoperatively using C-arm fluoroscopy. The surface projections of the pedicles of the superior and inferior adjacent vertebrae should be clearly marked to guide incision placement and ensure anatomical precision during surgical access.

4.5.2. Incision site selection

The incision should follow a longitudinal trajectory that connects the lateral borders of the marked pedicle projections. This orientation provides direct access to the target segment while maintaining alignment with underlying anatomical landmarks.

4.5.3. Layered dissection

Tissue dissection should be performed in a controlled, stepwise fashion through the skin, subcutaneous tissue, and lumbodorsal fascia, adhering strictly to the predetermined incision path.

4.5.4. Muscle separation

Under direct visualization, the intermuscular plane between the longissimus and multifidus muscles should be identified. Blunt digital dissection is recommended to separate the muscle fibers, exposing the zygapophysial joint and the lateral margin of the lamina of the superior adjacent vertebra.

4.5.5. Cannula and channel placement

Sequential dilators from the quadrant retractor system should be introduced to gradually expand the intermuscular corridor. The quadrant channel should then be placed and adjusted medially, typically at an angle of 10° to 30° relative to the sagittal plane. Longitudinal extension of the channel should be performed as needed to achieve sufficient exposure of the surgical field.

4.5.6. Incision extension (if required)

In instances where excessive skin tension is observed around the quadrant channel, a minor extension of the skin incision may be necessary. This adjustment reduces the risk of postoperative ischemia or wound margin necrosis and promotes optimal wound healing.

5. Conclusion

The clinical outcomes and postoperative follow-up data presented in this study indicate that the modified MIS-TLIF technique, employing a quadrant channel with a medially shifted working corridor, offers several advantages in the context of revision lumbar spine surgery. These benefits include reduced trauma to the paravertebral musculature, decreased intraoperative blood loss, shorter hospitalization, a low rate of postoperative complications, and accelerated recovery.

Collectively, these findings suggest that the modified approach is a safe, effective, and technically feasible alternative to conventional revision procedures. Its emphasis on anatomical preservation and procedural adaptability supports its potential for broader application in clinical practice. Nevertheless, the retrospective nature of the study and the relatively small sample size represent key limitations. Future prospective, multicenter studies with larger patient cohorts are necessary to validate these results and further define the role of this technique in revision spine surgery.
