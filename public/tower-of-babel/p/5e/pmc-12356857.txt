Machine learning analysis of pharmaceutical cocrystals solubility parameters in enhancing the drug properties for advanced pharmaceutical manufacturing
Authors: Tareq Nafea Alharby, Bader Huwaimel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A new computational framework based on machine learning was developed for prediction of Hansen solubility parameters in preparation of pharmaceutical cocrystals with improved properties. The models of Kernel Ridge Regression (KRR), Multi-Linear Regression (MLR), and Orthogonal Matching Pursuit (OMP) were optimized in prediction of three Hansen solubility parameters. Each model’s performance was assessed via measuring Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), R2, Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and Monte Carlo Cross-Validation (CV) scores using a Tabu Search method for optimization. The results demonstrated that KRR outperformed other models for predicting solubility parameters in the formulation. This comparative evaluation offers valuable perspectives on selecting models for similar regression assignments, stressing the significance of choosing the right algorithm according to particular output demands. The results are useful for design of medicines and screening coformers with solubility enhancement in pharmaceutical co-crystallization.

Introduction

Improving bioavailability of pharmaceuticals (e.g., small molecules) has been under focus of pharmaceutical industry which can be addressed by various engineering techniques. Poor solubility of medicines results in consumption of higher drug dosage which would lead to more severe side effects for patients. As such, improving drugs bioavailability is a game changer in pharmaceutical industry which is required for advanced pharmaceutical manufacturing1–3. Complex formation, co-crystallization, amorphous solid dispersion, nanocrystals, and salt formation are the key techniques to enhance bioavailability of medicines in aqueous solutions. Each technique possesses its advantages and disadvantages. The method of nanonization is reported recently as the most facile method for production of nanomedicine which can be operated in either wet or dry routes4–6.

The method of cocrystal formation can help improve not only bioavailability of medicines, but also other properties of medicine formulations such as stability, dissolution rate, hardness, etc7,8. For this approach, a coformer is used and molecular interaction is employed to form the cocrystal of API (Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient) and coformer. Basically, no covalent bond is formed in cocrystal formation, and other intermolecular forces are assumed such as hydrogen bond. Therefore, analysis of intermolecular interactions is crucial in the design of proper pharmaceutical cocrystals with enhanced bioavailability in aqueous media.

Different computational techniques can be used to analyze molecular interactions in pharmaceutical cocrystal formation which rely on the analysis of possible intermolecular forces9, however these models are computationally expensive to be developed for wide range of molecules. To have a broader data bank of drugs and coformers, data-driven models can be explored in combination with quantum chemical calculations. The quantum data can be used as inputs to data-driven models such as machine learning. Therefore, hybrid models can be built considering molecular structure of compounds for screening pharmaceutical cocrystals. Mahdi and Obaidullah10 used Transient Search Optimization and some regressive models for prediction of pharmaceutical cocrystals solubility parameters using some molecular descriptors.

Machine Learning (ML) has been recognized as a crucial approach in pharmaceutical engineering, providing advanced techniques to make predictions from complex datasets such as pharmaceutical solubility dataset11,12. ML encompasses a diverse range of algorithms that have the ability to acquire knowledge from data so that they can be used for making a broad data bank for drugs. The performance of three robust ML models: Kernel Ridge Regression (KRR), Multi-Linear Regression (MLR), and Orthogonal Matching Pursuit (OMP) was evaluated in predicting three specific outputs which are Hansen solubility parameters for pharmaceutical cocrystal coformers: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\delta\:}_{d}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\delta\:}_{p}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\delta\:}_{h}$$\end{document}. Each of these models offers unique advantages in predicting solubility parameters. KRR is renowned for its capacity to handle non-linear relationships in data by utilizing kernel functions to map inputs into high-dimensional spaces. In contrast, MLR is a direct method that expands linear regression to accommodate multiple outputs, making it appropriate for situations where the response variables have linear dependencies on the predictors. OMP is a greedy algorithm that is especially effective for high-dimensional data, as it iteratively selects the most pertinent features to construct a sparse predictive model.

For development of ML models, the Tabu Search method was utilized to optimize the models’ hyperparameters and enhance their robustness. The study emphasizes the importance of choosing a model that is customized to meet specific output needs, guiding professionals in selecting algorithms based on their data and desired results. The models and optimizers are developed for the first time in analysis and screening pharmaceutical cocrystal coformers based on input parameters which are molecular descriptors. The contribution of methodology is on screening the coformer for preparation of proper pharmaceutical cocrystals which can save time and cost of experimentation.

Methods

In this study, the objective was to predict three specific outputs, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\delta\:}_{d}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\delta\:}_{p}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\delta\:}_{h}$$\end{document}, using a variety of input features (86 features and 181 data points) via ML approach. The outputs are indeed Hansen solubility parameters which are correlated to molecular descriptors estimated via group contribution method and COMSO-RS thermodynamic model. The dataset is taken from the source published, and the details of inputs and the dataset can be found in13. Also, the same dataset was used by Mahdi and Obaidullah10 to build ML models and optimized them.

To optimize these ML models, the Tabu Search method was utilized. This metaheuristic optimization technique is designed to navigate combinatorial optimization problems effectively, ensuring that the models are fine-tuned for optimal performance.

Each ML’s accuracy was measured using several statistical criteria: the coefficient of determination (R²), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and Monte Carlo Cross-Validation (CV) scores. These criteria provided an overall view of the models’ predictive accuracy and reliability in estimation of Hansen solubility parameters.

All models and optimization procedures in this study were implemented using Python 3.8, which is an open-source software obtained from: https://www.python.org.

Tabu search

Designed to address combinatorial optimization problems, Tabu Search is a potent metaheuristic optimization method14. The algorithm begins by initializing with a starting solution, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{0}$$\end{document}, and setting up a tabu list, TL, which records recently made moves to avoid them temporarily. Alongside this, necessary parameters for the algorithm, such as the maximum size of the tabu list and the number of iterations, are also established15.

The core of the algorithm involves an iterative process. At each iteration, a neighborhood of solutions, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N\left(S\right)$$\end{document}, is generated from the current solution S. From this neighborhood, the best possible solution S’ is selected, provided it is not in the tabu list or meets certain aspiration criteria that justify overriding the tabu status. This ensures that the search can progress to potentially better solutions without revisiting recent moves that could lead back to local optima16.

Kernel ridge regression (KRR)

KRR is a regression method that extends the principles of ridge regression. The algorithm operates in a feature space defined by a kernel function and is able to capture intricate relationships between variables17,18.

KRR optimizes a regularized objective function, balancing accuracy in correlating the training data and a penalty for model complexity. The objective function in this method can be expressed as17,19:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\underset{w}{\text{min}}\frac{1}{2}{\sum\:}_{i=1}^{N}{\left({y}_{i}-{w}^{T}{\upvarphi\:}\left({x}_{i}\right)\right)}^{2}+\frac{{\uplambda\:}}{2}{w}^{T}w$$\end{document}.

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{i}$$\end{document} denotes the input vector for i-th training data vector, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{y}_{i}$$\end{document} stands for the target of the i-th data vector in the training set, and N denotes the total number of training data points. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varphi\:\left({x}_{i}\right)$$\end{document} maps input variables to a high-dimensional space via a kernel. In addition, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\uplambda\:}$$\end{document} represents the regularization parameter that balances data fitting and model complexity, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:w$$\end{document} represents the weight vector19.

Multi-Linear regression (MLR)

The MLR model allows for the simultaneous prediction of multiple response variables based on a set of predictor variables. It extends the traditional linear regression model to handle multiple outputs, capturing the relationships between the predictors and each response variable. The goal of MLR is to estimate a set of coefficients that minimize the prediction error while ensuring that each response variable is accurately predicted based on the same set of predictors20.

Mathematically, the Multi-Linear Regression can be expressed as follows21:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\underset{{\upbeta\:}}{\text{min}}\frac{1}{2{n}_{\text{samples}}}|Y-X{\upbeta\:}{|}_{Fro}^{2}+{{\upbeta\:}}_{0}$$\end{document}.

where Y represents the matrix of response variables, X stands for the matrix of predictors, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\upbeta\:}$$\end{document} is the coefficient matrix, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{n}_{\text{samples}}$$\end{document} is the number of samples, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:|\cdot\:{|}_{Fro}$$\end{document} stands for the Frobenius norm. The term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\upbeta\:}}_{0}$$\end{document} represents the intercept term.

Orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP)

This is a greedy algorithm designed to handle high-dimensional data by choosing the best features. It is suitable when dealing with sparse signals, where only a few predictors contribute significantly to the response variable22. OMP aims to find a sparse solution by adding one feature at a time, ensuring each step maximally reduces the residual error22,23.

Mathematically, OMP regression can be formulated as24,25:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\underset{{\upbeta\:}}{\text{min}}\frac{1}{2}|y-X{\upbeta\:}{|}_{2}^{2}\hspace{1em}\text{subject\:to}\hspace{1em}|{\upbeta\:}{|}_{0}\le\:T$$\end{document}.

where y represents the response variable, X is the matrix of predictors, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\upbeta\:}$$\end{document} is the coefficient vector, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:|\cdot\:{|}_{2}$$\end{document} denotes the Euclidean norm, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:|\cdot\:{|}_{0}$$\end{document} represents the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\text{l}}_{0}$$\end{document} norm, which counts the number of non-zero coefficients in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\upbeta\:}$$\end{document}. Parameter T stands for the maximum number of non-zero coefficients allowed.

Results and discussion

The regression task was performed using three different machine learning models: KRR, MLR, and OMP. Each model’s performance was evaluated for three different outputs: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\updelta\:}}_{d}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\delta\:}_{p}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\updelta\:}}_{h}$$\end{document}. The performance metrics considered were the R2 score, Monte Carlo Cross-Validation (CV) mean score, Monte Carlo CV standard deviation, RMSE, and MAE. In this study, an 80 − 20 train-test split was employed. Cross-validation analysis indicated that the influence of this random split on the final results was negligible. The mean R² score of the KRR model across the three outputs improved by 6% following hyperparameter optimization, while the improvement for the other two models was approximately 4%. This highlights the critical role of optimization in enhancing model performance.

For every output, Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) was employed for feature selection. Various numbers of features were assessed using this approach, and subsequently, a mean of R2 scores associated with the corresponding features was documented (refer to Fig. 1). The best Features Count was determined using Fig. 1 where the value of Mean R2 is the highest for each input. Finally, for each output, the selected sets of features with varying numbers are presented in Table 1. The detailed statistical parameters for all models and output are presented in Tables 2, 3 and 4 to compare the predictive accuracy of all models in calculation of three Hansen solubility parameters. For all outputs, the R2 values attained in this study is greater than those reported by Mahdi and Obaidullah10 which show the superiority of models developed in this research for correlating Hansen solubility parameters.

Conclusion

This study thoroughly evaluated three ML models for predicting the Hansen solubility parameters, i.e., δd, δp, and δh. By leveraging the Tabu Search optimization method, it was ensured that each model was fine-tuned to its optimal performance. The investigated system is a data bank of pharmaceutical cocrystal coformers to provide a screening tool for the formation of cocrystals. The findings reveal that KRR consistently outperforms the other models for the prediction of δp and δh, achieving R² scores of 0.9786 and 0.9878, respectively. This indicates that KRR is highly effective in capturing the complex relationships between the input features and these outputs, demonstrating its robustness and accuracy. On the other hand, MLR emerged as the best model for predicting δd, with an R² of 0.9353, highlighting its suitability for this specific task. The comparative analysis underscores the importance of model selection tailored to specific output requirements in regression tasks. While KRR’s superior performance for δp and δh highlights its general applicability for complex and non-linear data, MLR’s success with δd suggests that simpler models may sometimes be more effective depending on the nature of the target variable. Future research could explore the integration of these models with other advanced machine learning techniques and investigate their applicability to different types of datasets.
