Contrast-enhanced ultrasound for diagnosing subtypes of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma: a comparative study with poorly differentiated hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Nan Zhang, Yue Yang, Ke Lin, Bin Qiao, Dao-Peng Yang, Dong-Dong Jin, Bin Li, Dong-Liang Zhao, Xiao-Hua Xie, Xiao-Yan Xie, Ji-Hui Kang, Bo-Wen Zhuang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPathologically, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) is classified into small-duct (SD) type and large-duct (LD) type, each with distinct clinicopathological characteristics. The contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) features of the two ICC types remain insufficiently explored.PurposeTo evaluate liver CEUS imaging for differentiating the SD and LD types of ICC and further compare them with poorly differentiated hepatocellular carcinoma (pHCC).Materials and methodsA single-center retrospective study enrolled 252 patients with SD-type ICC, LD-type ICC, or pHCC between October 2017 and August 2023. Logistic regression analyses identified independent clinical, pathological, ultrasound, and CEUS predictors. Based on these features, a decision tree-based diagnostic model was developed. The model’s performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis in both the training and validation cohorts, as well as in subgroup stratified by tumor size ≤ 5 cm and > 5 cm. Differences in overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) based on the model were further analyzed.ResultsOverall, 252 patients (mean age, 58.4 ± 10.7 years; 174 males) with 140 SD-type ICC, 55 LD-type ICC and 57 pHCC were enrolled. Multivariate analysis revealed that AFP, CEA, CA19-9, HBsAg status, arterial phase enhancement pattern, washout time ≤ 45 s, and marked washout were independent predictors for tumor categories differentiation (all P <.05). The decision tree-based model incorporating the major features demonstrated excellent performance in both the training cohort (AUC 0.89) and validation cohort (AUC 0.88), as well as in tumor size ≤ 5 cm (AUC 0.90) and > 5 cm (AUC 0.84). OS was significantly worse in LD-type ICC patients compared to SD-type and pHCC (P <.05 for both), while RFS showed no significant difference.ConclusionsA user-friendly, decision tree-based diagnostic model was developed to accurately predict ICC subtypes and pHCC, facilitating improved clinical decision-making.SummaryThe decision tree-based diagnostic model effectively diagnosed small-duct type and large-duct type intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, as well as poorly differentiated hepatocellular carcinoma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40644-025-00923-8.

Introduction

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) is the second most prevalent primary liver cancer, accounting for approximately 15% of hepatobiliary malignancies [1]. The latest World Health Organization classification classifies ICC into small-duct (SD) type, typically in the periphery liver parenchyma, and large-duct (LD) type, mainly located around the second branches of portal veins in the peripheral liver parenchyma [2, 3]. Several studies have demonstrated that LD-type ICC has higher lymph node metastasis rates, vascular invasion, and worse prognosis than SD-type ICC [4–6]. Current study findings suggest that major hepatectomy with lymph nodes dissection and biliary reconstruction were recommended for patients with LD-type ICC whereas those with SD-type may only require liver resection [4]. Thus, differentiating these ICC subtypes is essential for treatment planning and prognosis prediction.

Due to the complex three-dimensional branching of the biliary system, distinguishing SD- and LD-type ICC on radiological images based on anatomic location alone is challenging. However, given their distinct pathological backgrounds, each subtype may present unique radiological characteristics [3]. Recently, Nam et al. [7] identified arterial hyper-enhancement, round or lobulated margins, and absence of bile duct encasement as significant CT features indicative of SD-type ICC. Similarly, Park et al. [8] and Xiao et al. [9] found that arterial phase hypo-enhancement, intrahepatic duct dilatation, and lack of targetoid restriction were independent predictors for LD-type ICC on MRI. Despite these findings, to our knowledge, few studies have focused on the role of contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) in distinguishing between SD- and LD-type ICC.

The CEUS Liver Imaging Reporting and Data System (CEUS LI-RADS) (v2017), has greatly improved the standardization of sonographic evaluation criteria [10, 11]. CEUS LI-RADS categorizes LR-M tumors as potential or confirmed non-hepatocellular carcinoma liver malignancies, mainly including liver metastasis and ICC. Usually, liver metastasis can be easily diagnosed from patient history. However, ICC accounts for only about 30% of LR-M lesions, while hepatocellular carcinoma, notably poorly differentiated hepatocellular carcinoma (pHCC), constituted up to 40% [12, 13]. Because pHCC may show early or marked washout, often categorized similarly to ICC within the CEUS LI-RADS framework [14]. Therefore, distinguishing between pHCC and ICC remains a significant diagnostic challenge.

This study aimed to evaluate the imaging features of liver CEUS to differentiate between the SD- and LD- types ICC, and to further compare them with pHCC. Additionally, we compared the outcomes among different categories after radical resection.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. The ethics committee waived the requirement for written informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized clinical data.

Patients and pathological diagnosis

Between October 2017 and August 2023, patients with ICC or HCC who underwent surgical resection were retrospectively identified through our hospital’s pathology database. The inclusion criteria were: pathologically confirmed ICC or HCC and preoperative CEUS conducted within 4 weeks of surgery at our institution. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) well/moderately differentiated HCC or other rare pathological types, (b) underwent preoperative neoadjuvant or local treatment, (c) poor/missing imaging data. Figure 1 shows the patient flowchart. The median time from preoperative liver CEUS to pathological confirmation was 15 days (range, 1–28 days).

Two pathologists (Y.L. with 5 years of experience, H.K. with more than 20 years of experience in hepatic pathology) reviewed all glass slides for each case in consensus. Histologic examination details are provided in Table S1.

B-mode US and CEUS examination

A sonographer with over 10 years of experience in CEUS performed B-mode ultrasound and CEUS. Detailed procedures are provided in Supplemental text 1. B-mode US identified target lesions and measured their size. For patients with multiple lesions, only the largest was selected. After switching to CEUS, SonoVue (Bracco, Milan, Italy) was administered, and images were captured in arterial, portal venous, and late phases.

Imaging analyses

Images were analyzed by two radiologists (B.W.Z and X.H.X, each with over 10 years of liver US experience) blinded to the patient records and diagnosis. Discrepancies were resolved by another senior radiologist (X.Y.X, over 20 years of experience). After consensus evaluation, the data analysis was conducted. B-mode US and CEUS features were independently assessed, including number, size, location, shape (regular or irregular), echogenicity (hypo-, iso-, hyper-, or mixed echo), tumor boundary (clear or unclear), cholangiectasis, cholangiolithiasis, necrosis, arterial phase enhancement pattern (homogeneous, heterogeneous, peripheral rim-like hyper-enhancement or hypo-enhancement), washout time, feeding artery, peripheral circular artery, twisted artery in tumor, marked washout, intra-tumoral vein, and vascular invasion. Table S2 details imaging feature definitions. Moreover, all lesions were categorized into three LI-RADS categories, with details in Supplemental Material 2.

Clinical data and prognosis of patients

Clinical information including age, sex, HbsAg, HCV-Ab status, alpha fetoprotein (AFP), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), and carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9) were collected. US and dynamic CT scans were scheduled every 3 months after surgery. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) after resection were analyzed. RFS was defined as the interval from surgery to either recurrence or death, whichever occurred first. OS was calculated from the date of surgery to death. The data were censored at the last follow-up for patients without a documented RFS/OS event. Radiologist (K.L) collected data using electronic medical records and follow-up imaging studies until April 3, 2024.

Statistical analysis

A consensus reading compared radiological features among the three tumor types. Interobserver agreement was investigated by Cohen’ κ value for binary variables and the weighted κ value for ordinal and categorical variables. Agreement levels were considered poor (κ < 0.20), fair (κ: 0.20–0.40), moderate (κ: 0.40–0.60), substantial (κ: 0.60–0.80), or excellent (κ > 0.80).

Continuous variables were reported as the mean ± standard deviation or median and interquartile range, and were compared using Student’s t tests or Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were reported as numbers and percentages and were compared using Pearson’s χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Participants were randomly assigned to training and validation cohorts in a 7:3 ratio. Additionally, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses identified the independent predictors in the training cohort with odds ratios and 95% confidence interval. To develop a diagnostic decision tree, we selected significant indicators that appear at least twice in pairwise comparisons and utilized a Python-based, open-source machine learning package (scikit-learn). The importance of each feature was determined by calculating the Gini importance. Diagnostic performance was analyzed by the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. A subgroup of participants with tumor size ≤ 5 cm and > 5 cm were assessed to compare diagnostic model performance. Survival outcomes after surgery were evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and pairwise comparisons were performed using the log-rank test. Moreover, a power analysis was performed to confirm the adequacy of sample size (Supplemental text 3 and Fig S1). Bonferroni correction was used for post hoc multiple comparisons for all statistical analyses. Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed (B.L., with > 10 years of experience) with R software (version 4.1.0; https://www.r-project.org) and Python software (version 3.8; https://www.python.org/).

Results

Clinical and pathological characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the patients’ clinical and pathological characteristics. Overall, 252 patients (mean age, 58.3 ± 10.7 years; 174 males) with 140 SD-type ICCs, 55 LD-type ICCs, and 57 pHCCs were included. A substantial number of patients in the pHCC group than those with ICCs had hepatitis B infection (P <.05 for both). The AFP level was markedly higher in the pHCC group compared to both SD- and LD-type ICC groups (P <.001 for both). The prevalence of elevated CEA level was significantly greater in LD- type ICC group than in SD-type ICC and pHCC groups (P <.001 for both), whereas CA19-9 level was considerably higher in patients with ICCs than in those with pHCC (P <.001 for both). Lymphatic and perineural invasion were significantly more frequent in the LD-type ICC group compared to the SD-type ICC and pHCC groups (P <.001 for both).

B-mode US and CEUS features and interobserver agreement assessment

B-mode US and CEUS imaging findings are described in Table 2. The median diameter of the SD-type ICC, LD-type ICC, and pHCC were 6.0 cm (IQR, 4.7–8.3 cm), 5.7 cm (IQR, 4.1–7.0 cm), and 5.7 cm (IQR, 4.3–7.7 cm), respectively. In the SD-type ICC group, there were 25 patients (18%) with multiple tumors, compared to 5 patients (9%) in the LD-type ICC group and 10 patients (18%) in the pHCC group. On B-mode US, the LD-type ICC showed a higher prevalence of unclear boundary, cholangiolithiasis, and cholangiectasis than SD-type ICC and pHCC (P <.001 for both, Fig. 2). Four CEUS arterial phase enhancement patterns were observed: peripheral rim-like, homogeneous or heterogeneous hyper-enhancement, and heterogeneous hypo-enhancement. The distribution of these patterns in SD-type ICC was 89 (64%), 13 (9%), 36 (26%), and 2 (1%), in LD-type ICC was 7 (13%), 3 (5%), 27 (49%), and 18 (33%), and in pHCC was 3 (5%), 14 (25%), 40 (70%), and 0 (0%), respectively. The median washout time of SD-type ICC and LD-type ICC were 35.0 s (IQR, 29.0–41.8 s) and 35.0 s (IQR, 30.0–43.0 s), which were significantly earlier than that of pHCC (P <.001 for both). Differences were also observed in terms of feeding artery, twisted artery in tumor, and marked washout (P <.05 for all).

CEUS LI-RADS category assignments

Descriptive analyses of patients among three groups according to LI-RADS categories are displayed in Table 3. The percentages of patients categorized as LR-M were 133 of 140 (95%) and 52 of 55 (95%) in SD- and LD-type ICC groups, both significantly higher than those of patients with pHCC (44 of 57, 77%) (P =.01 and P <.001, respectively). Interestingly, among the LR-M features analysis, arterial rim-like enhancement was more frequent in SD-type ICC than in LD-type ICC (P <.001). Meanwhile, the LD-type ICC group had a significantly higher percentage of early washout (< 60 s) and marked washout compared to the pHCC group (P <.001 for both), except for arterial rim enhancement (P =.29). Besides, all three LR-M features showed significant differences between SD-type ICC and pHCC (P <.001 for all).

Development of the diagnostic decision tree

Characteristics of the training and validation cohorts are shown in Table S2-5. Except for location (P =.02), no differences were found between the cohorts for other characteristics (P >.05, Table S6). Table 4 and Table S9 summarize the results of univariate and multivariate analyses for clinical and imaging features. Multivariable analysis revealed that the serological indicators including AFP, CEA, CA19-9, and HBsAg status, as well as CEUS features such as enhancement pattern in the arterial phase, washout time ≤ 45 s, and marked washout, were independent predictors. A decision tree model was constructed based on the features identified by multivariate analysis (Fig. 3). The importance of each feature is set out in Figure S2. When marked washout, cholangiolithiasis, CA19-9, and CEA were included in the construction of decision tree iteration, the classification diagnosis efficiency did not improve. Consequently, these features were excluded, and their importance was assigned to 0. Finally, the decision tree was built with five variables: rim-like hyper-enhancement, washout time > 45 s, cholangiectasis, HbsAg status, and AFP. The decision tree illustrates feature combinations and their negative predictive value for ICC subtypes and pHCC.

Diagnostic performance of the decision tree

The overall accuracies, with 95% CI, for the training and validation cohorts were 80.1% (73.5–85.7%) and 78.9% (67.6–87.7%). The decision tree model demonstrated high predictive accuracy for both SD-type ICC and pHCC, as determined by the ROC curve (Fig. 4). In the training cohort, the area under the ROC curve (AUC) of the decision tree model was 0.87 for SD-type ICC (compared to 0.90 for the validation cohort), 0.83 for LD-type ICC (compared to 0.84), and 0.94 for pHCC (compared to 0.91).

Postoperative survival outcomes

Patients with LD-type ICC who underwent surgical resection showed worse OS compared to those with SD-type ICC and pHCC (both P <.01). However, no evidence of a difference in RFS was observed among the three groups (P =.51). Regarding both OS (P =.03) and RFS (P =.51), the predictive categories from our model demonstrated outcomes consistent with the pathological types (Fig. 5).

Subgroup analysis of the tumor size

The stability of the decision tree model was evaluated in two subgroups of overall patients categorized by tumor size with a maximum diameter of ≤ 5 cm (n = 88) and > 5 cm (n = 164) (Fig S3). The analysis indicated AUCs of 0.80, 0.85, and 0.86 for predicting SD-type ICC, LD-type ICC, and pHCC in tumor size ≤ 5 cm, respectively. Conversely, the AUCs for tumor size > 5 cm were 0.92, 0.82, and 0.97, respectively, suggesting excellent discriminatory performance.

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that the enhancement pattern in arterial phase, washout time > 45 s, cholangiectasis, HbsAg status, and AFP level were independent and significant predictors of the two subtypes of ICC (SD-type and LD-type) and pHCC. Using these clinical characteristics, as well as B-mode US and CEUS features, we established a decision tree model to obtain good diagnostic performance in the internal validation cohort with AUCs of 0.90, 0.84, and 0.91 for predicting SD-type ICC, LD-type ICC, and pHCC. Moreover, we showed that the decision-tree model was associated with OS (P =.03) in patients with ICC subtype and pHCC who underwent resection, indicating its potential clinical application.

In our study, AFP level, hepatitis B virus infection, and cholangiectasis were incorporated into the model to identify three types of tumors. Serum AFP level and HbsAg status are predictive factors for pHCC compared to ICC, consistent with previous studies [15, 16]. Normally, adult hepatocytes synthesize only minimal amounts of AFP. However, during the process of malignant transformation in hepatocytes, the AFP gene is reactivated, resulting in an elevation in serum AFP levels [17]. Additionally, hepatitis B virus infection is a high-risk factor for the development of HCC. Intrahepatic bile duct dilation was more commonly observed in LD-type ICC, potentially associated with its growth pattern. LD-type ICC trends extend along the bile duct wall, invading the portal connective tissue, adjacent bile ducts, and hepatic parenchyma which may be more likely to cause cholangiectasis [18].

The enhancement patterns in the arterial phase of SD-type ICC are more likely to exhibit a rim-like hyper-enhancement pattern, whereas LD-type ICC typically presents with heterogeneous hyper- or hypo-enhancement. The findings could be due to variations in fibrous stroma architecture. Histologically, SD-type ICC typically exhibits a peripheral tumor cell arrangement surrounding a central fibrous stroma, whereas LD-type ICC is characterized by varying degrees of diffuse fibrous interstitial distribution [19, 20]. Therefore, rim-like hyper-enhancement is a primary CEUS feature for differentiating the two subtypes of ICC.

A portion of HCCs is classified under the LR-M category, which poses a challenge for diagnosing ICC using the LR-M criteria [21–23]. In this study, approximately 70% of pHCC (44 of 57) were categorized as LR-M. Notably, the LR-M diagnostic criteria differ between ICC subtypes and pHCC. SD-type ICC frequently presented arterial rim-like enhancement compared to LD-type ICC and pHCC. Early washout and marked washout were frequently detected in two ICC types compared to pHCC. Wang et al. [24] found that shortening the early washout cutoff to 45 s can improve the diagnostic performance to distinguish ICC and pHCC. Accordingly, we adjusted the onset of washout time from 60 s to 45 s and found that this adjustment improved the differential diagnosis between ICC and pHCC. Nonetheless, it is necessary to validate whether the LR-M criteria require further modification in a larger patient cohort.

Although there are differences in the clinicoradiological features among the SD-type ICC, LD-type ICC, and pHCC, there is still overlapping in their manifestations. Consequently, we established a decision tree model to assist in differentiating the three tumor types. Our diagnostic model including five commonly used clinicoradiological indicators is user-friendly and has demonstrated robust performance across training and validation cohorts. Furthermore, we verified its robustness in subgroups of different tumor sizes. Our tree-like diagnostic system offers significant advantages including a clear decision-making process and full interpretability. It also provides an approach for risk stratification of ICC and pHCC. To confirm its generalizability and robustness, large cohorts are required.

Consistent with previous studies [25, 26], our study found that patients with LD-type ICC had a worse OS compared to those with SD-type ICC, while SD-type ICC exhibits a similar OS to pHCC. Previous studies and our results have indicated that LD-type ICC tends to rapid lymphatic metastasis and perineural invasion [27, 28], and this further leads to a poor prognosis. In addition, Kinzler et al. [29] reported that SD-type ICC patients who received chemotherapy experienced a longer median survival, underscoring the potential benefits of chemotherapy in this patient group. This may be the reason why there was no difference in RFS among the three groups in our study, while the OS was poorer in the LD-type ICC group. The decision tree model developed in this study offers practical value for integration into clinical workflows, with following key applications: (1) Surgical planning optimization: Preoperative identification of LD-type ICC via the model (associated with worse OS) supports more aggressive surgical approaches, such as extended hepatectomy with lymph node dissection and biliary reconstruction, as recommended for this subtype. Conversely, predicted SD-type ICC or pHCC may require less extensive resection, reducing perioperative risks. (2) Prognostic risk stratification: Given the model’s correlation with OS, it can inform postoperative management. Patients predicted to have LD-type ICC may benefit from early adjuvant therapy or closer surveillance, while those with SD-type ICC or pHCC could follow standard monitoring protocols. These applications leverage the model’s interpretability and reliance on readily available features (AFP, HBsAg, CEUS findings), making it feasible for routine clinical use.

There were several limitations of this study. First, this was a single-center retrospective cohort study. Although this study was conducted at a high-volume center with CEUS, subspecialty expertise in liver CEUS may have introduced inherent biases in the CEUS examination and image analysis. This may limit the generalizability of our findings to other institutions with different imaging protocols or clinical practices. Second, while an internal test set was employed to evaluate the model’s ability to generalize, no systematic external validation was conducted. This lack of external validation restricts the model’s generalizability. To address this, future research will collaborate with multi-center institutions to collect external validation cohorts to verify stability, with potential recalibration of key feature thresholds based on external data. Third, the sample size in this study is limited, particularly due to the limited number of cases of LD-type ICC and pHCC. Thus, our results should be viewed with caution. Future studies will expand these cases to enhance subtype differentiation. Fourth, since we only included patients with ICC who underwent surgical resection, inoperable patients with advanced disease were not included in our study, which may introduce biases. Resectable tumors often have distinct biological features (e.g., less aggressive invasion or metastasis) compared to unresectable ones, potentially leading to an overrepresentation of early-stage disease and limiting the model’s applicability to advanced cases that may demonstrate different CEUS characteristics. Thus, future research will include inoperable advanced patients to verify the model’s performance, with potential development of advanced-case subtype models if needed.

In conclusion, we have developed and validated a practical decision tree model using five clinicoradiological features to differentiate ICC subtypes and pHCC, which could inform clinical decision-making. However, external validation across multi-center cohorts and inclusion of unresectable cases are necessary to confirm its generalizability. Future studies should also explore adjustments to the model based on diverse patient populations to enhance its clinical utility.
