Frailty in Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension: A Cohort Study
Authors: Gordon J Farley, Melanie Prakash, Harold I Palevsky, K. Akaya Smith, Jasleen K Minhas, Jason S Fritz, Steven C Pugliese, Rui Feng, Nadine Al-Naamani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rationale:Frailty is associated with increased morbidity and mortality. Patients with pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) are at a high risk of frailty; however, frailty has been understudied in PAH.Objectives:Understand the risk factors for and determine the impact of frailty on quality of life (QoL) and mortality in patients with PAH.Methods:A prospective single center cohort enrolled 80 patients with PAH. Demographic, metabolic, hemodynamic, and QoL data were collected, and frailty was assessed via the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) at baseline, 6-months and 12-months. Frailty or pre-frailty was defined as an SPPB score of ≤ 9.Measurements and Main Results:In this cohort of patients with PAH, 49% were frail. Mean age was 57.6 years, 82.5% were female, and idiopathic (33.8%) and connective tissue disease associated (32.5%) PAH were the most common etiologies. Compared to non-frail patients, frail participants were significantly older, had greater mortality risk, and had significantly increased fat mass index, android-to-gynoid ratio, and visceral adipose tissue area. Higher levels of IL-6 were significantly associated with odds of frailty, although IL-6 did not mediate the association of body fat on frailty. Frail patients had worse QoL and worse transplant-free survival compared to non-frail patients. The percentage of patients with frailty increased over 1 year from 54.2% to 64.4%.Conclusions:Frailty was prevalent in this cohort of patients with PAH and was associated with older age, higher risk for mortality and body fat. Frailty in patients with PAH was associated with worse QoL and worse survival.

Introduction

Frailty is a measurable clinical syndrome that leads to functional decline and increased risk for disability, falls, hospitalizations, and mortality.(1, 2) First described in the elderly, frailty is increasingly recognized in individuals with chronic illness.(3) Frailty is a dynamic process with a transitional state predisposing to frailty, known as pre-frailty.(4) Frailty is characterized by weight loss, weakness, and loss of independence, leading to a state of vulnerability arising from accumulation of clinical and psychosocial deficits.(3, 5) While weight loss is considered a cardinal manifestation of frailty, from a body composition standpoint, obesity has also been identified as a risk factor for both the development of frailty, as well as for progression along the frailty spectrum.(6) In patients undergoing lung transplant evaluation, frailty has been shown to be an independent risk factor for disability and waitlist mortality.(7-9)

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a rare progressive disease of the pulmonary vasculature associated with a high degree of morbidity, mortality, and healthcare economic burden due to medication costs, high readmission rates and prolonged length of stays.(10) Frailty can be assessed by the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), a commonly used tool in the evaluation of lung transplantation candidacy(11), or frailty can be quantified by the cumulative number of health deficits affecting an individual(5). As such, with an increase in age at time of diagnosis of PAH, increased prevalence of obesity, and the high morbidity of the disease, the PAH population is at high risk of frailty.(12-15) However, the prevalence and impact of frailty in PAH remain largely unexplored as highlighted by a recent review.(16) In a cohort study of patients who underwent lung transplantation, patients with PAH were more likely to have persistent physical frailty six months post-lung transplantation as compared to patients who had other lung diseases.(17) In hospitalized patients with PAH, frail patients, as assessed via cumulative deficit frailty index, had increased length of stay but no difference in one year mortality.(11) Obesity has been shown to be independently associated with pulmonary hypertension and has been shown to be associated with worse functional class, decreased exercise capacity, and worse quality of life (QoL) in PAH patients.(13, 15) Within this growing understanding, the intersection of frailty, body composition, and PAH is relatively unexplored.

To understand the risk factors for physical frailty as measured by the SPPB in patients with PAH, we conducted a prospective single center cohort study that enrolled incident and prevalent patients with PAH. We hypothesized that older age, female sex, and increased inflammation in the setting of obesity would be associated with a higher probability of frailty. Further, we hypothesized that frailty would be associated with worse QoL and survival in PAH, independent of other factors.

Methods

Study Population

The Fat in Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension (FAIRY) study is a prospective cohort of adult patients with PAH enrolled between 2018 and 2023. Patients presenting to the Penn Medicine Pulmonary Hypertension clinics with a diagnosis of PAH were screened for participation. Patients with PAH confirmed by right heart catheterization prior to enrollment with a mean pulmonary artery pressure ≥ 25 mm Hg, a pulmonary vascular resistance > 3 Wood units, and a pulmonary artery wedge pressure ≤ 15 mm Hg and who were on stable PAH therapy for three months, were approached for consent and invited to participate in the study. We excluded patients who had evidence of moderate or severe obstructive or restrictive ventilatory defects by pulmonary function tests, patients who had more than moderate aortic or mitral valve disease, or patients who had a left ventricular ejection fraction < 40%. The institutional review board approved this study (#832197), and informed consent was obtained.

Data collection

After informed consent, participants were scheduled for a baseline visit which included medical history, anthropometrics, vital signs, phlebotomy, echocardiography, SPPB assessment, emPHasis-10 (E10) questionnaire completion, and a whole-body dual-energy absorptiometry (DXA) scan. Patients were asked not to eat or drink, except for water, as well as to avoid heavy exercise for twelve hours before the study visit. Participants returned at 6 and 12 months for follow up visits with repeat interval testing.

SPPB

The SPPB calculates an aggregate score ranging from 0 to 12 based on a series of tests including: chair stands, balance, and gait speed. Lower scores indicate greater frailty. We used SPPB as a binary (≤9 to indicate frail/pre-frail status) and as a continuous scale.(18, 19)

Body Composition

A whole body DXA scan was performed to determine body fat measurements and body fat distribution.(20, 21) The fat mass index (kg/m2) was calculated as the total fat mass measured by DXA (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Visceral adipose tissue area was estimated from the fat mass located in the abdominal area after subcutaneous fat has been removed.

Quality of Life

The E10 questionnaire is a pulmonary hypertension-specific QoL instrument that is scored on a scale of 0 to 50 with higher scores indicating worse QoL.(22)

Echocardiogram

Echocardiograms were performed based on a research-grade protocol and all were interpreted according to the Echocardiography guidelines(23) by the Penn Echo Core blinded to the frailty status or other clinical variables.

Socioeconomic status (SES)

Poverty and the poverty transition have been shown to be an independent risk factors for frailty, particularly in older populations(24, 25). Using the participants zip code, we utilized the National Neighborhood Data Archive(26) to determine 1) the poverty level indicating the proportion of families with income in the past 12 months below the federal poverty level and 2) the neighborhood affluence indicating the proportion of households with income greater than $75,000.

REVEAL Lite-2

We calculated the Registry to Evaluate Early and Long-term pulmonary arterial hypertension disease management (REVEAL) Lite-2 score(31) for participants using NT-pro-B type natriuretic peptide, 6-minute walk distance, World Health Organization functional class, systolic blood pressure, heart rate and the estimated glomerular filtration rate. Participants were classified as low risk if total score was ≤ 5, intermediate risk if total score was 6 or 7, and high risk if total score was ≥ 8.

Phlebotomy was performed and serum and plasma samples were processed and stored at −80°C. The Human Metabolic Combo 1 panel, a panel of ten metabolic biomarkers, was measured using the Meso Scale Discovery Multiplex electroluminescence assay (Catalog No. K15281K, Meso Scale Diagnostics, Rockville, MD). Analysis was performed on seven of the ten biomarkers. The assays for Ghrelin, GIP, and GLP1 did not have standard. Therefore, analysis was not performed on these three biomarkers.

Follow-up

Of the 80 participants, 71 completed the 6-months follow-up visit and 64 participants completed the final 12-months study visit. The reasons for not completing study procedures were the COVID-19 pandemic and patient preference for remote visit (n=10) followed by missed study visits due to COVID-19 related shutdowns (n=4), and relocation out of state (n=2). Patients continued to be followed for their clinical care at our center, and their status was recorded at the time of lung transplantation, death or March 1, 2025, whichever occurred first. Patients who underwent lung transplantation after the completion of the 12-months study assessments (n=4) were censored alive at the time of the transplant. The vital status of all patients was known and accounted for at the end of follow-up, including those patients who were did not complete study visits and assessments.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as median and first- and third- quartiles (Q1, Q3). Categorical variables were summarized as counts and proportions. We computed marginal effects to determine the change in the predicted probability of frailty associated with a 1 unit change in a factor. We used logistic regression to assess the association between frailty and predictors adjusting models for a priori selected potential confounders: age, sex, PAH etiology, employment status, relationship status, right ventricular dysfunction, and REVEAL Lite 2 risk score.(27) Generalized additive models with loess smoothing functions were used to demonstrate the nonlinear association between body composition measures and odds of frailty. We also performed mediation analyses to explore whether metabolic biomarkers mediated the association between body composition and frailty using nonparametric bootstrapping estimation methods using the mediation package in R. We then used linear regression models to assess the association between frailty and quality of life. We similarly adjusted models for a priori selected potential confounders: age, sex, PAH etiology, fat mass index, and REVEAL Lite 2 risk score. Fat mass index and REVEAL Lite 2 scores were included in the model despite the potential for being on the causal pathway to account for their complex association with QOL and the potential bidirectional association.(28)

We then fit mixed-effects generalized linear regression models and logistic regression models with random intercepts to account for the repeated measures of frailty and body composition. We adjusted these models for the same variables. We constructed a Sankey Diagram to visualize the status of frailty over a one-year period. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for survival by frailty status, adjusting for age, sex, etiology of PAH and fat mass index. The baseline for the time to event models was the data of study enrollment and initial frailty assessment. We then determined frailty status at the 12-months visit and classified patients as: always non-frail, recovered frailty, newly frail, and always frail. Finally, we used a Kaplan-Meier plot to demonstrate survival by frailty status from Visit 12-months to end of follow-up for exploratory analysis. All patients who underwent lung transplantation completed the one-year assessment prior to their transplant (n=4) and were censored as alive at the time of transplantation. We did not impute missing variables or outcomes. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.1).

Results

Baseline Demographics

The study sample consisted of 80 patients diagnosed with PAH. Within this cohort of patients living with PAH, approximately half (n =39, 49%) were frail or pre-frail (Table 1). Idiopathic and connective tissue disease-associated PAH (predominantly systemic sclerosis) were the most common etiologies in both groups. Frail/pre-frail participants were older than non-frail participants (median age, interquartile range [Q1, Q3] 62.9 [48.5, 69.7] vs 52.7 [39.9, 60.1]). Increasing age was significantly associated with increasing frailty (average marginal effect (AME) 0.010 (95% CI 0.004, 0.017) per 1 year increase in age). Patients with frailty had higher BMI as compared to non-frail patients (AME 0.019 (95% CI 0.004, 0.035) per 1 kg/m2 increase in BMI). There were no differences in sex assignment at birth, race and ethnicity, etiology of PAH, relationship status, PAH medication regimen, or co-morbid conditions between the two groups. Compared to non-frail participants, frail/pre-frail participants were more likely to have Medicare insurance than private insurance (AME 0.336 (95% CI 0.114, 0.559)). Compared to non-frail participants, frail/pre-frail patients were also less likely to be employed or have a disability as compared to being retired (AME −0.375 (95% CI −0.669, −0.081) and −0.436 (95% CI −0.667, −0.204), respectively). Neither SES status by zip code nor the participant’s relationship status were associated with frailty. Frail/pre-frail participants were more likely to have advanced WHO functional class III/IV and shorter 6-minute walk distances than non-frail participants (AME 0.320 (95% CI 0.167, 0.473) and −0.002 (95% CI −0.003, −0.001) per 1 m increase, respectively). Frail/pre-frail participants were also more likely to be at intermediate (AME 0.533 (95% CI 0.316, 0.751) or high risk (AME 0.467 (95% CI 0.208, 0.725) of 1-year mortality as determined by REVEAL Lite-2 compared to non-frail participants. Frail/pre-frail participants had significant differences in body composition, including increased fat mass index (AME 0.030 (95% CI, 0.009, 0.051)), increased android to gynoid ratio (AME 0.740 (95% CI 0.098, 1.382)), and increased visceral adipose tissue area (AME 0.002 (95% CI, 0.0003, 0.004)).

Predictors of frailty

After multivariable adjustment, body composition metrics remained significantly associated with odds of frailty: adjusted OR (aOR) per 1 kg/m2 increase in BMI 1.23, 95% CI 1.08-1.47, aOR per 1 kg/m2 increase in fat mass index 1.44, 95% CI 1.17-1.94 (Table 2, Figure 1A), aOR per 0.1 unit increase in the android to gynoid ratio 3.81, 95% CI 1.90-10.01 (Figure 1B), and aOR per 10 cm2 increase in visceral adipose tissue area 1.28, 95% CI 1.10-1.57 (Figure 1C). These associations persisted over 1 year follow-up (Table 3).

Biomarkers and Frailty

In an unadjusted model, increased levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6) (OR 2.68; 95% CI 1.35-5.87, Table 5), C-reactive protein (CRP) (OR 5.27; 95% CI 1.75-18.19), fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF-21) (OR 1.90; 95% CI 1.08-3.52), and fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF-23) (OR 18.76; 95% CI 3.33-143.16) were significantly associated with increased odds of frailty. After multivariate adjustments, increasing odds of frailty only remained significantly associated with increasing levels of IL-6 (aOR 3.42; 95% CI 1.14-12.22, Table 3). However, circulating IL-6 levels did not appear to mediate the association between fat mass index, android to gynoid ratio or visceral adipose tissue area and the odds of frailty (proportion mediated 2.5%, 6.3%, and 4.1%, respectively, p>0.5 for all).

Association of frailty on Quality of Life (QoL)

Frail/pre-frail status was associated with clinically significant higher E10 scores (worse QoL) at baseline (β=6.8, standard error=2.4, p=0.006). Over a one-year follow-up period, patients with frailty had worse QoL as compared to non-frail patients even after adjustment for age, sex, PAH, etiology, fat mass index, and REVEAL Lite 2 risk score (β=2.70, p=0.038, Table 5, Figure 2).

Tracking changes in frailty longitudinally

Seventy-one participants (89%) completed the 6-months visit and 64 participants (80%) completed the 12-months visit. Frailty status was noted to be dynamic, with some patients improving from a frail/pre-frail status to a non-frail status. Twenty-four participants (37.5%) were non-frail throughout, 12 (18.8%) became non-frail, 13 (20.3%) became frail, and 15 (23.4%) remained frail for the duration of the study. We also observed the following baseline median frailty scores by frailty status: always non-frail 11.0 [10.0-12.0], resolved frailty 9.0 [8.0-9.0], newly frail status 10.0 [10.0-11.0], always frail 8.0 [7.0-8.5]. While we do not have access to concomitant patient specific factors that may have contributed to improvement in frailty status, such as use of outpatient physical therapy our pulmonary rehabilitation programs, over a one-year follow-up period, the percent of patients with frailty or pre-frailty increased from 54.2% to 64.4% (Figure 3). Baseline description characteristics of patients by final frailty status can be found in Supplemental Table 1.

Relationship between Frailty and Transplant-Free Survival

Participants were followed up for a median of 3.0 years [2.1, 4.3 years] and there was a total of 17 deaths in the cohort (13 frail/pre-frail and 4 non-frail). Frail/pre-frail status at baseline was associated with 3-fold increased risk of death (HR 3.04, 95% CI 1.18-7.86), even after adjustment for age, sex, etiology of PAH, and fat mass index (HR 3.55, 95%CI 1.21-10.42). Further adjusting for disease severity demonstrated increased risk of death although not statistically significant (HR 2.09, 95% CI 0.56-7.74). Patients who remained frail or became frail over the course of the study period were at increased risk of death (Figure 4). All patients in our cohort who underwent lung transplantation (n=4) completed their respective one-year assessment prior to transplantation.

Discussion

In this prospective study of patients with PAH, we found that physical frailty was highly prevalent with about half of patients classified as frail or pre-frail. Older age, higher mortality risk scoring, and greater body fat, particularly visceral adipose tissue, were associated with greater odds of frailty. Frailty in patients with PAH was also associated with worse QoL over time and associated with worse survival.

The prevalence of frailty among community dwelling individuals is estimated to be around 10.7-15%.(29, 30) In this cohort of patients living with PAH, the prevalence of frailty or pre-frailty was 49% similar to rates of frailty in hospitalized patients with PAH.(11) This is not surprising given that PAH is a cardiopulmonary disease with significant impairment in physical activity and is commonly associated with other co-morbidities, such as connective tissue disease, that can also increase the risk of frailty. Consistent with previous literature(1), odds of frailty were significantly associated with increasing age. While prior studies have identified that women are at increased risk of frailty compared to men(1), our study did not detect a statistically significant difference between sexes, perhaps due to the smaller representation of male sex in a female predominant disease. Surprisingly, etiology of PAH was not associated with frailty in our cohort. One possible hypothesis could be that the cardiorespiratory impairments leading to physical activity limitations have a greater contribution to the development of frailty than any other associated condition. While we observed association between some social determinants of health and frailty on univariate analysis, such as with employment and relationship status and Medicare insurance, these associations were not significant after multivariable adjustment, likely due to confounding with age.

Unique body composition metrics, including both baseline obesity and progressive weight loss, have been shown to increase the risk of frailty in adults.(6, 31) Interestingly, in this study, there was a significant association between frailty and body composition metrics, including each of BMI, fat mass index, android to gynoid ratio, and visceral adipose tissue, independently. As compared to BMI, fat mass index, a more precise measure of body fat and a better predictor of metabolic syndrome(32), as well as the android to gynoid ratio, a measure of more central adiposity, had stronger associations with frailty. We also identified visceral adipose tissue area to be significantly associated with frailty in patients with PAH. Previous studies have similarly identified regional visceral fat deposition as more metabolically active than subcutaneous tissue(33) with an association with systemic inflammation, as evidenced by increased IL-6 levels.(34) IL-6 has been implicated in acute and chronic inflammation, autoimmune disease, and bone resorption.(35, 36) While we also identified higher IL-6 levels in our frail/pre-frail patients, it is less clear that IL-6 mediated the association between visceral adipose tissue area and frailty, but our study may have been underpowered to detect such a relationship. A recent study also identified an association between BMI and inflammation as measured by C-reactive protein (CRP); however, BMI and CRP were associated with diverging clinical outcomes(37), also suggesting that inflammation is not the only factor influencing outcomes with obesity. An alternative potential explanation of this association between frailty and obesity may be that the worse functional status associated with frailty may lead to more sedentary lifestyles and ultimately increased visceral adiposity. Inflammation has been shown to drive cellular senescence especially in aging and leads to muscle atrophy and endothelial damage.(38) The pro-inflammatory state resulting from increased visceral adiposity, and the resultant cellular senescence, may represent one of the key drivers of the frailty condition.

Frailty status is a dynamic state and is potentially reversible. Interventions such as physical activity, exercise training, nutritional counseling, protein-calorie supplementation, psychotherapy and cognitive behavioral therapy are effective interventions to improve physical frailty.(39-41) Following hip fracture in older individuals, resistance exercise training in frail individuals led to a decrease in hospitalizations and nursing home placement.(42) Our study similarly suggests that frailty status is modifiable. We observed almost a fifth of patients reversed their frailty status by the end of the study. It is possible that improvement in PAH disease status allows for increased mobility leading to improvement in frailty status. Noting small sample size and lack of insight into patient participation in pulmonary rehabilitation programs, our study did not identify specific clinical patterns in patients who improved their frailty status during the study period.

Frailty is common in a variety of pulmonary conditions including end-stage lung disease, interstitial lung disease and COPD.(17, 43) Frailty is independently associated with all-cause mortality before and after transplantation in end stage lung disease.(17, 44) Additionally, claims-based frailty index scores, a novel frailty index model, have been shown to be predictive of PAH hospitalizations and pulmonary hypertension mortality, respectively.(45) Our study is in agreement with this emerging literature in describing a negative impact of frailty on PAH outcomes. In our cohort, patients with a resolved frailty status had improved survival compared to those who were always frail, a relationship which was no longer statistically significant with additional adjustment for disease severity but this is likely limited by statistical power in a relatively small cohort with a small number of events. As the drivers of the frailty condition continue to become more apparent, future efforts should target both frail patients and pre-frail patients who are at risk of frailty given the potential for improvement and possibly even resolution of this condition.

Frailty status may represent another indicator in the growing understanding of disease severity and prognostication within PAH. In this cohort, frailty was directly associated with increased REVEAL Lite 2 risk score and WHO functional class. Accordingly, there was an inverse relationship between frailty and 6-minute walk test distance. Higher risk status is associated with worse QoL(46); from this perspective, frailty may represent an indicator of worsening disease severity negatively impacting patients’ QoL. While frailty may represent another marker of disease and could potentially be useful in prognostication in PAH, the impact of frailty on patient quality of life is more certain.

There are several strengths to our study. To our knowledge, this is the first study to prospectively examine frailty in PAH using a validated physical frailty measure assessed several times over a year. A unique strength of this study is its examination of body composition metrics to further elucidate key drivers of the frailty phenotype. While whole body DXA is not part of routine clinical care for patients with PAH, measurement of visceral adipose tissue is possible on other imaging modalities such as CT scans and MRIs.(47-49) However, there were some limitations. This was a small single center study, and larger multi-center studies are required to validate our findings. The small sample size limited may have limited our ability to detect significant mediation effects of inflammation; however, our findings should be considered as hypothesis generating. However, the demographics of our PAH patient population are representative of the national PAH population. Patients with borderline PAH (mPAP between 20 and 25 mm Hg) were excluded from the current study as we focused on enrolling patients on stable PAH therapy. However, this focus on a sicker patient population also necessitates future investigations into the relationship between frailty and early disease manifestations of PAH. While some patients in our cohort were lost to follow-up during the COVID-19 pandemic and clinical research shut-downs, we had complete vital status ascertainment on all participants at the end of the study period. A small number of deaths during the study period made for preliminary inferences regarding outcomes, and we specifically note the exploratory analysis of survival by frailty status at end of follow-up.

Frailty and pre-frailty are highly prevalent in patients with PAH. Our study adds to the growing understanding of visceral adiposity and its relationship to systemic inflammation and the frailty condition. Frailty is associated with worse QoL and with decreased survival in patients with PAH. Future investigations should assess whether frailty reversal through physical activity prescriptions and nutritional counseling would be associated with improved outcomes in patients with PAH.
