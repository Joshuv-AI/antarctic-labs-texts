Early exposure: rising assault-related firearm injuries in school-aged children during COVID years
Authors: Chan Shen, Ning Li, John S Oh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFirearm injuries have become a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among pediatric patients in the United States, with the COVID-19 pandemic exacerbating this alarming trend. However, a knowledge gap persists regarding firearm injury trends and intent by age group. This study analyzes fatal and non-fatal pediatric firearm injuries using data from the Trauma Quality Programs (TQP) Participant Use File (PUF) from 2017 to 2022. Cases were categorized into four age groups: 0–5 years, 6–11 years, 12–14 years, and 15–17 years.ResultsOf the 32,931 cases identified, 12,647 occurred pre-COVID (2017–2019), while 20,284 occurred during the COVID period (2020–2022). A significant increase in both injury cases and mortality was observed across all age groups, with adolescents aged 15–17 years holding the largest proportion. Intent of injury varied markedly by age: unintentional injuries predominated in the youngest group, whereas assaults were the leading cause among all other age groups. Notably, assault-related injuries in the 12–14-year-old group surged during the COVID period and remained elevated through 2022, underscoring an urgent need for targeted interventions. These findings highlight critical age-specific differences in firearm injury patterns and emphasize the importance of tailored public health strategies.Keywords: Firearm injuries, Pediatric population, Trauma, COVID-19

Introduction

Firearm-related injury and mortality has become a primary public health crisis in the United States. In fact, 83.7% of all firearm deaths in high-income countries occurred in the U.S., where the firearm death rate was 11.4 times higher than peer nations [1]. Alarmingly, children and adolescents are particularly vulnerable to firearm violence. The CDC reported an increase in firearm-related deaths among children and youth from 2000 to 2020, [2, 3] during which firearm injuries surpassed motor vehicle crashes as the leading cause of pediatric mortality in the U.S. Exposure to firearm violence among pediatric populations is often shaped by a complex interplay of mental, developmental, social, and environmental factors, [4, 5] all of which were exacerbated by COVID-19-related disruptions such as lockdowns and school closures. However, whether pediatric firearm violence worsened during the pandemic remains understudied. Existing literature provides sparse evidence on how patterns of firearm injury and mortality may vary across pediatric age groups. Understanding these trends and age-specific differences is crucial for identifying the unique vulnerabilities of children and adolescents at different developmental stages and for informing targeted policy responses to mitigate firearm epidemic.

We aimed to study trends in pediatric firearm violence by comparing the pre-pandemic period to the COVID-19 era. We stratified our analysis by age group and by intent of firearm injury. We hypothesized that the onset of COVID-19 exacerbated the prevalence of firearm-related injuries and mortalities across all pediatric age groups, with adolescents facing a particularly heightened risk of interpersonal firearm assault.

Main text

We identified cases involving children aged zero to seventeen years with firearm injury as the mechanism of injury from the Trauma Quality Programs (TQP) Participant Use File (PUF) of the American College of Surgeons (ACS) for the years 2017 to 2022. The TQP PUF is the largest trauma registry in the U.S. and is widely accepted as a nationally representative data source for trauma research, capturing over 6.5 million trauma cases from participating trauma centers during the study period [6]. In the TQP PUF, intent of injury is defined using external cause-of-injury codes (E-codes) from the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes [7]. We stratified the cases into four age groups: 0–5 years (early childhood), 6–11 years (middle childhood), 12–14 years (pre-teen), and 15–17 years (teenager) [8].

A descriptive analysis was conducted to compare demographic, clinical, and hospital characteristics of pediatric with firearm-related injuries between the pre-COVID (2017–2019) and COVID (2020–2022) periods. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and compared between periods using Chi-square tests. Continuous variables (Injury Severity Score [ISS] and total Glasgow Coma Scale [GCS]) were reported as means and standard deviations, and compared using equal variance two-sample t-tests. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Table 1 shows some significant differences in patient characteristics between the pre-COVID and COVID periods. Distribution of age groups shifted slightly post-COVID, with a modest increase in the proportion of children aged 0–5 and 12–14, and a decrease in those aged 6–11 and 15–17 (p = 0.007). The proportion of female patients increased modestly from 16.0 to 16.8% (p = 0.047). There were significant shifts in racial/ethnic composition (p < 0.001), including an increase in the proportion of Non-Hispanic Black patients (from 56.7 to 61.7%) and a decrease in Non-Hispanic White patients (from 20.7 to 15.6%). A significantly higher proportion of patients were covered by Medicaid post-COVID (67.5% vs. 60.1%), with a corresponding decrease in private insurance and self-pay (p < 0.001). Injury ISS and total GCS were similar across periods, with no significant differences (p = 0.600 and p = 0.152, respectively); and overall mortality rates were not statistically different between the two periods (12.0% pre-COVID vs. 11.5% post-COVID, p = 0.195).

Figure 1a presents a time-trend analysis of the total number of pediatric firearm injury cases from 2017 to 2022, stratified by age categories: 0–5 years, 6–11 years, 12–14 years, and 15–17 years. The graph uses line plots to illustrate changes over time in the absolute number of cases within each age group, allowing for visualization of both temporal trends and age-specific burden. A substantial rise in firearm injury cases is observed across all pediatric age groups beginning in 2020, coinciding with the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Adolescents aged 15–17 consistently represented the largest proportion of cases across all years. Their numbers increased sharply from 3092 in 2019 to 4372 in 2020, continuing to rise to 5024 by 2022. Cases in the 12–14 age group also rose significantly—from 716 in 2019 to 1018 in 2020, reaching 1262 in 2022—indicating an increasing burden in early adolescence. Figure 1b displays firearm-related deaths among pediatric patients from 2017 to 2022, stratified by age categories. There was a consistent and notable rise in firearm-related deaths among all pediatric age groups since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020. Adolescents aged 15–17 experienced the greatest number of deaths each year. Mortality rose from 345 deaths in 2019 to 466 in 2020, peaking at 560 deaths in 2022—a 62% increase over this period. Deaths in the 12–14 age group also increased substantially—from 90 in 2019 to 136 in 2021 and 135 in 2022—demonstrating a growing impact of firearm injuries in early adolescence, which is a concerning trend.

Discussion

Our study is one of the first to examine age-specific trends and patterns of pediatric firearm injury and mortality in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. We found that the incidence of firearm related injuries and deaths increased sharply following the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 across all pediatric age groups and remained elevated through 2022. These findings are consistent with a growing body of research documenting heightened firearm violence among children during the pandemic [5, 9, 10]. For example, a multi-institutional study reported nearly a twofold rise in pediatric gunshot wounds following the 2020 shelter-in-place order (3.3%) compared to the 2017–2019 period (1.8%) [11]. Another nationwide cross-sectional study found a marked surge in gun-related deaths among children aged 0–17 during the pandemic compared to pre-pandemic trends, estimating an additional 1.12 child deaths per day due to gun violence since mid-March 2020 [12]. Analysis of CDC Wide-ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (WONDER) also revealed a 41.6% increase in pediatric firearm death rates between 2018 and 2021, with an 8.8% increase from 2020 to 2021 alone [13]. Our results corroborate this sharp rise in pediatric firearm injuries and deaths during the pandemic and further demonstrate that this concerning trend persisted into 2022.

Beyond confirming this troubling upward trajectory, our study further explored age-specific variations, underscoring the widespread impact of this public health crisis on both children and adolescents across all developmental stages. Notably, the rise in firearm injuries and deaths was most pronounced among teenagers aged 15–17, who accounted for the majority of the increase in fatal and nonfatal firearm incidents. This group had already sustained the highest proportion (76%) of pediatric firearm injuries between 2008 and 2019, [14] and our analysis revealed an alarming increase in their injury and mortality rates during the pandemic.

Our study found that assault was the most common intent of firearm injury across all pediatric age groups, except children aged 0–5 among whom unintentional shooting was the leading cause. This finding is partially consistent with prior analyses, which reported that assault accounted for the majority of firearm injuries in older children, whereas unintentional injuries were more prevalent in younger children [15, 16]. However, unlike much of the existing literature—which often compared pediatric populations between below and above 12 years old in broad age-group lens, [17]—our study leveraged a more nuanced categorization of age groups, allowing us to identify a previously overlooked risk of assault-related firearm injuries among children aged 6–11. This more nuanced breakdown revealed a troubling shift: the age of assault victimization in pediatric populations is lowering. Supporting this concern, a recent national-level analysis combining fatal and nonfatal firearm injury data suggested that assault was the leading intent of firearm-related injury and death across all pediatric age groups. Notably, unintentional injuries only slightly outpaced assault in nonfatal cases among children aged 0–5 and 6–11 [14]. Together with emerging evidence, our findings highlight a disturbing trend of assault-related firearm violence affecting increasingly younger children. This underscores the urgent need for development of tailored policy interventions to curb the spread of assault-related firearm injuries in younger children.

In our study, the proportion of firearm injuries resulting from assault increased with age, with adolescents aged 15–17 experiencing the highest share of assault-related firearm trauma. It reflects not only the dominance of assault intent in this age group but also a faster-growing trend in assault related firearm injuries over time. An age-stratified analysis suggested that the 15–17 age group faced a six-fold higher incidence of firearm injury and death from assault than 12–14 preteens, and this group also exhibited the steepest upward trend in both fatal and nonfatal firearm injuries after 2014 compared to other pediatric age groups [14]. Markedly, our study also identified a persistent rise in firearm assaults among 12–14-year-olds since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, indicating a potential demographic shift in the age pattern and context of firearm violence. Whether preteens are now facing heightened risk of exposure to or involvement in firearm assaults and what underlying social and structural factors may be driving this shift remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

The dramatic rise in firearm injuries and deaths during the COVID-19 pandemic may be partly attributed to increased gun ownership among family members such as parents and guardians [18, 19], as well as the worsening of mental health distress and substance use—both of which were exacerbated during this period. Compared to other developed countries, the United States has long maintained a significantly higher rate of owning guns [20], with 120.5 civilian firearms held by Americans in 2017 [21], contributing to the country’s disproportionately high rates of firearm related injuries and deaths relative to other OECD countries [1, 22]. Meanwhile, the social and political unrest felt during the pandemic further fueled this trend, with private gun purchases rising markedly [23]. One cross-sectional study using data from the Gun Violence Archive (GVA) reported that new firearm acquisitions increased from an average of 21.28 per 100,000 persons before COVID to 33.12 during the COVID period [19]. Many gun owners fail to adhere to firearm safety guidelines, [24] leaving their guns unlocked and loaded in the home where their children are surrounded but less supervised [15]. Therefore, it may be advisable to educate gun owners of safety issues and implement restrictive firearm purchase laws on a large scale. Research has shown that states with stronger gun control policies tend to have significantly lower firearm death rates compared to those with minimal or no legislative oversight [25]. In addition to policy reform, firearm reduction efforts should include practical interventions such as distributing gun locks and safes and facilitating community-based education programs [26].

Beyond the broader rise in firearm availability, the increasing burden of assault-related firearm violence among younger children may be linked to intra-household dynamics. Prior research shows that most firearm injuries and deaths in this age group occur in domestic settings, [27] often involving a parent or a parent’s intimate partner during episodes of family conflict [15]. These risks may have been exacerbated during the pandemic by economic insecurity, psychological stress, and substance use [28, 29]. Lockdowns and school closures further limited children’s access to external resources and disrupted community-based interventions that might otherwise have mitigated household risks [30]. Coupled with the surge in firearm purchases during this period, these conditions likely heightened the vulnerability of younger children to firearm-related assaults.

Our age-stratified findings highlight the importance of age-specific policies and interventions. For younger children, a safety net involving teachers, social workers, and healthcare professionals is essential to identify household risks early and reduce exposure to home violence. In contrast, adolescents are more frequently involved in firearm assaults linked to peer conflicts, gang activities, and, in some cases, drug-related activities [15]. Addressing firearm violence in this group requires multifaceted strategies. In addition to enforcing safe firearm storage and access regulations, policies that target illicit drug distribution, expand harm reduction initiatives, and provide community-based youth violence prevention programs may help reduce adolescents’ involvement in firearm-related assaults. Our analyses also carry important implications for trauma center preparedness. Given the differing clinical characteristics of pediatric firearm injuries across age groups [27], trauma centers should consider adapting strategies in triage, trauma-informed care, and resource allocation to better respond to the evolving patterns and needs associated with pediatric firearm violence.

Limitations

The findings of our study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the data were derived from trauma centers that voluntarily participate in the Trauma Quality Programs (TQP), which may not fully represent the full landscape of pediatric firearm injuries and deaths for all regions in the U.S., especially for areas lacking trauma-specific services. Second, the study captures only cases involving children and adolescents who were treated in ER or hospitalized, as the data do not contain firearm related injuries or deaths that did not result in transport to a trauma center. Given that a substantial proportion of pediatric firearm fatalities occur at the scene,[27] there is a pressing need for more comprehensive research that integrates data from both trauma centers and other sources such as medical examiner records and law enforcement reports. Third, our dataset lacks detailed information about the context and environment in which firearm injuries occurred. Additionally, the classification of injury intent relies on ICD-10-CM external cause codes, which are susceptible to misclassification [31], especially in pediatric cases where the child may be unable to clearly communicate the circumstances of the injury, or when caregivers intentionally withhold or misrepresent information. Moreover, these codes do not capture the complex sociocultural and environmental factors that shape the context and underlying intent of firearm injuries. Understanding how these contextual factors vary across pediatric developmental stages is paramount to developing effective, age-specific firearm prevention and intervention initiatives.
