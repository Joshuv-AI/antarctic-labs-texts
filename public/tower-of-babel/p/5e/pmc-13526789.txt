Hazard-portfolio patterns in US food recall severity reveal transferable pathogen signals and firm-specific compliance signals
Authors: Juk-Sen Tang, Peilun Li, Junhong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe U.S. FDA classifies food recalls into three severity tiers (Class I / II / III), a decision that drives public notification urgency and regulatory resource allocation. Using 28,448 openFDA enforcement records (2012–2025), we investigate the structural determinants of recall severity. Under standard evaluation (Known-firm scenario), a simple baseline assigning each firm its historically most frequent severity class achieves 92% of the best machine learning model’s performance (Macro-F1 = 0.818 vs. 0.893). Conditional mutual information analysis reveals that this firm-level concentration is driven primarily by stable hazard-product portfolios within firms: hazard category mediates 27–63% of the firm–severity association (across estimator methods and hazard granularities), with a robust firm-intrinsic residual of at least 37% under all specifications. We refer to this as hazard-portfolio-dominated firm-level patterns with non-negligible firm-intrinsic residual. To disentangle universal from firm-specific severity drivers, we evaluate predictive models under four regulatory scenarios ranging from triage of known firms to cold-start assessment of first-time recallers. We identify two distinct classes of food-safety signals. Hazard-intrinsic signals—particularly pathogen contamination (Salmonella, Listeria)—are universally associated with Class I severity regardless of the announcing entity, with 92% of pathogen-related Class I recalls correctly identified even for entirely unseen companies. This cross-firm transfer is robust to potential cross-announcer coupling (Class I recall changes by only -0.003 when records with explicit supply-chain markers are excluded; positive predictive value of the marker filter is 76% on manual audit). In contrast, compliance-related signals—labelling defects and GMP violations driving Class III recalls—are almost entirely firm-specific and fail to generalise across entities. Event-disjoint cross-validation ( distinct contamination events; firm-history Macro-F1 0.406, XGBoost 0.609) confirms that the XGBoost-over-firm-history increment expands from +0.075 (SKU-level) to +0.203 (95% CI [+0.123, +0.283]) under this stricter unit-of-analysis; the SKU-level figures are retained as headline values for comparability with the prior food-safety ML literature; the event-disjoint analysis is the stricter deployment-reliability diagnostic and is reported in parallel throughout. Sensitivity analyses confirm that post-classification language in the reason_for_recall field (present in 0.69% of records) does not function as a leakage signal: masking effects fall within the cross-version numerical reproducibility floor (|Δ | < 0.03). These findings have three practical implications. First, firm-level recall history is a powerful risk-profiling tool for targeted regulatory inspections, though its mechanism is hazard-portfolio rather than firm-intrinsic. Second, ML-assisted triage is reliable for pathogen-related recalls but requires mandatory expert review for compliance-related cases involving new entities. Third, previously reported ML accuracies of 90%+ on food recall databases likely overstate real-world reliability due to uncontrolled firm-level autocorrelation and SKU-level pseudoreplication, a concern relevant to both FDA and EU RASFF research. Introduction

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) classifies food recalls into three severity tiers—Class I (serious adverse health consequences or death), Class II (temporary or medically reversible effects), and Class III (unlikely to cause adverse consequences)—a decision that drives public notification urgency, regulatory resource allocation, and follow-up monitoring intensity1. The scale of this classification challenge is substantial: DeBeer et al.2 identified more than 35,000 FDA food recalls between 2002 and 2023, with annual volumes exceeding 3000 in peak years, each requiring expert evaluation by the Health Hazard Evaluation (HHE) Committee within two working days. In practice, however, documented delays of months to years between the first reported illness and formal recall announcement persist in high-profile cases3. The public health stakes are considerable: approximately 9.4 million foodborne illnesses, 55,961 hospitalisations, and 1,351 deaths occur annually in the United States4, imposing an economic burden exceeding $75 billion per year5. The FDA’s New Era of Smarter Food Safety blueprint has accordingly identified predictive analytics as a core element of modernised regulatory tools6, building on the preventive-control paradigm established by the Food Safety Modernization Act7.

Machine learning (ML) has emerged as a promising tool for automating risk assessment in food safety8,9. On the European Rapid Alert System for Food and Feed (RASFF) database, supervised classifiers have achieved accuracies ranging from 74% to 97.8% across various formulations10–12, and recent shared tasks have further demonstrated the potential of Transformer-based models for food-hazard text analysis13. Despite this progress, almost all existing studies target the EU RASFF database; to date, no peer-reviewed study has attempted systematic severity prediction on the U.S. openFDA food recall database—the largest publicly accessible food recall repository in the United States14. More fundamentally, a question that remains unexamined is whether these high reported accuracies reflect genuine learning of hazard–severity associations or, instead, the memorisation of entity-specific patterns present in regulatory databases.

This question arises because food recall databases are inherently panel data: the same firms contribute multiple records over time. A dairy processor with persistent environmental Listeria contamination will generate repeated Class I recalls; a confectionery firm with labelling compliance gaps will produce recurring Class III events. When data are split randomly into training and test sets—the prevailing practice in food-safety ML11,12—records from the same firm appear on both sides, allowing models to exploit firm-specific patterns rather than learning transferable hazard signals. Recent econometric work has formally demonstrated that standard cross-validation applied to panel data produces severely inflated performance estimates15. In clinical ML, the analogous problem—patient-level data leakage—has received extensive attention, with studies showing substantial performance inflation in histopathological image classification16 and ECG analysis17, and comprehensive taxonomies of leakage scenarios across scientific disciplines18. In food safety, however, entity-level evaluation bias has received almost no systematic attention. The only early observation is by Bouzembrak and Marvin19, who noted that accuracy dropped from 80% to 52% for unseen country–product combinations in RASFF food fraud prediction, but did not pursue a systematic decomposition.

Three gaps thus emerge. First, no study has examined the structural determinants of FDA food recall severity—specifically, the extent to which severity is driven by firm-level factors (a firm’s recall history and product portfolio) versus the intrinsic nature of the hazard itself. Second, food-safety ML research lacks the deployment reliability assessments now standard in clinical ML, where group-aware evaluation is routine. Third, no prior work has identified which food-safety signals transfer across firms (enabling generalisation to new entities) and which are firm-specific (useful only for known entities). Addressing these gaps is essential for translating ML tools from research prototypes to operationally reliable regulatory aids.

This study addresses these gaps using 28,448 openFDA enforcement records (2012–2025) through five contributions. First, we characterise the firm-level concentration structure of FDA recall severity, demonstrating that under standard evaluation a simple firm-history baseline captures 92% of the best model’s performance (Known-firm scenario), and decomposing this concentration into hazard-portfolio-mediated and firm-intrinsic components via conditional mutual information (firm-intrinsic residual ≥37% across all specifications). Second, we distinguish two classes of food-safety signals: hazard-intrinsic signals (particularly pathogen contamination) that transfer robustly across firms, and compliance-related signals (labelling defects, GMP violations) that are almost entirely firm-specific. Third, we evaluate predictive models under four regulatory scenarios ranging from known-firm triage to cold-start assessment of first-time recallers, complemented by event-disjoint cross-validation and a suite of robustness analyses (cross-announcer-coupling sensitivity, post-classification language masking, and feature-pipeline leakage checks). Fourth, we demonstrate that previously reported ML accuracies of 90%+ on food recall databases overstate real-world reliability due to uncontrolled firm-level autocorrelation and SKU-level pseudoreplication. Fifth, we propose a differentiated risk assessment strategy and evaluation recommendations for regulators assessing ML-based food safety tools.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section Data and regulatory context describes the data and regulatory context. Section Analytical approach presents the analytical approach. Section Results reports results across all evaluation dimensions. Section Discussion discusses implications for regulatory practice. Section Conclusions concludes with deployment recommendations.

Data and regulatory context

Data source and recall classification system

All records were retrieved from the openFDA Food Enforcement API14, which provides structured metadata for every FDA food recall event since 2012. The dataset spans 20 June 2012 to 25 February 2025 and contains 28,448 unique enforcement records after removing one duplicate. Each record includes the recalling firm’s identity, product description, reason for recall, distribution scope, and the severity classification assigned by the FDA.

The FDA classifies food recalls into three severity tiers following the Regulatory Procedures Manual1. Class I denotes situations where exposure to a violative product carries a reasonable probability of serious adverse health consequences or death—typically involving pathogen contamination or life-threatening allergen exposure. Class II covers cases where exposure may cause temporary or medically reversible health effects. Class III applies when the product is unlikely to cause any adverse health consequence, often involving minor labelling defects or Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) violations. This tiered system is central to regulatory decision-making, guiding public notification urgency, resource allocation, and follow-up monitoring intensity.

Each recall requires expert evaluation by the FDA’s Health Hazard Evaluation (HHE) Committee, which is mandated to complete its severity assessment within two working days of receiving a recall recommendation1. In practice, however, the overall recall timeline—from hazard identification to public notification—can extend far beyond this target, with documented delays of months to years in several high-profile cases3.

Three structural features of the openFDA data warrant explicit acknowledgement, as they shape the analytical approach described in Section Unit of analysis: SKU versus event level and the robustness analyses in Section Robustness analyses. First, the recalling_firm field records the entity that announced the recall, which may be the producer or a downstream distributor, retailer, or co-packer; openFDA does not provide a separate manufacturer identifier. National retailers (Kroger, Target, Publix) and distributors therefore appear in the dataset alongside producers. Second, the event_id field groups records within a single firm’s announcement but does not cross firms: multi-firm contamination events (e.g., the 2022 Salmonella outbreak traced to peanut butter products, which produced records under at least seven distinct event_id values) appear under separate event_id values for each announcing firm, and no openFDA field functions as a cross-firm contamination-event identifier. Third, the underlying unit of openFDA records is the SKU (one record per recalled product), not the contamination event: a single recall event may generate tens to hundreds of SKU records sharing the same severity classification. In our dataset, the 28,448 records correspond to 7,023 distinct event_id values, with 64.5% being singleton events and 25 events comprising more than 100 SKUs each. Among multi-SKU events, 93.3% have a single severity classification across all SKUs. We adopt the SKU level as our primary unit of analysis to maintain comparability with the existing food-safety ML literature, while reporting event-level robustness analyses throughout (see Section Unit of analysis: SKU versus event level and Section Robustness analyses) (Table 1).

Dataset overview and firm concentration

The target variable is the three-level severity classification: Class I (12,542 records, 44.1%), Class II (14,250, 50.1%), and Class III (1656, 5.8%). The pronounced imbalance toward the minority Class III motivated the use of balanced sample weights throughout model training.

A defining structural characteristic of this dataset is the highly concentrated distribution of recalls across firms (Table 2). Of the 5302 unique firms, nearly half (49.8%) appear only once, collectively contributing just 9.3% of all records. Conversely, the top 1% of firms (54 entities) account for 28.6% of all recalls, and the top 5% (266 firms) account for over half (51.1%). This concentration means that a small number of repeatedly recalling firms dominate the dataset.

Crucially, these high-frequency firms exhibit remarkably stable severity profiles. Among firms with ten or more SKU records (n = 513), the average SKU-level “severity purity”—the fraction of a firm’s recalls falling into its dominant severity class—is 0.93. For example, the most-recalled firm (Garden-Fresh Foods, Inc., 633 SKU records) has 96% of its records classified as Class I, while Newly Weds Foods (438 SKU records) has 99% classified as Class II.

However, this SKU-level metric is mechanically influenced by the dataset structure described in Section Data source and recall classification system: a single large unanimous recall event contributes many SKU records of identical severity. When aggregated to the contamination-event level, severity purity for firms with ten or more distinct events (n = 25) falls to 0.66, indicating that within-firm severity tendency, while substantial, is less deterministic than SKU-level metrics suggest. For instance, Garden-Fresh Foods’s 633 SKU records correspond to only 10 distinct contamination events, while Newly Weds Foods’s 438 records correspond to 11 distinct events. By contrast, Whole Foods Market’s 313 SKU records span 63 distinct events, illustrating that firms vary substantially in whether their recall activity is SKU-dense or event-dense (quantified further in Section Unit of analysis: SKU versus event level and Section Robustness analyses).

This pattern reflects the structural reality of food production: a firm’s recall profile is largely determined by its product portfolio, manufacturing processes, facility sanitation regime, and supply-chain structure20. A dairy processor with persistent Listeria environmental contamination will generate repeated Class I recalls, while a firm with labelling compliance gaps will produce recurring Class III events2. We characterise this pattern as hazard-portfolio-dominated firm-level concentration, supported by the quantitative decomposition in Section Conditional mutual information decomposition.

This firm-level clustering has a direct methodological implication: any predictive model trained on this dataset risks learning firm-specific patterns rather than generalisable hazard–severity associations. The analytical approach described in Section Analytical approach is designed to disentangle these two sources of predictability.

A finer-grained decomposition by specific pathogens provides additional insight (Table 4). Listeria (7,472 records) and Salmonella (3,434) together account for 89% of all pathogen-related recalls, and both are overwhelmingly associated with Class I severity (82% and 80%, respectively), consistent with their well-established acute health risk profiles2. Cronobacter (126/146 = 86% Class I) similarly reflects its severe risk to neonatal populations. Allergen subcategory analysis

Among allergen subcategories (Table 5), milk is the most frequently recalled allergen (3542 records), followed by peanut (1847), soy (1646), and wheat (1642). Taken together, these hazard–severity associations define two distinct classes of food-safety signals: hazard-intrinsic signals (pathogen contamination, high-risk allergens) where severity is largely determined by the biological nature of the hazard regardless of which entity announces the recall, and compliance-related signals (labelling defects, GMP violations) where severity reflects firm-specific quality management practices. This distinction is central to interpreting the analytical results that follow.

Analytical approach

Feature representation

A 1437-dimensional feature vector was constructed for each record, comprising three groups (Table 6): NLP features (1392 dimensions), structured features (40 dimensions), and temporal features (5 dimensions). NLP features include TF-IDF vectors (1000 dimensions, extracted separately from the reason-for-recall and product-description fields using sublinear scaling and bigrams), Sentence-BERT semantic embeddings (384 dimensions, via all-MiniLM-L6-v223), five binary hazard-category indicators derived from keyword matching, and three text meta-features (character lengths and word count). Structured features encode recall type (voluntary/mandated), distribution scope, state-group indicators, product category, and the reporting firm’s historical recall count (computed using only temporally preceding records to prevent look-ahead leakage). Temporal features include report date components and rolling recall counts. Mathematical formulations are provided in Supplementary Material S9.

Model selection

Based on recent large-scale benchmarking evidence that gradient-boosted tree models consistently match or outperform deep learning on medium-scale tabular classification tasks24,25, we adopted XGBoost26 as the primary analytical tool, with Logistic Regression and Random Forest as reference models. Hyperparameters were tuned using the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE)27 via Optuna28, with 3-fold stratified cross-validation and 20 TPE trials. Class imbalance was addressed through balanced sample weights inversely proportional to class frequency (Supplementary Material S1). A rule-based baseline assigning severity based on hazard-keyword presence was included for reference. Full model comparison results are provided in Supplementary Material S7.

Four regulatory scenarios

The evaluation protocol is the methodological core of this study. We designed four evaluation scenarios, each mapped to a concrete FDA deployment context (Table 7).

Evaluation scenarios and their regulatory interpretation.

The Known-firm scenario (R) uses a standard 80/20 random stratified split where the same firm may appear in both training and test sets. The New-firm scenario (G) ensures complete firm-level separation between training and test sets using GroupShuffleSplit, isolating cross-firm generalisation. The Prospective scenario (T) uses a fixed temporal cutoff (end of 2023). The Strictest scenario (GT) combines both constraints. This multi-scenario design is informed by the panel-data leakage framework of Cerqua et al.15, who showed that both cross-sectional and longitudinal dimensions of panel data can independently inflate apparent model performance.

For the R, G, and GT scenarios, experiments were repeated with five random seeds and results are reported as mean ± standard deviation. The T scenario uses a fixed cutoff and is deterministic.

The four scenarios form a natural 2 × 2 factorial design crossing firm overlap (same firms vs. new firms in test) with temporal overlap (IID vs. future test data), enabling decomposition of performance changes into firm-level autocorrelation, temporal drift, and their interaction (formulas in Supplementary Material S13).

Firm-history baseline

To quantify the explanatory power of firm identity alone, we constructed a non-model baseline: for each firm, the severity class most frequently observed in its training-set recalls is assigned as the prediction for all its test-set records. For firms absent from the training set, the global majority class is assigned. This baseline establishes the upper bound of performance achievable through entity memorisation alone, providing a reference point against which model contributions can be measured (formal definition in Supplementary Material S11).

We note that under firm-disjoint evaluation (scenarios G and GT), this construction defaults to the global majority class for all test records, since no test firms appear in training. The baseline therefore serves two distinct functions: in the Known-firm scenario it measures the upper bound of performance achievable through entity memorisation, whereas in firm-disjoint scenarios it degenerates to a non-informative null. For a more informative comparison under firm-disjoint evaluation, we additionally include the firm-blind hazard-keyword rule-based baseline (Supplementary Material S7), extended to all four scenarios in Section Results.

Supplementary analyses

Three additional analyses were conducted: (i) SHAP TreeExplainer29 applied to XGBoost for feature importance and per-class interpretation; (ii) feature ablation across six configurations (Full, No NLP, NLP Only, No Temporal, No SBERT, No TF-IDF) under both the Known-firm and Strictest scenarios to assess each feature group’s contribution to cross-firm generalisation; and (iii) identity masking, where all firm names in the text were replaced with a placeholder token and features re-extracted, to test whether the firm-level performance effect operates through explicit name tokens or structural patterns.

Unit of analysis: SKU versus event level

As described in Section Data source and recall classification system, openFDA Food Enforcement records are at the SKU level: one record per recalled product per firm announcement. A single contamination event may therefore generate multiple records sharing the same severity classification. This raises two methodological considerations.

First, treating SKU records as independent observations may inflate the apparent sample size relative to the number of underlying contamination events. Confidence intervals based on the 28,448 SKU-level observations are necessarily tighter than those based on the 7,023 distinct events.

Second, random train–test splits at the SKU level can place records from the same event into both training and test sets. This is a form of pseudoreplication: the same contamination event contributes near-identical records to both partitions, which a model may exploit without learning generalisable patterns.

We address these considerations through three analyses, reported in Section Robustness analyses: (i) event-disjoint cross-validation, in which all SKU records sharing an event_id are constrained to a single fold; (ii) event-level severity purity, computed by aggregating each firm’s SKU records to the event level before computing dominant class proportions (Section Dataset overview and firm concentration); and (iii) sensitivity analyses excluding records flagged with supply-chain markers that may indicate cross-firm announcer coupling.

We adopt the SKU level as our primary unit of analysis for three reasons: (i) it is the unit at which openFDA publishes data and at which FDA regulatory follow-up actions are tracked; (ii) it preserves product-level heterogeneity within events that may carry severity-relevant information (e.g., ready-to-eat versus cook-before-eat products within a single Listeria event); and (iii) it ensures comparability with the food-safety ML literature that operates on the same data structure11,12. The event-level analyses in Section Robustness analyses serve as robustness checks rather than the main analytical pipeline.

Under the Known-firm scenario, the firm-history baseline achieves Macro-F1 = 0.818—only 0.075 below XGBoost’s 0.893. This means that simply assigning each firm its historically most frequent severity class captures 92% of the ML model’s performance under standard evaluation.

Under firm-disjoint scenarios, the firm-history baseline collapses to approximately the global majority class (Macro-F1 = 0.207 under New-firm, 0.226 under Strictest)—a consequence of its construction (Supplementary Equation 19), which defaults to the global majority class when test firms are absent from training. A more informative comparison is against the firm-blind hazard-keyword rule-based baseline (Supplementary Material S7, extended here to all four scenarios). The rule-based baseline uses no firm information and remains in the range 0.444–0.495 across all four scenarios, as expected for a firm-invariant classifier. Against this informative null, XGBoost retains an increment of +0.07 to +0.12 under firm-disjoint scenarios—more modest than the +0.34 increment against the firm-history baseline would suggest, but substantively positive and confirming that the model learns generalisable hazard–severity associations beyond hand-coded keyword rules.

Event-level analysis (Section Robustness analyses) further clarifies that the genuine XGBoost-over-firm-history increment expands rather than contracts under stricter unit-of-analysis (+0.203 event-disjoint vs. +0.075 SKU-level), because the firm-history baseline benefits more from SKU pseudoreplication than XGBoost does.

Two patterns emerge. First, pathogen-related Class I identification is highly robust: within the pathogen subset under the New-firm scenario, Class I recall reaches 0.917 ± 0.009—meaning the model correctly identifies 92% of severe pathogen-related recalls even for entirely unseen firms. This is consistent with the hazard-intrinsic nature of the pathogen–Class I association documented in Section Hazard-type and severity class associations: Salmonella and Listeria contamination poses a severe health risk regardless of which entity announces the recall.

Second, labelling and compliance-related signals are almost entirely firm-specific. Labelling recalls show the steepest degradation from the Known-firm to New-firm scenario (0.805 → 0.390), and foreign-material recalls behave similarly (0.921 → 0.364). Allergen-related recalls show the most robust cross-firm transfer (New-firm F1 = 0.498, Strictest F1 = 0.583), likely because allergen subcategory signals (e.g., peanut vs. soy) encode transferable severity information as shown in Table 5.

Feature ablation confirms this pattern (Table 10). Under the Strictest scenario, removing all NLP features causes a 0.173-point drop in Macro-F1—2.6 times larger than the 0.067-point drop under the Known-firm scenario—confirming that NLP features, which encode hazard-intrinsic signals, are the primary drivers of genuine cross-firm generalisation. Conversely, structured features become counterproductive for unseen firms: the NLP-only model exceeds the full model by +0.014 under the Strictest scenario, because firm-specific features (prior recall counts, geographic patterns) introduce noise for new entities. Temporal drift and continuous learning

A continuous learning simulation across nine annual evaluation windows (Figure 7) shows that expanding-window retraining (mean F1 = 0.578) consistently outperforms both static (0.517) and sliding-window (0.547) approaches. The advantage of expanding over sliding suggests that data volume matters more than temporal recency, likely because the rare Class III requires a large data pool for adequate representation. This is consistent with the finding that temporal concept drift is modest relative to firm-level effects. A further challenge not captured by this simulation is the emergence of novel hazard types absent from historical training data—such as muscimol-containing mushroom products and lead-contaminated cinnamon incidents in 20243—which suggests that operational deployment would benefit from human-in-the-loop mechanisms for out-of-distribution hazards.

Robustness analyses

Three concerns about openFDA data structure motivate additional robustness analyses: SKU-level pseudoreplication, cross-firm announcer coupling in multi-firm contamination events, and post-classification language in the reason_for_recall field. We address each in turn.

Event-disjoint cross-validation

To assess whether SKU-level random splits inflate apparent model performance through pseudoreplication, we performed 5-fold event-disjoint cross-validation using GroupKFold partitioned by event_id, ensuring that all SKU records sharing a contamination event are constrained to a single fold ( distinct events). The firm-history baseline drops to Macro-F1 = 0.406 ± 0.049 (from 0.818 under SKU-level random split), while XGBoost obtains 0.609 ± 0.024 (from 0.893); the XGBoost-over-firm-history increment is +0.203 (95% CI [+0.123, +0.283]; paired t-test t = 7.03, p = 0.0022), reported in the final row of Table 8.

Notably, the increment expands from +0.075 (SKU-level) to +0.203 (event-level), rather than contracting. This occurs because the firm-history baseline benefits disproportionately from SKU pseudoreplication: under SKU-level random splits, the same contamination event’s SKUs are distributed across train and test, allowing the firm-history rule to exploit within-event consistency. Under event-disjoint partitioning this benefit is eliminated, exposing the firm-history baseline as a substantially weaker predictor than its SKU-level performance suggests. XGBoost, by contrast, retains most of its predictive power because it relies on hazard-semantic and structural features that generalise across events. The event-level analysis therefore strengthens rather than weakens our central conclusion that ML models capture genuine hazard–severity associations beyond firm-level memorisation.

Cross-announcer coupling sensitivity

The recalling_firm field records the announcing entity, which may be downstream of the contamination source (Section Data source and recall classification system). Cross-firm generalisation in the New-firm scenario could in principle be inflated if the same contamination event appears under multiple announcing firms across the training and test sets. We addressed this through three analyses (full detail in Supplementary §S14.2). First, 12 regex patterns capturing explicit supply-chain language (“supplier”, “notified by”, “received from”, etc.) identified 1,843 records (6.48% of the dataset), narrower than the 10.6% lower bound estimated by a reviewer. Second, a manual audit of 50 randomly sampled flagged records yielded a positive predictive value of 76% (38/50 genuine downstream announcements), implying an effective downstream share of approximately 4.9% ( PPV). Third, re-running the New-firm pathogen subset after excluding all 1,843 flagged records changed Class I recall by only -0.003 ( over 5 seeds). These three lines of evidence indicate that the cross-firm pathogen–Class I transfer documented in Section Transferable versus firm-specific signals is not driven by cross-announcer coupling.

Within-hazard analysis: choice of diagnostic

To assess whether firm identity adds predictive power conditional on hazard category, the natural test is a within-hazard comparison on single-pathogen subsets. We performed this comparison on Listeria-only and Salmonella-only subsets (Supplementary §S14.5) but found it diagnostically limited: both subsets are severely imbalanced (Class I 80–82%, Class III <0.2%), so the firm-history baseline degenerates to predicting Class I for nearly all records—the same “vacuous null” failure mode that arises for firm-disjoint scenarios (Section Firm-history patterns strongly predict recall severity under known-firm evaluation). We therefore addressed the underlying question through two complementary and more diagnostic routes.

First, on the allergen subset—whose severity distribution () is the most balanced among the major hazard categories and thus avoids the degenerate-null problem—firm-history achieves Macro-F1 = 0.779 and XGBoost 0.848, a +0.069 increment, providing a direct, diagnostically valid single-subset confirmation that firm identity carries information beyond hazard category.

Second, the conditional mutual information decomposition (Section Conditional mutual information decomposition) is robust to class imbalance and provides a single unified test across all hazards simultaneously, avoiding the multiplicity inherent in per-subset comparisons; the literal single-pathogen comparison remains available in Supplementary §S14.5. Together these analyses indicate that firm identity adds limited but non-zero predictive information once hazard category is conditioned on.

Conditional mutual information decomposition

To quantify the relative contributions of hazard category and firm identity to recall severity, we computed the conditional mutual information between severity and firm identity given hazard category, , compared to the marginal . If hazard portfolios fully explain the firm–severity association, the conditional MI should collapse toward zero; if firm identity contributes information beyond hazard portfolio, a residual persists.

We computed this decomposition under two estimator methods and two hazard granularities (Table 15). The plug-in estimator measures total observed mediation including finite-sample cardinality-bias contributions, while the permutation-null-corrected estimator subtracts the expected MI under random firm-label permutation (n = 50 permutations), measuring signal-only mediation. With 5302 firms partitioned across hazard subcategories the cardinality bias is substantial, so the permutation-corrected estimate is the more defensible point estimate; however, fine-grained conditioning produces small per-subcategory samples, inflating permutation-correction uncertainty. We therefore report both estimators across both granularities and treat the range as a robustness interval.

The robust conclusion across all four estimator–granularity combinations is that hazard category mediates between 27% (plug-in, coarse) and 63% (permutation-null, fine) of the firm–severity association, with a firm-intrinsic residual bounded below by 37% under every specification. The persistence of a firm-intrinsic residual across all specifications is consistent with food-production realities: even within the same hazard category, firms differ systematically in facility design, sanitation regimes, supply-chain controls, and inspection responsiveness.

Reproducibility and feature-pipeline sensitivity

We performed two further sensitivity analyses to assess robustness to feature-pipeline and computational-environment choices.

TF-IDF vocabulary leakage. TF-IDF vocabulary in our main analysis is fit on the full corpus prior to cross-validation partitioning, following standard preprocessing convention. To verify that this does not create vocabulary-level information leakage across folds, we re-ran the event-disjoint analysis with fold-internal TF-IDF fitting, re-fitting vocabulary within each fold’s training set. The result, Macro-F1 = 0.5989 ± 0.0353 (fold-internal) versus 0.6087 ± 0.0238 (full-corpus), gives Δ = -0.0098 (95% CI [-0.060, +0.040], paired t-test p = 0.62). The non-significant direction is opposite to what vocabulary leakage would predict, confirming that TF-IDF vocabulary leakage does not affect our findings. Sentence-BERT features use the frozen pre-trained all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model and are unaffected; the firm_prior_recalls feature uses a strict temporal cutoff (only records earlier than the focal record are counted), making it invariant to partitioning method.

Post-classification language masking. We identified post-HHE classification phrases (e.g., “serious risk”, “life-threatening”, “minor labeling defect”) that appear in reason_for_recall after HHE Committee severity assignment. These phrases are rare: a 15-phrase list derived from the FDA Regulatory Procedures Manual matches 197 of 28,448 records (0.69%). Masking them and re-extracting features yielded inconsistent effects across replication environments and phrase lists: +0.030 (rorqual, 15 phrases), -0.010 (local, 15 phrases), and -0.006 (local, 29 phrases). More directly, the Strictest-scenario Macro-F1 itself remains within the narrow band 0.553–0.583 across all three configurations (unmasked 0.553 vs. masked 0.583 on rorqual; unmasked 0.569 vs. masked 0.559 and 0.563 locally), a 0.030 span that brackets zero in both directions—masking the phrases does not materially change cross-firm generalisation performance. The absolute magnitude of all observed effects (|Δ | < 0.03) further falls within the cross-version XGBoost reproducibility floor (mean across seeds with identical inputs but different library versions; Supplementary §S15). If post-classification language functioned as a leakage signal, masking should systematically degrade performance in the same direction across all configurations; the observed sign-inconsistency and below-floor magnitude indicate it does not. Sentence-BERT cosine similarity between original and masked embeddings remains above 0.96 at the median, confirming minimal semantic perturbation. Complete phrase lists, regex specifications, and the reproducibility-floor analysis are provided in Supplementary §S14.3 and §S15.

Discussion

Firm-level patterns and hazard-portfolio effects

The central finding of this study is that FDA food recall severity exhibits strong firm-level concentration, with severity profiles driven primarily by the stable hazard-product portfolios that firms maintain over time, though a substantive firm-intrinsic component also persists. Several analyses elucidate the mechanism.

First, under standard evaluation, a firm-history baseline achieves 92% of the best ML model’s performance (Macro-F1 = 0.818 vs. 0.893, Known-firm scenario). Under firm-disjoint or temporal evaluation, this ratio drops substantially as the firm-history baseline degenerates to the global majority class; the more informative firm-blind rule-based baseline indicates that XGBoost retains a +0.07 to +0.12 increment across firm-disjoint scenarios, modest but substantively positive (Section Firm-history patterns strongly predict recall severity under known-firm evaluation).

Second, identity masking experiments (Section Feature contribution and identity masking, Table 14) confirm that the firm-level effect operates through structural patterns rather than firm-name tokens: masking causes changes of ≤ 0.019 in Macro-F1 across all four scenarios.

Third, event-disjoint analysis (Section Robustness analyses) reveals that when records are aggregated to the contamination-event level, the firm-history baseline drops dramatically (0.818 → 0.406)—confirming that the SKU-level baseline benefits from pseudoreplication. However, the XGBoost-over-firm-history increment expands from +0.075 to +0.203 (95% CI [+0.123, +0.283]) under event-disjoint evaluation, demonstrating that genuine event-level hazard–severity learning is understated rather than overstated in the original SKU-level analysis.

Fourth, conditional mutual information analysis (Section Conditional mutual information decomposition) decomposes the firm–severity association into hazard-mediated and firm-intrinsic components. Hazard category explains 27–63% of (depending on bias-correction method and hazard granularity), with the remaining 37–73% representing firm-intrinsic structure not captured by hazard portfolio alone; the 37% lower bound is robust across all estimator–granularity combinations.

We characterise these findings as hazard-portfolio-dominated firm-level patterns with non-negligible firm-intrinsic residual, a framing more precise than either “firm-level determinism” (which implies a firm-intrinsic mechanism dominating) or pure “hazard-portfolio determinism” (which implies no firm-intrinsic component beyond hazard). This is consistent with food-production realities: even within the same hazard category, firms differ systematically in facility design, sanitation regimes, supply-chain controls, and inspection responsiveness. Consistent with this picture, DeBeer et al.2 documented that Listeria and Salmonella account for approximately 40% of all FDA food recalls between 2002 and 2023, with many firms appearing repeatedly for the same pathogen—a pattern our firm-concentration analysis confirms, with the top 5% of firms accounting for over half of all recalls at a mean SKU-level severity purity of 0.93 (0.66 at the event level).

This finding extends and systematises the early observation by Bouzembrak and Marvin19, who reported an accuracy drop from 80% to 52% for unseen entity combinations in RASFF food fraud prediction but did not decompose the phenomenon. Our 2 × 2 factorial design reveals that firm-level autocorrelation and temporal continuity are highly collinear—removing either suffices to expose the true generalisation floor of ∼0.57—because recalls from the same firm cluster in time.

For regulatory practice, this firm-level concentration has a direct implication: a firm’s recall history is itself a powerful risk-profiling tool for targeted inspections, although its mechanism is hazard-portfolio rather than firm-intrinsic. Firms with escalating recall frequency or severity warrant heightened regulatory attention, independent of any ML model. This aligns with the preventive-control philosophy of the Food Safety Modernization Act7 and suggests that systematic monitoring of firm-level recall trajectories could complement existing inspection prioritisation frameworks. For food safety managers, the finding underscores the importance of addressing root causes—persistent environmental contamination, inadequate allergen controls—rather than treating each recall as an isolated incident.

A differentiated risk assessment strategy

The combination of hazard-type decomposition and multi-scenario evaluation enables a differentiated approach to ML-assisted regulatory triage, mapped to specific operational contexts.

Known-firm recalls with pathogen hazards. This represents the highest-confidence scenario. Under the Known-firm evaluation, 91% of predictions carry a maximum predicted probability ≥ 0.9, with 96.9% accuracy among these high-confidence cases. For Class I specifically, high-confidence predictions achieve 98.1% precision. These cases could receive expedited processing within the HHE Committee’s two-day evaluation window1, reserving expert attention for ambiguous cases.

Known-firm recalls with compliance-related hazards. ML predictions retain reference value (Known-firm Macro-F1 = 0.89) but are driven partly by firm-specific patterns. Predictions should be used as one input among several, with human verification for cases where the model’s confidence is low or the firm’s recent recall pattern deviates from its historical profile.

New-firm recalls with pathogen hazards. Despite the overall performance drop to Macro-F1 ≈ 0.57 for new firms, pathogen-related Class I recall reaches 92% even under the New-firm scenario. The hazard-intrinsic nature of pathogen–severity associations provides a reliable signal regardless of firm identity, making ML-assisted screening viable for this specific hazard–scenario combination.

New-firm recalls with compliance-related hazards. This is the least reliable scenario. Class III recall collapses from 0.726 to 0.100 under the New-firm evaluation, and labelling recalls show the steepest per-hazard degradation. These cases require full expert evaluation, as the model has no firm-specific context and compliance patterns do not transfer across entities.

This differentiated strategy is more operationally specific than a blanket recommendation to “use ML for known firms and experts for new firms,” and aligns with the FDA’s New Era of Smarter Food Safety vision of integrating predictive analytics as targeted decision-support tools6.

Recommendations for evaluating ML tools in food safety

Based on our findings, we propose three recommendations for regulatory agencies evaluating ML-based food safety tools—whether developed internally or proposed by external vendors.

First, any ML system proposed for regulatory use should be evaluated under at least two scenarios: a standard evaluation (for comparability with published literature) and a firm-disjoint or temporal evaluation (for deployment reliability). The gap between scenarios is itself an informative diagnostic—a large gap signals heavy reliance on entity-specific patterns and warrants caution for new-entity deployment.

Second, evaluation reports should disclose the degree of entity overlap between training and test sets (e.g., “X out of Y firms appear in both train and test”). This single statistic helps regulators assess the risk of inflated performance claims without requiring full replication.

Third, a simple firm-history baseline should be included. If a complex model does not substantially exceed this baseline under standard evaluation, entity-level memorisation—rather than genuine hazard learning—is likely driving the reported performance.

Fourth, report event-level (contamination-event-level) sample sizes alongside SKU-level record counts. In our analysis, switching from standard SKU-level random evaluation to event-disjoint cross-validation reduces best-model Macro-F1 from 0.893 to 0.609—a 0.284-point drop attributable solely to within-event SKU pseudoreplication. This drop is comparable in magnitude to the firm-disjoint penalty (the R-to-G transition), indicating that unit-of-analysis is as important as entity-overlap in honest benchmarking. Both openFDA and RASFF share the structural feature of records nested within larger contamination events, so this concern applies across the food-safety ML literature.

Fifth, include a hazard-keyword rule-based informative null baseline in addition to the firm-history baseline. The firm-history baseline, while valuable under standard evaluation for diagnosing entity memorisation, degenerates to the global majority class under firm-disjoint scenarios; a firm-blind rule-based baseline instead retains diagnostic value across all scenarios (Macro-F1 ≈ 0.44–0.50 in our data, Table 8). The gap between an ML model and this firm-blind informative null quantifies how much the model learns beyond keyword-level pattern matching.

These measures are computationally inexpensive and can be adopted with minimal disruption. We note that the high-performing systems reported in recent RASFF studies11,12 do not report group-aware evaluation results, leaving open the question of how much their performance reflects genuine pattern learning versus entity memorisation—a concern equally relevant to EU RASFF and U.S. FDA research contexts.

Limitations and robustness considerations

Unit of analysis

The openFDA records are at the SKU level, and a single contamination event may contribute many near-identical records (Section Unit of analysis: SKU versus event level). We adopt the SKU level as the primary unit of analysis to preserve product-level heterogeneity and maintain comparability with the food-safety ML literature, but this choice inflates apparent sample size relative to the 7,023 distinct contamination events and tightens confidence intervals accordingly. The event-disjoint cross-validation in Section Robustness analyses addresses this directly: although absolute Macro-F1 falls under event-level evaluation, the XGBoost-over-firm-history increment expands (+0.075 → +0.203), so the central conclusion is reinforced rather than weakened. We report both SKU-level and event-level metrics throughout so that readers can assess the sensitivity of any specific figure to this choice. We treat SKU-level and event-level results as complementary diagnostics rather than alternatives: the former preserves comparability with prior literature, the latter provides the stricter test of deployment reliability against the contamination-event-level data structure.

Post-classification language in reason_for_recall

The reason_for_recall field is written after the HHE Committee’s severity classification is known and may embed classification rationale, making NLP-derived features partly retrospective. We assessed this directly (Section Reproducibility and feature-pipeline sensitivity): post-HHE rationale phrases are rare (0.69% of records), and masking them changes Strictest-scenario Macro-F1 by |Δ | < 0.03 with inconsistent sign across phrase lists and compute environments—within the cross-version reproducibility floor. The core finding of firm-level concentration does not depend on text features in any case: the firm-history baseline achieves Macro-F1 = 0.818 without any NLP input, and even the No-NLP configuration under the Strictest scenario yields 0.395, above the 0.33 random baseline, demonstrating that structured features carry independent predictive information.

Cross-announcer coupling

The recalling_firm field records the announcing entity rather than the manufacturer, so multi-firm contamination events can appear under several announcers (Section Data source and recall classification system). Explicit supply-chain markers flag 6.48% of records, of which a manual audit suggests roughly 76% are genuine downstream announcements (effective share ≈ 4.9%). Excluding all flagged records changes the New-firm pathogen Class I recall by only -0.003 (Section Robustness analyses), indicating that the cross-firm pathogen transfer is not an artefact of announcer coupling. Nonetheless, the absence of a manufacturer identifier in openFDA is a genuine limitation; a dataset linking announcers to upstream producers would permit a more complete treatment.

Within-hazard analysis caveats

The within-hazard comparison (Section Robustness analyses) is most informative on the allergen subset, whose severity distribution is relatively balanced. The Listeria- and Salmonella-only subsets are heavily skewed toward Class I (80–82%), so the firm-history baseline degenerates toward a constant predictor on these subsets and the comparison has limited diagnostic power. The conditional mutual information analysis (Section Conditional mutual information decomposition) is imbalance-robust and provides the primary quantitative decomposition, but its permutation-corrected estimate carries wider uncertainty at fine hazard granularity owing to small per-subcategory samples.

Other limitations and future work

Only XGBoost was used for the multi-scenario evaluation; given that three gradient-boosted tree models differed by less than 0.003 under standard evaluation (Supplementary Material S7), we consider XGBoost representative, but cross-model validation would strengthen the conclusions. Identity masking replaced only firm names; brand names, addresses, and other indirect identifiers were not masked, and more aggressive anonymisation might further reduce residual entity patterns. Sentence-BERT embeddings were generated with the general-purpose all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model; domain-adapted language models for food-safety text might improve cross-firm generalisation. Future work should extend this analysis to the EU RASFF system for cross-database validation and explore integration of FDA internal health hazard evaluation reports for richer, prospectively available training signals.

Conclusions

Analysis of 28,448 FDA food recall records (2012–2025) reveals that recall severity exhibits strong firm-level concentration, driven primarily by stable hazard-product portfolios within firms: under standard evaluation, a simple firm-history baseline captures 92% of the best ML model’s performance (Known-firm scenario), reflecting persistent production-system determinants such as facility sanitation regimes and product portfolios. Conditional mutual information analysis indicates that this concentration is hazard-portfolio dominated, with a firm-intrinsic residual of at least 37% that no estimator explains away. Two distinct classes of food-safety signals emerge: hazard-intrinsic signals—particularly pathogen contamination (Salmonella, Listeria)—are universally associated with Class I severity regardless of the announcing entity, with 92% of pathogen-related Class I recalls correctly identified even for entirely unseen companies; in contrast, compliance-related signals—labelling defects and GMP violations driving Class III recalls—are almost entirely firm-specific and fail to generalise across entities. These findings define the operational boundary of ML-assisted regulatory triage: pathogen-related recalls can benefit from automated screening across all scenarios, while compliance-related cases involving new firms require mandatory expert review. Previously reported ML accuracies of 90%+ on food recall databases likely overstate deployment reliability due to uncontrolled firm-level autocorrelation and SKU-level pseudoreplication; we recommend that regulatory agencies evaluating such tools require scenario-specific performance reporting, entity-overlap disclosure, event-level sample-size reporting, and both firm-history and firm-blind rule-based baseline comparisons.
