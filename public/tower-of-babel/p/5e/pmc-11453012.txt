Deep learning-based characterization of pathological subtypes in lung invasive adenocarcinoma utilizing 18F-deoxyglucose positron emission tomography imaging
Authors: Dongbo Wu, Yingci Li, Mingyan Zhou, Feifei Gong, Jiankun Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of a deep learning (DL) model based on PET/CT images for distinguishing and predicting various pathological subtypes of invasive lung adenocarcinoma.MethodsA total of 250 patients diagnosed with invasive lung cancer were included in this retrospective study. The pathological subtypes of the cancer were recorded. PET/CT images were analyzed, including measurements and recordings of the short and long diameters on the maximum cross-sectional plane of the CT image, the density of the lesion, and the associated imaging signs. The SUVmax, SUVmean, and the lesion's long and short diameters on the PET image were also measured. A manual diagnostic model was constructed to analyze its diagnostic performance across different pathological subtypes. The acquired images were first denoised, followed by data augmentation to expand the dataset. The U-Net network architecture was then employed for feature extraction and network segmentation. The classification network was based on the ResNet residual network to address the issue of gradient vanishing in deep networks. Batch normalization was applied to ensure the feature matrix followed a distribution with a mean of 0 and a variance of 1. The images were divided into training, validation, and test sets in a ratio of 6:2:2 to train the model. The deep learning model was then constructed to analyze its diagnostic performance across different pathological subtypes.ResultsStatistically significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed among the four different subtypes in PET/CT imaging performance. The AUC and diagnostic accuracy of the manual diagnostic model for different pathological subtypes were as follows: APA: 0.647, 0.664; SPA: 0.737, 0.772; PPA: 0.698, 0.780; LPA: 0.849, 0.904. Chi-square tests indicated significant statistical differences among these subtypes (P < 0.05). The AUC and diagnostic accuracy of the deep learning model for the different pathological subtypes were as follows: APA: 0.854, 0.864; SPA: 0.930, 0.936; PPA: 0.878, 0.888; LPA: 0.900, 0.920. Chi-square tests also indicated significant statistical differences among these subtypes (P < 0.05). The Delong test showed that the diagnostic performance of the deep learning model was superior to that of the manual diagnostic model (P < 0.05).ConclusionsThe deep learning model based on PET/CT images exhibits high diagnostic efficacy in distinguishing and diagnosing various pathological subtypes of invasive lung adenocarcinoma, demonstrating the significant potential of deep learning techniques in accurately identifying and predicting disease subgroups.Keywords: Adenocarcinoma of lung, Positron-emission tomography and computed tomography, Deep learning

Introduction

Currently, lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with lung adenocarcinoma being the most common subtype, accounting for over 40% of cases [1, 2]. Among lung adenocarcinomas, invasive lung adenocarcinoma (ILADC) is the most common type. Invasive lung adenocarcinoma is divided into five pathological subtypes: lepidic predominant adenocarcinoma (LPA), acinar predominant adenocarcinoma (APA), papillary predominant adenocarcinoma (PPA), solid predominant adenocarcinoma (SPA), and micropapillary predominant adenocarcinoma (MPA) [3]. As the incidence of lung adenocarcinoma continues to rise, accurately distinguishing between different pathological subtypes has become increasingly important for improving clinical outcomes and prognosis.

PET/CT is widely used in the clinic as a crucial tool for the diagnosis, staging, restaging, treatment evaluation, and prognosis of lung cancer. However, previous studies have shown that although the maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) from FDG PET is related to pathological types, there is still controversy over whether SUVmax can effectively reflect specific pathological subtypes and growth patterns [4, 5].

In recent years, with the rapid development of artificial intelligence technology, deep learning, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNN), has shown great potential in medical imaging analysis [6, 7]. Unlike traditional methods that rely on manual feature extraction, deep learning models can automatically extract and learn complex features from large datasets, thereby achieving higher accuracy and consistency in pathological subtype classification [8]. Chaunzwa et al. have leveraged recent advances in radiomics and deep learning to develop models aimed at improving clinician accuracy and productivity in the diagnosis of early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), with a particular focus on adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma [9]. Huo et al. developed a deep learning model that successfully applied CT images for the classification of lung adenocarcinoma subtypes, significantly improving diagnostic accuracy [10]. This suggests that deep learning may help enhance the accuracy of pathological subtype classification. Therefore, this study aims to distinguish and investigate different pathological subtypes based on deep learning applied to PET/CT images, thus providing stronger support for the precise diagnosis and personalized treatment of lung cancer.

Materials and methods

Patients

We retrospectively included a cohort of 250 patients who were diagnosed with lung malignancies through whole-body 18F-FDG PET/CT scans at the Department of Positron Emission Tomography/Computed Tomography-Magnetic Resonance Center (PET/CT-MR Center) of Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital from January 2019 to December 2022. Among them, there were 117 males and 133 females, aged 38 to 83 years, with a median age of 59 years. This cohort included 67 smokers and 183 non-smokers (including those who had quit smoking for more than 3 years). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) suspected diagnosis of lung cancer based on PET/CT examination; (2) radical lung lesion surgery and mediastinal lymph node dissection; (3) postoperative pathology confirming invasive adenocarcinoma of the lung. Patients with a history of malignancies at other sites were excluded to avoid data confusion. All patients underwent surgery within two weeks following the 18F-FDG PET/CT test. Pathologists meticulously documented the pathological findings, categorizing them into specific pathological subtypes according to the experimental criteria. The clinical data and 18F-FDG PET/CT images were collected.

The PET/CT instrument and scanning protocol

The Department of PET/CT-MR Center of Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital is equipped with a state-of-the-art Discovery Elite PET/CT scanner (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). Additionally, the department is equipped with a GE Minitrace cyclotron and a TraceLab FX-FN chemical synthesis system for the production of positron drugs, ensuring a radiochemical purity of over 95%. The system includes a supplementary picture workstation and an image fusion processing system, both of which possess exceptional sensitivity and produce high-quality imaging results. All participants in this study fasted for a period of 4 to 6 h before undergoing an 18F-FDG PET/CT examination. Their blood glucose levels were controlled within the normal range (< 7 mmol/L) prior to the imaging procedure. The patient received an injection of an imaging agent through the cubital vein while in a calm state. Following the administration of the imaging agent, the patient was required to remain motionless for a duration of 40 to 60 min. During the examination, the patient first underwent a comprehensive whole-body spiral CT scan, with a voltage of 140 kV, a current of 150 mA, a scanning time of 0.5 s/rev, and a layer thickness of 3.75 mm. This was followed by a PET scan, where PET data were collected in 2D mode with 16 subsets and 3 iterations, acquiring data for 2.5 min per bed position, typically 5–7 bed positions per patient. Image reconstruction was performed using the ordered-subset expectation maximization method, with CT transmission scanning data used to perform attenuation correction on PET images. After correction, PET and CT images were automatically integrated on the Xeleris workstation. The SUV was calculated by the workstation based on the patient's body mass, injection dosage, and timing.

Image analysis

Without knowledge of the specific pathological results, two associate chief physicians with more than 5 years of experience conducted imaging analysis, measuring and recording lung lesions in 250 patients based on PET/CT images. The location of the lesion was recorded using CT images, ensuring the quality of CT images by employing breath-holding during the CT scanning process. The short and long diameters of the lesion on the maximum cross-sectional area were measured, as well as the density of the lesion (solid density, mixed density, ground-glass density), and the lesion signs (whether there were vacuoles or cavities, lobulation, spiculation, pleural indentation, etc.). Additionally, under the same grayscale, the SUVmax and SUVmean of lesions on PET images were measured, and the long and short diameters of the most concentrated layer of lesions were recorded. In case of disagreement, another chief physician with more than 15 years of experience reviewed the images and recorded the results. Finally, another physician was responsible for monitoring and documenting the patient's surgical pathology. Subsequently, any statistically significant differences in PET/CT scans among patients with different disease categories were assessed.

Denoising and dataset augmentation

The data for each patient includes PET/CT scans, basic personal information, diagnostic results from medical professionals, and pathology reports. To protect patient data and improve the model's training efficiency, the non-relevant regions of the original images are first subjected to denoising, retaining only the lung area.

As shown in Fig. 1, the three images on the left are the original, the brightness and contrast adjusted, and the CT images after removing irrelevant areas. The three images on the right are the original, the brightness and contrast adjusted, and the PET images after removing irrelevant areas (Fig. 1).

Comparison of CT (A) and PET (B) images before and after denoising

We referenced the mosaic data augmentation logic from YOLOv4. First, a random integer is generated and used (mod 250) to retrieve an image from the sample set. A second random integer determines whether the image undergoes a flip or a rotation. If a flip is chosen, the direction (horizontal or vertical) is decided by another random integer. For rotations, the integer determines whether a 180-degree or 360-degree (original image) rotation is applied. This process is repeated to obtain four transformed images, which are then combined into a new image arranged in a specific order (Fig. 2). This method is applied to both PET and CT images to create new images (Fig. 3). The new image is assigned a new index. Since the original PET/CT images have high integrity and a concentrated color gamut, random cropping and color adjustments were not included in our augmentation process. The enhanced dataset was expanded to over 2,500 copies.

Semantic segmentation

The segmentation network employs a U-Net network architecture for the purpose of extracting features. The system comprises a contraction network and an expansion network, both designed in the shape of a “U.” The contraction network takes an input image size of 512 × 512. After undergoing four rounds of downsampling, the image size is reduced to one-sixteenth of its original dimensions, specifically 32 × 32 pixels. During this time frame, picture characteristics are obtained by performing consecutive convolution and pooling processes. The expansion network on the right is the counterpart of the contraction network and gets resized back to its original dimensions of 512 × 512 after undergoing four upsampling operations. During this time frame, the missing boundary data is supplemented by combining the characteristics from the shrinkage layer that are associated with it, in order to enhance the precision of forecasting edge data. The loss function uses Dice Loss = 1—(2 ×|X ∩ Y| / |X| +|Y|). In this experiment, the model was constructed using the Keras deep learning framework. The image segmentation and feature extraction part is implemented by using the U-Net network, which consists of the encoder on the left (contraction network) and the decoder on the right (expansion network) in the figure below to form a structure similar to the letter U. The encoder part takes the original image of 512 × 512 as the input, and the shallow feature map is extracted by four rounds of downsampling with two 3 × 3 convolutional layers and one 2 × 2 maximum pooling layer. The decoder part extracts the deep feature map from one upsampled convolutional layer and two 3 × 3 convolutional layers, and the feature maps of the encoder-decoder stage are combined to obtain a new feature map.

Image classification

The classification network is built using the ResNet residual network architecture. The Residual Block successfully addresses the issue of vanishing gradients in deep networks, hence enabling improved training of deeper network architectures. Nevertheless, because our training data set had a limited number of samples, we designed a network topology consisting of only 18 layers. The acquisition of a characteristic matrix for a given distribution between every pair of neural network layers is described. Batch normalization ensures that the feature matrix follows a distribution with a mean of 0 and a variance of 1. Ultimately, we partitioned these images into training sets, validation sets, and test sets at a ratio of 6:2:2 for the purpose of training the model. The image classification part of the model is implemented using an 18-layer ResNet network. Its residual block can effectively solve the gradient vanishing problem of deep networks. Starting from the input end, features are first extracted through a 7 × 7 convolutional layer, then a 3 × 3 maximum pooling layer is used to reduce the image resolution, followed by 8 3 × 3 convolutional layers. There is a short-circuit connection between every two convolutional layers, which are combined into four residual units to extract the feature map, and finally, the classification results are output through one average pooling layer and a fully connected layer.

In this experiment, the training set, validation set, and test set were divided into a 6:2:2 ratio. A batch size of 128 samples was used for each iteration of training, and the entire training set was traversed after approximately 12 epochs. The validation set was used to predict and analyze the prediction results by using the model after each round of epochs. The two networks converged in the 13th round, and the loss function value was minimized. The model from this round was used to predict the test set and obtain the final prediction results.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 statistical software. An independent samples t-test was performed between the groups for the normally distributed continuous data expressed as x ± s. For non-normally distributed continuous data, the median (upper and lower quartiles) was used, and the k-independent samples non-parametric test was performed between groups. The categorical data were expressed as frequencies, and the chi-square test was used for comparison between groups. The 95% confidence interval and the area under the curve (AUC) was used to evaluate the predictive performance and was compared with the DeLong test. A significance level of P < 0.05 was utilized to determine statistically significant differences.

Discussion

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with lung adenocarcinoma being the most prevalent histopathological type [11, 12]. The aggressiveness and prognosis of different pathological subtypes vary, making early diagnosis crucial. Currently, the identification of lung cancer subtypes primarily relies on invasive diagnostic methods such as biopsy and postoperative pathology. However, imaging examinations remain the most accurate non-invasive diagnostic tools for lung cancer. In recent years, numerous studies have focused on using imaging techniques to predict lung cancer subtypes [13, 14].

This study aimed to investigate lung cancer subtypes using PET/CT imaging combined with a deep learning model. Our findings revealed statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) in PET/CT imaging characteristics among the four different subtypes, consistent with the results reported by Yang et al. [15] and Bu et al. [16]. Based on these findings, we established a manual diagnostic standard to evaluate the diagnostic efficiency of PET/CT imaging for different pathological subtypes. The results showed diagnostic accuracies of 0.664 for APA, 0.772 for SPA, 0.780 for PPA, and 0.904 for LPA, with significant statistical differences among the subtypes (P < 0.05). The highest accuracy was observed for LPA, suggesting that while PET/CT imaging holds potential for diagnosing lung cancer at the subtype level, its diagnostic efficiency still requires improvement.

To further enhance diagnostic efficiency, we incorporated a deep learning model based on PET/CT images. Significant progress has been made in the application of deep learning to medical image analysis. Studies by Perez-Johnston et al. [17] and Ding et al. [18] demonstrated the successful use of deep learning models in accurately predicting pathological subtypes such as MPA and SPA. This study applied a deep learning model to assess whether it could further improve the differentiation and prediction of pathological subgroups in invasive adenocarcinoma. The results showed that the diagnostic efficacy of deep learning models for pathological subtypes of invasive adenocarcinoma in the lungs was 0.864 for APA, 0.936 for SPA, 0.888 for PPA, 0.920 for LPA, significantly higher than the manual diagnostic model (P < 0.05), with particularly high performance in diagnosing SPA and LPA. This finding correlates with the varying proliferative capacities and characteristics of different subtypes [19–21]. LPA cells exhibit weaker malignant proliferation, slower progression, and better prognosis, typically presenting earlier in PET/CT imaging. In contrast, SPA cells have stronger proliferative capacity, poorer prognosis, and show higher metabolic activity with clearer malignant features in PET/CT imaging. Consequently, the deep learning model demonstrated higher diagnostic efficiency for these two subtypes based on PET/CT images.

The results of this study suggest that deep learning models can more accurately differentiate and diagnose pathological subtypes of lung adenocarcinoma compared to traditional manual diagnostics. This has significant implications for the early diagnosis of lung adenocarcinoma, potentially aiding in the timely selection of appropriate surgical strategies, such as lobectomy or sublobar resection. Furthermore, it can inform decisions regarding molecular targeted therapy and immunotherapy, as well as provide more accurate preoperative assessments of tumor invasiveness and postoperative prognosis. This contributes valuable support to personalized patient assessment, clinical decision-making, and postoperative follow-up in clinical practice.

However, this study has certain limitations. The relatively small sample size, comprising only 250 patients, posed a challenge. To address this, we employed data augmentation techniques to expand the sample size to 2,500 cases. For image segmentation, we selected the U-Net architecture, which is widely recognized for its effectiveness in medical image segmentation tasks, particularly in handling small datasets and extracting multi-scale features. The encoder-decoder structure of U-Net enables effective retention and reconstruction of image details, crucial for accurate lesion localization and feature extraction. This architecture has been extensively validated across various medical imaging applications, demonstrating its reliability and accuracy [22–24].

For the classification network, we chose ResNet. Given that deeper networks may lead to overfitting, ResNet was more efficient for handling smaller datasets [25]. The residual connections in ResNet help mitigate the vanishing gradient problem, ensuring stable training even in relatively shallow networks. ResNet strikes a good balance between complexity and performance, making it particularly suitable given our computational resource limitations and the characteristics of the augmented data.

A notable feature of this study is the use of an advanced two-stage detection model in the neural network, which, unlike traditional machine learning models, does not require manual feature extraction by developers. By modifying and fine-tuning the existing neural network architecture, we were able to uncover and utilize many hidden layer features, thereby improving image quality. However, some limitations remain. The small size of the original dataset, even after data augmentation, still imposes constraints on the model's complexity: the segmentation network's maximum depth is 5 layers, and the classification network's maximum depth is 18 layers. The relatively shallow depth of the ResNet model might affect the classification accuracy of certain subtypes, where deeper networks could potentially offer finer distinctions.

Moreover, external validation is crucial for ensuring the reliability and clinical applicability of the model, but this was not feasible in our study due to limitations in data availability. Nonetheless, we adopted strategies such as data augmentation, simplified model architecture, and reduced model parameters to mitigate the risk of overfitting and enhance the model's generalizability. In the future, we plan to collect more diverse, independent external datasets for more comprehensive validation of our model. This will help further evaluate and improve the model's stability and clinical applicability.

Conclusion

In our study, we found that the four different subtypes have different imaging manifestations in PET/CT imaging. And the deep learning model has the ability to differentiate and forecast the pathological subgroups of invasive adenocarcinoma to a certain degree.
