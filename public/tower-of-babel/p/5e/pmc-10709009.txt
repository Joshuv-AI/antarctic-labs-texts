Addressing health disparities: Palliative care for migrants and refugees in the Eastern Mediterranean Region
Authors: Hadis Ashrafizadeh, Maryam Rassouli
Published: 2023

An overview of the status of immigrants and refugees in the Eastern Mediterranean Region

The Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMR), where many emergency situations and global crises occur, is home to the largest refugee population in the world.1,2 Sixty-six percent of the world's refugees originate from this region,2 with over 62 million people in need of medical care across the region.3 The public health consequences that follow these incidents not only affect the refugee population itself but also have a significant impact on the host communities, playing a crucial role in determining the overall health of the entire region.1

This group is commonly regarded as marginalized and vulnerable. Despite having higher health care needs, they receive the least amount of care.4 These individuals often face greater risks due to their living conditions and limited access to quality health care.5 Several studies have demonstrated differences in health status and disparities in accessing health care among refugees and immigrants compared to the host population.6,7 For example, one study revealed that 20% of Syrian refugee patients with breast cancer sought treatment with distant metastasis, whereas in the Jordanian (host) population, this rate is 16%.8 Furthermore, a higher prevalence of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) has been reported in refugees compared to the host population.9

The increase in non-communicable diseases (NCDs), such as cancer, among refugee populations, necessitating preventive, therapeutic, and long-term care services, has made the management of long-term humanitarian crises in this region complex and costly.10 This is partly due to the health care service provision structures in these countries. For instance, in low- and middle-income countries, more than half of health care costs are related to Primary Health Care (PHC). However, less than 40% of the total PHC is funded by governments, and out-of-pocket expenses are often required to access these services.11 Consequently, these individuals are more exposed to a lack of access to primary care, including palliative and end-of-life care.12

The importance of palliative care for immigrants and refugees

A major challenge for policymakers is the lack of disaggregated data concerning the health of refugees and immigrants, as well as the management of NCDs within this population.5,13 A substantial number of individuals in this group suffer from chronic diseases such as cancer, necessitating expensive and long-term treatments.13

Statistics related to Syrian refugees and displaced individuals utilizing PHC services indicate that approximately 8% of these patients report complaints related to NCDs. In Jordan, nearly 30% of the refugee population is affected by NCDs, including conditions like hypertension and diabetes. Moreover, in Egypt, 78% of households have reported that at least one family member is suffering from a chronic disease.14

This vulnerable group faces a wide array of health and social challenges, language and cultural issues, and disorders stemming from life experiences and adverse societal conditions. Consequently, they have complex needs, often requiring specialized, long-term, or more comprehensive services, including palliative care.15

The importance of integrating palliative care into humanitarian health care has garnered increased attention since 2016. Palliative care services can play a crucial role in meeting the needs of vulnerable populations, but they are frequently overlooked.16 Marginalized individuals are often seen as having unique palliative care requirements.12 On the other hand, palliative care is an integral part of humanitarian and emergency responses, especially in the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic. However, many service providers remain unaware of its significance.17

As per the World Health Organization's definition, palliative care aims to prevent and alleviate severe health-related suffering in physical, emotional, social, spiritual, and functional aspects associated with chronic or life-threatening diseases, such as cancer. It seeks to promote dignity and a good death.18 Although recognized as a human right, global access to palliative care is unequal.19, 20 These inequalities are preventable consequences of discrimination, which, if not properly addressed, may lead to preventable deaths and their continuance.21

Health care disparities refer to differences that exist across racial and ethnic groups in terms of disease prevalence, health outcomes, care quality, and access to care.22

The Worldwide Hospice Palliative Care Alliance (WHPCA) has recently highlighted palliative care as one of the most inequitable areas of health care and emphasized the need for improved equitable access.23 Furthermore, various crises have substantially reduced humanitarian palliative care services.24 Research results indicate that in Jordan, a country hosting many refugees, humanitarian services are not available to all vulnerable groups. These service gaps have a negative impact on people's health, living conditions, social integration, and mental well-being,25 overshadowing their right to receive and access palliative care services.5

Despite the establishment of a national palliative care strategy since 2011, such care is not officially provided in refugee camps in Rwanda in terms of budgeting or clinical organization.26

In Jordan, the cost of cancer care for refugees amounts to 2.09 million euros per year.27 Therefore, it is understandable why refugees and immigrants have poorer access to cancer care compared to other residents of the Middle East.28 In the current context, with palliative care recognized as an integral part of the health care system, it is time to identify and eliminate barriers to accessing palliative care services for special groups and offer promising approaches to facilitate access and enhance the care experience for refugees and immigrants, who constitute a large marginalized and vulnerable community.

Challenges and barriers in providing palliative care to immigrants and refugees

While the factors contributing to disparities in palliative care utilization remain not fully understood, several studies have identified various obstacles hindering access to palliative care. These obstacles include racial and ethnic differences in knowledge, cultural beliefs, and treatment preferences, religious and spiritual beliefs, lack of family support, competing treatment preferences, societal stigma (treating these individuals as outsiders), distrust of the health care system due to past injustices, and the neglect of the experiences and needs of these vulnerable groups in policymaking.29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37 Additionally, inadequate funding and a lack of insurance coverage are significant challenges in delivering timely and high-quality services to refugees and immigrants.5

Recommendations for the development of palliative care for immigrants and refugees

The need for palliative care is universal, tangible, and ever-present. Through the entire process, it has become evident that opportunities exist to surmount challenges such as the lack of guidance and expertise in palliative care, access to medications, and the cultural nuances related to death and dying when delivering palliative care during humanitarian crises.38

Providing accessible and comprehensive palliative care is imperative. Integration of palliative care into the PHC system should be pursued with a focus on coordinating it with other health care practices. Services should be designed to be physically accessible and culturally compatible for diverse communities and minorities, with an emphasis on dignity-based care.12,39 Achieving minimal disruption in health care provision, particularly palliative care, for marginalized communities necessitates a combination of efficient management, political commitment, community collaboration, and the resilience of health care systems.38 To enhance equity, a multi-faceted approach is required that promotes change at various levels, including individual (patient and caregiver), interpersonal (health care team), organizational, and policy levels. Addressing disparities in palliative care should encompass improvements in clinical practice, as well as initiatives for diversity, inclusion, and accessibility in education, training, and research.40

Effective communication and interaction with patients and their families concerning treatment, care plans, and appropriate psychosocial services are indispensable in health care provision. Hence, early diagnosis and addressing the multifaceted and distinct requirements of refugees with advanced illnesses and their families, particularly in the realms of psychosocial and spiritual support, are of paramount significance. When formulating and assessing intricate interventions for this specific group in need of palliative care, health care providers must embrace a person-centered approach, acknowledging their unique beliefs, values, experiences, and multifaceted needs.41 For example, when tending to Muslim refugees with advanced illnesses, their end-of-life religious convictions and preferences should be taken into account, recognizing the influential role of religion and culture in this context.42

Furthermore, greater attention should be directed towards the role of nurses in delivering services to this vulnerable population. Nurses are inherently committed to addressing the health care and social needs of individuals, particularly those who are most vulnerable, a commitment underscored by the International Council of Nurses (ICN).43 The explicit mission of the nursing profession is to establish social justice by ensuring equality in health care access.44 Hence, it would be valuable to explore nurses' perspectives on the living conditions of refugee patients.45

The psychological trauma resulting from the events leading to seeking refuge often emerges when refugees are resettled in the host country. Addressing this process effectively necessitates appropriate training and experience, a goal that can be readily achieved by employing trained nurses.46

Refugee Health Nursing is a burgeoning nursing specialization rooted in a human rights approach and the consideration of social determinants of health. The role of nurses in refugee health care varies from one country to another and from one region to another.47 Nurses may be enlisted in the non-governmental sector, including well-known organizations like Médecins Sans Frontières, Save the Children, the International Red Cross and Red Crescent, or may serve in the front lines of their own countries embroiled in conflict and war.48 The primary responsibilities of refugee health nursing encompass providing primary care, exhibiting cultural responsiveness, promoting health literacy and empowerment, and assisting patients in navigating care systems. A pivotal aspect of refugee health nursing is the diagnosis and management of chronic diseases.47

It is imperative for member countries, UN agencies, civil society organizations, and charities to collectively enhance their involvement and responsibility.5 Moreover, there is a need to establish organizational capacities that support research on the health of refugees and immigrants.5 Further research is essential to investigate variations in the use and quality of palliative care, determinants of such differences, understanding the palliative care needs of patients and their families, and implementing interventions to reduce disparities and enhance care delivery to critically ill minorities, thereby customizing care models.22 Additionally, promoting awareness within the community, establishing cultural champions, providing culturally appropriate and translated resources, training professional interpreters in the concepts and terminology of palliative care, enhancing cultural competence among health care providers, conducting training to deepen the understanding of the refugee experience, employing culturally appropriate communication, facilitating cultural aspects of service delivery, investing time in building relationships with individuals, families, and communities, and ensuring access to telehealth services, particularly for those residing in rural and remote areas, are promising and inspiring approaches that can be embraced.18
