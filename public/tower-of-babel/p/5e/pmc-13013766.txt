Dermatologists’ Perspectives and Real-World Assessment of Alopecia Areata Severity in Adults in Taiwan: A Cross-sectional Survey
Authors: Chih-Chiang Chen, Chao-Chun Yang, Sung-Jan Lin, Jenny Austin, Samantha K. Kurosky, Kent Hanson, Melody Yen, Peter Anderson, Gerardo A. Encinas, Juliana M. Canosa, Simran Marwaha, Wen-Hung Chung

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAlopecia areata (AA) is an autoimmune disease characterized by nonscarring hair loss that may impact quality of life (QoL). After 2021, new therapies were approved to treat severe AA in Taiwan; however, there is no global definition of severe AA. Dermatologists may use other criteria in addition to scalp hair loss (SHL) when estimating severity. This study evaluates the impact of AA on patient QoL and how dermatologists assessed disease severity and managed AA in Taiwan prior to availability of new therapies.MethodsDermatologists completed the Adelphi Real World AA Disease Specific Programme™ cross-sectional survey. Each provided their perspective on assessing AA severity and available AA treatments, and information on patients consulting with AA. Results were analyzed descriptively and stratified by dermatologist-assessed AA severity.ResultsFifty dermatologists reported median (interquartile range) SHL of 45% (31–51%) and 76% (51–90%) as typical of “severe” and “very severe” AA, respectively, based on 285 patients. Introduction

Alopecia areata (AA) is an autoimmune disease characterized by patchy or complete nonscarring hair loss on the scalp, with or without additional hair loss on the face and/or body [1]. The disease course can be unpredictable, with periods of hair loss and regrowth. AA can develop at any age, but 40% of patients first present with symptoms before the age of 20 years, and up to 88% of cases appear by the age of 40 years [2–6]. Globally, AA has an estimated prevalence of 0.58–2% [7–10]. In Asia, the prevalence of AA varies across study populations and countries, ranging from 0.24% to 2.5% [11–13].

AA is associated with decreased quality of life (QoL). Individuals with AA experience higher levels of anxiety and depression compared with individuals without AA [3, 14–19]. Impacts on mental health have been seen in multiple studies conducted across countries worldwide [17, 20–23]. Patients with AA often report negative impacts on major life decisions in areas such as education, relationships, and career [24]. Studies evaluating the different aspects of the humanistic burden of AA (defined as the impact on health-related QoL and daily activities) have found workplace impairments across multiple countries globally [22, 25, 26].

Historically, therapies prescribed as treatment for AA have included topical, intralesional, and systemic corticosteroids; immunosuppressants; phototherapy; and oral or topical minoxidil [27, 28]. However, within the past 3 years, baricitinib (a JAK1/2 inhibitor [29]) and ritlecitinib (a JAK3/TEC family kinase inhibitor [30]) were approved to treat adults with severe AA and patients ≥ 12 years of age, respectively, in the USA, Japan, EU, China, Taiwan, and several other countries. Deuruxolitinib, another oral JAK1/2 inhibitor, is also approved to treat severe AA in adults in the USA [31]. Patients with ≥ 50% scalp hair loss were included in two phase 3 clinical trials for baricitinib, a phase 3 trial for deuruxolitinib, and a phase 2b/3 clinical trial for ritlecitinib. However, there is no globally accepted definition of severe AA, which leaves determination of disease severity at the discretion of the healthcare provider. As a result of the high impact of AA on QoL, dermatologists may rely on criteria other than degree of scalp hair loss when estimating severity.

This study aimed to describe the impact of AA on patient QoL as reported by their dermatologist, and to understand how dermatologists assessed disease severity and managed AA in Taiwan prior to the approval of baricitinib and ritlecitinib.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This study conducted a retrospective analysis of data from the Adelphi Real World AA Disease Specific Programme™—a cross-sectional survey with elements of retrospective data collection that captured information through responses from dermatologists—and a review of patient medical records in Taiwan from October 2021 to February 2022. DSPs™ are large, multinational surveys conducted in clinical practice that describe current disease management, disease-burden impact, and associated treatment effects (clinical and physician-perceived). The DSP™ is a point-in-time survey of physicians presenting in a real-world clinical setting, while the medical records were provided directly by the physicians participating in the DSP™.

Dermatologists participating in the study completed a survey that captured their perspectives on assessing the severity of AA and available AA treatments. Each dermatologist completed detailed record forms for six patients with AA (including one patient with currently mild AA, three with currently moderate AA, and two with currently severe or very severe AA) who visited the dermatologist for routine care based on information gathered during consultations and retrospective data from the patients’ medical records. This physician-reported questionnaire form contains detailed questions on patient demographics, clinical characteristics, and treatment history and impact of disease on patients' QoL. Completion of the physician-reported questionnaire was undertaken through consultation of existing patient clinical records, as well as the judgment and diagnostic skills of the respondent physician, which is entirely consistent with decisions made in routine clinical practice. Each patient’s AA severity was assessed subjectively by each dermatologist. Physicians also completed a survey which included their perceptions of AA diagnosis, severity, and treatment. Eligibility criteria for patient selection included age ≥ 18 years, a physician-confirmed diagnosis of AA, and no current involvement in a clinical trial for AA. A geographically representative sample of physicians were recruited to participate in the DSP™. Dermatologists were eligible to participate in this survey if they were personally responsible for treatment decisions and management of patients with AA and had a minimum monthly workload of at least six patients.

Data collection was undertaken in line with European Pharmaceutical Market Research Association guidelines [32], and as such this study does not require ethics committee approval. The study protocol was reviewed by the Western Institutional Review Board and deemed to be exempt. The study was also conducted in accordance with the International Council for Harmonisation Guideline for Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki. It should be noted that the survey was designed to facilitate understanding of real-world clinical practice, and thus physicians could only report on data they had to hand at the time of the consultation. Therefore, this represents the evidence they had when making any clinical treatment and other management decisions at that consultation. No tests, treatments, or investigations were performed as part of this survey. Physician participation was financially incentivized, with reimbursement upon survey completion according to fair market research rates. Patients were not compensated for participation.

Results from the study were analyzed descriptively and stratified by dermatologist-assessed percentage scalp hair loss at the time of data collection. Statistical tests were two-sided with a significance level set at α = 0.05. No adjustments were made for multiplicity.

Results

Participant Demographic Characteristics

Overall, 50 dermatologists in Taiwan participated in the study. Among these, 35 self-identified as hair specialists. Information on 300 patients was provided; however, 15 patients were excluded from the analyses because of missing records on percentage scalp hair loss.

Dermatologist Perceptions of AA Severity

Dermatologist-reported severity generally corresponded to levels of scalp hair loss (Fig. S1). Overall, 96% of patients subjectively categorized as currently having mild AA had < 20% scalp hair loss, 73% categorized as currently having moderate AA had 21% to ≤ 50% scalp hair loss, and 70% categorized as currently having severe/very severe AA had > 50% scalp hair loss. Despite this correlation, there was variability in dermatologists’ perceptions of severity (Fig. 1). Dermatologists reported median (IQR) scalp hair loss of 11% (11–26%) as typical for moderate AA, 45% (31–51%) as typical of severe AA, and 75% (51–90%) as typical of very severe AA. Dermatologists reported considering various clinical characteristics beyond extent of scalp hair loss when assessing disease severity (Fig. 2). The factors most frequently reported by hair specialists included extent of hair loss (reported by 68.6%), amount of scalp involvement (48.6%), and duration of AA (42.9%). Current Treatment Experiences and Prior Treatment History

Mean (SD) time on current therapy was 8.3 (13.0) months. At the time of the survey, 23.6% of patients had received ≥ 2 lines of treatment (Table S1). Among patients who had discontinued prior treatments, prior treatment lines lasted 6.7–9.5 months on average before patients switched to a new regimen. Patients with severe/very severe AA received a variety of first- and second-line treatments (Fig. 3), and 38.8% had received ≥ 2 lines of treatment at the time of the survey (Table S1). AA Impact on QoL

Dermatologists reported QoL impacts in patients regardless of disease severity; however, those with severe or very severe AA frequently had more severe QoL impacts. In patients with severe/very severe AA, dermatologists frequently reported moderate to severe levels of anxiety/worry (75.8% of patients), depression (69.9%), fear of AA persistence (84.8%), and fear of AA recurrence (79.8%, Fig. S2). Dermatologists also reported high levels of impairment in daily life and activities in patients with severe or very severe AA, including impairment in ability to do what they enjoyed (64.1%), ability to work or study (62.2%), and ability to conduct normal daily actions (58.3%).

Dermatologist Perception of AA Diagnosis and Treatment

Among all dermatologists, most felt equipped with the resources to diagnose AA and expressed confidence in doing so (Fig. 4). The majority of dermatologists considered AA to be a well-understood disease, and all felt that AA should be treated long term. However, few (20%) reported satisfaction with treatment options for severe AA, with 51% disagreeing with the statement. Discussion

In this cross-sectional survey study evaluating the disease burden in adult patients with AA living in Taiwan, dermatologists did not define AA disease severity solely based on extent of scalp hair loss, varied in their definitions of disease severity, and reported perceived impacts on patient QoL regardless of degree of scalp hair loss.

While prior studies have been published focusing on European populations [33], this study specifically examines a population of dermatologists and their patients with AA in Taiwan, and at a time point prior to the introduction of newer targeted therapies. As different cultures and regions may have different responses, there is value in examining how disease burden and severity are assessed across non-European populations.

Dermatologists in Taiwan considered 45% (IQR 31–51%) scalp hair loss to be typical in a patient with severe AA. This aligns with the Alopecia Areata Scale, which considers scalp hair loss ≥ 50% to be severe [34]. Although most patients with mild AA as subjectively determined by their dermatologist had ≤ 20% scalp hair loss, those with subjectively moderate AA had scalp hair loss ranging from 2% to 60%, and those with subjectively severe to very severe AA had 1–100% scalp hair loss, indicating their dermatologists were considering more than just scalp hair loss when evaluating AA severity. While standardized disease assessment scores such as the Severity of Alopecia Tool score [35] are of use to physicians, the results of this study indicate that dermatologists consider a wide range of scalp hair loss to be typical of different disease severity categories. Additional factors including extent of hair loss, duration of disease, patient’s previous response to treatment, amount of patient distress over hair loss, and AA impact on the patient’s QoL were considered of high importance in classifying AA severity. Of note, several of these factors are not measured by objective disease assessment scores, further highlighting the usage of subjective disease assessment in real-world practices.

Understanding outcomes with current treatment lines is difficult because of patients having been on therapy for variable durations. Overall, 62% of dermatologists reported that patients had not been on their current treatment long enough to assess satisfaction with their current therapy. Notably, approximately one-fifth of patients had received ≥ 2 lines of treatment at the time of the survey, and the proportion of patients with ≥ 2 prior treatment lines increased with severity. A lack of response to initial treatment may contribute to severity, with patients having to cycle through multiple treatments over time. Dermatologists considered the following treatment goals to be the most important: reducing scalp hair loss, reducing the psychological impact of AA, improving QoL, achieving long-term AA control, and reducing the impact on a patient’s ability to work or study [36]. However, the majority of dermatologists felt that there was a lack of satisfactory treatments for severe AA meeting longer-term patient needs and that severe AA was thus difficult to treat. Additionally, among patients with severe/very severe AA who initiated second-line treatment, the most frequently reported reasons for switching from first-line therapy were worsening of a patient’s AA and lack of initial efficacy. Collectively, this indicates that at the time of the survey, treatment goals for patients with severe disease were not being met with available therapies. Since the time of the survey, baricitinib, an oral JAK1/2 inhibitor, was approved to treat adults with severe AA, and ritlecitinib, an oral JAK3/TEC family kinase inhibitor, was approved to treat adults and adolescents aged ≥ 12 years with severe AA in Taiwan [37–39]. These therapies, which act on pathways implicated in AA disease pathogenesis, may potentially address unmet needs in patients with severe disease.

Dermatologists reported that patients in Taiwan face substantial psychosocial and QoL impacts due to AA, which increased with dermatologist-assessed AA severity. These results agree with those seen in other countries, such as the USA, Japan, Belgium, the UK, and Nepal [17, 20–23]. One study reported a non-linear relationship between scalp hair loss and health-related QoL based on the results of the Skindex-16 instrument [40]. Across the symptom, emotional, and functioning domains, patients with > 95% hair loss on the scalp reported better health-related QoL than patients with 50–94% hair loss; however, this difference may be due to differences in coping strategies between those with some hair loss and those with complete hair loss, such as the ability to conceal hair loss with a wig [41].

These findings suggest that a more comprehensive evaluation of the patient’s total AA burden should be considered when evaluating disease severity and selecting treatments that can provide the best opportunity for patients to achieve their treatment goals. Future research on patient perspectives and patient-reported outcomes may also be valuable in providing a clear picture on the extent of AA disease burden in Taiwan.

This study had some limitations. The number of dermatologists surveyed was limited, and their experiences may not reflect the experiences of all dermatologists who practice in Taiwan. Sampling quotas based on dermatologist-assessed disease severity may have biased the sample toward severity classes defined most strongly by clinical hair loss characteristics. Sampling was captured from consecutive patients seen by participating dermatologists; patients who are more likely to consult their dermatologist frequently were more likely to be included. Patients also could have been receiving any line of therapy at the time of the study, which limited sample size for several outcomes. Additionally, many dermatologists reported that patients had not been on their current treatment long enough to assess satisfaction. Examination of outcomes with prior treatment lines was limited to patients who had an inadequate response to prior therapies. Dermatologists reported QoL and psychosocial impacts on patients, but these data were not systematically captured in patient medical records and could be biased by the dermatologist’s interpretation of impacts on the patient.

Conclusion

Considering clinical, treatment, and QoL factors provides a more comprehensive approach to assessing baseline disease severity and informing appropriate treatment selection than scalp hair loss alone in patients with AA.
