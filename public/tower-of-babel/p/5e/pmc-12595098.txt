Unifying graph neural networks causal machine learning and conformal prediction for robust causal inference in rail transport systems
Authors: Mehmet Taciddin Akçay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Modeling the operational dynamics of intricate rail transit systems faces three significant challenges: addressing network-wide dependencies, differentiating correlation from causation, and accurately quantifying prediction uncertainty. Current methodologies generally tackle these issues separately, resulting in models that are either structurally simplistic, causally unclear, or overly confident in their forecasts. This paper presents a comprehensive framework that, for the first time, effectively combines Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), Causal Machine Learning (CML), and Conformal Prediction (CP) to resolve this dilemma. GNNs are utilized to capture the topological dependencies within the rail network, CML is employed to discern the unbiased causal impacts of operational interventions, and CP offers mathematically assured, distribution-free uncertainty intervals. Our empirical assessment using real-world operational data reveals a distinct differentiation in model performance: while GNN-enhanced hybrids excel in aggregate prediction tasks (CV R² ≈ 0.87), the proposed CML-CP framework realizes a transformative, order-of-magnitude decrease in causal effect estimation error (CV MAE: 124,758.04). Thus, the primary contribution of this research is not merely a singular “best” model, but rather a methodological roadmap that facilitates a paradigm shift from reactive data modeling to a proactive, strategic decision-support tool. This framework equips decision-makers to perform reliable what-if scenario analyses, supported by robust causal insights and valid uncertainty guarantees, leading to more resilient and efficient railway operations.

Introduction

In today’s complex and dynamic rail transport networks, it is crucial to develop comprehensive and interpretable models for both demand forecasting and system reliability through the integration of multilayered data sources. Graph neural network (GNN) based spatial-temporal convolution approaches have accurately predicted both station and line flows; the foundational studies in this field were established with the residual gated RRC-STGCN model, which successfully captures local and global dependencies1–3. Expanded GNN architectures and reinforcement learning have made significant contributions to infrastructure health and operational control tasks: The increase in prognostic accuracy in high-speed train suspension systems using multi-sensor graph transfer networks, fault localization without the need for raw data sharing through the federated GraphFL framework, the shared representation advantages in MTLMetro’s multi-task demand forecasting, and the capturing of passenger flow models at different scales using hierarchical hypergraph attention networks4–8; additionally, scalable train schedule re-planning and connected autonomous vehicle defense strategies have been developed using graph-based reinforcement learning. Probabilistic modeling and uncertainty quantification have been addressed through various paradigms to ensure reliability in safety-critical applications: Context-aware Bayesian networks for interpretable distribution predictions of train operation parameters9, reviews compiling methods ranging from Monte Carlo Dropout in autonomous vehicles to deep ensembles10–12, the generation of calibrated uncertainty distributions using deep ensemble-based spatial-temporal GNNs13, and the modeling of travel demand uncertainty with probabilistic GNNs14. Online adaptation and the management of epistemic uncertainty have also made significant advancements: Studies that adapt vehicle usage predictions through online learning during the COVID-19 period15, reviews encompassing sources of uncertainty and reduction strategies in embedded algorithms12, the calculation of epistemic uncertainty using two-layer decision-making architectures16, the enhancement of representational reliability through epistemically grounded security-focused reinforcement learning17, and the improvement of reliability in offline reinforcement learning with security-sensitive causal representations18. The role of graphical structures in operational decision forecasting has been established in both delay modeling and direct optimization: Spatial-temporal GNNs for rail system delay predictions19, optimization of the regularization loss function in passenger traffic forecasting through end-to-end learning20, the utilization of spatial and temporal dependencies in delay predictions with graph convolutional networks21, and GNN-based optimization of expanded tariffs in year-end events22. Methods for conformal prediction have been developed to provide valid interval forecasts under distribution shifts and intermittent feedback: Adaptive conformal interval methods for industrial time series addressing distribution shifts23–25, scalable uncertainty quantification using quantum conformal techniques26, and theoretically guaranteed conformal approaches for time series27. Finally, conformal applications based on graphical and sequential structures have been presented for remaining life estimation and meta-learning-based calibration methods for low data regimes: Inductive conformal LSTM-SNN hybrids for bird and UAV recognition28, conformal GNNs for landslide deformation prediction29, uncertainty quantification in fuel cell life prediction30, and cross-validation based conformal calibration with meta-learning in low data regimes31.

In9, Huang and Corman achieved an R² value exceeding 0.9 in train arrival time predictions by employing a Bayesian network approach to model uncertainties in train operations. In10 The review conducted by Wang and colleagues highlighted that uncertainty measurement methods used for autonomous vehicles have the potential to reduce accident risk by up to 15%. In13, Mallick and associates demonstrated over 90% reliability in traffic congestion predictions using their deep community-based spatiotemporal GNN model. Furthermore in14, Wang and his team successfully narrowed the uncertainty range in spatiotemporal travel demand forecasts to 20% by utilizing probabilistic graph neural networks. Despite the significant advancements in the literature, current studies typically focus on only one or two of the three main axes (network modeling with GNN, causal inference, and uncertainty measurement). The lack of an integrated framework that fully comprehends the complex and interconnected nature of railway operations; combines the network-cognizant representation power of GNNs with the interpretability and intervention analysis capabilities of Causal Machine Learning (Causal ML); and offers rigorous uncertainty guarantees that are distribution-independent through Conformal Prediction, represents a clear gap in the field. This article aims to precisely fill this gap. In this study, a unified theoretical and practical framework (A Unified Framework for Causal, Uncertainty-Aware, and Network-Cognizant Modeling) is presented, which integrates GNN, Causal ML, and Conformal Prediction for modeling railway transit operations. The proposed framework not only enhances the accuracy of operational predictions but also causally analyzes the potential outcomes of various interventions, providing decision-makers with a previously unavailable, holistic perspective by underpinning all these analyses with mathematically sound and reliable uncertainty intervals.

Research design and data collection

Dataset and feature extraction

The operational data utilized in this study is derived from the Istanbul Metropolitan Municipality (IMM) Open Data Portal and encompasses a dataset consisting of 288 observations32. This dataset includes 10 independent variables that encompass the physical and structural characteristics of the line (Line Code, Length, Age, Number of Stations) and operational dynamics (Year, Period, Frequency of Trips, Duration of Trips, Number of Vehicles, Number of Passengers) to predict our target variable, total Vehicle KM. Prior to modeling, categorical features were converted into numerical format to prepare the raw data for analysis, and standardization was applied to address scale differences among continuous variables. To assess the generalization performance and robustness of the developed model, a two-stage validation strategy was adopted: a hold-out test set was reserved for final and unbiased performance evaluation, while 5-fold cross-validation was employed on the training data to prevent overfitting and to reliably conduct hyperparameter optimization. Foundational models

The foundational framework of this study is based on the integration of three synergistic methodologies designed to address three fundamental challenges of railway operations: network dependency, causality, and prediction uncertainty. Firstly, Graph Neural Networks (GNN) have been utilized to capture the complex topological and operational dependencies inherent in the railway network. GNN learns rich, network-cognizant feature representations from spatial and temporal relationships to predict system-level metrics such as vehicle-kilometers traveled (VKT) by representing stations as nodes and lines as edges. However, since merely correlational relationships can obscure the true effects of operational interventions, our framework incorporates Causal Machine Learning techniques. This layer aims to quantitatively isolate and predict the causal effects of external interventions, such as tariff changes or failures, on VKT by controlling for confounding factors. Finally, to address the reliability gap of deterministic predictions in practical decision-making processes, the Conformal Prediction methodology is applied to ensure the validity of model outputs with theoretical guarantees. This approach equips each point prediction derived from the GNN and causal model with a distribution-free prediction interval that is guaranteed to contain the true value at a predefined confidence level (95%). This tripartite integration offers a causally robust and uncertainty-aware, cohesive solution for modeling railway operations.

Proposed hybrid frameworks

In order to demonstrate the fundamental logic and superiority of our ultimate unified framework, designed to address the multifaceted complexities inherent in railway operational systems, this study proposes and meticulously analyzes three pioneering hybrid structures based on the binary combinations of our core methodologies. These hybrids serve as a comprehensive ablation study, systematically revealing the marginal contributions and indispensability of each methodological component (network awareness, causality, and uncertainty). The first of these approaches, the GNN-Causal ML hybrid, integrates the rich, high-dimensional spatio-temporal features learned from the network topology and dynamics of Graph Neural Networks (GNN) as a robust set of control variables for a causal inference model. The primary aim of this integration is to eliminate spurious correlations that are prevalent in railway networks and can mislead naive analyses of intervention effects. Consequently, it can produce an unbiased estimate of the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) by isolating the impact of an intervention, such as changes in tariff, from the indirect and confounding effects that propagate throughout the network. The second hybrid presented, GNN-CP, combines the superior network-dependent predictive power of GNN with the theoretically robust uncertainty measurement capabilities of CP. This structure directly addresses the issue of overconfidence inherent in deterministic point predictions. Its output not only provides a VKT estimate but also guarantees a predefined coverage probability (95%) even within a finite sample. The third and final hybrid, Causal ML-Conformal Prediction, intentionally disregards the network structure and focuses on directly quantifying the uncertainty in predicting the causal effect of an intervention. This framework first estimates the causal effect of the intervention on the VKT and subsequently creates a confidence interval for this effect. This transition allows for a shift from the question, “How significant is the effect of the intervention?” to a deeper and more strategic inquiry, “How much confidence can we place in this effect estimate?” A comprehensive and comparative analysis of these three hybrid structures illuminates the blind spots where each methodological approach falls short on its own: GNN-CML overlooks uncertainty, GNN-CP is blind to causal mechanisms, and CML-CP neglects the critical role of network topology. Consequently, this systematic review clearly demonstrates that only the complete integration of GNN, CML and CP offers a synergistic and superior approach capable of simultaneously addressing the trilemma of network dependency, causality, and uncertainty in modeling railway operations.

This study systematically summarizes the operational workflows and methodological integration of the proposed GNN-CML, GNN-CP, and CML-CP hybrid frameworks by developing a comprehensive procedure that combines these approaches. The presented algorithm illustrates step-by-step how the outputs obtained from the training of base models (such as learned network representations or predictions) become meta-features or calibration scores for the second-stage processes. This structure clearly demonstrates how each hybrid model shares the same fundamental data preparation and parameter tuning steps while also highlighting how their analytical journeys diverge, ultimately producing different yet complementary outputs. The methodological framework outlined in this study aims to guarantee complete transparency in the data flow mechanism and the logical relationships among modules. The workflow initiates with Stage 0, where raw data (covariates X, intervention T, outcome Y) and the global graph structure (G) are organized, followed by the segmentation of datasets in Stage 1 utilizing standard validation techniques (e.g., Hold-Out, CV). Building upon this groundwork, the framework is segmented into three parallel hybrid pipelines in Stage 2. Hybrid 1 concentrates on standard predictive uncertainty, generating point predictions (Ŷ(x)) through a GNN model and converting them into assured Prediction Intervals (PI) via a Split-Conformal Prediction module. The framework subsequently introduces two separate pathways for causal inference. Hybrid 2, which tests our core hypothesis, demonstrates an advanced parameter transfer where network embeddings (Z_GNN) from a GNN extractor enhance the original controls (X). This augmented dataset (X_aug) is then input into a final DML model to yield GNN-supported CATE predictions (τ_gnn), striving for the most precise point estimate of the causal effect. Conversely, Hybrid 3 presents a methodologically unique, two-stage meta-learning strategy to develop valid Causal Intervals (CI). Initially, a “teacher” DML model is trained to produce high-fidelity CATE predictions, which act as an observable, “pseudo-true” target (τ_pseudo_true). Following this, a second “student” model is trained to emulate these teacher-generated targets. Conformal Prediction is subsequently applied to the prediction errors of the student model against this pseudo-true target to formulate the final, mathematically guaranteed Causal Intervals. Ultimately, in Stage 3, all outputs are assessed using metrics such as coverage rate and interval width. This structure facilitates a principled comparison that not only quantifies the value added by GNNs for point-estimate accuracy (Hybrid 2) but also robustly evaluates causal uncertainty through a valid, reproducible process.

Computational complexity and comparison with literature

In this section, we analyze the computational complexity of our integrated framework to evaluate its scalability and to position it within the existing literature by clarifying the trade-offs between complexity and analytical power. The total complexity of our framework is a composite of its three main stages:

GNN Module: The training of the Graph Convolutional Network (GCN), as implemented in our code using torch_geometric.nn.GCNConv, is the most computationally demanding step. The time complexity of a GCN is approximately O(L ⋅ |E| ⋅ F), where L is the number of GNN layers, |E| is the number of edges in the graph, and F is the feature dimension. This complexity arises because GCN performs full-graph propagation, meaning each layer’s computation involves aggregating information across all specified edges. In our study, where the graph is constructed using a k-nearest neighbors approach, |E| scales linearly with the number of nodes (|V|), but this formulation remains the most accurate representation of the workload.

Causal ML (DoubleML) Module: The complexity of this stage is dominated by the training of the nuisance models. Our implementation utilizes Random Forest Regressors for both the outcome model (model_y) and the treatment model (model_t). The complexity for training a Random Forest is approximately O(K ⋅ N ⋅ D ⋅ F’), where K is the number of trees (n_estimators), N is the number of observations, D is the maximum tree depth, and F’ is the number of features considered for splits. Since our DoubleML framework requires training two such models, the overall complexity remains in this order. In the GNN + CML pipeline, F’ includes both original and GNN-derived features, making this step more intensive than a standard DML application.

Conformal Prediction (CP) Module: The additional computational overhead of the mapie library for Conformal Prediction is negligible. The process involves training the base estimator (a Random Forest, whose complexity is already accounted for) and then calculating non-conformity scores for a calibration set. The most significant added step is sorting these scores, which has a complexity of O(N_calib ⋅ log(N_calib)), where N_calib is the size of the calibration set. This step is computationally trivial compared to the model training stages.

To justify the framework’s complexity, we compare it against two primary classes of models prevalent in the transportation literature:

Comparison with Predictive Spatiotemporal GNNs: Models like RRC-STGCN or other GCNs used for delay prediction share a similar GNN training complexity (often in the order of O(L ⋅ |E| ⋅ F)). They are designed for high-accuracy prediction. Our framework introduces the additional complexity of the DML module, specifically the O(K ⋅ N ⋅ D ⋅ F’) cost from training the Random Forest nuisance models. This added computational cost is the explicit and justified price paid to move beyond mere prediction to robust causal inference. While their goal is to answer “what will happen?“, our framework answers the far more complex strategic question, “what is the unbiased effect if we intervene?“.

Comparison with Non-Network Causal Models: Standard applications of causal inference (including DML on tabular data) would have their complexity limited to the DML module (O(K ⋅ N ⋅ D ⋅ F’)). These models are computationally cheaper as they lack the intensive GNN training step. However, they are methodologically incapable of capturing network dependencies, treating each rail line as an independent entity. This leads to a high risk of omitted-variable bias, as network effects act as unobserved confounders. The O(L ⋅ |E| ⋅ F) complexity of our GCN module is therefore the necessary investment to mitigate this bias by explicitly learning and controlling for the topological structure of the system.

Results and discussion

This section presents comprehensive analyses conducted to demonstrate the effectiveness and validity of the integrated modeling framework developed. To model the complex and dynamic nature of rail system operations, various methods have been evaluated both individually and in hybrid configurations such as GNN + Conformal Prediction, GNN + Causal ML, and Causal ML + Conformal Prediction. These methods include Graph Neural Networks (GNN), known for their high prediction accuracy, gradient boosting machines like XGBoost and CatBoost, and Conformal Prediction for measuring model uncertainty, as well as Causal Machine Learning (Causal ML) aimed at uncovering causal relationships between events. To impartially assess the performance and generalization capability of the models, two different and widely accepted validation methods in the literature have been meticulously applied: hold-out, where 80% of the dataset is allocated for training and 20% for testing, and 5-fold cross-validation. This dual-phase validation strategy ensures that the results obtained are statistically significant and reliable, thereby ensuring that the developed framework meets scientific standards.

To begin, we utilized SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) to assess the influence of each feature on the outcome model’s prediction of Vehicle_KM. This finding directly challenges the notion that the model’s efficacy is solely attributable to traditional features; rather, it affirms that the network-aware representations acquired by the GNN are essential to the model’s decision-making framework. Furthermore, to transition from importance to interpretation, we explored the correlation between these abstract GNN features and conventional graph-theoretic metrics. In contrast, features such as gnn_feat_1 and gnn_feat_3 reveal a significant negative correlation with the clustering coefficient, suggesting that the GNN differentiates between nodes that belong to dense, localized clusters and those that act as connectors between various sections of the network.

Overall, the findings from the SHAP analysis Fig. 15 and the topological correlation Table 2 offer a strong, multi-dimensional validation of our methodology. This illustrates that our GNN component is not simply a sophisticated regressor but a valuable feature extractor that effectively learns and applies valid, interpretable insights regarding the underlying network topology, thereby reinforcing the basis of our GNN-enhanced frameworks.

The empirical results, when holistically interpreted, present a nuanced narrative regarding the roles and synergies of predictive accuracy, causal inference, and network-aware modeling in rail transit operations. The central finding of this study is not simply the identification of a single superior model, but the elucidation of a spectrum of hybrid frameworks, each tailored to a distinct analytical objective. Our analysis reveals that the most significant methodological advancement, the CML + CP framework, paradoxically exhibits a modest R² value (0.680). This metric, however, should not be misconstrued as indicative of poor performance; rather, it is a testament to the model’s success in isolating the comparatively small variance attributable solely to the treatment effect, after having partialled out the vast variance explained by the system’s complex confounding factors. To exemplify this important distinction with a practical illustration from our field, let us consider the objective of assessing the causal impact of adding an additional vehicle to the fleet of a particular metro line (the “treatment”) on the total daily Vehicle-Kilometers (the “outcome”). A predictive model with a high R² would strive to estimate the total Vehicle-KM by utilizing all significant predictors at its disposal: the total length of the line, the number of stations, overall passenger density, and network topology characteristics derived from the GNN. These structural elements account for the majority of the variance in Vehicle-KM, thereby enabling the model to achieve a high R². Conversely, our CML + CP causal framework pursues a fundamentally different and more intricate objective: to accurately isolate the marginal effect that can be attributed solely to the addition of that one vehicle, after statistically controlling for the dominant influences of line length, passenger counts, and other confounding variables. The variance explained exclusively by the addition of one vehicle is, by nature, a very small portion of the total operational variance. Consequently, the model’s relatively low R² should not be interpreted as a failure; instead, it serves as clear evidence of its success in its precise task of isolating a small, specific causal effect. The effectiveness of the model is evaluated not by its R², but by the minimal error (CV MAE: 124,758.04) in estimating this particular causal quantity. This stands in stark contrast to the GNN-based hybrids (GNN + CML and GNN + CP), which function as powerful predictive enhancers, leveraging the GNN’s topological awareness to achieve high R² values (0.85–0.87) by modeling the outcome variable in its entirety. This key distinction delineates between models optimized for prediction and our primary contribution: a framework optimized for high-fidelity causal explanation.

This research consequently provides a critical answer to the pragmatic question of “which model should be used when?” Our findings propose a methodological roadmap for practitioners and researchers in transportation science. For objectives centered on forecasting, such as predicting future operational loads or passenger kilometers where aggregate accuracy is paramount, the GNN-integrated frameworks are the models of choice due to their superior predictive power. However, for ex-post policy evaluation or ex-ante impact assessment—where the precise, unbiased effect of a specific intervention is required—the CML + CP framework becomes indispensable. Its unparalleled precision in isolating the causal impact of adding a vehicle, for instance, provides a robust tool for evidence-based decision-making. Thus, our study moves beyond a monolithic view of model performance to offer a context-dependent guide for applying the right advanced methodology to the right operational question.

The implications of this synthesis extend significantly into both policy and theory. From a policy perspective, the CML + CP framework equips transit authorities with a tool to conduct highly reliable cost-benefit analyses, confidently attributing observed changes in operational metrics to specific interventions rather than to confounding network dynamics. This allows for the optimization of resource allocation with a degree of certainty that conventional predictive models cannot offer. Theoretically, our work forges a crucial link between the domains of geometric deep learning and econometric causal inference. We demonstrate that GNNs can be elevated from their typical role as ‘black-box’ predictors to serve as structured instruments for representing complex confounding relationships within a rigorous causal framework. This integration contributes to a new, more robust class of models that are structurally aware, causally sound, and cognizant of their own uncertainty.

The empirical findings, when interpreted in a comprehensive manner, reveal a complex narrative concerning the unique functions and interactions of predictive accuracy and causal inference within rail transit operations. A key discovery that requires careful consideration is the relatively low R² value (0.680) of the CML + CP framework, which sharply contrasts with its significant reduction in the error of causal effect estimation. This apparent inconsistency should not be interpreted as inadequate model performance. The R² statistic serves as an indicator of predictive accuracy, measuring the fraction of total variance in the outcome that a model can elucidate. Nevertheless, the aim of our CML + CP framework is not to fully predict the outcome but to accurately estimate the variance that can be exclusively attributed to the treatment effect, after accounting for the substantial variance explained by the intricate confounding factors of the system. In this regard, a modest R² reflects the model’s effectiveness in achieving this isolation. To provide the statistical evidence necessary to support this claim, we conducted an outcome permutation placebo test as shown in Table 6. In this test, the target variable was randomly permuted, and the entire training pipeline was re-evaluated. As anticipated, the predictive performance under the placebo condition was significantly inferior to that of the original model (observed MAE = 410141 compared to placebo mean = 4728474; empirical p-value (probability value) = 0.00498). This strongly suggests that our model is capturing a true signal rather than simply fitting to random noise. Equally significant is the assessment of the GNN + CML framework’s performance. It is accurate to note that its predictive accuracy (CV R² = 0.852) is only slightly superior to that of the standalone GNN (CV R² = 0.7864); however, this comparison misrepresents the fundamental aim of this hybrid model. The ‘added value of the causal component’ in the GNN + CML framework is not intended to substantially increase predictive R², but rather to enhance robustness and mitigate potential bias in the causal estimate itself. The intricate, topological features derived from the GNN act as effective proxies for unobserved or difficult-to-measure confounders (such as network centrality and passenger transfer patterns). By integrating these network embeddings as control variables, the causal model can more effectively separate the genuine impact of the intervention from confounding network dynamics. Consequently, although its predictive performance is comparable to that of a standard GNN, the GNN + CML framework is structured to yield a more dependable and theoretically robust causal inference, which represents a fundamentally different goal than mere prediction. This crucial distinction differentiates between models that are optimized for prediction and our primary contribution: a framework that is optimized for high-fidelity causal explanation.

A practical Decision-Support scenario: evaluating an intervention in trip frequency

This subsection clearly and practically presents how the proposed hybrid frameworks can support concrete decision-making in rail system operations, the data and infrastructure requirements involved, and how the model outputs can be integrated into dispatch systems. The proposed framework not only offers a theoretical method but also includes concrete implementation steps that can be directly applied in rail system operations to support dispatch decisions: three distinct intervention scenarios altering service frequency, advancing/postponing maintenance schedules, and adding or removing vehicles are each modeled as a “treatment” variable; neighboring line and network effects are adjusted using a GNN component, and causal effect magnitudes are calculated as CATE and ATE; the measure of uncertainty is reported as 95% confidence intervals through CP. For example, in a situation assessing an increase in service frequency during peak hours on a significant metro line, the framework could not only compute a Causal Average Treatment Effect (CATE) of an extra 8,500 Vehicle-Kilometers (VKM) daily but also offer an essential 95% confidence interval of [+ 6,200, + 10,800] VKM. The exclusion of zero from this interval provides decision-makers with robust statistical assurance that the observed positive effect is genuine and not merely due to random variations. The results are converted into numerical decision metrics, which encompass anticipated variations in VKT, their confidence intervals, and the consequent financial ramifications. Following our example,, the VKM forecast would be automatically converted into economic terms, indicating an anticipated additional operational expense of 12,000 UM (Unit Money) in contrast to a projected advantage of 18,500 UM, thereby determining a distinct net benefit of 6,500 UM, decision criteria are clearly defined from an operational perspective for instance, if the 95% confidence interval excludes zero and the calculated net benefit exceeds a predetermined operational threshold (such as equivalent to a vehicle-hour cost or a daily minimum economic return threshold), the proposal is automatically moved to the implementation line; otherwise, in the case of positive expected benefit, the proposal is submitted for operator approval (human-in-the-loop). The data contract and output schema for operational integration have been clearly defined: the input includes fields such as route ID, timestamp, service frequency, number of vehicles, maintenance events, passenger count, service durations, route length, and environmental indicators; on the output side, it encompasses query ID, target intervention definition, ATE/CATE predictions, lower/upper CI, expected ΔVKT, anticipated cost/benefit changes, recommended actions, and model metadata. The distribution architecture has been designed according to practical requirements: model development and retraining are conducted through offline batch processes; the production (inference) environment is delivered via models packaged in Docker containers through Representational State Transfer (REST) or gRPC services; for real-time data streaming, a messaging infrastructure similar to Apache Kafka is recommended, along with workflow tools akin to Airflow for orchestrating periodic tasks and monitoring dashboards similar to Prometheus/Grafana for integration and operational health monitoring. Latency targets have been characterized based on the usage layer: daily or weekly batches for strategic analyses; near-real-time pipelines for tactical planning (for instance, aiming for result generation within one hour); and for emergency dispatch recommendations, pre-computed scenario tables or lightly accelerated ‘surrogate’ models are utilized to achieve response times of less than five minutes while maintaining accuracy. Finally, this comprehensive process guarantees that a complicated operational inquiry such as ‘should we enhance service frequency?’ is converted into a straightforward, evidence-supported business case: ‘Increasing peak-hour frequency on this line is anticipated to generate a net benefit of around 6,500 UM daily with 95% confidence.’ This shifts the decision-making process from an intuition-driven evaluation to a solid, measurable, and risk-conscious analysis, facilitating a genuinely dependable, repeatable, and actionable incorporation into dispatch and operational software.

Conclusion

This research addressed three ongoing challenges in rail transit modeling—network dependency, causality, and uncertainty—not as isolated issues, but as an interconnected trilemma that necessitates a cohesive solution. Our primary finding is the formulation of a “performance-for-purpose” principle, which acts as an essential guide for both researchers and practitioners. We have empirically shown that while GNN-enhanced hybrids perform exceptionally well in aggregate prediction tasks, their main advantage lies in their forecasting capabilities. In contrast, the CML-CP framework is recognized as the distinctly superior instrument for high-fidelity causal inference, a critical function for policy evaluation. This differentiation resolves the apparent contradiction in performance metrics and elucidates the fundamental contribution of our work: delivering the appropriate tool for the appropriate analytical task.

Methodologically, we established a crucial connection between geometric deep learning and econometric analysis, positioning GNNs as effective instruments for depicting complex confounding structures. Practically, as demonstrated in our new decision-support scenario, this provides transit authorities with a reliable, uncertainty-aware tool for evidence-based decision-making. While we acknowledge the limitations of our current empirical scope, which sets the stage for future research, this work establishes the foundational groundwork for a new generation of analytical tools, fostering a paradigm shift from a singular focus on predictive accuracy to a more holistic, causally-informed, and uncertainty-aware approach to managing intricate operational systems.

Limitations and future research

Our proposed framework represents a notable methodological improvement by integrating network-aware, causal, and uncertainty-quantified modeling. However, we recognize several limitations inherent in this foundational study that highlight the need for essential future research. Firstly, and most importantly, our empirical validation is based on a singular experimental scheme, relying exclusively on observational data from the Istanbul urban rail network. While this serves as a strong proof-of-concept, the results may not be readily generalizable to other systems with differing characteristics. Future research must prioritize cross-regional and cross-scenario validation. Evaluating the framework’s adaptability to rail networks with diverse topological structures (e.g., monocentric versus polycentric cities), operational paradigms (e.g., freight versus passenger rail), and varying data densities is a vital next step to confirm its wider applicability. Secondly, our existing models were trained on data that reflects routine, everyday operations. As a result, the framework has not been validated in extreme, non-stationary scenarios. The true measure of a model’s capability to “address uncertainty” is its performance during events such as significant line disruptions due to technical failures or severe weather, or sudden passenger influxes from large-scale public events. Future iterations should integrate methods for anomaly detection and dynamic, online learning to guarantee the framework’s resilience and decision-support value during critical operational moments. Lastly, the interpretability of the GNN component, although partially addressed, continues to pose a challenge. Future research should aim to develop advanced eXplainable AI (XAI) techniques specifically designed for GNNs within causal contexts. Identifying which particular network features are most influential as confounders would not only improve transparency but also provide deeper, actionable insights for network planners and operators. Overcoming these limitations is crucial for evolving this framework from a robust analytical instrument into a completely integrated, practical strategic decision-support system.
