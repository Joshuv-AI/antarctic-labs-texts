Photothermal reprogramming of synovial M1 macrophages reshapes the pro-inflammatory microenvironment to reverse temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis
Authors: Yanyi Wang, Jiangyan Ren, Tingyu Ren, Baochao Li, Jianchuan Ran, Huijuan Wang, Ziwei Huang, Diya Xie, Tao Liu, Linzhong Yang, Jinglun Zhang, Caixia Zhang, Zhi Wang, Xingyu Luo, Lei Zheng, Xiaoji Xie, Huang Li, Wei Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis (TMJOA) involves progressive synovial inflammation-driven cartilage degeneration. While thermotherapy empirically alleviates symptoms, its immunomodulatory mechanism remains elucidated. Here, we revealed that targeted mild hyperthermia reprogramed synovial M1 macrophages via NF-κB pathway suppression, shifting their secretome to attenuate chondrocyte catabolism while enhancing anabolism. This thermally-modified macrophage conditioned medium concurrently promoted osteogenic mineralization. Capitalizing on this, we engineered folic acid-conjugated Y8 nanocomposites (FA-Y8 NPs) for precision M1-targeting photothermal therapy. Under 808 nm irradiation, FA-Y8 NPs achieved localized photothermal reprogramming of synovial M1 macrophages and attenuating cartilage degradation in CFA-induced TMJOA mice. This study reveals thermal immunomodulation of synovial macrophages as a potential mechanism for TMJOA remission and establishes a targeted nanoplatform for spatial control of joint inflammation.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04258-9.

Introduction

Temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis (TMJOA) represents a prevalent degenerative disorder with an incidence ranging from 14.6% in adults under 30 years old to 28–32% in the 30–80-year cohort. This disorder is typified by progressive synovitis, cartilage degradation, and subchondral bone destruction [1, 2]. However, current clinical management ranging from pharmacotherapy and physiotherapy to surgical intervention remains largely palliative, limited by systemic toxicity at efficacious doses and significant individual variation in efficacy [3]. Surgical reconstruction, typically reserved for end-stage disease, is invasive, costly, and often irreversible [4]. Therefore, developing targeted, effective, and minimally invasive therapeutic strategies to modify the underlying pathological process of TMJOA is an urgent need.

Emerging evidence highlights that synovial macrophages play a significant role in driving TMJOA pathogenesis through excessive secretion of inflammatory cytokines, leading to synovial hyperplasia and cartilage catabolism [5]. Among these, the imbalance between pro-inflammatory M1 and anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage subtypes critically determines disease progression [6, 7]. Modulating macrophage polarization has thus emerged as a favorable immunotherapeutic strategy. Recent studies have proposed engineered nanoparticles to regulate macrophage behavior through inhibiting recruitment, promoting apoptosis, or reprogramming M1 to M2 phenotypes [8, 9]. These findings collectively identify synovial macrophages as a key therapeutic target for reversing TMJOA-associated inflammation and tissue degeneration.

Superficial thermotherapy is a widely used conservative treatment for TMJOA that can alleviate pain and improve mandibular mobility by promoting vasodilation and metabolic waste clearance [10]. However, its underlying immunomodulatory mechanisms remain poorly defined. Meanwhile, conventional heat delivery methods such as electric heating pads or hot packs suffer from limited tissue penetration, poor temperature control, and potential collateral damage [10]. To overcome these limitations, photothermal therapy (PTT) emerges as an optimal alternative by converting photonic energy into localized hyperthermia through precisely controlled microscopic heat sources, predominantly mediated by near-infrared (NIR)-responsive nanomaterials [11, 12]. Although PTT has shown promise in spatiotemporal modulation of immune microenvironment, reactive oxygen species (ROS) elimination [13], tissue repair and osteogenesis, and enabling on-demand drug release in cancer therapy and antibacterial applications [14–17], its use in TMJOA field remains limited, with macrophage-centric therapeutic strategies largely unexplored [18]. Therefore, whether precisely controlled hyperthermia can reprogram synovial M1 macrophages to suppress inflammation and restore joint homeostasis during TMJOA progression within the context of PTT is of critical importance. Specifically, we sought to determine whether localized photothermal stimulation can shift M1 macrophages toward a reparative phenotype, how thermally reprogrammed macrophages influence chondrocyte and osteoblast function, and whether targeted photothermal nanotherapy can achieve joint protection superior to conventional anti-inflammatory treatments such as NSAIDs or corticosteroids.

Herein, we first investigated the mechanistic basis of thermal modulation of M1 macrophages and its downstream effects on chondrocyte anabolism and osteogenesis in TMJOA. Building on these findings, we engineered folic acid–conjugated Y8 nanocomposites (FA-Y8 NPs) that selectively target folate receptor β (FolRβ) on M1 macrophages to achieve spatial controlled PTT within the inflamed joint microenvironment. In vivo, intra-articular injection of FA-Y8 NPs followed by NIR irradiation achieved localized photothermal reprogramming of synovial M1 macrophages, effectively alleviating synovitis, preserving cartilage integrity, and mitigating bone remodeling (Scheme 1). Unlike conventional therapies such as NSAIDs and corticosteroids, which offer only transient symptomatic relief and carry significant systemic or joint-damaging side effects, this macrophage-targeted photothermal immunotherapy provides a mechanistic foundation and translational strategy for developing minimally invasive, precision-based treatments for TMJOA.

Methods

Materials

Complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA; F5881), lipopolysaccharide (LPS; L4391), β-glycerophosphate (β-GP; G9422), L-ascorbic acid (A5960) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). TGF-1 (PRP1017) was obtained from Abbkine (Wuhan, China). Insulin-Transferrin-Selenium Media Supplement (ITS Media Supplement; C0341), dexamethasone (ST1254) and sodium pyruvate (ST1661) were purchased from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM; C11965500BT), alpha-modified Eagle’s medium (α-MEM; C12571500BT) were obtained from Gibco (Waltham, USA). Y8 (S6777) was acquired from Derthon (Shenzhen, China). 1,2-Distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N [methoxy (polyethylene glycol)-2000] (DSPE-PEG2000; P001008-2 K), 1,2-Distearoyl sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-(polyethylene glycol)-folic acid (DSPE-PEG2000-FA; P006011-2 K) and 1,2-Distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-poly (ethylene glycol)-Rhodamine B (DSPE-PEG2000-RB; P006012-2 K) were purchased from ToYong (Shanghai, China). Tetrahydrofuran (THF; 1740010101600) and N, N-Dimethylformamide (DMF; 1250350101600) were obtained from Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China).

Human TMJ lavage fluid collection and inflammatory cytokine detection

TMJ lavage fluid samples were collected twice from the upper joint space of 10 TMJOA patients at Nanjing Stomatological Hospital, with approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (NJSH-2022NL-39). The baseline sample was collected during the initial visit prior to any therapy. The post-treatment sample was obtained after patients completed a one-week course of self-administered thermotherapy (application of a 40–45 °C warm towel or pads to the affected joint for 10 min, 2–3 times daily). The collection procedure was performed as previously described [19–21]. Briefly, after local anesthesia with 2% lidocaine, 1 mL of normal saline was injected into the joint and retained for approximately 30 s before aspiration; this cycle was repeated five times to obtain lavage fluid. The collected lavage fluid was immediately centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min to collect the supernatant [22]. Inflammatory cytokines including interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) were quantified using specified enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (IL-1β: 1110122, IL-6: 1110602, TNF-α: 1117202, Dakewe Biotech). Patient information for​ the TMJ lavage fluid analysis was​ provided in Table S1.

Cell culture

MC3T3-E1 osteoblastic precursor cells were maintained in α-MEM while mouse macrophages (Raw264.7 cells) and ATDC5 chondrogenic progenitor cells were cultured in DMEM, with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; 10099141 C, Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (G4003, Servicebio). All of the above cell lines were maintained in a controlled atmosphere incubator (37 °C, 5% CO₂).

Thermal stimulation and conditioned medium (CM) collection

Raw264.7 cells were subjected to thermal stimulation by incubation in a water bath at either 41–45 °C for 10 min, followed by replenishment with pre-warmed fresh 37 °C medium containing 100 ng/ml of LPS to mimic an inflammatory microenvironment, and continued culture in the incubator at 37 °C in humidified 5% CO2. Control groups included cells at 37 °C with or without LPS treatment. Conditioned media (CM) were collected 6 h post-stimulation, centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 15 min and stored at -80 °C for further application.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

FastPure Cell/ Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (RC112-01, Vazyme) was used to isolate total RNA Reverse-transcription reactions were performed using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix (R223-01, Vazyme). Real-time PCR amplification was the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q311-02, Vazyme) on a QuantStudio system (ABI Viia 7, Thermo Fisher). Relative gene expression was quantified using the ΔΔCt method for comparative analysis, with GAPDH selected as the housekeeping gene for normalization. Primers were synthesized by GenScript (Nanjing, China) based on the sequences listed in Table S2.

Western blot (WB)

Proteins were extracted with RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime). The protein samples were firstly separated by 4–12% SDS-PAGE (SLE019, Smart-Lifesciences) and transferred onto the PVDF membrane (IPVH00010, Merck Millipore). Next, the membrane was immersed in 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; A8806, Sigma-Aldrich), followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with corresponding primary antibodies. Subsequently, triple TBST (C520009, Sango Biotech) rinses were performed before exposing the membranes to horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein visualization was achieved through ECL chemiluminescence kit (E423-01, Vazyme), with subsequent densitometric analysis of band intensities conducted via ImageJ software normalized against β-actin loading controls. Antibody specifications are detailed in Table S3.

RNA transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq)

For RNA-seq, total RNA was isolated from Raw264.7 macrophages after different thermal stimulation. The paired-end library was prepared and then sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform, conducted by APExBIO Technology LLC.

CCK8 assay

The culture medium was replaced with fresh serum-free medium containing 10% CCK-8 detection reagent (C6005, NCM Biotech) after experimental treatments. Following 1–2 h incubation at 37 °C, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a SpectraMax M3 Microplate Reader (USA).

Live/dead staining

The Calcein/PI Cell Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (C2015, Beyotime) was utilized to distinguish live cells and dead cells based on calcein AM and propidium iodide staining. A confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM; A1, Nikon, Japan) was applied to capture fluorescent images.

EdU staining

ATDC5 cells were seeded in confocal dishes and exposed to various conditioned media. BeyoClick™ EdU Cell Proliferation Kit with AF555 (C0071S, Beyotime) were applied for detection after 24 h-incubation.

Immunofluorescence (IF) Staining

Following fixation with 4% PFA (G1101, Servicebio) for 10 min, the cells were permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100 (P0096, Beyotime) and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; A8806, Sigma-Aldrich) for half an hour. Primary antibody incubation was carried out overnight at 4 °C, after which the samples were treated with corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies. Nuclei were visualized by counterstaining with DAPI (C1006, Beyotime) for 10 min. All fluorescence images were captured with a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM; A1 Nikon, Japan). Antibody details are provided in Table S4.

Alcian blue staining

Chondrogenic differentiation medium was prepared by supplementing standard ATDC5 culture medium with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1, 1% ITS, 37.5 µg/mL L-ascorbic acid, 0.1 µM dexamethasone, and 1 mM sodium pyruvate. This medium was then mixed with the macrophage-derived CM at a ratio of 3:1 to generate chondrogenic CM (CCM) for ATDC5 cells culture. After a 7-day culture, the cells were stained with Alcian Blue Stain Kit according to the manufactures’ instructions (G1565, Solarbio).

Osteogenic differentiation

Osteogenic differentiation medium was prepared by supplementing MC3T3-E1 standard culture medium with 0.05 mM L-ascorbic acid, 10 mM β-GP and and 0.1 µM dexamethasone. This medium was then mixed with the macrophage-derived CM at a ratio of 3:1 to generate osteogenic CM (OCM) for MC3T3-E1 cells culture. MC3T3-E1 cells were cultured with OCM, with medium replaced every 3 days. After a 7-day culture, ALP staining was visualized by the BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Staining Kit (C3206, Beyotime) and ALP activity was quantified using the Alkaline Phosphatase Assay Kit (P0321, Beyotime). For mineralization analysis, cells were cultured for 21 days, fixed and stained with alizarin red S solution (G1450, Solarbio). The stained calcium deposits were then dissolved in 10% (w/v) cetylpyridinium chloride (HY-B1464, MedChemExpress) and the absorbance was quantified at 562 nm using a microplate reader.

Preparation of FA-Y8 NPs

FA-Y8-NPs were synthesized using the nano-coprecipitation method. In brief, DSPE-PEG-FA (2 mg) was dissolved in 1 mL of DMF. Separately, DSPE-PEG (2 mg) and Y8 (1 mg) were dissolved in 10 ml of deionized water and 2 mL of THF, respectively. The DMF solution of DSPE-PEG-FA and the THF solution of Y8 were mixed together, and gradually added dropwise into the aqueous DSPE-PEG solution to facilitate under ultrasonic oscillation to facilitate FA-Y8 NPs. Subsequently, THF was removed via rotary evaporation. The resulting dispersion was dialyzed, lyophilized, and finally stored at 4 °C in the dark for further use. For comparison, Y8 NPs were prepared following the same procedure but replacing DSPE-PEG-FA with DSPE-PEG. Similarly, RB-functionalized variants, including RB-FA-Y8 NPs and RB-Y8 NPs, were fabricated by substituting DSPE-PEG with DSPE-PEG-RB.

Characterization of FA-Y8 NPs

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) was used to prove the effective conjugation of folic acid on the surface of nanoparticles compared to Y8 NPs and FA-Y8 NPs. Transmission electron microscope (TEM) (FEI Talos F200X G2, USA) and the dynamic light scattering system (DLS) (BT-90, Bettersize) were used to measure the size and morphology of FA-Y8 NPs. The ultraviolet−visible−near-infrared (UV − vis−NIR) absorption spectra and the fluorescence spectrum of FA-Y8 NPs were obtained by a UV Vis–NIR spectrophotometer (Perkin Elmer LAMBDA 750) and a photoluminescence spectrometer (FLS980), respectively. For stability detection, FA-Y8 NPs were dispersed in 10%FBS, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; G4202, Servicebio), and DMEM medium. On day 1,3,7, the nanosize of FA-Y8 NPs were analyzed using digital photos and DLS detection.

Blood compatibility evaluation

To assess blood compatibility, a 2% red blood cell suspension was prepared from fresh mouse blood and incubated with PBS (negative control), 0.9% NaCl, deionized water (positive control), and FA-Y8 NPs at varying concentrations at 37 °C for 2 h. After centrifugation, the supernatant absorbance at 540 nm was measured to quantify released hemoglobin. Hemolysis percentage was calculated as: Hemolysis (%) = (ODexperimental - ODnegative control)/( ODpositive control − ODnegative control) × 100%.

Photothermal performance testing of FA-Y8 NPs

To assess the photothermal performance of FA-Y8 NPs in vitro, 2 mL of nanoparticle solutions at different concentrations were placed in cuvettes and irradiated (808 nm, 1.0 W/cm², 10 min). An infrared thermal camera (C3, USA) was employed to obtain real-time temperature changes. In addition, the temperature response of FA-Y8 NPs at a fixed concentration of 100 µg/mL was assessed under varying laser power densities. The photothermal properties of Y8 NPs and PBS were evaluated using the same experimental protocol, with PBS serving as the negative control.

In vitro active-targeting of FA-Y8 NPs

Raw264.7 cells were pretreated with LPS to stimulate M1 polarization and upregulate FolRβ expression. Rhodamine-labeled nanoparticles were then incubated with the macrophages for 2 h. To evaluate the specificity of folate-mediated targeting, competitive blocking was performed by pre-incubating cells with free FA (F7876, Sigma-Aldrich). After fixation, and permeabilization and blocking, cells were stained with FITC-conjugated phalloidin (RM02836, Abclonal) and DAPI (C1006, Beyotime). Fluorescence images were acquired using CLSM. Parallel samples were harvested and analyzed by flow cytometry after suspension.

Animals

Following ethical approval by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing University (IACUC-D2204005), all animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the ethical treatment of laboratory animals. Male C57BL/6J mice (8-week-old) were purchased from Jiangsu Huachuang Sino Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. To establish the TMJOA model [23, 24], 48 mice were randomly received a unilateral intra-articular injection of 20 µL 0.5 mg/mL CFA (F5881, Sigma-Aldrich, diluted 1:1 with saline) (CFA group) or saline (Saline group) into the right TMJ using a microsyringe [25–27]. Observations were observed at 0 day, 3 days, 1 week, and 4 weeks (n = 6 per group/time point). An additional 48 mice were randomly assigned to 8 groups to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of targeted PTT (n = 6 per group): (1) sham-operated group​ (Sham), which underwent the same surgical procedures including anesthesia and joint exposure as the CFA groups but received an intra-articular injection of saline instead of CFA; (2) CFA with saline (Saline); (3) CFA with saline + NIR (NIR); (4) CFA with Y8 treatment (Y8 NPs); (5) CFA with Y8 treatment + NIR (Y8 NPs + NIR); (6) CFA with FA-Y8 treatment (FA-Y8 NPs); (7) CFA with FA-Y8 treatment + NIR (FA-Y8 NPs + NIR); (8) CFA with diclofenac sodium (DCF; D408737, Aladdin). Seven days post-CFA injection, mice received 20 µL intra-articular injections of saline, nanoparticles (100 µg/mL) or DCF (5 mg/mL) into the right TMJ. For PTT, 808 nm laser was applied at a distance of 1 cm from the TMJ surface, maintaining the local temperature at approximately 41 °C for 10 min, with real-time monitoring via infrared thermography. Treatments were repeated weekly for 4 weeks. At the endpoint, TMJ tissues and major organs were collected and fixed in 4% PFA (G1101, Servicebio) for further analyses.

In vivo active-targeting and biodistribution of FA-Y8 NPs

RB-labeled FA-Y8 NPs and Y8 NPs were injected into the TMJ cavity of both CFA-induced TMJOA mice and healthy controls, respectively. (20 µL per mouse, n = 3 per group). At 12, 24 and 72 h post-injection, an AniView X spectral imaging system (Biolight Biotechnology, China) was employed to monitor the fluorescence signal of the TMJ. For biodistribution, major organs of mice were excised for ex vivo imaging after 48 h post-injection.

Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT)

Micro-CT was performed to assess the subchondral bone microstructure, as previously described [28–30]. Briefly, TMJ tissues were scanned using a SkyScan 1176 system (Bruker, Germany) equipped with an Aluminum filter (0.5 mm thickness) at 50 kV and 400 µA. The scanning parameters included an isotropic resolution of 8.88 μm/voxel, a rotation step of 0.600 degrees per projection, and a total angular rotation of 197.40 degrees to ensure high-quality reconstruction. A cubic Sect. (0.3 × 0.3 × 0.3 mm) from the midcenter of the subchondral bone was selected as the region of interest (ROI). Quantitative analyses of bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) were conducted using CTan software (v1.18.4.0, Bruker). Voxel data were standardized by calibrating grayscale values against a hydroxyapatite phantom of known density, and a determinate threshold was applied based on the mineral density distribution in control samples to ensure consistency. Three-dimensional reconstructions were generated with Mimics Research software (v21.0).

Histological analysis

Following decalcification in 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA; 1108GR500, BioFroxx) solution for 28 days, TMJ specimens were processed for paraffin embedding and sectioned into 5-µm sagittal slices. Histological evaluation was conducted using hematoxylin and eosin (H.E.) staining for general tissue morphology and safranin O-fast green (S.O.) staining to assess proteoglycan content in cartilaginous tissues. Briefly, for H.E. staining, sections were processed using a commercial H.E. Staining Kit (BH0001, Boerfu Biotechnology), with hematoxylin incubation for 3–5 min, followed by eosin counterstaining for 5 min according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For S.O. staining, sections were stained using a modified Safranin O/Fast Green Cartilage Staining Kit (G1371, Solarbio). The staining procedure comprised incubation in Fast Green solution for 5 min, brief differentiation in 1% acetic acid (< 15 s), and counterstaining with safranin O solution for 5 min. Sections were then dehydrated with 95% ethyl alcohol, absolute ethyl alcohol, and xylene and mounted with a resinous medium. Disease severity was quantified using the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) grading system and synovitis scoring criteria, as previously established [22, 31].

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analyses

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections underwent deparaffinization and rehydration via sequential ethanol gradients, followed by antigen retrieval using pepsin buffer at 37 °C for 2 h. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min. Then, all sections were blocked with goat serum for half an hour prior to overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C. For IHC detection, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB-2031, Maixin Biotech). Details of the antibodies used in this part are listed in Table S4.

Statistical analysis

All data in this study were presented as mean ± SD and analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.4). The data were obtained from results of at least three independent experiments. For comparisons between two groups, paired t test was applied to analyze TMJOA joint lavage fluid samples, while Student’s t test was employed for other experimental groups. The differences among multiple groups were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test combined with Tukey’s post hoc test.

Results

Thermotherapy attenuates synovial fluid inflammatory cytokines in human TMJOA patients

Given the empirical therapeutic potential of thermotherapy, we sought to assess its specific impact on inflammatory mediator secretion within the joint. To this end, we collected synovial lavage fluid from 10 patients with TMJOA and assessed the secretion of inflammatory cytokines using ELISA (Fig. 1A). Compared to non-thermotherapy patients, individuals who received thermal treatment exhibited marked downregulation of including IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α in the synovial fluid (Fig. 1B), suggesting a potential role of thermotherapy in alleviating the intra-articular inflammatory milieu.

Synovial M1 macrophages drive pathogenesis in CFA-induced TMJOA

Synovitis is characterized by increased infiltration of classically activated M1-like macrophages, which drive pro-inflammatory cascades and exacerbate joint tissue destruction [32, 33]. To elucidate the pivotal role of M1 macrophage-driven synovitis in TMJOA pathogenesis, we established a murine model via intra-articular injection of CFA. As illustrated in Figs. 1C-G and 3D reconstruction revealed apparent condylar surface irregularity in the TMJ region 3 days after CFA injection compared with Saline groups. The alternation was accompanied by cortical disruption and initial subchondral bone resorption, indicating initial TMJOA development. The degenerative changes became more escalated by day 7, with severe deterioration of the condylar cortex and loss of subchondral architecture. Micro-CT analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in the ratio of BV/TV (bone volume per tissue volume), Tb.Th (trabecular thickness) and Tb.N (trabecular number) along with increased Tb.Sp (trabecular separation) in the CFA group (Fig. 1D-G), reflecting early-stage trabecular deterioration. By day 28, extensive condylar remodeling along with pronounced sclerosis of the subchondral bone and joint deformity were observed in the CFA group. Concomitantly, subchondral bone density increased within the condyle, consistent with progression to moderate-to-advanced TMJOA. H.E. staining revealed thickened lining as early as 3 days after CFA injection, which intensified by day 7 with inflammatory exudate in the joint cavity. This chronic inflammatory state persisted up to day 28, characterized histologically by synovial lining cell hyperplasia, increased inflammatory cell infiltration, and lipid droplet accumulation and the quantitative evaluation of synovitis scores (Fig. 1H-I). Notably, we found that the expression of CD86 (a canonical marker of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages) was upregulated in inflamed TMJ synovium (Fig. 1J-K), suggesting robust pro-inflammatory immune engagement. Assessment of condylar cartilage via H.E. (Fig. S1) and S.O. staining (Fig. 1L) demonstrated a progressive loss of chondrocyte cellularity and proteoglycan depletion accompanied with reduced type II collagen (Col II) synthesis in the CFA group (Fig. 1N-O), which was verified by the significantly elevated OARSI scores (Fig. 1M). These results indicated that prolonged exposure to synovial inflammation aggravated progressive degeneration of the condylar cartilage. Collectively, our findings suggest that synovial M1-like macrophages may serve as potential regulators in promoting cartilage catabolism and driving TMJOA progression.

Thermal stimulation ameliorates macrophage inflammation through HSP70-dependent inhibition of NF-κB signaling

To elucidate the impact of thermal stimulation on macrophages under inflammatory conditions, we exposed LPS-stimulated macrophages to mild hyperthermia (41 °C) or moderate to severe hyperthermia (45 °C) for defined durations (Fig. 2A). Assessment of cell viability revealed a critical difference between these conditions. While mild hyperthermia had a moderate impact on macrophage survival and proliferation, treatment at 45 °C proved highly cytotoxic, resulting in a substantial reduction in cell density (Fig. S2), a marked increase in cell death according to live/dead staining (Fig. 2B, S3), and a loss of viability exceeding 50% as measured by CCK-8 assay (Fig. S4).

We next evaluated the impact of thermal stimulation on macrophage inflammatory response. As shown in Fig. 2C, qPCR analysis revealed that LPS stimulation significantly upregulated the transcription of typical inflammatory genes (Nos2, IL-6 and TNF-α) compared with normal macrophages. Notably, both mild hyperthermia (41 °C) and hyperthermia (45 °C) treatments markedly reduced the expression of these inflammatory genes (Fig. 2C). Similarly, ELISA results demonstrated that elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines in the LPS-stimulated macrophage supernatant were effectively counteracted by thermal treatment (Fig. 2D). The suppression of the inflammatory response was further confirmed at the protein level by Western blot, which showed a consistent downregulation of inflammatory proteins (Fig. 2E-F). Furthermore, WB also confirmed that the expression of CD86, a key M1 macrophage surface marker, was significantly induced by LPS and this induction was effectively reversed by both thermal treatments (Fig. 2E-F). These results demonstrate that thermal stimulation directly suppresses M1 macrophage polarization.

To further elucidate the underlying mechanism that how heat-mediated immunomodulation occurs, the transcriptomic study was performed via RNA sequencing. PCA of the transcriptomic data demonstrated distinct effects of thermal treatments. The 45 °C treatment markedly reversed the LPS-driven alterations along the primary component (PC1, 43.8% variance), reflecting a global restoration toward the baseline state. Conversely, the 41 °C treatment specifically modulated transcription along PC2 (23.8% variance), indicating that different hyperthermia temperatures engage distinct transcriptional programs (Fig. S5). Then, KEGG pathway analysis indicated that thermal intervention downregulated the NF-κB signaling pathway in LPS-stimulated macrophages (Fig. 2G-H). Given that NF-κB activation promotes inflammatory responses and drives M1 polarization [34–36], and that HSP70 has been reported to inhibit NF-κB activation [37, 38], we hypothesized that the anti-inflammatory effect of hyperthermia is mediated through HSP70-induced suppression of NF-κB signaling. WB analysis confirmed that the 41 °C treatment significantly enhanced HSP70 expression, which coincided with a reduction in phosphorylated p65 (p-p65), a critical regulator of the NF-κB signaling cascade (Fig. 2I, Fig. S6). In contrast, the 45 °C treatment group resulted in a significant decrease in HSP70 expression, below that of normal macrophages, implying that the overwhelming thermal stress may have induced cellular damage or initiated apoptosis, thereby shifting the regulatory outcome. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that photothermal treatment at different temperatures can selectively modulate or eliminate M1 macrophages. Specifically, 41 °C mild hyperthermia achieves this anti-inflammatory effect while preserving cell viability, whereas 45 °C hyperthermia suppresses inflammation primarily through extensive cytotoxicity, providing a flexible strategy for ameliorating inflammatory conditions.

In addition, our data revealed that inflammatory macrophages exhibit a markedly greater responsiveness to heat compared to resident joint cells like chondrocytes and osteoblasts (Fig. S7). This thermoplasticity provides a mechanistic basis for the targeted efficacy of mild hyperthermia, which preferentially disrupts pathogenic immune activation while preserving tissue homeostasis.

Thermal-treated macrophages protect chondrocytes from inflammatory damage

Based on the critical crosstalk between synovial macrophages and chondrocytes, we sought to determine how macrophage thermal conditioning influences chondrocyte behavior. Hence, we collected four types of CCM derived from pre-treated macrophages: #Con, #LPS, #LPS-41 °C, and #LPS-45 °C. These CM were then applied to chondrocytes to assess their functional impact (Fig. 3A). Live/dead staining revealed a marked reduction in viable (green) cells and a significant increase in dead (red) cells in the #LPS group compared to #Con group (Fig. 3B, Fig. S8A). In contrast, cultures treated with #LPS-41 °C and #LPS-45 °C exhibited higher viability and minimal cytotoxicity, which was confirmed by CCK-8 assays (Fig. S8B). Similar proliferation trends were observed in the EdU assay (Fig. 3C, Fig. S8C). qPCR analysis in Fig. 3D showed that #LPS downregulated Col2a1 and SOX9, while upregulating MMP3, MMP13, Nos2, and Ptgs2. These alterations denoted a shift toward increased catabolism and inflammation, and impaired anabolic function, characteristic of TMJOA-like chondrocyte degeneration. Notably, CCM from thermally primed macrophages (#LPS-41 °C and #LPS-45 °C) reversed this phenotype, restoring Col2a1 and SOX9 expression and suppressing catabolic and inflammatory genes. These findings were corroborated by WB (Fig. 3E-F) and immunofluorescence (Fig. 3G) analyses, which demonstrated increased Col II and reduced MMP13 expression in chondrocytes exposed to thermally modulated CCM. Alcian Blue staining further revealed severe proteoglycan loss in the #LPS group, whereas thermal-primed CCM preserved matrix production (Fig. 3H). Collectively, these results indicate that thermal intervention mitigates macrophage inflammation, thereby improving viability, promoting anabolism, protecting chondrocytes from inflammation-driven degeneration while suppressing catabolism.

Thermal-treated macrophages restore osteoblast function under inflammatory conditions

The interaction between macrophages and osteoblasts is also worthy of attention. To evaluate the immunomodulatory effects of thermal treatment on osteogenesis, osteoblasts were cultured in osteogenic induction medium supplemented with macrophage-derived conditioned medium (OCM: #Con, #LPS, #LPS-41 °C, and #LPS-45 °C), as illustrated in Fig. 4A. Under inflammatory conditions (#LPS), osteoblast proliferation was significantly impaired, as shown by live/dead staining (Fig. 4B), and CCK-8 assays (Fig. S9), accompanied by downregulation of key osteogenic markers, including RUNX2, ALP, OCN, and OPN at mRNA or protein levels (Fig. 4C-E). In contrast, osteoblasts cultured with #LPS-41 °C and #LPS-45 °C exhibited restored proliferative capacity and enhanced osteogenic differentiation. ALP staining (Fig. 4F) and quantitative analysis (Fig. 4G) revealed markedly reduced ALP activity in the #LPS group, which was rescued by OCM from thermally primed macrophages (#LPS-41 °C and #LPS-45 °C). Furthermore, alizarin red S staining demonstrated that mineralized nodule formation (Fig. 4H-I), suppressed by LPS-induced inflammation, was significantly improved in the #LPS-41 °C and #LPS-45 °C groups. Therefore, thermal conditioning of macrophages could mitigate inflammatory damage to osteoblasts.

Preparation and characterization of FA-Y8 NPs

In this study, Y8-loaded folate-targeted nanoprobes (FA-Y8 NPs) were developed via a nanoprecipitation approach for targeted and efficient PTT against M1 macrophages in the TMJOA microenvironment (Fig. 5A). As shown in Fig. S10A, the FA-Y8 NP suspension exhibited a homogeneous indigo-blue dispersion in aqueous solution. TEM imaging (Fig. S10B) revealed that both nanoprobe formulations were monodisperse and displayed a well-defined spherical morphology. DLS analysis presented average hydrodynamic diameters of approximately 125 nm for Y8 NPs and 138 nm for FA-Y8 NPs (Fig. S11, Fig. 5B). Additionally, zeta potential measurements yielded values of − 17.13 ± 0.11 mV and − 19.13 ± 0.11 mV for Y8 NPs and FA-Y8 NPs respectively (Fig. S12), indicating favorable colloidal stability and minimal electrostatic interaction with biological membranes.

FTIR was carried out to verify the grafting of FA on the surface of Y8 nanoparticles. The spectrum of FA-Y8 NPs (Fig. S13) exhibited a marked increase in the relative intensity of C–H absorption peaks at 2918 cm⁻¹ and 2889 cm⁻¹, along with the emergence of a C = O stretching vibration peak at 1740 cm⁻¹. Additionally, weak absorptions corresponding to C–N and N–H stretching vibrations from amide bands were observed at 1608 cm⁻¹. The appearance of these characteristic peaks confirms the successful conjugation of the FA-targeting peptide onto the Y8 nanostructure [39, 40]. Figure 5C showed that the UV–vis spectrum of Y8 NPs exhibited a distinct red shift compared with free Y8, with the absorption peak centered around 808 nm. FA-Y8 NPs displayed an identical absorption maximum, indicating that both nanoparticle formulations are suitable for photothermal applications under 808 nm laser irradiation.

Photothermal performance of FA-Y8 NPs

The photothermal performance of various solutions was evaluated under 808 nm NIR laser irradiation at 1.0 W/cm². The temperature change curves revealed that PBS exhibited negligible temperature elevation after 10 min of irradiation, whereas both Y8 NPs and FA-Y8 NPs presented remarkable temperature increases (Fig. S14), demonstrating the effective photothermal conversion capability of Y8-based formulations. Further investigations revealed a clear concentration-dependent and power density-dependent temperature rise in both Y8 NPs and FA-Y8 NPs across concentrations of 25–200 µg/mL and laser intensities of 0.5–1.25 W/cm² (Fig. S15, Fig. 5D-E). Specifically, FA-Y8 NPs (100 µg/mL) reached 46.1 °C under 1.0 W/cm² irradiation, and a further increase in power density to 1.25 W/cm² resulted in an additional 7.4 °C temperature rise (Fig. S16). These findings indicate that the photothermal response can be finely tuned by adjusting either nanoparticle concentration or laser power. Moreover, the solution color of FA-Y8 NPs remained stable before and after laser irradiation (Fig. S17). Repeated photothermal cycling based on four on/off cycles demonstrated consistent temperature profiles (Fig. S18), confirming the excellent thermal stability and repeatability of FA-Y8 NPs. These results suggest that the developed FA-Y8 NPs possess favorable photothermal properties suitable for further PTT applications in TMJOA therapy. The biological stability of FA-Y8 NPs was assessed in various physiological environments (Fig. 5F), including PBS, DMEM, and pure 10% FBS as a simulated serum condition. After 14 days of storage at ambient temperature, no visible color change or turbidity was observed in the solutions DLS analysis showed no significant variation in particle size (Fig. 5G, Fig. S19). These results indicate that FA-Y8 NPs exhibit excellent long-term physical stability under different storage conditions.

Biocompatibility, M1-macrohpage targeting capacity and in vivo biodistribution of FA-Y8 NPs

Despite the promising photothermal performance of FA-Y8 NPs, which suggests their potential for optimizing thermotherapy in TMJOA, ensuring good biocompatibility is essential for their future clinical translation. Therefore, we evaluated the cytotoxicity of FA-Y8 NPs using CCK-8 assays. The CCK-8 assay demonstrated that cell viability of macrophages remained above 90% after 24 h incubation with various concentrations of FA-Y8 NPs (Fig. 5H), indicating low cytotoxicity. The photothermal impact of FA-Y8 NPs on macrophage viability was determined primarily by the final temperature achieved, as a result of the specific combination of NP concentration and laser power. For instance, distinct combination pairs (e.g., 25 µg/mL NPs with 1.0 W/cm² and 100 µg/mL NPs with 0.5 W/cm²) that produced an equivalent temperature rise of about 10 °C consistently led to a comparable about 50% reduction in viability (Fig. S20). Hemocompatibility was also assessed across different concentrations of FA-Y8 NPs. Results showed that even at 500 µg/ml, FA-Y8 NPs did not significantly affect red blood cell viability, with a hemolysis rate below 3%. Similar results were observed for Y8 NPs (Fig. S21), confirming excellent blood compatibility. Collectively, these findings demonstrate satisfactory biocompatibility of FA-Y8 NPs in macrophages, supporting their use without compromising cellular activity in subsequent studies. Based on these results, a concentration of 100 µg/mL of FA-Y8 NPs was chosen for subsequent optimization experiments [41].

After cytocompatibility assessment, we next evaluated the specificity of FA-Y8 NPs toward M1 macrophages. To this end, resting and LPS-activated macrophages were incubated with rhodamine-labeled nanoparticles (Fig. S22) and examined using CLSM. LPS was employed to induce M1 polarization. CLSM images showed a high expression level of FolRβ in M1 macrophages compared with untreated cells and IL-4 induced M2 macrophages (Fig. 5I), which was consistent with previous reports [42, 43]. As shown in Fig. 5K, the strongest fluorescence was detected in the cytoplasm of LPS-activated M1 macrophages incubated with FA-Y8 NPs whereas no significant difference was observed in nonactivated macrophages exposed to Y8-based nanoparticles. This enhanced cellular uptake of FA-Y8 NPs is attributed to the upregulated expression and surface exposure of folate receptors on activated M1 macrophages, providing more binding sites for targeted internalization. To validate the role of folate in targeting, LPS-stimulated Raw264.7 cells were pre-treated with free FA before nanoparticle incubation. The results showed that pre-treatment significantly reduced nanoparticle uptake, particularly in the FA-Y8 NPs group (Fig. 5K), confirming the critical role of FA-mediated internalization. Flow cytometry analysis in Fig. 5J supported these findings, demonstrating superior cellular uptake and targeting efficiency of FA-Y8 NPs toward M1 macrophages. This selective targeting enables precise photothermal regulation of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, thereby effectively alleviating TMJOA-related inflammation, minimizing off-target damage, and enhancing the bioavailability.

Given their promising in vitro targeting performance, we proceeded to evaluate the in vivo distribution and specificity of FA-Y8 NPs in a CFA-induced TMJOA mouse model. Following intra-articular injection of Y8 NPs or FA-Y8 NPs, time-dependent fluorescence distribution was monitored using the AniView X spectral imaging system. As illustrated in Fig. 5L, FA-Y8 NPs exhibited significantly stronger fluorescence at the inflamed TMJ compared to Y8 NPs-treated CFA mice or FA-Y8 NPs-treated normal mice, with peak intensity observed at 12 h post-injection. Notably, the Y8 NPs group also showed moderate accumulation, likely due to enhanced vascular permeability caused by inflammation-induced joint barrier disruption. These findings suggested that the overexpression of FolRβ in inflamed joints enabled FA-Y8 NPs to specifically target inflammatory macrophages. Ex vivo imaging at 48 h post-injection revealed that FA-Y8 NPs were possibly cleared through the intestinal route, with minimal accumulation in major organs (Fig. 5M), indicating favorable biocompatibility and low systemic toxicity.

In vivo therapeutic potential of FA-Y8 NPs under NIR irradiation

After confirming the in vitro photothermal efficacy and M1 macrophage-targeting capability of FA-Y8 NPs, we further evaluated their therapeutic efficacy in a TMJOA mouse model. FA-Y8 NPs were administered via intra-articular injection 7 days after CFA-induction. A total of four doses were delivered over 4 weeks. Based on our previous findings, the treated mice subsequently received a 10-minute 808 nm near-infrared laser irradiation once weekly, with the local temperature precisely maintained at 41 °C to enable targeted reprogramming of synovial M1 macrophages in TMJOA while avoiding significant cytotoxicity (Fig. 6A). To prevent potential skin damage, mice were depilated around the TMJ region prior to each injection and laser treatment [11]. As shown in Fig. 6B, the temperature at the TMJ site following FA-Y8 NPs injection increased from 32.3 °C to 40.9 °C within 1 min and was maintained around 41 °C during 10 min of 808 nm NIR irradiation. After 4 weeks of treatment, TMJ tissues were collected for analysis. To evaluate the overall protective effect of FA-Y8 NPs after NIR irradiation on TMJ, we assessed the subchondral bone status of the TMJ through micro-CT. 3D reconstruction of the TMJ revealed subchondral bone sclerosis and irregular condylar surfaces of following CFA induction (Fig. 6C). The aforementioned pathological changes were ameliorated in FA-Y8 NPs + NIR treated mice. Quantitative analysis of the subchondral bone region showed that CFA mice exhibited decreased BV/TV, Tb.Th, Tb.N and increased Tb.Sp (Fig. 6D-G) when compared to Sham controls. Notably, FA-Y8 NPs + NIR treatment effectively reversed these impaired TMJOA-related subchondral bone microarchitecture and demonstrated the most pronounced therapeutic effect among all groups, as evidenced by a relatively intact condylar surface and minimal structural remodeling. Importantly, the therapeutic outcome was comparable to that of the conventional non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (DCF), with no statistically significant difference in bone parameters between two groups. Discussion

The clinical efficacy of traditional thermotherapy for TMJOA varies due to their limited penetration depth and inability to achieve precise and sustained temperature control. While mild hyperthermia is known to possess immunomodulatory potential, the critical parameters of treatment timing and duration remain poorly understood [44–46]. Our study revealed that a specific anti-inflammatory effect was observed at 41 °C, mediated through activation of the heat shock proteins, especially HSP70, and subsequent inhibition of the NF-κB pathway. This finding is corroborated in previous reports of mild hyperthermia for rheumatoid arthritis and peri-implantitis treatment [38, 46]. Notably, the effect of 45 °C was non-specific cytotoxicity wherein inflammation suppression is a comprehensive cellular failure. Crucially, this single, precisely-timed thermal reprogramming altered the macrophage secretome, yielding conditioned medium with enhanced chondroprotective and osteogenic effects. This suggests that even a single, well-timed intervention can durably shift the joint microenvironment from pro-inflammatory to pro-resolving.

In the current study, we engineered NIR-responsive FA-Y8 NPs with good photothermal conversion efficiency and active targeting capability toward M1 macrophages. In contrast to the nonspecific, superficial heating of traditional hot compresses, our FA-Y8 NPs-mediated PTT leverages the deep tissue penetration and precise spatial control of NIR light to deliver targeted thermal modulation directly to the M1 macrophages within the inflamed synovium [47, 48]. Therefore, this platform retains thermotherapy’s inherent safety while overcoming its historical limitations of imprecision and shallow reach.

The subsequent in vivo studies confirmed the successful implementation of this “target-and-reprogram” strategy. Upon NIR irradiation, we observed significant reduction in synovitis, a shift in the reduction of M1 macrophage, and concomitant preservation of both cartilage integrity and bone structure. This approach maximizes therapeutic precision by confining the photothermal effect primarily to FolRβ-expressing M1 macrophages, thereby minimizing off-target thermal damage to surrounding tissues [40, 49, 50]. When compared to conventional macrophage-targeting strategies for TMJOA, the FA-Y8 NP platform demonstrates distinct advantages rooted in its NIR-responsive design. For instance, compared to small-molecule drugs that often lack cellular specificity and exhibit systemic side effects, our FA-Y8 NPs enable localized action within the joint [26]. Furthermore, unlike broad-acting biologics (e.g., inhibitors targeting caspase-1 and NLRP3, the soluble epoxide hydrolase inhibitor (sEHi)) or passive nanocarriers that primarily function as drug delivery vehicles, our platform employs a physically-triggered, localized immunomodulatory stimulus to directly regulate the macrophage phenotype [51–54]. The incorporation of NIR-responsiveness confers both immunomodulatory and chondroprotective properties, thereby addressing multiple pathological processes in TMJOA within a single, targeted intervention.

However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. First, the absence of a time-gradient analysis precludes definitive identification of the precise therapeutic window for macrophage reprogramming. Future work should systematically evaluate thermal durations to establish the dose-response relationship between stimulation time and M1-to-M2 conversion efficiency, while concurrently monitoring HSP70 kinetics and off-target thermal stress. This optimization is essential for translating photothermal immunotherapy into clinically defined, personalized protocols for TMJOA management. Second, while NF-κB is identified as a key node, the upstream thermal sensors initiating this signaling modulation warrant further investigation. Third, whereas the CFA model provides a valid acute, inflammatory-driven phenotype, its extension to a chronic, biomechanically driven model (e.g., via aberrant occlusion) is necessary to enhance clinical relevance [55]. Subsequent research should systematically delineate the optimal therapeutic window and treatment regimen, with specific focus on intervention timing relative to disease progression and the number and frequency of sessions, in various stages of pathology. Finally, advancing this technology toward clinical translation necessitates further preclinical evaluation in large animal models that more closely mimic human TMJ anatomy and disease chronicity. These studies are crucial for comprehensively assessing the long-term biodistribution, biodegradation, and potential accumulation of FA-Y8 NPs. Although our initial data suggest an intestinal excretion route, definitive confirmation of the clearance mechanism and detailed metabolic fate requires quantitative analysis of biological samples in such a physiologically relevant model.

Conclusion

In this study, we identify M1 macrophages as a key cellular target of thermal therapy in TMJ osteoarthritis and reveal the differential regulatory mechanisms of hyperthermia at distinct temperatures on the macrophage inflammatory response.41 °C hyperthermia achieves a specific anti-inflammatory effect by leveraging the known inhibitory effect of HSP70 on the NF-κB pathway while 45 °C hyperthermia triggers non-selective cytotoxicity, making consequent suppression of inflammation a passive outcome of cellular functional collapse. Notably, conditioned medium from thermally treated macrophages enhances chondrocyte function and osteoblast differentiation, indicating a paracrine-mediated joint-protective effect. To translate this insight, we developed FA-Y8 NPs, a M1 macrophage-targeting nanoplatform enabling deep-tissue photothermal therapy under NIR irradiation. In vivo experiments reveal that FA-Y8 NPs + NIR effectively modulate M1 polarization, alleviate synovitis, preserve cartilage integrity, and retard OA progression. Our work shifts the paradigm of PTT from physical ablation to biochemical signaling modulation, presenting a precise and effective strategy for managing TMJOA.
