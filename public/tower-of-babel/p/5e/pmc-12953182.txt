Global Burden of Lead Exposure: Integrating Epidemiology, Molecular Mechanisms, and Mitigation Approaches
Authors: Gudisa Bereda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLead is a nonessential, toxic heavy metal that causes widespread environmental contamination, affecting humans, animals, plants, and ecosystems. Globally, an estimated 800 million children have blood lead levels (BLLs) above 5 µg/dL. Lead persists in the environment and accumulates in the body, particularly in bone, due to its chemical similarity to calcium.ObjectivesThis review aimed to summarize the prevalence, environmental sources, molecular and cellular mechanisms, dose‐dependent toxic effects, and mitigation strategies related to lead exposure.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar to identify relevant studies published between 2010 and 2025. Both peer‐reviewed and gray literature were qualitatively synthesized.ResultsThe findings indicated that lead exposure is pervasive, with detectable levels in 100% of participants in EU biomonitoring studies. In the United States, ~590,000 children under 5 years of age had BLLs above 3.5 µg/dL. Lead disrupts essential metal homeostasis, induces oxidative stress, causes mitochondrial dysfunction, disrupts calcium signaling, impairs heme synthesis, and triggers DNA damage, endoplasmic reticulum stress, cell membrane injury, and impaired signal transduction. Experimental studies in mice and chickens demonstrated dose‐dependent accumulation in blood, tissues, and organs, accompanied by significant decreases in Zn, Fe, Mg, and Ca levels and elevated oxidative stress markers. Effective mitigation strategies include regulatory policies, environmental remediation, occupational safety measures, public health education, maternal and child health interventions, food and water safety measures, technological innovations, and continuous monitoring.ConclusionThis review showed that lead remains a significant public health threat and underscores the need for targeted and equity‐focused interventions.

Introduction

Lead poisoning results from the accumulation of lead, a nonessential heavy metal, and even low levels of exposure can cause serious health effects [1]. It is a major global health inequity, arising from environmental, occupational, and household sources such as lead‐based paints, contaminated soil and water, industrial emissions, traditional products, and unregulated consumer goods. Children particularly those with pica are highly vulnerable due to their developing nervous systems and higher gastrointestinal absorption [2]. Globally, nearly one million deaths occur annually, with over 90% in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), reflecting a shift from regulated industrial exposure in high‐income regions to persistent informal and occupational exposure in LMICs [3]. Young children, pregnant women, and workers in high‐risk occupations are disproportionately affected due to biological susceptibility and exposure patterns [4]. Acute lead toxicity manifests with severe gastrointestinal, neurological, and renal symptoms, whereas chronic exposure causes cognitive impairment, peripheral neuropathy, anemia, hypertension, renal dysfunction, and reproductive harm [5].

Samarghandian et al. [6] reviewed 126 studies (2009–2020) on lead poisoning sources, symptoms, and management, highlighted traditional medicines, occupational exposure, and substance abuse as common sources, with neurological, gastrointestinal, and hematological disorders being most frequent. Othman and colleagues analyzed 104 Saudi foods and identified sweets (0.011–0.199 μg/g), vegetables (0.002–0.195 μg/g), legumes (0.014–0.094 μg/g), eggs (0.079 μg/g), and meat (0.013–0.068 μg/g) as major sources. Based on consumption data, vegetables (25.4%), cereals (24.2%), beverages (9.7%), sweets (8.2%), legumes (7.4%), fruits (5.4%), and dairy (5.1%) were the main contributors. Daily intake ranged from 22.7 to 24.5 μg/person/day, below JECFA limits [7].

Forsyth and colleagues examined 356 turmeric samples from India, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, and Nepal, finding 14% exceeded 2 μg/g Pb. Samples ≥ 18 μg/g showed Pb: Cr ratios ~1:1, suggested lead chromate adulteration, with Pb > 1000 μg/g in Patna, Karachi, and Peshawar. Modeled child blood Pb could be ten times higher than CDC thresholds, highlighting urgent need to ban lead chromate in turmeric [8]. WHO estimated that lead exposure accounted for nearly half of over 2 million chemical‐related deaths globally in 2019 and contributed 21.7 million DALYs, including 30% of idiopathic intellectual disability worldwide [9]. CDC defined blood lead levels (BLLs) ≥ 3.5 µg/dL as elevated, reflecting evidence that no safe exposure threshold exists [10, 11].

Zhou and colleagues reported that global deaths from lead exposure rose from 0.53 million to 0.90 million between 1990 and 2019, and DALYs increased from 16.02 million to 21.68 million, despite declining age‐standardized rates. The highest burden was observed among men, older adults, and populations with low SDI [12]. Rezaee and colleagues found that in the NAME region, age‐standardized death and DALY rates in 2019 were 23.4 and 489.3 per 100,000, mainly from cardiovascular and chronic kidney diseases [13].

Al Sukaiti and colleagues analyzed NHANES III data, reported higher mean BLLs in Black men (5.4 µg/dL) and women (3.4 µg/dL) than in White men (4.4 µg/dL) and women (3.0 µg/dL). Elevated BLLs were significantly associated with high blood pressure among Black participants. They also summarized studies from 2002 to 2003, including Lanphear and colleagues, showed cognitive impairment at BLLs as low as 2.5 µg/dL in 4853 participants, where the mean BLL was 1.9 µg/dL and only 2.1% exceeded 10 µg/dL [14]. Rezaee and colleagues reported that global lead‐related DALYs declined nearly 1% annually between 1990 and 2019, yet pooled mean BLLs in LMICs remained elevated, ranging from 1.66 to 9.30 µg/dL in children and 0.39–11.36 µg/dL in adults [3, 13].

The mechanisms of lead toxicity are mediated through oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, calcium mimicry, and epigenetic disruption [15]. Prevention strategies, chelation therapy, and emerging interventions of the lead toxicity comprises nutritional supplementation, phytoremediation, and nanotechnology remain central to control efforts [16]. The distinctive contribution of this review lied in its integrative approach, combining global epidemiological trends with detailed molecular and cellular mechanisms of lead toxicity. These findings are linked directly to practical, policy‐oriented mitigation strategies. By bridging mechanistic toxicology with population‐level evidence and equity‐focused public health actions, this review framed lead exposure as both a biomedical threat and a critical environmental justice concern.

Methods

This literature review integrated both qualitative and quantitative evidence on lead toxicity from January 2010 to March 2025. Literature searches were conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. Keywords included “lead toxicity,” “lead poisoning,” “prevalence,” “epidemiology,” “demographics,” “toxic mechanisms,” “interventions,” “mitigation strategies,” and “public health response,” combined with Boolean operators (AND/OR) to maximize results. Only English‐language publications on human health outcomes were included. Sources consisted of peer‐reviewed articles, gray literature, and reports addressing prevalence, mechanisms, epidemiological trends, climate‐related effects, and mitigation strategies across populations and regions. Studies lacking relevance or methodological rigor were excluded. Screening was conducted in two stages: first, titles and abstracts were reviewed; second, full texts of eligible studies were assessed. Data were extracted using a standardized matrix. Heterogeneity prevented meta‐analysis, but the narrative synthesis followed strict quality protocols. Methodological quality was assessed using SANRA [17], which evaluates six domains (justification, aims, search strategy, referencing, scientific reasoning, and data presentation) with scores from 0 to 12. This review achieved a SANRA score of 12, indicating high quality (Figure 1).

Prevalence of Lead Toxicity

Lead poisoning is a major global health concern, with children particularly vulnerable due to their developing nervous systems [18] (Table 1 and Figure 2). Yu estimated that about 800 million children worldwide, mostly in LMICs, have BLLs above the WHO threshold of 5 μg/dL. Reference values differ globally, with the CDC using 3.5 μg/dL and some LMICs using 10 μg/dL, complicating comparisons. In the United States, 590,000 children under 5 exceeded 3.5 μg/dL in 2016, with 4.3 million living in homes with lead‐based paint. Lead exposure causes cognitive, developmental, and behavioral impairments, with annual economic costs over $50 billion [11]. Al Sukaiti and colleagues reported that children are biologically and behaviorally more prone to lead exposure than adults because of frequent hand‐to‐mouth activity, higher gastrointestinal absorption, and the immaturity of the central nervous system. Overt clinical manifestations usually appear at high exposure levels, commonly when blood lead concentrations exceed 60 µg/dL [14]. In a related cohort, Canfield and colleagues examined 166 children aged 36 months with a mean BLL of 7.9 µg/dL, where 73% had levels below 10 µg/dL, yet measurable neurodevelopmental effects were still observed [14].

Prevalence of children lead toxicity.

Larsen and Sánchez‐Triana used blood lead estimates from the GBD 2019 data set to quantify global impacts. In 2019, children under 5 years lost an estimated 765 million IQ points, while 5.55 million adults (2.31–8.27 million) died from cardiovascular disease attributable to lead exposure (Figure 2). LMICs accounted for 95.3% of total IQ loss (729 million points) and 90.2% of cardiovascular deaths (5.0 million). The total global economic burden reached US$6.0 trillion (range 2.6–9.0 trillion), equivalent to 6.9% of global GDP, with 77% due to premature cardiovascular mortality and 23% from future income loss related to cognitive impairment [19]. Nurjannah and colleagues conducted the first population‐based survey in a nonindustrial Indonesian city (Banda Aceh), enrolling 130 children. Elevated BLLs (≥ 3.5 µg/dL) were detected in 24.6% of participants, with a mean level of 3.01 ± 1.14 µg/dL. Male sex, low maternal education, and corrugated iron roofing were independent predictors of elevated exposure [20].

Zarmawi and Haryanto studied 193 children living near tin mining sites in Indonesia and reported a geometric mean BLL of 5.5 µg/dL. Elevated lead exposure increased the odds of stunting nearly 10‐fold, independent of dietary intake and maternal education [26]. UNICEF estimated that nearly 800 million children worldwide approximately one in three have BLLs exceeding 5 µg/dL, with the highest burden in Africa, Asia, Central and South America, and Eastern Europe [21].

Tang and colleagues highlighted that more than 8 million Indonesian children exceed 5 µg/dL, despite the absence of a national surveillance system. Earlier work in Jakarta found 35% of children had levels ≥ 10 µg/dL, and 2.4% exceeded 20 µg/dL [22, 23]. Irawati and colleagues, Mansyur and colleagues, Zarmawi and Haryanto, and subsequent studies near battery recycling, mining, and smelting sites reposites reported prevalences ranging 34.9% to 69.5% with BLLs above 10 µg/dL [24, 26, 32]. Venkat and colleagues reported a cross‐sectional study of 92 Indian children aged 1–5 years, finding an 8.7% prevalence of lead toxicity (> 5 µg/dL), with thumb sucking and waste combustion significantly associated with elevated BLLS [31]. Li and colleagues analyzed 32,543 Chinese children aged 0–6 years, reporting a weighted prevalence of elevated BLLs (≥ 50 µg/L) of 4.1%, with regional variation and distinct age‐ and sex‐related patterns [25]. Risk factors included poor hygiene, indoor air pollution, contaminated water, traditional remedies, and passive smoking.

Li and colleagues noted that since the ban on leaded gasoline in 2000, childhood BLLs and poisoning prevalence in China have declined substantially over two decades [33]. Anyaegbu and colleagues documented childhood lead toxicity in Nigeria, identified 12.5% of school children with levels above 5 µg/dL, with higher prevalence among younger children and females [27]. Mitra and colleagues reported that 54% of 919 Bangladeshi children had BLLs exceeding 10 µg/dL, particularly in urban areas, with strong associations with industrial proximity and traditional medicines [29]. Avazpour and colleagues found a 12.0% prevalence of lead toxicity among Iranian children presenting with chronic abdominal pain, using cutoffs of 2.0 and 3.5 µg/dL [28]. Parween and colleagues emphasized that nearly 47% of Indian children have elevated BLLs, with neurocognitive impairment observed even below 5 µg/dL, underscoring the need for lower screening thresholds, mass surveillance, and public awareness [30].

Epidemiological Demography and Transition

In Ethiopia, lead exposure primarily affects children under 6 due to higher absorption and neurodevelopmental vulnerability, while high‐risk adults include informal miners, battery recyclers, painters, and auto repair workers [18, 34] (Figure 4). Men face direct occupational exposure, whereas women and children are indirectly affected through contaminated environments, particularly in urban and mining regions like Oromia and Benishangul‐Gumuz. Low‐income households are disproportionately affected by poor housing, unregulated consumer products, and unsafe water [35]. Globally, exposure patterns vary by income: in high‐income countries, children in low‐income urban areas remain exposed via legacy lead in paint, plumbing, and buildings, while construction and industrial workers face occupational risks [36]. In LMICs, industrial pollution, continued use of lead‐containing products, and weak regulatory enforcement sustain high exposure in children, with indirect effects on women [3]. Socioeconomic disadvantage further increases vulnerability.

Epidemiological transition across regions with stage.

The epidemiological transition of lead toxicity follows four stages (Figure 5): Stage 1 (Preindustrial/Early Industrial) involved widespread lead in household items, pottery, mining, and smelting, causing neurological, hematologic, and renal effects [37]; Stage 2 (Industrial Expansion) saw rapid industrialization and urban growth, with exposure from gasoline, emissions, plumbing, batteries, and paints, leading to neurodevelopmental deficits in children and chronic diseases in adults [36]; Stage 3 (Global Recognition/Regulation) introduced bans reducing exposure in high‐income countries, though LMICs lagged due to informal sectors, shifting the disease burden toward chronic outcomes [38]; Stage 4 (Postindustrial/Middle‐Income) reflected current conditions where high‐income nations address legacy contamination, while Ethiopia and similar LMICs continue to face exposure from urbanization, industrial pollution, and informal recycling, putting children at risk for cognitive impairment and adults at risk for hypertension, renal injury, and reproductive toxicity [39]. Despite some regulatory progress, ongoing informal practices and under‐resourced environmental health systems perpetuate exposure in LMICs [2].

Epidemiological transition stage and its health impact.

Salamanca‐Fernández and colleagues analyzed 17,790 EU citizens from 9 biomonitoring studies (2003–2019) across Belgium, the Czech Republic, Spain, and Germany, harmonized under HBM4EU, to explore blood lead exposure, sociodemographic determinants, and trends. Detectable Pb was observed in all participants, with a mean of 20.6 ± 16.7 µg/L. Older age, smoking, and urban residence were linked to higher levels, while recent sampling and higher education were associated with lower Pb. Pooled analyses showed no significant differences across countries, likely due to inter‐study heterogeneity [40]. EFSA set blood Pb benchmarks lower than WHO historical 100 µg/L: 12 µg/L for neurodevelopment, 15 µg/L for chronic kidney disease, and 36 µg/L for systolic blood pressure. Recent evidence suggested levels as low as 1–2 µg/dL may still cause adverse effects, indicating no definitive safe threshold [41].

However, legacy effects and challenges in some regions (like Africa and parts of Ethiopia) still pose significant public health threats.

Toxicokinetic and Dose‐Dependent Toxicity

Lead enters the body mainly through ingestion or inhalation, with rare dermal absorption. Children absorb 40%–50% of ingested lead versus 10%–15% in adults. Inhalation of fine particles (< 1 µm), especially in occupational settings, can reach 90% systemic absorption. Lead binds to erythrocytes, distributes to soft tissues (brain, liver, and kidneys), and accumulates in bones, where 70%–90% is stored and gradually released [42]. It crosses the placenta, exposing fetuses, and inhibits key enzymes, disrupting heme synthesis and causing anemia [43]. Lead is primarily eliminated via the kidneys, with minor excretion in bile, sweat, milk, hair, and nails [44, 45]. Its half‐life is ~30 days in blood, 40 days in soft tissues, and years in bone, enabling chronic toxicity.

Acute poisoning occurs with high‐dose, short‐term exposure, causing abdominal pain, vomiting, encephalopathy, seizures, anemia, and kidney injury at blood levels > 60–100 µg/dL [46]. Chronic toxicity arises from prolonged low‐to‐moderate exposure, leading to cognitive deficits in children, nephrotoxicity, hypertension, and reproductive issues in adults [47]. Children are especially vulnerable due to high absorption, immature blood–brain barrier, and neurodevelopment [48]. Pregnant women risk fetal exposure via mobilized bone lead [49]. Adults may develop kidney dysfunction, hypertension, and cognitive decline [49]. Sex differences affect susceptibility: estrogen facilitates lead excretion in premenopausal women, men are more prone to cardiovascular effects, and postmenopausal women face a higher risk from bone lead mobilization [50].

Yu and colleagues studied chronic Pb exposure in C57BL/6 mice (0.1, 0.5, and 1.0 g/L for 8 weeks), showed dose‐dependent increases in blood and tissue Pb, altered Zn, Fe, Mg, Ca, decreased renal catalase and glutathione, reduced colonic tight junction proteins, and gut microbiota shifts (increased Marvinbryantia, Ruminococcus 1; decreased Lactobacillus, Roseburia). PICRUSt analysis indicated disrupted metabolism, trace element homeostasis, and neurodegenerative pathways [51]. Hussain and colleagues exposed chickens to lead acetate (0–284 mg/kg) and observed decreased hemoglobin, packed cell volume, MCHC, and erythrocytes, increased WBCs and ALT, altered LDL, HDL, total protein, and high brain Pb accumulation, showing chronic Pb affects blood and organ function [52].

Yu and colleagues assessed Pb effects on HT‐29 cells (8 mM) and C57BL/6 mice (0–1000 mg/kg). Pb reduced cell viability by 50%, increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) by 200%, decreased ZO‐1 and occludin, caused colon damage and inflammation dose‐dependently, and altered gut microbiota composition (decreased Coprococcus, Oscillospira; increased Lactobacillus). PICRUSt analysis showed dose‐dependent metabolic alterations [53]. Anderson and colleagues reported Pb uptake in cocoyam from soils (0–10 ppm), with highest retention in leaves and potential health risks (bioconcentration factor 5.87, translocation factor 2.11) [54]. Yu and colleagues exposed mice (0–1000 mg/kg/day for 3 days), found dose‐dependent increases in liver Ca, Fe, Zn, decreases in blood Mg, Fe, reduced bone Ca, altered brain Mg, and oxidative stress without histopathology [55]. Slikker and colleagues highlighted that toxicants act via multiple mechanisms, with higher exposures overwhelming pathways and inducing new tissue injuries, illustrating dose‐dependent transitions relevant for risk assessment [56].

Molecular and Cellular Mechanism Toxicity

Lead is a systemic toxicant that exerts its harmful effects by disrupting a wide array of molecular and cellular processes [57]. In addition to promoting ROS generation and mitochondrial injury, lead interferes with calcium signaling, inhibits enzymes, impairs nitric oxide (NO) production, causes DNA damage, alters cell signaling cascades, affects epigenetic regulation, and damages cell membranes [58] (Table 2 and Figure 6).

Lead‐Induced ROS Generation and Oxidative Stress

Lead increases ROS, disrupting redox balance and causing molecular and cellular damage [66]. It inhibits antioxidant systems, including enzymes such as SOD, CAT, GPx, and molecules like GSH, by binding essential cofactors (Zn2+, Cu2+, and Se2−) and reducing detoxification capacity [67]. Pb displaces Zn2+ in SOD, impairing conversion of superoxide (O₂•⁻) to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which is normally detoxified by CAT or GPx, leading to accumulation of O₂•⁻ and H2O2 [66]. Lead also disrupts iron homeostasis, releasing Fe2+ that reacts with H2O2 via Fenton chemistry to form hydroxyl radicals (OH•), damaging DNA, proteins, and lipids. Additionally, Pb activates NADPH oxidase in endothelium, neurons, and immune cells, amplifying ROS, oxidative injury, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammation [67].

Nemsadz and colleagues reported that Pb exposure inhibits key antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, CAT, and GPx while depleting intracellular glutathione, promoting ROS accumulation and oxidative damage to lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids [59]. Dzugkoev and colleagues further showed that Pb‐induced oxidative stress disrupts vascular endothelium, increases lipid peroxidation, and activates pro‐inflammatory cytokines, contributing to systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction [60]. Szymański and colleagues reinforced that oxidative stress underlies multiple downstream injuries, including DNA damage, apoptosis, and neurodegeneration [61]. This persistent oxidative environment provokes chronic inflammation, contributing to cardiovascular and renal complications in exposed populations.

Mitochondrial Dysfunction Induced by ROS

Mitochondria are key targets of ROS in lead toxicity. Lead impairs mitochondrial function through multiple pathways [68]. ROS induced by lead cause lipid peroxidation, especially of cardiolipin, a mitochondrial inner membrane phospholipid essential for structural integrity and respiratory chain anchoring [69]. Oxidized cardiolipin disrupts membrane potential (Δψm) and ATP synthesis, promoting opening of mitochondrial permeability transition pores (mPTP) and release of cytochrome c, which activates caspase‐9 and caspase‐3, initiating intrinsic apoptosis [70]. Pb also directly inhibits electron transport chain complexes I and III, increasing electron leakage and ROS generation, inducing an energy crisis that accelerates cell death in neurons and renal cells [69]. ROS damage mitochondrial DNA, impairing ETC subunits, mitochondrial biogenesis, and respiration, contributing to long‐term organelle damage and progressive cellular degeneration [71]. Generalova and colleagues and Virgolini and Aschner reported that Pb‐induced mitochondrial dysfunction reduces ATP production, increases ROS, and promotes apoptosis, leading to organ‐specific injury in metabolically active tissues such as brain, liver, and kidney [62, 64].

Calcium Mimicry and Dysregulation

Lead exerts toxicity by disrupting calcium signaling and NO production, both critical for cellular function [72]. It mimics calcium ions, entering calcium pathways but failing to replace Ca2+ functionally, displacing calcium from proteins such as calmodulin, PKC, troponin, and synaptotagmin, which impairs signal transduction, muscle contraction, hormone release, and neurotransmitter exocytosis [73]. Pb also disrupts calcium stores in the endoplasmic reticulum, reduces mitochondrial calcium buffering, and inhibits calcium transporters (PMCA and SERCA), leading to cytosolic calcium overload, mPTP opening, cytochrome c release, oxidative stress, excitotoxicity, and intrinsic apoptosis [63, 72].

Nemsadz et al. [59] and Gudadhe et al. [63] reported that Pb mimics calcium ions, interfering with calcium‐dependent enzymes and neuronal signaling, thereby impairing vascular and neurodevelopmental processes. Virgolini and Aschner highlighted that Pb crosses the blood–brain barrier, alters calcium signaling, and contributes to neurocognitive deficits. In bones, Pb competes with calcium for deposition, causing impaired mineralization and structural weakness, particularly in children [62].

Enzyme Inhibition and Impaired Hemoglobin Synthesis

Lead strongly binds to sulfhydryl (‐SH) and carboxyl groups on proteins, inactivating many enzymes essential for metabolism and detoxification [74]. It inhibits δ‐aminolevulinic acid dehydratase (ALAD) and ferrochelatase, causing accumulation of neurotoxic δ‐ALA, reduced heme formation, and microcytic anemia, which impairs oxygen transport and contributes to hypoxia, fatigue, cardiovascular stress, and worsened neurodevelopmental outcomes in children [75]. Singh et al. [57] and Nemsadz et al. [59] also noted Pb inhibition of heme biosynthesis enzymes, leading to anemia and associated systemic consequences [76]. Lead further displaces metals like zinc and copper from antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, GPx, and GSH‐related), weakening defenses against oxidative stress [75], and disrupts mitochondrial Krebs cycle enzymes, including isocitrate dehydrogenase, impairing energy metabolism [77].

Nitric Oxide Modulation and Endothelial Dysfunction and Alteration of Enzyme Activity

Lead exposure disrupts NO regulation by affecting NOS isoforms such as eNOS, nNOS, and iNOS [78]. It lowers eNOS activity, reducing NO in blood vessels, which leads to vasoconstriction, increased vascular resistance, and endothelial dysfunction [79]. Oxidative stress from lead can uncouple NOS, particularly when tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) is depleted [80], generating superoxide (O2•⁻) instead of NO and forming peroxynitrite (ONOO−) that damages lipids, DNA, and proteins [78]. Lead also upregulates iNOS in immune cells, producing excess NO and promoting chronic oxidative/nitrosative stress and inflammation [81]. Nemsadz et al. [59] and Singh et al. [57] reported that Pb directly inhibits enzymes critical for metabolism and antioxidant defense, disrupting cellular homeostasis. Sulfhydryl‐containing enzymes are particularly affected, altering redox balance, glucose metabolism, and lipid metabolism.

DNA Damage and Gene Regulation

Lead causes both direct and indirect DNA damage. Directly, it binds to the DNA phosphate backbone and nitrogenous bases, disrupting the double helix and impairing replication and transcription, leading to chromosomal aberrations, micronuclei, sister chromatid exchanges, and strand breaks [82]. Indirectly, lead‐induced ROS oxidize DNA bases, forming lesions like 8‐oxo‐deoxyguanosine, which can cause mutations and cancer [83]. Lead also inhibits DNA repair enzymes in base excision and nucleotide excision pathways, reducing error correction and increasing genomic instability [75, 80]. Szymański et al. [82] and Dzugkoev et al. [60] demonstrated that Pb induces DNA strand breaks and epigenetic modifications affecting gene transcription. Gillis and colleagues reported that Pb alters expression of genes encoding metallothioneins, metabolic enzymes, and signaling proteins in immune cells, with some effects persisting after Pb removal [65]. These disruptions contribute to impaired DNA repair, increased apoptosis, and altered immune function, linking Pb exposure to cancer risk and chronic immune dysfunction.

Altered Cell Signaling Pathways

Lead disrupts key cellular signaling pathways that regulate growth, death, and immunity [84]. It alters MAPK pathways (ERK, JNK, and p38), activates NF‐κB, increases pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as TNF‐α and IL‐6, and impairs calcium signaling, affecting CaMK activity, neuronal plasticity, and memory [85]. Gillis et al. [65] and Virgolini and Aschner [62] reported that lead also disrupts G‐protein‐coupled signaling, MAPK cascades (Akt and p38), CREB, and gap junction communication, impairing neuronal plasticity, vascular responses, apoptosis regulation, and metabolic control, contributing to cognitive deficits, hypertension, and tissue‐specific damage.

Epigenetic Alterations and Endoplasmic Reticulum Stress

Lead toxicity also causes heritable changes in gene expression without altering the DNA sequence [86]. Exposure induces global DNA hypomethylation and site‐specific hypermethylation, affecting genes involved in neurodevelopment, immunity, and metabolism [87]. It disrupts histone acetylation and methylation, altering chromatin structure and transcription factor access, and dysregulates microRNA, which control gene expression posttranscriptionally, contributing to abnormal cell growth, differentiation, and apoptosis [88]. Singh et al. [57] and Virgolini and Aschner [62] noted that Pb triggers ER stress, leading to misfolded protein accumulation and activation of the unfolded protein response. Chronic ER stress induces apoptosis and organ dysfunction, particularly in the liver, kidney, and brain, intersecting with oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction to amplify cellular damage.

Cell Membrane Damage and Ion Transport Disruption

Lead damages cell membranes by generating ROS that induce lipid peroxidation, particularly of polyunsaturated fatty acids, altering membrane fluidity and permeability [89]. It also inhibits ion pumps such as Na+/K+‐ATPase and Ca2+‐ATPase, disrupting ionic gradients essential for neuronal activity, muscle contraction, and nutrient transport [90], and interferes with gap junctions, impairing coordinated cellular function in organs like the brain and heart [91]. Dzugkoev et al. [60] and Szymański et al. [61] highlighted that Pb‐induced lipid peroxidation compromises membrane integrity, ion transport, and cellular signaling, with neurons, endothelial cells, and hepatocytes particularly vulnerable, resulting in neurological deficits, vascular dysfunction, and metabolic disturbances.

Immune System Modulation

Gillis et al. [65] and Dzugkoev et al. [60] observed that Pb exposure increases secretion of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including IL‐6, TNF‐α, IL‐8, and MIP1‐α, causing chronic inflammation. Pb interferes with both innate and adaptive immune responses, impairing pathogen clearance and promoting autoimmunity. This immune modulation exacerbates tissue injury in organs such as the liver, kidney, and vasculature and increases susceptibility to infections.

Mitigation Strategies

Effective management of lead toxicity requires coordinated efforts across regulation, technology, public health education, nutrition, surveillance, and global collaboration [92]. While high‐income countries have successfully reduced exposure, LMICs continue to face challenges due to weak regulations, informal industries, poor infrastructure, and low awareness [93] (Table 3 and Figure 7). Regulatory benchmarks set by EFSA are stricter than WHO historical 100 µg/L: 12 µg/L for neurodevelopment, 15 µg/L for chronic kidney disease, and 36 µg/L for systolic blood pressure, although adverse effects can occur at 1–2 µg/dL, indicating that no safe threshold exists [40]. Public health guidelines, such as CDC et al., adopt a 5 µg/dL blood lead reference for children aged 1–5 years, updated every 4 years using NHANES data, to guide follow‐up and identify high‐risk populations [10]. Health effects at PbB ≤ 5, ≤ 10, and > 10 µg/dL emphasize the continued need for exposure reduction strategies [41].

Regulatory and Policy Interventions

Phasing out leaded gasoline, completed globally by 2021 through UNEP‐led efforts, has significantly reduced environmental lead exposure [113]. Similarly, banning or regulating lead in paints, toys, ceramics, cosmetics, and food containers has limited major exposure pathways [113]. Strengthening legal frameworks including mandatory product labeling, government audits of lead‐emitting industries, and enforcement of occupational safety measures such as BLL screening, PPE, ventilation, and worker education ensures compliance and protects high‐risk populations [114]. Standards for lead‐safe housing, renovation, paint abatement, and soil remediation further safeguard children [115]. Regional enforcement, through bodies such as the African Union or ASEAN, prevents illegal lead imports [116], while applying the “polluter pays” principle holds industries accountable for remediation and health costs [117]. Raj and Das [94] and Ghassemi Toussi et al. [95] highlighted the importance of robust regulatory frameworks to limit lead production, use, and emissions. Examples include the US Clean Air Act, European Union directives on heavy metals, and international conventions like the Minamata Convention on Mercury, enforced by national environmental agencies (e.g., US EPA and European Environment Agency), ministries of environment, and intergovernmental organizations. These regulations set permissible exposure limits, control industrial discharges, and enforce penalties, forming the backbone of national and international lead management strategies.

Environmental and Industrial Interventions

Environmental remediation is essential to reduce lead exposure in industrial and urban areas [107]. Soil can be managed through replacement, chemical stabilization, or phytoremediation using hyperaccumulator plants [110]. Water safety requires replacing old lead pipes, applying corrosion inhibitors, and monitoring quality, as demonstrated in Flint, Michigan [97]. Proper disposal and recycling of lead‐containing products, including batteries, e‐waste, and paints, prevent further contamination [118]. Industrial emissions can be minimized with HEPA filters, scrubbers, closed‐loop systems, and adherence to standards such as ISO 14001, which promote environmental management systems, while national authorities ensure compliance [98]. Safe mining and smelting practices, including dust control and tailings management, protect workers and communities [119]. Decontamination of hotspots via capping, excavation, chemical stabilization, and in‐situ treatment is crucial for former industrial and waste sites [120]. Gupta et al. [99] and Kumar et al. [96] highlighted that Pb contamination from industrial activities, battery manufacturing, and mining can be mitigated through these strict environmental controls and preventive strategies.

Occupational Health and Worker Protection

Protecting workers in high‐risk industries is crucial to reduce lead exposure [121]. Regular monitoring of BLLs, kidney function, and cognitive performance enables early detection [111]. Engineering controls such as ventilation, mechanization, and dust containment, combined with mandatory PPE use, hygiene training, and decontamination facilities, prevent exposure and take‐home contamination [100]. Worker rotation and training programs further minimize cumulative risk and promote safe practices [101]. Nemsadz et al. [59] and Ghassemi Toussi et al. [95] highlighted that workers in Pb‐intensive industries (batteries, mining, smelting, and paints) are particularly vulnerable. Occupational health measures include PPE, regular BLL screening, workplace ventilation, and industrial hygiene monitoring, with guidelines enforced by regulatory bodies such as OSHA (U.S.) and NIOSH.

Public Health and Educational Strategies

Public education is crucial for preventing lead exposure [122]. Community programs should raise awareness about hazards and common sources, including traditional cosmetics, pottery, folk remedies, and informal recycling [103]. Behavior change campaigns through media and schools can encourage safer practices like handwashing, wet‐mopping, and avoiding contaminated products. Health literacy initiatives empower marginalized populations to recognize risks and advocate for safer environments [104]. Targeted interventions in informal sectors, such as safer tools, education, and pathways to formalization, are essential to reduce exposure [13]. Sani and Amanabo [102] and Gudadhe et al. [63] highlighted the role of public awareness campaigns and education programs, including community education on Pb‐containing products, training healthcare providers to identify Pb toxicity, and promoting safe occupational practices. Ministries of health and public health NGO are responsible for implementing these initiatives.

Clinical and Nutritional Interventions

Medical management is crucial for lead toxicity. Routine BLL screening in high‐risk groups like children, pregnant women, and industrial workers enables early detection and intervention [123]. Elevated BLLs are treated with chelation agents such as EDTA, DMSA, and BAL under clinical supervision for moderate to severe cases [105]. Nutritional strategies, including diets rich in calcium, iron, and vitamin C and micronutrient supplementation (calcium, iron, and zinc) in children and pregnant women, reduce lead absorption [106]. Population‐level fortification of staple foods and nutrition education promoting fruits, vegetables, and whole grains provide cost‐effective prevention and support natural detoxification [105]. Singh et al. [57], Dzugkoev et al. [60], and Virgolini and Aschner [62] described these therapeutic approaches, including chelation and nutritional interventions, to mitigate oxidative stress and protect exposed populations.

Maternal and Child Health Interventions

Protecting mothers and children from lead exposure is essential due to their high vulnerability [108]. Prenatal BLL screening in high‐risk areas can detect early exposure and guide interventions to protect the fetus, with chelation considered if maternal levels are dangerously high and safe [109]. Ensuring schools and daycare centers are free from lead paint, contaminated water, and dust reduces childhood exposure [124]. Breastfeeding is generally recommended, but mothers with very high BLLs should be evaluated carefully, with risk–benefit decisions guiding feeding [108]. Gudadhe et al. [63] and Gillis et al. [65] emphasized these points, highlighting the need for maternal screening, prenatal nutritional supplementation, and minimizing exposure at home and in schools, with pediatric and obstetric healthcare providers, supported by ministries of health, responsible for implementing preventive measures.

Food and Water Safety Measures

Lead contamination in food and water remains a significant concern, particularly in LMICs [125]. Routine testing of food products such as spices (e.g., turmeric adulterated with lead chromate), canned goods, and imported items helps prevent dietary exposure [110]. Use of lead‐free packaging, nonglazed ceramics, and safe storage containers minimizes leaching into food [5]. Upgrading municipal water systems through pipe replacement, corrosion control, and regular monitoring ensures safe drinking water [111]. Raj and Das [94], Gupta et al. [99], and Ghassemi Toussi et al. [95] reported that lead enters humans via contaminated food and water, recommending interventions such as monitoring Pb levels in drinking water, removing lead pipes, testing food items, and promoting safe agricultural practices. Regulatory authorities like the FDA (U.S.), EFSA (EU), and national food safety agencies enforce these standards to safeguard public health.

Technological Innovation and Capacity Transfer

Technological innovation provides essential tools for early detection and prevention of lead exposure [112]. Low‐cost detection methods, including handheld X‐ray fluorescence analyzers and paper‐based BLL test kits, expand screening access, particularly in under‐resourced or rural areas [112]. Global knowledge sharing through conferences, research collaborations, and training programs promotes dissemination of best practices [126]. Capacity building trains environmental health professionals, toxicologists, public health officers, scientists, and policymakers in monitoring, remediation, and response strategies [112]. Kumar et al. [96] and Ghassemi Toussi et al. [95] highlighted the use of nanoparticles, microbial bioremediation, and cost‐effective soil and water decontamination techniques for lead mitigation. Universities, research institutions, and environmental technology companies play a central role in developing and implementing these solutions.

Surveillance, Research, and Monitoring

Surveillance and monitoring systems are essential for effective lead mitigation [105]. National lead exposure registries enable real‐time tracking of BLL trends, particularly among children and occupational groups [99]. Longitudinal cohort studies help identify delayed or subtle effects of chronic low‐level exposure on neurodevelopment, cardiovascular function, and kidney health [95, 105]. Advanced tools, such as geographic information systems and crowd‐sourced data platforms, can map environmental hotspots and guide targeted public health responses [101, 110]. Raj and Das [94], Ghassemi Toussi et al. [95], and Gillis et al. [65] highlighted the importance of continuous surveillance through biomonitoring programs (BLL surveys), environmental sampling of soil, water, and food, and reporting systems for occupational and accidental exposure. Agencies like the CDC, WHO, national environmental monitoring authorities, and research laboratories implement these activities to inform interventions and evaluate policy effectiveness.

Limitations of This Study

This narrative review has several limitations. First, it relied primarily on published literature, which may introduce publication bias and overlook unpublished data or regional reports. Second, the qualitative synthesis limits the ability to provide pooled quantitative estimates or conduct meta‐analyses. Third, variability in study designs, populations, exposure assessment methods, and reporting standards across included studies may reduce comparability and generalizability. Fourth, although recent studies were prioritized, some emerging data particularly from LMICs where lead exposure is often underreported may be missing. Finally, the review focused on broad molecular mechanisms and environmental sources but does not provide exhaustive coverage of all potential confounding factors, such as nutritional status, co‐exposures, or genetic susceptibility, which may influence lead toxicity.

Future Directions

Future research should prioritize longitudinal and large‐scale epidemiological studies, particularly in LMICs, to better quantify lead exposure and its health impacts. Integrated approaches that link molecular mechanisms with population‐level outcomes are needed to inform targeted interventions. Development and evaluation of context‐specific mitigation strategies including sustainable remediation technologies, public health policies, and educational programs are essential. Additionally, emerging tools such as nanotechnology and advanced biomonitoring should be explored to reduce lead bioavailability and enhance early detection. Collaborative efforts among governments, researchers, and communities are crucial for implementing scalable, equity‐focused solutions to protect vulnerable populations.

Conclusion

Lead exposure remains a pervasive global environmental and public health challenge, disproportionately affecting children and vulnerable populations in LMICs. Its toxicity spans multiple biological systems and is mediated through mechanisms such as oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, disruption of calcium signaling, impaired heme synthesis, DNA damage, and immune modulation. Despite substantial progress in regulatory control and remediation in high‐income regions, persistent environmental sources including contaminated food, water, and informal industrial activities continue to pose significant risks worldwide. This review underscored the urgent need for integrated, multilevel strategies that combine robust surveillance, sustainable environmental remediation, public health education, occupational safeguards, and targeted maternal and child health interventions. Advances in nanotechnology and biomonitoring offer promising avenues for reducing lead bioavailability and enabling early detection of exposure. Ultimately, bridging molecular insights with epidemiological evidence is critical for designing context‐sensitive, equity‐focused interventions that protect human health and safeguard ecosystems.
