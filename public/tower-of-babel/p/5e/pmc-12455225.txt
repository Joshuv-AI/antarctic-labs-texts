Awake prone lateral transpsoas lumbar interbody fusion with simultaneous posterior decompression and fusion: illustrative case
Authors: Christian Quinones, Wesley Jameson, Ryan Diaz, Enoch Kim, Deepak Kumbhare, Varsha Allampalli, Stanley Hoang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDAwake spine surgery under spinal anesthesia may reduce perioperative risk in older or medically complex patients. This report presents the first documented case of an awake, single-position prone lateral transpsoas interbody fusion at L3–4 with simultaneous posterior decompression and fusion to treat a 77-year-old male with neurogenic claudication and mechanical back pain. A technical approach was developed to complete the procedure within the approximate 3-hour duration of spinal anesthesia. Prone positioning enabled simultaneous access to lateral and posterior corridors. Intraoperative navigation, dual-surgeon coordination, and patient-specific anesthetic planning were used to streamline the workflow.OBSERVATIONSThe procedure was completed within the spinal anesthesia window without the need for conversion to general anesthesia. The patient remained hemodynamically stable and tolerated prone positioning. No lumbar plexus–related complications occurred, and both clinical and radiographic outcomes were favorable.LESSONSThis case supports the feasibility of awake, single-position prone lateral lumbar interbody fusion with concurrent posterior decompression and fusion. It highlights key considerations for patient selection, anesthetic planning, and intraoperative coordination. Ongoing refinement of this technique may broaden its applicability in high-risk populations by balancing operative efficiency with the benefits of spinal anesthesia. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25467

Awake spine surgery offers a favorable safety profile, improved outcomes,1–4 and reduced morbidity,5–8 expanding eligibility to higher-risk patients, while minimizing the negative effects of general anesthesia.5–7 It can be performed with local,1,9,10 epidural,11 or spinal anesthesia.12 By limiting medication side effects and allowing for intraoperative patient feedback, spinal anesthesia is preferred for its rapid onset, consistency, and duration.8 The primary challenges for spinal anesthesia are the 3-hour time limit for completion of surgery due to the half-life of the spinal anesthetic, and the motor blockade, which limits neurological assessment.13,14 These constraints underscore the importance of efficiency, achievable through single-position prone lateral surgery, which enables concurrent lateral and posterior access within the spinal anesthesia window.

Lateral lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF) has gained popularity over the past 2 decades due to its ability to achieve indirect decompression, restore disc space height, and improve spinopelvic alignment.15–18 Traditional LLIF requires repositioning from lateral to prone, increasing operative time. Prone lateral interbody fusion eliminates the need for repositioning, enabling simultaneous posterior and lateral access. This approach reduces operative time, improves lordotic correction,19 and lowers operating room costs.20 It also allows two surgeons to operate concurrently, significantly reducing surgical time.21

The integration of optimized anesthesia and surgical techniques reflects ongoing progress in spine surgery. This report describes the preoperative considerations and intraoperative workflow of a single-position prone LLIF with concurrent posterior decompression and instrumented fusion under spinal anesthesia. The procedure was completed in 138 minutes through a coordinated workflow between an attending and resident neurosurgeon. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the procedure and for the publication of protected health information and images. Approval was obtained from the Louisiana State University Institutional Review Board for review of operative outcomes. Any identifiable individual provided consent for publication of their image.

Illustrative Case

The patient is a 77-year-old male who presented with long-standing mechanical back pain and bilateral leg pain that worsened with walking, consistent with neurogenic claudication. His medical history included aortic stenoses after aortic valve replacement and coronary artery disease with more than 75% stenoses of the left anterior descending coronary artery and left circumflex artery. Physical examination revealed a right-sided foot drop, reportedly following right hip arthroplasty 2 years prior. Imaging demonstrated severe central stenosis at L3–4 from a calcified disc with severe foraminal stenosis due to collapsed disc space (Fig. 1A–E). Surgery was pursued following exhaustion of conservative management, which included interventional pain procedures and physical therapy. He was offered an awake, single-position prone LLIF with concurrent open posterior decompression and fusion at L3–4 under spinal anesthesia (Fig. 2). Prior to surgery, the patient and his family were counseled on the risks, benefits, and technical considerations of an innovative, previously undescribed procedure. Special emphasis was impressed on the patient and his family that while prone lateral techniques and spinal anesthesia have been used separately, their combination is novel. The patient expressed a preference for awake spine surgery due to his age, the desire to avoid the side effects of general anesthesia, and the reportedly smaller risks of intraoperative cardiac events.22

Operation

The patient was positioned sitting for spinal anesthesia (Fig. 3). Following sterile preparation and landmark identification, a 25-gauge needle was introduced into the L3–4 subarachnoid space. After confirming CSF flow, 1.8 mL of 0.75% hyperbaric bupivacaine with 200 μg of epinephrine and 20 μg of fentanyl was administered. Bilateral lower extremity motor blockade and a T5 sensory level were confirmed. Further sedation included 60 μg of dexmedetomidine, 2 mg of midazolam, and 25 μg of fentanyl. The patient was then transferred to the prone position on a Jackson table (Fig. 3). He immediately complained of significant discomfort in his left frozen shoulder, which was then adjusted by lowering the arm support. It should be noted that having the patient awake allowed proper positioning of the left shoulder, which otherwise might have been missed under general anesthesia. The incisions were marked under fluoroscopy. After the surgical field was prepped and draped, a reference frame was attached to the left posterior superior iliac spine and an internal bony fiducial23 was placed in the L3 spinous process for confirmation of navigation accuracy prior to 3D image acquisition with the O-arm.24

Navigation accuracy is especially important since the patient can move during awake surgery. Prior to 3D image acquisition, an internal bony fiducial, which was a 5-mm cranial Stryker screw (Fig. 4A), was inserted into the L3 spinous process (Fig. 4B) to improve navigational accuracy. Navigation accuracy was confirmed by touching the tip of the navigation probe to the internal bony fiducial (Fig. 4C). We find the bony fiducial technique to be a reliable confirmation of navigation accuracy compared to bony landmarks, since the fiducial is significantly more visible due to its bright metallic nature (Fig. 4D). The 3D image was uploaded to the navigation system, and instruments were registered by the scrub technician. The lateral and posterior incisions were made with both approaches proceeding concurrently, with the posterior approach surgeon on the right side of the patient and the lateral approach surgeon on the left side of the patient. The lateral approach was performed from the left side by the attending neurosurgeon. Blunt dissection through the abdominal muscles was followed by finger dissection to sweep the retroperitoneal content anteriorly. A navigated dilator was then advanced through the psoas muscle until docked at the L3–4 disc space (Fig. 4E). Sequential dilators were placed under directional electromyography (EMG) stimulation (Fig. 4F), which was found to be reliable and robust. Of note, under spinal anesthesia, EMG still remains effective as it monitors peripheral nerve activity independent of patient consciousness or cortical function.25,26 The lateral retractor was then placed and secured to the connector arm. An annulotomy was performed with an 11-blade, and the discectomy continued with a pituitary rongeur. The Cobb elevator was used to break the contralateral annulus. Care was taken to maintain instruments along the mid-vertebral body, which can be visualized on the navigation system by drawing a line along the desired trajectory for the interbody, as depicted by the orange line on the Stealth image (Fig. 4G and H). Disc preparation was completed using the sequential distractors (4 mm and 6 mm). An 8-mm-high trial (Fig. 4I) was placed with confirmation under fluoroscopy. A interbody cage (10 mm high, 6° lordosis) was gently malleted into the disc space under navigation (Fig. 4K and L). During cage insertion, the patient reported abdominal discomfort and nausea, possibly due to the force of malleting, which resolved after a brief pause and administration of 10 mg of metoclopramide. Posterior exposure performed by the resident surgeon standing on the right side occurred concurrently with the lateral approach. Following subperiosteal dissection to the lateral facet joints, entry points were identified using navigation. The pedicle screw entry point was created with a Midas drill (Fig. 5A),27 followed by pedicle cannulation with the gearshift (Fig. 5B) and preparation of the screw path by tapping (Fig. 5C), with eventual placement of the pedicle screw (Fig. 5D). Fluoroscopy confirmed proper screw placement, which was further verified with EMG stimulation to 30 mA with no responses. Of note, with navigation, the resident surgeon was able to place screws on both the left and right sides while remaining on the right side, eliminating the need to switch positions (Fig. 5E). This enabled the lateral surgeon to continue working on the left side without interruption.

Posterior decompression included L3–4 laminectomy, lateral recess decompressions, and foraminotomies. Careful examination of the dura at the level of spinal anesthesia confirmed the absence of a CSF leak, likely attributable to the small 25-gauge needle used to obtain intrathecal access for spinal anesthesia. Two rods were then placed, and correct hardware positioning was confirmed with fluoroscopy (Fig. 1L). Twenty milliliters of liposomal bupivacaine was administered subcutaneously for postoperative pain control, and the wound was closed (Fig. 5G).

The total time from initiation of spinal anesthesia to wound closure was 138 minutes, significantly under the 3-hour limit (Fig. 2). The posterior and lateral incisions were made at 37 and 43 minutes respectively. From incision to closure, the lateral stage lasted 64 minutes, with retractors in place for 28 minutes. The posterior stage took 101 minutes for exposure, screw placement, decompression and wound closure. Pedicle screw placement was completed in 21 minutes and was followed by a 26-minute decompression. The patient remained neurologically stable postoperatively, without psoas or lumbar plexus–related complications. CT and MR imaging demonstrated restoration of disc space height without endplate violation and adequate decompression of central and foraminal stenoses (Fig. 1F–J). The patient was discharged home at 3 days postoperatively, mainly due to elevated drain output from the posterior incision. At 8 weeks postoperatively, the patient was doing well with improvement of neurogenic claudication and with imaging showing stable instrumentation (Fig. 1M and N).

Discussion

Observations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of an awake, single-position prone LLIF with concurrent open posterior decompression and fusion under spinal anesthesia. The lateral stage was performed by the attending neurosurgeon, and the posterior stage was assisted by a neurosurgical resident. This workflow allowed completion of surgery within the 3-hour window limit of spinal anesthesia.28

Benefits and Risks

Awake spine surgery may reduce general anesthesia–related side effects, particularly in older patients,29 and lower overall perioperative risk,22 allowing inclusion of previously medically ineligible candidates. It also reduces morbidity, enables intraoperative patient feedback, and decreases overall medication administration.5 Faster recovery rates may reduce hospital costs,2,22 length of stay, and narcotic use and facilitate earlier ambulation.1,30–32 These outcomes support the need for continued refinement of awake protocols to expand access and address the needs of patients with diverse or complex clinical profiles.

Patient Selection and Anesthetic Technique

LLIF in patients with disc collapse and central stenosis is associated with symptomatic improvement.33,34 By restoring disc height with a large implant, LLIF was an ideal surgical option for the patient’s L3–4 disc collapse, central canal stenosis, and foraminal stenosis. Open decompression was necessary due to the calcified disc herniation and the significant stenoses from ligamentum flavum hypertrophy. Indirect decompression with percutaneous instrumentation alone would have reduced operative time but would not have achieved adequate decompression. The patient’s history of coronary artery disease favored the use of spinal anesthesia, which improves hemodynamic control, reduces myocardial stress, attenuates sympathetic response, and preserves venous return by avoiding mechanical ventilation.35 Spinal anesthesia is combined with conscious sedation, and adjunctive medications are used to maintain intraoperative stability and postoperative pain control.2,30,36–38 In this case, we used a hyperbaric solution of long-acting bupivacaine, which, although slightly shorter in duration than isobaric preparations according to a meta-analysis,39 has comparable onset of sensory and motor blockade and heart rate variability.40 Spinal anesthesia with dexmedetomidine and long-acting bupivacaine minimized hypotension and supported recovery. Conversion to general anesthesia occurs in approximately 2% of awake cases in a recent series.30 As planned preoperatively, the anesthesia team had airway equipment including a laryngeal mask and video laryngoscope prepared. In case of airway compromise, the surgical site should be closed quickly with staples, covered with Ioban, and the patient repositioned for rapid sequence intubation.

Optimizing Workflow and Navigation Accuracy

Preoperative planning included imaging review, patient orientation, and coordination with anesthesia. Navigation is essential in helping to reduce surgical time by limiting the use of fluoroscopy for both screw and interbody placement.41 In this study, the bony fiducial marker provided high-visibility navigation accuracy confirmation, which is especially important during awake procedures with potential patient movement. Moreover, the distal pedicle screws at L3 were placed prior to disc preparation to preserve anatomical accuracy. A horizontal orange line drawn at the mid-vertebral body on the navigation screen aided visualization of a consistent lateral trajectory during disc space preparation and interbody placement. These measures enhanced navigation precision and enabled simultaneous two-surgeon workflow to reduce operative time.

Intraoperative Neuromonitoring

Intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM) is valuable during LLIF for identifying proximity to the lumbar plexus, with decreasing triggered EMG (tEMG) thresholds indicating proximity to motor nerves.26,42,43 While tEMG may be used alone, the ideal setup includes motor evoked potentionals and EMG,44 both of which depend on intact neuromuscular junctions and are influenced by anesthetic technique.45 In awake surgery, transcranial stimulation is not feasible, limiting monitoring to EMG. In this case, tEMG provided reliable information during the transpsoas approach, supporting its feasibility under spinal anesthesia, a finding not previously reported. Moreover, declining EMG signals during retraction show 80% sensitivity for postoperative neuropraxia.42 Therefore, given the limitations of IONM,46 surgeons should minimize lateral retractor time on the psoas, which is the most modifiable factor influencing lumbar plexus–related injury.33,34,42

Lessons

This case highlights the feasibility of performing an awake, single-position prone LLIF with concurrent open posterior decompression and fusion under spinal anesthesia. The prone lateral approach is particularly well suited to fit within the 3-hour time constraint of spinal anesthesia. Successful execution depended on procedural expertise, an optimized anesthetic strategy, precise intraoperative navigation, and multidisciplinary coordination. As the demand for spine surgery continues to grow, especially among medically complex and aging populations, further refinement of awake techniques may expand surgical access, mitigate perioperative risk, and enhance operative efficiency. Future studies are warranted to validate outcomes, standardize protocols, and support broader adoption across diverse clinical settings.
