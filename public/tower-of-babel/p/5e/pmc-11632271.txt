Use of ivabradine in children with junctional ectopic tachycardia after pediatric cardiac surgery; two‐centre experience
Authors: Hacer Kamali, Erkut Öztürk, Mehtap Çiftçi, Hasan Candas Kafali, Gülhan Tunca Şahin, Sertaç Haydin, Ali Can Hatemi, İbrahim Cansaran Tanıdır, Alper Güzeltaş, Yakup Ergül

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlthough amiodarone is traditionally used in the treatment of postoperative junctional ectopic tachycardia (JET), the search for new treatments is ongoing. The diagnosis of JET was made with the support of the electrophysiology team and treatment was based primarily on whether JET disrupted hemodynamics.ResultsThis study was conducted at two high‐volume centers, which record a total of 1130 pediatric cardiac surgeries within a year. INTRODUCTION

Junctional ectopic tachycardia (JET) is the most common tachyarrhythmia experienced following surgery for congenital heart disease. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 The approximate incidence of JET following congenital heart diseases (CHDs) surgeries is 5.6%–14%, although it varies between medical centers. 5

JET usually starts within the first 72 h following surgery, is often self‐limiting, and spontaneously resolves within 1 week. 6 However, because a rapid heart rate and atrioventricular (VA) dyssynchrony cause insufficient ventricular filling and a reduction in cardiac stroke volume, JET can cause significant morbidity and mortality.

Notwithstanding the consolidation of conventional preventive measures, research on new drug treatments is actively being pursued. Pharmacotherapy options include the utilization of intravenous amiodarone, procainamide, and flecainide. In exceptionally rare instances, patients who exhibit resistance to pharmacotherapy and present with hemodynamic instability may necessitate the consideration of more profound interventions, such as extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. 7

Ivabradine is a selective inhibitor of the hyperpolarization‐activated, cyclic nucleotide‐gated (HCN4) channels involved in the funny current‐related depolarization.

Ivabradine is a medication that reduces heart rate by inhibiting the I(f) current responsible for the inherent pacemaker activity of the sinoatrial node.

As it slows down, the sinoatrial node rate‐dependent slowing of conduction at the AV node, with a reduction in the ventricular rate, has also been demonstrated in patients with atrial fibrillation. 8

Furthermore, ivabradine is commonly used to lower heart rates in adult patients with heart failure when the heart rate is not adequately controlled with B‐blockers. 9 Initially employed in congenital JET as an adjunct to amiodarone and other drugs, the favorable outcomes achieved with ivabradine provided impetus for its use in postoperative JET cases. 10 , 11

In recent years, a growing body of literature—comprising a limited number of case examples and studies—has emerged, reporting the efficacy and safety of ivabradine in the treatment of postoperative junctional JET. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 The aim of our study is to present our experience with the use of ivabradine in patients with postoperative JET at two medical centers.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study population

This study was conducted at two high‐volume medical centers in which a combined total of 1130 pediatric cardiac surgeries were performed within a year. Patients who developed JET after pediatric cardiac surgery between January 2022 and January 2023 were followed up prospectively and documented. All patients under 18 years of age who experienced JET during the postoperative period were included in the study; informed consent for ivabradine administration was obtained from the parents or legally authorized representatives or guardians of these patients. Patients identified as being at a high risk for postoperative arrhythmias were equipped with two atrial and two ventricular epicardial pacing wires. All study participants were monitored during the postoperative period and evaluated using a 12‐channel electrocardiogram (ECG). To clearly identify the P waves, we also employed atrial tracings from temporary pacing wires. The ECGs were reviewed by a pediatric cardiologist and confirmed by a pediatric electrophysiologist (YE). A transthoracic echocardiogram was performed on all participants daily.

Clinical assessment

A JET diagnosis based on ECG readings is characterized by the following: a narrow complex tachycardia—in the absence of a branch block—at heart rates ranging from 170 to 260 beats per minute (considering ventricular rates exceeding 95th percentile for age) and AV dissociation when the ventricular rate is greater than the atrial rate (Figure 1A), and to enhance visibility of the P waves, we reinforced the diagnosis by observing that the tachycardia was AV dissociated on the atrial ECG with the assistance of temporary pacing wires (Figure 1B). The diagnosis was supported by administering adenosine to patients thought to have a 1:1 retrograde conduction (Figure 1C). When diagnosing restrictive physiology, we initially assess the patients for risk factors. After that, we examine the patient's clinical presentation, rhythm with ECG and atrial ECG and echocardiographic assessment.

As a strategy, we typically retain PFO in all of our patients.

Medical treatment protocol

The two medical centers involved in this study adhered to the treatment and management protocol outlined in Figure 2. The medical treatment protocol commenced with the following nonpharmacological interventions: sedation, with, or without paralysis; core body cooling, maintained at a target temperature range of 34–35°C; optimization of serum electrolytes; correction of fever, anemia, and acidosis; reduction of inotropic agents; and preload augmentation.

JET diagnosis and management.

In patients with stable blood pressure, we initiated medical treatment with dexmedetomidine at a dose of 0.5 mcg/kg per hour as the first therapeutic approach.

We administered ivabradine exclusively to patients who had stable hemodynamics but with a heart rate exceeding 160–170 beats per minute. The ivabradine dosage was 0.1 mg/kg per dose, administered twice daily. In cases where JET had developed recently and there was a clinical indication that hemodynamics might deteriorate, both amiodarone and ivabradine were initiated simultaneously in patients with a heart rate exceeding 180 beats per minute, who also required inotropic support. The standard amiodarone dosage was 10 mg per kg of body weight daily, typically without the need for a loading dose. If there was no discernible clinical response, a second dose of ivabradine was administered 6 h later.

In exceptionally refractory cases, when the cardiac output remained satisfactory, we escalated the amiodarone dosage to 20 mg/kg/day and introduced flecainide 100 mg/m2/day divided into 2 doses by oral use as a treatment option of final resort (Figure 3). We continue 24 h to antiarrhythmic therapy after patients have converted to sinus rhythm.

Management of medical therapy.

Follow‐up

The duration from the initiation of treatment to the restoration of sinus rhythm and the time required to achieve rate control were documented. Based on a study by Arvind et al., 13 age‐appropriate levels of ventricular rate control were defined as follows: 140 beats per minute for infants under 3 months of age, 120 beats per minute for children aged 3 months to 2 years, and 100 beats per minute for children older than 2 years. Until discharge from the hospital, all children were followed up on for recurrence of arrhythmia, and a Holter examination was performed in refractory cases.

Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical variables were expressed as the median (interquartile range [IQR]) and n (%), respectively. The data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software, version 17.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL).

RESULTS

The JET rhythm in the other three patients started on postoperative days 2, 3, and 7.

The median heart rate during JET was 180 beats per minute (ranging from 160 to 210 beats per minute). Ten out of the 26 study participants (38%) exhibited impaired hemodynamics, five (19%) required peritoneal dialysis, and four (15%) presented with restrictive physiology. For their medical treatment, we used the following regimens: ivabradine alone in 14 (54%) cases; ivabradine and amiodarone in 10 (38%) cases; ivabradine and flecainide concurrently in 2 (8%) cases (Table 2).

When assessing the time to ventricular rate control following medical treatment in patients with JET, we observed a median duration of 12 h, with a range of 5–22 h. The median time required for a return to sinus rhythm in these JET cases was 55.5 h (ranging from 6 to 168 h). The median number of ivabradine doses administered was 4, with a range of 1 to 14 doses. The median ventilation time was 75.5 h (12–336 h), and the median duration of intensive care hospitalization was 11.3 days (3–90 h).

None of the 26 study participants experienced a JET relapse after treatment cessation. In addition, no side effects related to ivabradine use were observed in any of the patients, and the vast majority returned to sinus rhythm—except for one patient who presented with JET and a complete AV block.

DISCUSSION

We aim to contribute to the existing literature by sharing our experience with the use of ivabradine—a medication that has gained popularity in JET treatment—across two high‐volume centers. Our study is considerable in the literature, highlighting a substantial number of cases and providing valuable insights into the efficacy and outcomes associated with ivabradine use in treating postoperative JET.

Postoperative JET typically manifests within a median of 4 h after surgery but can present as late as 48 h postoperatively. 16 In our study sample, we observed that the onset of JET was consistent with the literature in 65% of patients on postoperative day 0, and 23% of patients on postoperative day 1. Although JET has been reported to occur after various cardiac surgeries, including the Blalock‐Taussig (BT) shunt procedure, 17 it predominantly occurs after cardiac surgeries involving the crux of the heart. Consistent with these findings, we observed in our study that JET occurs predominantly after AV canal repair, tetralogy of Fallot correction, and ventricular septal defect correction procedures.

Our primary goal with the treatment administered in this research was to control the heart rate and subsequently achieve sinus rhythm using antiarrhythmic medication. Although amiodarone remains the most commonly used drug in the medical treatment of JET, concerns have arisen due to its extensive side effect profile. Cases of JET can lead to multi‐organ dysfunction due to decreased cardiac output, often manifesting as impaired liver function characterized by elevated liver enzymes. Consequently, the administration of amiodarone may be limited in such patients. In addition, patients presenting with low cardiac output at the onset of JET have been observed to have a higher likelihood of being unresponsive to amiodarone treatment. These circumstances underscore the ongoing search for new medical treatments for JET.

In addition to being a rate‐lowering drug that acts by inhibiting the cardiac pacemaker I(f) current underpinning the native pacemaker function of the sinoatrial node, ivabradine directly targets the AV node and the Purkinje system. Considering these factors, we hypothesized that ivabradine might play a role in the treatment of JET.

Initial studies on ivabradine use in JET treatment were conducted primarily on patients with congenital JET. Notably, as in the studies by Saleh et al. and Dieks et al., as well as a previous study from our clinic by Ergul et al., ivabradine use has been shown to be successful in congenital JET cases. 10 , 11 , 18

Subsequently, ivabradine has been found to be efficacious in controlling heart rate and improving left ventricular ejection fraction, as demonstrated in a study by Bonnet et al. 19 Their study involved ivabradine use in children aged 6 months to 18 years who had dilated cardiomyopathy and symptomatic chronic heart failure. Consequently, ivabradine gained approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2019.

A 2021 study by Arvind et al. in the Journal of the American College of Cardiology compared ivabradine and amiodarone in treating children with postoperative JET. 13 Unlike previous studies where ivabradine was used as adjunctive therapy, this study demonstrated its efficacy as monotherapy. While amiodarone was more effective in controlling ventricular rate due to its intravenous administration, there was no significant difference between the two drugs in the time to sinus rhythm. Although ivabradine had a higher recurrence rate, this was managed with additional doses, whereas amiodarone's longer action duration likely reduced recurrence. 13

Our study sample has a lower median age and weight—although the maximum heart rate is similar—than the study sample in the study by Balaji Arvind et al. 13 In our study, the median time for ventricular rate control was 12 h, while the median time to achieve ventricular rate control was 8 h in the Arvind et al. study. The median time to sinus rhythm was 55.5 h in our study, vis‐à‐vis 22 h in the Arvind et al. study. The median dose of ivabradine administered until sinus rhythm was achieved was 4 in our study, in contrast to the median dose of 2 in the Arvind et al. study. After achieving control over the patients' heart rate, we observed overall improvement in their hemodynamics; therefore, since the rhythm was sinus, we did not intervene additionally and continued with the ongoing treatments. In addition, the median ICU stay was 11.3 days (271 h) in our study, vis‐à‐vis 93.2 h for the ivabradine group in the Arvind et al. study. 13

In conclusion, our study findings indicate that ivabradine can be utilized as a monotherapy in treating children diagnosed with JET who have intact hemodynamics and an elevated heart rate following cardiac surgery. Furthermore, it can serve as an adjunct treatment for patients with impaired hemodynamics or those prone to hemodynamic deterioration.

In our study, the dosage was extrapolated from earlier reports. Dosing within a range of 0.1 to 0.2 mg/kg/day, eventual reversion to sinus rhythm was achieved in all patients without any adverse effects. However, it is noteworthy that Bonnet et al. employed higher doses, reaching up to 0.60 mg/kg/day, in children with cardiomyopathy. 19 The most significant advantage of ivabradine is its lack of negative inotropy or alteration of action potential, despite an increase in diastolic duration. 20

To date, the reported side effect profile of ivabradine is as follows: visual disturbances, including diplopia and phosphenes 21 ; bradycardia in the acute phase; and prolongation of the QT interval in adults, particularly when administered concurrently with other drugs known to extend the QT interval. No adverse effects attributable to ivabradine were observed in our study. Examples of anticipated adverse side effects include symptomatic bradycardia, hypotension, and derangement of renal or hepatic function. Bradycardia is easily managed using atrial pacing with temporary epicardial pacing wires.

Ivabradine is administered orally, rendering it impractical for use in children who are not being fed or are restricted from oral consumption. In addition, oral medications generally exhibit a delayed onset of action. These factors limit the utility of ivabradine in infants and children with established JET and hemodynamic instability, where early rhythm control is crucial. Furthermore, the parenteral formulation of ivabradine has not received FDA approval and is unavailable in most countries, including ours. In addition, because ivabradine has been approved by the FDA for use in children aged >6 months, its use remains off‐label in the treatment of heart failure, sinus tachycardia, and JET cases in infants younger than 6 months.

It is commonly believed that oral ivabradine medication may not be as effective as intravenous (IV) administration of amiodarone because orally administered ivabradine has an absolute bioavailability of 40%, with peak concentration achieved at 1 h and a half‐life of 2 h. However, widespread adoption of the intravenous (IV) form of ivabradine is supported by recent studies, and ventricular rate control can be achieved more quickly with this form of the drug. Although not investigated in our study, the use of the IV form of the drug may potentially reduce the likelihood of recurrence in postoperative JET.

Initiating ivabradine therapy early, as soon as a junctional rhythm is identified, enables rhythm control before the onset of hemodynamic instability.

Our initial experience with oral ivabradine in postoperative JET has shown ivabradine to be safe and effective for treating postoperative JET.

STUDY LIMITATIONS

Data on the use of ivabradine in the pediatric population are limited, and larger randomized controlled trials are warranted before a change in the current protocols for the treatment of JET can be implemented. With the broader availability of intravenous ivabradine, a randomized trial can be conducted to compare its efficacy with that of other drugs as a first‐line therapy in managing postoperative JET. Furthermore, our study is a nonrandomized observational study. The absence of a control group in the study is one of the limitations of the study. Comparative studies on amiodarone or oral/IV ivabradine will provide additional insights into the existing literature. The lack of a control group to determine what would happen if ivabradine were not used in patients with junctional ectopic tachycardia, which might be treated by amiodarone or flecainide only, makes it difficult to clarify the actual benefit of ivabradine in this situation. This is considered to be a limitation.

CONCLUSION

Early initiation of ivabradine as a primary treatment—as soon as a junctional rhythm is detected—facilitates rhythm control before the onset of hemodynamic instability. The use of ivabradine serves as an adjunctive therapy to amiodarone, aimed at reducing heart rate, especially in cases where hemodynamics is compromised or prone to deterioration. This approach improves hemodynamics until the native sinus rhythm is reestablished. Furthermore, ivabradine boasts a low side effect profile, does not interfere with cardiac contractility, and lacks negative inotropy. These characteristics make it especially feasible to initiate treatment early.
