Best microbiological practice recommendations for the diagnosis of peri-prosthetic joint infections: the CRIOGO bacteriology reference center network
Authors: Pascale Bémer, Chloé Plouzeau-Jayle, Carole Lemarié, Didier Tande, Anne Gougeon-Jolivet, Rachel Chenouard, Claudie Lamoureux, Sophie Reissier, Louise Ruffier d’Epenoux, Laura Chaufour, Marie-Frédérique Lartigue, Stéphane Corvec
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiagnosis of prosthetic Joint Infections (PJI) remains challenging at the microbiological level. Despite the implementation and adoption of new methods, an heterogeniety of microbiological practices is still observed. These CRIOGO recommendations (Centre de Référence des Infections Ostéo-articulaires du Grand Ouest) aim to set out some basic principles for the diagnosis of PJI in microbiology laboratories. The CRIOGO microbiologists’ team who have been working together for 15 years were invited to provide regional recommendations for microbiology diagnosis based on their practical expertise. These recommendations were developed through a systematic review of the literature and discussion amongst experts. The proposed CRIOGO recommendations were voted on upon by a 10 local panelists via the Delphi process. Total consensus was reached on all 27 recommendations that received 100% agreement. The CRIOGO microbiologists’ team proposed practical guidelines in response to the most frequently asked questions received at our laboratory reference centres. A short, comprehensive answer supported by literature backs up the argument. Out of the 27 detailed CRIOGO recommendations, 22 achieved 100% consensus. This work was initiated to harmonise our daily practices for managing PJI diagnosis within the CRIOGO network in line with the literature.

Introduction

Despite the introduction of innovative techniques in microbiology laboratories, diagnosing prosthetic joint infections (PJIs) remains challenging [1]. In 2008, when the French expert reference centres were created, microbiologists at the six CRIOGO university teaching hospitals (see Fig. 1) decided to standardise practices by harmonising microbiological methods. The first prospective multicentre cross-sectional study, MICROBIOS, addressed the following: (i) evaluation of 16S rRNA gene PCR performance [2, 3], (ii) optimisation of microbiological diagnosis [3], and (iii) the role of histology in the diagnosis of PJI [4]. We then assessed the implementation of a standardised multiplex PCR in our network [5]. Finally, optimising microbiological diagnosis led us to work on early antibiotic adaptation in order to limit the impact of broad-spectrum postoperative antibiotic therapy [6].

Regional map of the CRIOGO network

After these multicenter evaluation, our team decided to harmonize the methods of PJI diagnosis among the six CRIOGO centers.

We were regularly asked by orthopaedic surgeons, infectious diseases specialists and microbiologists to answer a large number of diagnostic questions and provide practical answers for all practitioners involved in the management of PJI. In addition to the French, European and international guidelines, which remain the benchmarks, there is a continuing need to establish basic principles and detailed recommendations on how to make the best possible diagnosis of PJI in light of the literature [7, 8]. The aim of this work was to develop the CRIOGO microbiology recommendations for PJI diagnosis.

Methods

The initiative to standardise our microbiological practice was launched at the CRIOGO expert meeting held in Rennes in January 2019. All participants agreed to be contacted by e-mail to discuss and establish recommendations and answer frequently asked questions.

Given the huge publications in this topic, the Delphi method was chosen as the optimal approach to limit bias from different expert opinions during the recommendation formulation process. A process similar to that previously reported was used [9]. The microbiology team defined 27 questions to be answered.

A systematic literature review related to each question was performed using key words. Searches were limited to recent articles published in English language.

The expert team of microbiologists (12 microbiologists from the six CRIOGO centers) wrote the responses according to the litterature analysis. Subsequently, panelists (10 different microbiology experts from other hospitals) were invited by e-mail to submit requests and give their feedbacks on quotation (0 no agree at all and 10 totally agree) on all the 27 questions according to their personal microbiological experiences in the diagnosis of PJI. The following quotation was selected: Proposal accepted if median of the distribution of quotations ≥ 7, proposal uncertain if median > 3.5 and < 7, inappropriate proposal ≤ 3.5.

This method allowed the committee to check and question the experts in case of difficulties in understanding the questions. Indeed, each question proposed can be commented on by panelists using accurate and available publications. Finallly, the specific points raised by the panelists for each question were discussed. Expert opinions, scientific publications and open discussions were organized in case of discrepancies. Finally, the completed questions with their corresponding answers and quotation were discussed by the expert group in order to finalise the recommandations.

Results

The 27 questions, accompanied by expert commentary, yielded 27 corresponding answers with Delphi statements ranging from 0 to 10. When put to a vote among the 10 regional panellists, all statements achieved more than 80% consensus in the first Delphi round, thus concluding the voting process. Twenty-two questions had a 100% consensus.

Q1. In the event of suspected prosthesis infection, joint puncture is recommended

Median cotation (MC): 10

In the case of intra-articular fluid collection or abscess in contact with an osteoarticular prosthesis, it is recommended to perform a puncture [10–12].

Puncture allows the establishment of a diagnostic score combining major and minor criteria [13].

Q2. In the event of proven prosthetic joint infection, joint puncture should not delay medical and surgical management

MC: 10

According to French Health Authority, infection is evident in the form of abscess, fistula or purulent discharge within a month of prosthesis implantation and requires urgent medical and surgical treatment (severe sepsis, see recommendation 2, HAS 2014) [13]. Karczewski et al. compared the occurrence of relapses at two years between patients who had preoperative bacteriological documentation and those who did not. There was no significant difference between both groups. However, the reinfection rate (recurrence) at two years was lower in the group with bacterial documentation before revision surgery [14].

Q3. Preoperative puncture must be performed under strict conditions of surgical asepsis (controlled environment in the operating theatre or interventional radiology room)

MC: 10

Preoperative sampling must be performed under strict conditions of surgical asepsis (controlled environment in the operating theatre or interventional radiology room) as an increase in airborne bacterial concentration in an open surgical setting may influence the occurrence of surgical site infections [15–17].

Q4. A single puncture should be divided between a single anaerobic blood culture bottle and a sterile Jar

MC: 7.5

Culture using a blood culture bottle detected more pathogens than agar culture and fewer contaminants [18].

Regarding the growth of Cutibacterium acnes, thioglycolate-based liquid media and Schaedler agar outperform blood culture bottles and chocolate agar [19]. We therefore recommend that the joint fluid should be divided as much as possible between a sterile jar intended for inoculation with one mL on conventional media and with 5 to 10 mL in an anaerobic blood culture bottle.

Q5. Joint fluid cytology is useful for diagnosing prosthesis infection

MC: 8

Considering French recommendations, a leukocyte count >1,700/mm3 with >65% neutrophils (PMN) is generally highly suggestive of infection [20]. According to European recommendations supported by ESCMID and ICM, the cut-off for leukocytes is set at >3,000/mm3 and for PMN at >70% [4, 21].

Other thresholds proposed for acute infections are: leukocytes >10,000/mm3 with PMN >90% [22]. In case of dry punctures, the injection of saline into the joint disrupts the leukocyte count, and is therefore not recommended [21].

Q6. In addition to joint fluid puncture, percutaneous (or tru-cut) biopsy(ies) are recommended

MC: 10

According to the French Health Authority, preoperative trucut tissue biopsy(ies) (capsule, synovium) as a complement to joint puncture increases the yield of specimens [13]. A recent study highlighted the performance of biopsy(ies) before surgery revision in late PJI [23]. Nevertheless, given the possibility of serious side effects, It would be reserved for non-contributory or dry joint fluid aspiration cases [13, 21, 24].

Q7. Preoperative puncture may be combined with sampling for histological purposes

MC: 8

It is difficult to recommend this type of analysis, as there are very few studies and/or recommendations regarding histological analysis associated with preoperative puncture [25].

Q8. Subcutaneous swabs and samples should be not used to diagnose prosthesis infection

MC: 10

Superficial swabs from wounds or fistulas should not be avoided, as they are most likely to be contaminated with skin flora [26]. The concordance between fistula and intraoperative tissue cultures revealed no difference according to prosthesis location, acute/chronic infection, nor bacteria [27]. Similarly, intraoperative swab cultures are less effective than tissue cultures taken from the same sites [28].

Q9. Intraoperative specimen cultures are not affected by antibiotic prophylaxis

MC: 10

Pre-incision prophylaxis is considered the major advance in reducing prosthesis infections without significantly affecting intraoperative culture results [29]. The administration of antibiotic prophylaxis is routinely recommended, even in the absence of prior microbiological documentation [30–33].

Q10. It is recommended that 4 samples be collected intraoperatively for microbiological purposes

MC: 10

Developments in grinding techniques allowing the release of bacteria present in the biofilm and culture media have allowed the number of samples to be reduced, particularly in the diagnosis of PJI [20, 26]. According to Kheir et al., the minimum number of samples to obtain two positive cultures is four, and the most positive cultures are obtained with five samples [34]. The number of samples could be reduced from five to four thanks to the use of automated grinding and blood culture bottles, revealed by a percentage of agreement for the diagnosis of 99.7% with four samples (higher senstivity and specificity) [3]. Finally, the recent Mayo Clinic study shows that taking five or more samples does not increase diagnostic accuracy and suggests that either three periprosthetic samples inoculated into a blood culture bottle, or four samples cultured on standard agar plates and liquid culture media should be taken [35].

Q11. Preference should be given to samples in contact with the material, and to joint fluid

MC: 10

Numerous clinical studies, literature reviews and consensus conferences recommend preoperative or intraoperative periprosthetic sampling [20]. Bjerkan et al. recommend interface membrane biopsies rather than joint capsule or synovial fluid samples [36].

In the CRIOGO study, cultures from samples in contact with material and joint fluids were more likely to be positive than bone samples [3]. Samples may also include synovial, subfascial and capsular biopsies, depending on the surgeon’s intraoperative findings suggesting sepsis [37].

Q12. In the event of chronic prosthesis infection, it is recommended that a specimen be taken for histological purposes

MC: 10

Histological analysis is recommended [20], and is included as a minor criterion in a new infection score developed by the MSIS and the ICM [8, 22].

The most commonly polymorphonuclear neutrophil (PMN) score used is 5 to 10 PMN per field in 5 or more fields [11]. A new threshold of 23 PMN in a maximum of 10 fields achieved the same diagnostic accuracy in a shorter time [38]. The prospective multicentre CRIOGO study validated this 23 PMN threshold [4]. More recently, immunohistochemistry and histochemistry techniques allowed to lower the threshold for PMN detection [39].

Q13. Sampling for pathological purposes must be carried out on the periprosthetic interface membrane

MC: 10

The type of the tissue sampled has evolved over time. In 2009, the SPILF recommended the analysis of bone or synovial tissue [20]. Subsequent studies have focused on the peri-prosthetic interface membrane between the prosthesis and adjacent bone, or the joint pseudocapsule [38].

Q14. Automated grinding of samples is strongly recommended

MC: 10

Solid samples (bone fragments, tissue) should be crushed allowing the release of the bacteria present within the biofilm. Mortar and pestle should be avoided [26]. Automated grinding is strongly recommended [2, 3].

Q15. Sonication of material is not recommended

MC: 7.5

The interest of sonication was highlighted on prosthetic material [40]. Nevertheless, this technique has never been compared to optimized culture with grinding and use of blood culture bottle [3]. Culture of intraoperative tissue samples has been shown to be more sensitive and possibly more specific than sonication for the diagnosis of infections on orthopedic device [41]. In some cases, tissue culture in blood culture bottles achieved similar sensitivity to fluid culture after sonication of the prosthesis [42]. On the other hand, in some cases (antibiotics, delicate or slow growing microorganisms) the two tests combined would give the highest sensitivity without compromising specificity [41]. Indeed, it may be of interest to combine with multiplex PCR in complex cases [43].

Ultimately, sonication does not appear imperative if culture is well conducted and optimized.

Q16. Direct examination for bacteria by Gram stain is not useful

MC: 7

The sensitivity of direct bacteriological examination is low (6%), while the specificity is close to 100% [20, 26, 44]. Gram staining cannot be routinely recommended for tissue samples.

Q17. To culture samples, the minimum media to use are: chocolate agar, anaerobic medium (liquid or solid), and an anaerobic blood culture bottle

MC: 10

Given the bacterial epidemiology of osteoarticular infections, at the very least: a chocolate agar incubated under CO2, a medium for anaerobic bacteria, whether liquid or solid, and a blood culture bottle, preferably anaerobic [19], should be inoculated [3].

Q18. Inoculation of intraoperative samples into a blood culture bottle improves microbiological diagnosis

MC: 10

Inoculation of a blood culture bottle improves microbiological diagnosis [45, 46]. The percentage of positive samples with blood culture bottles for aerobic bacteria was 83% compared to approximately 70% with agar media [3]. The best sensitivity was obtained with the combination of blood culture bottle, chocolate agar and liquid or solid medium for anaerobes [3]. Anaerobic bottles should be resin-free for optimal growth of Cutibacterium acnes: Lytic bottles on BACTEC (Becton Dickinson) and SN on BacT/ALERT (bioMérieux) [19].

Q19. The incubation period for cultures on solid medium is 7 days

MC: 10

In the prospective study carried out by the CRIOGO network, 96.7% and 98.9% of microbiological documentation diagnoses were obtained after 5 and 7 days respectively [6]. Therefore, the CRIOGO recommends storing solid media for 7 days.

Q20. The incubation period for cultures on liquid medium is 10 days

MC: 10

Incubation of media, including blood culture bottles, has been recommended for up to 14 days [26].

According to the prospective CRIOGO study, it is possible to reduce the incubation period from 14 to 10 days [3]. In fact, only 1.1% of infections were diagnosed from a blood culture bottle after the 7-day incubation period, and 1 case after 10 days [6]. It is therefore likely that the reommended incubation period will soon be reduced, in line with the improved efficiency of blood culture bottles [21]. For C. acnes, a delay of 10 days has been considered sufficient to make the diagnosis of infection [47]. The risk of detecting contaminants increases between 10 and 14 days [48].

Q21. MALDI-TOF identification and antibiotic susceptibility testing should be performed on the different colony morphotypes

MC: 10

Mass spectrometry identification and antibiotic susceptibility testing must be performed on all the aspects of isolated colonies. Identification of Staphylococcus aureus small colony variants (SCV) will be particularly important, as they are often more resistant to antibiotics [12, 49].

Q22. A single antibiotic susceptibility testing is performed for strict pathogenic microorganisms

MC: 10

A single antibiotic susceptibility testing is performed for bacteria that do not belong to the skin flora (Staphylococcus aureus, Enterobacteriaceae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa), or for strict pathogens (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Salmonella spp., Listeria monocytogenes, Campylobacter spp., Pasteurella spp.), or when colonies have an identical morphotype [10, 20].

Q23. At least two antibiotic susceptibility testings are carried out for skin flora bacteria

MC: 10

Identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing must be performed on all aspects of isolated colonies for the same bacterial species. A least, two antibiograms must be performed on two different samples, as it is common to observe multiple resistance phenotypes for the same species of bacteria from skin flora [20, 26].

Q24. Molecular biology should be used in cases of strong suspicion of prosthesis infections when culture results are negative

MC: 10

The performance of molecular biology techniques generally shows good specificity and a gain in sensitivity over culture mainly in treated patients [2, 11, 50, 51]. Automated rapid PCR can be performed at the time of sampling for methicillin resistance genes in staphylococci to guide probabilistic treatment [13].

The use of molecular biology methods should be considered when there is a strong clinical suspicion and previous antibiotic therapy, or to search for microorganisms that do not grow in conventional culture (Mycoplasma Spp., Trophyrema whippleii, Coxiella burnetii…).

Q25. Molecular techniques include specific PCRs for specific microorganisms (S. aureus, etc.) and 16S rRNA gene PCR-sequencing

MC: 10

The preferred molecular techniques for diagnosing culture-negative infections are specific PCRs that look for specific microorganisms (S. aureus, coagulase-negative staphylococci, C. acnes, C. burnetii, T. whipplei, Mycoplasma Spp.) [50].

16S rRNA gene PCR is a broad spectrum PCR with limited sensitivity, useful in treated patients [2]. Because of its low sensitivity and the risk of contamination inherent in the technique, 16S rRNA gene PCR results must be interpreted in the light of other clinical and microbiological aspects [52].

Multiplexed PCRs for the identification and detection of resistance genes have variable and inadequate sensitivities and are expensive [5]. The proposed panels are sometimes incomplete (no C. acnes or Corynebacteria).

Some teams are beginning to investigate metagenomic approaches using next-generation sequencing (NGS) for PJI [53, 54]. To date, these techniques are rarely used in routine, due to their technical and analytical complexity.

Q26. Samples should be kept for up to three months after the culture results

MC: 10

A part of the sample should be kept frozen (at -80 °C or, failing that, at -20 °C) ideally two to four months after the result has been obtained. This allows time for further investigations (mycobacterial, fungal, molecular biological techniques).

Q27. Bacterial strains should be kept for up to three years after results are returned

MC: 10

Storing prosthesis infection strains for three years allows additional antimicrobial susceptibility testings with new molecules, as well as typing methods for different isolates in the event of recurrence. Strains should be frozen rather than stored in agar tubes.

Discussion

The microbiological diagnosis of PJI has evolved over the last few decades, with innovative methods such as fast-track MALDI-TOF identification, blood culture bottle use, rapid antimicrobial susceptibility testing and specific or multiplex PCR. Whole genome sequencing is also expected to become a common practice in the near future. However, microbiologists at local and national levels have observed considerable variation in diagnostic strategies. There is therefore a need for standardisation in order to compare results and create databases that can be used for epidemiological analysis, methodological comparison, and clinical and microbiological research projects.

The aim of this work was to provide rapid answers to critical questions based on expert opinion and rigorous evaluation of the current literature. Many critical aspects of the diagnosis were therefore discussed (Table 1), including the role of synovial puncture and cytology, the impact of per-op biopsy, the interest of histological analysis, the number of samples required during a revision procedure, the best samples to use, bead meal processing, the advantages of sonication, the best media to use and how many, the incubation time, the usefulness of blood culture bottle and when to perform molecular tests [20, 26].

Main recommendations for the microbiological diagnosis of prosthetic joint infections

These 27 recommendations from the CRIOGO network address all these questions. Expert opinions and medical literature were discussed to reach the best possible consensus. Panelists carried out a voting exercise using a DELPHI process, based on their practices, knowledge and experience.

A strong consensus of 82% was reached. Agreement was also reached on the remaining five questions. While these recommendations do not cover all aspects of PJI diagnosis, they will help colleagues harmonise current microbiology procedures with those of reference centres.

The main limitation of these recommendations is the small number of experts (n = 12) and panelists (n = 10) involved in the regional work (risk of selection bias). Furthermore, while valuable, regional variations in pathogens or resources must be considered. However, the medical evidence for several issues has been presented in accordance with the literature. A more robust consensus involving more experts will be referred to national-level guidelines, which are due to be developed in France in 2026 and will include some experts from this group belonging to the CRIOGO network.

Conclusion

The CRIOGO microbiologists provide a concise argument and comprehensive recommendations for diagnosing PJI. These recommendations are not intended to replace international guidelines, but rather to reflect the daily practices of six reference microbiology laboratories. They will standardise what is recommended for an accurate PJI diagnosis.
