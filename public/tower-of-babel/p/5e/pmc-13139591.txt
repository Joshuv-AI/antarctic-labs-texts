Synthesis, characterization, and biological activities of two new pyrazole Schiff base derivatives
Authors: Suzan Matar, Abdel-Aziz Abu-Yamin, Deeb Taher, Ibrahim A. M. Saraerah, Mahmoud Salman, Sultan A. Saghir, Fatima Ahmad, Sajeda Matar, Lina Buqa’en, Ali Elrashidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of new heterocyclic scaffolds remains a priority in medicinal chemistry due to the rising challenges of microbial resistance and cancer. In this study, we explored the synergistic potential of combing thiophene-substituted pyrazoles with phenolic Schiff bases. We synthesized two novel derivatives, 3a and 3b, via the condensation of 5-Amino-3-(2-thienyl) pyrazole with salicylaldehyde and 5- bromo-2-hydroxybenzaldehyde. These structures were confirmed using FTIR, 1H and 13C -NMR, UV- visible, mass spectroscopy, and elemental analyses. While both compounds were evaluated for their biological activities, compound 3b—the brominated derivative emerged as a multifaceted bioactive lead. Quantitative microdilution assays showed that 3b exhibited broad spectrum antibacterial activity, with MIC of 0.4 mg/mL against S. aureus and 1.2–2.0 mg/mL against gram negative strains of E. coli, P. aeruginosa and K. pneumoniae. where 3a remained inactive. Furthermore, it showed potent antifungal efficacy against clinical isolates of Candida species with MIC of 0.75 mg/mL, a significant improvement over the 2.0 mg/mL observed for 3a. Cytotoxicity assay indicated that 3b effectively inhibited the growth of MCF7, PC3 and MDA231 cancer lines. While both compounds were confirmed non-mutagenic via the Ames test. 3b revealed a tendency for red blood cell (RBC) lysis at concentrations ≥ 0.25 mg/mL. The integration of a bromide substituent in 3b significantly enhances bioactivity compared to 3a, suggesting that halogenation is key to bioactivity. Our findings suggest that compound 3b is a potential candidate base for tackling both drug-resistance infections and human malignancies.

Introduction

It is notable that treatment of infectious diseases is an ever-increasing problem due to the advent of drug resistance in them, known as multi-drug resistant (MDR) pathogens which create significant challenges for healthcare systems worldwide1,2.

Consequently, there is an imperative need to develop stronger and novel antimicrobial compounds that can overcome the limitations of current antibiotics3.

Cancer remains a major global health threat because proper treatment options are often limited or ineffective4,5. This challenge is aggravated by the fact that cancer patients often become immunocompromised due to their illness or chemotherapy treatment. Cancer patients are susceptible to secondary life-threatening hospital acquired infections that lead to serious negative outcomes, including prolonged hospital stay, increased disability, and significantly higher healthcare costs6. Urgently, the situation requires the discovery and development of novel compounds that can tackle a broad spectrum simultaneously agents effective against MDR infections and cancer. Identifying a single compound with a multifaceted action is essential for improving patient outcomes and reducing the economic strain on healthcare systems worldwide7. New therapeutic drugs that contain a heterocyclic core are at the forefront of pharmaceutical research8,9. Schiff bases have emerged as a prominent class of bioactive compounds in medicinal and coordination chemistry due to their synthetic accessibility, structural adaptability, and wide-ranging biological relevance. They are typically synthesized via the straightforward condensation of primary amines with aldehydes or ketones, generating the characteristic azomethine (–C = N–) linkage, which serves as a key pharmacophoric and coordination site10,11. This facile synthetic strategy enables systematic modulation of steric and electronic features through rational substituent design, allowing precise structure–activity relationship investigations. Structurally, Schiff bases are of particular importance as versatile ligands, where the imine nitrogen, often in combination with additional donor atoms, promotes strong chelation and stabilizes diverse coordination geometries, making them valuable scaffolds in metal-based drug design12. Biologically, Schiff bases and their metal complexes exhibit a broad spectrum of pharmacological activities, including antibacterial, antifungal, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant effects, which are closely linked to the presence of the azomethine group, substituent electronic effects, and their ability to engage in hydrogen bonding, π–π interactions, and metal chelation with biological targets13. These combined synthetic, structural, and biological features establish Schiff bases as multifunctional molecular platforms with significant potential for the development of new therapeutic agents, particularly in the context of antimicrobial drug discovery14. Pyrazoles (1,2-azoles) are a family of five-membered heterocycles that are particularly valuable in chemical synthesis. They have proven to be a remarkable scaffold for the synthesis of biologically active compounds. Many synthetic methods and analogues have been developed over the years. Pyrazole nuclei are found in various structures and are used in a variety of fields, including agriculture, medicine, and technology15–17. In addition to being one of the most important heterocyclic compounds, pyrazoles have wide range of medical applications that focus mainly on antibacterial18, antifungal19, antispasmodic20, anti-inflammatory21, analgesic22, antihyperglycemic23, hypoglycemic24, antineoplastic25, antidepressant effects26 and are widely utilized in biopharmaceuticals and insecticides such as Celebrex27. Consequently, in recent years, pyrazole-based compounds have become a target of great interest.

This study reports the synthesis and characterization of two new Schiff base ligands, (E)-2-(((5-(thiophen-2-yl)-1H-pyrazol-3-yl)imino)methyl)phenol (3a) and its bromo-substituted analogue (3b), followed by comprehensive biological evaluation. The compounds were screened for antibacterial and antifungal activities, anticancer (cytotoxic) effects against human tumor cell lines, and preliminary safety through mutagenicity and hemolysis assays. The results provide a foundation for the rational design of new derivatives with enhanced biological activity and potential for future drug development. The insight gained from this study are crucial, providing our laboratory with the basis to design and develop new derivatives of the core bioactive structure identified in this project. The ultimate goal of this research is to develop new compounds with enhanced and unique biological activity, advancing progress toward clinical drug development.

Materials and methods

Materials and general considerations

5-Amino-3-(2-thienyl)pyrazole (1), Salicylaldehyde (2a), 5-bromo-2-hydroxybenzaldehyde (2b) and solvents were purchased from commercial sources and used as received. All syntheses were carried out under aerobic conditions. All chemicals, including solvents, 5-Amino-3-(2-thienyl) pyrazole (1), Salicylaldehyde (2a), 5-bromo-2-hydroxybenzaldehyde (2b), were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used without additional purification.

Instruments

The following methods were used to collect spectral data: Infrared spectra were obtained using a Thermo Nicolet NEXUS 670 FT-IR spectrometer. NMR spectra were recorded with a Bruker Avance III 500 FT-NMR spectrometer (1H NMR at 500.303 MHz, 13C{1H} NMR at 125.813 MHz) at room temperature. Chemical shifts (δ) are reported in parts per million (ppm) relative to tetramethylsilane using the solvent as an internal reference (CDCl3): 1H NMR δ = 7.26 ppm; 13C{1H} NMR δ = 77.16 ppm. Coupling constants (J) are reported in Hertz (Hz), and integrations are reported as the number of protons. The melting points were determined using an aelabuk melting point apparatus. Microanalysis was performed by using a Thermo FLASHEA 1112 Series instrument. High resolution mass spectra were recorded with a Brukermicr OTOF QII with an Apollo II ESI source. UV–Vis absorption bands were measured by using UV–Vis double beam spectrophotometer model UV 1900i (SHIMAZDU).

General procedure for the preparation of 3

A solution of 1 (0.660 g, 4.00 mmol) in ethanol (10 mL) was added to solution of the respective aldehyde (4.00 mmol) in 10 mL ethanol. The reaction mixture was stirred at reflux temperature for 3 h. The completion of reaction was monitored by using TLC (n-hexane − ethanol 3:7). A rotary evaporator was used to remove the solvent, and the resulting yellow-green and pale-yellow precipitates were recrystallized from absolute ethanol at a low temperature (− 8 °C). The obtained solids were dried under vacuum.

Ames mutagenicity assay

Mutagenic potential of 3a and 3b was evaluated using a modified 96 well Ames test (AMES-MOD ISO, EPBI) with Salmonella typhimurum TA100, histidine auxotroph. Mutagenicity is detected via reversion mutations restoring histidine prototrophy yielding visible colonies in histidine-deficient medium28.

Stocks (17 mg/mL in 20%DMSO) were diluted/filtered (0.22 μm) to final concentration of 50, 75, and 150 μg/plate (10% DMSO). Tests were conducted ± S9 rat liver fraction for metabolic activation with anthramine + S9 served as positive control29.

Overnight TA100 cultures were exposed in duplicates to 200μL exposure medium plus compounds/controls (sodium azide as positive control, 10% DMSO as negative control; blanks for sterility; ± S9). Incubated at 37 °C for 100 min. Post-exposure, 1600μL/well was transferred to reversion tubes (reversion medium with water and master mix, then 200μL aliquoted into 48 wells/plate. Plates were sealed and incubated at 37 °C for 2 days.

After incubation, the plates were visually assessed. The bromocresol purple in the media acts as pH indicator changing colour as reverted (mutated) bacteria grow and metabolize the nutrients. Yellow and partially yellow wells were scored as positive (mutagenic effect), while purple wells were scored as negative (no mutagenic effect). To validate the test, purple coloured was observed in the blank wells (sterility assessment).

The induction factor (IF) was calculated to quantify the mutagenic potency. A compound was considered genotoxic if the IF value was greater than 2. The Induction Factor (IF) was calculated as follows: Induction factor (IF) = number of revertants in test plates / number of spontaneous revertants in negative control28,29.

Ex-vivo haemolysis assay

Haemolytic potential of 3a and 3b was assessed via ex-vivo RBC lysis, quantifying hemoglobulin release at OD421 as a marker of membrane damage30. All experimental protocols were approved by the ethical committee [Medlab IRB-approved (No. ACM-PRIF-006)]. Informed consent was obtained from all the volunteers prior to participation in this study and the methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations30. Blood (6 mL) from 10 consenting donors was collected in K2-EDTA tubes. Samples were centrifuged (500 × g, 5 min), plasma discarded and replaced with 150 mM NaCl, recentrifuged and then pelleted RBCs resuspended in PBS. Pooled pellets were diluted 1:50 in PBS. In V-bottom 96-well plates, 20μL compound stocks and 180μL RBC suspension (triplicates) yielded final concentrations of 1, 0.5, and 0.25 mg/mL. Plates were shaken for 5 min, incubated at 37 oC for 1 h and then centrifuged (500 ×g, 5 min). Supernatants (100μL) were transferred to flat-bottom plates for OD 421 reading (ELISA reader). 20 μL of 20% triton X-100 and PBS were used as positive and negative controls, respectively.

Haemolysis (%) was calculated relative to Triton X-100 maximum as follows: (sample OD -negative control OD) / (positive control OD—negative control OD) X 100.

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation for the indicated number of separate experiments. Three independent experiments were performed for each study. Student’s ‘t’ test was exploited to evaluate the results. A P-value < 0.05 was the established threshold for statistical significance28.

However, the more sensitive Microdultion assay revealed significant activity. Compound 3b demonstarted antimicrobial activity against certain Gram-negative strains including E.coli, P.aeruginosa, and Kebsiella pneumoniae. The observed MIC ranged from 1.5 (± 0.31) to 2 (± 0.3) mg/mL and MBC ranged from 2 (± 0.5) -17 (± 2.0) mg/mL. Further on, compound 3b proved to be an effective antifungal agent against all test fungal strains (Candida spp) with a promising MIC of 0.75 (± 0.15) mg/mL and MFC of 1.5 (± 0.2) mg/mL.

Compound 3a also exhibited moderate anticandidal activity but was less potent than 3b, showing MIC of 2 mg/mL and MFC of 17 mg/mL. Statistical analysis using students t-test confirmed 3b compounds significantly lowered MICs and MBCs/MFCs versus 3a for S. aureus ATCC 25,923 (p < 0.05) and pooled Candida strains (p < 0.01) indicating superior potency.

These findings clearly indicate that our Schiff base pyrazole preparations hold significant promise as anticandidal agents. Specifically, 3b is the preferred candidate showing better efficacy against Candida strains and demonstrating some activity against Gram negative bacteria. The observed differences in antimicrobial activity are likely due to the compound’s varying mode of action and the structural differences of the target microorganisms. This pattern aligns with recent reports on newly designed heterocyclic antimicrobial agents, where systematic structural modifications and computational analyses have been used to optimize potency, spectrum and selectivity51,52.

Some studies suggest that the ability of Schiff base compounds to penetrate the microbial cell membrane is linked to their degree of hydrophobicity49,53. Recent research points to a correlation between the antimicrobial/antibiofilm activity of new agents and their capacity to induce reactive oxygen species (ROS)54-56,59. Other possibilities were explained by the existence of potential mechanism that includes a reduction of bacterial NO. synthase activity in Gram-negative bacilli60 or ROS production as an antifungal agent59. It is well established that the distinct cell wall structures of Gram-positive bacteria, Gram-negative bacteria, and Candida profoundly influence susceptibility61.

While these are plausible speculations, a definitive understanding of the mechanism requires further investigation into the compounds’ antioxidant and biological activities which are beyond the current study’s scope.

In vitro anticancer activity: MTT assay

MTT assay was performed to evaluate the potential cytotoxicity activity (anticancer) of compounds 3a and 3b against various human cancer cell lines. This In vitro cell based assay works by quantifying the metabolic activity of the mitochondrial enzyme succinate dehydrogenase. A reduction in metabolic activity is measured in 96 well plates which serves as indicator of reduced viability or cell death caused by the tested compounds. The primary challenge encountered was the significant difference in the solubility of the two tested compounds in the solvent DMSO. Compound 3a showed a maximum solubility of 3 mg/mL in 1% DMSO while compound 3b demonstrated a much higher solubility of 250 mg/mL in 1% DMSO and these concentrations were stock solutions.

Compound 3a exhibited generally low anticancer activity across the tested cell lines, with inhibition percentage not exceeding 38%. Consequently, an IC50 value could not be determined for 3a. The highest inhibition of 38% was observed on dermal fibroblast (normal) cells at concentration above 0.75 mg/mL. However, a milder effect of 3a was noted on PC3 (prostate cancer) cells with 27% inhibition above 0.375 mg/mL. Low efficacy of 3a was observed with A549 (lung cancer) and MDA (breast cancer). Compound 3a showed very low inhibition at 11% and 7%, respectively both above 0.375 mg/mL. Despite sharing the same synthetic nuclei unit, 3a and 3b exhibited significantly different cytotoxic activities. This difference is proposed to be directly related to the substantial difference in solubility. Compound 3a with its low maximum solubility of 3 mg/mL displayed the least anticancer activity compared to 3b maximum solubility 250 mg/mL. This correlation between solubility and anticancer activity is consistent with findings from previous studies57,58, suggesting that improved solubility for 3b enhances its cytotoxicity potential.

The synthesized Schiff base 3a and 3b share a 5-(thiophen-2-yl)-1H-pyrazole core linked to an O-hydroxybenzylidene imine, a scaffold frequently highlighted in recent reports as privileged motif in anticancer lead design59–61.

The current study showed that pyrazole-based Schiff bases and hydrazones bearing thiophene rings and electron withdrawing substitute showed enhanced cytotoxicity and clear structure–activity relationships, particularly when halogens were introduced on the aromatic ring attached to the imine nitrogen. Within this context the markedly higher activity of 3b relative to 3a can be rationalized by the presence of the 4-bromo group, which is expected to modulate electronic distribution, increase lipophilicity, and improve interaction with biological targets, in line with the halogen-dependent enhancements in anticancer potency reported in these structurally related systems.

Ex vivo human red blood cell haemolysis assay

The haemolysis assay was conducted as a crucial measure of the ex vivo safety of the compounds 3a and 3b. This assay evaluated the rupture of RBCs (erythrocytes) and the subsequent release of hemoglobin, which when free in plasma , poses a risk of damage to vital organs such as the heart, liver and kidney62.

Toxicity assessment via the haemolytic assay provides valuable, complementary information to the cytotoxicity results obtained against both normal and cancer cell lines30,58.

In this evaluation, both 3a and 3b demonstrated a toxic effect (haemolytic activity) in vitro against pooled human red blood cells when compared to positive and negative controls. In this evaluation, both 3a and 3b demonstrated a toxic effect (haemolytic activity) in vitro against pooled human red blood cells when compared to positive and negative controls. As illustrated in Fig. 9, both compounds induced high levels of haemolysis reaching nearly 100% across all tested concentrations (1, 0.5, and 0.25 mg/mL) Statistically, the haemolytic activity of both compounds at all tested concentrations was significantly higher than the negative control (10% DMSO; p < 0,001) and was comparable to the 100% lysis induced by the positive control (20% Triton X-100; p > 0.05). While the percentage of haemolysis appeared roughly equivalent at the highest concentrations, a slight concatenation-dependent trend was noted to 3b with subtle increase in haemolysis at 0.25 mg/mL compared to 1 mg/mL, marked as an asterisk in the Fig. 9. This difference between 3a and 3b at the lowest tested concentration was statistically significant (p < 0.05), suggesting that 3b may possess slightly higher haemolytic potency at lower concentrations.

In summary, while 3b emerges as a potent, non-genotoxic anticancer lead, its high haemolytic profile highlights a significant physiological challenge. These findings suggest that while the compound is highly effective at cellular level, its future clinical application will likely depend on specialized delivery systems, such as nanoparticle encapsulation, to shield erythrocytes while preserving its cytotoxic effect against malignant tissues.

Conclusion

The growing global burden of multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens, particularly in hospital-acquired infections, highlights the urgent need for new antibacterial, antifungal, and anticancer agents that are both effective and safe. In this study, we designed and synthesized two previously unreported pyrazole Schiff base derivatives, (E)-2-(((5-(thiophen-2-yl)imino)methyl)phenol (3a) and its 4-bromo analogue (3b), via a straightforward one-step condensation reaction. Their structures and purity were verified by elemental analysis, FT-IR, 1H-NMR, 13C-NMR, and mass spectrometry, and the introduction of a bromine atom at the para position relative to the hydroxy group in 3b led to distinct spectral and electronic features consistent with its different biological behaviour.

Our biological investigations show that 3b functions as a novel dual-acting lead, displaying broad-spectrum antibacterial and antifungal activity—particularly against Candida spp. and selected Gram-negative bacteria—together with marked cytotoxic effects against cancer cell lines, especially MCF-7, while preserving an acceptable safety margin toward normal fibroblasts. In contrast, 3a exhibited only modest antifungal activity and negligible anticancer effects, emphasizing the crucial contribution of the 4-bromo substituent and the associated physicochemical changes to the enhanced potency and selectivity of 3b. Collectively, these results indicate that 3b is, to our knowledge, the first pyrazole–Schiff base of this type to demonstrate concurrent antimicrobial and anticancer activities at the tested concentrations, establishing it as a promising scaffold at the intersection of infection control and oncology.

On the basis of these findings, a logical direction for future work is the rational design of metal complexes derived from 3b. Coordination with appropriate metal ions is anticipated to further tune redox properties, lipophilicity, and molecular interactions with biological targets, with the potential to strengthen both antimicrobial potency and anticancer cytotoxicity. Such metal–Schiff base constructs may ultimately provide multifunctional agents capable of treating primary malignancies while simultaneously preventing or managing secondary, often drug-resistant infections—an approach of particular relevance in immunocompromised and critically ill patients. This study therefore introduces a new bioactive ligand family and offers a solid, structure-guided foundation for subsequent optimization and preclinical development.
