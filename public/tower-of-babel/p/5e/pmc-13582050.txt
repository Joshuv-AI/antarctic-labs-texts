When nothing else matters: proctectomy for complex perianal Crohn’s disease—indications, timing, techniques, and outcomes
Authors: T. Yamamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Complex perianal fistulizing Crohn’s disease may progress to a functionally end-stage anorectum characterized by refractory proctitis, fixed anorectal stenosis/stricture, cavitating or recurrent pelvic sepsis, and complex branching fistulas, where repeated local salvage offers diminishing returns. This narrative review synthesizes predominantly observational evidence and consensus guidance on indications, timing, operative strategy, and outcomes of definitive proctectomy/abdominoperineal resection (APR). Decision-making should be structured and time-bound: Define anatomy and occult sepsis with pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and examination under anesthesia (EUA), and perform EUA-guided biopsy when malignancy is suspected or surveillance is impractical. When reversibility is uncertain, planned temporary diversion can be used as a 3–6-month trial with explicit targets and stop rules; failure to meet milestones should prompt timely discussion of definitive resection. Operative choice (proctectomy/APR versus proctocolectomy) and dissection plane (close intersphincteric versus wider excision beyond the sphincter complex) should prioritize complete excision of chronically infected tissue and oncologic safety. Because perineal wound morbidity and persistent perineal sinus are common, outcomes depend on preoperative optimization, explicit dead-space/closure planning (primary closure, delayed management, omentoplasty, or flap reconstruction), and a postoperative pathway for early imaging and stepwise salvage. Appropriately timed surgery can provide durable control of sepsis and pain and reduce recurrent urgent interventions.

Introduction

Perianal fistulizing Crohn’s disease (PFCD) is a disabling manifestation of Crohn’s disease that often requires prolonged, coordinated medical, radiologic, and surgical care [1–4]. Management emphasizes early sepsis control, precise delineation of fistula anatomy (pelvic magnetic resonance imaging [MRI] and/or examination under anesthesia [EUA]), and continence‑preserving strategies, aiming to avoid permanent diversion when feasible [1–4]. PFCD is common, resource‑intensive, and can markedly impair quality of life through chronic drainage/odor, abscess flares, and pain [5, 6]. Perianal disease may also present early in the course of Crohn’s disease [7].

Despite advances in multidisciplinary management, a subset of patients develop a refractory course characterized by ongoing pelvic sepsis, progressive anorectal destruction, severe pain, complex fistula networks, and/or fixed anorectal stenosis in the setting of active proctitis [2, 8]. In this setting, management priorities often shift to symptom control and functional recovery, even when this requires permanent diversion [6].

When the anorectum becomes functionally end‑stage, further local procedures may increase scarring, exacerbate stenosis, and compromise continence without delivering durable symptom control [2]. Temporary fecal diversion can improve symptoms for some patients; however, restoration of bowel continuity is achieved in only a minority, and many ultimately require definitive proctectomy with a permanent stoma [9]. Proctectomy/abdominoperineal resection (APR) should therefore be discussed as a definitive option once goals shift toward reliable control of sepsis and pain and a reduction in repeated urgent interventions, with explicit counseling regarding perineal wound morbidity and the potential for delayed healing [1, 10, 11].

In this review, a “functionally end-stage anorectum” refers to irreversible anorectal damage characterized by refractory sepsis and/or proctitis, fixed anorectal stenosis, severe fibrosis, loss of meaningful anorectal function or continence, and inability to achieve acceptable quality of life despite optimized multidisciplinary management.

Methods

This narrative review addresses the indications, timing, technical considerations, and outcomes of proctectomy for complex PFCD. The evidence base is predominantly observational, supported by guideline and consensus statements. PubMed/MEDLINE was searched from inception to January 2026 using terms related to Crohn’s disease, perianal fistula, diversion, proctectomy/abdominoperineal resection, perineal wound morbidity, and persistent perineal sinus. No language restrictions were applied; when available, English-language full texts were prioritized. We prioritized guidelines, systematic reviews, and cohort studies; smaller case series were included when addressing uncommon complications or technical issues. Reference lists of key articles and guidelines were hand-searched. Evidence was synthesized qualitatively.

Definition and prognostic features of complex perianal fistulizing Crohn’s disease

Complex PFCD encompasses high or branching fistulas (often with multiple external openings, secondary extensions, and abscess cavities), rectovaginal involvement, active proctitis, anorectal stenosis/stricture, and/or impaired continence—features that commonly necessitate repeated drainage procedures and multidisciplinary planning [2, 4, 12]. Among these, active rectal disease and fixed stenosis are consistently adverse prognostic factors: They sustain pelvic sepsis, restrict surveillance and operative access, and increase the likelihood of permanent diversion or proctectomy when salvage strategies fail [11–16]. High-quality pelvic MRI (±EUA) remains central to anatomic mapping and decision‑making [3, 11]. Because symptom burden and treatment intensity can outweigh radiologic anatomy in day‑to‑day life, treatment success should be defined with the patient, incorporating function, safety, and the cumulative burden of ongoing care [6, 8, 17, 18]. Alternatives to definitive surgery

Temporary fecal diversion

A diverting stoma can reduce fecal contamination and help control symptoms and pelvic sepsis in therapy‑refractory PFCD, either to stabilize patients while systemic therapy is optimized or as a bridge to definitive resection [1, 2, 8, 11, 12]. Meta-analyses report symptomatic improvement in approximately half of patients; however, durable restoration of bowel continuity is achieved in only a minority, and many ultimately remain diverted and/or proceed to proctectomy/proctocolectomy [9]. Diversion is best approached as a planned, time‑bound trial (often approximately 3–6 months) with explicit targets and stop rules (Table 2; Fig. 1) [1, 12]. Diversion does not eliminate malignancy concern in long-standing fistulas or a severely diseased rectum; if surveillance is not feasible or malignancy cannot be reasonably excluded, definitive resection should not be deferred [19, 20]. Failure of diversion may be defined by persistent or recurrent pelvic sepsis, ongoing severe pain or drainage, repeated abscess-related admissions or examinations under anesthesia, inability to discontinue antibiotics or opioids, progressive anorectal stenosis, or inability to achieve adequate surveillance because of ongoing inflammation or pain. Earlier conversion to definitive surgery should be considered when these features persist despite optimized multidisciplinary management.

Local procedures and adjuncts

Local procedures—loose setons for drainage, advancement flaps, and ligation of intersphincteric fistula tract-based repairs—can be considered once sepsis is controlled and the rectum is sufficiently quiet to make closure attempts meaningful [1, 2, 4, 12]. When refractory proctitis, extensive branching/cavitating sepsis, or fixed anorectal stenosis dominates, repeated local procedures rarely change the trajectory and may add scarring that complicates later definitive surgery and worsens function [11–13, 16]. Combined medical–surgical care remains central: prompt abscess drainage, loose setons for ongoing drainage, MRI/EUA reassessment, and escalation of systemic therapy [1–4, 12]. Antitumor necrosis factor (TNF) agents have the most robust evidence base; in selected patients with controlled rectal inflammation, staged surgical closure after anti-TNF induction (PISA/PISA-II) may reduce re-interventions compared with anti-TNF alone [21, 22]. Darvadstrocel improved combined remission versus placebo in ADMIRE-CD, but subsequent ADMIRE-CD II did not show a statistically significant difference versus placebo; availability is jurisdiction-dependent given the European Union (EU) marketing authorization withdrawal (December 2024) [23–25]. Classification frameworks (e.g., Geldof et al.) may help align disease state with realistic strategies, including diversion or proctectomy [26].

Emerging advanced therapies, including ustekinumab, vedolizumab, risankizumab, guselkumab, and Janus kinase inhibitors/small molecules may provide benefit in selected patients with refractory PFCD, although evidence specifically addressing avoidance of definitive surgery remains limited. These agents may be considered within multidisciplinary salvage strategies, particularly when active luminal disease coexists; however, prolonged therapeutic cycling without objective improvement may delay necessary definitive surgery.

Preoperative evaluation and optimization

Imaging and mapping

Pelvic MRI is central to mapping fistula anatomy, delineating secondary extensions, and identifying occult abscesses in PFCD [3]. EUA complements MRI, particularly when pain or stenosis limits office examination and restricts endoscopic assessment [1–3, 16]. Where feasible, imaging should be paired with endoscopic assessment of rectal disease activity, with biopsies when malignancy is a concern [3, 19, 20]. Extensive fibrosis, large or recurrent abscess cavities, multiple branching tracts, and associated rectal stricturing often signal a difficult-to-reverse trajectory [2, 11, 12, 16]. In patients whose pain or stenosis precludes reliable surveillance, a low threshold for EUA with biopsy and multidisciplinary review is appropriate [3, 19, 20]. The aim is to define the dominant driver and to plan definitive surgery, including the intended perineal closure strategy [1, 10, 11].

Multidisciplinary preparation

Preoperative readiness should include nutritional and anemia optimization; smoking cessation when applicable; coordinated perioperative planning for biologics/small molecules and minimization of systemic corticosteroids; stoma site marking and counseling; counseling regarding sexual and urinary function; and upfront discussion of perineal wound strategy and the likelihood of delayed healing [10, 27]. Optimization is not only medical but also psychological and logistical. Early involvement of stoma therapists, pain specialists, dietitians, and, when appropriate, mental health support can support postoperative adaptation and reduce decisional regret [6, 8]. Perioperative medication management should be individualized and coordinated across gastroenterology, colorectal surgery, and anesthesia teams. When possible, avoid high-dose systemic corticosteroids at the time of surgery, correct iron deficiency/anemia, treat active infection, and coordinate biologics/small molecules to balance infection and wound-healing risk against the risk of luminal flare [1, 27].

Shared decision-making

Treatment success should be individualized and defined explicitly (e.g., sustained pain control; cessation of drainage and odor; freedom from repeated EUAs/admissions; return to work; reduced opioid requirements) [6, 8]. When goals shift from closure to reliable control of sepsis and pain, the trade-offs of definitive surgery—especially perineal wound morbidity and the expected timeline for healing—should be discussed in concrete terms [1, 10].

Operative strategy and dissection planes

Operative strategy is guided by the extent of luminal disease, prior resections, rectal/anal involvement, and concern for fistula-associated malignancy. Isolated end-stage anorectal disease with a salvageable colon is usually treated with proctectomy/APR and permanent diversion, whereas extensive Crohn’s colitis often favors total proctocolectomy with end ileostomy [1, 11, 28]. The dissection plane should prioritize complete excision of chronically infected or fibrotic tissue and, when relevant, oncologic safety. In selected benign cases, a close rectal dissection—often along an intersphincteric plane—may limit the perineal defect (Fig. 2a). However, in anorectal Crohn’s disease, particularly when inflammation or sepsis extends beyond the rectal wall into the mesorectum and pelvic floor, such a limited plane may leave residual inflamed or infected tissue and is therefore thought to increase the risk of postoperative perineal wound complications. In these settings, a more extensive (“wider”) dissection outside the sphincter complex is often chosen to remove diseased tissue and achieve durable sepsis control (Fig. 2b). Consistent with this concern, persistent mesorectal inflammatory activity after proctectomy has been associated with higher perineal complication rates, and intersphincteric proctectomy with end-colostomy has been reported to be followed by early, severe proximal colonic Crohn’s recurrence in a small retrospective series [29, 30]. Minimally invasive pelvic dissection (laparoscopic or robotic) may reduce abdominal-wall morbidity in selected patients, but it does not remove the dominant challenge in PFCD—perineal wound morbidity [10]. Perineal wound morbidity after proctectomy

Mechanisms of impaired perineal wound healing

Perineal wound morbidity after proctectomy for Crohn’s disease is frequent because surgery is performed in a chronically inflamed, contaminated, and often septic field. Long-standing fistulizing disease, repeated drainage procedures, and dense pelvic fibrosis can result in poorly vascularized tissue planes and a substantial pelvic dead space, promoting fluid accumulation, bacterial burden, and chronic biofilm formation. Systemic factors common in this population—malnutrition, anemia, corticosteroid exposure, and other immunosuppressive therapies—can further impair collagen synthesis and host defense, delaying wound healing [1, 11, 27].

Persistent perineal sinus (PPS) is a key long-term complication (Fig. 3a). It is commonly defined as an incompletely healed perineal wound persisting for > 6 months after proctectomy, sometimes with a cephalad tract extending into the presacral or pelvic cavity [31–33]. Risk factors and clinical impact

Delayed perineal healing and PPS are multifactorial. In a classic proctocolectomy cohort, younger age, rectal involvement, perianal sepsis, high fistulas, extrasphincteric excision, and fecal contamination at operation were associated with PPS; on multivariable analysis, age, rectal involvement, and fecal contamination remained independent predictors [31]. Across Crohn’s cohorts, ongoing pelvic/perineal sepsis at the time of surgery is repeatedly linked to impaired healing [34]. Persistent inflammatory activity within the pelvis/mesorectum has also been associated with postoperative complications, suggesting that disease activity within the operative field contributes risk beyond technical factors alone [29]. Complex fistula anatomy (e.g., high tracts or rectovaginal fistula) and severe local tissue compromise from repeated interventions may further increase the likelihood of nonhealing [14, 26, 31]. Additional reported contributors include malnutrition, anemia, smoking, diabetes/poor glycemic control, and continued immunosuppression [27, 32, 33]. Clinically, delayed healing is consequential: It can prolong hospitalization, increase outpatient visits and reoperations, and attenuate postoperative gains in quality of life [6, 11, 17, 18].

Prevention and management of persistent perineal sinus

Perineal wound strategy should be incorporated into preoperative counseling and operative planning, because decisions made at the index operation—extent of excision, dead-space management, and the need for reconstruction—strongly influence long-term healing [1, 27, 32, 33]. In selected high-risk patients, planned dead-space obliteration (e.g., omentoplasty) and/or immediate tissue transfer (e.g., gracilis or rectus-based flaps) may reduce chronic sinus formation [32, 33, 35–38]. Core preventive principles include maximizing sepsis control before definitive resection (drain abscesses and stabilize inflammation) [1, 3, 27], minimizing pelvic dead space with well-vascularized tissue coverage when needed [32, 33], and tailoring closure to local risk (reoperative fields, large defects, and poor tissue quality) [33, 35–37]. Intraoperatively, autonomic nerves should be preserved where feasible; however, in end-stage anorectal Crohn’s disease, the priority is complete excision of chronically infected and fibrotic tissue to reduce residual septic foci [1]. Practical steps that may mitigate dead-space-related morbidity include peritoneal closure when possible, thoughtful drain placement, meticulous hemostasis to avoid hematoma, and tension-free perineal closure [1, 27, 32, 33]. When extensive dead space, ongoing contamination risk, or poor local tissue quality is anticipated, a preplanned reconstructive strategy is often preferable to a “wait and see” approach [33, 35].

If PPS develops, the first step is to define the anatomy and exclude undrained sepsis or presacral extension, typically with cross-sectional imaging and, when indicated, EUA [3, 32, 33]. Management should address systemic drivers of nonhealing (e.g., nutrition, anemia, steroid exposure, and overall inflammatory control) in parallel with local wound care [27, 32, 33]. Local salvage options include repeated curettage/debridement and negative-pressure wound therapy to reduce bioburden and promote granulation [32, 33, 37]. When conservative measures fail or when the cavity is deep or recurrent, delayed reconstruction with well-vascularized tissue (e.g., gracilis or rectus-based flaps) is often required to obliterate dead space and achieve durable closure [32, 33, 35–37]. Perineal closure and reconstruction at the index proctectomy

The choice between primary closure alone, omentoplasty, and myocutaneous flap reconstruction should be individualized on the basis of sepsis burden, nutritional status, prior perineal surgery, defect size, and access to reconstructive expertise [11, 32, 33, 35–37]. Omentoplasty may help obliterate pelvic dead space and support perineal healing by providing well-vascularized tissue, but the evidence is heterogeneous: Some series suggest benefit in selected patients, whereas other contemporary data report no consistent reduction in re-intervention or pelvic sepsis, underscoring the importance of careful patient selection and durable sepsis control [32, 38, 39]. Myocutaneous flap reconstruction (e.g., gracilis or rectus-based flaps, including the vertical rectus abdominis myocutaneous [VRAM] flap) provides robust vascularized coverage and is often favored for high-risk or reoperative perineums and large defects, with systematic reviews and cohort studies generally reporting higher primary healing rates in these settings; early coordination with plastic/reconstructive surgery is therefore valuable [33, 35–37]. Postoperative pathway and follow-up

Postoperative management should anticipate key drivers of delayed recovery: perineal wound care, pain control, early mobilization, and stoma adaptation [11, 27, 41, 42]. For high-risk perineal closures, a standardized pathway (drain plan, clear triggers for imaging, and early access to wound nurses) may reduce delayed recognition and readmissions [27, 32, 33, 41, 42].

Analgesia plans should aim to minimize prolonged opioid exposure. Multimodal regimens and early mobilization can improve recovery; patients with long-standing PFCD and chronic pain may benefit from preoperative pain-specialist input for expectation setting and tapering plans [27, 41, 42]. After definitive surgery, coordination with gastroenterology remains essential because Crohn’s disease may still flare in the remaining bowel; postoperative medical therapy should be individualized [1, 27]. Follow-up should include nutritional status and anemia/iron deficiency [27, 43]. Early stoma-therapist follow-up and patient education on hydration and output management protect quality-of-life gains after resolving pelvic sepsis [6, 17, 18, 44]. Bone health should also be assessed and managed as part of longitudinal care [27, 45]. Sexual and urinary dysfunction can follow pelvic dissection, particularly in reoperative fields [1, 27, 46, 47]. Preoperative counseling and a plan for postoperative assessment/referral (urology, pelvic floor physiotherapy, and sexual health) are important [27, 46, 47].

Stoma-related issues (skin irritation, high output, dehydration, and parastomal hernia) can become the new limiting factor if not anticipated [27, 44].

Cancer risk in long-standing perianal fistulizing Crohn’s disease and implications for surgical timing

Population-based data suggest an increased risk of anorectal cancer in patients with Crohn’s with anorectal fistulas, with risk accruing over years of fistula duration [48]. Fistula-associated anorectal malignancy in Crohn’s disease is rare, but diagnosis is often delayed because pain, strictures, induration, and chronic discharge can mask early cancer and make surveillance difficult [18, 49]. Refractory or changing symptoms should prompt pelvic MRI and EUA with targeted (and, if needed, repeat) biopsies [49, 50]. Emerging data also highlight the potential contribution of anal high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) infection and squamous intraepithelial lesions in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) populations, which may further inform surveillance strategies [51]. Additional cohort data similarly report malignancy rates among patients with Crohn’s disease with perianal fistulas [52]. Both squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma (including mucinous variants) have been reported, typically after long disease duration and chronic local inflammation [19, 49, 53–55]. Red flags include a new/enlarging mass, escalating pain, new bleeding, rapid change in drainage, or nonhealing ulceration; persistent suspicion warrants repeat targeted biopsies under anesthesia and multidisciplinary review [49, 50, 53, 55]. When surveillance cannot be achieved because of severe pain or fixed stenosis, cancer concern may justify earlier definitive resection rather than continued cycles of temporizing procedures [49, 50].

A practical decision framework

This framework centers on four steps: (1) define anatomy and dominant drivers with MRI and EUA; (2) clarify goals and reversibility, including whether meaningful sphincter preservation remains realistic; (3) when uncertainty persists, undertake a planned diversion trial with predefined endpoints and time-bound reassessment; and (4) proceed to proctectomy when the clinical trajectory is effectively fixed or when cancer concern becomes the overriding driver.

Many patients move through cycles of sepsis control (drainage), optimization of systemic therapy, and, in selected cases, diversion before a definitive decision. To minimize therapeutic inertia, reassessment should be time-bound and anchored to objective milestones and patient-defined outcomes. Documenting objective milestones (e.g., abscess-related admissions, number of EUAs, antibiotic courses, opioid requirements, inability to perform adequate surveillance, and cumulative time lost to symptoms) helps teams and patients recognize when the balance has shifted toward definitive resection. Multidisciplinary review can improve both timing and execution. PPS is often multifactorial (residual cavities, occult tracts, impaired perfusion, ongoing inflammation, and occasionally malignancy). Before repeated debridement, re-imaging and a structured assessment for a persistent pelvic source are essential, with targeted cancer evaluation when clinical features warrant.

Conclusions

Complex PFCD may progress to an end-stage anorectum in which repeated local salvage offers diminishing returns and can worsen scarring, stenosis, and dysfunction. Decision-making should therefore be structured and time-bound, integrating MRI ± EUA mapping, the dominant biologic and functional drivers, and patient-defined goals; in selected patients, diversion can be used as a predefined trial to clarify reversibility, but reversal is not guaranteed. When the trajectory is effectively fixed—due to refractory proctitis, fixed stenosis, cavitating sepsis, or severe pain—or when adequate surveillance is not feasible, including cancer concern, definitive proctectomy/APR may provide the most reliable pathway to durable sepsis and pain control and improved predictability, although available evidence remains predominantly observational.
