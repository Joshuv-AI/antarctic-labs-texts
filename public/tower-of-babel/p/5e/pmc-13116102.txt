Genetic correlation analysis of Alzheimer's disease and stroke implicates PHLPP1 as a shared locus in individuals of African ancestry

AbstractINTRODUCTIONNeuropathological studies indicate a strong association between Alzheimer's disease (AD) and stroke, yet the molecular mechanisms underlying this association remain unclear.METHODSLocal genetic correlation analysis was conducted with LAVA (Local Analysis of [co]Variant Annotation) using the results from genome‐wide association studies on AD and stroke in individuals of African ancestry. BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia affecting 6.7 million individuals in the United States and incurring an estimated national financial burden of $345 billion annually. 1 AD is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by pathophysiological changes in the brain (such as the accumulation of amyloid‐β plaques and neurofibrillary tangles of hyperphosphorylated tau protein) and cognitive decline (including loss of memory). 2 Genetic variation plays an important role in AD, with over 75 genetic AD risk loci identified to date. 3 Vascular risk factors and cerebrovascular changes–such as diabetes, 4 hypertension, 5 and stroke 6 –also predict the incidence of AD.

Stroke results in lasting deficits to global cognition, attention, processing speed, memory, language, and motor skills (with the largest rate of decline observed in memory) 7 and increases the risk of AD by 60%. 6 In addition, cerebrovascular events are associated with more rapid cognitive decline in patients with AD, 8 and post mortem studies indicate that individuals who have both AD pathology as well as cerebral infarcts are more likely to develop dementia than patients with AD pathology alone. 9 , 10 A genetic correlation (rg ) analysis found a significant global correlation between small vessel stroke and AD (rg = 0.37) and identified four significant shared pathways between the two traits (three of these involve lipid transport and one involves immune response). 11 Another study used a gene‐based association analysis to identify 16 genes shared between AD and ischemic stroke, many of which act in the immune system. 12

The exact contribution of cerebrovascular disease to AD remains unknown. AD and stroke share numerous pathophysiological changes in brain tissue, such as hypoperfusion, oxidative stress, immune exhaustion, inflammation, and abnormal expression of amyloid‐β and tau proteins, 13 which together may lead to altered neurotransmission, synaptic loss, neurodegeneration, and a resulting decline in cognitive ability (i.e., dementia). 14 It has been hypothesized that by inhibiting the clearance of amyloid, cerebrovascular disease may make an individual more susceptible to AD. 15 Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is a leading cause of stroke and results from the build‐up of amyloid‐β, the same protein found in neuritic plaques in AD. 16 In patients with early‐onset AD and CAA, duplication of the amyloid precursor protein (APP) gene was observed along with increased amyloid deposition. 17 AD and stoke could therefore act in synergy, increasing the risk of dementia. 18 However, it is also possible that AD and stroke are two independent, yet convergent, traits with similar pathophysiological processes and shared genetic risk factors. 11

Ethnicity is another important risk factor for both AD and stroke. Incidence of AD is 64% higher in people of African versus European descent according to a recent meta‐analysis. 19 Symptom severity (neuropsychiatric symptoms, cognitive decline, functional impairment) is also greater in African ancestry individuals, 20 who are 50% more likely to experience a stroke and 70% more likely to die from a stroke than European ancestry individuals. 21 Despite this disparity, racial minorities (including individuals of African ancestry) are vastly underrepresented in both dementia 22 and stroke 23 research.

To disentangle the mechanistic contribution of cardiovascular disease (CVD) to AD in individuals of African ancestry, the current study examined the genetic correlation between stroke and AD in individuals of African ancestry followed by functional fine‐mapping of identified shared genetic loci.

METHODS

Local genetic correlation analysis of AD and stroke in African ancestry

Local genetic correlation between AD and stroke was estimated using LAVA (Local Analysis of [co]Variant Annotation), 24 which uses genome‐wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics from multiple traits as input. For AD, summary statistics were obtained for model 1 (controlling for age, sex, and principal components of population stratification) from the most recent and largest GWAS on AD in individuals of African ancestry from the Alzheimer's Disease Genetics Consortium (ADGC), 25 which included 2,903 AD cases and 6,265 healthy controls (Table S1). For stroke, African ancestry‐specific GWAS summary statistics were obtained from the GIGASTROKE initiative, a multi‐ethnic meta‐analysis of all recent major stroke GWAS with a total sample size of 1,614,080, 26 and 3,961 stroke cases and 20,030 healthy controls of African ancestry (Table S2). Both summary statistics used genome build GRCh37 and a description of the datasets included in both GWAS can be found in their corresponding publications. 25 , 26 LAVA partitioned the genome into 2,687 semi‐independent blocks based on linkage disequilibrium (LD) using the 1,000 genomes phase 3 African reference panel 27 and estimated the local genetic correlation between AD and stroke in each of these regions. The Bonferroni corrected p‐value threshold for indicating significant genetic covariance of these LD blocks between both traits was derived by dividing 0.05 by 2687 or p < 1.86×10−5.

Impact of genetic ancestry at regions of shared genetic overlap

Significant regions of overlap resulting from LAVA were examined in an independent meta‐analysis conducted by the African Dementia Consortium (AfDC; https://africandementiaconsortium.org/). Whole genome sequencing (WGS) data (n = 373) and TOPMED imputed array data from the Recruitment and Retention for Alzheimer's Disease Diversity Genetic Cohorts in the Alzheimer's Disease Sequencing Project 28 , 29 (n = 180) were collected from 553 Africans from Nigeria, Ghana, Ethiopia, Kenya, and Mozambique (275 clinically diagnosed AD cases; 278 controls). The minimum age requirement for this analysis was 55 for AD cases (Mage = 72.9, SDage = 8.7) and 65 for cognitively normal controls (Mage = 76.7, SDage = 8.1). Association analyses were run separately for the WGS and imputed datasets using SAIGE and controlling for age, sex, and population stratification as measured by PCs 1‐3. Results were then meta‐analyzed using Metasoft.

To evaluate whether any significant regions of genetic overlap in individuals of African ancestry are also involved in AD or stroke etiology in individuals of European ancestry, results from the largest AD GWAS on individuals of European ancestry (111,326 clinically diagnosed/proxy AD cases; 677,663 healthy controls) 3 and the European ancestry‐specific results from the GIGASTROKE GWAS (73,652 stroke cases and 1,234,808 healthy controls) 26 were also examined.

RNA expression

Genes implicated in the local genetic correlation analysis were first followed up using the Agora AD knowledge portal (https://agora.adknowledgeportal.org/). Agora uses more than 2100 RNA‐seq samples collected from over 1100 AD cases and controls from three studies: The Religious Orders Study and Memory and Aging and Project, 30 the Mayo RNAseq study, 31 and the Mount Sinai Brain Bank. 32 Agora harmonizes these samples and reports both overall expression and differential expression between AD cases and controls across nine brain regions: Anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), cerebellum (CBE), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), frontal pole (FP), inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), parahippocampal gyrus (PHG), superior temporal gyrus (STG), and temporal cortex (TCX). Agora also calculates a target AD risk score that ranges from 0–5 and is based on various multi‐omic results, including GWAS studies, quantitative trait locus (QTL) studies, phenotypic evidence from human and/or animal models, transcriptomic data from RNA‐seq profiling, and proteomic data from label‐free quantitation (LFQ) and Tandem Mass Tagging (TMT) shot‐gun profiling methods. 33

RNA expression was also analyzed across different cell types using publicly available human single nucleus sequencing data (GSE157827) 34 and zebrafish single cell sequencing data that have been enriched for gliovascular cells (GSE225721). 35 PHLPP1 expression levels were assessed for 9 cell types in humans: astroglia, microglia, oligodendrocytes (OD), oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs), excitatory neurons, inhibitory neurons, endothelia, fibroblasts, and pericytes; and 12 cell types in zebrafish: astroglia, microglia, OD, OPC, neurons, immature neurons, neuroblasts, immune cells, endothelia, lymph endothelia, pericytes, vascular smooth muscle cells (vascular SMC). The cells that did not fall into one of these 12 categories were analyzed separately and labeled “other”.

To create the Seurat object for human transcriptome analysis, we used the Seurat package (version 5.0.2) in R (version 4.4.0.10). 36 We filtered out any cells with less than 200 expressed genes, and with genes expressed in less than 3 cells. Following the normalization of the dataset, the FindVariableFeatures() function was utilized with the 2000 top variable features. Anchors were identified with the FindIntegrationAnchors function and integration was performed using the IntegrateData function. We used “DoubletFinder” 37 to remove doublets and performed the rest of the analyses on singlets only. The integrated Seurat object included 101,292 cells (55,716 for AD, and 45,576 for Control) with 30,412 features. The data were scaled using all genes, and 30 PCAs (RunPCA) were identified using the RunPCA function in the “Seurat” package. 36 Thirty‐six clusters were identified with resolution 1. The main cell types were defined using aquaporin‐4 (AQP4) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) for astroglia; SLC17A7 and NRGN for excitatory neurons; GAD1 and GAD2 for inhibitory neurons; PDGFRB, MCAM and GRM8 for pericytes; C3 and DOCK8 for microglia; PLP1 and MOBP for OD; PDGFRA and VCAN for OPC; and FLIT1 and CLDN5 for endothelial cells.

We used the same methods and parameters as described above for creating a Seurat object with the zebrafish dataset. The main cell types were identified as described elsewhere. 35 , 38 , 39 Briefly, we used s100b and gfap for astroglia; sv2a, nrgna, grin1a, grin1b for neurons; pdgfrb and kcne4 for pericytes; cd74a and apoc1 for microglia; mbpa and mpz for OD; aplnra for OPC; myh11a and tagln2 for vascular SMC; lyve1b for lymph endothelial cells; and kdrl for vascular cells. To find the average, aggregate, and percent expression of the genes, we used the AverageExpression, AggregateExpression and PrctCellExpringGene functions, respectively.

Differential expression was assessed using a linear model, with AD set as the reference level. Beta values therefore represent the difference in expression between controls and AD (Control–AD). In humans, comparisons in expression levels for each cell type were observed between AD cases and age‐matched controls; and in zebrafish comparisons were evaluated between animals injected with Aβ42 (AD model) versus those injected with phosphate buffered saline (PBS; control group).

Enhanced Hi‐C capture analysis

In situ HiC libraries from iPSC‐derived AD‐relevant cells and AD brain autopsy (Brodmann area 9) samples (described in Table S3 and previously published elsewhere 40 , 41 , 42 ) were prepared as previously described. 43 Primary Hi‐C data analysis was performed with HiCorr 44 and DeepLoop 45 pipelines. BWA 19 was used to map each sequencing read independently to the hg38 reference genome. After removing duplicate and non‐uniquely mapped reads, Two SAM files for each end were merged into a single file. Read pairs were assigned to restriction enzyme MboI fragments and read pairs located within same fragment were discarded. The Mbol fragments were assigned to the 5 kb bins and the Hi‐C data were represented as two‐dimensional contact matrices of 5 kb anchors. HiCorr explicitly corrects distance effects in a joint function with other biases followed by an implicit bias‐correction step to balance the “visibility” of all 5 kb bins in the genome. HiCorr outputs matrices of observed/expected ratios for chromatin interaction profiling. The LoopEnhance function in the DeepLoop package was applied to the HiCorr‐corrected contact matrices. These enhanced contact matrices were used for enhanced Hi‐C Capture Analysis (eHiCA) analysis.

To examine chromatin interactions at identified regions of genetic overlap, eHiCA was performed on the Hi‐C data. 43 Like virtual 4C plots, eHiCA visualizes interacting signals from any bait‐of‐interest with the signals extracted from DeepLoop‐enhanced Hi‐C contact matrices. As a result, the plotted signals are enhanced with observed versus expected ratios. To perform eHiCA, a 5 kb region‐of‐ interest (the “bait”) is first derived from the two haplotypes. The enhanced Hi‐C contact matrix data involving the “bait” are then extracted, capturing the chromatin interactions between the “bait” and the genes/regions with which it physically interacts. The loop strength of surrounding regions is visualized using gradient heatmaps.

For this analysis, a 5 kb region centering the top hits in the region of genetic overlap for both AD and stroke were used as baits. In addition, LD patterns of the top single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in African Ancestry were queried using LDlink (ldlink.nih.gov) on data from African populations. Variants with R 2 > 0.6 for either variant were retained and sorted on position (N = 83). 5 kb baits were designed to encompass these variants for a total of 25 baits. For each bait, the average loop strength between the bait and the region within ± 2 Mb surrounding the bait across samples of the same tissue or cell type was visualized using gradient heatmaps. These heatmaps were loaded into the University of California at Santa Cruz (UCSC) Genome Browser and overlaid with H3K27ac ChIP‐seq data from seven cell lines from the ENCODE project47 to annotate functional DNA elements near the chromatin loops.

RESULTS

Local genetic correlation analysis of AD and stroke in African ancestry

To examine the genetic overlap between AD and stroke in individuals of African ancestry, an analysis of local genetic correlation was conducted using LAVA by partitioning the genome into semi‐independent regions based on LD. Of the 2687 regions entered into the analysis, 1098 were dropped by LAVA for various quality control reasons, such as too few SNPs in the region or negative variance estimated for either phenotype. Of the 1589 remaining regions, 250 show a nominally significant r g between AD and stroke, with four of these regions including known AD loci: FERMT2, TSPOAP1, SHARPIN, and TNIP1 (Table S4). A locus at chr18:59695907‐ 61178035 surpassed the Bonferroni corrected p‐value threshold for multiple testing at chr18q21 (rg = 0.77, p = 2.41×10−6). Examination of the LD structure and genetic association patterns at this locus identified disease‐associated loci exerting an effect in the same direction in both traits. The effect allele is the allele for which the reported effect size (β) is estimated. The top signal in the African ancestry AD GWAS 25 is chr18:60662938 (rs512453, effect allele = T, other allele = C, effect allele frequency = 0.90, β = 0.25, p = 6.02×10−5), which is located on the 3′ end of PHLPP1 and shows strong support from nearby SNPs (Figure 1A). According to 1000 Genomes reference data, this variant is common in both Africans and Europeans, but is much less polymorphic in Africans (African allele frequency = 0.97, European allele frequency = 0.56). 27 , 46 The top signal in the African ancestry stroke GWAS 26 is chr18:60380912 (rs73465039, effect allele = T, other allele = G, effect allele frequency = 0.06, β = 0.22, p = 1.80×10−4), which shows strong support from nearby SNPs in the promoter region of PHLPP1 (Figure 1B). The effect allele for this variant occurs somewhat rarely in Africans (allele frequency = 0.07) and not at all in Europeans. 27 , 46 The top SNPs from the African ancestry AD and stroke GWAS are not in LD with each other based on r 2 and therefore may represent two LD blocks that each show strong regional support by other variants (D′ is high between the top hits from both GWAS however; Figure S1). Notably, rs80108507, a significant SNP from the African ancestry stroke GWAS that is in high LD with the stroke top hit, is an eQTL affecting PHLPP1 expression levels in oligodendrocytes. 47 This region of genetic overlap on PHLPP1 represents a potentially novel AD‐risk locus that may inform future research on the shared causal relationship between cerebrovascular health, neurodegenerative disease, and genetic ancestry.

Impact of African ancestry in AD

To assess the impact of African ancestry in AD and follow up on the results from the local genetic correlation analysis, the chr18q21 region was examined using results from an independent meta‐analysis of 553 Africans conducted by the AfDC. Visualization of these results reveal two potentially distinct LD blocks: one located between ZCCHC2 and PHLPP1 with top variant chr18:60297877 (rs306218, effect allele = T, other allele = C, effect allele frequency = 0.18, β = 0.75, p = 4.46×10−5; Figure 1E), and another located in PHLPP1 with top variant chr18:60519473 (rs76310307, effect allele = C, other allele = G, effect allele frequency = 0.06, β = 1.03, p = 2.55×10−4; Figure 1F). The chr18q21 region results from this analysis are reported in Table S5 along with the results from the AD 3 , 25 and stroke GWAS. 26 Significant variants resulting from the AfDC meta‐analysis are not overlapping with the significant variants from the African ancestry AD 25 and stroke 26 GWAS. Some of these variants are missing in the other datasets, but most are not significant and not showing LD with the top hits from the African ancestry AD and stroke GWAS according to r 2 estimates based on 1000 Genomes reference data. 27 As the AD and stroke GWAS were conducted on mostly African American participants, and the AfDC meta‐analysis was conducted on participants from five African countries, one reason we may not observe overlap could be that the degree of African ancestry may be modulating the effect in this region.

Association of genetic variation at Chr18q21 with AD and stroke in individuals of European ancestry

To determine whether there is also a relationship between AD and stroke in individuals of European ancestry at chr18q21, results from European AD 3 and stroke 26 GWAS were also examined in this region. The top hit in the European stroke GWAS 26 in the chr18q21 region is chr18:60668973 (rs111345592, AF = 0.75, β = −0.03, SE = 0.01, p = 2.78×10−4) with strong regional support from a group of other SNPs near the 3′ end of PHLPP1 (Figure 1D). The top hit in the European AD GWAS 3 at chr18q21 is chr18:60184001 (rs34079742, AF = 0.36, β = ‐0.05, p = 1.04×10−6; Figure 1C). This SNP is not present in 1000 Genomes, but examination of other top SNPs reveals three distinct LD blocks in this region: One on top of ZCCHC2 (rs595765, allele frequency = 0.62, β = 0.04, p = 1.80×10−5, Figure S2A), one on the 5′ end of PHLPP1 (rs144225393, allele frequency = 0.06, β = ‐0.06, p = 1.88×10−4, Figure 2B), and one on the 3′ end of PHLPP1 (rs72343350, allele frequency = 0.08, β = ‐0.06, p = 4.90×10−5, Figure S2C). The results of both the European and African ancestry AD and stroke GWAS are reported in Table S5 along with the results from the independent AfDC meta‐analysis. Many significant SNPs (p < 5×10−4) from the European ancestry AD GWAS are also nominally significant in the European ancestry stroke GWAS (rs595765, rs1108168, rs9951519, rs4941139, rs1053951, rs12150719, rs7226431, rs2051468, rs7227643, rs4941141, rs1606890, rs1473980, rs9944724; see Table S5), indicating that some genetic overlap between the two traits also exists in individuals of European ancestry at chr18q21. Although both ancestries show disease association within PHLPP1, the variants most strongly associating with either AD or stroke differ between the European ancestry GWAS and the African ancestry GWAS (see Table S5). This suggests that different genetic variants may be affecting the relationship between AD and stroke in individuals of European ancestry compared to those in individuals of African ancestry.

RNA expression of PHLPP1 in humans and zebrafish

The top hits from the African ancestry AD 25 and stroke 26 GWAS in the region resulting from the local genetic correlation analysis were both in PHLPP1, which is highly expressed across nine brain regions (Figure S3A). PHLPP1 is upregulated in AD cases compared to controls in seven of these regions (Figure S3B) including the DLPFC (p = 2.99 × 10−6) and the PHG (p = 2.07 × 10−11), which are commonly affected by AD. In line with this notion, the target AD risk score for PHLPP1, indicating its likelihood of being involved in the etiology of AD, is relatively high at 3.27 out of 5 (https://agora.adknowledgeportal.org/).

To examine expression of PHLPP1 in AD and stroke relevant cell types, single cell RNA sequencing data were examined in both humans and zebrafish, which are widely used in genetic studies of human disease as 82% of disease‐related genes in humans have a zebrafish ortholog. 48 PHLPP1 is highly expressed across multiple cell types relevant to stroke and AD in humans, including neurons, OD, and astroglia (Table S6 and Figure S4). Significant expression change between AD cases and controls is observed in OD (p = 0.01; Figure S5), where expression is upregulated in controls compared to AD cases. In zebrafish, phlpp1 is also highly expressed in astroglia (Table S7 and Figure S6) where it is expressed significantly higher (p = 0.002; Figure S7) in the AD model compared to the control model. Together these results suggest that PHLPP1 is highly expressed in brain tissues and cell types that are relevant to both AD and stroke pathophysiology.

Enhanced Hi‐C capture analysis at Chr18q21

Since spatial DNA arrangement is an important epigenomic feature that influences the cis‐regulatory architecture across the genome, we used eHiCA to assess whether the AD and stroke top hits converge on shared regulatory elements. This analysis was conducted using genome build GRCh38 and examined the interacting signal within ± 2 Mb of twenty‐five baits (each measuring 5 kb; Table S8) spanning chr18:62654838‐63017127. LD between either the AD or stroke top SNP and variants with r 2 > 0.6 is reported in Table S9. The results (Figure S8) reveal a similar pattern of chromatin interaction for baits 1–11 (with bait 10 containing rs73465039, the most significant stroke SNP), baits 12–20 (encompassing SNPs in LD profiles of both top SNPs), and baits 21–25 (with bait 23 containing rs512453, the most significant AD SNP). Figure 2 shows representative baits for the three observed patterns. All baits encompassing the stroke top hit (promoter region of PHLPP1) and its LD variants show strong chromatin interaction with the 3′ end of the gene. The reverse is true for the baits used for the AD top hit (3′ end of PHLPP1) and its LD variants, which interact strongly with the promoter region. Baits in intron 1 of PHLPP1 encompassing SNPs from both LD profiles show interaction with both the promoter and 3′ end of PHLPP1. Not only do baits representing the top hits from the African ancestry AD and stroke GWAS interact strongly with each other, but they also both interact with the same chromosomal regions of six additional genes: the promoter region of PIGN, RELCH, BCL2, VPS4B; and the 3′ end of ZCCHC2 and KDSR. Interaction between the two association signals (top SNP and surrounding haplotype) do not appear to be different between African or European ancestry, or dependent on cell type (brain BA9, microglia, and spheroids enriched for neurons and oligodendrocytes), which is consistent with the RNA expression data from human brain described above.

DISCUSSION

Local genetic correlation analysis identified a region of genetic overlap between AD and stroke in individuals of African ancestry on chromosome 18q21. While the stroke top hit is in the promoter of PHLPP1 and the AD top hit is on the 3′ end of the gene, eHiCA analysis shows strong chromatin interactions not only between the two top hits, but also with functional elements in six surrounding genes. This suggests that both the AD and stroke loci are functionally modifying the same set of genes at and around PHLPP1.

PHLPP1 belongs to the Pleckstrin Homology domain Leucine‐rich repeat Protein Phosphatases (PHLPP) family, which inhibit protein kinase B (AKT), 49 protein kinase C (PKC), 50 and MAPK. 51 AKT plays a role in cell survival and growth signaling, and its dysregulation is associated with the development and progression of multiple neurodegenerative diseases, including AD. 52 , 53 AKT protects neurons from Aβ‐induced neurotoxicity, and activation of the AKT pathway is thought to delay the progression of AD. 53 , 54 AKT also promotes angiogenesis and can potentially relieve oxidative stress associated with stroke. 55 , 56 Deletion of PHLPP1 in mice resulted in enhanced AKT activation and reduced neurovascular damage after middle cerebral artery occlusion. 57

In line with these notions, PHLPP1 is highly expressed across brain regions, is highly expressed in AD and stroke relevant cell types, and is differentially expressed between AD cases and controls. PHLPP1 is upregulated in AD cases versus controls in most brain regions according to bulk RNA expression data (agora.adknowledgeportal.org). Cell‐type‐specific single‐nucleus RNA sequencing analyses suggest that PHLPP1 is upregulated in astroglia (in a zebrafish model of AD 35 ) but downregulated in oligodendrocytes (in human AD cases 34 ). These differences in PHLPP1 expression across datasets should not be interpreted as contradictory however, as they reflect distinct biological contexts, including bulk tissue versus cell‐type–specific analyses, different brain regions, and species‐specific disease models. Direction of differential expression does not directly indicate whether therapeutic suppression would be beneficial. The modest observation (p = 0.01) that PHLPP1 expression is lower in oligodendrocytes in AD cases may, for example, reflect a compensatory attempt to enhance AKT signaling in a vulnerable cell population, rather than provide evidence against a pathogenic role of PHLPP1. The increased expression of phlpp1 in astroglia in the zebrafish Aβ42 model could reflect an early or acute response to amyloid toxicity, which may differ from chronic late‐stage human AD. Such differences are well documented across neurodegenerative disease models. 58

Analyses contrasting the local association patterns at the AD locus in African Americans with individuals with higher degree of African ancestry showed different patterns of effects, suggesting that local and/or global ancestry may be modulating the effect at this locus. Our independent meta‐analysis of WGS data from Africans identified an LD block on top of PHLPP1 that did not overlap with the African ancestry AD GWAS that was conducted mostly in African Americans. 25 Examination of this region in the largest available GWASs in individuals of European ancestry revealed suggestive signals at PHLPP1 in both AD 3 and stroke, 26 but with a different set of variants than those shown in the African ancestry GWAS. We also identified this locus as one of two genome‐wide significant loci in analyses using admixture mapping to prioritize genetic regions associated with AD in African Americans. 59 This evidence points to an effect of genetic ancestry on the relationship between AD and stroke at chr 18q21 and highlights the importance of examining genetic relationships across a diverse range of ancestral populations.

Analysis of chromatin interactions with the top hits from the African ancestry AD and stroke GWAS indicate that the two regions carrying the disease associated haplotypes interact with each other in the PHLPP1 gene: the stroke top hit shows strong chromatin interaction with the genomic region in the 3′ end of the gene where the AD top is located, and the AD top hit shows strong chromatin interaction with the genomic region in the 5′ end of the gene where the stroke top hit is located (Figure 2). Both top hits are also interacting with the same functional elements of six nearby genes: the promoter region of PIGN, RELCH, BCL2, VPS4B; and the 3′ end of ZCCHC2 and KDSR. These genes act in a number of AD‐relevant pathways, including Aβ production, 60 , 61 cholesterol transport, 62 , 63 RNA metabolism, 64 cell survival, 65 , 66 and intracellular trafficking. 67 , 68 , 69 , 70

A single gene can influence multiple traits, a phenomenon known as pleiotropy that is ubiquitous in the study of human genetics and disease. 71 It is important to study pleiotropy to understand the relationship between multiple phenotypes and potentially uncover biological pathways that are shared between them. Past studies have examined genetic overlap between AD and ischemic stroke, but these studies were mostly conducted on individuals of European ancestry and estimated global correlation. Global correlation examines the relationship between two traits across the entire genome but does not consider local relationships at specific genetic loci. The present study utilized genome‐wide LD information to partition the genome into semi‐independent LD blocks and then estimated the correlation between AD and stroke at each of these regions, allowing the discovery of potential local relationships that may be missed by averaging effects across the entire genome. As the partitions are ∼1 MB on average, the possibility remains that we missed additional correlations between smaller loci that are diluted by including additional variants in the 1 MB window.

The current study nominates PHLPP1 as a potential therapeutic target shared between AD and stroke in both individuals of African and European ancestry. Our analyses suggest that the disease‐associated genetic variation at this locus is modified by genetic ancestry, and in individuals of African ancestry also by degree or origin of African ancestry. It is possible that by inhibiting AKT, PHLPP1 is acting in a causal pathway for both AD and stroke. Evidence from prior functional studies suggests that suppression of PHLPP1 enhances AKT activation and may confer neurovascular protection. 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 , 57 Our genetic and regulatory data nominate PHLPP1 as a shared locus for AD and stroke; however, the direction and timing of therapeutic modulation are likely to be cell‐type– and disease‐stage–specific. eHiCA analysis suggests that the genomic regions underlying the top hits in PHLPP1 are also interacting with functional elements in nearby genes. Further research is warranted to determine how these genes contribute to a shared mechanistic pathway between AD and stroke, how genetic ancestry effects this mechanism, and whether this locus should be considered as a target for future interventions.
