Nr2e3-Deficient Mice Show Evidence for Elevated Photoreceptor Bioenergetics and Retinal Visual Cycle Activity Based on OCT Metabolic Biomarkers
Authors: Bruce A. Berkowitz, Robert H. Podolsky, Tom Burgoyne, Matthew J. Hayes, Ryan Katz, Rida Waseem, James Soetedjo, Ehimenmen S. Ataman, Robin Roberts
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeEx vivo studies show that photoreceptors of Nr2e3-deficient (rd7) mice display (1) cone-like juxtanuclear mitochondria suggesting higher bioenergetics than in wild-type (WT) mice, and (2) reliance on a cone-only “retinal” visual cycle—two hypotheses examined in vivo herein.MethodsWe measured two optical coherence tomography (OCT) metabolic biomarkers in normal and rd7 mice, both on a background with inefficient mitochondria (C57BL/6J), under light and dark conditions. The first biomarker is the external limiting membrane-retinal pigment epithelium (ELM-RPE) thickness which contracts in response to subretinal space acidosis limiting retinoid availability. The second biomarker is the sub-ELM hyperreflective band profile shape aspect ratio which increases when the subretinal space is acidotic and mitochondria are inefficient. We also measured retinal layer thicknesses, mitochondrial configuration by electron microscopy (EM), and visual performance using optokinetic tracking.ResultsCompared with WT mice, rd7 mice showed more compact mitochondria (EM) distributed over larger-than-normal regions of photoreceptor inner segments (OCT and EM), and subnormal ELM-RPE thickness; OCT biomarkers did not change with light-dark conditions. The outer nuclear layer (ONL) was slightly thinner than normal, and visual performance was normal. Unexpectedly, inner retina thickness was increased.ConclusionsThe data suggest that rd7 mice have higher-than-normal photoreceptor metabolism based on widespread distribution of abnormally less elongated mitochondria, and OCT biomarker evidence of more efficient energy production. The slightly thinner ONL despite reduced ELM-RPE thickness/impaired RPE-based retinoid recycling is consistent with rd7 mice using the cone-specific visual cycle to maintain adequate retinoid levels for vision.

The nuclear receptor Nr2e3 functions as a key transcriptional regulator of photoreceptor fate specification, promoting rod development while suppressing cone-specific gene expression.1–3 Without Nr2e3, patients develop hereditary enhanced short (S) wavelength cone sensitivity syndrome (ESCS) with retinal structural abnormalities, including supernumerary S-cones, outer retinal rosette formation, and modest outer retinal thinning.1–3

Nr2e3-deficient (rd7) mice exhibit the above abnormal outer retinal structural aspects of enhanced S-cone sensitivity syndrome.3 Evidence to-date from ex vivo studies suggest that rd7 mice have hybrid photoreceptors with aspects of rod and cone morphology, and cone-biased function.1,4,5 For example, rd7 photoreceptors look like rods but show juxtanuclear mitochondria, as documented by electron microscopy (EM).1,4 Juxtanuclear mitochondria are found in cones and not typically in rods.1,4 It remains unclear if juxtanuclear mitochondria are associated with elevations in mitochondrial output in vivo relative to that in normal rod photoreceptors.6–8 In addition, rod photoreceptor cells from isolated retina without access to the subretinal space-dependent RPE visual cycle engage the cone-only and subretinal space-independent “retinal” visual cycle to produce rhodopsin for the photoreceptors.5 It is not known if the retinal visual cycle is engaged in the rd7 retina in vivo.

To address the above knowledge gaps, optical coherence tomography (OCT) metabolic biomarkers were used to noninvasively evaluate mitochondrial activity and RPE visual cycle in vivo. Normally, increased metabolic activity (e.g. in dark versus light) produces more waste products (e.g. CO2 and lactate) that in-turn causes outer retinal acidosis.9–22 This subretinal space acidosis results in contraction of the external limiting membrane – retinal pigment epithelium (ELM-RPE) thickness via upregulation of a water-removing co-transporter on the apical RPE.9–22 An important consequence of this ELM-RPE compression is that the subretinal space becomes geometrically constrained and water mobility is reduced as measured by diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).14,15 Furthermore, such spatial restriction normally modifies the organization of components within the interphotoreceptor matrix including interphotoreceptor binding protein (IRBP) –a retinoid chaperone, anti-oxidant, and pro-survival factor that is involved with the regeneration of rhodopsin.10,11,14,15,18,22–32 We reason that inappropriate ELM-RPE contraction in the light will reduce the mobility of water and IRBP leading to defective retinoid handling and impaired rhodopsin regeneration with eventual photoreceptor degeneration as shown, for example, in IRBP knockout mice.29

Another consequence of subretinal space acidosis is its impact on mitochondrial distribution within the photoreceptor inner segment which in-turn is a function of mitochondrial efficacy.9,10,16,26,33 This distribution can be measured by the mitochondrial configuration within photoreceptors as measured by the aspect ratio (MCP/AR) of the OCT hyperreflective band immediately posterior to the ELM.9,10,16,26,33 Higher MCP/AR values (i.e. a rounder profile shape) within the inner segment indicate greater reflective surface areas from mitochondria, and lower values indicating a lower reflective surface area; these interpretations are based on comparisons to EM data.9,10,16,26,33 When subretinal space pH normally decreases—such as during dark adaptation—MCP/AR increases in mice with inefficient mitochondria (e.g. C57BL/6J), but remains the same or decreases in mice with efficient mitochondria (e.g. C57BL/6NTAC and 129S6/ev).9,10,16,26,33

In this study, we noninvasively tested for evidence of elevated metabolism, and RPE visual cycle retinoid deficiency as measured by ELM-RPE contraction and modified processing of MCP/AR, under light and dark conditions in rd7 mice. In addition, we tested the visual performance of rd7 and control wild-type (WT) mice because it was unclear whether anatomic outer retinal malformations per se lead to abnormal visual behavior.

Methods

All mice were treated in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research, and with specific authorization by the Wayne State University Division of Laboratory Animal Resources Institutional Animal and Care Use Committee (IACUC). For the cross-sectional OCT studies performed at Wayne State University, 2 to 3 month old male C57BL/6J mice (WT; n = 10 light and n = 8 dark; Jackson Laboratories, Bar Harbor, ME, USA), and 2 to 3 month old male Nr2e3rd7/rd7 mice (rd7; n = 9 light and n = 4 dark; Jackson Laboratories, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) were housed and maintained in 12-hour/12-hour light-dark cycle laboratory lighting. A cross-sectional selection of mice (a subset of the above when possible) were examined by EM (n = 3 mice/group; 15 inner segments evaluated/mouse), and by OKT (WT n = 19 and rd7 n = 7). After scanning, mice were humanely euthanized by an overdose of ketamine/xylazine followed by cervical dislocation, per our Wayne State University Institutional Animal and Care Use Committee-approved protocol. Data in this study were collected from the left eye to minimize potential left-right eye differences between groups and to allow for potential comparisons with our previous results.

Optical Coherence Tomography

Mice anesthetized with 100 mg/kg ketamine (Covetrus, Portland, ME, USA) and 6 mg/kg xylazine (MWI Animal Health, Boise, ID, USA) were examined by OCT (Envisu UHR2200; center wavelength at 840 nm; Bioptigen, Durham, NC, USA) in the morning (i.e. before noon). Mice were dark adapted overnight, and the following day, imaged in the dark or after room-light (standard overhead laboratory fluorescent lighting; 800–900 lux) adaptation for just 1 hour prior to imaging. To dilate the iris, 1% atropine sulfate was used, genteal was applied to smooth fur back away from the eye, and Systane Ultra (Alcon, Geneva, Switzerland) was used to lubricate the eyes.

From the central retina, we collected radial volume scans with the following parameters: A-scans/B-scans = 1000 lines; B-scans/volume = 1000 scans; and frames/B-scan = 1 frame. One hundred images were extracted from B-scan numbers 450 to 549 (representing inferior–superior retina) and then registered (in-house script for R software; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Briefly, rigid body registration with RNiftyReg (a function in R) was used to rotate the image and interpolate signal at each pixel. Next, columns of pixels are translated up or down to maximize alignment with the rest of the retina – a piecewise rigid-body registration effectively flattening the image to align retinal layers for further analysis. From these 100 inferior-superior oriented images, a Z-stack minimum (Min) intensity projection was obtained to suppress the appearance of rosettes and swirls commonly seen in Nr2e3rd7/rd7 mice, allowing for further analysis; the same Z-stack minimum (Min) intensity projection processing was performed in WT mice; no evidence was found that Z-stack minimum (Min) intensity projection processing distorted laminar border definition or would change interpretation of rescaled and rotated processing of MCP/AR (rrMCP/AR; Fig. 1). The reflectance/intensity values are obtained from log-based images (default in the Bioptigen system); we have not found evidence that this influences the biomarkers measured herein.9

OCT Image Analysis

Laminar boundary segmentation were estimated with a previously described machine learning model-based computer program.27 Briefly, the machine learning model was a U-net convolutional neural network trained using the “dice loss” function and the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 0.001), with 665 previously labeled images for training and 166 and 356 images for validation and testing, respectively. To improve the performance of the model, its predictions were postprocessed by applying a shortest-path algorithm. From the model-based estimates, segmentation was then performed with an R script. The intensity values used to generate the ellipsoid zone (EZ) reflectivity profile shape are measured from a log-based image with a 16-bit depth (default in the Bioptigen system). In a preliminary study, we compared images before and after converting them from log to linear values using a simple, empirically derived equation and found a similar EZ reflectivity profile shape difference with either approach; however, further studies in this area are needed.

Once segmented, inferior and superior retinas (i.e. 350–624 µm from the optic nerve head (ONH) on the inferior and superior sides) were each analyzed; we started at 350 µm to ensure that images were analyzed without influence from the ONH, and the outer retina is relatively uniform in all OCT data. We measured the ELM-RPE thickness from in-house R scripts that objectively extracted layer boundaries obtained after searching the space provided by a machine-learning estimates (“seed boundaries”) as above. The ELM and RPE are initially identified by local signal maxima and the R script determined the ELM-RPE thickness by calculating the distance from ELM to the basal side of the RPE at the level of Bruch's membrane.34–36 This analysis generated a spreadsheet of distances from the ONH and layer thickness. The output image had the basal aspect of the RPE held in a fixed position without stretching the image; this non-stretched image was used to generate the A-line reflectivity profiles shown for both groups in Figures 1 and 2.

(A) Light-adapted images with minimum Z-stack processing of representative WT and rd7 retinas (same as in Figure 1B) with the superior retina on the right and inferior retina on the left. The optic nerve head region (± 350 µm) is blacked out. ELM reflectance (left-most peak) presented differently in WT versus rd7 mice as a likely consequence of ELM fragmentation in rd7 mice.82 The shape of MCP is summarized by the inverse of the aspect ratio (i.e. minor:major) of the ellipse matching the second moment of the profile. Images are scaled the same.

We analyzed rrMCP/AR from 10 inferior retina windows and 10 superior retina windows; each window was sized to be 19 (width) × 49 (height) pixels (windows are not shown in the Figures). In each window, the hyperreflective band immediately posterior to the ELM was displayed and its intersection with the baseline manually marked (MATLAB script; MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). The program then found the local minimum near this mark in all 20 windows in an unbiased fashion. Connecting the two local minimums on either side of the hyperreflective band defined its baseline. Note that the middle of each window was used as the distance for each window so distances for the rrMCP/AR are slightly different from those for ELM-RPE and outer nuclear layer (ONL). Next, we measured the profile shape for the averaged A-line in each window. Using ImageJ software, under Analyze/Set measurements, we check the “Fit ellipse,” “Shape descriptors,” and “Invert Y coordinates.” The Fit ellipse measurement performs its “fitting” by obtaining the profile shape second moment, orientation, and centroid, and then generating an ellipse with the same second moment, orientation, and centroid. The rationale for generating an ellipse as a descriptor of an arbitrary shape based on the second moment is described formally in the literature.37 This process in ImageJ software is described at https://github.com/imagej/ImageJ/blob/master/ij/process/EllipseFitter.java. The advantage of reframing the hyperreflective band in terms of an ellipse is that an ellipse's shape can simply be described as an unbiased minor/major aspect ratio; note that in the ImageJ output, the minor/major calculation is presented under the “Round” column. More work is needed to determine if the ellipse aspect ratio is an optimal shape description for these types of studies.9,16,38

To reduce variability and thus better characterize the profile shape, here, we have refined MCP/AR with rrMCP/AR of the A-lines. This minimized the impact of reflectivity and baseline differences within and between mice. Each A-line is rotated so the slope of its baseline is zero. Then, the profile is rescaled such that its greatest reflectance is set to 100, with 0 set to the left-most aspect of where the A-line intersects the baseline (MATLAB script). The rrMCP/AR was performed on all A-lines in this study. The rrMCP/AR does not appear to be a function of ELM-RPE thickness.10

Electron Microscopy

At Wayne State University, mice were euthanized immediately following 1 hour of light-adapted OCT data collection and the left eye collected for EM analysis. Six control and 6 rd7 mouse eyes were fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde and 2% glutaraldehyde in 0.15 M cacodylate buffer for 1 hour at room temperature. These samples were then shipped to University College London where the cornea was cut away, the lens removed, and the eyes fixed for a further 1 hour at room temperature. Eyes were incubated in aqueous 1% osmium tetroxide and 1.5% potassium ferricyanide for 1 hour on ice. En bloc staining was performed using undiluted UA-Zero (Agar) for 2 hours at room temperature. The eyes were then dehydrated through an ethanol series (50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%), followed by infiltration with a 1:1 mixture of propylene oxide and Epon resin overnight at room temperature. Samples were placed in fresh Epon for 4 hours, replaced with new Epon for a further 4 hours at room temperature, and embedded overnight in Epon at 60°C. Ultrathin sections (100 nm) were cut and imaged using a JEOL 1400Plus transmission electron microscope equipped with an Advanced Microscopy Technologies NanoSprint12 camera (AMT Imaging Direct, Woburn, MA, USA). Four eyes per group were examined.

In a subset of fixed eyes, serial block face-scanning electron microscopy (SBF-SEM) was performed as follows. Fixed mouse eyes were washed twice in phosphate buffer pH7.4. Then incubated for 1 hour in 2% osmium tetroxide/1.5% ferricyanide. They were then washed 3 times for 10 minutes in ddH2O and incubated with freshly made up aqueous 1% thiocarbohydrazide for 20 minutes. They were then washed 3 times for 10 minutes in ddH2O and incubated for 30 minutes in 2% osmium tetroxide without ferricyanide. They were washed 3 times again and incubated overnight at 4°C in 1% aqueous uranyl acetate. After a further wash, the samples were then incubated in freshly made Walton's lead aspartate solution pH5.5 for 30 minutes. Samples were then washed and dehydrated through alcohol as described above. They were then further dehydrated 2 times for 10 minutes in acetone, and then infiltrated overnight in 50% acetone/Durcapan resin mix. They were then incubated for 8 hours in 100% Durcapan resin and finally embedded in Durcapan resin cured for 48 hours at 60°C. Samples were cut into 0.7 × 0.7 × 1 mm cubes, orientated onto aluminium pins, polished, and imaged in a Zeiss Sigma VP SEM fitted with a Gatan 3View door (for automatic serial sectioning) and an Onpoint back-scatter electron detector.

EM Image Analysis

ImageJ software was used to measure mitochondrial shape (e.g. aspect ratio/major:minor ratio) and distribution within the inner segment. For the distribution, the distances from the center of each mitochondrion to the distal and proximal borders of the inner segment were measured. SBF-SEM images were segmented using Amira 6.0 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Optokinetic Tracking

Two visual performance metrics were measured from awake and freely moving mice. The first is the spatial frequency threshold (SFT or “acuity,” in cyc/deg [c/d]), or the highest grating spatial frequency that triggered head tracking.39,40 Contrast sensitivity (CS) was measured by taking the inverse spatial frequency threshold (i.e. the inverse Michelson contrast [unitless]) at a grating setting of 0.06 c/d (near the nominal peak contrast sensitivity) using the optokinetic tracking (OKT) reflex (OptoMotry; CerebralMechanics, Inc., Lethbridge, Alberta, Canada), as described previously.39,40 In brief, a vertical sine wave grating is projected as a virtual cylinder in three-dimensional coordinate space on computer monitors arranged in a quadrangle around a testing arena. Unrestrained mice were placed on an elevated platform at the center of the arena. An experimenter used a video image of the arena from above to view the animal and follow the position of its head with the aid of a computer mouse and a crosshair superimposed on the mouse head. The x, y positional coordinates of the crosshair are centered on the hub of the virtual cylinder, enabling its wall to be maintained at a constant “distance” from the animal’s eyes and thereby adjusting the spatial frequency of the stimulus to a fixed viewing position. When the cylinder was rotated in the clockwise (CW) or counterclockwise (CCW) direction and the animal followed with head and neck movements that tracked the rotation, it was judged whether the animal’s visual system could distinguish the grating. CW and CCW tracking provide a measure of left and right eye SFT and CS.39,41 One set of SFT and peak of CS measurements can reliably be obtained in 30 minutes.

Statistical Analyses

Data are presented as means with 95% confidence intervals, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using linear mixed models with the Kenward-Roger method for calculating degrees of freedom in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

We applied a consistent modeling strategy across all OCT-based measurements. The initial model included fixed effects for strain (WT versus rd7), light condition (i.e. light versus dark adaptation), side (inferior versus superior), distance from the ONH, and all possible interactions. Random effects included a random intercept and random distance from the ONH for mouse nested within strain and condition, along with a power spatial power residual covariance structure to account for any residual spatial correlation between measurement locations not accounted for by the random coefficients.

Model selection proceeded in three stages. First, we evaluated alternative random effect structures and residual variance models, including random intercepts for mouse within strain and heterogeneous residual variances across strain, condition, or both. We used Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) to select the optimal random effects and variance structure. Second, we assessed whether modeling distance using cubic regression splines improved model fit based on AIC/BIC comparisons. Third, we further evaluated random spline coefficients if the spline model was favored over a linear relationship between the outcome and distance from the ONH. We used a forward-selection strategy beginning with a single random spline coefficient and continuing to add other random spline coefficients provided the AIC/BIC improved or the model showed signs of overfitting.

Using the best-fitting model structure, we performed backward elimination of nonsignificant higher-order interactions to arrive at the final model for each measurement. Plots of predicted means from each model were compared with plots of raw data to assess model fit and identify potential systematic deviations. All statistical comparisons were based on these final models, using linear contrasts to estimate and test means and effects of interest.

For OKT, we used a linear mixed model for contrast sensitivity but used a generalized linear mixed model for acuity because this outcome was not normally distributed. The model for acuity used a gamma distribution with the default log link. Both models included the fixed effects of strain, direction (CW versus CCW), and the interaction between strain and direction. The model also included a random intercept for direction.

For the mitochondrion characteristic data from EM, we first used principal components to visualize how well separated rd7 and WT mice were overall, conducting analyses separately by retina side (Supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Fig. S1). The size of the variable loadings for each principal component indicated that the different mitochondrion characteristics contributed mostly independent information. Mitochondrion distances from the proximal/distal border of the IS were the most important variables describing the differences between rd7 and WT mice (see Supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Fig. S1). The second most important variable was the mitochondrion aspect ratio. Given these results, we decided to focus our analyses on these characteristics. We used separate linear mixed effects models for each side to examine the mitochondrion aspect ratio. The model included the fixed effect of strain and random intercepts for mouse within strain, image within mouse and strain, and inner segment within image, mouse, and strain. Mitochondrion aspect ratio was not normally distributed, and a log transformation worked better than a generalized linear mixed effects model, leading us to use log-transformed data in all analyses.

Preliminary analysis of mitochondrion distance from the inner segment borders suggested that inner segment size could differ between rd7 and WT mice, potentially leading to different distances that reflect inner segment size rather than the relative distribution of the mitochondria. We therefore analyzed a normalized distance to the proximal inner segment border: proximal distance/median (proximal distance + distal distance) where the median was taken for a given inner segment within an image. We used a proportional odds model with random effects to examine the change in mitochondria distribution based on the normalized proximal distance, binning the distance into 20 equally spaced bins. We used a Bayesian approach for this analysis because not all bins contained mitochondria. As with the mitochondrion aspect ratio, the model included the fixed effect of strain and random intercepts for mouse within strain, image within mouse and strain, and inner segment within image, mouse, and strain. We fit this model using brms() in R software, using N(0.5) priors for both the intercepts and the strain coefficient.

Results

ELM-RPE Thickness

As shown in Figure 3, rd7 mice showed significantly contracted ELM-RPE thickness which is consistent with greater acidosis/metabolic activity than in WT mice in both the inferior and superior retina during light and dark conditions. In addition, dark-adaptation did not cause the ELM-RPE to contract compared with light conditions in rd7 mice consistent with more efficient metabolism (see Fig. 3). In contrast, dark-adaption caused ELM-RPE to contract in WT mice consistent with less efficient metabolism (see Fig. 3).9,11,16,17

rrMCP/AR

As summarized in Figure 4, spider graphs revealed that rrMCP/AR was lower in rd7 mice compared with WT mice in the inferior retina. In the superior retina, the rrMCP/AR difference between rd7 mice and WT mice was dependent on the distance from the ONH. The rd7 mice showed lower-than-WT rrMCP/AR implying a reduced reflective surface area of mitochondria and predicting changes in their configuration when evaluated by EM data (see below). Functionally, rrMCP/AR did not decrease in light relative to dark conditions in rd7 mice, whereas it did increase in the light in WT mice, as in agreement with previous reports in mice with more efficient metabolism (see Fig. 4).9,11,16,17

Analysis of Inner Segment Mitochondria

Analysis of the EM images revealed altered mitochondrial characteristics in rd7 photoreceptors. Representative images showed visually distal redistribution of mitochondria in rd7 mice compared to WT (Figs. 5A, 5B). Quantitative modeling of normalized proximal distance supported this pattern with rd7 mitochondria having a 2.4-fold increase in the odds of being positioned farther from the proximal border (95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.53–10.3, posterior probability = 72.8%; Fig. 5C). Despite statistical uncertainty due to small sample sizes, this pattern is consistent with a wider-than-WT mitochondrial distribution and is consistent with low rrMCP/AR results. The rd7 photoreceptor mitochondria also exhibited significantly reduced elongation (aspect ratio [major:minor axis] of rd7 mitochondria was 72.1% [95% CI = 57.5–90.4%] of WT, P = 0.007; Fig. 5D) which would be expected to reduce the reflectance surface area compared with controls. Together, the EM results support both positional and morphological features that would be expected to modify the overall mitochondrial reflective surface area and contribute to rrMCP/AR.

Discussion

In this study, we first needed to address the problem of outer retinal anatomic irregularities (i.e. rosettes and whorls) in rd7 mice that distort normal retinal laminar spacing and thus confound the extraction of the retinal layer thicknesses and metabolic biomarkers from OCT data. To minimize the impact of whorls and rosettes, we took advantage of the fact that these structural abnormalities are not uniformly distributed across the retina. Thus, we were able to suppress their appearance by collecting OCT data with a radial acquisition pattern, extracting 100 images that span the inferior-superior direction, and then calculating a Z-stack minimum (Min) intensity projection from those 100 images. In addition, Z-stack minimum (Min) projection processing did not modify the position of layer boundaries or the EZ profile shape compared with these biomarkers measured from a Z-stack mean projection image (see Figs. 1C–E). Compared with manually removing images with whorls/rosettes, minimum Z projections also provide objective/unbiased image processing that utilizes all 100 images while suppressing the impact of whorls/rosettes.

We also implemented modified MCP/AR processing in which each A-line was rotated so its baseline slope equaled zero and then rescaled so that its highest reflectance equaled 100 (0 is set to the left-most aspect of where the A-line intersects the baseline). This rotation and rescaling minimized the impact on the profile shape aspect ratio of differences in baseline slope and reflectivity within and between images. There is strong evidence that photoreceptor mitochondria act as a main OCT reflectance source.9,42–44 This interpretation has been cogently argued elsewhere, and we have supported this interpretation using EM and other gold-standard assays in mouse strains with different mitochondrial activity.9–11,33,45 Although non-mitochondrial interpretations of this hyperreflective band have been proposed based on adaptive optics OCT data, there have been few attempts that support this alternative interpretation against gold-standard assays, to our knowledge.9,42 Together, the novel imaging processing performed in this study allowed for the noninvasive extraction of OCT metabolic biomarkers and laminar thickness information from rd7 retinas for the first time.

Evidence That rd7 Photoreceptors Have Greater and More Efficient Metabolic Activity

It has been shown that rd7 photoreceptors demonstrate dark currents and light responses that resemble WT rods and not cones.5 Thus, it seems reasonable to postulate similar light-dark changes in subretinal space pH in both rd7 and WT mice.9,11,14,17–20,22,46,47 In WT C57BL/6J mice, ELM-RPE contracts based on the extent of subretinal space acidosis produced by, for example, elevated mitochondrial activity.10,14,15,18,47 We reason that the ELM-RPE contraction in rd7 mice compared with WT mice controls is consistent with high metabolic activity producing greater acidosis.

ELM-RPE contraction is normally relatively greater in dark-adapted mice with inefficient mitochondria (C57BL/6J) than in dark-adapted mice with efficient mitochondria (e.g. 129S6).26 However, in this study, we did not observe a light-dark differences in ELM-RPE and rrMCP/AR in rd7 mice.9–11 The rd7 mice are on a C57BL/6J background that contains a natural nicotinamide (NAD) nucleotide transhydrogenase (Nnt) mutation causing mitochondrial inefficacies in WT C57BL/6J mice.26,48 Normal C57BL/6J mice with their inefficient mitochondria show an increase in rrMCP/AR during dark-induced acidosis (original data were reprocessed as rrMCP/AR; not shown).9 In contrast, mitochondria in rd7 C57BL/6J mice appear efficient based on findings of stable ELM-RPE and rrMCP/AR during both light and dark conditions.9–11 Therefore, the stability of rrMCP/AR in light- and dark-adapted rd7 mice is characteristic of how efficient mitochondria respond to acidosis in support of the above ELM-RPE results.9–11 The above considerations suggest that rd7 photoreceptor mitochondria appear relatively more efficient than those in WT mice.

Evidence that rd7 Photoreceptors Have Access to the Cone Retina Visual Cycle

The abnormal ELM-RPE compression in light-adapted rd7 mice is also a useful biomarker of constraint on the distribution of IRBP within the interphotoreceptor matrix due to cellular barriers being closer together than normal.14,15,24,25 Studies show that impaired distribution of IRBP/availability of retinoid in the light results in progressive photoreceptor atrophy.23–25,29,49–56 For example, when retinoid transfer is damaged, as in IRBP knockout mice that normally use the RPE visual cycle, photoreceptor degeneration occurs much faster and is more severe than the modest thinning in this and another study.56,57 Because our OCT biomarkers find only slight ONL thinning and evidence for impaired RPE visual cycle (i.e. contracted ELM-RPE), our data are consistent with the presence of an active cone retina visual cycle previously demonstrated ex vivo by Wang et al.5

Evidence for Greater Mitochondrial Coverage Within the Inner Segment in rd7 Versus in WT Photoreceptors

An increase in the spatial extent of the mitochondrial coverage within the inner segment in rd7 mice is expected to reduce rrMCP/AR compared with WT values.9 Indeed, rrMCP/AR was subnormal in rd7 mice, and the EM results showed a pattern consistent with photoreceptor mitochondria being more widely distributed within the inner segment than in WT mice.4,58 Only regions farthest from the ONH on the superior side of the rd7 retina showed normal rrMCP/AR between strains (see Fig. 4). More work is needed to identify the underlying reasons for the differences between superior and inferior retina. Nonetheless, a wider mitochondria distribution within rd7 photoreceptors measured by OCT and EM are also consistent with an overall higher metabolic output with an associated elevation in CO2 and lactic acid production, acidification of the subretinal space, and thinning of ELM-RPE.6–10,12,14,16,17,22,26,27,47 Because of the inferior/superior differences in rrMCP/AR found with OCT (see Fig. 4), and the uncertainties regarding where exactly the EM samples were obtained on each side, we limited analytical comparisons between EM and OCT to the inferior retina.

We also considered if overhead lighting conditions more strongly illuminated the inferior retina, leading to the spatial differences seen in Figure 4. To address this, we first note that one expects a larger inferior ELM-RPE change between light (presumed higher subretinal space pH) versus dark (presumed lower subretinal space pH) conditions. This was not evident in this study (Supplementary Fig. S2). As noted in the methods, rrMCP/AR is not a function of ELM-RPE thickness, and the results shown in Figure 4 support this conclusion but require additional investigation.

Comparing the Present OCT Metabolic Biomarkers Results to Previously Published EZ Biomarkers

The OCT results in this study do not support a common assumption in the field that a lower-than-normal EZ biomarker indicates subnormal mitochondrial activity.59 We think the different interpretations arise from an underappreciation that mitochondrial distribution alters the OCT reflectance from the inner segment. Additionally, there is a lack of standardization across studies with some studies using, for example, EZ-RPE thickness or a single point of EZ reflectivity, neither of which reflects mitochondrial distribution.60,61 More work is needed to determine if EZ-RPE and ELM-RPE thicknesses are correlated, a finding that would suggest a re-interpretation of a reduced EZ-RPE index as a proxy for acidosis and retinoid availability.9,12,60,62 In addition, it is not possible to distinguish a decrease in a single point of EZ reflectivity as a loss of mitochondria or change in their distribution within the inner segment.9 In addition to our alternative interpretation of these OCT biomarkers, we note an advantage of rrMCP/AR is that it is not a function of total optic path clarity or variations in baseline slope of the A-line.61 Together, the above considerations support examining both ELM-RPE and rrMCP/AR in future studies as a validated platform of photoreceptor metabolism in vivo.

Secondary Findings

Inner Retinal Thickening in rd7 Mice

An incidental finding in this study is the observation of a previously unreported supernormal inner retina thickness consistent with edema in light-adapted rd7 mice. In general, edema develops when more water enters than leaves the tissue. More work is needed to identify the factors involved in the regulation of inner retinal hydration. One possibility is that Müller cells and their highly expressed water channel AQP4 are contributing to maintaining water homeostasis.63–69

It is less clear if there is perturbed hydration regulation in the outer retina. The major water channel in photoreceptors, AQP1, is reported to be downregulated in rd7 mice, as well as in mice with cone-like photoreceptors.64,65,70,71 Additional studies are needed to investigate the mechanisms underlying the different spatial patterns of ONL thinning shown here under dark and light conditions.22

Together, the above considerations raise the possibility that inner and outer retinal layer hydration are abnormal in rd7 mice, a hypothesis that requires additional study.

Visual Performance Indices of rd7 Mice Are Normal

Here, we tested the hypothesis that, in rd7 mice, the anatomic outer retinal malformations alone cause abnormal visual behavior. Unremarkable SFT and CS indices were measured in rd7 mice in this study despite the presence of retinal dystrophy, elevated bioenergetics, and evidence for abnormal laminar hydration. These findings are in-line with previous studies that showed that a greater number of S-cones and photoreceptor whorls in rd7 mice did not alter electrophysiologic function, and other mouse models with retinal whorls show normal visual performance indices.72,73 We also note that S-cones peak sensitivity in mice is in the UV range rather than the blue range, and thus are not primarily tested by OKT.74 More work is needed to assess S-cone-driven performance and night blindness status in these mice, both of which are abnormal in patients with excessive S-cone syndrome.75

Limitations

Contraction of the ELM-RPE occurs as a result of subretinal space acidosis from high mitochondrial activity based studies in genetically distinct mice, as well as with oxidative stress and in response to various drugs.9–22 Still, ELM-RPE thickness does not directly measure subretinal space acidosis. Thus, we cannot rule out that some other step(s) in the pathway between mitochondrial respiration and subretinal hydration are disturbed in rd7 mice. One possibility is that sodium or potassium handling could be abnormal in rd7 mice, a situation that could lead to atypical subretinal water handling. More work is needed to continue to test the contributions of subretinal space acidosis (e.g. with ammonium chloride76,77), mitochondrial activity, and alternative mechanisms (e.g. monovalent ion hypothesis).

Another limitation of this work is that rrMCP/AR does not interrogate individual mitochondria and their activity. Nonetheless, rrMCP/AR is useful for evaluating the mitochondrial configuration within photoreceptors because it tracks with EM and metabolic efficiency in mice with and without Nnt.9,45 In this study, the EM results suggest that both mitochondrial distribution and shape contribute to the lower rrMCP/AR in rd7 mice.

Conclusions

After implementing novel noninvasive image processing to suppress the impact of anatomic irregularities in dystrophic retina, we provided evidence that rd7 photoreceptor inner segment mitochondria are overall more widely distributed (rrMCP/AR and EM) and individually more compact (per EM) than in WT mice. The wider distribution of compacted mitochondria should indicate higher activity with subretinal space acidosis/contracted ELM-RPE, a well-established signaling pathway.10,14,15,18,47 Our other major finding is that contracted ELM-RPE is linked to unchanged or reduced MCP/AR, which occurs in mice with efficient mitochondria (129S6/ev and C57BL/6NTac) in contrast to the higher MCP/AR that occurs in mice with inefficient mitochondria (C57BL/6J).10,26,45 Consistent with the above evidence for high metabolic activity/subretinal space acidosis, we note that neither ELM-RPE nor MCP/AR changes between light (presumed higher subretinal space pH) versus dark (presumed lower subretinal space pH) conditions, a pattern that also occurs in mice with efficient mitochondria (129S6/ev). Our results support the hypothesis that rd7 photoreceptors display more active and efficient mitochondria than those of WT mice.

In addition, we infer an active cone retinal visual cycle from the OCT data, in support of a previous conclusion based on electrophysiology.5 More work is needed to identify the source of inner retinal thickening also noted in this study. Intriguingly, combining rd7 mice with genetic photoreceptor degeneration models results in remarkable long-lasting neuroprotection.78–80 Our results raise the possibility that some combination of abundant and efficient photoreceptor mitochondria and/or an active retina visual cycle in rd7 mice contributes to photoreceptor survival in retinal degeneration models.78,81
