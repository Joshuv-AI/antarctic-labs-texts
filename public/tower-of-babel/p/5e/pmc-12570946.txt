Smoked out or chewed up: trends in tobacco consumption in India using National Family Health Survey data from 2015 to 2016 and 2019 to 2021
Authors: Ankeeta Menona Jacob, Jeni Jacob, Monisha Mary Mary P, Avinash K Shetty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo assess and compare the patterns of smoked and smokeless tobacco use in India and to identify demographic and socioeconomic factors associated with tobacco use through secondary data analysis of National Family Health Survey (NFHS-4) (2015–2016) and (NFHS-5) (2019–2021) datasets.Study design and settingA comparative weighted sample secondary data analysis was conducted using individual sampling weights in SPSS V.29.0, encompassing all 29 states and 7 union territories of India.ParticipantsA total of 8 11 808 individuals from NFHS-4 (699 686 women aged 15–49 years and 1 12 122 men aged 15–54 years) and 825 954 individuals from NFHS-5 (724 115 women aged 15–49 years and 101 839 men aged 15–54 years), were included in the analysis.Main outcome measuresChanges in smoked and smokeless tobacco use between the two survey rounds, stratified by age, gender, residency, socioeconomic status, and education.MethodsPrevalence estimates were summarised as percentages with 95% CIs. Differences between NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 were assessed using the Z test for proportions, with significance at p<0.05.ResultsBidi and cigarette use declined modestly among men, while gutkha/paan masala use showed the sharpest reduction among women. The overall prevalence of tobacco use declined from 45.5% to 40.8% among men (–4.7 points) and from 6.8% to 4.0% among women (–2.8 points). Bidi use showed the steepest reduction in the study. Among men, it was (14.9%–14.2%), while cigarette use declined modestly (13.7%–13.3%). Rare smoked forms such as pipe, hookah and cigar remained uncommon. Among women, the prevalence of smoked tobacco was negligible, whereas smokeless forms were more frequent but that too showed decline, with the largest reduction observed in gutkha/paan masala (2.2%–1.4%).ConclusionsTobacco use in India declined modestly between NFHS-4 and NFHS-5, with greater reductions in smoked than in smokeless forms. Nonetheless, smokeless tobacco remains more prevalent among women, and significant disparities persist across gender, rural–urban residence and socioeconomic strata.Keywords: Tobacco Use, Health Surveys, Smoking Reduction, Prevalence, PUBLIC HEALTH

Introduction

Tobacco smoking is linked to a considerable rise in a variety of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including cancer, heart disease, diabetes, lung disease, stroke, infertility, blindness and communicable diseases such as Tuberculosis (TB).1 The tobacco epidemic is one of the world’s most significant public health threats, killing more than 8 million people each year.2 3 The Global Adult Tobacco Survey (GATS) (2016–2017) estimated about 28.6% of people (15 years and over) in India use tobacco.4 India is the third-biggest tobacco-producing country and the second-largest consumer of tobacco in the world.5 Tobacco in India causes over a million adult fatalities annually, accounting for 9.5% of all deaths. Smokeless and non-smoking tobacco use is prevalent in 28.6% of adults, 42.4% of males and 14.2% of women, using tobacco.6 Among 270 million tobacco users in India, 200 million use smokeless tobacco (SLT), 100 million use smoked forms and 32 million users use both forms. Tripura has the greatest rate of tobacco usage (64.5%), followed by Mizoram (58.7%) and Manipur (55.1%).4 Despite the significant burden of tobacco and its related diseases, recent evidence on tobacco usage in India is limited to the GATS-2, 2016–2017. Other sources, such as the National Sample Survey (NSS) and the National Family and Health Survey (NFHS), have been used to direct policy changes and shape tobacco control interventions in India.

Studies on the NSS in 2016 have shown that in the poorest households’ use of tobacco was still prevalent. Still, the richest households showed a decline in tobacco use between 2000 and 2012. Bidi use declined overall, but cigarette and SLT use increased, especially among lower socioeconomic groups.7 A study on the GATS-2 survey in India on 13 329 respondents aged between 15 and 24 years (44.9% males, 55.1% females). 11.9% tobacco use was reported, with 5% smoking, 10.9% using SLT and 2% engaging in dual use.8 The study also reported higher odds of tobacco use among individuals aged 20–24 years, rural residents and unmarried individuals. Trends of tobacco use among Indian males from 1998 to 2021 have seen a decline in smoked forms but a plateau in smokeless forms of tobacco use.9 The NFHS-5 data showed that rural residents were 1.6 times more likely to smoke than urban residents. Age and gender had a profound bearing on smoking patterns.10 Studies conducted only on males using NFHS-5 data, like the study done by Kumari N et al, showed that males aged 45–54 years have significantly higher odds of smoking (2.5 times), smokeless use (1.4 times) and dual use (2.2 times) compared with younger males. Widowers, Scheduled Tribe males and manual labourers also showed a higher rate of tobacco use. Socioeconomic disadvantages—like low education, tribal or scheduled caste status and lower wealth—also significantly contribute to tobacco inequality.11

Another study analysing tobacco use in females using NFHS-5 data showed that poor education and literacy status was associated with tobacco use.3 Sharma et al reported that the reduction in tobacco use between GATS-1 (2009–2010) and GATS-2 was less pronounced among women compared with men. Strengthening tobacco control measures targeting women presents a vital opportunity to enhance their health outcomes and can further yield multigenerational benefits by positively influencing the health of their children.12 The existing literature thus focused on tobacco use in smoked or smokeless form, but lacks data tracking changes in patterns of tobacco use in recent years by means of nationally representative data. This study addresses these critical gaps by quantifying changes in both smoked and SLT use. This is product-specific and gender-disaggregated and with socioeconomic insights. It helps to identify high-risk population groups who may benefit greatly from targeted tobacco control interventions. The study aims to assess and compare the patterns of smoked and SLT usage in India using the secondary data analysis of NFHS-4 (2015–2016) and NFHS-5 (2019–2020) datasets. It reveals crucial areas for intervention and policy implementation for tobacco control in India due to the lack of current Census and GATS survey data.

Methodology

Data sources

An important survey in India that provides data on health and development that is typical of the whole country is the NFHS.13 The NFHS-4 (2015–2016) provides India’s comprehensive population, health and nutrition data. Conducted under the Government of India Ministry of Health and Family Welfare’s stewardship. It was led by the International Institute for Population Sciences. The survey received funding from the US Agency for International Development, the United Kingdom Department for International Development, the Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation and many others. The survey used a stratified two-stage sample strategy, including urban and rural regions from all states and union territories, assuring district, state and national representation.14 The NFHS-5 (2019–2021) provides India’s health, population and nutrition data, covering new topics like preschool education and disability.15

NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 surveys included variables of smoked tobacco (SFT), such as cigarettes, pipes, cigars, hookah, beedis and other forms of tobacco and SLT. The current study also examined SLT trends of chewing tobacco, using snuff, gutkha/paan masala with tobacco, paan with tobacco and khaini.

Study design

The study analysed secondary data from the NFHS-4 and 5, with approval from the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) Programme. After obtaining the necessary permits, secondary data on tobacco usage from NFHS IV (2015–2016) and V (2019–2020) were gathered from the DHS programme. The study permission was granted on 17 August 2022, via authorisation letter 172 162 from the DHS Programme, and data analysis was conducted between September 2022 and September 2023. This study involves human participants but was not approved by an Ethics Committee(s) or Institutional Board(s).

Study population

In NFHS-4, 28 586 primary sampling units (census blocks) were identified from 2011 census data—systematic random sampling selected 22 households per unit. Eligible participants included all women aged 15–49 and a random subsample of men aged 15–54, with a 98% household response rate and 92% individual response rate for men, totalling 112 122 interviews.14 Missing data handling was calculated for each type of tobacco use and sociodemographic variable to calculate the missing responses, which were found to be <1% and no systematic pattern was observed. NFHS-5 includes national, state/UT and district estimates gathered from 24 550 households, 28 468 women and 4174 men using Computer-Assisted Personal Interviewing on tobacco consumption.15

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Data analysis

Descriptive variables and operational definitions

The current study focuses on the comparative analysis of smoked and SLT usage. It emphasises variations across gender, urban–rural residency, state of residence, wealth index (quintiles) and socioeconomic status. In this study, the descriptive variables include self-reported information on tobacco consumption patterns, categorised into SFT (such as cigarettes, pipe, cigars, beedis and others) and SLT (including chewing tobacco, snuff, gutkha/paan masala with tobacco, paan with tobacco and khaini) as shown in table 1.

Operational definitions

Smoked tobacco

SFT includes Cigarettes, Cigarillos, Pipes, Bidis, Kreteks and Hookahs. Cigarette smoking is the most widespread kind of tobacco consumption globally.1

Smokeless tobacco

SLT comprises chewable, suckable or sniffable goods, including dip, snuff and chewing tobacco. Popular SLT products in India include khaini, gutkha, betel quid with tobacco and zarda.16 17 This study, the descriptive variables included self-reported information on tobacco consumption patterns, which were categorised into SFT (such as cigarettes, pipe, cigars, beedis and others) and SLT (including chewing tobacco, snuff, gutkha/paan masala with tobacco, paan with tobacco and khaini), as shown in table 1.

Socioeconomic status was measured by the wealth index, which is a composite index of ownership of household assets, characteristics of housing conditions and basic services accessibility. These are divided into five quintiles ranging from Poorest to Richest. The authors retained the DHS-provided quintiles without further modification, thus preserving national representativeness and comparability across the two surveys and previously published articles on tobacco consumption using DHS data.18 19

For descriptive, gender-disaggregated prevalence estimates, sampling weights (v005) were normalised by dividing by 1 000 000. Frequencies and weighted tabulations were performed using SPSS’s WEIGHT CASES command. The weighting procedure produces unbiased population estimates. No inferential statistics (eg, SEs, CIs) were reported.16 Since only descriptive statistics were used, which negated the use of SPSS’s Complex Sample module-related analyses. All the analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows V.29.0 (IBM, Armonk, New York). Tobacco-free population data from the study were used for the construction of the trends in tobacco use using data visualisation, since the total smoked and SLT consumption computation was not possible because of dual users of tobacco. These maps were created using Microsoft Excel for Microsoft 365 (Microsoft, Redmond, Washington), and for the Map chart .NET software was used. To visualise the tobacco prevalence changes between the 2020 and 2015 surveys of NFHS data sets for combined tobacco use for males and females in India, maps were generated using MapChart (https://www.mapchart.net/india.html, accessed on 8 August 2025). Geographic variations were colour-coded according to prevalence ranges, and prevalence values were overlaid on the maps using the Paint application in Windows to enhance interpretability. All analyses were weighted using the individual sampling weights provided in the NFHS datasets to ensure representativeness at the national and state levels. The descriptive nature of the analysis precluded the need for imputation, thereby enabling listwise deletion and complete case analysis of the SPSS data sets. The current study is in line with DHS methodological recommendations for reporting of descriptive statistics. This is because a negligible amount of missing data would adversely affect the estimated values. Adjustments for clustering and stratification, inherent in complex survey design, were not applied in this analysis. Missing data on tobacco-related variables were minimal (<1%) and excluded listwise; no imputation procedures were performed. Changes in prevalence between NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 were assessed using Z-tests for differences in proportions.

Trend of smoked form consumption between NFHS-4 and NFHS-5

Online supplemental tables 1 and 2 present the trends in SFT consumption among men and women in India between NFHS-4 (2015–2016) and NFHS-5 (2019–2021). Among men, the largest reductions were observed in bidi smoking, which declined from 14.3% to 13.3% (–1.0 percentage points), and cigarette smoking, which decreased from 13.7% to 13.3% (–0.4 points). Pipe use fell slightly (0.20% to 0.15%; –0.05 points), while the use of other smoked forms remained unchanged (0.5% in both rounds). In contrast, cigar use rose modestly (0.50% to 0.63%; +0.13 points) and hookah use showed a small increase (0.60% to 0.57%; +0.03 points).

Women consistently reported much lower levels of SFT use compared with men. Cigarette smoking remained very low and stable at 0.1% across both surveys. Bidi smoking showed a marked decline from 0.5% to 0.02% (–0.48 points), while hookah use halved from 0.2% to 0.1%. Cigar use, already rare, dropped from 0.1% to negligible levels, and other smoked forms decreased from 0.6% to 0.3% (–0.3 points).

Overall, these findings indicate that while Bidi and Cigarette use among men have declined modestly, rare forms such as Pipe, Hookah and Cigars remain uncommon with minor changes in consumption. Among women, the prevalence of all smoked forms was extremely low and declined further between NFHS-4 and NFHS-5, highlighting a persistent and widening gender gap in tobacco consumption in India.

Trend of smokeless form consumption between NFHS-4 and NFHS-5

As shown in online supplemental tables 1 and 2, the prevalence of SLT use declined significantly among both men and women between NFHS-4 (2015–2016) and NFHS-5 (2019–2021). Among men, the highest reduction was observed in gutkha/paan masala with tobacco, which declined from 14.9% to 14.2% (–0.7% points). Chewed tobacco and paan with tobacco also showed a significant decrease from 5.6% to 5.3% (–0.3 points). Khaini use declined modestly, while snuff remained negligible in both surveys. Although small decreases were also noted in Hookah and other smoked forms, these were not statistically significant. Among women, the prevalence of smokeless forms was higher than that of SFT, although still substantially lower than among men. The greatest decline was seen in gutkha/paan masala with tobacco, which fell from 2.2% to 1.4% (–0.8 points, statistically significant). Paan with tobacco and khaini use also showed notable reductions of 0.7% and 0.4%, respectively. Snuff remained negligible, and pipe use showed a marginal, non-significant increase.

Traditional forms such as chewing tobacco and snuff appear to be declining, likely reflecting the impact of targeted public health messaging and reduced social acceptability. However, gutkha/paan masala continues to represent the most prevalent form of SLT, underscoring the need for stricter enforcement of existing bans and culturally tailored interventions to accelerate reductions in this product.

Discussion

This study demonstrates a modest but consistent decline in both smoked and SLT use in India between NFHS-4 (2015–2016) and NFHS-5 (2019–2021), with reductions observed across most states and union territories. Declines were more pronounced among men, particularly for smoked forms, whereas women reported much lower overall prevalence but smaller reductions over time. Sociodemographic factors such as rural residence, lower education and lower wealth were significantly associated with higher tobacco use among both genders. These findings align with trends reported in the GATS (2016–2017) and other national studies, confirming a gradual downward trajectory in tobacco use. It reinforces the role of NFHS data as a critical tool for guiding tobacco control policy and programme implementation.17

The current study findings also complement the findings of Gupta et al, where there was a decreasing trend in tobacco consumption seen from the NFHS-2 to NFHS-5.9 A study conducted by Sarkar et al showed that rural residence and female gender were significantly associated with tobacco in the smoked form, which was similar to the present study findings.10 The present study findings are consistent with findings of Lahoti et al, which highlight the declining trend of tobacco consumption between the 2 GATS surveys, which has been a trend even in the NFHS-5. Reduction in tobacco consumption in any form was associated with better wealth index, women, urban residence and better educational status, which was also seen in the present study.20 The current study consolidates the comparison of SLT consumption among women from the GATS survey conducted by Verma et al and Ghosal et al, where there was a reduction in tobacco use among women. However, the current study records a <5% reduction in SLT consumption among women in the majority of the states in India.21 22 The findings of the current study are consistent with those of Grover et al based on secondary analysis of NFHS-4 data, reported a higher prevalence of tobacco use among pregnant women in rural areas compared with urban areas, and among those belonging to the poorest wealth quintile compared with wealthier groups. An inverse relationship between education and tobacco use was also observed.23 In a related study, Pasupuleti et al suggested that the widespread use of SLT among women in India may be attributed to factors such as social sanction, lower cost and easy availability. Furthermore, the cultural acceptance of tobacco, policy-related gaps and limited healthcare access contribute to the disproportionate impact of tobacco use on the health of rural populations.24

The current study findings, however, contradict the findings of Bhawna et al of GATS-I, which showed that khaini was the most popular form of tobacco after bidis. In contrast, the current study found that gutka/with paan masala was more commonly used by both males and females. There was a declining yet not significant reduction in khaini consumption.25 A study assessing the reduction in tobacco consumption after the implementation of the WHO’s Framework Convention on Tobacco Control found a declining trend in smoked and SLT use to the tune of 20.5% from 1987 to 2016. Furthermore, it shows the continual impact of this policy in the latest round of NFHS-5, as seen in the current study.26 According to Vidya et al, SLT is more widely used in India than SFT, primarily because it is more affordable and easily accessible, which is similar to the present study. The high consumption draws attention to the weaknesses in SLT control mechanisms and their lax enforcement, which are frequently caused by a lack of funding, capacity, training and research. Significant regional variations still exist despite India’s implementation of national and state-level tobacco control programmes, which resulted in a noteworthy drop in total tobacco usage between 2009–2010 and 2016–2017. Crucially, SLT has not been treated equally under many tobacco control laws that apply to smoking products.27 The WHO target for reduction in tobacco use is 30% by 2030, relative to 2015. While countries like Russia had a substantial reduction of 21.5%, countries like India and Bangladesh, in the Asian context, showed a modest reduction between 13.1% and 19.9% by 2016. This has further declined by 4.7% among men aged 15–54 and 2.8% among women aged 15–49 years in the latest round of NFHS-5.28

Several factors play a crucial role as potential drivers of observed trends in the current study findings. The implementation of the Cigarettes and Other Tobacco Products Act (COTPA) provisions, through the state tobacco control cells nationwide, may have influenced the declining trend. However, studies have also shown that the poor performance of the COTPA Act in terms of regular training schedules, poor engagement of stakeholders, civil societies and poor accountability may have contributed to a slower decline than expected, given the COTPA Act provisions.29 However, as seen in many studies, the COTPA Act implementation and enforcement intensity remain patchy across India. This might have contributed to the slow decline in the tobacco consumption rates and the state-wise variations in decline seen between the two surveys.30 Even though cigarettes were bought under the 28% Goods and Services Tax (GST) ambit, there was a slight decrease in affordability during the initial periods of implementation of this tax on cigarettes. However, the consumption rates did not decline post-GST implementation, despite the highest tax slab in India.31 However, a slight yet significant reduction in SLT and Bidi use was seen in states with high value-added taxes (VAT) like Uttar Pradesh, Karnataka and Maharashtra. Compared with the other states which paid lesser VAT, these were also the states which showed a decline of >5% especially among men and 0%–5% reduction in tobacco consumption in women in the present study.32 A price relative to income analysis has shown that cigarettes have become less affordable post-GST implementation. The more indigenous forms of tobacco, like bidis and chewed forms of tobacco, became more affordable in 2017–2018 compared with 2007–2008.33 However, even with this, a declining trend was seen in all forms of smoked and SLT products in the current study. Khaini, hookah and some other smoked forms showed an increase in consumption, which was not significant.

A study by Kanakaria et al highlights that comprehensive awareness of the ill effects of tobacco remains suboptimal among both users and non-users. Targeted interventions addressing men and individuals from the rural poorest quintiles should be integrated into the implementation and enforcement of COTPA provisions, as evidenced in the present study, which has similar findings.34

The state-wise variation in tobacco consumption warrants more targeted and specific strategies for control of tobacco, especially in states that have shown an upward trend in tobacco consumption.17 Using NFHS-5 data, Dutta et al found that the Northeastern states including Mizoram, Manipur and Tripura had the highest consumption of tobacco among women, whereas Punjab and Puducherry had the lowest amounts. Mizoram and Manipur had the highest prevalence of usage among men, while Puducherry and Punjab had the lowest prevalence.35 Tobacco control in the North Eastern states, though challenging, needs to be prioritised as the risk of other NCDs like cancers is very high.36 37 Goel et al investigated smoking patterns among Indian women over the age of 15 by analysing data from multiple significant, nationally representative surveys conducted between 1993 and 2009, such as the NSS, NFHS and GATS. Between 2005 and 2010, they discovered that female smoking almost doubled during that time. Additionally, there was a clear negative relationship between literacy and smoking rates: women with no formal education smoked significantly more than those with more years of education.38 According to Jose et al, women in rural areas were more likely to smoke, whereas women in urban areas were more likely to use tobacco in any form, especially SLT. compared with women in upper economic categories, those in the lower wealth index quartiles (poorest and poorest) were more likely to smoke. Compared with women with primary, secondary or higher education, illiterate women similarly exhibited greater rates of tobacco use. Both SLT use and smoking were significantly correlated with factors like education, wealth, religion and tribal status. The highest prevalence of tobacco usage was recorded by women in the northeastern regions, while the lowest prevalence was reported by women in the southern states. These regional variances could be explained by disparities in cultural customs, social acceptance and the unequal application of tobacco control policies nationwide.18

Pathak et al reported that bidi smoking was concentrated among the poorest sections, whereas cigarette use was more frequent among wealthier groups, reflecting affordability differences—since both the median and minimum price of bidis in India are substantially lower than those of cigarettes. GATS-2 data also showed that smoking was more common in rural regions, most likely because of rural inhabitants’ extensive participation in bidi rolling and tobacco farming, which raises their risk and exposure. Since solutions must also take into account these communities’ reliance on the tobacco business for their livelihood, addressing this issue is still difficult.39 Gender-sensitive strategies may also be needed in most of the states, given the lower rate of decrease of tobacco consumption seen in the current study, especially for SLT.40 Gupta et al, analysing NFHS men’s data over a 22-year period (1998–2021), found that SLT continues to be the most widely used form in India, with its prevalence showing little change between NFHS-4 and NFHS-5. Khaini and chewing tobacco mixed with paan-masala or gutkha were identified as the most used SLT products, while cigarettes remained the predominant SFT product, a pattern consistent across all NFHS rounds. The use of both SLT and SFT was negatively associated with education and wealth, with lower prevalence observed among more educated and economically better-off groups. The persistence of SLT use reflects its deep cultural embedding, being tied to social status and identity, with practices such as offering and sharing SLT serving as a form of social bonding. In addition, its affordability and easy accessibility contribute to its continued widespread use. These findings highlight the need for strengthening existing measures with region-specific and subgroup-focused policies to address the most vulnerable populations.9 COTPA Act-related enforcement and implementation may be needed to supplement the higher rates of GST on tobacco products.30 32 There is a need for culturally sensitive community-based tobacco cessation strategies that can help in further reduction of tobacco consumption through the National Tobacco Control Programme, with a special focus on rural areas.41 Community-based culturally tailored programmes integrating pharmacotherapy, Nicotine Replacement Therapies and Cognitive Behavioural Therapies would be key to achieving substantial reductions in further rounds of the tobacco survey.41

Limitations

Several methodological considerations warrant attention. Although survey weights were applied, clustering and stratification were not adjusted for, which may have led to differences in the CIs and p values reported, which need to be interpreted especially when looking at statistical significance. Missing data were minimal and excluded listwise; however, no imputation was conducted. While NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 are both large, nationally representative surveys, differences in sampling strategy, timing of data collection and question wording across survey waves may influence the direct comparability of estimates. Self-reported tobacco use is subjected to recall and social desirability bias, which may be greater among women. These limitations may potentially underestimate the true prevalence of the actual use of tobacco in these groups. Reasons for the decline seen in tobacco consumption across the two surveys were not explored, which may hold keys to understanding sociocultural aspects in designing culturally appropriate interventions. However, these trends may not diminish the trends observed but may affect the comparability of the results of the study findings.

Future directions

The study findings echo the need for strengthening tobacco control efforts in India, with an impetus on smokeless forms than smoked forms, as seen in the study, where the decline in smokeless forms was much lower and more prevalent among the rural population and those who are weaker economically. Strengthening the implementation of legislative measures under the COTPA Act, especially for smokeless forms like ghutka and khaini, is needed. Health education measures need to address any myths and false beliefs regarding SLT. Also, the reasons for higher use among women, along with qualitative studies, are recommended—to understand the cultural and behavioural determinants of tobacco use. Surveillance on tobacco consumption is needed in further NFHS rounds, where sociocultural factors affecting their use will also be captured.
