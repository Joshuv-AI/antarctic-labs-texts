How to Use Quantum Chemistry for Analyzing Mass Spectrometry Data
Authors: Kim Greis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The gas-phase environment of a mass spectrometer is the ideal system to benchmark advanced computational methods, as the properties of ionic species derived from mass spectrometry experiments are identical to the target of (quantum) computational predictions. This is particularly applicable for gas-phase ion spectroscopy techniques, where lasers are interfaced with mass spectrometers to gain further information about isolated ions. Complementing experimental results with computational findings allows one to increase the value of the results and to better interpret the data. The calculation of isolated particles in the gas phase is straightforward and requires fewer approximations than more complex systems. This review serves to give a brief overview of where quantum chemistry and mass spectrometry mutually benefit each other. The main topics covered in this review include fragmentation methods, ion–molecule reactions, ion mobility-mass spectrometry, and ion spectroscopy methods.

INTRODUCTION

The fields of mass spectrometry (MS) and quantum chemistry (QC) can benefit tremendously from each other.1,2) This account is general in nature but not exhaustive, and it reflects a personal perspective. The object of interest in both fields is almost identical: isolated particles in the gas phase. Isolated gas-phase particles require the least number of approximations in QC. Condensed-phase calculations, on the other hand, are typically more complex and require further approximations, as more than a single molecule needs to be considered to represent such systems.

This review should encourage people to apply quantum-chemical methods to complement their experimental MS data. While a certain knowledge of QC is helpful, a deep understanding of QC is not required to get started applying these methods. Likewise, programming skills and experience with high-performance computers (HPCs) are always helpful, but not strictly necessary. In more complex cases, it is often possible to reach out to local QC research groups to start collaborations.

To assess how reliable a computational method can predict some molecular properties, such as dissociation energies, vibrational frequencies, or fluorescence emission spectra, high-quality experimental data of the exact system are necessary. MS data of isolated ions is most suitable for benchmarking computational methods. The gas phase avoids the complications of strong intermolecular interactions, which would be important to accurately describe solution-phase measurements.

QC calculations are complementary to MS and can help interpret MS results. An advantage of MS compared to the solution phase is that it can be used to generate reactive species, which are stable inside the mass spectrometer in the absence of reactants.3,4) However, a mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) can be ambiguous, and in certain cases it is not obvious what the exact molecular structure of a fragment ion is, even if the structure of the precursor ion is known.5) In this work, a rough guideline will be presented to showcase some of the available QC methods and how they can benefit MS experiments, covering fragmentation methods, ion–molecule reactions (IMRs), ion mobility-MS (IM-MS), and ion spectroscopy (Fig. 1). Most of what is written in this review paper is true for organic molecules, while some applications to inorganic compounds, such as metals and metallic clusters are covered as well.

OVERVIEW OF COMPUTATIONAL CHEMISTRY METHODS

QC tools have become increasingly available and user-friendly. This section gives a broad overview of some basic knowledge on computational methods before introducing the specifics useful for MS applications. Due to the rapid development of HPCs in the past decades, QC calculations have experienced a tremendous increase in speed. Ab initio methods, such as coupled cluster (CC),6) Møller–Plesset (MP) perturbation,7) or density functional theory (DFT)8,9) provide accurate results for a large range of systems. The computing time of QC methods typically scales polynomially with the system size. When N is the total number of basis functions used in the calculation, the computational cost increases for CC methods, MP2, and hybrid DFT with N6–7, N5, and N3–4, respectively.10) However, due to their computational expense, their applicability for large systems quickly becomes limited. Due to its favorable scaling behavior, DFT is the most widely used method, and depending on the exact method chosen, it can provide reliable results for systems containing hundreds of atoms at a reasonable computational cost.

Besides QC methods, the computational chemistry toolbox is complemented by force field methods. In contrast to the previously mentioned methods, they are based on classical physics and depend on empirical parameters, which speeds up the calculation by several orders of magnitude (typically scales with N), but also reduces accuracy. In combination with MS, their usage can mainly be recommended for conformational sampling or low-level screening. As computational expense scales with the system size, larger biomolecular systems can only be studied with force field methods, considering current computational power. A tradeoff between QC and force field methods is given by semi-empirical methods, which rely on QC principles, but approximate large parts of the calculation with empirical parameters. Semi-empirical methods can offer a compromise between speed and accuracy and are therefore also popular for conformational sampling.

As of now, there are dozens of QC software packages available. While a lot of basic functions are similar, there is a huge discrepancy in their specialized functions as well as in their user-friendliness. This work should not serve to advertise a specific QC package, as all of them have their raison-d'être. Some commonly used QC software packages are Gaussian (Gaussian Inc, Wallingford, CT, USA),11) ORCA (FAccTs GmbH, Cologne, Germany),12) and Turbomole (TURBOMOLE GmbH, Karlsruhe, Germany).13) A more complete list of QC software packages and their respective advantages is found in the literature.14)

When it comes to the prediction of accurate geometries and molecular properties, DFT methods are often the best QC methods that can be applied at a reasonable computational expense. There are several categories of DFT methods grouped according to Perdew’s “Jacob’s ladder” (Fig. 2A).15) Generally, a higher category performs better than a lower category. The lowest category is the local-density approximation, which assumes a uniform electron density. To improve this, the generalized-gradient approximation (GGA) introduced the gradient of the density. GGA already performs reasonably well on many systems and has been further improved by the introduction of meta-GGA, which also considers the kinetic energy density. DFT methods became significantly more popular upon the introduction of hybrid DFT methods, which calculate part of the exchange energy with Hartree–Fock, a wavefunction-based method. On its own, Hartree–Fock typically performs less well. Hybrid DFT methods are among the most used QC methods, especially by scientists who are not theoretical chemists. They are often employed to calculate molecular geometries, spectra, and other properties relevant for MS studies. DFT methods have been further improved by introducing double-hybrid DFT, which adds perturbative correlation based on MP2. Double-hybrid functionals are already too expensive beyond calculating single-point energies for most systems of interest in MS studies. Both hybrid and double-hybrid DFT functionals can reach “chemical accuracy”, which is defined in the caption of Fig. 2B. Within a specific category of the “Jacob’s ladder”, the performance of functionals cannot be ranked in an obvious way, and often the performance is dependent on the system and the application.

In terms of QC methods, hybrid DFT methods are most likely sufficient for most applications concerning organic molecules in their ground state. Once a system contains unpaired electrons, as is often the case in systems containing metals or metal clusters, the quantum-chemical method needed to accurately describe the research question becomes significantly more expensive.16) More complex systems can be calculated using more advanced methods, such as CC17) methods or complete active space self-consistent field (CASSCF) methods.18) However, these are often computationally expensive and are more challenging to set up. Therefore, they can only be applied to small systems, and their integration into automated workflows is challenging. Promising approximations are available,19,20) but limitations to larger systems remain.

Typically, quantum-chemical workflows (Fig. 3) start with building a geometry corresponding to the scientific question. A geometry can be automatically built using Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES),21) but for beginners using a software for molecular visualization like GaussView22) or Avogadro23) (there are many others available) is more convenient. For larger biomolecules or polymers, building a geometry becomes significantly more challenging, and there are some tools to automatically add amino acid or nucleotide residues. Avogadro, which is freely available, also contains a tool for rough force field optimization of the assembled geometry. Using a reasonable geometry to start the workflow will save computational time and generally reduces failure during calculation. Besides using software, a geometry can also be obtained from cryo-electron microscopy or crystal structures in databases.

Steps shown with dashed lines are optional and may be omitted depending on the focus of the study. CCS, collision cross section; MS, mass spectrometry.

While the three-dimensional structures of rigid small molecules are often straightforward, those of flexible molecules are not. Depending on the size, they can adopt a perhaps countless number of conformations. To obtain the chemically most reasonable and energetically most stable conformation, it is recommended to sample the conformational space of the molecule of interest. There are several tools available for this endeavor. A tool that has become quite popular for conformational sampling using semi-empirical methods is CREST,24,25) which is freely available. Besides conformational sampling, it also offers sampling of protonation, deprotonation, and metalation sites, which are highly relevant for MS studies. CREST relies on the affordable semi-empirical and force field methods implemented in the xtb software.26) All structures generated by CREST are optimized at the chosen (semi-) empirical level. Besides CREST, it is also worth mentioning the conformational sampling tool GOAT,27) which is implemented in ORCA12) and yields similar results to CREST. Besides the xtb methods, GOAT can also be run using DFT methods, which improves sampling quality, but drastically increases computing times. In certain cases, conformational sampling by approaches like CREST and GOAT might overlook conformers due to insufficient sampling of the conformational space. The use of deep-learning-based force fields has been proposed to overcome this challenge.28,29)

After having successfully sampled the conformational space of the ion of interest, the next step is structure optimization. The system size dictates the method that can be used for higher-level structure optimization. Usually, this would be done with hybrid DFT. However, there are numerous DFT functionals that can be used, and navigating through this jungle can be quite confusing for beginners. Fortunately, there are best practice guides15) and benchmark studies30) that can help choosing the right functional and basis set for a specific research question. Two popular functionals are PBE031) or B3LYP.32,33) It is recommended to check benchmarking studies, and if enough computational resources are available, one should not always rely on one specific functional but instead test several methods.30,34,35)

In addition to choosing a DFT functional, most QC methods require selecting a basis set as well. In a simplified way, a basis set defines the set of spatial functions used to represent molecular orbitals. Therefore, choosing a “larger” basis set systematically improves the quality of the calculations. For most MS studies, it makes little sense to go beyond triple zeta basis sets, such as def2-TZVP.36) A triple zeta basis set uses three functions to describe each atomic orbital. The scaling of the computational cost with respect to the number of basis functions was discussed in the overview section of this review. Besides the Ahlrichs basis sets (e.g., those starting with def2), Pople37) and Dunning38) basis sets are also popular. While results of good quality can be obtained with both, one needs to consider that Pople basis sets were developed in a pre-DFT era, and Dunning basis sets are mainly devised for wavefunction-based methods, such as CC. Some DFT methods, such as the composite DFT functionals B97-3c39) and r2SCAN-3c,40) do not require choosing a basis set, as the basis set is already integrated into and optimized for the method. Additionally, DFT typically does not include long-range electron correlation. Therefore, it is recommended to add some kind of dispersion correction, such as the D3(BJ)/D4 corrections41–44) implemented in various QC software packages.

After having obtained an optimized conformer ensemble, the following steps are dependent on the research question and will therefore be discussed in the respective sections, where selected examples with literature citations will be given. This article is not supposed to exhaustively discuss all literature related to QC in MS but should give an overview of where QC can be usefully applied in MS, while citing relevant literature for the interested reader.

MS METHODS

Over the past decades, MS has become an established technique in industrial and academic analytical chemistry laboratories. While MS is most known for measuring the m/z value of analytes, the core strength of MS lies in its versatility and in what is possible beyond measuring m/z values.45–47) The MS methods discussed herein include fragmentation methods, IMRs, IM-MS, and various ion spectroscopy methods, such as infrared (IR), ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis), and fluorescence ion spectroscopy. There are additional ion spectroscopy methods, such as anion photoelectron spectroscopy studying metal complexes,48) small organic molecules,49) and large biomolecules,50) X-ray ion spectroscopy of proteins51) and metal oxides,52) as well as circular dichroism ion spectroscopy.53) A broad overview of more ion spectroscopy methods is found in the literature.54) However, they are not the focus of this review. Furthermore, there have been other exciting extensions to the MS toolbox in recent years that will not be covered here, such as charge detection MS,55–57) soft landing of ions,58–61) or multistage fragmentation using the Omnitrap.62,63)

Fragmentation mass spectra and IMRs

Fragmentation methods are implemented in most commercial MS instruments. Through fragmentation, relevant structural information about analyte ions beyond m/z can be obtained. In the case of biomolecules, it can be used to determine large fractions of a biomolecular sequence.64–66) Over the years, a plethora of fragmentation methods has been developed and reviewed in the literature.67–69) Here, the focus lies on some commonly used methods. Collision-induced dissociation (CID) is likely the most commercially accessible fragmentation method. Additionally, electron- and photon-based activation methods, such as electron-capture dissociation (ECD), electron-transfer dissociation, UV photodissociation (UVPD), and IR multiple photon dissociation (IRMPD) can be found on commercial platforms.45,67) These methods are more challenging to implement, as they involve alignment of an ion beam with an electron or photon beam. CID, on the other hand, can be induced by merely increasing the collision energy in a collision cell. However, because ECD and UVPD usually generate fragments different from those generated by CID, they are highly relevant for broad sequence coverage in biomolecules.70–74) Besides fragmentation methods, ion–molecule and ion–ion reactions can be used for deeper structural analysis.75,76) In MSn workflows, fragmentation methods and IMRs can be combined to obtain deeper insight into chemical reactivity in the gas phase.77,78)

In this section, how computational methods can be used to predict mass spectra is discussed. For large biomolecules, which often carry several charges, calculating which charge states can hypothetically be achieved in electrospray ionization (ESI) is straightforward. However, predicting the charge state distribution in the mass spectrum is more challenging. Recently, a molecular dynamics model was developed that accurately predicts charge state distributions for large proteins in positive and negative ion modes (Fig. 4A).79) Considering the complexity of the ESI process, the excellent agreement achieved in this study is quite remarkable. This agreement is achieved by simulating the ESI droplets with “experimentally relevant droplet compositions and behavior”, allowing for proton transfer and containing ammonium acetate. It should be noted that this approach is not rooted in QC, but uses force field molecular dynamics. Accurately modeling the ESI process would require considering not only the large protein, which is already hardly feasible for QC methods, but also surrounding water and buffer molecules.

Besides predicting charge state distributions in ESI, tools have been developed to predict fragmentation mass spectra during electron impact ionization (EI) and CID.80–87) These tools are often used in combination with semi-empirical methods, such as those from the xtb package, as is the case for QC × MS and its successor QC × MS2 (Fig. 4B). Comparison with experimental data shows that these tools can indeed accurately predict fragmentation patterns88,89) and also provide reasonable suggestions for fragment ion structures.90) Additionally, products of isomeric rearrangement reactions can be predicted by the GOAT-REACT tool, implemented in ORCA.27)

Beyond the prediction of fragmentation mass spectra and fragment ion structures, QC is very useful to map the energetics of reaction trajectories and related transition states (TSs). The combination of IMRs with fragmentation methods (mainly CID) in MSn instruments is very powerful to comprehensively understand the fundamental reactivity of molecular ions in the gas phase and how it compares to the solution phase. Additionally, with CID, ionic intermediates can be generated, which would not be stable in the condensed phase.91–93) In the vacuum, these species persist long enough for their reactivity to be probed with IMRs. A special type of IMR is ozonolysis, where analyte ions react with ozone, which is particularly useful to differentiate double-bond positions in isomeric lipids.94,95)

QC calculations can help to assess the likelihood of a mechanism in the gas phase. In certain cases, there is more than one possible pathway leading from the reactants to the products, and it is not always intuitive to assess which one is more likely to occur.96) With DFT calculations, it can be determined by which TSs and intermediates reactants and products are linked and what the energetics of each step are. Besides electronic energies, enthalpy and Gibbs energies are accessible through frequency calculations. Experimental reaction energies can be obtained via guided ion beam MS97) studies, which have been reported with computed results for hydration energies,98,99) decomposition of peptides,100,101) and many others.

TSs are traditionally challenging to localize and optimize using computational methods. For a minimum on the potential energy surface (PES, reactants, products, intermediates), optimization from a similar starting structure is usually sufficient to localize it (Fig. 5). This is because geometry optimizations compute the energy gradient, which indicates the required change in geometry to reduce the energy. The structure is then iteratively adjusted to yield an energetic minimum. The most straightforward way to confirm whether an optimized structure is indeed a minimum is to do a frequency calculation. The idea is derived from calculus, where one identifies stationary points using the first derivatives and then uses the second derivatives to determine whether the point is a minimum or maximum. In QC, the second derivatives form the Hessian matrix and vibrational frequencies are obtained from the square roots of the eigenvalues of the mass-weighted Hessian. If a structure has no imaginary frequencies, it corresponds to a true minimum. TSs, on the other hand, are located at saddle points of the PES, which means that unless a starting structure is very close to the TS, it would never optimize into the TS geometry. Additionally, the calculation must be instructed to locate a saddle point. This requires evaluating the force constants at the beginning of the calculation to avoid relaxation into a minimum geometry.

Fig. 5. Exemplified potential energy diagram (one-dimensional simplification of a PES) obtained during a scan of a reaction coordinate. Example geometries are shown as red data points. Optimization of the geometries 1, 2, and 3 would lead to the reactants, whereas optimization of the geometries 5, 6, and 7 would lead to the products. Optimization of 4 would only lead to a transition state, if it is optimized as a transition state, otherwise it would relax into either the reactant or the product geometry. PES, potential energy surface.

There are several methods to locate a TS. A commonly employed method, which is typically available in QC software, involves scanning a coordinate. When a coordinate involved in the reaction is chosen (e.g., breaking or forming a chemical bond), then the obtained curve, when plotting the energy as a function of the bond distance, often exhibits a maximum (Fig. 5). This curve can be treated as a one-dimensional approximation of the PES. The apparent maximum would actually be a saddle point on the 3N-6-dimensional PES and therefore be geometrically very close to a potential TS. Depending on how close it is, its optimization will lead to an optimized TS. In some cases, when optimizing the TS is challenging, scanning the area around the maximum with a finer step size to get as close to the TS geometry as possible is recommended. It is important to carefully monitor the optimization steps, as it is not unlikely that the TS geometry will optimize into a minimum structure during optimization. The frequencies of the potential TS geometry indicate whether it is indeed a TS, a minimum, or a quantum chemically irrelevant geometry. A TS has one imaginary frequency (also called a negative frequency), which represents the vibrational mode involved in forming or breaking a bond, while all other frequencies are positive. As discussed before, a minimum would only have positive frequencies and a structure with multiple imaginary frequencies is likely not fully optimized. Additionally, it is worth noting that there are additional methods to calculate TSs like QST2 or QST3, which usually provide reactant and product geometries and, for QST3, also a guessed TS geometry as input.102)

Localizing TSs and determining reaction energies is popular in gas-phase studies on organometallic complexes. For example, it was reported that gas-phase bipyridine ligated platinum complexes can undergo “rollover”-cyclometallation, whereas the respective palladium and nickel complexes do not engage in this type of reactivity.103) For similar palladium complexes equipped with a diphosphine ligand, two competing reactions occur in the gas phase: (1) β-methyl elimination and (2) Pd–C bond homolysis (Fig. 6).104) Rationalizing these fragmentation pathways by DFT shows that in this process highly reactive species are formed, which can subsequently be used in potentially catalytic IMRs for the activation of small molecules. It was shown that phenanthroline-ligated metal complexes can be activated in the gas phase and subsequently be used to decompose acetone.91,96) A large number of organometallic reactions occurring through CID and IMRs inside of mass spectrometers were reported.77,105–107) In particular, activation of inert small molecules like carbon dioxide or nitrogen by metal complexes or clusters was shown by combining IMR experiments with DFT calculations.108–115) In addition to organometallic reactions, organic reactions of reactive species can be conducted and modelled using this methodology.116–120) Besides IMRs, the energetics of ion–ion reactions were modelled with DFT calculations.121,122)

IM-MS

IM-MS has been commercially available for 20 years and has since then become a key method in MS. Most major MS vendors hold some sort of IM patent and sell their respective implementations of IM-MS instruments.45,123) While the detailed mechanism of each IM method is different, the core principle usually remains the same: ions are separated by interacting with a drift gas, not dissimilar to chromatographic separations.124) One of the key strengths of IM-MS is its ability to differentiate isomeric and isobaric species that cannot be resolved by MS alone.125,126) Besides, for larger molecules IM-MS can also separate conformers and thereby give structural insight into the folding behavior of, e.g., proteins.127–129) The separation efficiency increases with increasing drift path. Therefore, cyclic implementations of IM-MS, such as cyclic travelling wave IM-MS130) and structures for lossless ion manipulation (SLIM) IM-MS,131) are extremely powerful for separating isomers and conformers.

Besides separating isomers, IM-MS also allows us to extract collision cross sections (CCSs) from drift times, which are instrument-independent parameters unique to the probed ion and can be stored in databases as well as computationally modelled.132–134) A CCS depends on the employed drift gas (e.g., nitrogen or helium). CCSs can be determined without calibration with drift-tube IM-MS and with calibration for travelling-wave IM-MS and trapped IM-MS (Fig. 7). It is not physically possible to determine CCSs with differential MS or field-asymmetric IM-MS (FAIMS). However, it was shown recently that using a machine learning (ML)-based calibration, CCSs of unknown analytes can even be extracted from these techniques, with an error of only 2.6%.135) The authors of the study noted that this approach only works for molecules which are similar in size and in chemical class to those that were used in the training set of the ML model. Furthermore, it has been shown that CCSs can be determined in Orbitrap136–138) and Fourier transform ion cyclotron resonance139–141) MS instruments, without the need for a designated ion-mobility element. A challenge regarding accurate CCS determination with calibration is that the calibrant set should be structurally similar to the investigated species, both in terms of chemical properties (e.g., for peptides a peptide calibrant should be chosen) and charge state, which adds another layer of complexity to calibrating IM data. Several niche calibrants have been suggested,142–146) and improved calibrant-independent calibration methods have been proposed for travelling wave IM-MS.147) There are large databases containing experimental CCSs, which can be used for annotating peaks in untargeted analysis.148–150) When there is no match in the database, computed CCSs can be used for annotation,151) provided that structural candidates are distinguishable by CCS.

A CCS can be determined computationally. While CCS calculations applying QC methods would be ideal, the computational expense is currently too high due to the number of trajectories that need to be considered in such calculations. Fortunately, force fields provide sufficient accuracy for most applications. Traditionally, there are three main methods to determine a CCS computationally (Fig. 8). The projection approximation (PA) and the elastic hard sphere scattering (EHSS) method can be used to rapidly calculate a CCS based on a molecular geometry. Briefly, in PA, a CCS is approximated by rotationally averaging the projected area of the molecule. It does not consider the ion-neutral interaction potential and therefore underestimates CCSs. EHSS additionally considers the scattering effects of the drift gas molecules. A more reliable and physically more accurate way to compute CCSs is the trajectory method (TM). Besides considering the geometry, it also considers partial charges of the atoms and properly treats long- and short-range interactions. The long- and short-range interaction potentials in TM are typically calculated using force field methods, such as the MMFF94 force field.152) A more detailed introduction into computing CCSs and the physical background can be found in the literature.133) Besides, it is nowadays possible to rapidly predict CCSs with ML.153,154) However, when using such methods, one should always be cautious and ideally compare the results with those from more established methods.

CCS, collision cross section; EHSS/DHSS, exact/discrete hard-sphere scattering; PA, projection approximation; TM, trajectory method.

Several programs are freely available to compute CCSs with the TM. Some popular ones are MobCal,156,157) hpccs,158) iMoS,159) or Collidoscope.160) To obtain a good geometry for CCS calculations, DFT optimizations are typically used for small to mid-sized molecules. There are different schemes available for calculating partial charges. It is typically discouraged to use Mulliken charges, while it is recommended to use Merz–Kollman–Singh161) or CHELPG162) charges instead. Furthermore, atomic polar tensor charges are reliable but expensive to calculate, as they require a frequency calculation. For large molecules, structures are often optimized and their partial charges computed with force field or semi-empirical methods. A common starting structure for large biomolecules would be their crystal structure, which can be found in databases.

CCS calculations have been used by various research groups to make structural assignments for both very small and large molecules.163) Mean absolute errors as low as 2% have been reported between experimental and predicted CCSs.157) Errors depend on the drift gas, system size, and chosen CCS prediction algorithm. Current challenges involve accurate parametrization of the TM in specific drift gases, potentially leading to deviations from experiment, and hence the use of scaling factors.145,164,165) Current methods enable reliable prediction in targeted and untargeted analysis.149,166–168)

Computed CCSs have not only been useful for assignments in untargeted analysis but can also be highly useful for distinguishing protomers,169–171) differentiating isomeric glycans (Fig. 9A),172–175) elucidating fragmentation mechanisms in MS studies,176) and for studying biomolecular ions177–181) or metal-containing compounds (Fig. 9B).155,164,182–186) To further strengthen structural assignments of analyte ions, IR ion spectroscopy yields unprecedented information about gas-phase ions, as detailed in the next section.

Gas-phase ion spectroscopy

The history of gas-phase ion spectroscopy is almost as long as that of IM-MS, but because its implementation is rather challenging, it has not been fully commercialized yet.187) Ion spectroscopy enhances the information that can be obtained from an analyte ion to an unparalleled degree. Due to Coulomb repulsion, the ion density is generally too low to measure a typical absorption spectrum of trapped ions. Therefore, it is not the absorption of light that is measured in ion spectroscopy experiments, but the effect of light on the m/z-selected species (Fig. 10). Such effects caused by photons can be, for example, photofragmentation of the trapped ions or the emission of fluorescent photons. Monitoring this effect as a function of the wavelength of the laser results in a spectrum.45,46) Accordingly, the umbrella term for these types of spectroscopies is action spectroscopy. Although such setups are not commercially available, action spectroscopy techniques have become increasingly popular over the past two decades.46,54)

(A) Absorption spectroscopy: The Lambert–Beer law relates the transmitted light intensity I at a given wavelength λ to the incident intensity I0, the absorption cross section σ, the path length L, and the particle density n. (B) Action spectroscopy: The corresponding expression, derived from the Lambert–Beer law, relates the number of unaffected ions N at a specific wavelength λ to the initial number of precursor ions N0, the absorption cross section σ, and the photon fluence ϕ. There are several reasons why ion spectroscopy setups are more complex than regular mass spectrometers. Besides a mass spectrometer, a tunable laser is necessary as a light source. Operating such a laser requires expert knowledge and elevates the safety requirements. Additionally, aligning an ion beam with a laser beam can be challenging, especially when setting up such an instrument for the first time. The company MS Vision (Almere, Netherlands) has converted second-hand commercial (IM) mass spectrometers into ion spectroscopy instruments.188–190) Cryogenic ion traps have also become commercially available through Isospec Analytics SA (Renens, Switzerland), an EPFL spin-off that applies cryogenic infrared ion spectroscopy (sometimes referred to as CIRIS) to the structural identification of glycans, metabolites, and other small molecules.190–192) In a separate industrial–academic collaboration, an IRMPD spectroscopy instrument was set up in an analytical laboratory of the pharmaceutical company Johnson & Johnson (Beerse, Belgium).193) Due to the recent progress in this field and the related technologies, commercialization might be possible within the next few years. Besides IR action spectroscopy, this review will also touch upon UV-vis and fluorescence ion spectroscopy.

Infrared ion spectroscopy

IR ion spectroscopy is likely the most widely used ion spectroscopy method and has been implemented in a range of laboratories around the world.194–211) As the name states, it allows to obtain IR spectra of the analyte ions. It can be conducted at room temperature (IRMPD spectroscopy) and at cryogenic temperatures (either IR photodissociation/messenger-tagging or helium droplet IR spectroscopy).45,46) In the case of IRMPD, the fragmentation yield is monitored as a function of the IR wavenumber (Fig. 11A). In cryogenic messenger-tagging IR spectroscopy, the dissociation of a tagging molecule, such as N2, from the analyte ion is monitored to generate an IR spectrum (Fig. 11B).45) The temperature matters, as significantly narrower bands can be obtained at lower temperatures.212,213) The lowest temperature at which IR spectra of biomolecular ions were obtained at is likely 0.37 K, which is the temperature of superfluid helium nanodroplets, a matrix that is used in cryogenic IR spectroscopy (Fig. 11C).214)

Fig. 11. Comparison of IR action spectroscopy methods. (A) IRMPD spectroscopy: The instrumentation and operating principle rely on resonant irradiation, where absorption of multiple IR photons excites the intact parent ion until it reaches the fragmentation threshold. Both fragment ions and remaining parent ions are subsequently detected as a function of the wavenumber. (B) Messenger (tagging) spectroscopy: Ions are tagged with weakly bound buffer-gas atoms or molecules, such as N2, H2, He, or Ar, in a cryogenic ion trap. Upon resonant absorption, typically of a single photon, the tag detaches, and the resulting bare ion is detected. (C) Cryogenic spectroscopy in helium nanodroplets: Ions are captured by helium nanodroplets in an ion trap and cooled to 0.37 K. Resonant irradiation excites the ion, which is then rapidly re-cooled by evaporative cooling of the droplet. After repeated excitation–cooling cycles, the ion is released from the droplet and detected. Reprinted with permission from reference.45) Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International License. From a computational chemistry point of view, simulating IR spectra is straightforward, if the molecule is not too large. In the case of flexible molecules, it is advisable to sample their conformational space and screen multiple candidates as outlined in the computational section.212,215,216) To obtain an IR spectrum of a geometry-optimized molecular ion, one needs to calculate the harmonic frequencies, ideally at the same level of theory that was used for the optimization. The harmonic frequencies can be obtained through the Hessian matrix, which represents the second derivatives of the energy with respect to the nuclear coordinates. As the name implies, harmonic frequencies are derived from the harmonic oscillator approximation, while real molecular vibrations are anharmonic. To account for this, harmonic frequencies are typically scaled by an empirical scaling factor, which can differ between DFT methods. A typical scaling factor that has worked well for methods such as PBE0+D3/6-311+G(d,p),31) PBE0+D3/def2-TZVPP, or CAM-B3LYP/def2-TZVPP217) is 0.965 for the 1000–1800 cm−1 region.3,92) The scaling factor that would need to be applied in the 3000–3800 cm−1 region, dominated by N–H and O–H stretches, could be slightly different, as N–H and O–H typically exhibit increased anharmonic behavior compared to the rest of the molecule.212) Additionally, their anharmonicity is heavily influenced by H-bonds.

Another computationally significantly more expensive way of accounting for anharmonicities is to use anharmonic models like VPT2218) or GVPT2219) to perform the frequency calculation. Besides predicting the frequencies closer to their real value, these models also predict overtones and combination bands, which are forbidden in the harmonic approximation. However, even in the anharmonic models, the frequencies often still need to be shifted or scaled to match experimental frequencies.220) The performance of anharmonic models is strongly dependent on the studied system and the employed computational method,221) whereas harmonic frequency calculations are considered very stable and reliable.222) Taking together the reliability of the harmonic approximation and the substantial computational cost related to calculations using anharmonic models, the harmonic approximation should be the first method of choice.

From an experimental point of view, the usage of gas-phase IR ion spectroscopy could be sorted into two categories. It can be used for pure analytical and for physical-organic research questions. For analytical research questions, it is sufficient to assign a spectrum to a specific molecule/ion, while for physical-organic research questions, a more precise three-dimensional structure is necessary for a deeper understanding of the underlying physics and chemistry. QC calculations can be relevant for both, but their importance leans more toward physical-organic chemistry research questions. Gas-phase IR ion spectroscopy has been used in combination with QC calculations to assign the structures of clusters of organic molecules223–226) and metal salts/oxides,114,227,228) biomolecules, such as glycans,214,215,229) lipids,95,230,231) glycolipids,220) nucleobases,232) and metabolites.193,233,234) Additionally, it has been used to characterize fragment ions in MS, such as peptide,235,236) glycerolipid,216,237) or RNA fragments.238) For glycerolipid fragments (Fig. 12A), it was not clear if the fragment ions are linear, form five-membered dioxolane or six-membered dioxane rings. Based on the reported IR ion spectra and their computed counterparts, it was confirmed that after loss of their phosphate headgroup, only five-membered dioxolane fragments are formed. Often, fragment ion structures in MS/MS spectra are assigned with databases. A recent study has shown that a large fraction of those structures in databases is wrong.233) By combining IR ion spectroscopy and QC, their true structure can, however, be accurately elucidated. Besides, gas-phase IR spectroscopy has been used to study reactive intermediates,239) which cannot be readily studied in the solution phase due to their short lifetime, such as glycosyl cations,93,240–245) fluorenyl (Figs. 12B and 12C),3) norbornyl cations,5) or Breslow intermediates.246) The reported spectra for fluorenyl cations showcase how closely experimental and computed spectra can align over a large spectral range. In the case of glycosyl cations, the intermediates of the glycosylation reactions and relevant effects, such as neighboring93,240) and remote participation,241,243) could be demonstrated. However, while there is a significant body of work that is correlating the gas-phase structure and solution-phase reactivity, there is an ongoing debate as to what extent this is generally true.247) Besides, it has to be considered that in the gas-phase environment, especially under CID conditions, unwanted rearrangement reactions can occur, rendering structural analysis challenging, but opening up a whole avenue of physical-organic chemistry research questions.5,215,242,244,248,249)

IR ion spectroscopy has also been used to study chromophores, such as dyes or porphyrins.250) The intermediates in the electrocatalytic reduction of O2251) and CO2252) by porphyrins were characterized by messenger-tagging IR spectroscopy and QC. Furthermore, cryogenic IR ion spectroscopy was employed to inform on protonation sites253–255) and determine the binding and release mechanisms in ionophores.256,257) In a hallmark study (Fig. 13),258) the triplet state of the chromophore lumiflavin was studied. Generating the triplet state in the gas phase required excitation with a visible laser at 426 nm, intersystem crossing of the S1 state into the triplet state T1, and subsequent probing using an IR laser with a 12-ms delay. Combining this approach with a tickle radio frequency allows us to efficiently remove non-excited species from the trap and record a clean spectrum of lumiflavin in its triplet state (Fig. 13E), which is clearly different from lumiflavin in its singlet state (Fig. 13B). Without using a tickle radio frequency, the spectrum is co-populated by both singlet and triplet state lumiflavin (Fig. 13D). Employing more than one laser for probing ions does not only allow to probe triplet states but can also be used for isomer/conformer-selective ion spectroscopy studies.54,249,259)

UV-vis ion spectroscopy

The working principle of UV-vis ion spectroscopy, sometimes referred to as electronic spectroscopy, is similar to that of IR ion spectroscopy, as it relies on wavelength-resolved photofragmentation.260) While IR spectroscopy is very structure-sensitive, UV-vis spectroscopy studies the electronic transitions in a molecule and is therefore suitable for studying the electronic structure and photophysical properties of ions. While the technique is highly sensitive to molecules with a π-system,261–265) it is not strictly limited to such systems.266) Similar to IR ion spectroscopy, this technique can be conducted at room temperature and at cryogenic temperatures. An additional indirect method to measure UV-vis spectra in the gas phase, which only works for fluorescent analytes, is to monitor the intensity of the emitted fluorescence as a function of the excitation wavenumber.267) Fluorescence ion spectroscopy will be introduced in more detail in the next section.

The prediction of UV-vis spectra is more challenging than that of IR spectra. An IR spectrum is related to the molecular frequencies that occur in the electronic ground state. Ground-quantum-chemical methods are established for a wide range of systems. Electronic transitions, on the other hand, involve both ground and excited states. While methods for calculating excited states have improved, it is generally less established and less reliable than ground-state calculations. Advanced wavefunction methods, such as CASSCF18,268) methods, work well for excited states, but their application is limited to the smallest systems. Excited-state calculations are more accessible to a wide range of users by time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT) calculations. However, TD-DFT is less accurate than advanced wavefunction-based methods and does not always accurately capture excited-state dynamics (ESD) (e.g., in the case of a conical intersection).269)

An excited-state geometry can be significantly different from a ground-state geometry. Therefore, optimization of the studied system in the excited state is necessary. In many QC software packages, analytical excited-state gradients are available for TD-DFT methods, but not necessarily for the advanced methods. When optimizing an excited state, it is important to indicate which excited state should be optimized (typically the first excited state) and to define how many excited states are included in the vertical excitation calculation (Fig. 14). Generally, the excited-state calculation becomes less error prone (e.g., due to more accurate state ordering) when considering more excited states during the vertical excitation step, but it also increases the computational time. For excited-state calculations, it is even more important to choose a suitable DFT functional and an adequate basis set than for ground-state calculations.

Fig. 14. Schematic ground-state (S0) and excited-state (S1) PESs illustrating vertical excitation (absorption, blue) and radiative emission (fluorescence, green). Because nuclear motion is slow compared to electronic excitation, absorption occurs vertically and typically populates a vibrationally excited level of S1 (gray). Rapid vibrational relaxation and internal conversion bring the molecule to the vibrational ground state of S1. From this relaxed geometry, the molecule undergoes a vertical radiative transition back to S0 (fluorescence), landing in a vibrationally excited level of the ground state. PES, potential energy surface.

The vertical excitation energies provided in TD-DFT calculations correspond to the UV-vis transitions observed in the respective spectra. However, the match between an experimental spectrum and computed UV-vis transitions can be mediocre, and a systematic rescaling of UV-vis transitions, as in the case of harmonic frequencies, is not possible. The results depend more heavily on the applied DFT functional, and solvation effects often play a crucial role in determining the position of a transition. Similar to harmonic frequencies, the calculations provide a line spectrum, in which the positions and shapes of the experimental spectrum are not accurately reproduced. A more advanced way of calculating more accurately shaped UV-vis spectra is calculating the Franck-Condon (FC) spectrum, as electronic-vibrational couplings are taken into account in the transition. Calculating FC spectra is available in various QC software packages. In ORCA, FC spectra can be calculated using the ESD module.270,271) This is computationally more expensive and requires submitting the ground-state and excited-state Hessians as input. The results still depend on the method, e.g., the DFT functional, but are generally of high quality, as the method considers the excited-state geometry and vibronic coupling. In some cases, better results, like an improved spectral shape, are achieved when omitting low frequencies in the provided Hessian, as the relative error on low frequencies is generally higher than for high frequencies. It is possible that TD-DFT cannot find the right excited-state minimum. In such cases, CC methods or CASSCF might be necessary to get an accurate geometry of the excited state. However, when using the Adiabatic Hessian After a Step (AHAS) approximation,271) implemented in ORCA, a ground-state structure and Hessian are sufficient to calculate an FC spectrum. This approximation generally works well if the excited-state geometry is similar to the ground-state geometry.

UV-vis action spectroscopy has been used in combination with QC to study biomolecular structures (Fig. 15A),272–275) metal clusters,276) carbocations,277,278) and carbon clusters,279) for the differentiation of isomers280) and protonation sites.261,263,281,282) Figure 15B highlights that 8-bromoquinazoline protomers, previously separated by FAIMS, show clearly distinguishable UV-vis action spectra.261) Additionally, it can be used to probe the UV-vis spectra of dyes in the absence of solvents to determine intrinsic photophysical properties.283,284)

Fluorescence ion spectroscopy and FRET

Fluorescence ion spectroscopy, both wavelength- and time-resolved, can be realized without photofragmentation.285–287) This is mainly possible because fluorescence spectroscopy does not rely on measuring the change in transmission of a photon beam but instead measures the emitted photons of an excited fluorophore. These photons, when generated in the gas phase, can be guided through lenses to detectors operating at atmospheric pressure. In fact, these are the same detectors that are used for solution-phase fluorescence studies.288) Fluorescence ion spectroscopy allows probing the photophysical properties of fluorophores without perturbation by solvents or counterions.289) Additionally, it captures the effects of surrounding charges on the photophysical properties of a fluorophore,288) as MS allows for m/z selection of a particular species. Fluorescence ion spectroscopy can be conducted at room temperature and at cryogenic temperatures.287) At lower temperatures, emission bands narrow and the maxima blue-shift.290) An effect in fluorescence spectroscopy that allows mapping the structure of larger biomolecules is Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET). This non-radiative energy transfer between two chromophores can lead to a reduction in fluorescence lifetimes and intensity of the donor dye. This non-radiative energy transfer typically occurs when the emission spectrum of the donor dye overlaps with the absorption spectrum of the acceptor dye (also called quencher). As this decrease in fluorescence is distance-dependent, it can theoretically be used to map the structures of biomolecules in both the solution and the gas phase.291–293) Additionally, there is also an action spectroscopy form of gas-phase FRET.294,295)

From a computational point of view, the calculation of fluorescence spectra and the estimation of fluorescence lifetimes proceeds in a similar way to the prediction of UV-vis FC spectra. If the ground- and excited-state geometries are similar, then it is expected that absorption and fluorescence spectra mirror each other. In the software ORCA, the calculation of fluorescence spectra can also be invoked with the ESD module,270,271) and requires both ground-state and excited-state Hessians. Even without excited-state Hessians, fluorescence spectra and lifetimes can be obtained using the previously mentioned AHAS method.

Gas-phase fluorescence spectroscopy has been used to study the intrinsic properties of dyes in the absence of solvents. For example, it has been studied how the alkali metal-ion adduct formation with dyes or the encapsulation of dyes by cucurbiturils influences the dispersed fluorescence spectrum.296,297) Gas-phase fluorescence studies also revealed that certain dyes, such as proflavine, mainly emit fluorescence from their S2 state, effectively breaking Kasha’s rule stating that fluorescence is only emitted from the lowest excited state.267,298) The gas-phase fluorescence spectrum of the fluorophore oxyluciferin has been studied at 100 K to determine the intrinsic color of this fluorophore, which is related to bioluminescence in fireflies (Fig. 16A).299) The chromophore anion of the GFP protein, originating from a bioluminescent jellyfish, is in its isolated form not fluorescent in solution. In the gas phase, however, it was shown to light up at 100 K.300,301) The photophysics of this and other molecules can be so complex that TD-DFT does not predict structures that correspond to the experimental findings, as shown by non-fluorescent TD-DFT minima. In such cases, if the system size allows it, advanced multireference methods can be used to predict the global minimum structures of the excited-state PES more accurately.

Besides measuring fluorescence as a function of the wavelength, fluorescence can also be measured as a function of time, leading to fluorescence decay curves. From such curves, fluorescence lifetimes can be extracted. Typically, fluorescence lifetimes of isolated dyes in the gas phase are longer than their solvated counterparts.302) This is due to a reduction of non-radiative decay channels in isolation. In the presence of fluorescence donors and quenchers, the distance-dependent quenching of fluorescence lifetimes can be observed.302) The fluorescence lifetimes of quenched and non-quenched species can be converted to FRET efficiencies, which can be linked to distances via the R0 constant.293,303) R0 is typically constant for a specific donor–acceptor pair in a defined solvent. However, this constant is not known in the gas phase, because its determination requires quantum yields and overlap integrals of the dyes, which are not straightforward to obtain in the gas phase. Nevertheless, the constant has been approximated previously to estimate distances in biomolecules in the gas phase, which were compared to distances in conformationally sampled structures, providing reasonable matches in some cases.304,305) Additionally, quenched fluorescence lifetimes can be compared to understand how certain effects, such as collisional or photon energy, complexation, and posttranslational modifications, influence the structures of a biomolecule without the need of calculating absolute distances.302,306) It has been determined that nearby charges and aromatic moieties can influence the FRET efficiency (Fig. 16B).288,307) Fluorescence lifetimes can be computed, and although the computed value is usually not exact, it tends to be on the same order of magnitude as the experimental value.

CONCLUSION AND OUTLOOK

The aim of this review is to provide an overview of how QC and computational methods can generally complement MS experiments and increase the knowledge that can be gained from such experiments. There are certainly further fields related to MS that benefit from QC and are not mentioned in this article. Additionally, it should be stressed that there is a large body of work related to this topic containing many excellent articles that, unfortunately, cannot all be discussed in this review.

The benefit of QC becomes mainly apparent in the advanced MS techniques, such as ion spectroscopy. However, the benefit is mutual: computational chemists benefit from MS data. Spectral data of ions obtained by MS methods are most suitable to benchmark computational methods, as it is the most intrinsic form of data that can be obtained for an ion without external perturbation. In turn, computational data can help mass spectrometrists to assign structures, understand mechanisms, comprehend charge distributions, and analyze spectra.

In terms of MS, this review covered techniques that have been commercialized, such as IM-MS, and some that have not been commercialized, such as all ion spectroscopy methods. Recent efforts by smaller MS companies for custom-built ion spectroscopy setups help democratizing advanced MS methods.189,190) However, the support of large vendors is necessary to make these techniques broadly available. Besides, the combination of IMRs and ion spectroscopy allows one to probe the effects of solvent molecules on ion properties, which could bridge the gap between the gas and the solution phase.

It can be expected that the barrier for using QC software and HPCs will decrease further in the future and that it will be even more user-friendly to do QC calculations. The computational power will further increase, whereas the code of QC software packages will become more efficient. More methods will be developed that further increase the accuracy of the results obtained at a lower cost. Furthermore, artificial intelligence/ML methods are already being used in computational chemistry, and it can be expected that in the near future, AI/ML tools that provide high-quality results will be widely accessible. However, as with any AI tool, they need to be thoroughly tested before being used in research.
