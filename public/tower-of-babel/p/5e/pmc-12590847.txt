Untangling choices: how awareness and socio-economic status shape malaria vaccination decisions among Ghanaian women
Authors: Richard Kwame Ansah, Sampson Tackie, Killian Asampana Asosega, Kassim Tawiah, Baaba Abassawah Danquah, Sampson Takyi Appiah, Rhodaline Abena Twum, Maurice Omane-Adjepong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria remains a major public health challenge in sub-Saharan Africa, making effective vaccine uptake a priority. Although individual factors such as awareness, education, and place of residence are known to influence vaccination decisions, there is a gap in understanding how these socio-economic attributes interact with social context to shape choices under peer influence. This study addresses this gap by applying the Multi-Population Curie-Weiss Model, a statistical mechanics framework, to analyse binary vaccination decisions influenced jointly by private incentives and social interactions. Data were obtained from the 2019 Ghana Malaria Indicator Survey (GMIS), which included 3,004 women. Analysis showed that 93.54% of respondents intended to vaccinate their children, whereas 6.45% did not. Among those in favour, 72.10% had received formal education, while 27.90% had not; 40.10% were aware of the malaria vaccine, whereas 56.90% were unaware. Among respondents who did not intend to vaccinate, 78.35% were educated and 21.65% were uneducated; 28.87% were aware of the vaccine, while 71.13% lacked prior awareness. In the absence of social interactions, factors such as education, vaccine awareness, and place of residence were associated with vaccination decisions. Rural women with some education demonstrated a modest increase (1.62%) in intrinsic motivation, indicating a positive association between education and vaccination. When social interactions were incorporated, educational attainment and vaccine awareness exhibited significant associations with vaccination choices. Notably, the interaction strength among urban educated women yielded a negative estimate (-4.06%), suggesting limited imitative effects, whereas rural uneducated women appeared to exert greater social influence on their educated rural counterparts. These findings provide insights for public health authorities seeking to design strategies to improve malaria vaccine uptake and reduce the disease burden among vulnerable populations in Ghana.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05613-1.Keywords: Malaria vaccine, Social interaction, Partial least squares, Ordinary least squares, External field

Background

Children under five years old are highly susceptible to various diseases, making vaccination a crucial public health strategy. The World Health Organization (WHO) highlights vaccines as the safest and most cost-effective way to prevent over a million childhood deaths annually [1]. Significant advancements in childhood immunization programs over the past three decades have substantially reduced sickness, disability, and death from infectious diseases, particularly for young children in developing countries, as noted by Touray et al. [2] and the WHO [3].

According to Chan [4], the Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI), launched globally by WHO in 1974 and implemented in Ghana in 1978, has played a vital role in increasing childhood immunization coverage worldwide from 5% in 1974 to at least 80% by 2014. Despite this progress, challenges in achieving full immunization coverage persist in developing nations, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), which accounts for over half (52%) of global under-five mortality, as stated by Ehreth [5] and the IGME [6]. Bridging this gap requires equitable access to vaccines, irrespective of socio-economic, demographic, or geographic disparities, according to the WHO [7].

According to the Ghana Statistical Service [8], vaccination coverage in Ghana increased from 47% in 1988 to 79% in 2008, though it slightly declined to 77% by 2014 [9]. Disparities remain, particularly between rural and urban areas, leading to some children missing critical immunizations, a problem highlighted by Harris et al. [10]. Achieving full immunization coverage is vital for reducing childhood mortality and aligns with Sustainable Development Goal 3 (SDG 3) for universal quality healthcare. This necessitates the involvement of all stakeholders, including mothers and caregivers, while considering household-level conditions.

Malaria remains a leading cause of childhood mortality, despite a nearly 40% global decline in deaths between 2000 and 2019. In 2020, 627,000 malaria-related deaths were reported, with 80% occurring in children under five. According to Willyard [11], the WHO recommends the RTS,S/AS01 vaccine for children in regions with moderate to high malaria transmission. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reported that this vaccine reduced clinical and severe malaria cases by one-third among children aged 5–17 months over four years, demonstrating a strong safety profile and feasibility for implementation without interfering with other health behaviours [12]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has recommended a second malaria vaccine, R21/Matrix-M, for use in children in malaria-endemic regions. According to Datoo et al. [13], R21/Matrix-M demonstrated approximately 75% efficacy against symptomatic malaria in phase 2 trials, exceeding the WHO’s target for malaria vaccines. It has been licensed in Ghana, Nigeria, and Burkina Faso, and is expected to be more affordable and easier to produce at scale compared to RTS,S, potentially enabling broader coverage [14, 15]. The introduction of two WHO-recommended malaria vaccines presents both opportunities and challenges for national immunization programmes, particularly in determining optimal allocation, delivery strategies, and community engagement to ensure high uptake.

According to a report by the Ministry of Health [16], malaria significantly impacts children in Africa, resulting in approximately 500,000 deaths each year among children under the age of five. In 2019, Ghana integrated the malaria vaccine into its routine immunization programme for children under two years of age, following pilot implementation in 42 districts across seven regions based on the WHO’s recommendation. In 2021, Ghana reported 5.7 million confirmed cases of malaria and 275 related deaths, with children under five accounting for 1.6 million cases and 125 deaths. In response to this ongoing burden, the National Immunization Technical Advisory Group (NITAG) recommended expanding the program to additional districts.

Despite the introduction of the RTS,S and R21/Matrix-M malaria vaccines in Ghana in 2019 and 2023, respectively, vaccine hesitancy persists. Studies such as that by Saaka et al. [17] indicate that, although awareness is increasing, up to 29% of parents particularly younger mothers and those with limited antenatal care remain hesitant to vaccinate their children. Factors such as misinformation, low trust, and regional differences also influence acceptance, according to Grant et al. [18], suggesting that awareness alone is insufficient to ensure vaccine uptake among Ghanaian women, a point also made by Bam et al. [19].

Tabiri et al. [20] investigated malaria vaccine uptake in Sunyani Municipality, Ghana, finding that while 90.6% of children received the first two doses, only 78.1% completed the full three-dose series. Factors contributing to drop-out included lack of information, vaccine shortages, and discomfort. Maternal education, especially at the university level, was positively correlated with vaccine uptake, and sub-municipality of residence was a significant determinant.

Existing research on malaria vaccine acceptance among children under five years of age in Ghana has not fully explored how women’s awareness and socio-economic status jointly influence vaccination decisions. The present study addresses this gap by examining the interrelationship between these factors and the acceptance of malaria vaccines for young children. The analysis employs the Multi-population Curie-Weiss Model, a statistical mechanics framework that captures the interplay between social context and individual incentives in binary decision-making, such as the choice to vaccinate or not. This modelling approach offers novel insights into the roles of social influence and awareness in shaping mothers’ decisions regarding malaria vaccination.

Methods

Multi-population Curie-Weiss model

Within statistical mechanics, models such as the Multi-population Curie-Weiss Model have been widely used to explain how collective behaviour emerges from social interactions and individual satisfaction, as demonstrated by Brock and Durlauf [21–23]. Similar dynamics have been identified in biological, ecological, and socio-economic systems [21, 24, 25]. Kuran [26] noted that even minor structural changes, such as the introduction of new technologies, can lead to widespread behavioural shifts. For instance, Gallo and Barra [27] applied the model to investigate suicide methods in Italy, whereas Opoku et al. [28] examined variations in educational attainment by gender and residence across five African countries. Further applications, including those by Ansah et al. [29], Burioni et al. [30], and Osabutey et al. [31], illustrate the model’s capacity to explore health outcomes, energy consumption, and broader social phenomena.

To examine how socio-economic attributes and awareness influence mothers’ decisions regarding malaria vaccination, a modelling framework grounded in statistical mechanics is employed. Specifically, the multi-population Curie-Weiss model, a binary choice framework extensively used in social and behavioural sciences, is applied to represent collective decision-making under the combined influence of individual preferences and social interactions [21, 27]. This formulation enables the analysis of how personal incentives and social imitation jointly shape vaccination behaviour.

The multi-population Curie-Weiss model captures how binary vaccination choices emerge from both private incentives and peer influence. Each woman’s decision depends on her own socio-economic characteristics (education, vaccine awareness, and residence), but social imitation occurs within and across similar groups. For example, if most educated rural women vaccinate their children, another woman in the same group may feel socially encouraged to do the same. Positive interaction terms capture such alignment, whereas negative terms reflect resistance or independent decision-making [27]. This framework enables us to study not only who is likely to vaccinate but also how collective behaviour arises within socio-economic segments.

In simple terms, the model captures collective behaviour by considering both individual characteristics (private incentives) and the influence of peers (social incentives) [29]. The private incentive reflects how a woman’s education, place of residence, and awareness of the malaria vaccine directly affect her decision to vaccinate her child. The social incentive captures how the decisions of other women with similar characteristics can influence her choice, either encouraging imitation or divergence. For example, if many women in a urban community with some formal education are in favour of vaccination, this shared attitude can increase the likelihood that other women in the same group will also vaccinate their children. Conversely, if awareness is low in an urban neighbourhood, social interactions might reinforce reluctance to vaccinate. In this way, the model links socio-economic attributes and awareness to vaccination behaviour, while also accounting for how group dynamics can amplify or dampen individual decisions.

Study design

An analytical cross-sectional study was conducted involving females across all ten regions of Ghana, utilizing data from the 2019 Ghana Malaria Indicator Survey (GMIS). The GMIS was designed based on the 2010 Population and Housing Census (PHC) framework developed by the Ghana Statistical Service (GSS). In 2019, Ghana underwent an administrative reorganization that expanded the number of regions from ten to sixteen, comprising 260 districts, municipalities, and metropolitan areas. However, these newly created regions were not incorporated into the survey design due to their recent establishment. Consequently, the 2019 GMIS sampling frame was based on the ten regional boundaries defined in the 2010 PHC. The GMIS served as a nationally representative survey aimed at collecting data on malaria-related indicators across both urban and rural areas within the ten administrative regions (Western, Central, Greater Accra, Volta, Eastern, Ashanti, Brong Ahafo, Northern, Upper East, and Upper West) as delineated in the 2010 PHC. The sampling frame for the 2019 GMIS consisted of all census enumeration areas (EAs) from the 2010 PHC, as provided by the GSS [16, 34].

In the 2019 GMIS study, the sampling process was executed in two stages and categorized into 20 strata based on urban and rural divisions within each region. During the first stage, 200 Enumeration Areas (EAs) were chosen, comprising 97 urban and 103 rural areas, with selection based on size and conducted independently within each stratum. The second stage involved selecting 30 households from each stratum, culminating in a total of 6,000 households. The sampling frame was organized within each stratum for implicit stratification and proportional allocation at lower administrative levels. For a comprehensive overview of the 2019 GMIS sampling, including data collection methods, tools, and quality control, refer to the full report. The analysis in this study specifically included data from 3,004 women aged 15–49 years [34].

Study variables

The study examined the acceptance of malaria vaccinations as the dependent variable. Acceptance of malaria vaccinations was classified into two categories: acceptance of child vaccination (coded as +1) and denial of child vaccination (coded as −1). Three independent variables were considered: type of residence (rural or urban), educational attainment (with or without formal education), and vaccination awareness (yes or no) within the surveyed households. Educational attainment, place of residence, and vaccine awareness were selected as key socio-economic and awareness variables based on evidence from malaria immunization studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa. Existing research has consistently demonstrated that these factors are major determinants of vaccine uptake. For instance, a systematic review of vaccination barriers in the region identified awareness and geographical distance as principal obstacles to immunization [35]. Moreover, studies conducted in Ghana have revealed a strong association between maternal awareness and willingness to vaccinate children, with significant disparities observed between urban and rural populations [17]. Additional evidence indicates that maternal education and household wealth contribute substantially to the rural–urban gap in vaccination coverage [36]. Collectively, these findings justify the inclusion of the selected variables in examining malaria vaccine acceptance.

Attributes of the population

Tables 2 and 3 summarize the vaccination choices and residential status of the 3,004 women surveyed in the 2019 Ghana Malaria Indicator Survey (GMIS). Among these participants, 93.54% preferred to vaccinate their children against malaria, while 6.45% did not. A breakdown of vaccination decisions by education and residence revealed notable patterns. Among those who chose to vaccinate, 72.10% were educated and 27.90% were uneducated. Additionally, 43.10% were aware of the malaria vaccine, while 56.90% lacked prior awareness. Within this group, 39.89% were educated rural women, 32.21% were educated urban women, 21.00% were uneducated rural women, and 6.90% were uneducated urban women.

Conversely, among those who declined vaccination, 78.35% were educated and 21.65% were uneducated. Interestingly, only 28.87% of these women were aware of the malaria vaccine, while 71.13% were unaware. In terms of subgroup composition, 43.81% were educated rural women, 34.54% were educated urban women, 19.59% were uneducated rural women, and 2.06% were uneducated urban women.

Non interacting case

Whether the group decides to permit their children to receive a malaria vaccination depends on the sum of the individual private incentives, αj. If the total is positive, the decision will be YES; if it is negative, the decision will be NO. Table 5 presents the estimated coefficients for the non-interacting model, representing the sum of private incentives (∑αj) across different subgroups defined by education, residence, and awareness of the malaria vaccine. The results indicate that females with no education in urban areas exhibit the highest combined private incentives (0.3810), followed by females with no education in rural areas (0.2814). In contrast, the lowest private incentives are observed among urban females who are not aware of the vaccine (0.0727), suggesting that lack of awareness substantially reduces the propensity to vaccinate regardless of residence. Interestingly, while education appears to enhance incentives in rural areas (0.1998 vs. 0.2814 for some vs. no education), the opposite is observed in urban settings, where the educated group records a lower sum of incentives (0.2056) compared to their non-educated counterparts (0.3810). Similarly, awareness plays a stronger role in rural settings (0.2045 vs. 0.1087) than in urban settings (0.1433 vs. 0.0727). Overall, these findings highlight the complex interaction between education, residence, and awareness in shaping private incentives for malaria vaccination. In each of the eight cases, the base private incentive α0 is positive, which suggests that women would be inclined to agree to their children receiving a malaria vaccination.

Interacting case

In the interactive model, the utility function considers both private and social incentives. When the social incentive, denoted as Ygg′^ for groups g and g′, is positive, individuals within these groups are more likely to emulate each other’s choices. Conversely, if Ygg′^ is negative, women in these groups tend to make different choices rather than conform. The decision to vaccinate one’s child against malaria depends on the total private incentives, represented by αj. A positive sum of αj leads the group to favour vaccination for their children, whereas a negative sum results in the group opting not to vaccinate their children against malaria.

Note that Y11^ indicates the interaction strength among females in rural areas with some level of education when they interact with each other. As illustrated in Table 6, the estimated value of Y11^ is negative, which suggests that imitation is perceived as not beneficial in this group. Model diagnostics and validation

In this section, the fit of the interaction model to the data is evaluated. To determine the parameters of the interaction model described in [38], the partial least squares (PLS) method was employed. In the model, Ug represents the dependent variable, while m¯g represents the independent variable. PLS predicts a set of dependent variables by analysing a set of independent variables (predictors) and constructing orthogonal factors, referred to as latent vectors, from these predictors. These latent vectors are ranked according to their eigenvalues in descending order. The selection of latent vectors is based on their ability to optimally explain the covariance between the independent and dependent variables [39].

Analyses were performed using MATLAB software version R2016a to generate the results. As shown in Table 7, the first five latent vectors explain 99.90% of the variance in Ug and 88.66% of the variance in m¯g, indicating that the model adequately accounts for the variance in both the dependent and independent variables for modelling or prediction purposes. Additionally, the root mean square error of prediction (RMSEP) decreases with an increasing number of latent vectors for both m¯g and Ug.

Discussion

This study provides essential insights into factors associated with malaria vaccination decisions among women in Ghana, focusing on socio-economic status and awareness about the malaria vaccine. An analysis of data from the 2019 Ghana Malaria Indicator Survey (GMIS) reveals a relationship among educational attainment, awareness of the malaria vaccine, and place of residence in vaccination decision-making. Women with higher education and those residing in urban areas showed greater vaccine acceptance rates, aligning with previous studies linking education to health-enhancing behaviours, including immunization uptake [40–42].

Social influence plays a role in vaccination decisions, particularly among uneducated women in rural areas, who in turn affect the decisions of their educated counterparts living in the same communities. This suggests that community-driven health campaigns could be especially effective in rural Ghana by using community leaders and local networks to raise awareness [20, 43, 44]. Interestingly, urban women with some level of education did not exhibit a significant imitative effect, implying that vaccination decisions in these populations may be shaped more by individual factors, such as media exposure and access to health services, than by social influence. This contrast between urban and rural settings is consistent with findings from similar studies in Sub-Saharan Africa, where community influence tends to be stronger in rural areas due to closer social ties and established leadership structures [45, 46].

The model indicates that awareness may function as an intermediate variable transmitting the effects of structural factors, such as education and residence, to vaccination decisions. For instance, educated urban women are more likely to be aware of vaccination campaigns, and this awareness subsequently drives the positive private incentive toward vaccine acceptance. These findings suggest that awareness not only directly influences vaccination decisions but also mediates the relationship between socio-economic status and collective behaviour, consistent with observations in behavioural epidemiology.

Policy implications and comparative perspectives

The findings highlight several policy implications. Education-centred initiatives may significantly improve vaccine uptake, as evidenced by programs in other regions of Africa with similar social structures [44]. Policies that provide educational access and community-based information dissemination could amplify vaccine acceptance rates. These policies should consider unique factors that influence rural and urban populations differently, potentially tailoring approaches for each context. Furthermore, it is essential to involve local leaders and prominent members of the community in health initiatives, particularly in rural settings where social influence plays a significant role. Comparable strategies employed in malaria-endemic regions such as Kenya and Malawi have proven effective in utilizing community influence to enhance awareness and acceptance of vaccines [47, 48]. Future research should build upon these findings by exploring social interactions and information dissemination methods that are most effective in urban versus rural settings. Additionally, longitudinal studies could help to better establish the causality between socio-economic factors and vaccination decisions; this would provide a stronger basis for targeted health interventions.

Conclusion

This study explored how education, residence, and awareness of the malaria vaccine relate to Ghanaian women’s decisions to vaccinate their children, applying the multi-population Curie-Weiss statistical mechanics model to data from the 2019 Ghana Malaria Indicator Survey. In the non-interactive setting, educational attainment, vaccine awareness, and residence were each associated with vaccination choices. When social influence was considered, awareness and education continued to show associations, although the direction and strength of these associations varied. For instance, rural women without formal education appeared to influence the decisions of their educated rural peers, whereas urban women with some education showed little evidence of such influence. These patterns suggest that community-based approaches in rural areas may have potential to encourage vaccine uptake, although further evidence is required before making policy recommendations.

While the results highlight meaningful patterns, this study is limited by its use of cross-sectional, self-reported data, which restricts causal inference and may introduce recall or social desirability bias. The multi-population Curie-Weiss model simplifies complex decision-making processes into binary choices and assumes uniform social interactions within groups, which may not fully capture real-world behavioural variability. Additionally, some potentially influential factors, such as cultural beliefs and household income, were not included in the analysis.

For future research, developing a more comprehensive understanding of the role of community dynamics in health decisions will be crucial for creating effective malaria vaccination policies. This work adds to the growing body of research on malaria vaccination acceptance and provides practical insights that may inform public health initiatives aimed at reducing malaria through increased vaccination coverage among children under five.
