Adding N to Water‐Stressed Sorghum bicolor Shifts Root Functional Traits and Uptake Strategies, but Without Short‐Term Improvements in Water Use Efficiency or Productivity
Authors: Sarah Tepler Drobnitch, Nora E Flynn, Joshua A Wenz, Louise H Comas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGiven the co‐limitation of water and nitrogen (N) acquisition under drought, it is essential to mechanistically explore how plants respond to these limitations individually and in combination. To understand the general effect that N availability plays in water use efficiency across Sorghum bicolor ssp. bicolor , which has diverse mechanisms conferring tolerance to water limitations, we grew six genotypes ranging in drought tolerance under greenhouse conditions under three treatments: full water/high N, limited water/high N, and limited water/low N availability. We measured a suite of above‐ and below‐ground plant traits to understand growth allocation and physiological responses. Under high N but limited water, two genotypes increased allocation to fine root production and reduced allocation to coarse root production, which could limit deep water foraging under field conditions. In contrast, limited N and water availability together stimulated coarse root production in those genotypes. High N with limited water did not increase net photosynthesis or total plant biomass production compared to limited N and water availability in any genotype. These results suggest that adding N to S. bicolor ssp. bicolor is unlikely to be an effective strategy for rescuing growth or yield under drought conditions. Moreover, limiting coarse root growth may have detrimental effects under field conditions. It is crucial for future studies to follow up on the effects of N and water availability on the grain yield of S. bicolor ssp. bicolor under water‐limited field conditions to better understand the potentially negative effects of high nitrogen on water foraging under drought.Keywords: nitrogen limitation, root allocation, Sorghum bicolor, stress physiology, water limitation, WUEi

Introduction

Drought and water limitation are increasingly prevalent stressors of plants in ecosystems from grasslands to rainforests (Choat et al. 2012; Chen et al. 2022; Smith et al. 2022; Abel et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024). While the impact of water limitation on plant physiology, productivity, and reproduction is well‐studied, our grasp of the impact of multiple stressors on plants (water, nutrient, or thermal) is much weaker (Cross et al. 2015; Pavanetto et al. 2023). Given that plants access nitrogen in the soil either via soil water or microbial activity, the reduction in available soil nitrogen during water limitation may have a contributing effect on plant growth that might otherwise be attributed to drought stress alone (Goodger and Schachtman 2009; Pivovaroff et al. 2016; Luo and Luo 2017; Song, Wang, et al. 2019; Sigala et al. 2020; He et al. 2023; Schlesselmann et al. 2025).

Crop plants present a particularly fertile opportunity to examine the separate and combined effects of water limitation and nitrogen due to their short life spans, well‐characterized phenotypes, and accessible genetic diversity. Sorghum is a globally important food staple that is increasingly planted worldwide because of its productivity in water‐limited environments (Mutava et al. 2011; Borrell et al. 2014; Visarada 2018). In water‐limited environments, sorghum has been observed to reduce shoot size and, thus, pre‐flowering water demand, theoretically increasing water availability during grain filling and leading to greater yield (Borrell et al. 2014). Many cereals have been shown to increase root production and shift specific root functional traits in response to limited water (Henry et al. 2012; Grzesiak et al. 2019; Lynch 2019; Flynn et al. 2023). Herbaceous root systems have the potential to shift not only overall root mass or root angles, but also relative coarse/fine root allocation, specific root length, specific root area, and overall rooting volume. Sorghum bicolor may also shift root functional traits to withstand water stress, but, thus far, below‐ground research on sorghum under drought conditions has barely scratched the surface (Mutava et al. 2011; Borrell et al. 2014; Bell et al. 2018; Demissie et al. 2023).

Since sorghum is adapted to water‐limited environments, it is also likely adapted to N‐limited environments since N uptake is limited by bulk flow through the soil. Sorghum can take up more N than maize under dry conditions (Lemaire et al. 1996), but sufficient soil moisture is still needed for sorghum to be able to optimize the use of N fertilizer (Sigua et al. 2018). Additionally, nitrogen limitation may initiate leaf senescence in S. bicolor , which is preceded by a rising C:N ratio in leaves (Chen et al. 2015). In other crop systems, high N has been shown to ameliorate the impacts of drought stress on crop production (Shangguan et al. 2000; Saneoka et al. 2004; Randhawa et al. 2017; Song, Li, et al. 2019). A handful of studies have investigated the potential of providing nitrogen to sorghum in water‐limited environments (Abadi et al. 2014; Farhadi et al. 2022; Obour et al. 2022); however, given the phenotypic diversity of sorghum across Africa and Asia, we sought to evaluate this potential in a diverse set of S. bicolor ssp. bicolor genotypes (Traore and Maranville 1999; Turner et al. 2016; Drobnitch et al. 2021; Table 1). We also wished to observe the specific traits that could benefit from N fertilization under water stress to better understand the mechanisms by which N fertilization improves plant productivity and overall yield.

Our objective was to evaluate the impact of water and N availability on sorghum shoot and root growth during the vegetative stage of development in a greenhouse pot study. We selected six diverse sorghum genotypes (Table 1) that both represent a broad genetic range of S. bicolor ssp. bicolor (Tao et al. 2021) and a broad range of phenotypic (leaf area, grain yield, plant size, tillering potential) variation (Turner et al. 2016) to explore sorghum's response to water and N availability. We tested the hypothesis that water limitations would decrease shoot biomass but increase total root length and the relative biomass allocation to roots. We also tested the hypothesis that under limited water availability, increased N availability would increase shoot biomass, N uptake, photosynthetic rate (A n), photosynthetic nitrogen use efficiency (PNUE), and instantaneous water use efficiency (WUEi) relative to treatments with limited N and water availability.

Material and Methods

Genotype Selection and Experimental Design

This study took place in a greenhouse at the USDA‐ARS Crops Research Laboratory in Fort Collins, CO, as described in Drobnitch et al. (2021). Six diploid lines of grain S. bicolor were selected to span a range of geographic origin across Africa, variation in WUE, and grain yield response under drought (Turner et al. 2016; Table 1). For S. bicolor ssp. bicolor , geographic origin correlates closely with genetic distance, with West African and South African lineages separated from North African lineages (Tao et al. 2021). Seeds of the six genotypes were germinated in the dark at 25°C in Petri dishes on filter paper with fungicide solution (Maxim XL, Syngenta). The seeds were germinated for 1 week, or until they had at least 2 cm each of shoot and root tissue, prior to transplanting into 7.57 L black polypropylene pots filled with fritted clay (Emerald Profile Greens Grade; PROFILE Products LLC).

Given issues with germination and survivorship of some genotypes, ultimately, 15 full experimental blocks were established with one pot of each sorghum genotype. Three additional blocks were established with four to five genotypes, rather than the full six, to provide additional replication where possible. Genotypes were randomized spatially within blocks. Pots were irrigated with a system that included an in‐line Dosatron (model D14; Dosatron Inc.) to supply water‐soluble fertilizer. During the initial growth stages, plants were well‐watered with an irrigation solution supplemented with Grow More fertilizer (20‐20‐20, 3.90% ammoniacal N, 5.90% nitrate N, and 10.20% urea N; Grow More Inc.), delivered at 80 ppm N to all pots. Ambient irradiance provided approximately 1000 μmol photons m−2 s−1 at solar noon on a clear day. A supplementary bank of LED lights provided an additional 300 μmol photons m−2 s−1 in a 14/10 h day/night regime. The greenhouse was maintained on a day/night cycle of 28°C/17°C. At the 8‐week mark, S. bicolor plants had grown to between V4 and V8, with most individuals at V5/V6 (data not shown). Extensive non‐destructive plant phenotyping was carried out at this point (Drobnitch et al. 2021).

After 8 weeks, blocks were randomly assigned to three treatments (see Drobnitch et al. 2021 for a spatial treatment map). Five blocks were assigned to a full water‐high N (FW‐HN) treatment, which continued to receive 100% replenishment of daily evapotranspiration (ET) and 80 ppm N. Five full blocks and the three incomplete blocks were assigned to a limited water‐high N treatment (LW‐HN), which was supplied with 30% of control ET and 80 ppm N, and five blocks were assigned a limited water‐low N (LW‐LN) treatment, which was given 30% of control ET and 30 ppm N. The irrigation solution used for the LW‐LN treatment was similar to that used for the other treatments but supplemented with Hoagland stock solutions to bring essential nutrients (P, K, Na, Ca) other than N up to the full levels supplied to the FW‐HN and LW‐HN treatments while reducing N. Within the FW‐HN treatment, three blocks were selected at random to be weighed each day to track mean daily ET, providing a growth‐adjusted reference for calculating limited watering in other treatments. The FW‐HN treatment was watered by hand, resupplying the daily ET used by each plant being weighed individually, and the mean of the daily ET used among the pots being weighed was distributed to the remaining FW‐HN plants. These experimental treatments were implemented for 2 weeks, after which destructive measurements occurred. All genotypes clearly showed strong stress responses to the 2‐week drought, with daily leaf rolling and increased basal leaf senescence compared to the FW‐HN treatment. Final green leaf counts declined in the LW treatments, whereas the FW treatment continued to add 0.5–1 leaf per week (data not shown).

Non‐Destructive Measurements

Gas exchange measurements were taken during week 9 of the experiment from a subset of blocks in each treatment: three FW‐HN blocks, four LW‐HN blocks, and four LW‐LN blocks. Measurements were made between 0800 and 1100 h, which was when we found leaves to be most photosynthetically active. From each plant, the lamina of the 3rd collared leaf from the top was placed into a fluorescence/gas exchange cuvette (LI‐6400–40, LI‐COR Biosciences), avoiding the midrib. Leaves were permitted to acclimate to conditions in the cuvette (1200 PAR, 25°C T leaf, 400 ppm CO2) for 10 min or more until the rate of photosynthesis remained constant, at which point an instantaneous measurement of net photosynthetic rate (A n) was made. Temperature and vapor pressure deficit (VPD) were kept between 24.6°C–30.2°C and 1.36–2.45 kPa, respectively, for measurements. Gas exchange measurements with a LiCOR 6400, which provide calculations of stomatal conductance, photosynthetic rates, respiration rates, and percentage of active PSII sites in the leaf membrane, are highly labor‐intensive, and the tradeoff is necessarily limited sample size.

Destructive Measurements and Root Scanning

After 2 weeks of treatment implementation (10 total weeks of plant growth), shoot biomass was cut from the base of the plant. Leaves were scanned with a LI‐3100C leaf area meter (LI‐COR Biosciences). Specific leaf area (SLA) was calculated as leaf area divided by leaf mass. Most plants had a dense root mass at their base (root crown) that could not be separated into individual roots and was collected as a single unit for biomass. The remaining roots were removed from the pots, thoroughly rinsed, and collected through a 2 mm sieve. All plant material was dried at 60°C and weighed. Root systems were rehydrated for 24 h and separated into coarse and fine roots based on their branch position. Fine roots were defined as the terminal two to three branches (< 0.15 mm in diameter). The functional roles of coarse versus fine roots are distinct, even in herbaceous cereals. Coarse roots, regardless of age or absorptivity, are optimized for the long‐distance transport of water within the stele and critical for providing the backbone and reach of the root system, whereas fine roots are the major absorptive organ with a vastly larger surface area to soil volume ratio (Comas et al. 2013; Freschet, Roumet, et al. 2021). A subsection of coarse and fine roots was scanned and analyzed for root length in WinRHIZO (Regent Instruments Inc.), dried and reweighed to quantify specific root length (SRL, m g−1). Unscanned roots were dried and weighed separately. Specific root length (SRL) was calculated as root length divided by root mass. Total root length for each root fraction was determined as the product of SRL and the total root dry mass of each root fraction. Measurement and analysis of a well‐developed sorghum root system after 10 weeks of growth were required to address trade‐offs in fine versus coarse root allocation, but this was highly labor‐intensive and necessitated limited sample sizes.

N Content Measurements

Individual oven‐dried leaves used in photosynthesis and the bulk remainder of shoot material for each plant were ground and passed through a 2‐mm sieve prior to analysis of tissue N concentration via combustion (Ward Laboratories Inc.). Total N uptake was calculated by multiplying the mass of the plant material by its corresponding N concentration.

Nitrogen and Water Use Efficiency Calculations

Photosynthetic nitrogen use efficiency (PNUE) was calculated as A n divided by leaf N concentration per leaf area of the leaf used for photosynthesis measurement. Instantaneous water use efficiency (WUEi) was calculated as A n divided by leaf stomatal conductance.

Statistical Analysis

The effects of water and N availability (FW‐HN, LW‐HN, LW‐LN) and sorghum genotype, and the interaction between them, were assessed separately for limited water and full water samples using a two‐way ANOVA with N treatment, genotype, and their interaction as fixed effects and block as a random effect. Response variables were checked for normality and homogeneity of variance. All analyses were carried out using JMP Pro 18.

Genotypes had greater green shoot biomass under full water than under limited water availability (Table 2, Figure 1). Within the water‐limited treatment, N application level had no significant effect, and there was no interaction between genotype and N availability (p = 0.410 and 0.97, respectively; Table 3). Total leaf area measurements show a similar response to full water and a similar lack of response to N fertilization among genotypes (data not shown).

Photosynthesis

Net photosynthetic rates (A n) of control plants differed significantly by genotype (p = 0.0212), with Btx623 and Btx642 having a significantly greater A n than SC56 (Table 2). These genotypic differences disappeared when water was limited, with A n on average less than 50% of well‐watered A n across genotypes (Table 3, Figure 2). Greater N availability did not significantly increase A n (20.03 ± 1.53 μmol photons m−2 s−1) relative to the low N treatment across all genotypes (16.84 ± 1.64 μmol photons m−2 s−1). There was no interaction between genotype and N treatment under limited water availability.

Photosynthetic Nitrogen Use Efficiency

Mean PNUE of well‐watered plants was 137.5 ± 34.5 μmolCO2 g−1 N s−1 and varied too much to be statistically significant between genotypes (p = 0.130, Figure 3). When water was limited, PNUE was considerably lower but not significantly different across N treatments (p = 0.339; Table 3, Figure 3). There was also no significant difference among genotypes in the water‐limited treatment, nor was there an interaction between N availability and genotype (p = 0.191 and 0.616, respectively).

Fine Versus Coarse Root Metrics

Total coarse root length (TCRL) followed the same patterns as coarse root biomass across genotypes (data not shown). Genotypes had similar TCRL under full water availability (p = 0.510). Within the water‐limited treatment genotype had a significant effect (p = 0.0013, Tables 2 and 3, Figure 4A), as did N level. Under limited water availability, TCRL was much longer when N was limited, compared to when N was high (13396.7 ± 672.8 cm vs. 7798.4 ± 559.8 cm: p < 0.0001). The interaction between genotype and N level was also significant (p = 0.0013). In particular, Btx623 and Tx7000 each had significantly greater TCRL than some or all other genotypes when both water and N were limited (p < 0.05; Fisher's LSD).

Boxplot of mean instantaneous water use efficiency (WUEi) by genotype and combined watering/N treatment. Combined treatments are FW‐HN (Full water‐High N), LW‐LN (Limited water‐Low N), and LW‐HN (Limited water‐High N). Error bars indicate standard error.

Simultaneously, fine root biomass, length, and SRL showed a different pattern. Fine root biomass significantly differed between genotypes when water was available (mean = 5.10 g, p = 0.0066), with Tx7000, Btx623, and Btx642 growing more fine roots than other genotypes. When water was limited, mean fine root biomass did not significantly differ from well‐watered plants (mean = 6.04 g), and differences among genotypes were maintained when water was limited (p < 0.0001).

As a consequence, when water was limited, the ratio of fine root length to coarse root length (FR:CR) responded significantly to N availability, with the direction and magnitude of the response depending on the genotype (p = 0.0017). Water‐limited BTx623 and Tx700 grown under high N had significantly reduced allocation to coarse roots relative to those grown under low N (29.2 ± 1.5 vs. 22.1 ± 1.8; p < 0.01, Figure 4B); all other genotypes showed similar ratios of allocation to fine versus coarse roots regardless of N treatment (p < 0.0001).

Water Use Efficiency

The water‐limited, low N treatment resulted in significantly higher WUEi than water‐limited, high N plants (Figure 4; p = 0.0034). There were no significant differences among genotypes, nor significant interactions between N availability and genotypes (p = 0.11 and 0.10, respectively).

Discussion

The six diverse S. bicolor ssp. bicolor genotypes had very different responses in the allocation of their root systems to N and water limitation. In two genotypes in particular, high N under water limitation significantly reduced coarse root allocation, thus a smaller “backbone,” while leaving fine root allocation unchanged, potentially handicapping these genotypes' potential for accessing deep water sources in the soil profile (Comas et al. 2013; Eissenstat 1997; Lynch 2013). In all aboveground metrics, however, the six genotypes responded similarly to water limitation and nitrogen availability. Greater N availability under limited water availability did not result in greater rates of photosynthesis or shoot or total biomass. Thus, among the six genotypes, the hypothesis that greater N availability during water stress would increase productivity relative to LW‐LN was not supported. Moreover, photosynthetic NUE and instantaneous water use efficiency were both lower with greater N availability under limited water availability across all genotypes. Although high N did not significantly affect aboveground biomass under limited water, plants growing under limited water and high N actually produced less total biomass due to lower root biomass than those growing under limited water and N, which may have stimulated root production. It is crucial for future studies to follow up on the effects of N availability on the growth and grain yield of S. bicolor ssp. bicolor under water‐limited conditions in the field, where root growth and soil resource acquisition are not constrained by a pot, and the full functional implication of trait shifts in root architecture and allocation can be evaluated.

The observation that LW‐HN leads to a reduction in the coarse root production track is novel. In general, fine roots function primarily for resource acquisition and are cheaper and faster to produce than coarse roots, while coarse roots function primarily for structural support and long‐distance resource transport (Eissenstat 1997). As the primary resource‐acquiring organ of the root system, plants proliferate fine roots wherever patches of nutrients are encountered (Hodge 2006; Freschet, Pagès, et al. 2021), particularly when nutrient‐limited plants are fertilized (King et al. 2002). Coarse roots are structurally essential for successful exploration of deeper soils during water stress (Freschet, Pagès, et al. 2021). In this study, coarse root allocation was consistently increased under water and N limitation, although the magnitude of the increase varied among genotypes. This is consistent with previous observations of root thickening in S. bicolor subjected to water stress (Djanaguiraman et al. 2024). However, when water was limited, but N availability was high, Tx7000 and Btx623 substantially decreased allocation to coarse root production, leading to a dramatically greater ratio of fine to coarse root length. Nitrogen may affect this change in root production type via an increase in one of the many signaling and growth hormones that incorporate nitrogen, in particular abscisic acid, indole‐acetic acid, and cytokinins (Wright et al. 1983; Zhan et al. 2017; Bardhan et al. 2025; Kunwar et al. 2025).

In addition to proliferating more roots, shifts in root morphology can also lead to plants having greater root length for the same allocation in mass (quantified by SRL). Morphology leading to longer fine and coarse roots under water and N limitation is supported by previous findings. Longer SRL is frequently associated with faster root growth, which is important for a dynamic response to resource shortages, but a longer SRL also enables plants to increase root hydraulic conductance by decreasing the resistance for water entering the xylem and has been associated with drought tolerance (Ostonen et al. 2007; Zobel et al. 2007; Trubat et al. 2012; Comas et al. 2013). Increasing SRL increases the volume of soil explored per unit biomass invested in the fine roots (Ostonen et al. 2007) and can improve N uptake by crops in a field setting with heterogeneous distribution of soil nutrients (Hodge 2006). On the other hand, plants may benefit equally if SRL remains the same or even decreases under high N but limited water, as fine roots do not need to explore long distances to reach sources of N.

It has been hypothesized that high N but limited water, compared to low N and water availability, enhances N uptake and maintains physiological function to improve plant growth under water stress (Shangguan et al. 2000; Saneoka et al. 2004; Randhawa et al. 2017; Song, Li, et al. 2019). The reality of plant responses to high N under low water availability appears more complicated. While applying more N may increase N uptake, this did not result in greater shoot biomass production or improved PNUE here. Furthermore, where low N and water availability increased significant allocation to root biomass, the smaller root biomass of plants growing under high N and limited water did not benefit from a compensatory increase in shoot biomass. It may be that the N additions also stimulated early or more complete stomatal closure, reducing the total hours of gas exchange available to the growing plants. This premature stomatal closure could be the result of increased N metabolism in the plant, either relating to the production of ABA or nitrate reductase (Guo et al. 2003; Jia and Davies 2007; Wilkinson et al. 2007; Cramer et al. 2008; Dinh et al. 2017; Ding et al. 2018; Drobnitch et al. 2024). However, given that WUEi is actually higher in the low N treatment (where we would hypothesize more open stomata and more water loss, as the inverse of the above hypothesis) without a significant increase in biomass production or photosynthetic rate, our results do not align with the idea that the high N treatment was characterized by more stomatal closure. In any case, our study provides no evidence to support previous findings that higher N can relieve water stress and improve shoot growth in cereals (e.g., Cossani et al. 2012; Xiong et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2016; Song, Li, et al. 2019).

The impact of N availability on water‐stressed S. bicolor ssp. bicolor may change depending on the form of N available to plants. Previous studies have noted that S. bicolor ssp. bicolor prefers to take up more ammonium than nitrate, and the addition of ammonium as fertilizer frequently leads to increased yields relative to nitrate (Bernardo et al. 1984; Camberato and Bock 1989; Traore and Maranville 1999). Nitrogen was supplied as ammonium, nitrate, and urea in this study, but a follow‐up study would be needed to assess the differential effect of ammonium and nitrate availability across the diversity of S. bicolor ssp. bicolor germplasm under water stress and their specific signaling potentially mediating stomatal or root responses.

Successful plant growth strategies involve maximizing growth and reproduction and minimizing the cost of resource acquisition (Maire et al. 2009; Trubat et al. 2012). This study indicates that drought‐stressed grasses may experience decreased root allocation when N limitation is alleviated, but that this physiological shift may not lead to increased compensatory aboveground or reproductive capacity. Results here do, however, support a synergistic concept of co‐limiting resources where plants limited by both water and N shift their root architecture, which can increase their rooting depth in pursuit of resources, while high N under limited water availability can impede deep mining of the soil profile (Lemaire et al. 1996; Kunrath et al. 2020). Increasing root biomass is also desirable in wild and agricultural ecosystems because of the positive effects it can have on soil structure, carbon storage, and soil water holding capacity (Czarnes et al. 2000; Daynes et al. 2013; Gautam et al. 2020). Depending on the type of sorghum grown, grain or forage, most of the aboveground biomass is often removed during harvest, which leaves roots as the major sources of soil carbon. Increasing soil carbon also has implications for improving water holding capacity, which is desirable in water‐limited agroecosystems (Williams et al. 2016). From this viewpoint, the addition of N in water‐limited systems may decrease the sustainability of agroecosystems.
