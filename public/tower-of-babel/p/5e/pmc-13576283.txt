Generational variation and stabilization in resynthesized allotetraploid Brassica juncea derived from diploid progenitors B. rapa and B. nigra
Authors: Shikha Tripathi, Pooja Garg, Sneha Gupta, Ranjeet Kushwaha, Jyoti Sharma, Shiv Shankar Sharma, Sujata Kumari, Anamika Kashyap, Naveen Singh, Rashmi Yadav, Ramcharan Bhattacharya, Akhilesh Kumar, Mahesh Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolyploidy is a major driver of plant evolution and crop improvement, generating novel variation in morphology, physiology, and agronomic traits. Brassica juncea (AABB, 2n = 36), a natural allotetraploid derived from B. rapa (AA) and B. nigra (BB), is an important oilseed and vegetable crop; however, its narrow genetic base limits further breeding gains. Resynthesized B. juncea (RBJ), developed from known progenitors, provides a tractable system to investigate polyploid stabilization, trait diversification, and generational variation. This study evaluated RBJ across nine generations (F1–S8) to elucidate generational variation in morphological, molecular, cytological, and oil content traits during progressive stabilization.ResultsSubstantial variation was observed for key yield-related traits, including siliqua length, seeds per siliqua, and thousand-seed weight. High estimates of heritability, genotypic variance, and genetic advance indicated their potential utility in selection based improvement. Comparative analyses revealed a clear generational progression, characterized by relatively enhanced performance in early generations, increased recombination-driven variability in intermediate generations, and the partial stabilization of several traits in later generations. Generation mean analysis suggested the involvement of additive, dominance, and epistatic gene effects in trait inheritance. Molecular analysis using SSR markers confirmed the amphidiploid origin and genomic integrity of RBJ generations. Cytological assessments, pollen viability assays, and flow cytometric analysis collectively demonstrated stable chromosome numbers, improved fertility, and maintenance of ploidy stability across successive generations.ConclusionsThe study provides valuable insights into the generational variation and stabilization of morphological, molecular, and oil content traits in resynthesized B. juncea. The findings suggest that variability arising from polyploidization and interspecific hybridization undergoes gradual reorganization across successive generations, leading to increased trait stabilization and more consistent expression of selected agronomic characteristics. Collectively, these results contribute to the understanding of early stabilization processes in RBJ, highlighting resynthesized polyploids as useful systems for studying variation and stabilization in allopolyploid crops.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09240-z.Keywords: Resynthesized Brassica juncea, Generational stabilization, Phenotypic variability, Heritability, Generation mean analysis

Background

Polyploidy is a major driver of plant evolution, with 30–80% of angiosperms estimated to be polyploid [1–5]. Polyploids often differ from their diploid progenitors in terms of morphology, physiology, and ecology, facilitating diversification, niche expansion, and speciation [3, 5, 6]. These attributes also enhance agricultural value by improving yield, resilience, and adaptability. While the study of natural polyploids is complicated by uncertain parentage, resynthesized allopolyploids from known progenitors provide a more tractable system. Findings from these studies increasingly show that polyploidization generates novel phenotypic diversity and transcriptomic shifts, frequently driven by gene dosage effects, changes in regulatory networks, and epigenetic remodeling [3, 7–13].

Brassica juncea (AABB, 2n= 4x = 36, Indian mustard), an allotetraploid derived from interspecific hybridization between B. rapa (AA, 2n = 2x = 20) and B. nigra (BB, 2n = 2x = 16) followed by spontaneous chromosome doubling, is cultivated worldwide as an oilseed, vegetable, and condiment crop [14]. It is valued for key agronomic traits such as drought tolerance and pod shattering resistance [15], making it well-suited for cultivation in semi-arid regions. However, its limited genetic base has constrained breeding progress [4, 14], whereas its diploid progenitors possess extensive genetic diversity [7–10]. The resynthesis of B. juncea from its progenitor species presents a promising strategy for expanding its genetic variability, introducing novel alleles, and promoting inter-subgenomic recombination [12, 16]. This can also enhance heterosis and long-term adaptability [5].

The impact of polyploidy on plant morphology is well-documented, particularly in relation to early-generation genomic instability and long-term phenotypic adaptation. Over successive generations, polyploids tend to undergo morphological diversification as they adapt to genomic and physiological changes associated with genome duplication [17]. In B. juncea, such changes may be observed as enhanced vigor, increased yield potential, and modified flowering behavior traits that are pivotal to agronomic performance and adaptability [18]. Genetic parameters, including heritability, genetic variance, and gene action, play a crucial role in understanding the inheritance and expression of traits in plant breeding programs. These parameters help quantify the relative contributions of genetic and environmental factors to phenotypic variation, thereby guiding effective selection strategies [19]. Estimating these parameters enables breeders to predict genetic gain and design crosses that maximize the improvement of economically important traits [20]. Accurate assessment of genetic parameters is essential for optimizing breeding efficiency, especially in complex traits influenced by multiple genes and environmental interactions [21].

Generation mean analysis (GMA), first proposed by Hayman [22] and later elaborated by Mather and Jinks [23], is a widely used biometrical approach for studying the genetic architecture of quantitative traits in breeding populations. Unlike classical Mendelian ratio-based tests, GMA provides reliable estimates of additive, dominance, and epistatic gene effects by analyzing the mean performance of different generations. Its major advantage lies in its ability to detect non-allelic interactions such as additive additive, dominance × dominance, and additive × dominance effects, which play a crucial role in polygenic trait expression. By partitioning the components of genetic variance and identifying the predominant type of gene action, GMA helps breeders formulate appropriate breeding strategies and select suitable generations for the improvement of complex traits in breeding programs.

Molecular characterization using genome-specific markers is fundamental for validating hybridity and assessing genomic stability in polyploidy breeding programs. In the present study, simple sequence repeat (SSR) markers were employed to confirm the hybrid status of resynthesized Brassica juncea (RBJ) lines and to monitor the inheritance of A- and B-genome segments across successive generations. These markers allowed the detection of genome-specific alleles, providing clear evidence of successful hybridization and chromosome doubling, and offering insights into the genomic integrity and stability of the resynthesized lines [24]. In polyploid crops like B. juncea, such molecular tools are essential for tracing hybrid genome composition and ensuring the fidelity of resynthesis efforts. Oil content profiling further complements molecular validation by highlighting the functional effects of polyploidization. In B. juncea, variations in oil content may reflect the adaptive modulation of metabolic pathways and transcriptional control rather than direct gene dosage effects alone [25]. In RBJ lines, shifts in the biochemical (oil) trait reflect broader physiological adjustments accompanying genome merging and doubling, with potential implications for agronomic performance and product quality.

This study aims to investigate the generational dynamics and stabilization of morphological, molecular, cytological, fertility and oil content traits in resynthesized allotetraploid B. juncea, spanning from the F1 to S8 generations. By evaluating changes across these generations, this study seeks to elucidate the extent of variability introduced through polyploidization and hybridization and to understand how these traits evolve toward genomic stability, phenotypic uniformity, and agronomic potential.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growing environment

The plant material used in this study was generated over the years at ICAR–National Institute for Plant Biotechnology (ICAR-NIPB), New Delhi (Table 1). Resynthesized B. juncea (RBJ) lines (AABB; 2n = 36) pertaining to successive generations (F1, S1- S8) were developed through interspecific hybridization between B. rapa genotype NRCPB rapa 8 (female parent, AA; 2n = 20) and B. nigra genotype Nigra Tall (male parent, BB; 2n = 16). A B. juncea cultivar, Varuna (AABB; 2n = 36), was included as a reference genotype. Controlled emasculation and hand pollination were performed to obtain F1 hybrids, followed by chromosome doubling to produce synthetic amphidiploids corresponding to RBJ, following established procedures for synthetic Brassica polyploids [26–28]. Successive RBJ generations (S1-S8) were advanced through self-pollination of multiple fertile plants within each generation. Advancement to subsequent generations was primarily based on successful fertility and sufficient seed production rather than strict phenotypic selection for agronomic traits (Figs. 1 and 2). Seeds of different generations were maintained under uniform storage conditions in moisture-proof containers at approximately 4 °C until sowing to minimize seed deterioration [29]. All seed lots showed uniform and adequate field establishment under identical growing conditions, indicating comparable seed viability across generations. Field evaluation was conducted during the Rabi season (2022–23) at the experimental farm, Pusa Campus, New Delhi.

The experiment was conducted using a randomized block design (RBD) with three replications. All the evaluated plant materials were randomized within each block, ensuring that each experimental entry appeared once per block. In each block, every entry was sown in three rows, each 3 m in length, with 45 cm spacing between rows and 15 cm spacing between the plants. Standard crop management and recommended agronomic practices were uniformly applied throughout the experiment to ensure optimal plant growth.

Chromosome doubling by colchicine

Seedlings identified as interspecific in nature were subjected to chromosome doubling by treatment with 0.2% colchicine for 7 days on alternate days [30]. A cotton swab soaked in colchicine was placed over the axillary bud region for 48 h.

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was isolated from young leaves using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method, as described by [31]. The DNA pellet was dissolved in 50 µL of TE buffer. DNA integrity and quality were assessed by loading 2 µL of each sample on a 0.8% agarose gel along with a λ-DNA ladder, followed by electrophoresis and visualization under ultraviolet (UV) light.

Confirmation of hybridity and molecular diversity analysis

A set of 110 SSR markers specific to the AA (B. rapa), BB (B. nigra), and AABB (B. juncea) genomes was employed to confirm the hybridity in the F1 generation and to assess genetic diversity across all generations. The sequence information of all the SSRs markers is given in Supplementary Table 1. These markers have been documented in earlier studies [32–34]. All the SSRs were amplified by PCR in a 20 µl volume having 50 ng genomic DNA, 1.0 U Taq DNA polymerase, 1x PCR assay buffer with 1.5 mM MgCl2, 10 pmol of each primer (forward and reverse) and 100 µM dNTPs mix (Thermo Scientific). All the primers were amplified using conventional PCR with the following cycling program: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, primer annealing at 55–65 °C for 1 min (varied with primer), primer extension at 72 °C for 2 min, and final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The program was designed to retain the samples at 4 °C until they were collected and stored at -20 °C.

The amplified PCR products were mixed with 1 µl of 1X loading dye (bromophenol blue) and resolved on 2.5% agarose gels in 1X TAE buffer and stained with ethidium bromide. The bands were visualized under UV light using a gel documentation unit. Amplicons were scored manually using a 50-bp DNA ladder as a reference, and a data matrix was constructed based on amplicon size.

Pollen viability test

A common method to assess pollen viability is by staining and direct count of the pollens from anther [35]. For this purpose, anthers from mature unopened flowers were collected and placed in 1% acetocarmine solution [36]. After that, the pollen grains were carefully released onto a clean microscope slide by gently squeezing the anthers and subsequently examined under a compound light microscope at 10x magnification (Carl Zeiss, Axiolab 5, Germany). The pollen grains that were round in shape and stained red or pink were considered viable, indicating the presence of nuclear chromatin, whereas shriveled, pale, or translucent grains were considered non-viable. Cytogenetic analysis

Chromosome counting in mitotic root tip cells was performed following the protocol of [37], with modifications from [38]. Root tips were harvested in the morning and pre-treated with 0.002 M 8-hydroxyquinoline, followed by fixation in Carnoy’s solution (ethanol: acetic acid, 3:1) and storage in 70% (v/v) ethanol. For slide preparation, root tips were enzymatically digested at 37 °C for 45 min, treated with 45% acetic acid, and subsequently spread on a clean slide using a fine needle before being gently squashed under a coverslip. After freezing in liquid nitrogen and removing the coverslip, the slides were air-dried and stained with 10 µL of 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Chromosomes were visualized under UV illumination using a fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss Axiolab 5, Germany) equipped with a 100x oil-immersion objective (1000x total magnification).

Ploidy analysis using flow cytometry

Young leaves from the selected plants of each generation were used for flow cytometric analysis. Leaf tissue from the reference standard (B. juncea cv. Varuna) and from each sample was finely chopped in 1 mL of hypotonic propidium iodide (PI) lysis buffer [39] with minor modifications. The buffer contained sodium citrate tribasic dihydrate, RNase A (2 mg/mL), PI (50 µg/mL), and Tween-20 (0.3% v/v). To reduce interference from cytosolic and phenolic compounds, β-mercaptoethanol (1%) and PVP-40 (1%) were added. The homogenate was filtered through a 10-µm cell strainer and transferred into 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tubes for a 15-min incubation. The isolated nuclei suspension (2,000–5,000 nuclei per sample) was analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD Accuri, USA) equipped with a 552 nm laser and a 588/15 band-pass filter. The reference standard (Varuna) was included as an external control for each run. The fluorescence intensity (median value) of G0/G1 was recorded for the reference control and samples. Morphological traits evaluation

The morphological traits of RBJ lines pertaining to different generations, along with their parents, were investigated in the field. The observations were recorded for three randomly selected plants for each trait. The different traits analyzed were plant height (PH), main raceme length (MRL), number of siliquae on main raceme (SMR), siliqua length (SL), number of seeds per siliqua (SS), number of siliqua per plant (SP), number of primary branches (PB), number of secondary branches (SB), thousand seed weight (TSW), and oil content (OC).

Oil content analysis

Seed samples were analyzed for oil content using a non-destructive method [40]. The seeds (~ 5 g) were placed in a ring cup and scanned on a FOSS NIRS™ DS3 6500 spectrometer (FOSS, Hillerød, Denmark) and the reflectance spectra were recorded. NIRS spectra normalization, scatter correction, and statistical analysis were performed using the Global program in WINISI software version 3.10 as per Brassica model. The data for each sample was recorded in a replicated manner.

Statistical analysis

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) for seed yield component and associated agronomic traits in the RBJ generations, their progenitors, and the B. juncea cultivar Varuna was conducted following the procedures laid down by Kempthorne [41]. Genotypic and phenotypic variances, heritability, and genetic advance (GA) were estimated using the formulas of Johnson et al. [42], while GA (%) was computed as per [43]. The genotypic and phenotypic coefficients of variation (GCV and PCV) were calculated according to [44] and [45]. Correlation analysis was performed using the metan package in R v4.2.0 [46]. To assess the gene effects, scaling tests C and D were calculated following [47]. The means and variances of the parental, F1, S1 and S2 with S1, S2 and S3 generations were then used to estimate the components of gene action through the weighted least squares method [23]. Variance components (additive, dominance, and environmental) were partitioned as described by [48]. When any of the scaling tests were significant, genetic parameters were estimated following the approach of [22], which allows the assessment of the population mean (m), additive effects (d), dominance effects (h), and non-allelic interactions, such as additive × additive (i), and dominance × dominance (l).

Results

Genomic and cytological dissection of RBJ generations and their reproductive viability

Genome-based confirmation of hybridity using SSRs

Hybridity of the developed amphihaploid (F1) and successive selfed generations (S1–S8) of resynthesized B. juncea (RBJ) was assessed using a robust panel of genome-specific simple sequence repeat (SSR) markers. The marker set comprised 40 A-genome-specific, 32 B-genome-specific, and 38 AB-genome-targeting primers. From this set, 38 markers showed clear and reproducible polymorphism between the parental genotypes and were selected for further analysis (Supplementary Table 1). Among these polymorphic markers, 24 exhibited clear co-dominant amplification of alleles corresponding to both parental genomes and were therefore used to confirm hybridity (Fig. 3). The remaining 14 polymorphic markers amplified only one parental allele or produced less distinct banding patterns, and were therefore not considered sufficiently informative for hybridity confirmation. Across successive selfed generations (S1–S8), SSR analysis revealed variation in parental allele patterns among RBJ populations, likely reflecting genomic restructuring and stabilization processes commonly associated with early-generation resynthesized polyploids.

Pollen viability profiling reveals male fertility status

Pollen viability across RBJ lines from multiple generations, along with their parental genotypes and the B. juncea cultivar Varuna, is presented in Fig. 4a. Pollen sterility was pronounced in the F1 amphihaploid hybrids, with near-complete non-viability observed (approaching 100%; Fig. 4b). However, fertility restoration was evident in the S1 generation following spontaneous chromosome doubling, as indicated by over 60% pollen fertility (Fig. 4b). Across the S2 to S8 generations, the resynthesized lines demonstrated a wide range of pollen fertility, varying from 70% to nearly 85%, with an average fertility rate of approximately 77% (Fig. 4b).

Cytological confirmation of chromosome number in synthetic B. juncea

Mitotic chromosome counts were performed for all RBJ across successive generations (F1, S1–S8), along with their diploid progenitors and the B. juncea cultivar Varuna. Cytological analysis at the F1 stage revealed 18 chromosomes, confirming the amphihaploid nature of the hybrid. In contrast, the diploid parents, B. rapa and B. nigra, exhibited 2n = 20 and 2n = 16 chromosomes, respectively. All the resynthesized RBJ generations from S1 through S8 consistently displayed 36 chromosomes, identical to the chromosome complement observed in the Varuna cultivar (Fig. 5). These cytogenetic assessments confirmed the stable formation and maintenance of the expected amphidiploid chromosome complement across successive RBJ generations.

Mitotic chromosome numbers in successive generations of RBJ, compared with their diploid progenitors and the B. juncea cultivar Varuna, illustrating chromosome number stability across generations. (Scale bar: 500 μm)

Flow cytometric determination of genome ploidy level

Flow cytometric analysis was conducted to assess the ploidy levels of all RBJ lines across successive generations (S1–S8), along with their diploid progenitors and the B. juncea cultivar Varuna, which served as the tetraploid reference standard. Varuna exhibited a characteristic G0/G1 fluorescence peak at a median value of 320,421.50, corresponding to its known tetraploid (2n = 4x) status. The F1 hybrids showed a G0/G1 peak consistent with diploid (2n) ploidy, confirming their amphihaploid nature-precisely half of the genomic content of the allotetraploid Varuna. The diploid progenitors- B. rapa and B. nigra similarly displayed diploid ploidy levels. In contrast, all resynthesized RBJ lines from S1 to S8 generations exhibited tetraploid profiles, aligning closely with Varuna (Fig. 6) (Supplementary Table 2). These results, in conjunction with cytological observations, strongly support successful chromosome doubling and stable ploidy maintenance through successive generations of synthetic B. juncea.

Genetic insights from morphological trait analysis across generations

Analysis of variance was carried out on data recorded for morphological traits evaluated under a randomized block design (RBD) (Supplementary Table 3). Analysis of variance showed highly significant (P = 0.001) genotypic difference for all the studied traits. This indicated the presence of a sufficient level of genetic variability among the evaluated plant materials for yield components and associated agronomic traits, which may be exploited in future Brassica breeding initiatives.

Plant height (PH) showed high genetic variability as indicated by large genotypic variance (GV = 728.78) and phenotypic variance (PV = 773.57), coupled with high heritability (H² = 94.21%) and a genetic advance as a percentage of the mean (GAM) of 26.36%, suggesting the involvement of additive gene effects and potential responsiveness to selection. Similarly, the main raceme length (MRL) and siliquae on the main raceme (SMR) displayed high heritability estimates (82.56% and 89.36%, respectively) with GAM values of 27.22% and 40.30%, respectively, indicative of substantial genetic control over these traits. Remarkably, siliqua length (SL) and seeds per siliqua (SPS) exhibited very high heritability (94.06% and 97.75%) along with high Genotypic Coefficient of Variance (GCV: 28.71% and 72.00%) and GAM (57.37% and 146.65%), suggesting that these traits are under strong genetic influence and are amenable to effective selection. Similarly, the number of siliquae per plant (NSPP) recorded the highest phenotypic variance (PV = 3,174,473.38) and a GAM of 151.63%, indicating substantial variability for this trait. However, part of this variation may also reflect fertility recovery and stabilization processes across successive generations of resynthesized Brassica lines. Whereas the number of primary branches (NPB) showed non-significant genotypic effects and negligible detectable genetic variance, indicating predominant environmental influence on trait expression under the present experimental conditions. In contrast, the number of secondary branches (NSB) showed moderate heritability (58.65%) and a GAM of 27.15%, supporting moderate genetic control.

For the seed-related traits, thousand seed weight (TSW) and oil content (OC) showed high heritability (90.49% and 80.94%) with GAM values of 65.42% and 31.51%, respectively, indicating that these traits are largely heritable and can be improved through direct selection.

Comparative performance of resynthesized lines across successive generations relative to B. juncea cv. Varuna

The RBJ generations exhibited considerable variability for all morphological and yield-associated traits across successive generations (S1-S8) compared with the parental lines (P1 and P2) and B. juncea cv. Varuna (Fig. 7; Supplementary Table 4).

Trajectories of mean values (± SE) for different yield contributing traits across parental lines (P1: B. rapa, P2: B. nigra), successive RBJ generations (S1–S8), and the B. juncea cultivar Varuna is shown separately as a reference cultivar (Error bars represent standard error)

Plant height (PH) varied widely across all the evaluated generations ranging from 138 to 243 cm, with S6 exhibiting the minimum and S5 the maximum values among the RBJ generations. In addition to these extremes, some RBJ generations including S1 and S4 exceeded the plant height of the cultivar Varuna (213.3 cm), suggesting enhanced vegetative vigor. This pattern suggests that favorable allele combinations were rapidly expressed during the early and intermediate stages of stabilization in RBJ. The main raceme length (MRL) ranged between 41.0 and 72.8 cm, with S8 generation showing the minimum and S1 generation exhibiting the maximum expression. S1 exhibited markedly longer racemes than Varuna, suggesting improved reproductive axis development in the early generations. The number of siliquae on the main raceme (SMR) extended from 25 to 72, with the lowest value in S2 generation and the highest in the S4 generation, suggesting efficient resource allocation and raceme fertility particularly in early generations. For yield components, siliqua length (SL) spanned 2.56 to 4.97 cm, with the lowest among RBJ generations in S5 and the highest in S2, comparable to Varuna. Seeds per siliqua (SPS) showed a broad range (13.9–47.0 overall), with S4 producing the fewest and S8 the highest among the RBJ generations, while Varuna remained moderate. The number of siliquae per plant (NSPP) displayed substantial variation (347–7854), with the lowest value among RBJ generations in S2 and the highest in S6. Both differed notably from Varuna, emphasizing the variable fertility restoration among the resynthesized generations. Architectural traits also demonstrated marked differentiation across the generations. The number of primary branches (NPB) ranged from 6 to15, with S2 exhibiting the lowest branching and S3 the highest. The number of secondary branches (NSB) varied from 18 to 46, with S4 displaying the minimum and S6 the maximum. Notably, S3 surpassed Varuna in terms of branching capacity, indicating an improved canopy architecture.

Seed quality traits showed considerable variation as well. Thousand seed weight (TSW) ranged from 1.47 to 4.77 g, with S7 displaying the lowest value and S1, the highest among RBJ generations, the latter closely matching the performance of Varuna. Oil content (OC) varied between 20.3 and 46.8%, with S4 recording the lowest and S1 (37.46%) the highest among the RBJ generations, a level comparable to and slightly exceeding that of Varuna (36.84%). Overall, the performance of the RBJ lines reflects dynamic phenotypic changes across generations rather than a strictly linear progression of trait improvement. The S1 generation showed relatively higher raceme length, thousand seed weight, and oil content; however, this pattern may partly reflect reduced seed number and altered resource allocation typical of early synthetic polyploid generations with incomplete fertility stabilization. Across the intermediate generations, trait expression fluctuated, with specific strengths emerging in S2 (siliqua length), S3 (primary branching), S4 (siliquae on the main raceme), S5 (plant height), and S6 (siliquae per plant and secondary branching). These shifts suggest the dynamic recombination-driven variability that is typical of early polyploid generations. In the later stages, several traits showed signs of stabilization, with some generations matching or slightly surpassing Varuna in terms of yield components, plant architecture, and seed quality attributes. Together, these observations indicate dynamic phenotypic variation across successive RBJ generations, followed by partial stabilization of several traits in later generations, likely reflecting the gradual consolidation of favorable phenotypic and allelic combinations during RBJ stabilization. While some generations approached or matched Varuna for specific agronomic traits, the results primarily highlight the value of RBJ populations as sources of phenotypic diversity and experimental material for studying stabilization processes in newly resynthesized polyploids.

Correlation studies

A comprehensive correlation analysis revealed that siliqua length (SL) plays a crucial role in determining the yield in B. juncea (Fig. 8). Siliqua length (SL) showed one of the strongest positive correlations with seeds per siliqua (SPS) (r = 0.79), indicating that plants with a higher number of seeds per siliqua tend to exhibit longer siliques. SL was negatively correlated with the number of siliquae per plant (NSPP) (r = − 0.81), suggesting that plants investing in longer siliquae typically produce fewer siliquae. Thousand seed weight (TSW) aligned positively with SL (r = 0.71) and negatively with SPS (r = − 0.71), reflecting differences in how plants allocate resources to seed development. Oil content (OC) showed positive relationships with SL (r = 0.52) and TSW (r = 0.48), implying that longer siliquae and heavier seeds favor oil accumulation. Architectural traits such as plant height (PH) and main raceme length (MRL) were also strongly associated (r = 0.66), with MRL showing a moderate correspondence with siliqua production on the main raceme (SMR) (r = 0.68). Pollen viability (PV) also showed moderate positive associations with seeds per siliqua (SPS) and thousand seed weight (TSW), suggesting that progressive fertility restoration may contribute to improved reproductive performance across RBJ generations. Overall, the results underscore that yield potential in B. juncea is not driven by a single trait, but rather emerges from the integrated effects of siliqua morphology, seed number, and seed weight acting in unison.

Generation mean analysis results

Generation mean analysis was performed to determine the nature and magnitude of gene effects influencing yield associated traits in a cross between B. rapa (NRCPB rapa 8; P1) and B. nigra (Nigra tall; P2). The analysis incorporated parental lines (P1, P2), their hybrid, and successive selfed generations (S1, S2, and S3) to examine inheritance patterns under the present experimental framework (Table 3). In the analyses we found that scaling test C was significant for plant height (PH: 55** cm), number of siliquae on main raceme (SMR: -98.67**), siliqua length (SL: 3.58** ), number of seeds per siliqua (SPS: -16.12**), number of siliquae per plant (NSPP: -7235.33**), number of secondary branches (NSB: -18.67**), and thousand seed weight (TSW: 3.97**). Scaling test D was significant for PH, main raceme length (MRL: -28.5*), SMR, SL, and SPS. The significance of these scaling tests suggested that the additive–dominance model alone was inadequate for most traits, implying the possible involvement of non-allelic gene interactions. Consequently, a five-parameter model was used to estimate the different genetic effects.

The significant epistatic components observed for several traits suggest the involvement of non-additive interactions in trait inheritance under the present generation mean analytical framework, indicating that both additive and non-additive effects may contribute to phenotypic variation in the RBJ generations.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the genetic, cytogenetic, and molecular characteristics of RBJ (S1–S8) derived from interspecific hybridization between B. rapa and B. nigra, and compared them with a cultivated B. juncea genotype. Because seed lots from different generations were produced in different years, variation in storage duration cannot be completely excluded as a potential factor influencing early plant establishment. However, all seed lots were maintained under uniform storage conditions and evaluated simultaneously under identical field conditions, and showed uniform field establishment, suggesting minimal differences in seed vigor among generations. The discussion below integrates molecular validation, cytogenetic confirmation, genetic parameter estimation, generational phenotypic evaluation, trait associations, and gene action analyses under a generation mean analytical framework to provide a comprehensive understanding of RBJ stability, fertility, phenotypic variation and their potential relevance for Brassica improvement.

SSR-based genotyping provided robust molecular evidence for the amphidiploid origin of the RBJ lines by confirming the simultaneous presence of alleles derived from both parental genomes. The detection of co-dominant amplification patterns using genome-specific SSR markers in the amphihaploid (F1) and successive selfed generations (S1–S8) validates true hybridity and stable transmission of parental genomic components, as illustrated by representative amplification profiles, consistent with the established utility of SSR markers for hybridity assessment in polyploid crops [49, 50]. Importantly, the extensive allelic variation observed across successive generations likely reflects dynamic genome restructuring during early polyploid stabilization, a phenomenon widely reported in newly formed and resynthesized allopolyploids and attributed to homoeologous recombination, genomic rearrangements, and altered allele dosage [51, 52]. Together, these results confirm the synthetic origin of RBJ lines and demonstrate the usefulness of SSR markers for validating hybridity and monitoring parental allele inheritance in resynthesized Brassica populations.

While SSR markers provided reliable confirmation of hybridity and allowed monitoring of allele inheritance across generations, they offer limited genomic resolution for investigating genome-wide processes in resynthesized polyploids. Consequently, the present study cannot directly address mechanisms such as subgenome dominance, homoeologous exchange, or broader genome stabilization dynamics. Higher-resolution genomic approaches, including genotyping-by-sequencing (GBS) or whole-genome resequencing, would enable a more detailed characterization of these processes in RBJ. Such analyses are currently being pursued in to further investigate genomic restructuring and subgenome interactions in these RBJ lines.

Cytological and reproductive analyses further supported the progressive stabilization of the resynthesized generations. The pollen viability assay revealed variable but generally improved male fertility status across successive RBJ generations. Chromosome counts consistently confirmed the expected amphidiploid number (2n = 36), and flow cytometry indicated stable ploidy levels across generations. The observed increase in pollen fertility is therefore more likely associated with progressive meiotic stabilization and improved chromosome pairing during early generations of the resynthesized polyploid rather than changes in chromosome number. Similar patterns of fertility recovery accompanying meiotic stabilization have been reported in newly formed Brassica allopolyploids [13, 27]. Together, these results indicate increasing cytogenetic stability of RBJs upon selfing, which may support their future use as pre-breeding material in Brassica improvement programs.

The assessment of genetic parameters revealed substantial variation across parental lines, RBJ generations, and B. juncea cultivar. Traits such as plant height, main raceme length, siliqua length, seeds per siliqua, and thousand seed weight showed relatively high estimated heritability and genetic advance under the present experimental conditions suggesting their potential responsiveness to selection. Conversely, traits like number of primary branches displayed lower heritability, implying greater environmental influence or non-additive gene effects, while secondary branches showed only moderate genetic control. These findings highlight key yield-related traits as promising targets for selection and align with earlier reports that resynthesized Brassica polyploids display broad variability owing to segregation, genomic rearrangements and novel allelic combinations [13, 27].

However, because the RBJ generations represent segregating populations advanced through multiple selfed plants and evaluated under a single environmental condition rather than fixed independent genotypes, the reported heritability and genetic variability parameters should be interpreted as comparative estimates reflecting relative phenotypic consistency among generations rather than precise measures of true genetic heritability. Variation among generations may therefore arise from segregation, fertility restoration, and stabilization processes commonly observed in newly formed Brassica polyploids, particularly in the earlier generations where limited sampling may influence variance estimation.

The generational patterns revealed by the comparative performance of the RBJ lines across generations relative to that of B. juncea cv. Varuna closely mirror the stabilization processes typically observed during the early stages of allopolyploid development. The relatively enhanced performance of S1 across multiple morphological and seed-quality traits may reflect the initial heterotic effects commonly reported in newly synthesized Brassica allopolyploids, where novel allele combinations and expression patterns emerge immediately after genome merging [53]. Such early-generation shifts in trait expression may also reflect compensatory relationships among yield components, as seed number, seed size, and reproductive structures in crop plants often compete for assimilates during reproductive development [19, 54]. The fluctuations in trait performance across the intermediate generations (S2–S6) are consistent with the phenotypic instability and genomic changes commonly associated with early-generation polyploids, including homoeologous recombination, and chromosomal adjustments [28]. Similar patterns of early instability followed by progressive stabilization have been documented in resynthesized B. napus, where stabilization and fertility were shown to be strongly influenced by interactions between parental genotypes and allelic variants affecting meiosis [55]. Recent studies on resynthesized Brassica juncea have likewise demonstrated substantial phenotypic and molecular variability in early generations, highlighting the importance of synthetic polyploid populations for studying stabilization processes and generating novel trait combinations [26]. By the later generations, several traits in the RBJ lines showed partial stabilization, with some generations approaching the performance of Varuna for specific traits reflecting transition towards stabilization [56].

The patterns observed in the RBJ generations are broadly consistent with previous studies on resynthesized Brassica polyploids, where early generations often exhibit phenotypic variability, fertility instability, and genomic restructuring before gradual stabilization in later generations [13, 27]. Similar processes have been reported in other newly formed allopolyploids, where homoeologous recombination, segregation, allele dosage changes, and selection across generations contribute to the stabilization of both genomic structure and phenotypic traits [57, 58].

Correlation analysis highlighted important relationships among agronomic traits. Siliqua length exhibited strong positive associations with seeds per siliqua and thousand seed weight, indicating that these characteristics can be targeted simultaneously in selection programs to enhance productivity. Conversely, negative associations between siliqua length and number of siliquae per plant and between thousand seed weight and seeds per siliqua point to inherent trade-offs that breeders must carefully balance. Pollen viability also showed positive associations with several reproductive and seed-related traits, suggesting that progressive fertility restoration may contribute to improved reproductive performance across RBJ generations. These patterns are consistent with physiological linkages reported in other Brassica species, where siliqua length has been shown to positively influence seed weight in B. napus [59] and similar positive correlations of siliqua traits and seed yield have been demonstrated in B. juncea [60].

Generation mean analysis provided additional insights into the genetic control of these traits. Classical generation mean analysis (GMA) commonly employs six generations–including backcrosses (P1, P2, F1, F2, BC1, and BC2) to partition additive, dominance, and epistatic effect [61, 62], but our study utilized a five-generation design (P1, P2, S1, S2, S3) composed solely of consecutive selfed generations. While the absence of backcrosses limits certain contrast-based estimates, selfed generations such as S2 and S3 remain informative for observing allele fixation, segregation, and persistence of epistatic interactions – factors relevant to stabilization and trait inheritance in resynthesized polyploids. Under the present analytical framework, scaling tests suggested that the additive-dominance model may not fully explain variation for several traits indicating possible non allelic interactions. Additive effects were favorable for siliqua length, seeds per siliqua, thousand seed weight, and oil content, while negative additive effects were observed for plant height, siliquae on the main raceme, number of siliquae per plant, and secondary branches. Dominance effects contributed variably across traits, and significant additive × additive and dominance × dominance epistatic interactions for most traits suggested the involvement of both additive and non-additive genetic effects during early RBJ stabilization [63–65]. However, these interpretations should be considered within the limitations of the present experimental design and sampling framework.

By combining phenotypic, molecular, and cytogenetic evidence, the present study demonstrates that resynthesized B. juncea is progressively stabilizing across generations, with fertility restoration and trait performance for several traits approaching that of B. juncea cultivar. Importantly, RBJs represent new combinations of parental alleles generated through interspecific hybridization and associated genomic changes, resulting in expanded variability, among agronomic traits. The novelty of this study lies in its integrated, generation-by-generation evaluation of morphological performance, quantitative genetic parameters, and cytogenetic stability across successive RBJ generations, providing insights into the process of polyploid stabilization in B. juncea. These findings contribute both to the broader understanding of phenotypic variation and stabilization in synthetic polyploids and may support future efforts aimed at broadening the genetic base of cultivated mustard. Future work should involve multi-environment evaluation of advanced RBJ lines and integration of higher-resolution genomic tools such as SNP arrays or pangenome-based analyses to better characterize genomic restructuring and identify loci underlying agronomic performance.

Conclusion

The present study elucidates generational changes in morphological traits, genetic parameters, and ploidy stability in resynthesized B. juncea, providing insights into similarities and differences among successive RBJ generations, their diploid progenitors, and the cultivated B. juncea variety. The results demonstrate that RBJ generations harbor substantial phenotypic and quantitative genetic variability for yield-related and associated agronomic traits, with key characters such as siliqua length, seeds per siliqua, and thousand seed weight exhibiting high heritability, genotypic variance, and genetic advance, indicating their potential utility in selection based improvement programs. Comparative analyses revealed distinct generational transitions, characterized by relatively enhanced heterotic gains in the S1 generation, followed by segregation and recombination-associated variation and progressive trait stabilization in later generations. Correlation analyses highlighted the integrated contribution of siliqua morphology, seed number, and seed weight to yield, revealing both synergistic relationships and trade-offs critical for multi-trait selection. Generation mean analysis suggested the involvement of additive, dominance, and significant epistatic interactions in trait inheritance, emphasizing the need to consider both additive and non-additive gene effects in future breeding and selection strategies. Molecular validation using SSR markers confirmed the amphidiploid origin of RBJs, while cytological analyses and pollen viability assays demonstrated stable chromosome numbers, consistent ploidy levels, and improved male fertility across generations. Overall, the RBJ populations represent valuable experimental and pre-breeding material for investigating stabilization processes and generating phenotypic variability. Collectively, these findings provide important insights into the generational variation and stabilization of morphological, molecular, and cytogenetic traits in RBJ, illustrating how variability introduced through polyploidization and hybridization may progressively transition towards greater stability, phenotypic consistency, and improved performance for specific agronomic traits across generations.
