Comparative safety and effectiveness of stents in drainage of pancreatic fluid collections guided by endoscopic ultrasonography: A systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Shengbiao Chen, Yaxin Zhang, Yuwei Wu, Bibi Chen, Handong Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Endoscopic ultrasonography-guided drainage is an effective treatment for pancreatic fluid collections (PFCs). However, the optimal stent strategy for endoscopic ultrasonography-guided drainage of PFCs is still unclear. This study aims to compare the clinical outcomes of different stent types to identify the best option.Methods:Databases including PubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane were searched up to August 2024. The analysis focused on comparisons between bi-flanged metal stents (BFMS), double-pigtail plastic stents (DPPS), fully covered self-expanding metal stents (FCSEMS), lumen-apposing metal stents (LAMS), and the combination of LAMS and DPPS (LAMS + DPPS).Results:A total of 38 studies, involving 4265 patients with PFCs, were analyzed. In all of these patients, LAMS (risk ratio [RR]: 1.07, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.02–1.13) and BFMS (RR: 1.08, 95% CI: 1.01–1.15) showed higher clinical success rates compared with DPPS. LAMS + DPPS reduced infection rates compared with BFMS (RR: 0.32, 95% CI: 0.10–0.99), DPPS (RR: 0.19, 95% CI: 0.06–0.62), and FCSEMS (RR: 0.23, 95% CI: 0.07–0.76). Within the pancreatic pseudocysts subgroup, LAMS (RR: 1.08, 95% CI: 1.01–1.16) and LAMS + DPPS (RR: 1.24, 95% CI: 1.03–1.48) demonstrated higher clinical success rates compared with DPPS. Similarly, in the walled-off necrosis subgroup, LAMS (RR: 1.10, 95% CI: 1.02–1.17), LAMS + DPPS (RR: 1.19, 95% CI: 1.01–1.41), FCSEMS (RR: 1.14, 95% CI: 1.03–1.26), and BFMS (RR: 1.11, 95% CI: 1.03–1.20) all showed higher success rates compared with DPPS, with BFMS exhibiting a lower bleeding rate than DPPS (RR: 0.32, 95% CI: 0.18–0.99).Conclusion:Newer metal stents, including BFMS, LAMS, and LAMS + DPPS, offer advantages over plastic stents like DPPS alone. BFMS is preferable when bleeding risk is a concern, while LAMS + DPPS is more effective in reducing infection rates in PFCs.

1. Introduction

Pancreatic fluid collections (PFCs) frequently complicate pancreatic disease, significantly compromising patients’ quality of life and overall health.[1,2] Endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS)-guided drainage has become a key intervention for PFCs, enabling effective fluid drainage and symptom relief.[3,4] Initially, double-pigtail plastic stents (DPPS) were widely used, but their narrow diameter often led to complications like stent occlusion.[5] Advances in technology introduced fully covered self-expanding metal stents (FCSEMS) with wider lumens, improving drainage efficacy.[6] However, issues like stent displacement and peritonitis persisted, leading to the development of bi-flanged metal stents (BFMS) and lumen-apposing metal stents (LAMS), designed to enhance stability and safety during drainage procedures.[7,8] Nevertheless, the widespread adoption of LAMS has raised safety concerns, particularly regarding reports of severe bleeding shortly after implantation.[9,10] Combining LAMS with DPPS has been suggested to mitigate these risks, but clinical evidence supporting this approach is limited.[11]

Despite advancements in stent technology, there remains a lack of conclusive evidence to determine the optimal stent strategy.[12,13] Furthermore, previous meta-analyses have been limited in scope, often comparing only 2 stent types. Park et al conducted a notable network meta-analysis, currently the only one of its kind in the field, evaluating DPPS, FCSEMS, and LAMS.[14] However, due to the limited number of studies available at that time, their analysis focused solely on DPPS, FCSEMS, and LAMS, without evaluating metrics such as hospital stay and procedure time. Therefore, our study performed a network meta-analysis to refine the classification and provide a more comprehensive analysis of stent strategies used in clinical practice, aiming to offer evidence closer to real-world application.

2. Methods

The review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses statement.[15] This review was registered prospectively in Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews under the registration number CRD42024579250.

2.1. Search strategy and inclusion criteria

A systematic search was carried out across PubMed, EMBASE, and the Cochrane database up to August 2024, supplemented by ongoing and completed studies identified through ClinicalTrials.gov. The search details are provided in Supplementary Table 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1. We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and non-RCTs without restrictions on time, location, or language. Exclusions included case reports, abstracts, comments, and letters. The population consisted of patients with PFCs, including pancreatic pseudocysts (PP) and walled-off necrosis (WON).[16,17] The intervention involved EUS-guided drainage with any type of stent.

2.2. Outcomes

The outcomes were categorized into primary and secondary outcomes. The primary outcome was defined as clinical success, indicated by a reduction in PFC size by more than 50% at 4 weeks postoperatively, along with the complete resolution of clinical symptoms. Secondary outcomes included death, bleeding, recurrence, occlusion, infection, stent migration, hospital stay, procedure time, and total cost.

2.3. Data extraction

Two researchers (SC and YZ) conducted separate examinations of the complete manuscripts of eligible studies and extracted pertinent data to create a database. All differences among the investigators were resolved through dialogue, and if needed, by the determination of a third assessor.

2.4. Risk of bias and quality of evidence assessment

For the RCTs, the Risk of Bias (RoB) 2 tool (The Cochrane Collaboration) was used to evaluate the risk of bias.[18] For non-RCTs, the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions tool was applied,[19] and the credibility of the network meta-analysis results was assessed using the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) application.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Data were presented as numbers and ratios for dichotomous variables, and as means with standard deviations for continuous variables. Currency values were standardized using exchange rates from the same year. If the original paper presented data other than the mean and standard deviation, we employed the method described by Shi et al to assess the normality of the data and recalculated them using the methods described by Luo et al and Wan et al.[20–22] For continuous variables, results were expressed as mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), while dichotomous data were represented as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs.

Stata 15 software (StataCorp) was utilized for the network meta-analysis to estimate effect sizes using consistency or inconsistency models. For closed-loop treatments, node-splitting analysis was employed to assess inconsistency between direct and indirect comparisons. Probability values were synthesized and presented as the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA), accompanied by treatment rankings. The efficacy of each intervention was expressed as a percentage relative to an assumed optimal intervention, with higher SUCRA values indicating a greater likelihood of effectiveness.

Egger’s test was applied to detect publication bias among studies, with a minimum inclusion of 10 studies. Additional assessments of asymmetry and publication bias across studies were performed using a comparison-adjusted funnel plot alongside Egger’s test, with a P-value below .10 indicating significant asymmetry and publication bias.[23]

3.2. Primary outcomes

Figures 2 to 5 present the direct evidence of primary endpoints obtained from the network meta-analyses, focusing on clinical success rates. In the overall analysis, both the LAMS group (RR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.02–1.13) and the BFMS group (RR: 1.08, 95% CI: 1.01–1.15) demonstrated significantly higher clinical success rates than the DPPS group (Fig. 2). This promising trend continued in the subgroup analysis for PP, where the LAMS group (RR: 1.08, 95% CI: 1.01–1.16) and the LAMS + DPPS combination (RR: 1.24, 95% CI: 1.03–1.48) both demonstrated higher success rates compared with the DPPS group (Fig. 3). When examining WON, several stent types also performed well: the FCSEMS (RR: 1.14, 95% CI: 1.03–1.26), LAMS (RR: 1.10, 95% CI: 1.02–1.17), LAMS + DPPS (RR: 1.19, 95% CI: 1.01–1.41), and BFMS (RR: 1.11, 95% CI: 1.03–1.20) all exhibited superior success rates compared with the DPPS group (Fig. 4). Moreover, specific stent types such as NAGI (RR: 1.09, 95% CI: 1.02–1.16) and AXIOS (RR: 1.06, 95% CI: 1.01–1.12) further reinforced this trend, demonstrating higher success rates relative to the DPPS group (Fig. 5).

3.3. Secondary outcomes

The network meta-analysis results for secondary outcomes are detailed in Figures 2 to 5 and Supplementary Figures 5 to 8, Supplemental Digital Content 6. Due to the limited number of included studies and heterogeneity, the subgroup analyses for PP were restricted to evaluating only clinical success, recurrence, infection, and bleeding, while for WON, they were limited to assessing only clinical success, death, recurrence, occlusion, infection, and bleeding. Where direct comparisons revealed no significant differences, we report SUCRA values, which rank treatments based on their probability of being the best option; a higher SUCRA percentage indicates a greater likelihood of superiority.

3.3.1. Death rates

When examining death rates, no significant differences emerged among the groups. Nonetheless, the LAMS + DPPS group attained the highest SUCRA ranking of 91.1% (Supplementary Fig. 5, Supplemental Digital Content 7). This trend persisted in the subgroup analysis for WON, where the LAMS + DPPS group again secured the top spot with a ranking of 91.1% (Supplementary Fig. 7, Supplemental Digital Content 8). Similarly, in the subgroup analysis by specific stents, the AXIOS + DPPS group also demonstrated strong performance, achieving a SUCRA ranking of 86.6% (Supplementary Fig. 8, Supplemental Digital Content 9).

3.3.2. Recurrence rates

The overall PFC analysis showed no significant differences in recurrence rates across groups (Fig. 2); however, the LAMS group had a SUCRA ranking of 81.0%, suggesting a higher probability of being the most effective option (Supplementary Fig. 5, Supplemental Digital Content 7). Notably, the subgroup analysis for PP indicated that the LAMS group experienced significantly lower recurrence rates compared with the DPPS group (RR: 0.38, 95% CI: 0.16–0.89; Fig. 3). Furthermore, the subgroup analyses by WON and specific stents revealed no significant differences in recurrence rates, with the LAMS group, including its AXIOS model, maintaining the highest SUCRA ranking (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8, Supplemental Digital Content 8).

3.3.3. Occlusion rates

In the overall PFC analysis, no significant differences in occlusion rates were found (Fig. 2), with the LAMS + DPPS scheme ranking highest at 92.1% by SUCRA (Supplementary Fig. 5, Supplemental Digital Content 7). In the WON subgroup, LAMS + DPPS significantly reduced occlusion rates compared with DPPS (RR: 0.23, 95% CI: 0.07–0.74) and BFMS (RR: 0.27, 95% CI: 0.09–0.85; Fig. 4). Among specific stents, its AXIOS + DPPS variant achieved a SUCRA ranking of 67.4% (Supplementary Fig. 8, Supplemental Digital Content 9).

3.3.4. Infection rates

In the overall PFC analysis, the LAMS group (RR: 0.38, 95% CI: 0.19–0.75) and FCSEMS group (RR: 0.45, 95% CI: 0.22–0.95) significantly lowered infection rates compared with the DPPS group, with the LAMS + DPPS scheme further reducing rates versus BFMS (RR: 0.32, 95% CI: 0.10–0.99), DPPS (RR: 0.19, 95% CI: 0.06–0.62), and FCSEMS (RR: 0.23, 95% CI: 0.07–0.76; Fig. 2).

For PP, the LAMS group markedly reduced infection rates compared with DPPS (RR: 0.18, 95% CI: 0.03–0.92), and LAMS + DPPS showed even greater reductions versus DPPS (RR: 0.02, 95% CI: 0.00–0.56) and FCSEMS (RR: 0.02, 95% CI: 0.00–0.71; Fig. 3).

In the WON subgroup, LAMS significantly outperformed DPPS (RR: 0.46, 95% CI: 0.23–0.91) and FCSEMS (RR: 0.42, 95% CI: 0.19–0.92; Fig. 4).

Among specific stents, AXIOS reduced infection rates compared with DPPS (RR: 0.42, 95% CI: 0.18–0.99), and its AXIOS + DPPS variant further lowered rates versus DPPS (RR: 0.22, 95% CI: 0.07–0.73), while WALLFLEX (RR: 4.90, 95% CI: 1.21–19.79) and MT-ST33-103 (RR: 4.58, 95% CI: 1.17–18.01) showed higher rates compared with AXIOS + DPPS (Fig. 5).

3.3.5. Bleeding incidences

In the overall analysis of PFCs, bleeding rates showed no notable differences across the stent groups (Fig. 2), yet the BFMS stood out with a SUCRA ranking of 92.0%, hinting at its potential as a top performer (Supplementary Fig. 5, Supplemental Digital Content 7). For PP, no significant variations in bleeding rates emerged, with the LAMS earning the highest SUCRA ranking of 80.4% (Supplementary Fig. 6, Supplemental Digital Content 6). However, this shifted in the WON subgroup, where BFMS markedly reduced bleeding rates compared with DPPS (RR: 0.42, 95% CI: 0.18–0.99; Fig. 4). When examining specific stent models, no significant differences surfaced, though the NAGI had the highest SUCRA ranking of 71.8% (Supplementary Fig. 8, Supplemental Digital Content 9).

3.3.6. Stent migration

Stent migration was exclusively evaluated within the subgroup analysis of specific stent models. In the subgroup analysis of specific stents, the MT-ST33-103 stent exhibited significantly lower stent migration rates compared with DPPS (RR: 0.17, 95% CI: 0.04–0.76), NAGI (RR: 0.11, 95% CI: 0.02–0.60), and WALLFLEX (RR: 0.14, 95% CI: 0.03–0.64; Fig. 5).

3.3.7. Hospital stays

In the overall PFC analysis, the LAMS group exhibited significantly shorter stays compared with the DPPS group, with an MD of 2.95 days (95% CI: 0.11–5.79; Fig. 2). The subgroup analysis by specific stents further highlighted variations among specific stent types within the LAMS group when compared with the DPPS group. The NAGI stent showed an MD of 3.20 days (95% CI: 2.05–4.35), indicating a substantial reduction in hospital stay. The AXIOS stent demonstrated an MD of 3.70 days (95% CI: 0.82–6.59), while the AXIOS + DPPS combination achieved the most significant reduction, with an MD of 7.60 days (95% CI: 0.13–15.07). The SPAXUS stent recorded an MD of 2.00 days (95% CI: 0.01–4.00; Fig. 5). Additionally, the AXIOS + DPPS combination stood out with a SUCRA ranking of 92.1%, suggesting a high likelihood of being the most effective option for minimizing hospital stay duration among the stent types assessed (Supplementary Fig. 8, Supplemental Digital Content 9).

3.3.8. Procedure times

In the overall PFC analysis, the analysis indicated that the DPPS group had significantly longer procedure times compared with the LAMS group, with an MD of 21.36 minutes (95% CI: 7.12–35.59; Fig. 2). Subgroup analysis highlighted that the AXIOS group had significantly shorter procedure times compared with the DPPS group, recording an MD of −17.07 minutes (95% CI: −33.49 to 0.66; Fig. 5).

3.3.9. Total costs

As for total costs, there were no significant differences observed among the various groups in the overall PFC analysis (Fig. 2). Nevertheless, the LAMS group held the highest SUCRA ranking at 81.3% (Supplementary Fig. 5, Supplemental Digital Content 7), suggesting a potential advantage in cost-effectiveness. Subgroup analyses for PP, WON, or specific stents were not conducted.

3.4. Risk of bias and quality assessment

For RCTs (Supplementary Fig. 9, Supplemental Digital Content 10), most studies showed a low risk of bias, though some had issues with randomization and missing data, resulting in an overall moderate risk of bias. For non-RCTs (Supplementary Fig. 10, Supplemental Digital Content 11), the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions tool indicated a higher risk of bias, particularly due to confounding and inconsistent outcome measurements.

CINeMA evaluations of outcomes (Supplementary Figs. 11–16, Supplemental Digital Content 12) indicated that confidence in the network meta-analysis results ranged from moderate to very low. Key concerns include within-study bias, imprecision, and indirectness, which significantly impact the overall confidence ratings across different comparisons.

4. Discussion

We found that BFMS, LAMS, and LAMS + DPPS all demonstrated significant advantages over DPPS in terms of clinical success, contrasting with earlier meta-analyses that were limited by the number of included studies. Our study included more relevant studies, providing a more comprehensive analysis. While DPPS remains a viable option due to its established role and ease of use, our findings suggest that advanced stent technologies like BFMS and LAMS may offer superior outcomes, especially in clinical success rates.[13,60]

Our findings also highlight the evolving landscape of stent usage. Historically, DPPS has been a mainstay in clinical practice due to its simplicity and availability. However, our data suggest a shift towards more advanced stents like LAMS and BFMS, which appear to offer better clinical outcomes in certain patient populations. The use of LAMS combined with DPPS, designed to enhance stent patency and reduce adverse events, showed promise, particularly in reducing post-procedural infection rates.[47,30] Nonetheless, beyond the advantage in infection rates, LAMS + DPPS did not show significant improvements in clinical success or other outcomes compared with LAMS alone. This finding suggests that while the combination may be beneficial in specific contexts, LAMS alone remains a robust option.

Even within the same stent type, such as AXIOS and SPAXUS, structural differences exist. For instance, Takasaki et al found that AXIOS has a higher anchoring force, which reduces the risk of stent migration compared with SPAXUS.[61] However, a recent RCT reported that the rigid spines of AXIOS may increase the risk of bleeding relative to SPAXUS.[57] Therefore, we conducted a more detailed comparison of various clinically common stents. While the MT-ST33-103 stent showed a statistically significant reduction in migration, its limited global use and the inclusion of only 1 study warrant cautious interpretation of these results.[38] For the other stents compared, no statistically significant differences in migration or bleeding rates were found, suggesting that the choice among them may ultimately depend on other factors, such as physician preference or specific patient characteristics.

One of the strengths of our study is the comprehensive assessment of costs. We evaluated the economic costs of DPPS, FCSEMS, and LAMS, while time costs, including hospital stay and procedure time, were thoroughly analyzed across all 5 stent strategies. LAMS showed clear advantages over DPPS in reducing both hospital stay and procedure time. Although LAMS has a higher initial cost, this is offset by reduced hospital stays and lower infection rates, leading to no significant difference in total cost between LAMS and DPPS. These findings emphasize the importance of considering both economic and time costs when selecting the most appropriate stent strategy in clinical practice. However, the generalizability of cost-related findings may be limited by differences in device prices, hospitalization costs, and reimbursement policies across healthcare systems.

These clinical findings should also be interpreted considering differences across the included studies. RCTs and retrospective studies may represent different clinical contexts, with RCTs generally using more standardized protocols and retrospective studies reflecting real-world variation in patient selection, procedural strategy, and follow-up practice. Operator experience and institutional practice may also influence outcomes such as procedure time, adverse events, and the need for further intervention.[50,55] Therefore, the optimal stent strategy should not be determined solely by pooled ranking results but should be individualized according to collection type, bleeding risk, infection or occlusion risk, and local resource availability.

This study has some limitations. First, although the total sample size was relatively large, most included studies were retrospective. In these studies, stent selection may have been influenced by patient characteristics, disease severity, collection complexity, and operator preference, which may introduce selection bias and residual confounding, thereby limiting causal inference. In addition, although clinical success in our analysis was defined as a reduction in PFC size by more than 50% at 4 weeks postoperatively, together with complete resolution of clinical symptoms, the exact definitions varied slightly across the original studies, including differences in imaging criteria, symptom assessment, and follow-up time points, which may have influenced the pooled estimates.[50,57] Additionally, the recent introduction of 20-mm LAMS products resulted in varying LAMS diameters across studies. While a 20-mm LAMS may offer superior drainage, it could also suffer from a “valve” effect, where necrotic tissue seals the stent opening, potentially diminishing its clinical benefits.[62] Although Boxhoorn et al found no significant difference in outcomes between 15- and 20-mm stents, our study lacked sufficient trials to perform a subgroup analysis by LAMS diameter.[48] Furthermore, the CINeMA evidence grading indicated moderate to low confidence in some outcomes, highlighting the need for future RCTs to more thoroughly evaluate different stent types and procedural contexts to strengthen the evidence base.

5. Conclusion

BFMS, LAMS, and LAMS + DPPS, as newer metal stent strategies, offer significant benefits over the plastic stent DPPS for EUS-guided drainage of PFCs. In addition, BFMS is linked to a lower risk of bleeding compared with DPPS, while LAMS + DPPS is more effective in reducing infection rates compared with BFMS and DPPS. Therefore, the choice of a novel metal stent should be tailored to the specific clinical context, including bleeding risk, infection risk, collection complexity, and resource availability.
