Phylogenomic discordance among plastid genes in endangered Maltese Limonium
Authors: Dolores R. Agius, Peter Poczai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chloroplast genomes are widely used in plant phylogenetics, yet plastome-wide analyses increasingly reveal heterogeneity among gene trees. Limonium, a genus of over 600 species, centred in the Mediterranean basin, exhibits substantial taxonomic complexity driven by hybridization, apomixis, and polyploidy, but plastome data from its centre of diversity have been lacking. We report the first complete plastomes from Mediterranean Limonium, sequencing the endangered Maltese endemics L. melitense (154,139 bp) and L. zeraphae (154,142 bp), and compare them with seven plastomes from the Irano-Turanian and Chinese-Japanese regions. Genome sizes range from 150,515 to 174,033 bp, with variation primarily associated with inverted repeat (IR) expansion and contraction and differences in repeat content. Highly variable coding genes (ndhF, ycf1, rpl32) and several intergenic regions were identified as candidate molecular markers, and 382 simple sequence repeats were detected across nine plastomes. Structural comparisons revealed IR boundary shifts, repeat variation, and pseudogenization of rpl22 in L. zeraphae. Plastome-wide maximum likelihood analyses strongly supported the monophyly of Limonium and recovered geographically structured clades. To evaluate phylogenetic concordance, we analysed individual plastid protein-coding genes and inferred a species tree under the multispecies coalescent. Gene-tree conflict was localized to specific nodes, particularly involving the placement of L. otolepis and relationships among East Asian taxa, indicating heterogeneous plastome signal despite strong concatenated support. Selection analyses based on Ka/Ks ratios and complementary site-mode approaches identified six genes (ccsA, rpl22, rpoA, rps8, ycf1, ycf2) exhibiting signatures of positive selection. These results demonstrate dynamic plastome evolution and localized phylogenetic discordance within Limonium, refining the evolutionary framework of the genus and informing plastome-based inference in recent plant radiations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55600-y.

Introduction

The genus Limonium Mill. (Plumbaginaceae Juss.) has a cosmopolitan distribution, that includes an uninterrupted presence from the Mediterranean shorelines through the Irano-Turanian region to the Chinese Japanese region1,2. It has over 600 species listed in the Catalogue of Life3, with most species being endemic to a restricted geographical area. Seventy percent of the species are located in the Mediterranean area, contributing substantially to the ecosystems and biodiversity of this region4. The current view, supported by the work of Lledó et al.5, is that migration patterns in the Mediterranean have followed an east–west direction, with eastern groups generally being older than the western groups.

Limonium species are perennial herbs and shrubs that grow in coastal areas, from sandy beaches to maritime cliffs and salt marshes, as well as lagoons, meadows, steppes and deserts of the continental interior. Erben6 described the species as facultative halophytes, escaping interspecific competition by growing predominantly on saline and metal-rich soils. This genus includes sexual and asexual species and the ubiquity of hybridization, apomixis and polyploidization make its taxonomic identification notoriously difficult5,7–10. In such a genus, the clarification of species boundaries is often a very difficult task11–13 although necessary for the purpose of conservation. Commonly known as sea-lavender, statice or marsh rosemary, this genus includes medicinally important species such as L. sinense (Girard) Kuntze from which anti-viral and anti-cancer bioactive compounds have been isolated14. Extracts from L. spathulatum (Desf.) Kuntze, L. algarvense Erben and L. delicatulum (Girard) Kuntze have enzymatic inhibitory activity against acetylcholinesterase and butyrylcholinesterase making them possible remedies for neurodegenerative disease such as Alzheimer’s15–17. Also included are model halophytes for salt tolerance and multicellular salt gland studies such as L. bicolor Kuntze18–20, and decorative species such as L. sinuatum (L.) Mill.21.

Limonium melitense Brullo and L. zeraphae Brullo (Fig. 1) are endemic to the island of Malta (central Mediterranean)22. These perennial shrubs inhabit the rocky shoreline within a few metres of the sea, which is also the site of numerous, conflicting anthropogenic activities22,23. Due to the small size of the islands, high population density and extensive coastal activity, they are listed as ‘vulnerable’ VUA2 species according to IUCN protocol24. Both are diploid (2n = 2x = 18) and reproduce sexually22. These halophytes shed their leaves during the dry, arid Maltese summer to conserve water. They also possess multicellular salt glands on both sides of their leaves (personal observations DRA), making them plants of special interest for gene mining for these traits25,26.Figure 1Images of (a) Limonium melitense (b) L. zeraphae and (c) detail of L. zeraphae inflourescence.

Attempts have been made to resolve the complexity within the genus Limonium at the infrageneric level using various techniques including morphometry10,27–30, anatomy31, genetic studies utilizing chloroplast regions4,5,10,12,32–34 or only nuclear loci31. The only common chloroplast region for all these studies was the trnL—trnF region. Some of the studies included the rbcL4,5,33, matK4,10, and the petD intron with the petB—petD spacer10. The number of species of Limonium included is also limited to four32 to 2014. Selection of species included in these studies is often regional and therefore providing minimal resolution at the genus level12,32,33. The only study that included the Maltese endemic species L. melitense and L. zeraphae is the morphometric study of Domina and Mazzola29, who sampled species of Limonium from Sicily and neighbouring islands. However, genetic studies, covered only a very small proportion of the entire chloroplast genome, and the regions chosen for their study were not the optimal in discriminatory power for this genus, as will later be shown here. In each cases no preliminary, taxon-specific investigations were carried out to determine which region of the chloroplast genome has the greatest diversity index.

The chloroplast is associated with biosynthesis, gene expression and photosynthesis35. It is a semiautonomous eukaryotic organelle, with its own DNA and primarily matrilineal inheritance. The chloroplast genome organization is highly conserved within land plants, showing a characteristic quadripartite structure. However, recent studies have identified gene trees incongruences amongst themselves and when compared with the species tree in the plastomes of a wide range of angiosperms36–38. These conflicts were attributed to various factors, including varying evolutionary rates amongst chloroplast genes, horizontal gene transfer39, heteroplasmic recombination, both intra- and interspecific40 and incomplete lineage sorting37.

Prior to these findings, the plastome had been proposed as a ‘DNA super barcode’41. It has often been used in species identification, analysis of genetic diversity42,43 phylogenetic studies, taxonomic queries44,45 and to answer questions on evolution46,47.

Darshetkar et al.48 analysed the plastomes of four Limonium species namely L. bicolor, L. tetragonum (Thunb.) Bullock, L. aureum (L.) Chaz. and L. tenellum (Turcz.) Kuntze. They reported the expansion of the inverted repeat (IR) regions to include ycf1, the loss of the rpl16 intron and pseudogenization of rpl23. The present work analyses more than doubles the number of Limonium plastomes and includes two species from a different geographical area, namely the Mediterranean Sea, hence covering a wider geographical area (Table 1; Fig. 2). Recent advancement in next-generation sequencing technology, with the second and third generation being made available within the last 30 years, has made large sequencing projects feasible even to low budget projects. Plant genomic studies have increased exponentially within the past decades56. These include plastome sequencing for non-model species from throughout the plant kingdom35.

A number of plastomes from this genus were recently published, all of which from the Iran-Turanian and Chinese-Japanese region and none from the Mediterranean shoreline. Here we present the plastome sequences of two Limonium species, endemic to Malta, a Mediterranean island and compare them with seven other plastomes from this genus that are available in international databases.

Objectives of the study:To sequence, using PacBio HiFi reads, assemble, describe and compare the plastome of L. melitense and L. zerapahae;To carry out comparative analyses of the structure, characteristics, gene content, nucleotide (nt) differences, repetitive sequence content, codon usage bias and pairwise genetic distances of nine Limonium spieces plastomes;To conduct an in-depth phylogenetic analysis that integrates both gene trees and the species tree from whole chloroplast genome sequences and explore the phylogenetic position of these Maltese endemic Limonium species both at a local level and in the wider group of the family Caryophyllales.

Methods

Sample collection, DNA extraction and sequencing

The plant material was obtained from the Maltese shoreline according to environmental permit instructions. Inflorescence stems were collected in March, prior to flowering, after three days of dark treatment, from Ġnejna Bay for L. melitense (35° 55′ 14.7″ N 14° 20′ 24.3″ E) and Bengħisa for L. zeraphae (35° 48′ 34.3″ N 14° 32′ 15.7″ E). The samples were identified by DRA and Professor Salvatore Brullo (University of Catania). Voucher specimens were prepared from the same material and stored in the Argotti Botanical Gardens Herbarium (ARG) of the University of Malta (Voucher Code for L. melitense—ARG-DGA-0002; Voucher Code for L. zeraphae—ARG-DGA-0003). The plant samples were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and shipped on dry ice to Toulouse in France for high molecular weight (HMW) DNA extraction. HMW DNA extraction and SMRTbell (Pacific Biosciences (PacBio), San Francisco, CA, USA) library preparation were carried out at the Plant Genomic Resources Centre (CNRGV), Castaneta-Tolosan, France of the National Research Institute for Agriculture, Food and the Environment (INRAE), Toulouse, France. A Qiagen Genomic DNA extraction kit was utilized on 0.7 g of plant material according to manufacturer’s instructions. DNA quality and concentration were assessed using NanoDrop 2000C spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA); Qubit Fluorometer and double-stranded Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity reagent Assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Utilizing the HMW DNA sample, a HiFi SMRTbell library was constructed using SMRTbell Template Prep kit 2.0 (PacBio, CA, USA) according to PacBio recommendations (PN 101-853-100 v05). HMW DNA was sheared by using the Megaruptor 3 system (Diagenode, Liège Science Park, Liège, Belgium) to obtain 20 kbase average size fragments. Following the enzymatic treatment on 10 µg of sheared DNA sample for removing single-stranded overhangs and DNA damage repair, ligation with overhang adapters to both ends of the targeted dsDNA molecule was performed to create a closed, single-stranded circular DNA. A nuclease treatment was performed using SMRTbell Enzyme Cleanup kit 2.0 (PacBio, CA, USA). A size-selection with Blue-Pippin system (Sage Science, Beverly, MA, USA) was performed to remove fragments less than 10 kbases on purified samples with 1 × AMPure PB beads (PacBio, CA, USA). The size and concentration of the final library were assessed using the FemtoPulse system (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and Qubit Fluorometer and Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity Reagent Assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) respectively.

Sequencing primer v5 and Sequel II DNA Polymerase 2.2 were annealed and bound, respectively, to the SMRTbell library. The library was loaded on 2 SMRTbells 8 M at an on-plate concentration of 70 pM using adaptive loading. Sequencing was performed on the Sequel II system with Sequel II Sequencing kit 2.0, and run with a movie time of 30 h with 120 min pre-extension step and software v10.1 PacBio by Gentyane Genomic Platform (INRAE-Clermont-Ferrand France).

Chloroplast genome assembly and annotation

Chloroplast-specific sequences were selected from whole genome HiFi reads and de novo assembled using oatk (available at https://github.com/c-zhou/oatk; Refer to supplementary information for command line used)57. The chloroplast genome of L. melitense and L. zeraphae were annotated online in the GeSeq pipeline58 of CHLOROBOX using Chloe v0.1.0 annotation package and the plastome of L. aureum (NC_045399.1)59 as a reference genome. The two plastomes were manually curated by aligning to all seven available Limonium plastomes in NCBI Reference Sequence (RefSeq) database (Table 2), utilising Jalview v2.1160, and by Blastn searches61. We applied the rule as in Barrett et al.62 to annotate a gene as pseudogenized. Briefly, if more than 10% of the open reading frame is disrupted, relative to that of the reference, by an internal stop codon or non-triplet frameshift or large deletion it is marked as a pseudogene. Transfer RNAs (tRNAs) sequence and structure were confirmed by RNAscan-SE v2.063. Analysis of plastome structure

The circular maps of the plastomes were drawn, using OrganellarGenomeDRAW (ORDRAW v1.3.1)64 according to the manually curated genome annotation. The IRs and gene structure at the junction sites of all plastomes were visualized using IRplus65. The nine Limonium species genomes were aligned in progressiveMauve (version snapshot 2015/2/25)66,67 with default parameters. The plastome of L. aureum (NC_045399.1)59 was used as a reference.

Analysis of plastome content

To evaluate sequence informativeness and data quality across the alignment, a custom script (alignment_stats.py; available in supplementary information) was used (Poczai, this study). This script computed alignment length, the number of variable, constant, singleton, and parsimony-informative sites, GC content, gap proportion, Shannon entropy68, and the Townsend informativeness score69. These metrics offer insights into the evolutionary potential of the dataset and allow objective assessment of sequence conservation and diversity. Visualizations by means of violin plot which were generated using Seaborn70 and Matplotlib71.

The GC content and nt proportions were analysed in GFAstats v1.3.972. The nine available Limonium species plastomes were aligned by means of MAFFT v7.52673. The genic and intergenic regions were extracted in Jalview v2.1160. The nt diversity (π) and haplotype diversity (Hd) of the genic and intergenic regions were calculated using DnaSP v674.

The ratio of nonsynonymous substitution rate (Ka) to synonymous substitution rate (Ks), namely ω, was calculated by means of KaKs Calculator v2.0 with the genetic code set to table 11 (Bacterial and Plant Plastid code) and the model set to NG75,76 but previously the multiple sequence alignment fasta file was converted to a maf file utilising msaconverter (https://github.com/linzhi2013/msaconverter). The maf file was converted to an axt file in maftoaxt77. The substitutions were taken as differences from the plastomes sequence of L. aureum (NC_045399.1)59. This enables distinction between neutral mutations (ω ≈ 1), from negative (purifying) selection (ω < 1) and positive (adaptive) selection (ω > 1).

The relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) of the nine Limonium species chloroplast genomes was determined and analysed using MEGA v11.0.1378 for all aligned protein-encoding genes (PCGs) which include a total of > 15,000 codons. RSCU value of > 1 indicates bias towards that specific codon, a value equal to 1 indicates that the codon has no usage preference, and a value of < 1 indicates a less-than expected usage.

Analysis of repeat sequences and simple sequence repeats

The online tool MIcroSAtellite (MISA)79 was utilized to identify the microsatellites in the nine Limonium species chloroplast genomes with the simple sequence repeats (SSR) motif lengths set at mononucleotide, minimum number of repetitions of 10; dinucleotide, minimum number of repetitions of 6; trinucleotide to decanucleotide, minimum number of repetitions of 5. Direct, palindromic, tandem and supermaximal repeats were identified using VMATCH (http://www.vmatch.de/ accessed 13.05.2026)80,81 with a minimum length of 20 and a Hamming distance of 182. Briefly after constructing an index of repeated sequences using MKVTREE with the -dna -pl -allout and -v options. Direct repeats (-d and -l option), palindromic sequences (-p and -l options) and supermaximal repeats (-supermax and -l options) ≥ 20 were identified using VMATCH.

Pairwise genetic distances83 of the full chloroplast genomes were calculated in MEGA v11.0.1378.

Phylogenomic analysis

To understand the phylogenomic position of these nine Limonium species, a maximum likelihood (ML) phylogeny was constructed. Eighty two plastomes from the noncore Caryophyllales clade FTPP (including Plumbaginaceae, Polygonaceae Juss., nom. cons., Tamaricaceae Link and Frankeniaceae Desv.)84,85 were downloaded from NCBI Reference Sequence Database. Five plastomes from the Globular Inclusion Clade of Caryophyllales (namely from the families Aizoaceae Martinov, Barbeuiaceae Nakai, Talinaceae Doweld, Montiaceae Raf.)86 acted as the outgroup (Supplementary Table 2). The full sequences of these 96 plastomes were aligned in MAFFT v7.25673 removing one IR to avoid over representation of genes. The MAFFT alignment was trimmed using ClipKIT87 in smart-gap mode to remove poorly aligned or gap-rich sites. Poorly aligned sites were defined as alignment columns with excessive gaps, ambiguous homology, or high proportions of missing data that may introduce phylogenetic noise. ClipKIT employs a site-based trimming strategy that retains parsimony-informative and constant sites while removing columns dominated by gaps beyond a defined threshold (≥ 90% in smart-gap mode).

Phylogenetic inference was conducted using IQ-TREE v2.3.688. The best-fitting substitution model was selected using ModelFinder Plus with partition merging (MFP + MERGE)89, based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)90. Maximum-likelihood (ML) tree searches were performed in 10 independent runs, and the best-scoring tree was subsequently subjected to ultrafast bootstrap analysis with 10,000 replicates91 to assess branch support. The site concordance factor (sCF) was calculated according to Minh et al.92.

To detect signitures of positive selection, two complementary methods were used, both implemented in HyPhy v2.593,94, (i) A Mixed Effects Model of Evolution (MEME), which identifies episodic diversifying selection affecting a subset of branches95, and (ii) the Fast Unconstrained Bayesian AppRoximation method (FUBAR), which detects pervasive diversifying selection acting consistently across the phylogeny96. Sites were considered high-confidence only if identified by both methods (MEME p < 0.05 and FUBAR posterior probability ≥ 0.9).

Gene-tree conflict analysis

Recent plastome-wide phylogenetic studies have revealed frequent discordance between chloroplast gene trees and species trees inferred from the same data36–38. To assess phylogenetic conflict among plastid genes, protein-coding genes were extracted from plastid genomes and aligned using MAFFT73. Individual gene trees were inferred with IQ-TREE88 using 1,000 bootstrap replicates. A species tree was then estimated with ASTRAL-III v5.7.897 under the multispecies coalescent model. Branch support was evaluated using local posterior probabilities, and node support was further assessed using normalized quartet scores.

Gene-tree discordance for 79 genes across the plastome was quantified using PhyParts v0.0.194 which summarized concordant and conflicting signals relative to the ASTRAL species tree using a 50% support threshold (− s 0.5). The PhyParts results were visualized with pie charts indicating the proportions of concordant and conflicting gene trees at each node, generated with the phypartspiecharts.py script. (https://github.com/mossmatters/phyloscripts/tree/master/phypartspiecharts.py; accessed 7 February, 2026). Gene trees pruned to this threshold were subsequently inspected and grouped according to shared topological patterns.

Plastid marker phylogenetic analysis

To assess the phylogenetic placement of the Maltese Limonium taxa within the Mediterranean lineage of the genus, we integrated plastome-derived sequences with the plastid marker dataset of Koutroumpa et al.4, which is based on the barcode regions matK, rbcL, and trnL-F (Supplementary Table 3). These three plastid markers were excised from the complete plastome assemblies of the Maltese taxa and combined with sequences from vouchered Mediterranean specimens included in the original dataset, focusing on taxa assigned to the Mediterranean lineage.

Sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7.25673 with default settings and concatenated into a combined plastid marker dataset. Phylogenetic inference was conducted in IQ-TREE v2.3.688, following the same analytical framework used for the plastome phylogeny. Model selection and partition merging were performed using ModelFinder89 with the MFP + MERGE option, and nodal support was assessed with 10,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates. The tree was rooted using L. otolepis (NC_065861.1) sequences as the outgroup.

Comparison of the nine Limonium plastomes

The plastomes of the endemic Maltese species were compared to seven other Limonium species’ plastomes present in public databases (Table 2). None of these seven plastomes were for species from the Mediterranean basin, which is the centre of diversity of this genus, but rather from the Irano-Turanian and Chinese-Japanese regions (Fig. 2, Table 1). The lengths range from 150,515 bp for L. tenellum to 170,033 bp for L. sinense (mean = 155,777 bp; SD = 7,062 bp). The length of the LSC region has a range of 96,128 bp in L. sinense to 84,194 bp for L. zeraphae. The mean length of the LSC is 85,749 bp (SD = 3,895 bp). The longest SSC region is that of L. tenellum at 23,753 bp while the shortest is that of L. bicolor at 12,965 bp. The average length of the SSC region, for all nine Limonium species plastomes is 14,250 bp (SD = 3,568 bp). The IRs also ranged in size from 21,064 bp for L. tenellum to 32,194 bp in L. sinense. The mean length of the IRs is 25,111 bp (SD = 2,907 bp).

Of the nine Limonium plastomes in this study, the lowest percent GC content is that of L. sinense at 36.7%, while the highest is that of L. tenellum at 37.1%. The mean GC content of all 9 plastomes is 37.0% (SD = 0.11%). A similar distribution of percent GC content per region is observed in all nine Limonium species plastomes. The highest percent GC content per region is in the IRs (mean = 41.1%; SD = 0.67%). The LSC and SSC have an average GC content of 35.3% (SD = 0.14%) and 31.6% (SD = 0.62%) respectively.

All Limonium species in this study have 37 copies of tRNA genes except L. tenellum which has 36 tRNA genes. In this species trnN is present as a single copy while in the other species it is duplicated in the IR region. The four rRNA genes are duplicated in all the Limonium species except L. tenellum. In the latter case the plastome has only one copy of rrn4.5 and rrn5 but two copies of rrn16 and rrn23.

The annotated NCBI files of L. franchetii, L. tenellum and L. tetragonum do not include any pseudogenes while the remaining Limonium species in this study have two copies of rpl32 pseudogenized.

Phylogenomic analysis

Maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree generated by IQ-TREE utilising the concatenated gene set (Supplemantary Fig. 4) and the ASTRAL species tree (Fig. 10) were performed on 96 plastome sequences comprised of 97 genes and of 222,253 nucleotide positions of which 128,852 were constant and 93,401 were variable among taxa. Of these, 36,661 were parsimony-informative while 56,740 were singleton sites (mutations found in only one taxon). The alignment had a balanced GC content (36.4%) and moderate entropy (0.268) with good informativeness score (0.417). Support values across the nodes of the inferred phylogenetic tree were evaluated using both UFBoot and Shimodaira-Hasegawa approximate likelihood ratio test (SH-aLRT) metrics. The dataset demonstrated high average support, with mean bootstrap (BS) and SH-aLRT values of approximately 75.5% and 79.2%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 5). Median values for both metrics were similarly high, suggesting a consistent signal across most internal branches. The standard deviation in BS (22.1) and SH-aLRT (24.6) reflected moderate heterogeneity in node confidence, typical of plastome-scale datasets. The quartile distribution suggests that at least 75% of the nodes have SH-aLRT values exceeding 67%, the common threshold for moderate support. The combined support metrics indicate that while the majority of nodes in the tree are well supported, a subset of branches may require additional data or alternative methods to improve resolution.

The average sCF value was 57.6%, indicating a relatively strong and consistent site-level support for the branches of the plastome-inferred phylogeny. The majority of nodes exhibited sCF values above 50%, suggesting that most plastid sites support the resolved topology. Nodes with lower sCF values were generally associated with short internodes or recent divergences, where insufficient phylogenetic signal or alignment noise may have contributed to uncertainty. The absence of extremely low sCF values (e.g. < 20%) suggests a lack of significant topological conflict across the plastome data, reinforcing the overall reliability of the inferred tree.

Phylogenetic placement of Maltese taxa

In the best ML tree inferred from the concatenated plastid marker dataset (Fig. 12), both Maltese endemic species were unequivocally nested within the Mediterranean lineage, supporting a clear Mediterranean biogeographic origin. The analysis included 87 taxa (Supplementary Table 3) and three plastid partitions (matK, rbcL, and trnL-F), comprising a total of 3,018 aligned nucleotide sites with low levels of missing data (6.45%). ModelFinder selected separate best-fit substitution models for each partition under an edge-linked proportional partition scheme (TPM2u + F + G4 for matK, HKY + F + I for rbcL, and K3Pu + F + G4 for trnL-F)89. The best-scoring ML tree had a log-likelihood of -5,926.418. Both L. melitense and L. zeraphae were characterized by relatively short branch lengths and plastid haplotypes that were not strongly differentiated from those of geographically proximate taxa. Overall resolution within the Mediterranean lineage was moderate, with deeper relationships generally supported, whereas several terminal and subclade-level relationships remained weakly resolved, reflecting the limited phylogenetic signal of standard plastid markers in recently diverged lineages. Discussion

Limonium chloroplast genome structure and comparative analysis

The classification and identification of plants in the genus Limonium is traditionally based on their morphological characteristics29. However, their varied modes of reproduction systems, including both sexual and asexual (apomixis), the frequent occurrence of hybridisation and polyploidisation34, and geographical and ecological factors that may continuously change their characteristics12, result in considerable taxonomic complexity. Recent taxonomic studies have utilized the plastome to assess the genetic relationship between related species of Limonium from the Irano-Turanian and the Chinese-Japanese regions48. However, the centre of diversity of this genus is the Mediterranean sea4 and yet no Limonium species’ plastome from this region has as yet been sequenced and published. Here we present the first two plastomes from this region, namely those of L. melitense and L. zeraphae from the island of Malta (central Mediterranean). We compared them to each other and to seven other published ones from the Far East.

The chloroplast genome of nine species in the genus Limonium range in size from 150,515 to 174,033 bp, indicating variation in size as a primary difference48,59,84,98. Of note is the fact that our analysis of the L. sinense plastome sequence did not identify any misassemblies as indicated by Darshetkar et al.48. However, this plastid genome is 20 kbp longer than most of the other plastomes included in this study. We attribute this difference in length to the large number of repeats. Moreover, according to Darshetkar et al.48 petB and petD are not intron containing genes in L. tetragonum and L. bicolor, however in our review of these genomes we found evidence for these introns (Supplementary Fig. 6).

PacBio HiFi reads were used to sequence the chloroplast genome of the two endangered Limonium species from Malta. These were de novo-assembled and a number of differences were identified including difference in gene content, difference in the boundaries of the quadripartite plastome, in repeat sequences, and π in specific genes and intergenic regions. These results demonstrate that some highly variable regions of these genomes can be correlational study-specific DNA barcodes for identifying these two species locally and analysing phylogenetic relationships.

Inverted repeat regions are considered the most conserved regions in the chloroplast genome and their contraction or expansion is the main cause of chloroplast genome size variation101. In genome evolution, the difference between species of the same genus is often associated with the expansion and contraction of the IR regions102. IRplus detects the 4 junctions in each plastome included in the search and an optimum consensus radius to search and plot the genes is established. With these given coordinates for each junction, the program plots the calculated relative positions of the genes within the corresponding radius of each track65. Limonium melitense and L. zeraphae have a distinct distribution of genes at the IR boundaries when compared to the other seven Limonium species from the Far East. There are also differences in these boundaries between these two species. Beside differences in lengths of the IR and LSC regions, the JLA and JLB junctions show gene arrangement differences.

The boundaries of the plastome of L. tenellum show major differences from the rest of the species in this study. Darshetkar et al.48 attributed this difference to the wrong assembly or annotation. However, if one analysis the full length of the SSC region of this species (Supplementary Fig. 1) the boundary genes are present further towards the centre of this region and IRplus65, which only shows a length from the boundary up to the first gene of the combination of plastomes, does not show these genes. A further corroborating fact to this is that the SSC region in L. tenellum is 23,753 bp long while the SSC region of the other 8 Limonium species has a range of 12,965–13,517 bp.

The Limonium species plastomes vary substantially in length (150,515–174,033 bp). This is unexpected, as nearly all of the main lineages of plants show a limited size range in chloroplast genome size103. These plastomes have similar gene content48,84,98 but have intergenic regions of variable lengths, which may be the main reason for the size variation. Variable intergenic region lengths have been previously observed and is documented to be one of the main reason for genome size variation in chloroplasts104–106. The outliers of genome size are in fact L. sinense with a plastome size of 174,033 bp98 and L. tenellum with a genome size of 150,515 bp84. L. sinense has been shown in the study presented here to have a plastome with the highest proportion of SSRs, direct repeats, palindromic repeats and supermaximal repeats which accounted for the much larger plastome size. However, this plastome, which was assembled using the plastome of Plumbago auriculata as a reference, might include some reference bias. The plastome of P. auriculata is also exceptionally long at 168,765 bp48 so reference bias in the assemble of L. sinense plastome cannot be excluded by the present study. The range in size for the remaining seven plastomes is 154,139–154,673 bp. The plastome of L. tenellum is 3,624 bp shorter that the lower value of this range. Of note is the fact that this plastome has the longest SSC region at 23,753 bp (range of other eight SSC regions is 12,965–13,517 bp) but the shortest IRs at 21,064 bp (range of the other 8 IRs is 28,481–32,194 bp). The expanded SSC region is due to the incorporation of a number of genes including ycf1, trnN, trnR, rrn5 and partial rrn4 (11 bp) which in other Limonium plastomes are duplicated in the IRs. This implies that while L. tenellum has one copy of these genes, the other Limonium species’ plastomes have two copies, which accounts for the difference in size of these plastomes.

Differences in chloroplast genome sequence can be used to develop barcodes for the identification of species10. A Hd of 1 in a gene implies that there are nine alleles for that gene in the nine Limonium species plastomes under study while a Hd of zero implies that there is only one allele in the nine species under study for this specific region. Sites with high Hd could also serve as molecular markers to identify one species from another. From π and Hd of the nine Limonium species included in this study three genes were identified, namely ndhF, ycf1 and rpl32 and five intergenic regions (rpl32—trnL-TAG, ndhF—rpl32, trnM-CAT—rps14, ndhH—rps15 and psbI—trnS-GCT) with a π > 0.05. All these sites are potentially useful for identification of the various Limonium species. The coding regions, intergenic regions and introns tend to have an Hd > 0.8 and a relatively low π (< 0.01) which implies many haplotypes with little variation between them reflecting a relatively recent expansion107.

To date different barcodes have been used in order to identify and build phylogenetic relationships within the genus Limonium. These include trnL—trnF region, which was found in this study to have a π of 0.021 and Hd of 0.806, the rbcL (π = 0.008; Hd = 0.778), matK (π = 0.016; Hd = 0.972), petD intron (π = 0.011; Hd = 0.778) and the petB—petD intergenic region (π = 0.013; Hd = 0.778). None of these loci will satisfactorily resolve the nine Limonium species included in this study. Plastome loci, which would enable the molecular identification of the sympatric L. melitense and L. zeraphae have also been identified. The authors are also aware that only a very small proportion (1.5%) of Limonium species have been included in this study. The genus Limonium includes approximately 600 species with a cosmopolitan distribution that includes the Northern and to a lesser extent the Southern Hemisphere4. A much larger sample of intra- and interspecific plastomes has to be sampled to estimate the true diversity within this genus.

The GC content is an important indicator for determining phylogenetic interspecific relationship108. The total GC content and the GC content of the IRs were identical for the two Maltese endemics included in this study. The biggest difference in GC content was found between the SSC regions (31.53–31.58%). This region is the major site of difference between these two plastomes from other aspects as well, as will be indicated below. The other seven plastomes have the same pattern of variation in GC content between regions as the Maltese endemics. The IRs have the highest percent content of G and C (40.64–42.84%). This was attributed to the abundance of tRNA and rRNA genes, which are predominantly composed of GC bp108. The high GC content of genes within the IRs has also been reported in bryophytes110, ferns109,110, cycads111 and other angiosperms109.

Repeated sequences play a role in various processes, such as gene activity, genome organization, DNA replication, recombination and repair, difference in mutation frequency and evolutionary rate of species112. Long repeats are here defined as repeated sequences with length ≥ 20 bp. Direct repeats occur in consecutive sequences on the same strand of DNA, while palindromic repeats occur on the complementary strand in reverse direction. A repeat is supermaximal if it never occurs as a substring of any other maximal repeat, where a maximal repeat is a substring occurring more than once in the sequence. Simple sequence repeats are the most commonly used and effective molecular markers in evolutionary studies113. They are stable and show substantial polymorphism. They are therefore ideal molecular markers that are widely used in the identification of closely related species, genetic diversity research, and molecular breeding113,114. The nine Limonium plastid genomes showed an unequal distribution of polymorphic SSRs, with differences in the distribution, quantity, size and kind of SSR motif. The SSRs identified in the nine Limonium chloroplasts in this study were mostly mononucleotide and dinucleotide repeats. The repetitive units were mainly composed of either A or T, with A/T repeats and AT/AT repeats predominating as in the case of the genus Ceratostigma. We also confirm the findings of Darshetkar et al.48, that the mononucleotide repeats are the most common and that the highest number of SSRs in the genus Limonium are found in the LSC region. However, due to more stringent criteria for the identification of the SSRs in this study, the IRs did not consistenly have a higher number of SSRs than the SSC regions as in the case of L. bicolor and L. otolepis (3:4), L. sinense (4:6) and L. tenellum (1:5). L. melitense and L. zeraphae are unique among the Limonium species included in this study in have a single trinucleotide repeat each, but with different numbers of mononucleotide repeats (34 and 33 respectively) from each other. Consistently the highest number of all types of repeats were identified in L. sinense’ plastome, which also happens to be the longest one included in this study and therefore we conclude that the extra length is not due to the gene content but due to repeated sequences. As previously observed115, repetitive sequences rarely contained G and C bases, possibly due to the three hydrogen bonds content of this base pair. This makes them more resistant to disruption and less conducive to genetic recombination.

The RSCU value is the ratio of the observed codon usage frequency to the expected usage frequency when all synonymous codons for a particular amino acids are used with equal frequency. An RSCU > 1 implies codon preference. Codon usage bias can affect the efficiency of gene transcription and translation. A comprehensive understanding of codon preference is beneficial for exploring evolutionary relationships among species116–118. Codon usage bias analysis revealed that the frequencies of the 61 codons in the Limonium species are biased towards codons ending in A or T(U) while less preferred codons ended in G or C. This corroborates the lower GC% content (Table 2) and is in support of the hypothesis that codons in higher plants tend to have A/T(U) endings117.

Whole chloroplast pairwise genetic distances between species could be low as in the case of Neltuma chilensis and N. glandulosa (0.000295)119, Primulina vestita and P. secundiflora (0.000418)120 and Styrax macrocarpus and S. zhejiangensis (0.000300)121. In this study, the lowest genetic distance was found to be 0.000375 between L. tetragonum and L. franchetii, which compares well with documented values for other pairs of species, and therefore pairwise genetic diversity distinguishes all 9 species included in this study, including the Maltese endemics.

The ratio of Ka to Ks substitutions has been widely used to assess genomic evolution and the impact of selective pressures on genes47,104,105. A ratio greater than 1 indicates that a gene is undergoing positive selection, a ratio equal to 1 indicates neutral selection and a ratio smaller than 1 reflects purifying selection acting on the gene122,123. This ratio is important for inferring evolutionary rates and for understanding adaptive development among species124.

In chloroplast PCGs, synonymous substitutions are usually more frequent than non-synonymous substitutions, with ratios of Ka:Ks < 147. In this study out of 75 PCGs, only four are under strong positive selection and therefore undergoing rapid evolution by the environment in some of the Limonium species under study. These being the genes for Cytochrome c biogenesis protein, NADH dehydrogenase subunit 5, large ribosomal subunit protein and protein TIC 214. The remaining (71 PCGs) are in purifying selection. In this study, most Ks values are higher than Ka values, implying a relatively slow evolution of Limonium species. Neutral theory states that most of the point mutations, such as nucleotide substitutions in the intergenic regions, introns and the pseudogenes that are not translated into proteins, are neutral or nearly neutral meaning that they are not under natural selection124. Of note is the observation that Maltese endemics have a Ka:Ks > 1 in most of these genes, implying divergence from the reference which is from the Far East. These analyses will eventually be revisited with reference to other Mediterranean species of Limonium.

The RSCU is one of the commonly used parameters to measure codon usage bias. Codon usage bias is a critical factor determining gene expression and cellular functions by influencing diverse processes such as RNA processing, protein translation and protein folding125. It also reflects the origin, mutation patterns, genetic drift and evolution of the species or genes. The RSCU values of 30 codons were all > 1 with the majority (29:1) ending in either A/T(U). This has frequently been observed in angiosperms126,127. Known codon usage patterns can be used as predictors of expression levels and functions of genes and open reading frames and can be used in designing primers for polymerase chain reaction amplification of genes, introducing point mutations and other breeding research125.

Sites detected by both MEME95 and FUBAR96 are the strongest evidence for adaptive substitution because they show both episodic and pervasive signatures of dN > dS. These positions also correspond to amino-acid shifts between clade I and clade II, supporting their potential functional relevance. rpoA encodes the α-subunit of the chloroplast RNA polymerase128. An arginine to serine substitution at position 340 replaces a positively charged, large, strongly hydrophilic amino acid with a polar, uncharged, smaller amino acid with a hydroxyl group near the C-terminus of the polypeptide chain. This might be disrupting an ionic interaction or a binding site to a negatively charged molecule such as a nucleic acid. The Tic214 protein, encoded by the ycf1 gene, is a component of the translocon at the inner chloroplast membrane that mediates nuclear-encoded proteins into the chloroplast stroma. It is the largest subunit, forming a structural bridge and contributes to the protein translocation pathway129. The lysine to methionine substitution, at position 878, replaces a positively charged amino acid with a neutral one containing a sulphur atom. This might disrupt an ionic bond. Another change in the same gene results in substituting amino acid 1544, which is a lysine, with asparagine, a charged amino acid for a polar one. This might also disrupt a salt bridge or weaken an interaction with a negatively charged molecule. The third identified substitution within this protein is at amino acid position 1659. This replaces histidine with asparagine. The former amino acid is a proton donor and can possibly carry a positive charge while the later is polar and cannot ionise.

Plastid gene discordance and the phylogenetic position of Limonium

The broad sampling of 96 plastomes from four families had the aim not merely to show that the Maltese taxa belong to Limonium, but to assess plastome evolutionary dynamics and gene-tree conflict within a stable, well-supported backbone framework. Ninty six plastomes were also included to ensure robust phylogenetic placement of the Maltese taxa within the broader Caryophyllales framework. Phylogenetic position is inherently hierarchical, and accurate placement of two newly sequenced species requires adaquate sampling across related genera and families to stabilize the backbone relationships, reduce long-branch attraction, and ensure correct rooting.

Broader sampling also improves model performance in maximum likelihood analyses and is essential for meaningful evaluation of plastid gene-tree discordance under the multispecies coalescent framework. Sparse sampling can inflate support values and obscure topological instability. The approach presented here follows current standards in plastome-scale phylogenomics and ensures that the inferred placement of the Maltese taxa is robust within both genus- and family-level contexts.

The results presented here reveal a significant discordance between gene tree and species tree within the chloroplast genome data of Limonium species. This is evidence for the complexity of their evolutionary relationships. Typically chloroplast genomes are treated as a single unit because of their relatively stable inheritance patterns, however our results show that this assumption does not always hold true. This type of discordance has also been documented in other terrestrial plants36–38.

The genus Limonium is distinctly monophyetic with the Maltese endemic species, L. melitense and L. zeraphae forming a sister group to the eastern species. The resulting phylogenomic tree splits the nine Limonium species, included in this study, into three sister groups while L. otolepis stands on its own as a branch between the Mediterranean endemics and the eastern species. This observation in fact coincides with its geographical distribution where it occupies the Middle East (Fig. 2; Table 1) and is in support of its infrageneric classification where it is the only one in section Limonium and subsection Hyalolepideae. The other six Limonium species form a monophyletic clade in the phylogenomic tree and, in fact, they were all classified infragenerically into section Plathymenium. Beyond the section level of classification, the phylogenomic tree does not support the assigned taxa. The ML tree here presented places L. tenellum and L. sinense in one sister group while infragenic classification places them in different subsections namely Rhodanthae and Chrysanthae respectively. Moreover, the current classification places L. sinense and L. tetragonum in the same subsection, namely Chrysanthae, however, the plastome phylogenomic tree puts them in different sister groups.

Consistent with the findings of San et al.38, in the genus Limonium it is observed that functionally related plastid genes may exhibit distinct and sometimes conflicting phylogenetic histories. Core-supporting genes, including several involved in photosynthesis, transcription, and plastid maintenance (e.g. atpB, psaA, rpoB, ycf2), produced highly congruent topologies and branch-length patterns, collectively defining the dominant plastid phylogenetic signal within Limonium. Near-core genes, many of which encode components of the ATP synthase, ribosomal proteins, or photosystem function (e.g. atpA, rbcL, rpl2), largely retained this signal but showed increased topological instability at deeper nodes, particularly affecting the placement of the Maltese taxa and L. otolepis. In contrast, outlier genes—disproportionately enriched for ndh genes, photosystem subunits, ribosomal RNAs, and rapidly evolving open reading frames (e.g. matK, ycf1)—exhibited substantial topological discordance and heterogeneous branch lengths. This pattern suggests that lineage-specific rate variation, functional constraints, or differential selective regimes across plastid gene classes could contribute to conflicting phylogenetic signals within the chloroplast genome.

Both the ML and species tree analysis recovered a topology in which Frankeniaceae is sister to Tamaricaceae, while Polygonaceae is sister to Plumbaginaceae. Each of these families form a well-supported monophyletic group. This result is congruent with the findings of Walker et al.130, who used transcriptome data to resolve relationships across Caryophyllales. Whereas their analysis showed moderate-to-high local posterior probabilities (0.80–0.95) and gene-tree conflict, ML plastid genome-based analysis recovered these relationships with maximal support (BS = 100) and moderate concordance (> 65% of gene trees).

Previous work on Frankeniaceae and Tamaricaceae using two chloroplast loci (rbcL and trn-S—trn-G intergenic region) together with the nuclear ribosomal region 18S, supported a monophyletic clade comprising of Reaumuria and Hololachna131, a pattern also recovered in our plastome phylogeny. In the ML tree presented in the present study, Myricaria was not monophyletic; three species (M. bracteata, M. paniculata and M. laxiflora) formed a well supported clade, whereas M. prostrata and M. elegans appeared within the monophyletic clade that include all the five sampled Tamarix species. This is in contrast with the plastid and nuclear barcode phylogeny of Gaskin et al.131, who recovered Tamarix as a strongly supported monophyletic group. In contrast our ML results align with those of Wang et al.132, who found Tamarix and Myricaria to form a strongly supported monophyletic group that excludes Reaumuria and Hololachna. On the other hand 49 out of 79 gene trees support the separation between Tamarix and Myricaria as monophylatic genera.

Within Polygonaceae, the monophyly of Bistorta, Persicaria and Rumex is well established133 and our results are consistent with this. Relationship among Muehlenbeckia, Fallopia and Reynoutria have previously been studied, using five chloroplast and two nuclear loci, revealing a monophyletic clade in which each genus was also monophyletic134. In our plastome phylogeny four species of Fallopia form a group (BS = 100; 42% of gene tree support) together with Muehlenbeckia. However, F. multiflora, Reynoutria japonica, and Polygonum cuspidatum formed a distinct, well-supported monophyletic clade. Fallopia multiflora (Thunb.) Haraldson has previously been placed in Reynoutia as Reynoutria multiflora (Thunb.) Moldenke; however, under the currently accepted taxonomy it is treated as Pleuropterus multiflorus (Thrnb.) Turcz. Ex Nakai135. Likewise, Polygonum cuspidatum Siebold & Zucc. is currently regarded as a synonym of R. japonica Houtt.135, which likely explains this pattern. Only 21 out of 79 gene trees support this grouping. Our plastome phylogeny revealed a deep divergence between F. multiflora and the two R. japonica samples, supporting the recognition of Pleuropterus as a separate genus. Although no vouchered specimens were available for either the R. japonica accession or the sequence submitted under Polygonum cuspidatum, the close similarity among these sequences suggests that they represent a single taxon. Polygonum argyrocoleon, P. aviculare and P. cognatum form a monophyletic clade which confirms the phylogenomic relationship presented in Cao et al.133, however in this case P. ajanense and P. cuspidatum are not included in their study and nor is Reynoutria japonica. Knorringia sibirica (Laxm.) Tzvelev forms a monophyletic group with members of Fallopia, Muehlenbeckia, Atrophaxis and Polygonum which confirms the work of Schuster et al.136. In our analyses, Polygonum ajanense formed a clade with Koenigia alpina and K. divaricata. This is also strongly supported by 64 out of 79 gene trees. Koenigia cyanandra was recovered as sister to K. forrestii, with both together forming a clade sister to K. lichiangensis. These results are consistent with the findings of137 and support the taxonomic revision proposed by Xue et al. 138, who transferred Polygonum ajanense to the genus Koenigia. Calligonum, Pteroxygonum, and Fagopyrum were each recovered as well-supported monophyletic genera (BS = 100; 65, 61 and 60 out of 79 gene trees in concordance respectively) and together formed a closely related lineage. This relationship is consistent with the results of Schuster et al.136, who inferred comparable phylogenetic affinities using two chloroplast loci and one nuclear marker.

Chloroplast phylogeny of Maltese Limonium species

Koutroumpa et al.4 assembled the most comprehensive taxonomic sampling of the genus Limonium available to date and provided a biogeographic framework for Mediterranean diversity within the group. A particularly notable aspect of their study was the extensive synthesis of taxonomic and phylogenetic information, achieved through a critical review of online databases, regional floras, and published studies. Based on this synthesis, the authors assigned species to clades within their molecular phylogeny and provided explicit justification for the phylogenetic placement of taxa that were not directly included in the molecular analyses. While these inferred placements were not directly tested using molecular data, they constitute well-founded working hypotheses that can be evaluated through expanded taxon sampling, an approach taken in the present study.

Within this framework, Mediterranean Limonium was interpreted as originating from a single Mediterranean radiation (clade B1) derived from an ancestral Eurasian lineage. This radiation was characterized by pronounced geographic structuring across the Mediterranean Basin, with frequent recent speciation and largely regionally shared chloroplast haplotypes, consistent with recurrent local diversification and limited long-distance dispersal. Maltese endemic species were not included in the sampling of Koutroumpa et al.4; however, based on their geographic position, they were implicitly assumed to belong to the central-eastern Mediterranean subclade.

Incorporating the Maltese taxa into the plastid phylogenetic framework confirmed these biogeographic expectations at a broad scale. Our analyses resolved L. melitense as closely related to L. bocconei (Lojac.) Litard., in agreement with the hypothesis proposed by Koutroumpa et al.4. This relationship is consistent with previously noted morphological affinities, as L. melitense belongs to the L. bocconei aggregate within Limonium subgen. Limonium22, which forms part of the Mediterranean lineage. Limonium bocconei is native to Sicily and the Isole Egadi, and the close phylogenetic placement of L. melitense therefore strongly suggests a Sicilian or southern Italian origin for the Maltese taxon; a biogeographic hypothesis previously proposed and here corroborated by plastid phylogenetic evidence.

Compared to the relatively clear phylogenetic affinities observed for L. melitense, the second Maltese taxon, L. zeraphae exhibited a more ambiguous phylogenetic signal. Although L. zeraphae was unequivocally resolved within the Mediterranean clade its precise phylogenetic relationship within this lineage remained unresolved. The species has traditionally been regarded as morphologically allied to L. graecum Brullo139; however, while L. graecum was firmly placed within the Mediterranean lineage, it did not belong exclusively to the central–eastern Mediterranean subclade. In the plastid phylogeny, L. zeraphae did not position closely with L. graecum or with L. melitense and lacked a strongly supported association with any single regional lineage. This pattern likely reflects either an earlier divergence within the Mediterranean radiation or a more complex evolutionary history involving introgression or chloroplast capture, processes that are well documented in Limonium and consistent with the reticulate evolutionary dynamics of Mediterranean taxa34,140.

Results from the full plastid genome phylogeny provided complementary evidence for the relationship between the two Maltese endemics. In the plastome-based analysis, L. melitense and L. zeraphae formed a well-supported sister pair, albeit within a much more limited taxon sampling. This congruence at the plastome level suggests a shared evolutionary history of the two Maltese taxa and indicates that the lack of resolution observed for L. zeraphae in the plastid marker tree is more likely attributable to marker choice. These results indicate that the Maltese Limonium flora comprises closely related Mediterranean lineages with partially overlapping but possibly not identical phylogeographic histories shaped by regional dispersal and island colonization.

Plastid phylogenetic discordance in Limonium

Plastid genomes have traditionally been regarded as structurally conserved, uniparentally inherited, and evolving as a single, non-recombining locus, assumptions that have underpinned their widespread use in plant phylogenetics and DNA barcoding141. This perceived genomic coherence has justified concatenation-based approaches to plastid data across broad taxonomic scales142. However, growing empirical evidence shows that plastid genomes do not always conform to this simplified model and that substantial gene tree discordance may occur even within a single plastome143. Treating the chloroplast genome as a single evolutionary unit may not always provide an accurate phylogenetic signal144. In such cases, well-supported gene trees can nonetheless differ from the underlying evolutionary history of species, thereby reducing the reliability of phylogenetic inferences based exclusively on chloroplast data.

Our analyses of Limonium plastid genomes reflect this emerging view, revealing a strongly supported backbone topology alongside localized conflict among individual plastid genes. Such discordance is increasingly recognized in plastid phylogenomic datasets145 and is commonly attributed to heterogeneity in evolutionary rates146, systematic error36, functional constraints147, and information content among plastid loci37. Genes involved in core photosynthetic and transcriptional processes tend to be more conserved and phylogenetically concordant146, whereas genes with relaxed selective constraints or accelerated evolution, such as several ndh genes, matK, and large open reading frames, frequently contribute to topological instability38. Biological processes intrinsic to plastid genome evolution may further contribute to gene tree conflict. Plastid genomes exhibit structural organization driven by recombination-dependent replication148, gene conversion149, and repair mechanisms that can generate alternative genomic arrangements and sequence variants150. These processes can produce heteroplasmy151, which may lead to stochastic sampling of plastid haplotypes and influence inferred gene trees36. In addition, plastid inheritance is not always strictly maternal152; biparental inheritance153 and paternal leakage154 have been documented across angiosperms, creating opportunities for plastid introgression155 and chloroplast capture or repeated backcross following hybridization156. Such processes are particularly relevant in Limonium, a genus characterized by frequent hybridization, apomixis, and reticulate evolution34,140. The discordance among plastid genes in Limonium likely reflect a combination of rapid diversification, heterogeneous plastid gene evolution, and biologically meaningful processes such as inter-plastome recombination157 and heteroplasmy158.

It might be important to highlight that our analyses have direct implications for plastid marker–based phylogenetic inference in Limonium. The commonly used barcode loci matK and rbcL do not contribute to the core plastid phylogenetic signal in this genus but instead behave as outlier genes exhibiting elevated topological discordance. As a result, phylogenetic reconstructions based solely on these markers may be sensitive to locus-specific evolutionary dynamics and may not reliably resolve relationships within the Mediterranean radiation. The use of alternative plastid markers with higher concordance, e.g., atpB, clpP, ndhD or multi-locus plastid datasets from plastome-scale analyses based on species tree inferences are likely to provide more robust phylogenetic resolution in Limonium. These results highlight the importance of evaluating plastid marker performance in a lineage-specific context and caution against treating plastid genomes or individual barcode loci as strictly single-locus datasets in groups with complex evolutionary histories.

Conclusion

In our study, the first plastomes from Mediterranean Limonium were generated, providing genomic resources from the centre of diversity of the genus. Comparative analyses revealed largely conserved plastome structure across nine species, with genome size variation primarily associated with inverted repeat dynamics and repeat content, while gene order remained stable. Despite this overall structural conservation, localized differences in gene content, IR boundaries, and sequence polymorphism distinguished the Maltese endemics and supported their recognition as distinct species. Plastome-wide phylogenomic analyses confirmed the monophyly of Limonium and placed the Maltese taxa as a well-supported sister lineage to East. Asian species. However, gene-tree analyses revealed concentrated conflict at specific nodes, indicating that plastid genomes in Limonium did not behave as a strictly uniform evolutionary unit. This heterogeneity, together with signatures of positive selection in several genes, supported a model of dynamic plastome evolution within the genus. Commonly used plastid barcode loci did not represent the dominant phylogenetic signal in Limonium, emphasizing the importance of lineage-specific evaluation of plastid markers and plastome-scale species-tree approaches in complex radiations. These results refined the evolutionary framework of Limonium and demonstrated the need to account for plastid gene-level conflict in phylogenomic inference. Given that the present study includes approximately 1.5% of described Limonium species, expanded and geographically balanced plastome sampling, encompassing both interspecific and intraspecific diversity, will be essential to capture the full extent of genomic variation and to refine phylogenetic inference within this taxonomically complex genus.
