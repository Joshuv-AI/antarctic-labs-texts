The association between physical activity and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease: Evidence from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey and UK Biobank
Authors: Yiping Shen, Meiyu Chen, Shiqi Zhu, Jiaxi Lin, Lihe Liu, Chengyi Zhu, Jinzhou Zhu, Chunfang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThere is limited prospective evidence on the association of physical activity with metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease. We aimed to use data from two major databases to systematically quantify the correlation of physical activity with the risk of metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease.MethodsThis study used data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2007–2020) and UK Biobank. Initially, a cross-sectional study was conducted using the data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data to assess the links between physical activity and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease using weighted logistic regression analysis with appropriate sample weights to ensure national representativeness. Subsequently, Cox proportional hazards regression and restricted cubic splines models were applied to infer causality and evaluate the potential dose–response relationships in the UK Biobank cohort over a median follow-up period of 14.18 years. Additionally, subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of the results.ResultsIn the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, higher physical activity levels were inversely associated with metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease risk (odds ratio: 0.78, 95% confidence interval: 0.63–0.97). This finding was supported in the UK Biobank cohort (hazard ratio: 0.57, 95% confidence interval: 0.34–0.76). A nonlinear dose–response relationship existed between physical activity and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (p = 0.023 for a nonlinearity). Subgroup analyses highlighted stronger effects in overweight/obese individuals (hazard ratio: 0.62, 95% confidence interval: 0.43–0.88), suggesting that this high-risk population may derive particular benefit from increased physical activity.ConclusionsAnalyses of two large databases demonstrated a nonlinear dose–response association between higher physical activity levels and reduced metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease risk. Moderate physical activity levels (600 to <3000 metabolic equivalent of task–min/week) were significantly protective, and high physical activity levels (≥3000 metabolic equivalent of task–min/week) conferred additional benefits.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), characterized by hepatic steatosis and metabolic dysregulation, poses a significant global health burden due to its progression to cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and extrahepatic complications such as cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes. 1 Formerly termed nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), MASLD represents the most prevalent chronic liver condition. 2 MASLD affects approximately 32% of the global population and accounts for approximately 30% of liver-related mortality worldwide. 3 Due to the lack of effective medication, current treatment options for MASLD remain limited, primarily focusing on lifestyle modifications such as weight loss and dietary changes.

Physical activity (PA) serves as a modifiable factor in promoting systemic metabolic health and mitigating chronic disease progression. PA has been identified as a therapeutic strategy to prevent liver diseases. Robust evidence indicates that regular PA enhances insulin sensitivity, facilitates visceral fat reduction, and improves mitochondrial function, thereby preventing hepatocellular damage. 4 Additionally, beyond hepatic benefits, when combined with nutritional interventions, PA exhibits synergistic effects in reducing all-cause mortality among cancer survivors. 5 Notably, PA improves cardiovascular fitness and glycemic control, reducing the risk of MASLD-associated comorbidities, including type 2 diabetes and atherosclerosis.6,7 In general, PA is a cost-effective and scalable intervention that can be easily integrated into daily life, making it a promising strategy for MASLD prevention and management.

However, previous studies remain anchored in NAFLD frameworks. The present study focused on the relationship between PA and MASLD. According to the multisociety Delphi consensus statement, MASLD is diagnosed using positive diagnostic criteria, requiring the presence of hepatic steatosis and at least one cardiometabolic risk factor (e.g. obesity, diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia), whereas NAFLD is diagnosed via exclusion, requiring the absence of excessive alcohol consumption and other liver diseases. 2 Therefore, MASLD clearly emphasizes the central role of metabolic dysfunction in disease pathogenesis. Moreover, most previous studies were predominantly cross-sectional in design, which limited the ability to establish causal relationships or assess the long-term impact of PA on MASLD progression and regression. 8 Meanwhile, several studies were confined to single-database analyses, limiting the generalizability of the findings, particularly across populations with diverse geographic and demographic characteristics. 9 Furthermore, empirical studies quantifying the dose–response effects of PA on MASLD progression remain scarce, limiting the understanding of potential nonlinear associations. Finally, the benefits of exercise in specific populations, such as those with obesity or prediabetes, remain unclear, impeding the formulation of precision-based exercise regimens for MASLD prevention in metabolically vulnerable cohorts.

Herein, by exploring the association between PA and MASLD via analyses of two major databases, we aimed to identify effective and accessible preventive measures that are critical to curb this escalating public health crisis.

Methods

Study design

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), sponsored by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), is a nationally representative, cross-sectional survey to assess the health and nutritional status of the US population using a complex, multistage probability sampling design (www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes.htm).

The UK Biobank (UKB) is a large-scale prospective cohort study that recruited over 500,000 participants aged 40–69 years across the UK between 2006 and 2010. At recruitment, participants completed touchscreen-enabled questionnaires, underwent physical measurements, and provided blood samples. The cohort has since undergone multiple follow-up assessments, with the study database undergoing continual maintenance and regular updates (www.ukbiobank.ac.uk).

For the sample from the NHANES and UKB, we employed stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure the reliability of our analyses. Figure 1(a) and (b) provide an overview of the enrollment process.

Flowchart of participants from the (a) NHANES and (b) UKB).

The NHANES enrolled a comprehensive cohort of 66,148 participants, covering the continuous survey years from 2007 to 2020. After applying the exclusion criteria, the analysis was conducted on a sample size of 4458 participants.

Similarly, for the UKB cohort, the study sample initially included 502,135 participants. Following the exclusion criteria of the flowchart, our final analytical cohort comprised 17,121 participants with complete PA data (MET-min/week).

Definition and assessment of MASLD

In the NHANES, liver transient elastography was missing in most of the interview cycles. Thus, the assessment of MASLD status primarily relied on the fatty liver index (FLI). FLI is a noninvasive and accurate tool for assessing steatotic liver disease (SLD), with high sensitivity and specificity. The equation is listed below: 10 FLI=(e0.953×ln(TG)+0.139×BMI+0.718×ln(GGT)+0.053×WC−15.7451+e0.953×ln(TG)+0.139×BMI+0.718×ln(GGT)+0.053×WC−15.745)×100

The formula includes the variables of triglyceride (TG), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), body mass index (BMI), and waist circumference (WC). According to previous studies, participants with a FLI <60 were considered to have a low probability of hepatic steatosis, whereas those with a FLI ≥60 are at a higher risk. 10 Therefore, participants with a FLI ≥60 were diagnosed with SLD. To establish a diagnosis of MASLD according to the Delphi criteria, participants with viral hepatitis or excessive alcohol consumption (≥30 g/day for males and ≥20 g/day for females) were excluded. Consequently, MASLD was defined as SLD with a combination of the presence of at least one cardiometabolic risk factor: (a) BMI ≥25 kg m−2 or WC ≥94 cm for males and ≥80 cm for females; (b) fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels ≥100 mg/dL, 2-h post-load glucose levels ≥140 mg/dL, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥5.7%, a diagnosis of diabetes mellitus (DM), or treatment using hypoglycemic medications; (c) systolic blood pressure (SBP)/diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥130/85 mmHg or current use of antihypertensive medications; (d) fasting plasma TGs ≥150 mg/dL or treatment using lipid-lowering drugs; and (e) plasma high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels <40 mg/dL for males and <50 mg/dL for females or treatment using lipid-lowering drugs. 2

In the UKB, MASLD was determined using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) codes K76.0 and K75.8. Participants were followed up from the date of enrollment until the date of MASLD diagnosis, death, or date of follow-up (30 September 2021 for England, 28 February 2018 for Wales, and 31 July 2021 for Scotland), whichever occurred first. 11

Definition and assessment of PA

In the NHANES, the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ), developed by the World Health Organization (WHO), was used to assess individuals’ PA. 12 In accordance with the WHO analysis guide, the utilization of the MET serves as a quantifiable unit for assessing the level of PA. 12 The NHANES provides the recommended MET values for each PA. The formula is as follows: PA (MET-min/week) = MET × weekly frequency × duration of each PA. 8 In the UKB, PA was based on self-report, using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) short form. Although GPAQ and IPAQ short form were used to evaluate PA in NHANES and UKB cohorts, respectively, studies have shown that the two tools exhibit similar trends and results in measuring PA levels.13,14 According to the IPAQ data processing guidelines, total PA was computed as the sum of all activity, measured as metabolic equivalents (MET-min/week), which were produced by multiplying the duration of walking as well as moderate and vigorous PA by 3.3, 4.0, and 8.0 METs, respectively. 15 Based on the PA data, participants’ PA levels were classified as low (total PA < 600 MET-min/week), moderate (600 to < 3000 MET-min/week), and high (≥3000 MET-min/week).16,17 Details of the UKB classification of the three PA levels are provided in Table S1.

Covariates

We used questionnaires and self-reports to collect on covariates, including age, sex, ethnicity, BMI, educational level, Townsend deprivation index (UKB only), poverty/income ratio (PIR) (NHANES only), smoking status, alcohol consumption status (NHANES only), frequency of drinking (UKB only), hypertension, and diabetes. 18 BMI was calculated by dividing the body weight (kg) by the square of the height (m2). Additionally, we classified BMI according to the WHO criteria in the UKB as follows: (a) underweight, <18.5 kg/m2; (b) normal weight, 18.5–24.9 kg/m2; (c) overweight, 25.0–29.9 kg/m2; and (d) obese ≥30.0 kg/m2.

In the NHANES, income levels were assessed using the PIR, which is calculated by dividing the total household income by a factor specific to household size and composition. PIR was categorized into two levels (<1.0 and ≥1.0). 19 Diabetes was defined by an FBG level ≥126 mg/dL, HbA1c ≥6.5%, and/or self-reported doctor diagnosis and/or antidiabetic treatment. 20 After measuring blood pressure thrice under quiescent conditions, the average values of SBP and DBP were calculated. Individuals with an SBP ≥130 mmHg and/or DBP ≥80 mmHg were considered as having hypertension. 21 Additionally, patients with self-reported doctor diagnosis and/or antihypertensive treatment were classified as hypertensive.

In the UKB, area-based socioeconomic status was derived from the postcode of residence, using the Townsend deprivation index. 22 Data regarding the diagnosis of hypertension and diabetes were collected from the self-completed, baseline assessment questionnaires. Data on alcohol consumption frequency were collected using a self-reported lifestyle questionnaire.

Statistical analyses

This study employed a two-phase analytical design to systematically evaluate the association between PA and MASLD. First, we conducted cross-sectional analyses of the NHANES data using weighted multinomial logistic regression models to examine the preliminary associations. Second, cohort analyses using Cox proportional hazards models were performed for the UKB data to assess potential causality. These analyses were further supplemented with restricted cubic splines (RCS) analysis, subgroup analyses, and sensitivity tests, thereby providing a comprehensive assessment of the long-term effects of PA on the risk of MASLD. This multidesign approach enhanced the reliability and validity of our findings.

Given the different designs of the two cohorts, we employed distinct risk estimation methods to assess the association. NHANES is a cross-sectional study, which captures exposure and outcome at a single time point; this limits the assessment of temporal relationships. Therefore, logistic regression was performed to estimate odds ratios (ORs) for the association between PA and MASLD. In contrast, the UKB is a prospective cohort study with a longitudinal follow-up, enabling evaluation of incident MASLD over time. Accordingly, Cox proportional hazards models were employed to estimate hazard ratios (HRs), reflecting the long-term risk associated with PA. This dual analytical design allows the comprehensive evaluation of both cross-sectional associations and longitudinal validation. Similar analytical strategies have been applied in previous epidemiological studies.23,24

To ensure sample representativeness, the NHANES analyses were weighted using appropriate sample weights from the official NHANES website. Baseline characteristics of participants were presented as mean (SD) or median (interquartile range (M (Q1, Q3)) values for continuous variables and as percentages (%) for categorical variables. In the NHANES, variables identified as statistically significant in univariate analysis were further analyzed using multinomial logistic regression. For the UKB cohort, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to calculate the cumulative MASLD risks using three categories of PA (low, moderate, and high), with log-rank tests used for comparisons. Cox proportional hazards models were applied to estimate HRs and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between PA and incident MASLD risk. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using the Schoenfeld residual method. Table S2 presents the results of the Schoenfeld residuals test for the proportional hazards (PH) assumption in the Cox regression model. 25 Although the global test indicated no violation (p = 0.462), educational level showed significant time-dependence (p = 0.017). Therefore, we stratified the Cox models by the covariate to accommodate differing baseline hazards across educational levels. All other covariates satisfied the PH assumption (all p > 0.05). Furthermore, a correlation matrix analysis was performed to evaluate collinearity between all included covariates, and no multicollinearity was detected. RCS models with three knots were conducted to estimate the dose–response association between total PA and MASLD. Subgroup analyses were performed to investigate the consistency of results. To assess the robustness of our findings, several sensitivity analyses were also conducted. First, we adopted a threshold of 1200 MET-min/week to distinguish between moderate and high PA levels based on some studies. Second, to reduce the potential reverse causation, we repeated the main analyses after excluding outcomes that occurred within the first 2 years of follow-up. Third, participants with missing covariates were excluded.

Missing covariate values were imputed with the random forest algorithm using the multivariate imputation by chained equation (MICE, version 3.17.0) package. Detailed missing rates for the covariates used in this study are provided in Table S3.

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In total, 1756 individuals (39.4%) were classified with low PA, 1344 (30.1%) with moderate PA, and 1358 (30.5%) with high PA. Individuals with higher PA levels were more likely to be younger and female, have a lower BMI, be highly educated, have greater economic advantage, less cigarette use, and a reduced incidence of hypertension and diabetes.

Over a median follow-up period of 14.18 years, a total of 181 participants (1.06%) developed MASLD in the UKB database. The baseline characteristics of the participants according to PA patterns are presented in Table 2. The cohort predominantly comprised participants of White ethnicity (93.2%), with a median age of 58 years and balanced sex distribution (49.3% female). Significant differences (p < 0.01) were observed across PA groups for most variables; individuals with low levels of PA exhibited a higher BMI, greater proportions of obesity, lower educational level, lower frequency of alcohol consumption, and a higher smoking prevalence than participants with high PA levels. The Townsend deprivation index, a measure of socioeconomic status, was inversely associated with PA levels, with higher levels linked to lower deprivation indices (higher socioeconomic status). Metabolic profiles varied substantially, with participants with low PA levels showing higher prevalence of diabetes and hypertension.

Association between PA and MASLD

The Kaplan–Meier curves showed significant differences in the cumulative risk of MASLD development stratified by PA patterns (log-rank p < 0.001) (Figure 2). As expected, low PA levels exhibited the steepest hazard accumulation over time. Table 3 presents the associations between PA and incident MASLD risk in the NHANES cohort. The fully adjusted model revealed that higher total PA (log-transformed) was associated with a decreased risk of MASLD (OR: 0.85, 95% CI: 0.75–0.96, p = 0.01). Consistently, both participants with moderate (OR: 0.79, 95% CI: 0.64–0.97) and high (OR: 0.78, 95% CI: 0.63–0.97) PA levels had significantly lower odds of MASLD than individuals with low levels of PA (both p < 0.05).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

The results of the subgroup analyses of the UK Biobank cohort are illustrated in Figure S1. It revealed important variations in the protective effect of PA against MASLD risk, and no significant interactions were observed across subgroups (all p-interaction > 0.05). Notably, after adjusting for BMI as a confounding factor, the overall model's p-value increased, underscoring the critical importance of BMI-stratified subgroup analysis. PA showed stronger benefits among individuals with BMI >25 kg/m2 (HR: 0.62, 95% CI: 0.43–0.88, p = 0.008) than among those with BMI ≤25 kg/m2 (HR: 0.86, 95% CI: 0.28–2.62, p = 0.794). This finding highlighted an important protective effect of PA in those with a higher BMI, indicating that BMI may be a key modifier of PA effectiveness. Age-stratified subgroup analyses demonstrated robust protective effects in participants aged <65 years, whereas the effects were nonsignificant in older adults. Consistent positive impacts were observed in both sexes. In addition, educational level and smoking status exhibited negative trends, with significant risk reduction in college-educated individuals (HR: 0.54, 95% CI: 0.38–0.76, p < 0.001) and never smokers (HR: 0.55, 95% CI: 0.35–0.88, p = 0.012). These findings collectively suggest that PA interventions are most impactful when targeted toward specific high-risk subgroups, particularly overweight/obese individuals and middle-aged adults.

To ensure the robustness of our findings, we performed several sensitivity analyses, all of which consistently supported the primary results. Table S4 presents the results of the sensitivity analyses for the association of PA patterns with MASLD risk. First, we designated 1200 MET-min/week as the moderate-to-high threshold. Encouragingly, this approach yielded results that were highly consistent with our primary analysis, confirming the reliability of our conclusions. Second, we excluded participants within 2 years of follow-up. Importantly, the results remained consistent with the initial analysis, indicating the durability of the observed correlations over an extended period. Furthermore, upon confining our analysis to those participants with complete covariate data, there was no substantial change, thereby further reinforcing the robustness of our findings.

Discussion

Our study utilized data from both NHANES and UKB database to elucidate the relationship between PA and MASLD risk. The integration of cross-sectional and longitudinal cohorts revealed a nonlinear inverse association between PA levels and MASLD risk, with progressively higher PA levels correlating with a greater risk reduction. This association was observed across different demographics and was robust to various analyses, including RCS, subgroup, and sensitivity analyses. These results underscore the potential utility of PA as a modifiable lifestyle intervention to reduce the growing global burden of MASLD, which is in agreement with previous studies. 26

Our findings confirmed an inverse relationship between PA and MASLD risk, consistent with earlier NAFLD studies. Several observational studies have shown that PA reduces the incidence of NAFLD. An Italian cross-sectional analysis involving 191 individuals has revealed an inverse correlation between liver fat content and regular physical exercise. 27 A study involving 139,056 Koreans has indicated that spending >5 h daily in a sedentary posture increases the ultrasound-detectable risk of NAFLD. 28 Another Korean study has demonstrated that people who exercise ≥3/week for at least 30 min for >3 months halved their risk of developing NAFLD. 29 Another study using the NHANES 2017–2018 data has shown that PA (≥600 MET-min/week) is negatively associated with NAFLD risk. 8 These findings consistently support the positive effects of exercise training on improved liver health, particularly in terms of liver fat reduction. However, the present study expanded the evidence to the MASLD framework and represents one of the first studies to systematically examine these relationships using the 2023 MASLD diagnostic criteria, which combines hepatic steatosis with cardiometabolic risk factors. This distinguishes our work from earlier NAFLD-centric research.

Although a prior study has shown that individuals who engaged in at least the recommended amount of leisure-time PA had lower risk of MASLD, its cross-sectional design, relatively small sample size, and exclusive focus on the American population constrained causal inference and generalizability. 9 In contrast, our study extended the scope by utilizing two large-scale databases to include participants from diverse geographic and demographic backgrounds. This robust approach ensured more accurate and stable results. Additionally, by integrating cross-sectional analyses with longitudinal validation, we established temporality and reinforced causality. This represents a critical advancement over single-design studies. In addition, previous studies have only measured the frequency of leisure-time PA without considering the change in PA levels. We calculated the amount of PA by using a combination of frequency, intensity, and duration.

Our study revealed that even moderate PA levels conferred significant protection against MASLD. After adjustments for possible confounders, Model 3 showed a 21%–22% reduction in the disease risk in the NHANES cohort and a more pronounced 35%–43% lower long-term risk in the UKB cohort compared with the low-PA levels group. This is particularly relevant for clinical practice as it suggests achievable PA targets for high-risk populations.

The dose–response relationship further highlighted that although high PA levels (≥3000 MET-min/week) offer additional protection, the most significant risk reduction occurred at moderate intensities, making PA an accessible intervention for most individuals. However, we found the wide CIs at higher PA levels in the Figure 3. It may be attributed to the decreasing sample sizes in the higher exposure strata. To enhance the precision, a larger sample size or additional data from other studies may be necessary to provide more robust conclusions about the relationship between high levels of PA and MASLD.

The subgroup analyses provide important insights into the differential effects of PA across population subgroups. Most notably, we observed a particularly strong protective effect of PA against MASLD risk in individuals with BMI >25 kg/m2, whereas no significant association was observed among normal-weight individuals. This disparity likely stems from three interrelated mechanisms. First, some studies have demonstrated that overweight/obese individuals exhibit substantially higher baseline NAFLD prevalence, inherently amplifying the absolute risk reduction potential of exercise interventions in this subgroup.30,31 Second, exercise exerts targeted improvements on obesity-associated metabolic disturbances, particularly insulin resistance and visceral adiposity, which were key drivers of hepatic steatosis. Mechanistic studies have revealed that exercise enhances hepatic fatty acid oxidation capacity and reduces inflammatory cytokines, with these effects being more clinically consequential in metabolically compromised overweight individuals. 32 Third, synergistic effects between exercise and weight modulation further differentiate outcomes. For instance, exercise combined with intentional weight control (≥5% body weight reduction) reduces NAFLD risk by 34.9% in overweight populations, whereas normal-weight individuals, despite benefiting from exercise-induced metabolic optimization, experience smaller absolute risk reductions due to lower baseline hepatic fat content.30,31,33,34

Our study has certain limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although we adjusted for major known confounders such as socioeconomic status and lifestyle factors, residual confounding from unmeasured variables, particularly genetic predisposition and dietary patterns, cannot be entirely ruled out. Emerging evidence has suggested that genetic variants such as PNPLA3 rs738409, which independently modulate hepatic lipid metabolism, may attenuate PA effect in observational studies. 35 Second, the diagnostic methods for MASLD differed between the two databases. In the NHANES cohort, the identification of MASLD relied on the FLI combined with metabolic dysfunction criteria, whereas the UKB utilized the ICD-10 codes. Although the FLI serves as a practical screening tool for steatosis with validated accuracy, its limited sensitivity for detecting fibrosis may have resulted in underdiagnosis in terms of advanced MASLD cases. Third, although the total amount of PA was analyzed, the differential effects of specific PA types such as aerobic and resistance exercise remain unexplored. One review has revealed clinically meaningful differences in NAFLD, depending on activity type, with resistance exercise being more feasible than aerobic exercise for NAFLD patients with poor cardiorespiratory fitness or those who cannot perform or participate in aerobic exercise. 36 Fourth, as this study was based on secondary analyses of publicly available databases, the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Although the longitudinal UKB cohort and sensitivity analyses enhanced robustness, external clinical validation is needed. Future studies should incorporate device-measured PA assessments, prospective clinical designs, randomized exercise interventions, and mechanistic investigations to validate these findings and elucidate mechanistic pathways.

Conclusion

This study provides robust evidence that higher PA levels are associated with a reduced risk of MASLD. Achieving at least moderate PA levels (600 to <3000 MET-min/week) was significantly protective against MASLD, with high PA levels (≥3000 MET-min/week) conferring additional benefits. These findings suggest that sufficient weekly PA, achieved by walking, performing moderate- or vigorous-intensity exercise, or their combination represents a practical strategy for MASLD prevention, particularly in overweight and obese individuals. Future research should clarify the optimal type, pattern, and underlying mechanisms of PA to inform individualized lifestyle prescriptions.
