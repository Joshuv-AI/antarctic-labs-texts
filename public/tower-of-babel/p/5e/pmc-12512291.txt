Adherence status and influencing factors of acute and preventive pharmacotherapy among migraine patients in China
Authors: Cancan Chen, Zizi He, Jing Zhang, Longteng Ma, Li Kang, Hui Su, Zhenjie Ma, Shuqing Wang, Yunyun Huo, Shengyuan Yu, Ye Ran, Zhao Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the current adherence status and influencing factors for acute and preventive pharmacotherapy among migraine patients in China.MethodsUtilizing a prospective cohort design with retrospective identification, migraine patients first attending the Headache Clinic (Chinese PLA General Hospital, May–November 2024) were prospectively assessed for treatment adherence and influencing factors via structured telephone follow-ups.ResultsAmong 456 enrolled patients (75.9% female, 24.1% male), migraine subtypes included probable migraine (14.9%), migraine without aura (72.6%), migraine with aura (4.2%), and chronic migraine (8.3%). Good adherence rates were 30.5% for acute and 53.3% for preventive pharmacotherapy. Key non-adherence behaviors for acute medication: Abstinence from medication during attacks (63.1%) and administration after pain progression to moderate/severe intensity (35.0%). Primary reasons: mild headache deemed non-requiring medication (43.3%) and fear of potential adverse effects (36.0%). Key non-adherence behaviors for preventive medication: incomplete treatment course duration (76.4%), failure to obtain prescribed medications (15.3%), and non-adherence to prescribed medication schedule (11.1%). Primary reasons given for nonadherence to preventive medication included perceived prescription inappropriateness (43.1%) and adverse effects that occurred (19.4%). Multivariate regression analysis revealed that adherence to acute pharmacotherapy increased with higher educational level, medical insurance coverage, satisfaction with pain relief, and enhanced patient valuation of pharmacotherapy (all p < 0.05). For preventive medication, adherence was associated with adverse effects, satisfaction with treatment efficacy, and enhanced patient valuation of pharmacotherapy (all p < 0.05). Good adherence independently predicted fewer monthly headache days (MHD: 3.4 ± 3.9 days vs. 7.7 ± 6.1 days, p < 0.01).ConclusionSuboptimal medication adherence persists among Chinese migraine patients, with patient valuation of pharmacotherapy being the strongest modifiable factor. Interventions should prioritize patient education, personalized treatment plans, and tailored adherence strategies to improve outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-025-02156-3.Keywords: Migraine, Medication adherence, Treatment barriers, Pharmacotherapy optimization

Introduction

Migraine is a common recurrent neurological disorder with an approximately 14.4% global prevalence [1] and an annual prevalence of 9.3% in China [2]. It is characterized by repeated attacks of moderate to severe headache often accompanied by nausea, vomiting, photophobia, and phonophobia, with serious impacts on quality of life and work productivity. Migraine is the leading cause of years lived with disability (YLDs) among the under-50s [3]. Lack of adequate treatment and management contributes to the high disease burden, and the efficacy of pharmacological treatment has been linked to long-term adherence which remains suboptimal, both in acute and preventive therapy [4, 5]. The American Migraine Prevalence and Prevention (AMPP) study considered that preventive medication should be given to approximately 40% of migraine patients but uptake was only 13.0% [6]. A Canadian study also indicated declining adherence to preventive medication over time, with 50% discontinuation at three months [7]. Hepp Z et al. found that only 26%–29% chronic migraine patients in the United States continued to take oral preventive medications at six months and 17%–20% at twelve months [8]. Another U.S.-based study reported a non-adherence rate of up to 70% for acute migraine medication [9–11], contributing to more frequent attacks, greater disability, and higher healthcare costs [12].

Previous studies on medication adherence among migraine patients have predominantly focused on Western populations, with scarce data from China [13]. Culturally specific factors—including health beliefs, education levels, health insurance systems, and medication access—significantly influence treatment adherence and differ substantially between Chinese and Western contexts. Currently approved medications for the acute and preventive treatment of migraine in China encompass all drug classes recommended in contemporary treatment guidelines. However, the diversity of available agents within specific classes—notably triptans and gepants—remains limited (Further details regarding available agents are presented in Supplementary Material, Table S1). The present study investigated the status and influencing factors of medication adherence among migraine patients in China, with particular attention to these culturally and systemically distinctive factors, in order to inform targeted management strategies and public health initiatives for reducing the disease burden.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a prospective cohort design with retrospective identification of the cohort. The study was conducted in two phases. First, a retrospective cohort was established: based on the headache clinical decision support system (CDSS) database [14], patients who had their first visit at the headache center of our institution between May 1, 2024, and November 30, 2024 were retrospectively screened according to predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria. All data used in this analysis were derived exclusively from the prospectively registered real-world study (Registration No. ChiCTR2200064494). Only baseline information (demographic characteristics, medical history, and prior medication use) was extracted from electronic medical records to preliminarily identify the potential study cohort, with no prospective data collected at this stage. Subsequently, prospective follow-up and data collection were carried out: for the eligible patients identified above, key research outcome-related data were gathered through structured telephone interviews and standardized electronic questionnaires. Critical data that were unavailable during the retrospective phase included medication types, timing and dosage, reasons for discontinuation, recent headache attacks, awareness of migraine, medication satisfaction, and whether a headache diary was maintained. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Chinese PLA General Hospital (Approval No. 2024813).

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria: patients who met the diagnostic criteria of the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3), including probable migraine, migraine without aura, migraine with aura, chronic migraine and medication overuse headache (MOH) secondary to migraine; consented to complete the questionnaire based on the CDSS for headache disorders; and aged 18–65 years. Exclusion criteria: incomplete clinical records; the patient denied any headache episodes or use of preventive medications from the time of consultation until the follow-up appointment.

Study procedures

Trained interviewers conducted structured telephone interviews using standardized electronic questionnaires, gathering variables across four key domains: (1) Acute Treatment: Included occurrence of post-visit headache attacks, medication use, adverse reactions (occurrence and manifestations), satisfaction with pain relief and functional recovery, and reasons for non-adherence. (2) Patient Understanding of Migraine: Covered awareness of triggers, ability to recognize headache onset, and perceived importance of analgesics. (3) Preventive Treatment: Captured prescription and actual use of preventive medications, type, dosage, frequency, adverse effects, satisfaction regarding pain and function, number of headache attacks in the prior month, and reasons for non-adherence. (4) Follow-up Data: Documented headache days and moderate-to-severe pain days over the past three months, and use of a headache diary (details in Supplementary Materials). The interview will be administered three months following the patient’s initial consultation. Patients were instructed to check medication packaging or prescription records before answering questions on drug types and dosages to minimize recall bias.

Adherence was clearly defined for both preventive and acute medications. Good adherence to preventive medication was defined as taking the drug as prescribed for more than 80% of the treatment period [15]. In contrast, poor adherence to preventive treatment included any of the following: deviation from the prescribed dosing schedule, failure to complete the treatment course (i.e., less than 3 months), refusal or inability to obtain the medication, self-initiated use during a symptom-free period as a preemptive measure, or non-use of guideline-recommended medications (excluding medications prescribed by specialist doctors in formal medical institutions) [16]. For acute medications, good adherence was characterized by using the drug within the prescribed parameters and at the instructed time point during a migraine attack [15, 17]. Poor adherence to acute medication was defined as either administration only after pain had progressed to moderate or severe intensity, medication overuse (according to ICHD-3 criteria: NSAIDs on ≥ 10 days/month, triptans or combination analgesics on ≥ 8 days/month) [17], or complete avoidance of acute medication during an attack. Significant headache improvement was separately defined as a ≥ 50% reduction in monthly headache days (MHD) [18]. Regarding the description of medication overuse in MOH patients, it is necessary to clearly distinguish between two types of medication overuse behaviors: historical overuse that led to the MOH diagnosis at enrollment and the medication overuse assessed during the follow-up period. Upon enrollment, all patients had received clear instructions from specialists to discontinue overused medications and to initiate guideline-recommended preventive therapy. The term “poor adherence due to medication overuse” as defined in this study specifically refers to the recurrence or persistence of acute medication overuse during the prospective follow-up phase, occurring after explicit medical advice had been provided. Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed by Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance by Levene’s test and variables meeting the criteria were analyzed by the independent sample t-test and otherwise by Mann-Whitney U test. If all expected frequencies of categorical variables in each cell were ≥ 5 and the total sample size was ≥ 40, Pearson χ² test was used for analysis and if any expected frequency in a 2 × 2 contingency table was < 5 or if the total sample size was < 40, Fisher’s exact test was applied. Unordered categorical variables were analyzed by correlation test. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Other variables are expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR). All graphical representations in this study were generated using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Variables with p < 0.1 in univariate analysis were included in a multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent influencing factors. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Selection was based on the consistency of effect direction for collinear variables with variance inflation factor (VIF > 5). For collinear variables showing the same direction of association with the outcome, either positive or negative, the variable with clearer biological or clinical relevance was retained, according to the principle of theoretical priority. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Results

A total of 456 patients were enrolled; 110 (24.1%) were male and 346 (75.9%) were female. The cohort included 69 patients (15.1%) with probable migraine, 341 (74.8%) with migraine without aura, 19 (4.2%) with migraine with aura, and 38 (8.3%) with chronic migraine. 17 patients (3.7%) with chronic migraine also met the diagnostic criteria for medication overuse headache. 452 patients (99.1%) experienced headache attacks between clinic visits and follow-up and required acute pain medication. 154 of these patients (33.8%) were prescribed both acute and preventive medication (Table 1).

452 patients used acute medications, 138 (30.5%) with good adherence and 314 (69.5%) with poor adherence (Fig. 2 A). As patients could report more than one type of non-adherent behavior, the percentages do not sum to 100%. The most prevalent non-adherent behavior was omitting medication during attacks (63.1%), followed by delaying medication use until headache reached moderate-severe intensity (35.0%). In addition, 7.6% of patients continued to overuse medication after outpatient treatment, and 8.6% used medication not recommended by clinical guidelines (Fig. 2 A). We next investigated the reasons behind these non-adherent behaviors (Fig. 2B). Similarly, as multiple reasons could be selected, the total percentage exceeds 100%. The primary reason for poor adherence was misidentification of a mild headache that did not require medication (43.3%). 36.0% of patients refused medication due to concerns about potential adverse effects but only 3.8% discontinued medication due to adverse effects occurred. 17.2% reported dissatisfaction with the treatment. 13.1% of patients demonstrated suboptimal adherence attributed to preemptive medication use due to anticipatory fear of headache attacks. 8.60% preferred non-pharmacological approaches (Fig. 2B). NSAIDs were the most frequently used acute medication, accounting for 48.7% of prescriptions, and ibuprofen was the most frequently used (44.3%) (data are provided in Supplementary Table 4). The good adherence rate of NSAIDs was relatively low at 30.9%. Triptans were the second most common option (25.0%) with a good adherence rate of 41.6%. Acetaminophen was used by 7.1% and combination analgesics by 16.2% of patients with respective good adherence rates of 21.9% and 23.3%. Gepants comprised only 2.4% of prescriptions but demonstrated the highest adherence rate among all medication classes (72.7%). (Fig. 2 C).

Among 154 patients receiving preventive medication, good adherence was observed in 82 (53.3%) and poor adherence in 72 (46.8%) (Fig. 3 A). As patients could report more than one contributing factor, the percentages do not sum to 100%. Treatment duration of less than three months accounted for poor adherence by 55 patients (76.4%, Fig. 3 A). The reasons underlying poor adherence are shown in Fig. 3B. The leading reason for poor adherence was patients’ belief in perceived prescription inappropriateness (43.1%), followed by adverse effects occurred (19.4%). Other reported reasons included mild headache deemed non-requiring medication (13.9%), suboptimal therapeutic efficacy (13.9%), fear of potential adverse effects (11.1%), and inability to access prescribed medications (8.3%, Fig. 3B). Differences in adherence were observed for different types of prescribed preventive medication. The calcium channel blocker flunarizine was the most frequently prescribed preventive medication, comprising 35.7% of prescriptions with 65.5% good adherence. Antiepileptic drugs ranked second in prescription frequency, with topiramate accounting for 31.8% and sodium valproate 10.4% of prescriptions. Adherence variables: Topiramate demonstrated 53.1% good adherence versus 43.8% for sodium valproate. Calcium channel modulators (pregabalin and gabapentin) accounted for 11.0% and 3.3% of prescriptions with good adherence rates of 52.9% and 52.0%, respectively. 6.5% of patients were prescribed amitriptyline and had a poor adherence rate of 40.0% (data are provided in Supplementary Table 5) (Fig. 3 C).

The incidence of adverse effects was notably lower among patients using acute medications (5.1%) compared to those on preventive treatments (30.5%). Medication discontinuation attributable to adverse effects was infrequent in the acute medication group (3.8%). The most commonly reported adverse effects associated with acute medications included nausea (17.4%), drowsiness (17.4%), dizziness (17.4%), and abdominalgia (8.7%). In contrast, preventive medications were predominantly associated with drowsiness (36.2%) and limb numbness (19.1%); other frequently observed adverse effects comprised dizziness (8.5%),blurred vision (8.5%) bradyphrenia (4.3%), memory deficit (4.3%), weight gain (4.3%), and xerostomia (4.3%) (Detailed information is provided in Table S2 of the Supplementary Materials).

Patients with good adherence had fewer MHD at follow-up compared with those with poor adherence (3.4 ± 3.9 days vs. 7.7 ± 6.1 days, p < 0.01), despite no significant difference in baseline MHD between the two groups. Median baseline MHD was 6.5 [3.5, 12.7] days for patients with good adherence and 6.0 [3.2, 13.3] days for those with poor adherence (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

For acute migraine treatment, patients with higher education levels demonstrated significantly better medication adherence than those with compulsory education or less (high school: OR = 3.2, 95% CI: 1.3–7.8; college or above: OR = 2.6, 95% CI: 1.2–5.7). In addition, migraine with aura (OR = 4.0, 95% CI: 1.3–12.0) and chronic migraine (OR = 3.4, 95% CI: 1.5–7.7) demonstrated significantly better adherence compared to migraine without aura.

By contrast, the presence of nausea during headache attacks (OR = 1.7, 95% CI: 1.1–2.7), reimbursement of medication costs through insurance (OR = 1.7, 95% CI: 1.0-2.6), satisfaction with medication-induced pain relief (OR = 1.4, 95% CI: 1.2–1.6), awareness of headache triggers (OR = 1.9, 95% CI: 1.1–3.5) or higher patient valuation of pharmacotherapy (OR = 1.9, 95% CI: 1.1–3.3) were associated with better adherence (Fig. 4) (Univariate analysis results are presented in Supplementary Table 1). Adverse effects (OR = 0.4, 95% CI: 0.2–0.9) were the main factors causing poor adherence to preventive medication. Higher adherence was associated with satisfaction with headache relief (OR = 1.3, 95% CI: 1.0-1.5) and higher patient valuation of pharmacotherapy (OR = 4.5, 95% CI: 1.7–11.9, Fig. 5) (Univariate analysis results are presented in Supplementary Table 2). Multivariate analysis of 74 patients (48.1%) achieving ≥ 50% MHD reduction identified good adherence as an independent predictor of significant symptom improvement (OR = 8.9, 95% CI: 3.8–21.2, Fig. 6) (Univariate analysis results are presented in Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

Poor adherence to migraine medication remains a major clinical challenge and is associated with high clinical and economic patient burdens. The current study found that adherence to both acute and preventive medication among 456 Chinese migraine patients was generally low and confirmed adherence to be an independent factor influencing the effectiveness of pharmacological migraine treatment. The adherence status and factors influencing adherence in real-world clinical practice were investigated to inform migraine management strategies.

Acute migraine medication adherence

Suboptimal adherence to acute migraine treatment was shown by a good adherence rate of only 30.5%, consistent with findings from studies in other countries [9, 19]. NSAIDs remained the first-line choice for acute treatment for most patients, a pattern potentially explained by their lower economic cost and greater accessibility compared with triptans [20]. However, the good adherence rate was 30.9%, lower than for triptans (41.6%). A prospective near-time electronic diary study, which required participants to record medication use immediately after a migraine attack, found that 105 out of 301 (35%) patients using triptans maintained adherence for at least two-thirds of attacks over a 2–6 months period. 7,673 documented episodes of migraine, probable migraine or suspected migraine included 3,273 episodes (43%) of nonadherent triptan use [19]. Patients with migraine with aura or chronic migraine showed better medication adherence than non-aura counterparts. This may reflect distinct prodromal features (e.g., visual/sensory auras) acting as treatment cues in aura migraine, while chronic migraine’s persistent burden enhances treatment motivation. Knowledge of migraine triggers and awareness of medication efficacy also emerged as independent predictors of adherence, consistent with previous studies on adherence to triptan therapy [21]. Nonadherent behavior may be largely associated with insufficient patient understanding of migraine management.

Higher educational attainment predicted better acute migraine medication adherence, with significantly stronger compliance observed in patients completing > 9 years of education. This disparity likely stems from health literacy differences: limited education correlates with insufficient understanding of treatment necessity, while educated patients better comprehend therapeutic rationale, enhancing adherence. In addition, patients experiencing vomiting also demonstrated better adherence. As vomiting/nausea reflects core migraine pathophysiology, these symptoms may indicate heightened treatment responsiveness [22, 23]. Furthermore, vomiting can amplify disease severity perception, potentially fostering more proactive treatment behaviors [23]. These findings indicate that improving patient awareness and perception of illness may enhance adherence to acute migraine therapy [24].

Migraine patients commonly underestimate the need for early symptom intervention while overemphasizing medication adverse effects concerns. Our study identified delayed treatment initiation as the primary adherence barrier, with 85.0% of patients withholding medication before pain progression to moderate/severe intensity. These findings align with Foley et al. and Gallagher et al., whose research revealed parallel behavioral patterns: 69% adopted a “wait-and-see” approach before medication intake, while 46% treated attacks only at severe stages [11, 25]. The timing of acute medication use is recognized as a significant factor in reducing migraine-related disability and improving treatment satisfaction [26]. Intervention within one hour of onset or before the headache progresses to moderate or severe intensity has been associated with better therapeutic outcomes [27]. Notably, cultural beliefs also shaped distinctive therapeutic concerns among Chinese migraine patients. The traditional Chinese adage “all medicines carry some toxicity” contributed to medication hesitancy, with 35.9% avoiding prescribed treatments due to adverse effect concerns. However, our study observed only 3.8% actual adverse effects incidence during acute treatment, revealing a substantial perception-reality gap in treatment risks. Therefore, patient education should emphasize guidance on optimal timing for acute medication use while reinforcing instruction in pain severity assessment and medication safety.

Preventive migraine medication adherence

The proportion of current patients with good adherence to preventive medication was 53.2%. By contrast, Lipton RB et al. reported 13.0% of patients to consistently use preventive migraine medication on a daily basis [28]. Andrew Toye Ojo and Hepp Z et al. found only 24% adherence to preventive medication [7, 8] and a separate survey of patients with migraine and cluster headache showed 32.8% adherence to preventive medication [29]. Such previous studies consistently indicate a low proportion of patients with good adherence. The current study found a higher rate of good adherence which may be explained by two factors. First, the current patients were recruited from a tertiary specialist outpatient clinic in Beijing, China, where individuals typically have above-average educational and economic status, greater health awareness, and access to medical resources, which may contribute to better adherence. Second, patients engaged with regular telephone follow-ups, which may have introduced selection bias since commitment to follow-up may have correlated with better treatment adherence. Thus, adherence rates may have been overestimated relative to the general population. However, the proportion of patients with good adherence remained relatively low, and the main feature of this was treatment discontinuation. Preventive migraine treatment usually requires at least 6 months of continuous use for optimal outcomes, but 35.7% of patients used medication for less than three months [28, 30].

Patients’ knowledge level of medications also influenced adherence to preventive therapy. A total of 43.1% of patients with poor adherence perceived “inappropriate prescriptions”, potentially attributable to the fact that most preventive migraine medications included in International Headache Society practice recommendations—like topiramate, sodium valproate, and amitriptyline—lack regulatory approval for migraine indications [31]. These agents are formally indicated for conditions such as epilepsy, depression, anxiety, or vertigo in their package inserts. This regulatory misalignment likely contributes to low adherence, whereas flunarizine—as one of few approved oral agents for migraine prevention—demonstrated significantly higher adherence (65.5%) in our cohort. Inconsistencies between indicated uses and migraine treatment have previously been reported to reduce patient acceptance [32]. A substantial gap exists between patient understanding of migraine and pharmacological rationales, highlighting deficiencies in patient education. This observation is consistent with the recognition of the importance of medication being the most significant factor influencing adherence to headache treatment. Adverse effects of preventive medication were also an independent risk factor for poor adherence. Flunarizine’s favorable safety profile, characterized by a lower risk of adverse effects, may also contribute to its higher adherence rates among patients [33]. By contrast, topiramate had a lower patient adherence (53.1%), perhaps due to a higher rate of adverse events [34]. Previous studies have shown frequent adverse effects of established preventive migraine treatments, which negatively impact treatment adherence and clinical effectiveness [4]. More recent preventive agents, such as CGRP monoclonal antibodies, CGRP receptor monoclonal antibodies and small-molecule CGRP receptor antagonists, have improved safety profiles and may enhance patient adherence, although costs have limited widespread use [35–37]. Gepants were prescribed in only 2.4% of current cases but had a high adherence rate of 72.7%.

Multifactorial influences on adherence: non-pharmacological contributors

Demographic characteristics and baseline clinical headache features, such as attack frequency and pain intensity, were not found to be associated with medication adherence in the current work, consistent with findings of previous studies in other countries [38]. Health insurance coverage had a positive impact on medication adherence, also in alignment with international experience [37]. Only 36.1% of patients were reimbursed for headache medications through insurance, suggesting that more generous insurance coverage may improve adherence among headache patients.

Limitations

The single-center study recruited participants from a specialist outpatient clinic within an academic tertiary hospital, where patients typically exhibit greater disease severity and higher medical adherence. This selection bias may have led to an overestimation of adherence rate. Moreover, the relatively small sample size limits statistical power for analyses. Population homogeneity likely attenuated the observed effects of demographic variables on adherence. Attrition bias is another potential concern: a subset of patients was excluded from the analysis due to refusal to participate in follow-up or loss to follow-up. Given that these individuals generally exhibit poorer adherence, the omission of their data may have introduced bias into the study findings. To address these limitations, future research should employ multicenter, longitudinal study designs to validate and generalize the present findings.

Conclusion

Medication adherence among Chinese migraine patients was associated with clinical outcomes but adherence was suboptimal. Patients’ perceptions of medication were the most influential factor regarding adherence. A model to combine enhanced patient education, optimized treatment choices and robust long-term management may improve medication adherence and enhance disease control and long-term prognosis. Future research may explore digital interventions and shared decision-making models for improved adherence.
