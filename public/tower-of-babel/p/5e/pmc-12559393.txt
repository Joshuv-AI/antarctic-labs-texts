Triglyceride-glucose index variability and risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality
Authors: Jimin Park, Duk Ho Kim, Jun-Hyuk Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index is a reliable marker of insulin resistance with predictive value for cardiovascular risk. While metabolic variability independently impacts cardiovascular outcomes and mortality, the clinical significance of TyG index variability remains unclear. We investigated whether higher TyG index variability increases the risk of mortality. This retrospective cohort study analyzed data from 3,094,062 individuals who participated in the National Health Screening Program from 2009 to 2014. TyG index variability independent of the mean (VIM) was calculated and participants were categorized into quartiles. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was used to estimate hazard ratio (HR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Over a median follow-up of 9.1 years, a total of 137,339 (4.44%) deaths were recorded, including 15,261 (0.56%) from cardiovascular causes. Mortality rose stepwise across TyG index VIM quartiles. Compared to Quartile 1, Quartile 4 had the highest risk of all-cause mortality (HR 1.18, 95% CI 1.16–1.21, P < 0.001) and cardiovascular mortality (HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.06–1.21, P < 0.001). Higher TyG index variability is associated with increased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Serial monitoring of changes in TyG index may contribute to reducing mortality risk through early detection and intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21324-8.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of mortality worldwide, responsible for more than one third of all deaths1,2. In Korea, deaths due to CVD steadily increased between 2010 and 2018, reaching 123 per 100,000 persons3. These numbers urge early identification of individuals at risk to help prevent mortality and adverse outcomes.

Insulin resistance (IR) is a key driver of cardiometabolic disease and is associated with increased risk of CVD, even in individuals without diabetes4–6. It contributes to disease progression through an interplay between chronic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, leading to atherosclerotic plaque formation7. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has been widely studied as a simple and reliable surrogate marker for detecting IR8,9. Unlike the euglycemic clamp, TyG index is non-invasive and cost-effective and has demonstrated superior performance compared to the homeostasis model assessment for IR in predicting cardiovascular (CV) and metabolic outcomes10–12.

Elevated TyG index has been associated with CV events such as stroke or myocardial infarction, and mortality. While most studies have focused mainly on a single baseline measurement of TyG index13,14, emerging evidence suggests that increasing TyG index trajectories are associated with risk of CV events or all-cause mortality15. Recent literature also emphasizes the clinical relevance of metabolic marker variability—for example, high glucose variability has been linked to incident CVD and mortality in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)16,17. A very recent prospective study of 5,220 Iranian adults aged ≥ 30 years, followed for a mean of six years, found that higher TyG variability was independently associated with T2DM and hypertension (HTN) but not with incident CVD or all-cause mortality18. The modest sample size and relatively short follow-up period may have limited statistical power to detect relationships with definitive clinical endpoints. Collectively, these findings raise the question of whether pronounced variability in TyG index drives fatal outcomes.

We therefore conducted a large-scale retrospective cohort study to examine the association between visit-to-visit variability in TyG index and subsequent risks of all-cause and CV mortality. We hypothesized that greater TyG index variability would be independently linked to higher mortality, beyond the influence of mean TyG index levels, thereby providing a simple dynamic marker for stratifying CV risk in routine clinical practice.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study utilized data from the National Health Screening Program conducted by the Korea National Health Insurance Service (NHIS). Figure 1 depicts the selection process for the study population. Initially, 3,105,813 individuals who consecutively participated in the biennial National Health Screening Program during the 2009–2010, 2011–2012, and 2013–2014 were identified. Individuals with missing TyG index values (n = 1376), younger than 19 years (n = 1723), who died before 2015 (n = 8646), or with a TyG index standard deviation (SD) of 0 (n = 6), were excluded. Consequently, 3,094,062 participants with three available TyG index measurements were included in the final analysis. The variability of the TyG index was assessed using measurements obtained at three separate time points, corresponding to each biennial screening exposure period. Participants were categorized into four groups based on quartiles of the TyG index variability independent of the mean (VIM): lowest quartile (Q1, n = 773,763), second quartile (Q2, n = 772,810), third quartiles (Q3, n = 774,300), and highest quartiles (Q4, n = 773,189). Data source

This study used anonymized data from the Korea NHIS, a mandatory universal healthcare system covering approximately 97% of the South Korean population; the remaining 3% are enrolled in the medical aid program or are temporary or undocumented residents19. Managed and funded by the government, the NHIS database includes comprehensive records on sociodemographics, medical diagnoses and treatments (coded by the International Classification of Diseases, tenth revision [ICD-10]), and results from the biennial National Health Screening Program, offered free of charge to all adults aged 19 years or older under Article 52 of the National Health Insurance Act. These screenings provide anthropometric, laboratory, and health-related behavioral data. Data on causes and dates of deaths are obtained through linkage with Statistics Korea, using deidentified personal IDs.

Assessment of TyG index variability

For each participant, we calculated the TyG index at three examinations using the following formula9:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{TyG index }} = {\text{ log }}[{\text{fasting serum triglyceride }}\left( {{\text{mg}}/{\text{dL}}} \right){\text{fasting blood glucose }}\left( {{\text{FBG}},{\text{ mg}}/{\text{dL}}} \right)/{2}].$$\end{document}

The mean value of TyG index and its SD were then derived. Individuals with SD = 0 were excluded to allow logarithmic transformation. To obtain the scaling exponent β that removes any residual dependency on the mean, we fitted a sample-wide linear regression of log (SD) on log (mean). TyG index VIM was subsequently computed for each participant and expressed on a percentage scale18:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{VIM }} = { 1}00 \times {\text{SD}}/{\text{mean}}^{\beta }$$\end{document}where β is the slope from the log–log regression. Higher VIM values reflect greater visit-to-visit variability in TyG index. Unlike other variability indices that are highly correlated with the mean, VIM has its strength in being less affected by the mean value20.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. Information on mortality was linked to Statistics Korea, which recorded the dates and causes of death using ICD-10 codes. The secondary outcome was CV mortality, which included death due to ischemic heart disease (defined using ICD-10 codes I20–I25), heart failure (defined using ICD-10 code I50), and cerebrovascular disease (defined using ICD-10 codes I60–I69). Mortality events were recorded from 2015 until December 31, 2022; participants without events were followed up until this date.

Covariates

Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated using directly measured height and weight, with obesity defined as BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, defined by Korean Society for the Study of Obesity21. Waist circumference (WC) was measured at the midpoint between the lower border of the rib cage and the top of the iliac crest. Blood pressure was measured after a minimum of five minutes rest, and both systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) values were recorded. Following an overnight fast of at least eight hours, venous blood samples were collected to measure FBG, serum total cholesterol, triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, and creatinine levels. Low-income status was determined based on the eligibility for medical aid or belonging in the lowest quintile of income as calculated by NHIS premiums. Smoking behavior was categorized as either current smoker or non-smoker. Alcohol consumption was measured and classified as either alcohol consumer or non-alcohol consumer. Regular physical activity was defined as engaging in vigorous-intensity exercise on three or more days per week or moderate-intensity exercise on five or more days per week. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated based on the CKD-EPI equation22. Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score, based on 17 comorbidities defined using ICD-10 codes, was calculated for each participant and categorized into three groups: no comorbidity (score = 0), low comorbidity (score = 1) and high comorbidity (score ≥ 2)23. T2DM was defined as FBG ≥ 126 mg/dL or a diagnosis based on ICD-10 codes E11–E14 accompanied by ongoing antihyperglycemic treatment. HTN was identified as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, DBP ≥ 90 mmHg, or a diagnosis using ICD-10 codes I10–I13 or I15, in conjunction with antihypertensive therapy. Dyslipidemia was defined as total cholesterol ≥ 240 mg/dL or ICD-10 code E78 with current lipid-lowering medications.

Statistical analysis

Data for categorical variables are presented as numbers (%) and data for continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median [25th, 75th] based on the results of normality test. The Chi-square test was used for categorical variables to analyze differences among groups, while analysis of variance or Kruskal–Wallis test was used for continuous variables.

Cox proportional hazard regression analysis was performed to estimated hazard ratio (HR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) for all-cause mortality and CV mortality. TyG index VIM was analyzed as both a categorical variable (quartiles, with Q1 as reference) and a continuous variable (per 1-SD increment). Adjusted survival curves were drawn to compare cumulative incidence rates of all-cause and CV mortality among TyG index VIM quartile groups. A restricted cubic spline curve with four knots was also plotted to assess the dose–response association of TyG index VIM and risk of all-cause mortality. Both the adjusted survival curves and the spline curve were adjusted for age, sex, and baseline TyG index. Adjustments were made in multivariable models. In Model 1, adjustments were made for age, sex, and baseline TyG index. In Model 2, adjustments were made for variables in Model 1 plus low income, smoking status, alcohol use status, and physical activity. In Model 3, adjustments were made for Charlson Comorbidity Index, HTN, and dyslipidemia in addition to all the other variables in former models. We conducted a sensitivity analysis using three alternative metrics of TyG index variability: SD, coefficient of variation, and average real variability (ARV). Subgroup analysis was conducted for age (< 50, 50–69, ≥ 70 years), sex, obesity, T2DM, HTN, and dyslipidemia.

The net reclassification index (NRI) analysis was used to quantify improvement in risk classification for all-cause mortality when TyG index VIM was added to the prediction model of baseline TyG index24. The predictive values of baseline TyG index and TyG index VIM were also evaluated using Harrell’s concordance index, Heagerty’s integrated area under the curve (iAUC) and Heagerty’s AUC at 9.1 years, the median follow-up time25–28. The 95% confidence interval of NRI, iAUC, and AUC were calculated using the bootstrapping method.

All statistical analyses were carried out using SAS Enterprise Guide (version 7.1; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R software (version 4.0.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was set to two-sided P value of < 0.05.

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The Institutional Review Board of Nowon Eulji Medical Center approved the study (approval no. EMCS 2023-12-018). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the Institutional Review Board of Nowon Eulji Medical Center waived the need of obtaining informed consent.

TyG index VIM and the risk of all-cause mortality

During a median (25th–75th) event accrual period of 9.1 (8.5–9.5) years, a total of 137,339 deaths occurred, corresponding to 4.4% of the study population. Figure 2 illustrates an adjusted survival curve depicting the survival probability across the different TyG index VIM quartiles. As TyG index VIM quartile increases, there is a stepwise decrease in survival probability. Notably, individuals in Q4 demonstrated the lowest survival probability, while those in Q1 showed the highest (P < 0.001). Table 2 shows the results of the Cox proportional hazard regression examining the association between TyG index VIM and risk of all-cause mortality. Across all models, individuals in Q4 showed the highest risks of all-cause mortality. In the unadjusted model, the HR (95% CI) was 1.04 (1.02–1.05) for Q4, whereas the HR (95% CI) was 0.98 (0.97–1.00) for Q3, indicating a significantly lower risk. In Model 1, the HRs increased across quartiles, with HRs (95% CI) of 1.03 (1.01–1.04) for Q2, 1.04 (1.02–1.06) for Q3, and 1.16 (1.14–1.18) for Q4. Similar trends were observed in Model 2 and Model 3. In Model 2, the HRs (95% CI) were 1.04 (1.02–1.06), 1.06 (1.03–1.08), and 1.19 (1.17–1.22) for Q2, Q3, and Q4 respectively. In Model 3, the HRs (95% CI) were 1.04 (1.01–1.06) for Q2, 1.05 (1.03–1.07) for Q3 and 1.18 (1.16–1.21) for Q4. Supplementary Table S1 shows the results of the Cox proportional hazard regression analysis treating TyG index VIM as a continuous variable. The HR (95% CI) of all-cause mortality was 1.07 (1.07–1.08) per 1-SD increase in TyG index VIM after adjusting for variables in Model 3. Discussion

This nationwide cohort study evaluated the association between visit-to-visit variability in the TyG index and mortality in a large, population-based dataset. Individuals with higher TyG index VIM exhibited a stepwise increase in both all-cause and CV deaths. Our results corroborate earlier evidence linking IR markers to adverse outcomes while extending current knowledge by showing that dynamic fluctuations—rather than static levels—reflect metabolic instability and disease progression with important implications for long-term prognosis. Firm evidence supports the TyG index as a proxy for IR and a predictor of CVD and related mortality8,13,29. Moreover, recent studies have highlighted the importance of observing longitudinal changes in TyG index. In a large Korean cohort, an increasing TyG trajectory over four years conferred the highest risks of all-cause and CV mortality15. Another study found significant associations between increasing TyG index trajectory group and risk of stroke in patients with HTN30. These trajectory-based studies offer valuable insights regarding the value of longitudinal patterns of IR and consequent CV outcomes. However, this approach clusters individuals into groups with similar trajectories and are not able to determine individual changes in TyG index. For instance, individuals grouped in the same trajectory group may in fact have very distinct TyG index patterns31. Our study addresses these concerns by employing the variability of TyG index to assess individual fluctuations in IR.

Two Chinese cohorts have examined TyG variability and incident CVD. A study by Chen et al.32 reported that higher TyG index variability predicted incident CVD in 27,600 Chinese adults aged ≥ 60 years over 6.2 years of follow-up period, even among individuals without T2DM or obesity. Similarly, Li et al.33 reported a significantly higher CVD risk with greater TyG variability in a separate cohort of 49,579 adults followed for a median of 9.0 years. Building on these observations, our study extends the evidence by encompassing a broader age spectrum and a more heterogeneous population. By quantifying visit-to-visit fluctuations in the TyG index, we assessed their impact on long-term all-cause and CV mortality, thereby refining risk stratification and informing preventive strategies. We also found that incorporating TyG index VIM as a continuous variable into a model with the baseline TyG index led to a statistically significant improvement in risk reclassification for all-cause mortality (NRI = 0.072; 95% CI, 0.021–0.123). This highlights the importance of considering TyG variability, in addition to a single baseline measurement, for more effective patient risk stratification (Supplementary Table S3).

Several explanations rooted in biological processes and lifestyle factors have been proposed to account for the link between IR variability and increased mortality, although the precise mechanisms remain unclear. First, dynamic shifts in glucose and triglyceride levels can trigger a cascade of adverse physiological processes. Transient spikes in glucose and lipids are known to promote oxidative stress, vascular inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction, all of which are mechanisms in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis and CVD34–36. Moreover, metabolic instability may lead to increased atherosclerotic plaques, adding to the risk of subsequent adverse CV events3. Collectively, these findings suggest that not only persistently high IR but also variability in IR may be a clinically important indicator of metabolic disarray with prognostic significance for long-term outcomes. Second, unhealthy lifestyle behavior may be a possible underlying factor in the association between higher TyG index variability and mortality. For example, a high carbohydrate diet has been known to increase glycemic variability37, which in turn heightens the risk of CVD16. Triglyceride variability, which may be increased during episodic alcohol consumption38, also shows an association with the incidence of CV outcomes and mortality39. These unhealthy lifestyle behaviors effectively lead to significant fluctuations in glucose and triglyceride levels, thereby increasing variability of TyG index. Thus, in clinical practice, monitoring TyG index variability could serve as a marker to identify individuals with poor lifestyle choices that predispose them to increased adverse outcomes.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to utilize TyG index variability to assess its association with risk of mortality in a nationwide, representative cohort. The analysis accounted for a wide range of well-known risk factors for CV mortality40. Despite the strengths, several limitations should be considered. First, a causal relationship between variability of TyG index and mortality could not be established owing to the observational nature of this study. Future studies such as randomized controlled trials or mendelian randomization studies should be conducted. Second, we assumed that each participant’s TyG index variability remained consistent throughout the entire follow-up period. Changes in lifestyle, medication use, or underlying metabolic status could have altered TyG index levels during the event-accrual period. Therefore, the observed associations may not fully reflect the dynamic nature of TyG index variability over time. Lastly, this study only utilized data from Korean participants, limiting the generalizability of results and requires external validation to other ethnicities.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that higher variability in TyG index is associated with increased risk of all-cause mortality and CV mortality. These results highlight the potential value of serial monitoring of changes in the TyG index, which could contribute to reducing mortality risk through early detection and intervention in high-risk individuals.
