Real-space diffusion theory from quantum mechanics using analytic continuation
Authors: Leonardo Di G Sigalotti, Otto Rendón, José-Rubén Luévano
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe show that a physical correspondence between Brownian motion and quantum mechanics can be established by formal analytic continuation if Wick rotation (t→it) is replaced by the mirror symmetric, complex conjugate time transformation t→±it. Invariance of the square modulus of the wave function under this transformation reveals a tight connection between Born's interpretation of the probability density and the proper time t2=(−it)(+it), as being both the result of a time symmetry breaking in the informational content of the microscopic (quantum) world, which takes place as we move to a macroscopic level. We find that under the transformation t→±it, the Schrödinger equation and its complex conjugate conforms with a stochastic-mechanical model of the Brownian motion of a particle. From the present analysis, Nelson's osmotic velocity arises naturally and the quantum phase function admits a classical interpretation in terms of a continuous (scalar) potential field that influences the motion of the particle. The maximum phase variation defines the direction of the osmotic and current velocity. Whereas the paper focuses on the correlation between quantum mechanics and Ficks's second law for a constant diffusion coefficient, a discussion is also provided on the possibility of a quantum mechanical formulation for anomalous diffusion, where the diffusion coefficient is a function of space and/or time.Keywords: Quantum mechanics, Analytic continuation, Brownian motion, Stochastic processes, Markov processes

Introduction

It is a well-known fact that using Born's statistical interpretation of the square modulus of the wave function, i.e., |Ψ|2=Ψ⋆Ψ, as the probability density, where the superscript ⋆ denotes complex conjugation, the time-dependent Schrödinger equation can be rewritten in a form that looks exactly as the continuity equation of fluid dynamics for the time evolution of the probability density [1], [2]. If, on the other hand, we adopt Bohm's polar form of the wave function [3]

where the amplitude R and the phase S are both real functions of space and time, i=−1 is the imaginary unit and ħ is the Planck constant divided by 2π, replace it into the time-dependent Schrödinger equation, and separate the real from the imaginary part, we end up with two (coupled) real equations for R and S. One equation looks remarkably similar to the Hamilton-Jacobi equation of classical mechanics if S stands for Hamilton's principal function. The other equation turns out to be the continuity equation for the probability density ρ=R2, with the Hamilton-Jacobi (current) velocity v=∇S/m.

Quantum hydrodynamics formulations

In the late 1926 Madelung [4] showed that the Schrödinger equation can be written in a form similar to the continuity and Euler's equations of hydrodynamics, which are known in the literature as the equations of quantum hydrodynamics. Madelung derived these equations by replacing the wave function given by Eq. (1) into the time-dependent Schrödinger equation. This was interpreted as a Madelung fluid whose movement is determined by the Schrödinger equation with density ρ and current (local) velocity v. A particle suspended in the fluid will move with the local velocity accompanied by some kind of random fluctuations. In contrast to Bohmian mechanics, Madelung's quantum hydrodynamics describes only the averaged statistical characteristics of the fluid but not the particles trajectories.

The similarities between quantum mechanics and Brownian motion are not surprising and have also been noted by Fürth [5], Wiener and Siegel [6], Comisar [7], and Bourret [8] among other authors. In a seminal paper, Nelson [9] suggested a stochastic-mechanical model based on the assumption that the Brownian motion of a free particle of mass m is described by a Markov-Wiener process with diffusion coefficient ħ/2m, in which the Schrödinger equation is derived as a pasting of Newton's second law and the Fokker-Planck equations for a Brownian diffusion process. Based on Nelson's stochastic mechanics, McClendon [10] developed a real-space diffusion theory of multiparticle quantum systems, yielding a three-dimensional particle trajectory picture for density-functional theory. Since the Fokker-Planck equation can be derived from a Markov process, as was pointed out by Chandrasekhar [11], Collins [12] demonstrated that a direct link between a Markov process and quantum mechanics can be established without making recourse to concepts of Newtonian mechanics such as the Langevin equation.

Madelung's hydrodynamical interpretation of quantum mechanics was revisited by Sanz et al. [13] in a study of particle diffraction using the Bohmian formalism of quantum trajectories. In a recent paper, by taking the gradient of the resulting Hamilton-Jacobi equation, Agrawal [14] derived a differential equation for the probability density that can be associated with a Markov diffusion process, which locally looks like a Brownian motion with drift equal to v+(ħ2/m)(∇R/R) and variance parameter σ2=ħ/m. The implications of Schrödinger's equation as a diffusion equation were revisited by Mita [15], who considered these two equations as the basic equations of quantum mechanics and interpreted the probability density as a probability fluid. The relation between quantum and classical models of diffusion was also recently reviewed by Morro [16], who emphasized that a correct model of diffusion must be based on the dynamics (i.e., the equations of continuity and motion) of a mixture, as diffusion is inherently related to the motion of mixture constituents. Although it is clear that in the limit of very small ħ classical mechanics is contained into Schrödinger's wave mechanics, the manner in which classical behavior emerges from quantum mechanics and vice versa has not yet been completely elucidated and is still an open problem.

An alternative formulation of the diffusion effect in quantum hydrodynamics was recently provided by Bonilla-Licea et al. [17], based on an axiom postulated by Heisenberg who stated that kinematic quantities in quantum mechanics, such as the velocity, must be treated as complex quantities. In their formulation, the velocity was expressed as v+D(∇ρ/ρ), where v is identical to Madelung's current velocity, while the second term, which is multiplied by an imaginary diffusion coefficient D=iħ/2m, is a dispersive velocity. The real form of the continuity equation is recovered by adding an imaginary diffusion current, −D∇ρ, to compensate the contribution of the imaginary dispersive velocity. This procedure gives rise to the so-called Smoluchowski equation, which has the form of an irreversible Fokker-Planck-type equation. However, this differs from Nelson's [9] formulation which works with a real diffusion coefficient.

On the other hand, Nottale [18], [19] developed the theory of scale relativity, where a generalization of Einstein's principle of relativity to scale transformations is proposed in a fractal, non-differentiable space-time. Within the framework of this theory, the laws of physics are scale-covariant, that is, they maintain their form under resolution transformations. Using this scale-covariance, Nottale [19] developed a formal derivation of the Schrödinger equation from the geodesics of fractal space-time, employing classical variational principles. More recently, Lindgren and Liukkonen [20] advanced the idea that the imaginary structure of quantum mechanics can be understood in terms of the relativistic invariance and the stochastic optimization on spacetimes. In particular, they argued that diffusion has to be considered not only in three-space dimensions but also in the time dimension and that the imaginary nature of quantum mechanics comes from the structure of the Minkowski metric.

Wick rotation

Moreover, it has long been noticed that Wick rotation [21], a procedure in which the proper time t is replaced by the pure imaginary time it (i=−1), converts the Schrödinger equation into a classical diffusion equation in a straightforward manner [22]. However, as was argued by Ord [23], the nature of formal analytic continuation (t→it), which links both equations, prevents any simple interpretation of quantum mechanics in terms of Brownian paths. In addition, unlike the concentration of particles in the diffusion equation, or the temperature in the heat equation, the wave function Ψ is not physically observable and so it does not correspond to any known physical entity. These difficulties led Ord [23] to start off with a symmetric binary random walk on a lattice as a model of Brownian motion, finding that the diffusion equation approximately describes the probability density, while the Schrödinger equation approximately describes the correlations in the particle paths. He claimed that this correspondence unites the two equations as being the result of different projections of the same classical probabilistic system. The correlation between the diffusion equation and the Schrödinger equation was further investigated by Okino [24], who derived the Schrödinger equation from the classical diffusion equation for the collective motion of microparticles by applying the transformation t→it, which was justified by the impossibility of identifying between microparticles in a collision problem.

Aim and plan of the paper

In this paper we examine the implications of formal analytic continuation, using the generalization of Wick rotation proposed by El Naschie [22], [25], where the imaginary time it is replaced by a mirror symmetric, complex conjugate time ±it. The reason for such an extension lies on the realization that the proper time t is uniquely defined by the intersection set of a forward running time set (+it) and a backward running time set (−it) so that t2=(−it)(+it) in perfect analogy with Born interpretation of the probability density |Ψ|2=Ψ⋆Ψ. In addition, since imaginary numbers always occur in pairs, as a complex conjugate, we must then simultaneously always have ±it. The irreversibility of t, which is associated with an increase of entropy and is related to diffusional processes, implies the reversibility of ±it, which is intrinsically related to the quantum unitary evolution. Unlike Wick rotation, this new procedure provides a powerful conceptual interpretation of the transition from quantum to classical mechanics in terms of a dimensional symmetry breaking as we pass from the microscopic (quantum) space-time, where the two symmetric times ±it coexist, to the macroscopic (4-dimensional) space-time, where t is our one-directional, real time.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we transform the wave function under t→±it, while in Section 3 the corresponding diffusion-like equations are derived from the Schrödinger equation and its complex conjugate. Section 4 shows how these diffusion equations can be combined to derive three coupled differential equations for the amplitude and for the real and imaginary parts of the phase function, leading to modified quantum hydrodynamics equations. Section 5 shows that under stationary conditions these new equations predict the correct form of the mean energy of a free particle. Finally, Section 6 contains a discussion on the possibility of a quantum mechanical formulation for heterogeneous and time-dependent diffusivities and Section 7 summarizes the main conclusions.

In their work, however, the diffusion coefficient is imaginary and given by the equivalence D=iħ/2m [5], as may be deduced directly from the Schrödinger equations (13) and (14). Other formulations as, for example, that advanced recently by Bonilla-Licea et al. [17], who assumed a complex velocity, derived quantum hydrodynamics equations with an entirely imaginary diffusion current/velocity. In contrast, the transformation t→±it has the effect of converting the imaginary diffusion coefficient into a real one (D=ħ/2m).

Anomalous diffusion

It is clear from the various existing quantum formulations that Schrödinger equation is connected with Einstein diffusion theory on the basis of Brownian motion, where by Brownian motion we mean the random motion that particles suspended in a fluid experience due to their frequent collisions with other particles. In particular, this process can be modeled by assuming that the particles move chaotically and independently of their past history. It is notorious that for a free particle (or mixture of particles) Schrödinger equation reduces to a regular diffusion equation (Fick's second law) with a constant diffusion coefficient. According to Agrawal [14], the emerging diffusion process can be represented by the stochastic differential equation

which can be thought as a process that locally looks like a Brownian motion with drift equal to v⋅v+u⋅u and variance parameter ħ/m. The second term on the right-hand side of Eq. In this process the drift is equal to the sum of the current and osmotic (dispersive) velocity.

However, very recent work by Wang et al. [31] and Liang et al. [32] has shown that if the diffusion coefficient is either space- or time-dependent the diffusion process is completely different from the standard Brownian motion. In particular, they found that nonlinear clocks in time and/or space affect the properties of stochastic processes and generate anomalous diffusion. Their analysis of time- and space-scaled fractional Brownian motion and based on fractional Brownian motion running with nonlinear time and space clocks has led to a variety of different non-Brownian diffusion processes such as nonergodic, non-Gaussian and aging diffusion. Therefore, the following questions arise: Is it possible to derive a formal quantum mechanical formulation for a spatially- and/or temporally-dependent diffusivity? And if so, what should be changed in the existing standard formulations?

In order to provide a possible answer to the above questions, we consider a quantum model based on the two evolution processes contained in quantum mechanics, namely a deterministic evolution solving the Schrödinger equation and a non-deterministic evolution associated with the collapse of the wave function in a measurement process. As an example, let us consider the problem of measuring the position x∈R3 of a free microparticle of mass m with resolution ΔVx, moving in the three-dimensional physical space R3, where ΔVx is a (spherical) volume around the point x. The quantum trajectory of the particle can be defined statistically by means of the following measurement protocol [33]: (a) Successive measurements of the particle position with resolution ΔVx are performed using an isotropic measurement instrument so that the uncertainty of the measured observable (i.e., the particle position) does not increase from its measured value during the subsequent evolution (nondemolition measurements) and (b) the succession of position measurements is performed in time intervals Δt so that the particle trajectory is defined by straight lines joining consecutive pairs of measured positions, i.e., xn and xn+1, for n=0,1,…N. The time is then defined at constant intervals t0, t1=t0+Δt, t2=t1+Δt,...,tn=tn+1+Δt. However, the development of a quantum formulation for anomalous diffusion is beyond the scope of the present work, and will be considered in future investigations.

Concluding remarks

It has long been noticed that Wick rotation (t→it) converts the deterministic, Schrödinger equation of wave mechanics into a non-deterministic, diffusion equation with real diffusion coefficient (D=ħ/2m). However, the nature of this transformation prevents any simple interpretation of quantum mechanics in terms of Brownian motion. In this paper, we have shown that if Wick rotation is replaced by a mirror symmetric, complex time transformation t→±it, it is then possible to provide a physical interpretation of the correspondence between quantum mechanics and Brownian paths in terms of a dimensional symmetry breaking in the informational content of the microscopic (quantum) space-time, which takes place as we move towards a macroscopic level, where t=(−it)(+it) is the only time that we can measure.

We find that under t→±it the square modulus of the wave function |Ψ|2=Ψ⋆Ψ remains invariant. This reveals that Born's interpretation of the probability density and the proper time t, defined by the intersection of −it and +it [22], have both the same origin. Both the Schrödinger equation and its complex conjugate can be combined into a single evolution equation for |Ψ|2, which under t→±it turns out to look exactly as the continuity equation of fluid dynamics. For Schrödinger waves of the form Ψ=Rexp⁡(S/ħ), where R is a real function and S=SR+iSI is a complex function, the Schrödinger equation and its complex conjugate split up into four diffusion-like equations, which have the form of Fick's second law. The osmotic velocity, u, follows from the real part of the forward drift [10] applied to the transformed wave functions Ψ±. In this sense the present formulation conforms with Nelson's [9] stochastic model for the Brownian motion of a particle. In terms of the amplitude, R, and phase, S=SR+iSI, of the transformed wave functions, the diffusion equations can be combined to result in a set of three coupled differential equations, namely a continuity equation for the probability density, a modified Euler's equation for the current velocity, v, and a time-dependent differential equation for the osmotic velocity.

The present formulation differs from Nelson's [9] stochastic mechanics in that the continuity equation is essentially derived from a time-dependent, differential equation for the amplitude of the wave function in much the way as in Madelung's [4] formulation of quantum hydrodynamics. The same is true for more recent formulations reported by Agrawal [14], Mita [15], Morro [16] and Bonilla-Licea et al. [17]. In spite of differences with Nelson's [9] stochastic mechanics, the differential equation for the time rate of the osmotic velocity as derived by the present formulation is identical to the one obtained by Nelson for the Markov process of a particle in an external field. As a final remark, the differential equation for the time rate of the current velocity is remarkably similar in form to the one reported by Mita [15] and differs from Nelson's [9] and Bonilla-Licea et al.'s [17] for a source term of the form (ħ/2m)∇(∇⋅u), where ∇⋅u expresses the variation of the diffusion fluctuations at different points in space.

In the light of the present results, the phase function admits a classical interpretation in terms of a continuous scalar field that influences the motion of the particle, while the square of the quantum wave amplitude maintains its statistical interpretation as the probability distribution of the particle positions. Although the main focus of the present work is the existing correlation between quantum mechanics and Fick's second law with a constant diffusion coefficient, it is possible to develop a quantum formulation for heterogeneous and time-dependent diffusivities in the sense derived by Wang et al. [31] and Liang et al. [32] for low-dimensional disordered systems and mesoscopic systems with randomly distributed impurities [34], [35]. However, the development of such quantum formulations will be investigated in a future work in this line of research.
