Assessing the spatial risk of wild birds in avian influenza transmission using global risk score
Authors: Hong-Dar Isaac Wu, Day-Yu Chao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Accumulated evidence supports the critical role of migratory wild birds in highly pathogenic avian influenza virus (HPAIV) spread and evolution. An effective surveillance strategy to study HPAIV dispersal in wild birds and identify critical interfaces between wild birds and poultry on the landscape for potential interspecies transmission and virus evolution will be essential. This study integrates conditional likelihood with epidemiological research designs to investigate the risk of poultry farm outbreaks due to the introduction of HPAIV by migratory birds from the Taiwan citizen scientist dataset. The prediction accuracy of poultry farm outbreaks was evaluated by employing the global risk score (GRS) of multiple species of wild birds under different significance level. Although 11 wild bird species demonstrated significant associations (P < 0.05) with HPAIV poultry farm outbreaks, the bird species with P < 0.1 yielded the optimal area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. Without prior adjustment of environmental factors, the posterior adjusted likelihood results suggested that the agricultural wetlands yield notably strong positive odds ratio estimates for all species. The GRS under adjustment of environmental factors, a linear combination of the 27 migratory species and 27 resident species, was 0.320, yielding a sensitivity of 0.81 and specificity of 0.77. The framework of GRS developed in this study can be applied to different countries for enhanced surveillance of HPAIV.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17678-8.

Introduction

Highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI), a zoonotic infectious disease, has been considered a severe threat to public health1. Periodically, human infections associated with HPAI virus (HPAIV) have been detected, in particular, by the Eurasian (goose/Guangdong/1996 [Gs/Gd]) lineage2,3. With the continued evolution and circulation of HPAIV, Gs/Gd lineage has been involved in multiple waves of global influenza epizootic outbreaks with genetically distinct virus clades and subclades4. In contrast to previous clades of Gs/Gd lineage, clade 2.3.4.4 showed more persistent spillovers to local domestic poultry, impacting the poultry farming industry with tremendous economic losses5,6. The emergence and transmission of clade 2.3.4.4 continue to provide virological, epidemiological, and ecological evidence in support of the critical role of migratory wild birds in HPAIV spread and evolution7–9. Therefore, developing a wild-bird risk score will assist in outbreak prediction as well as implementing targeted surveillance.

The challenge of studying HPAIV dispersal in wild birds is the lack of HPAIV prevalence and movement data10,11. The longitudinal studies documented the HPAIV prevalence in wild bird populations have been established in disparate geographical locations with related avian host ecology; however, such data cannot be easily generalized globally due to the constrain, mainly in low pathogenic influenza viruses in restricted locations such as Alaska12. On the contrary, a global systemic surveillance of HAPI will be ideal but not feasible due to resource constraints. Therefore, a research framework to design effective surveillance strategies to study HPAIV dispersal in wild birds and identify critical interfaces between wild birds and poultry across the landscape for potential interspecies transmission and virus evolution will be essential13.

Since many different wild bird species may be involved in HPAIV transmission, with varying levels of interaction with each other and poultry premises, citizen science initiatives, such as eBird, are capable of enhancing our understanding of species abundance and distributions without relying on any field- or laboratory-led surveillance to identify potential wild bird hosts, and can be rapidly deployed as a target surveillance system against pathogens with wild reservoirs of infection in areas with limited wild host testing capacity14. As the biases usually come along from semi-structured data collection procedures in collecting citizen science data, assessing species-level drivers of virus incursions into poultry flocks can be challenging, which requires advances in novel epidemiological approaches and analytical computation15–17.

Over the past ten years, bird flu outbreaks have occurred in Taiwan almost every year18,19. During this period, avian influenza virus can often be detected in the carcasses of dead migratory birds20,21. In this study, we proposed an analytical framework utilizing the Taiwan eBird dataset by integrating conditional likelihood with epidemiological research design, matching, and counter-matching, for estimation of the significance level of migratory bird species introducing HPAI into the poultry farms. The prediction accuracy of poultry farm outbreaks was evaluated by employing the global risk score (GRS) of multiple species of wild birds under different significance levels.

Materials and methods

The main island of Taiwan was divided into 4,762 squares, each measuring 3 km by 3 km, to be consistent with our previous publication14,19 (as the example shown in Fig. 2). The time frame of this study was between January 2015 and December 2017, and the reason for choosing this time frame is due to the major HPAI poultry farm outbreaks in Taiwan since 2003, caused by the emergence of clade 2.3.4.4. The timeline is consistent with the poultry farm outbreaks globally. Based on the surveillance system in Taiwan, the outbreaks continued but became endemic with fewer outbreaks of poultry farms reported to the Bureau of Animal and Plant Health Inspection and Quarantine (BAPHIQ), Taiwan, after 201822. Epidemiological design and statistical analysis

When analyzing spatiotemporal data with HPAIV as the outcome variable, it typically entails a substantial number of explanatory variables, the number of wild bird species, which were included as covariates. To estimate the relationship between HPAIV outbreaks and the presence of each bird species, this study introduces a comprehensive analytical framework that integrates epidemiological matched-pair design, statistical conditional likelihood, and the concept of adjustment into a single estimation structure. Let’s consider a scenario with K bird species and N 3 km-by-3 km grids. Among these N grids, Q of them are case grids, signifying that each of these Q grids contains at least one HPAIV outbreak on poultry farms. The remaining \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\text{N}-\text{Q}$$\end{document} grids are classified as control grids, where no outbreaks occurred during the study period. It’s important to note that in our analysis, geographical grids without bird-sighting records have been excluded due to a lack of information.

Algorithm for making conditional likelihood inference

The following steps were used to estimate the distribution for each bird species with the flowchart shown in Fig. 1.

ROC refers to the receiver operating characteristic curve.

A flowchart of the research framework. A computational procedure is iteratively implemented for all selected different bird species. The propensity score (PS) matching is implemented by 1:m matching with m = 3 and 5; k refers to the k-th selected bird species, with k ranging from 1 to K = 68. ROC refers to the receiver operating characteristic curve.

Step 1. For bird species (k) ranging from 1 to K, utilize the ZIP model to determine the association of each species (k), whether it is present or absent, based on the presence of all other species25. This is accomplished through the application of both Model (A1) and Model (A2), accounting for spatial correlation with details described in Appendix 1. In this step, Model (A3) elucidates the specific “propensity score” (PS) for each species (k). In brief, formula (A1) represents a logistic regression that accounts for spatial autocorrelation in the data structure, estimating the probability of a species being a ‘structural zero.’ Model (A2), using the same covariates as (A1), models the Poisson distribution to estimate the probability of a ‘sampling zero,’ reflecting the chance of non-observation. Finally, model (A3), which serves as the basis for matching, is used to derive the propensity score. During this step, the PSs are first estimated without adjustment for environmental factors, and then re-estimated with such adjustment. Figure 1 provides the flowchart of the estimation procedure, where adjustment for environmental factors is also possible at the step of constructing the conditional likelihood.

Step 2. A grid-specific propensity score (PS) is determined for the presence of species k for each case grid in Step 1. Subsequently, a subset of m control grids is selected from the nd available grids. These m control grids are chosen based on matching criteria in their PS values using the “nearest neighbor” score; or through counter-matching, which involves inverse sampling-probability weightings.

Step 3. Create a conditional likelihood function when the sole covariate is the presence-absence status, represented as a binary dummy variable, for species k. During this step, the parameter estimate for species k is derived through conditional likelihood inference.

In step 1, the practice of dichotomizing all risk variables is utilized to mitigate bias, albeit at the cost of some statistical efficiency. Moving to step 2, we apply the matching and counter-matching concept to rigorously control all other ‘risk factors’ collectively. Prior to reaching step 2, a variable selection procedure may be implemented when the number of adjustment variables is substantial. This includes techniques such as the ‘greedy orthogonal algorithm‘26 and the utilization of penalty-based information criteria27. Furthermore, in step 3, during conditional likelihood estimation, there is the option to treat the matching score as a risk factor or simply allow it to be matched out28–30.

However, it’s possible to perform adjustment after establishing the PSM without adjustment by incorporating the environmental variables into the conditional likelihood. In this particular situation, known as ‘posterior adjustment,’ Eqs. (1) and (2) incorporate the covariate vector x, which comprises both the single bird-species variable and the environmental variables.

Constructing a global risk score (GRS) of multiple species of wild birds

According to the conditional likelihood analysis, several significant bird species have been identified. However, two main issues arise. Firstly, the significance determinations rely on a statistical convention, specifically employing a p-value of 0.05 as the threshold for significance. Consequently, altering this threshold would lead to changes in the number or subset of significant variables, namely bird species. Secondly, while many individual bird species may not exhibit significance in separate analyses, we intend to estimate the cumulative impact of these birds as a collective entity on the outcomes in question. It would be overly simplistic to model “significance” solely based on a summary index, such as the total count of birds or a measure of diversity. This prompted us to develop a GRS of multiple species of wild birds as an indicator of the likelihood of an avian influenza outbreak within a geographical area. Using the conditional likelihood approach, various threshold configurations for the p-value, acting as a variable-selection criterion, yield different subsets of explanatory variables (X). Subsequently, a risk score is formulated by selecting a linear combination of Xs (birds) with the highest predictive capability through conventional logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. To expand the list of high-risk bird species and derive the corresponding risk score, we undertook several steps detailed in the second part of the Appendix 1.

Next, 68 distinct wild bird species were individually matched as described in Step 2 of the Method section to individually estimate their associations with HPAI outbreaks on poultry farms, with or without adjustment of environmental factors. Among 68 species analyzed, 11 demonstrated significant associations with HPAIV poultry farm outbreaks. These significant associations were identified in both conventional matching and counter-matching, for the scenarios of ‘without adjustment’ (Table 1) and ‘with prior adjustment’ (Table 2) for environmental factors. In both tables, columns 1 and 2 display the outcomes of 1:3 and 1:5 conventional matching, while columns 3 and 4 depict the results of counter-matching. Compared to the bootstrapped McNemar statistic as shown in column 5, as published by Wu et al.14two more wild bird species were identified with significant association with HPAIV poultry farm outbreaks, including Caspian tern, and peregrine falcon.

Among 11 wild bird species shown in Table 1, the black-headed gull exhibits highly significant odds ratios (OR) ranging from 3.93 to 4.86 for the two matching designs. In contrast, with posterior adjustment as reported in the last column of Table 3, the OR is 4.48 (95% confidence interval (CI):1.23–16.32). It’s worth noting that in the case of posterior adjustment, we do not include environmental factors during the propensity score matching stage. These OR estimates are closely aligned, indicating coherent results. Another highly significant species to note is the long-toed stint. Its odds ratio estimates range from 1.63 to 2.48 for the two matching scenarios, both with and without prior adjustment. Additionally, when posterior adjustment is employed, the odds ratio remains significant, which equals 1.67 (95% CI: 1.03–2.70) (Table 3).

Discussions

HPAIV continues to evolve through re-assortment with low-pathogenic avian influenza virus (LPAIV) in wild and domestic birds during global dissemination. To reveal the mechanism how such adaptation across the domestic and bird-wild bird interface through HPAI evolution, a quantitative evaluation of the risk for AIV exchange at the interface would enable the strategic allocation of limited resources for spatial targeting surveillance for AIV in wild birds and poultry. In this study, we developed a GRS to assess the spatial risk of different wild bird species contributing the poultry farm outbreaks. Previously, Wu et al. proposed an analysis framework based on propensity score matching with the propensity score (of each suspected bird species in each geographical grid) estimated through a zero-inflated Poisson regression model and then constructed a matched-pair McNemar \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\upchi\:}}_{1}^{2}$$\end{document} statistic with the aid of bootstrap resampling to produce an estimate of the significance level of migratory bird species introducing HPAI into the poultry farms14. To improve the computational efficiency, we integrate conditional likelihood with epidemiological research design, matching, and counter-matching, to estimate the risk of individual wild bird species associated with the HPAIV poultry farm outbreaks. Compared to our previous approach using McNemar with bootstrapping statistics, conditional likelihood approaches greatly reduce the computational time with similar significance results. Finally, the GRS of the wild bird species contributing the poultry farm outbreaks were constructed based on the selection of bird species with the significance level under 0.1 and the prediction accuracy was validated with AUCs, which were greater than 80% after adjustment for environmental factors when both migratory and residential bird species were considered or only migratory bird species. The frameworks of GRS development can be customized based on the observational wild bird species distribution in different countries. The optimal GRS cutoff could be used to enhance the current AIV surveillance for continued monitoring of associations between wild hosts and detected outbreaks in poultry premises as an early-warning sign of a possible virus host range expansion into a new wild species that is not currently subject to surveillance32,33.

To quantify the risk for AIV exchange at the interface of domestic poultry and wild birds, we previously introduced the propensity score matching method and employed bootstrap resampling to generate multiple iterations of McNemar \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\upchi\:}}_{1}^{2}$$\end{document} values, as described in a previous study14. However, such an approach was unable to incorporate more variables into the model build-up although propensity scores can be calculated either with or without the inclusion of environmental factors. When no adjustment is made, the resulting estimate is referred to as the ‘naive estimate.’ In this study, we refrain from presenting an extensive list detailing the “effects” of all significant variables (bird species) and a set of potential environmental and terrestrial factors. Instead, we incorporate all the variables deemed essential for adjustment by estimating a propensity score that encompasses all the necessary adjustment variables. Then the conditional likelihood techniques were applied based on traditional matching or counter-matching configurations to estimate the effects of individual bird species on HPAIV poultry farm outbreaks considering prior and posterior adjustment. To effectively synthesize the information, we utilized a comprehensive approach that integrated epidemiological 1:m matching, and counter-matching, with flexibility in selecting “m” (e.g., m = 1, 3, and 5). This method was implemented to ensure the consistency and comparability of results obtained through various conditional likelihood inferences, promoting an alignment between them. Interestingly, two additional wild bird species were found statistically significantly associated with HPAIV poultry farm outbreaks (P-value < 0.05 under two-tail test), including Caspian tern, and peregrine falcon, which were not identified using McNemar with bootstrapping statistics. Regarding posterior adjustments, we will not combine them with prior adjustments during the likelihood construction. In other words, if prior adjustments have been applied (as seen in Table 2), no additional posterior adjustments will be conducted. As shown in Tables 3 and 11 bird species previously identified as having significant relevance, it is evident that, in most cases, farmland (FL) and agricultural wetlands (AW) are more likely to be linked with HPAIV poultry farm outbreaks following posterior adjustment. This suggests that these two types of land cover contribute positively to the occurrence of avian influenza outbreaks19,34,35. Moreover, it underscores the potential to simultaneously account for the cumulative effects of the same land cover factors, which are advantageous for the survival and foraging of multiple bird species. Therefore, such conditional likelihood techniques can be used to portray the influences of these diverse bird species at the interface of domestic and wild birds taking into account different ecological variables, including bird behaviors or virus genotypes.

At the stage of model building for propensity score estimation, our approach is for logistic regression with all variables being dichotomized into binary categories without considering the abundancy of wild birds, which is one of the study limitations. While this approach may inevitably introduce some inefficiency in our efforts to avoid bias, an extension of this method can be considered when the number of checklists is categorized into several ordinal groups. With this approach, we have the flexibility to assess both linear and nonlinear effects by introducing dummy variables. For a specific bird species, let’s say “k,” if we categorize the reported checklist number into “three” categories based on defined cutoff points (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\text{U}}_{k}\le\:{\text{c}}_{1},\:{\text{c}}_{1}{<\text{U}}_{k}<{\text{c}}_{2},$$\end{document} or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\text{U}}_{k}\ge\:{\text{c}}_{2}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\text{c}}_{1}{\:\text{a}\text{n}\text{d}\:\text{c}}_{2}$$\end{document} are two selected cutoff points), then the variables in Eqs. (1) and (2) can be adjusted to account for linear or non-linear effects. It’s important to acknowledge, however, that this approach may also introduce bias due to potential misclassification.

The unique epidemiological pattern of clade 2.3.4.4 HPAI H5 is potentially shaped by eco-evolutionary processes through (i) the interaction between the viruses and a wide range of wild birds; (ii) the continued selection for both higher transmissibility and virulence, as observed in ducks36–39 or recently in milk cows40. It is necessary to enhance global surveillance and improve the understanding of the ecological properties that contribute to and sustain the transmission of HPAIV in wild birds such as species composition, population size, and demographic migration patterns. Polygenic risk scores (PRS) are becoming an increasingly popular approach to predict complex disease risk by deriving weighted scores after summing the number of risk increasing variants that participants harbour41. With the complexity of different wild bird species interacting with the poultry farms, the PRS framework motivates us to construct a poly-species GRS to summarize the avian influenza-associated wild bird species profile in the increasing large-scale population-level AIV-associated poultry farm outbreaks. Our study provides a robust approach to investigate the risk at the interface of domestic and wild birds. The high prediction accuracy of the GRS greater than 80% suggested its potential in outbreak prediction and implementing targeted surveillance.
