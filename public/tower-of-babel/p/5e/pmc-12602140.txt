Challenges, Ethical Considerations, and Best Practices of Using AI-Assisted Translation in Medical Writing: Examples From Japanese-English Translations
Authors: Takako Kojima, Tomoko Morita, Helena A Popiel
Published: 2025

Artificial intelligence (AI) has greatly changed how we perform translations, providing user-friendly tools that produce immediate results. These AI translation tools are now commonly used not only in everyday life but also in academic publishing, healthcare, and professional communication, where speed and convenience are important,1,2 resulting in a substantial reduction of language barriers.3

With the globalization of medical research, non-native English-speaking researchers face increasing pressure to publish in English-language journals. Particularly in the past few years, this has driven the use of AI translation tools, which offer speed and convenience. However, it is important to recognize that each language has unique characteristics—such as grammatical, syntactic, and cultural differences—that AI translation may not always capture accurately.2,4 Understanding these challenges is critical to maintain clarity, accuracy, and professional integrity in academic writing.

Ethical Considerations in AI-Assisted Medical Translation

Authors must recognize that AI cannot assume authorship or responsibility for scientific content. According to the guidelines by the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors and the Committee on Publication Ethics, which are guidelines followed by many prominent medical journals, AI cannot be credited as an author. Furthermore, regardless of whether AI is used or not, the ‘human’ author is fully responsible for the accuracy and integrity of the work.5,6,7,8,9

Transparency is also critical in medical publishing. Authors should clearly disclose the use of AI-assisted tools when they are used for drafting, editing, or translation.2 Failure to do so could compromise the credibility of the work, violate ethical norms, or introduce errors into publications. Therefore, adherence to established guidelines is essential to uphold ethical and professional standards.10,11

Challenges in Japanese–English Medical Translation

Despite advances in AI-assisted translation, challenges remain with accuracy, nuance, and context. Language is complex, and effective translation involves more than just matching words. Furthermore, it also requires an understanding of cultural context, professional norms, writing style, and idioms.12 Studies in Japan have shown that even advanced AI can struggle in these areas. For example, when translating Japanese medical papers into English using DeepL, about 94% of the translation matched the original Japanese text. Still, errors often appeared in complex sentences, method descriptions, and specialized terminology.13 Similar findings have been reported in technical medical contexts, such as reporting adverse events for medical devices, where AI translations, though generally acceptable, still showed inconsistencies and inaccuracies in technical terms and nuanced expressions.4

Despite these challenges, many researchers rely on AI translation tools to help with preparing manuscripts, communicating with colleagues, and interacting with patients in English. Although these tools can save time and reduce workload, they also raise questions about responsibility. Mistakes in AI-generated translations can cause confusion, damage credibility, and even affect patient safety. Therefore, it is imperative that authors carefully review AI translations before submitting their manuscripts.

We will now introduce some examples of how using AI translations can result in inappropriate expressions in medical writing, focusing on Japanese to English translations from our experience as medical editors working in Japan. We hope these examples help illustrate the way a particular language’s characteristics can affect the result of AI translations into English, and the dangers of relying on AI translations.

Example 1

Intended sentence: The patient was a 45-year-old woman.

AI translation: 45 years old, female

The Japanese equivalent of the AI-translated phrase “45 years old, female” is actually frequently used at the beginning of case reports written in Japanese. This is owing to the Japanese language having the unique characteristic that sentences are often understandable and can be spoken or even written without a subject. However, the English translation is an incomplete sentence that is inappropriate for an academic manuscript. This is an example of why authors must always check translations made by AI, as well as have an understanding of the differences in the characteristics between English and their own native language, so that they can identify these problems during the proofreading process.

Example 2

Intended sentence: “When I woke up, the doctors were all around me.”

AI translation: “When I woke up, my teachers were around me.”

This is an example of a quote taken from a paper that we edited, which was about the actual experiences of patients in a hospital. As a bilingual editor, the use of the word “teacher” in this sentence suggested that the author had used a translation tool to create an English version of their paper. In Japanese, the word equivalent to “teacher” in English is “sensei”, and has a broad meaning. The Chinese characters that make up this word literally mean “being born before (yourself)”, and refer to people with a large amount of skill, experience, and knowledge. Therefore, not only teachers, but a wide variety of professionals, such as doctors, lawyers, architects, tax accountants, etc., are all referred to as “sensei”. This is a good example demonstrating the importance of authors not only checking AI translations, but having an understanding of differences in the use of various words between their native language and the English language.

Example 3

Intended sentence: Clinical guidelines outline the most standard medical practices.

AI translation: Clinical guidelines represent the greatest common denominator in medical practice.

This sentence is from an abstract of a Japanese conference presentation for which an English abstract was also required, demonstrating an AI translation resulting in a sentence that sounds unnatural and confusing. This is because the Japanese word that was translated into English as “greatest common denominator” is often used in the Japanese medical field to mean “the most standard/common” or “targeting the greatest number of patients.” However, it is not a common phrase to use in English, and it gives more of a mathematical connotation. Again, this highlights the importance of authors checking their AI-generated translations and being aware that many phrases in one language often do not have the same meaning when directly translated into another.

Example 4

Intended sentence: Furthermore, this manuscript has not been submitted to any other journal.

AI translation: Furthermore, this content has not been submitted to any other magazines.

This sentence is from a cover letter that was originally written in Japanese, which was then translated into English using AI. The English translation sounds unnatural to a native English speaker, because periodical publications of academic manuscripts are usually referred to as “journals” rather than “magazines”. However, in Japanese, the words for “journal” and “magazine” are the same. The Japanese word “zasshi” is used for any type of periodical publication. When clarification is required, people will put an adjective before the word, such as “fashion magazine”, “economics magazine”, “medical magazine”, etc. Hence, authors send their manuscripts to what could be translated in English to “academic magazines.” Again, this is an example of how authors need to check AI translations, as well as to be aware of the differences in the use of specific words among different languages.

Example 5

Intended sentence: The patient’s chief complaint was increased mucus in stool.

AI translation: There has been an increase in mucus flight.

This is from a case report originally written in Japanese about a patient whose chief complaint was increased mucus-containing stool, which was then translated using AI, resulting in a nonsensical English sentence. In Japanese, the same character, or kanji, often has multiple meanings, and sometimes they can have entirely different and unexpected meanings. In this example, the Japanese character for “stool” can also mean “flight”, as in airplane flights. This is thus an illustrative example of a misinterpretation arising from a distinctive feature of the Japanese language, which uses combinations of characters, which can often have multiple meanings, to make words. In addition, as described above, Japanese can often be written and understood without a subject in the sentence, and here, the original Japanese text omits the essential subject “The patient’s chief complaint is,” relying solely on the expression “There has been an increase in mucus in the stool,”, resulting in further confusion.

Conclusion

These examples illustrate problems with AI translation from Japanese to English that may be commonly found in the medical/research setting (Table 1). To manage the increasing use of AI among younger generations, we are also educating medical and nursing school students to be aware of the shortcomings of AI, as well as the importance of recognizing errors made by AI translation.

Examples of errors in Japanese–English AI-assisted translation

Although this paper focuses on Japanese, other languages have their own unique structures and nuances that can affect AI translation accuracy. Therefore, non-native English-speaking researchers should carefully review translations from any language.

While AI-assisted translation tools will continue to evolve, it is important to recognize that AI serves as a support tool rather than a replacement for the human author in medical writing.14 Authors must use AI with careful review, ethical awareness, and adherence to professional writing standards.15,16,17 By following best practices, researchers can leverage AI translations safely and effectively, ensuring a high-quality, internationally acceptable standard of medical writing.
