Ultrasonography-guided canal decompression combined with vertebroplasty and cement-augmented pedicle screw fixation for stage III Kümmell’s disease with neurological deficits: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Cheng Zhang, Yachao Zhao, Qing Li, Wen Xu, Chuanhong Dou, Guangqing Cao, Dongjin Wu, Jie Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPercutaneous vertebroplasty or kyphoplasty is the preferred procedure for stage I and II Kümmell’s diseases (KDs), but there exist controversies on the operative option of stage III KD. This study aimed at exploring the safety and efficacy of ultrasonography-guided canal decompression (UG-CD) combined with vertebroplasty and cement-augmented pedicle screw fixation (CA-PSF) for treating stage III KD with neurological deficit (ND).MethodsBetween September 2017 and December 2023, all patients who received the UG-CD combined with vertebroplasty and CA-PSF for managing stage III KD with NDs were reviewed retrospectively with their demographic and operation data, and complications recorded. Besides, the scores of Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), together with imaging data including the kyphotic Cobb angle (KCA), wedge angle (WA), spinal canal area (SCA) at the narrowest level as well as anterior (AHR) and middle (MHR) height ratios were measured and compared between pre- and post-operation.ResultsA total of eleven patients with a mean age of 70.09 ± 2.98 years old were included in our study with their surgical time, hospitalization length, blood loss, and follow-up time being 150.91 ± 17.94 min, 202.09 ± 39.95 ml, 8.18 ± 1.17 days, and 16.91 ± 4.09 months, respectively. During the final follow-up, the KCA, WA, VAS scores, ODI scores, AHR, MHR, and SCA were significantly improved (P < 0.01). Intraoperatively, one case suffered a transient decrease in the motor evoked potential. Another case experienced a cerebrospinal fluid leakage postoperatively that was then successfully treated.ConclusionUG-CD combined with vertebroplasty and CA-PSF could be a feasible procedure for safely and effectively handling stage III KD with NDs.Keywords: Ultrasonography, Vertebroplasty, Cement-augmented pedicle screw fixation, Stage III Kümmell’s disease, Neurological deficit

Introduction

Kümmell’s disease (KD), as first described by Hermann Kümmell in 1895 [1], is defined as the avascular osteonecrosis of vertebrae secondary to delayed posttraumatic vertebral collapse, and often happens after osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture (OVCF) [2]. As evidenced, KD mainly affects those segments located at the thoracolumbar junction among the elderly with a prevalence of 7–37% [3], which can lead to low back pain, neurological deficits (NDs), and kyphosis deformity that would seriously reduce their living quality [4].

Based on the clinical and radiological manifestations, KD can be divided into the following three stages [5, 6]: stage I, the loss of vertebral height < 20%, with or without back pain; stage II, the loss of vertebral height > 20%, with or without ND; stage III, vertebral posterior cortex breakage and retropulsion that contribute to spinal canal stenosis and/or cord compression and induce continuous back pain, with or without ND. For the treatments of stage I and II KD, both percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) and kyphoplasty (PKP) can achieve satisfactory efficacies including immediate pain alleviation and vertebral height restoration [6–8]. However, there exist controversies on the surgical options for stage III KD, with the vertebroplasty being unable to correct severe kyphosis deformity, vertebrectomy inducing a great surgical trauma, and pedicle screw fixation (PSF) risking looseness among the elderly with osteoporosis [9, 10].

To address the above controversies, a combination of vertebroplasty and cement-augmented PSF (CA-PSF) has been recently advocated for managing stage III KD with satisfying outcomes [10–14]. Nevertheless, it remains a hard work to precisely locate the site of spinal canal stenosis or cord compression before decompression and dynamically observe the decompression result during operation. There exist studies highlighting the value of ultrasonography in spine surgery [15–17], but with as yet no report of its applications in the stage III KD. Here, this study aimed at filling this gap through retrospectively investigating the feasibility, safety, and effectiveness of ultrasonography-guided canal decompression (UG-CD) combined with vertebroplasty and CA-PSF for therapy of stage III KD with NDs.

Methods

The present work acquired an approval from the Ethics Committee of The Second Hospital of Shandong University (approval number: KYLL-2020(LW)-061), and then performed under the principles set out in the Declaration of Helsinki. Every subject wrote an informed consent for participating in this study and publishing individual data and images.

Participants

From September 2017 to December 2023, all patients who received the UG-CD combined with vertebroplasty and CA-PSF for handling stage III KD with NDs were reviewed retrospectively. Inclusion criteria: (1) single-segment stage III KD with ND, such as weakness and/or sensation changes in the lower limb, sphincter dysfunction, or perineal numbness that were diagnosed by the clinical and radiological performances; (2) bone mineral density (BMD) T values ≤ -2.5; (3) spinal canal stenosis or cord compression caused by the retropulsed posterior wall of diseased vertebrae. Exclusion criteria: (1) Two- or multi-segment KD, or pathological vertebral fracture induced by infection, tumor or other factors; (2) with a previous spinal surgery, rigid kyphosis, or serious illness that cannot tolerate such an operation; (3) with incomplete clinical data or the follow-up period less than one year. The following demographic data of included cases were recorded: age, sex, BMD and follow-up time.

Surgical procedures

All procedures were executed by a senior surgeon from the same operation team. To be specific, patients were placed in a prone position to maintain spine hyperextension. A posterior midline approach was selected for exposing bilateral screw insertion points, after which pedicle screws, augmented with bone cement, were inserted into adjacent two segments above and below the diseased vertebrae. Then, both of laminas and facet joints on the side with more serious canal stenosis or cord compression were resected while the other side might also need a resection to handle residual kyphosis via the Smith-Peterson or Ponte osteotomy [18]. Then, bilateral screws were connected temporarily with two rods for facilitating subsequent canal decompression and vertebroplasty below.

After observing the exact location of spinal canal stenosis or cord compression induced by retropulsed posterior wall of the diseased vertebrae from the sagittal and axial planes using an ultrasonic probe within the normal saline medium, the retropulsed bone fragments were pushed and compacted ventralward using a L-shaped compressor until a satisfying decompression was achieved and verified by the ultrasonography again under neurophysiological monitoring. Next, the cleft within the collapsed vertebrae was filled with bone cement prior to its fixation by two polyaxial screws. Then, bilateral rods were reinserted and screws appropriately compressed to correct kyphosis under C-arm fluoroscopy, after which both facet joints and laminas of the fixed levels were decorticated for grafting of bones. Finally, the incision was washed and sutured.

Postoperative management

The application of antibiotics began at half an hour before operation and lasted for the first 24 h postoperatively, combined with anti-osteoporotic treatments. The drainage tube was removed at a volume of less than 50 ml/day. At about four to five days postoperatively, ambulation was allowed under the protection of waist circumference.

Surgical safety

The operative data including surgical time, blood loss, and length of hospitalization, along with postoperative complications such as cement leakage, implant failure, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage, adjacent vertebral problems, infection, and neurological deterioration were recorded.

Surgical efficacy

All patients were examined preoperatively and received routine follow-ups after operation. The Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) from zero (no pain) to ten (the most severe pain) was applied to evaluating pain levels and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) to assessing disability levels. On a lateral X-ray film, the kyphotic Cobb angle (KCA) was measured by the Cobb’s method defined as the angle between the vertical lines across the superior endplate of the upper vertebrae and the inferior endplate of the lower vertebrae, while the wedge angle (WA) referred to the angle between tangent lines of the superior and inferior endplates of diseased vertebrae. In addition, the anterior (AHR) and middle (MHR) height ratios of diseased vertebrae, described as diseased vertebral height/mean adjacent vertebral height × 100%, and spinal canal area (SCA) located at the narrowest level were measured.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were done with the SPSS 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA) software and presented as means ± standard deviation for the continuous variables. The functional scores and radiological parameters at the last follow-up were compared to those preoperatively using the Paired-Samples T or Wilcoxon Signed Rank test based on the results of normal distribution and homogeneity of variance tests. It was considered statistically significant when the P value < 0.05.

Results

Patient demographics

As set out in Table 1, a total of 11 patients (four males and seven females) undergoing the UG-CD combined with CA-PSF and vertebroplasty for stage III KD with NDs were included in our study, with their age ranging from 63 to 76 years (mean, 70.09 ± 2.98), follow-up time ranging from 12 to 24 months (mean, 16.91 ± 4.09), and BMD T scores ranging from − 4.00 to -2.60 (mean, -3.15 ± 0.48). For clinical manifestations, varying degrees of back pain were involved in all cases, accompanied by numbness and weakness of the lower extremities in eight cases, intermittent claudication in five cases, and perineal numbness and sphincter dysfunction in two cases. Eight of the included cases had a distinct history of minor trauma. Regarding the diseased vertebrae, T11 or T12 was implicated in one case, L1 in six cases, and L2 in three cases.

Surgical safety

All procedures were successfully carried out, with the operation time ranging from 125 to 180 min (mean, 150.91 ± 17.94), blood loss ranging from 150 to 275 ml (mean, 202.09 ± 39.95), and hospitalization length ranging from 7 to 10 days (mean, 8.18 ± 1.17). To achieve a satisfying correction of kyphosis, the Smith-Peterson osteotomy was conducted in four cases and Ponte osteotomy in two cases. The motor evoked potential (MEP) amplitude decreased by about 40% in one case intraoperatively but recovered after decompression. Postoperatively, none of cement leakage, loosening of the screws, adjacent segment problems, and infection was occurred while one case suffered a CSF leakage that was then successfully treated.

Surgical efficacy

As revealed in Table 2, the scores of VAS (2.18 ± 0.60 vs. 7.64 ± 0.81) and ODI (31.45% ± 6.46% vs. 68.36% ± 8.38%), KCA (13.99° ± 5.09° vs. 33.08° ± 8.94°), and WA (9.64° ± 3.94° vs. 22.63° ± 7.94°) were remarkably lower at the final follow-up relative to those before operation (P < 0.01). Conversely, the AHR (74.33% ± 11.48% vs. 26.39% ± 21.24%), MHR (71.03% ± 16.81% vs. 35.25% ± 22.78%), and SCA (1.54 ± 0.23 cm2 vs. 0.88 ± 0.17 cm2) at the last follow-up were much higher than those preoperatively (P < 0.01). Discussion

KD is described as ischemic vertebral necrosis after delayed posttraumatic vertebral collapse, and often occurs following OVCF among elderly women [19]. In addition, KD mostly involves those segments at the thoracolumbar junction (T10-L2) which is featured by a large range of motion and the site of stress concentration [20–22]. Similar findings were observed among our included subjects as well, which displayed that stage III KD with NDs affected the aged women more than men, and all diseased segments were localized at T11-L2, especially L1.

As a special type of OVCF, an important radiological feature for the diagnosis of KD is the intravertebral cleft or vacuum sign that indicates nonunion of fracture [23]. Besides, posterior cortex breakage of the diseased vertebrae and its retropulsion into the canal, representing stage III KD, were involved in each case, and contributed to individual NDs including numbness and weakness of the lower extremities, intermittent claudication as well as perineal numbness and sphincter dysfunction. It has been well recognized that conservative strategies are less effective for managing KD, and related to complications of chronic back pain, progressive kyphosis, and delayed NDs [10, 24]. By restoring vertebral body height, either of PKP and PVP can instantly alleviate back pain for stage I and II KD [25, 26], whereas neither of them is suitable for dealing with stage III KD alone as they are incapable of handling severe stenosis or kyphosis, but bring a high risk of cement leakage that may cause neurological deterioration [14, 25]. To lower the risk of cement leakage, we blocked the posterior cortex breakage via pushing and compacting it ventralward using a L-shaped compressor, following which the AHR and MHR of diseased vertebrae were also well-restored by vertebroplasty.

To simultaneously handle severe stenosis and kyphosis that were implicated in stage III KD, an open surgery is often recommended. As suggested, anterior surgery can facilitate a thorough canal decompression via directly resecting the diseased vertebrae and avoid extra injuries to the posterior ligament complex, but it can only correct limited kyphosis while create a big surgical trauma that would endanger those elderly patients with comorbidities [9]. By contrast, posterior operation is preferable for a better kyphotic correction through osteotomy and PSF, whereas the presence of osteoporosis among the elderly would restrict the application of traditional PSF due to the potential risk of instrumentation failure induced by screw loosening [14, 27]. Since each included case had osteoporosis, we thus adopted the CA-PSF to enhance the pull-out strengths of screws, as evidenced by a previous study [28]. At the last follow-up, both of KCA and WA were well restored without the occurrence of any implant-related event, highlighting the safety and effectiveness of CA-SF for coping with stage III KD.

Considering that the diseased vertebrae was osteoporotic and owned an inherent weak area in its posterior cortex, namely vertebrobasilar vein foramen, the retropulsed posterior wall could be pushed and compacted effortlessly. However, it is still a difficult work to accurately locate the sites of canal stenosis and cord compression prior to decompression, and then dynamically observe the decompression result during spine surgery. Here, we first proposed the UG-CD for stage III KD. Intraoperatively, the ultrasonography was first conducted to visualize the location of canal stenosis or cord compression from the sagittal and axial planes, followed by placement of the L-shaped compressor to push and compact the retropulsed bone fragments ventralward under the real-time guidance of ultrasonography till a good decompression was accomplished. During surgery, the amplitude of MEP decreased by about 40% in one case but recovered after decompression without neurological deterioration. Postoperatively, another case suffered CSF leakage which was successfully prevented by placing him in a Trendelenburg position, and then removing his drainage tube at seven days after operation. At the last follow-up, the VAS scores, ODI scores, and SCA were remarkably improved, which suggested the efficacy of UG-CD for management of stage III KD.

Here, we declare that this work has some limitations: First, it was a retrospective cohort study containing a small sample size, which is mainly attributed to the rarity of stage III KD. Second, follow-ups should be continued to observe the long-time safety and outcomes of this procedure for handling stage III KD with NDs. Third, there is no control group treated by other treatment for this disorder, which would reduce the robustness of evidence offered by the present study.

Conclusion

This is the first study that reported the application of UG-CD combined with vertebroplasty and CA-PSF for managing stage III KD with NDs. As indicated above, this procedure could be a feasible surgical option for safely and effectively treating stage III KD with NDs, and possessed the superiorities of visualized decompression under the real-time guidance of ultrasonography, reduced surgical trauma and radiation exposure, and lowered risk of cement leakage. However, it is noteworthy that standardized anti-osteoporosis must be maintained postoperatively since KD is usually secondary to OVCF among the elderly. High-quality studies with big samples and long-time follow-ups are warranted to provide more robust evidence on this topic.
