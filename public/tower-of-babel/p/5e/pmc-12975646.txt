Effect of Social Support Reciprocity on Mental Health Status Among Community‐Dwelling Older Adults: A Cross‐Sectional Study of the Kashiwa Cohort Study
Authors: Natsumi Hirose, Toshiaki Takahashi, Tomoki Tanaka, Bo‐Kyung Son, Weida Lyu, Katsuya Iijima, Gojiro Nakagami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsReciprocity of informal social support is associated with improved mental health in older adults. While several studies have examined support provision or receipt separately, this study clarifies reciprocity's effect on mental health among older adults in Japanese urban areas.MethodsA 2014 survey targeted community‐dwelling adults aged ≥ 65 years in Kashiwa City, Chiba, Japan. Quality of life was assessed using the WHO‐Five Well‐Being Index (0–25 points) with a cutoff of < 13 points indicating suspected depression. Independent variables included four mutually exclusive categories of receiving and providing emotional and instrumental social support. Sex‐stratified multivariate logistic regression was used to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for suspected depression. Ethical approval was obtained from The University of Tokyo (No. 21‐192).ResultsData from 1302 participants were analyzed. The mean age was 74.7 (standard deviation, 5.5) years, and 623 (47.8%) were women. Suspected depression was identified in 93 men (7.1%) and 80 women (6.1%). Compared to those with no support exchange, the ORs for reciprocal emotional support were 0.16 (CI: 0.06–0.42; p < 0.001) for men and 0.44 (CI: 0.75–2.58; p = 0.365) for women. For instrumental support, ORs were 0.90 (CI: 0.28–2.86; p = 0.871) for men and 0.30 (CI: 0.11–0.80; p = 0.016) for women.ConclusionSocial support reciprocity positively influences mental health among older adults living in urban Japan. Facilitating environments for mutual social support helps maintain mental health in urban‐dwelling older adults in Japan.

Introduction

Social support is an important factor for the mental health of older adults living in urban areas in Japan. In an aging society, understanding the role of social support in later life has become increasingly important, as social support positively affects the mental health status of older adults [1]. The definition of social support varies widely. However, the concept which integrates various ideas from previous research, is described as “a collective term for tangible and intangible actions that individuals perceive, receive, or are intended to receive and carry out, aimed at maintaining and promoting their well‐being and physical and mental health” [2]. Social support can be divided into formal and informal types [3]. In general, formal support comes from public services, whereas informal support, provided by family and friends, involves ongoing personal interactions. Thus, informal support may positively impact mental health [4]. In Japan, where the birth rate continues to decline and the population is aging rapidly, the importance of informal social support is expected to grow.

Both providing and receiving informal social support positively affect the mental health of older adults [5, 6]. In particular, reciprocity is generally described as the process of “give‐and‐take,” referring to the balance in the social exchange between individuals [7]. This process is considered important for the mental health of older adults [8, 9, 10]. Furthermore, the impact of reciprocal social support has been reported to vary according to sex, type, and amount of support [11]. From a measurement perspective, reciprocity requires simultaneous assessment of both providing and receiving social support, rather than examining each component independently.

Generally, social connections tend to be stronger in rural areas than in urban areas because agriculture, which remains the primary livelihood for many rural residents, requires cooperative work [12]. As a result, rural residents place greater importance on participating in community activities, such as neighborhood associations, than urban residents [12]. In contrast, urban residents tend to live more independently, and their higher mobility often weakens social ties [12, 13]. Therefore, the impact of social support on the mental health of rural and urban populations is expected to differ due to the strength of their social connections.

Reciprocity in social support is important for the mental health of older adults in rural areas [8, 9, 10]. However, due to the lack of research, the impact of reciprocity in social support on the mental health status of older adults in urban areas remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to clarify the impact of reciprocity in social support on the mental health status of older adults in an urban area of Japan.

Methods

Study Setting and Participants

A secondary analysis was performed using data obtained from the Kashiwa prospective cohort study [14]. Overall, 1308 participants aged ≥ 65 years living in Kashiwa City were included in the Kashiwa study. Participants were randomly recruited from a registry of Kashiwa City residents in Chiba Prefecture, an urban area adjacent to Tokyo, Japan. The inclusion criteria were older adults (≥ 65 years) who were not classified as requiring nursing care or support. We excluded patients with missing items on the WHO‐Five Well‐Being Index and social support index [15]. Data were collected between September 2014 and November 2014 at welfare and community centers.

This study strengthens the reporting of observational studies in the epidemiological guidelines for cohort studies. The ethics committee of The University of Tokyo approved the study protocol (No. 21‐192), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Variables

Outcome: WHO‐Five Well‐Being Index (WHO‐5)

The outcome measure was the Japanese version of the World Health Organization‐Five Well‐Being Index (0–25 points) [16]. The WHO‐5 is a self‐reported instrument that measures mental well‐being and detects suspected depressive symptoms. It consists of five statements relating to the past 2 weeks [17]. Each statement was rated on a 6‐point scale, with higher scores indicating better mental well‐being. It consists of five items (e.g., “Over the last 2 weeks, I have felt cheerful and in good spirits”) rated on a 6‐point scale. Each question is rated from 0 to 5, with 0 representing “at no time” and 5 “at all time” [18]. We followed the criteria outlined in the official guidelines, with a cutoff of < 13 points indicating suspected depression [19]. In this study, the WHO‐5 Well‐Being Index had a Cronbach's alpha of 0.89.

Exposure: The 2‐Way Social Support Scale

Social support was assessed using the 2‐way social support Scale [20]. The participants were asked four questions regarding receiving and providing emotional and instrumental support: whether they usually listen to someone's complaints, whether they usually complain to someone, whether they usually help someone with housework, and whether someone helps them with housework. Participants were asked to answer either “yes” or “no.” We used a composite variable combining emotional and instrumental support. Therefore, the four mutually exclusive categories of receiving and providing emotional and instrumental social support served as the independent variables. Reciprocity was evaluated as present if both providing and receiving social support were present.

Covariates

We incorporated demographic variables such as age, sex, body mass index (BMI), cohabitation status, marital status, frailty assessment for the three groups, medical history, economic status, presence or absence of income‐generating employment, and participation in local organizations. Cohabitation status was categorized as “living alone” or “living with others.” Marital status was categorized as “married,” “divorced,” “widowed,” or “single.” Participants were also categorized as “non‐frail,” “pre‐frail,” or “frail” [21].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted to examine the association between reciprocity of social support and suspected depression. Multicollinearity among the independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor, with variables exhibiting high multicollinearity excluded from the analysis. We analyzed the male and female subgroups to investigate potential sex‐specific associations. A multivariate logistic regression model was used to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for suspected depression after adjusting for relevant covariates. We included four mutually exclusive categories—receiving and providing emotional and instrumental social support and receiving or providing support separately—in that model to examine the effects with and without considering reciprocity. Furthermore, to examine how receiving and providing social support influences the risk of suspected depression when reciprocity is not considered, we conducted a multivariate logistic regression analysis. Social support variables were used separately for receiving and providing emotional and instrumental social support. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using StataNow/MP version 18.5 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Regarding covariates, non‐frailty was significantly associated with a lower risk of suspected depression in both men and women. Among men, poorer economic conditions were significantly associated with a higher risk of suspected depression, and osteoporosis was significantly associated with a lower risk of suspected depression. Among women, stroke was significantly associated with a lower risk of suspected depression. In addition, participation in study‐related social activities and rare participation in social activities were significantly associated with a higher risk of suspected depression compared with frequent participation.

Discussion

This secondary analysis of data from a cross‐sectional study performed in 2014 in Kashiwa City, Japan, examined the impact of reciprocity in social support on the mental health status of older adults living in urban areas. We found that reciprocal emotional support for men and instrumental support for women was significantly associated with a decreased risk of suspected depression in Japanese urban areas. When receiving and providing support were examined separately, receiving support alone was associated with suspected depression in both men and women; however, when receiving and providing support were examined together, only reciprocal support was associated with mental health.

Few studies have investigated the relationship between the reciprocity of social support and mental health in Japan. In recent years, the rapid migration of younger generations to and from urban areas has made it difficult for them to establish stable interpersonal relationships. Additionally, the aging population in residential development areas and apartment complexes has weakened social connections, exacerbating issues such as social isolation and solitary deaths [22]. Therefore, the effects of social support and relationships differ between rural and urban areas in Japan. In rural areas, reciprocity in social support has been found to affect mental health and suicidal ideation positively [10]. However, the impact of the reciprocity of social support on the mental health status of older adults remains unclear. Therefore, it is necessary to clarify the relationship between the reciprocity of social support and the mental health status of older adults living in urban areas.

Previous studies, with a focus on reciprocity, have examined the effects of receiving or providing social support separately and reported that even one‐sided support could positively affect mental health status among older adults [5, 6]. In the present study, we also found that providing or receiving social support alone had a significant negative impact on mental health (Supporting Information S1: Tables 1–4). However, when receiving and providing support were examined together in the logistic regression analysis, reciprocal social support, defined as the combination of both receiving and providing support, was associated with mental health. This finding highlights that reciprocity in social support plays an important role in promoting better mental health. Therefore, when referencing the previous literature or conducting further research on the impact of social support on mental health, it is important to avoid overvaluing one‐way social support without considering reciprocity.

In this study, reciprocal emotional support was significantly associated with fewer depressive symptoms in men than in women. A previous study reported that receiving emotional support is important for reducing loneliness and improving mental health [23]. Generally, women are more likely to rely on acquaintances, friends, and children for support and to have a broader network [24]. This finding suggests that women often talk to others and usually give and receive emotional support. Conversely, a significantly higher proportion of men report relying on their spouses [24, 25]. Consequently, men may have had fewer opportunities to express their feelings. Therefore, having a place where they can both provide and receive emotional support may be important to their mental health. Additionally, it is important to provide support that enables men living in urban communities to both receive and provide emotional support.

Reciprocal instrumental support was significantly associated with fewer depressive symptoms in women. Generally, women have multiple roles at home and in the workplace, which contributes to higher levels of daily stress compared to men [26]. This finding suggests that individuals tend to seek effective ways to alleviate their burdens and value on practical assistance. However, simply receiving practical support does not reduce the risk of suspected depression; instead, assisting others without feeling obligated may help maintain good mental health. Therefore, the mutual exchange of instrumental support may help alleviate daily stress, thereby reducing suspected depression in older women.

This study identified the factors associated with suspected depression (Tables 2 and 3). Frailty was associated with higher odds of suspected depression in both men and women. This finding is consistent with previous research that reported a bidirectional relationship between frailty and suspected depression [27]. Factors such as reduced physical activity, social disconnection, and chronic systemic inflammation may explain this association [28]. In men, perceived economic hardship was significantly associated with a higher risk of suspected depression, consistent with previous studies suggesting that economic difficulties may lead to suspected depression, mediated by lifestyle factors such as insufficient nutrition [29]. In contrast, osteoporosis was associated with a lower risk of suspected depression, which contradicts studies suggesting that suspected depression is associated with reduced bone mineral density, possibly mediated by lifestyle, hormonal changes, and medications [30, 31]. The mechanisms, including sex‐related factors, remain unclear, and further investigation is needed [32].

In women, stroke was associated with a lower risk of suspected depression, contradicting previous research [33, 34]. However, the details of the association between stroke and suspected depression remain unclear [33, 34], and the discrepancy found in this study is difficult to explain within its scope. Additionally, women who rarely participated in community activities had a significantly higher risk of depression than those who participated in daily activities. This finding supports the idea that frequent participation in diverse activities can reduce suspected depression, especially among women with limited social interactions due to caregiving or household roles [35]. However, participation in study‐related activities was significantly associated with a higher risk of suspected depression than non‐participation, contradicting previous findings [35]. Autonomy in social participation benefits mental health, whereas obligatory participation may harm well‐being, especially among older women [36]. These results suggest that psychological stressors unique to learning activities, such as obligation and lack of achievement, may negatively impact mental health, highlighting the importance of motivation and the quality of participation. Taken together, this study suggests that creating opportunities for older adults to both provide and receive social support, rather than focusing on one‐way support, may help promote better mental health in urban communities.

This study has several limitations. First, there is a possibility of reverse causation due to the cross‐sectional design. Individuals with suspected depression may avoid social interaction, making it difficult to determine the causal direction. Therefore, reverse causation does not fully explain these results. Second, the impact of the reciprocal balance between providing and receiving support on mental health remains unclear due to the absence of a quantitative assessment of the amount of support and the identity of the provider or recipient. Understanding the cause‐and‐effect relationship in more detail requires long‐term studies that consider both the amount of support given and the identity of the provider. Third, potential selection bias should be considered during data collection. The participants in this study were mostly independent individuals who could visit the venues on their own. The sample predominantly consisted of relatively healthy residents with high levels of health awareness. Therefore, the data reflect this specific population, making it difficult to account for the potential biases in the dataset.

Conclusion

In conclusion, reciprocity in social support positively affects the mental health of older adults living in urban areas. Reciprocal emotional support is more important for men, whereas reciprocal instrumental support is more important for women. Creating an environment in which older adults can both provide and receive social support may help maintain mental well‐being among those living in urban areas of Japan. Recognizing the importance of reciprocal social support can foster mutual aid and help improve support systems for older adults in Japan.
