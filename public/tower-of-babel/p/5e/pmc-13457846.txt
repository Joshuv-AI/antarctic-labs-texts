YTHDF2-driven degradation of BARX2 mRNA accelerates osteoarthritis progression by enhancing the TOP2A/WNT/β-Catenin axis
Authors: Rui Zhang, Qian Liang, Tao Lan, Sheng Li, Jian Zhang, Yuchen Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Emerging evidence highlights the critical role of transcription factors (TFs) in diverse diseases, including osteoarthritis (OA). However, the identification of key TFs in OA pathogenesis needs further understanding. BarH-like homeobox 2 (BARX2), significantly downregulated in osteoarthritis (OA) cartilage and IL-1β-stimulated chondrocytes, plays a protective role in OA pathogenesis. Mechanistically, methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) / methyltransferase-like 14 (METTL14) complex-mediated N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification was found to enhance BARX2 mRNA degradation via the YTH N6-methyladenosine RNA binding protein 2 (YTHDF2) - dependent manner, resulting in decreased BARX2 expression in osteoarthritic human chondrocytes. BARX2 was shown to mitigate interleukin-1beta (IL-1β) - induced damage in osteoarthritic human chondrocytes by suppressing Wnt / β-catenin signaling pathway. Conversely, BARX2 knockdown exacerbated this damage. Additionally, we discovered that BARX2 inhibited the activation of Wnt / β-catenin pathway and reduced IL-1β-induced osteoarthritic chondrocyte damage by binding to the topoisomerase II alpha (TOP2A) promoter to suppress TOP2A transcriptional expression. Lastly, our data demonstrated that BARX2 ameliorate OA progression by inhibiting TOP2A-mediated Wnt / β-catenin pathway activation in DMM-induced male OA mice. In conclusion, YTHDF2-mediated decay of m6A-modified BARX2 mRNA by METTL3/14 reduces BARX2 levels, contributing to OA progression via upregulation of TOP2A and consequent activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA), a chronic inflammatory disorder of non-infectious origin, is marked by the deterioration of articular structures. This condition is triggered by various factors such as trauma, senescence, and excessive body weight1, and has become a predominant contributor to disability worldwide2,3. In recent decades, the prevalence of OA has increased significantly, a trend linked to the aging of the global population and changes in lifestyle habits4. Despite this, the advancement of effective therapeutic strategies for OA has been impeded by gaps in our understanding of its underlying pathogenic mechanisms5. Hence, there is an urgent need to explore new pathogenic pathways in OA and pinpoint effective therapeutic targets.

Transcription factors (TFs) are proteins capable of binding to specific DNA sequences, which can be either individually or in conjunction with other proteins to facilitating or impeding the recruitment of particular genes to RNA polymerase, thereby regulating gene expression6. A growing body of research indicates that the misregulation of TFs is closely associated with the onset and progression of numerous diseases, including OA7–9. For instance, Forkhead box O (FoxO) family of TFs plays a crucial role in cartilage development, maturation, and aging10–12. SRY-box transcription factor 6 (SOX6) and SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9) are TFs that promote chondrogenic differentiation and proliferation in chondroprogenitor cells within the cartilage mesenchyme13–15. Hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF-1α) safeguards articular cartilage by regulating chondrocyte adaptation to hypoxic conditions16. Conversely, hypoxia-inducible factor 2 alpha (HIF-2α) acts as a catabolic regulator, inducing the overexpression of several catabolic enzymes that contribute to cartilage degradation17. Collectively, these findings underscore the significant role of TFs in OA pathogenesis. Nonetheless, the identification of key TFs involved in OA progression remains an area of ongoing research, with many yet to be discovered.

The m6A represents the most abundant and reversible epitranscriptomic mark within mammalian mRNA, involving a complex interplay among “writers”, “erasers”, and “readers”. The m6A writer complex, comprising the catalytic component methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) along with the cofactors methyltransferase-like 14 (METTL14) and Wilms tumor 1-associating protein (WTAP), is responsible for the installation of m6A marks. In contrast, the m6A erasers, fat mass and obesity associated protein (FTO) and AlkB homolog 5(ALKBH5), are tasked with the removal of these marks, thereby executing a dynamic regulatory function. These m6A marks are then interpreted by m6A reader proteins, modulating mRNA activity and engaging in a spectrum of physiological and pathological processes18. Although the functions of m6A modification in arthritis have been widely recognized19, the crucial role of the METTL3-METTL14 complex in osteoarthritis has not yet been systematically revealed.

In this study, we first demonstrated that YTH N6-methyladenosine RNA binding protein 2 (YTHDF2) facilitates the degradation of BarH-like homeobox 2 (BARX2) mRNA by recognizing N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modifications introduced by the methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) / methyltransferase-like 14 (METTL14) complexes, leading to the downregulation of BARX2 in osteoarthritic cartilage. Additionally, our findings highlighted the protective role of BARX2 in the progression of OA. Importantly, our research clarified that BARX2 plays a critical role in suppressing the Wnt / β-catenin signaling pathway and alleviating OA progression by inhibiting the transcriptional expression of topoisomerase II alpha (TOP2A), as confirmed by both in vitro and in vivo experiments.

Results

Downregulation of BARX2 in osteoarthritic cartilage and IL-1β-stimulated human chondrocytes

In our endeavor to identify pivotal TFs in OA pathogenesis, we sourced OA-specific transcriptomic data from the CEO database, including datasets GSE113825 and GSE16464, for detailed data mining. Our analysis identified nine TFs that were differentially expressed across both datasets, with Pituitary tumor-transforming gene 1 (PTTG1), Hairy/enhancer-of-split related 2 (HEY2), Forkhead box M1 (FOXM1), BARX2, and Enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) exhibiting concordant expression patterns as well as T-box 3 (TBX3), Jun proto-oncogene (JUN), FBJ murine osteosarcoma viral oncogene homolog B (FOSB), and FBJ murine osteosarcoma viral oncogene homolog (FOS) displaying discordant expression levels (Fig. 1A). To establish an in vitro model of OA chondrocyte injury, human chondrocytes were exposed to varying concentrations of IL-1β, revealing a progressive decline in BARX2 expression in response to escalating IL-1β concentrations, indicative of a concentration-dependent effect (Fig. 1B). Additionally, RT-PCR and Western blot analysis were utilized to measure both mRNA and protein expression levels of BARX2 in human chondrocytes exposed to 10 ng/mL IL-1β at specific time intervals, and observing a pronounced time-dependent decrease in BARX2 expression (Fig. 1C, D). Meanwhile, the results of clinical samples showed that the expression level of BARX2 in cartilage tissue of OA patients decreased significantly with the degree of cartilage damage (Fig. 1E), suggesting that BARX2 participated in OA progression. Furthermore, we established an OA cartilage injury model (Fig. 1F, G) and conducted a series of experiments to verify the expression changes of Barx2 in the cartilage tissues of mice with or without OA. The validation in an animal model demonstrated a significantly decreased protein and mRNA expression level of BARX2 in the cartilage tissues of OA mice induced by DMM for 8 weeks as compared to those of sham-operated controls (Fig. 1H–J). BARX2 is down-regulated in OA chondrocyte by METTL3/METTL14-mediated m6A modification through the m6A reader YTHDF2-dependent manner

A substantial body of evidence has highlighted the significant impact of m6A methylation on mRNA expression and the progression of OA20, we identified three putative m6A modification sites within the BARX2 mRNA sequence using SRAMP software (Fig. 2A). Subsequently, MeRIP-qPCR analyses revealed a pronounced increase in m6A methylation of BARX2 mRNA in human chondrocytes stimulated with IL-1β and in cartilage tissues from a DMM-induced OA model (Fig. 2B). Employing RM2Target software, we predicted that the m6A methylation of BARX2 mRNA is potentially regulated by the m6A “writers” METTL3 and METTL14, a finding conserved across human and murine species. To validate this, we developed adenoviral constructs for the targeted knockdown of METTL3 and METTL14 (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). Subsequently, RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses demonstrated that IL-1β potently induced the upregulation of METTL3 and METTL14, and concurrently downregulated BARX2 expression in human chondrocytes (Supplementary Fig. 1C, D; Fig. 2C). In contrast, the silencing of METTL3 or METTL14 markedly restored BARX2 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels in human chondrocytes stimulated by IL-1β (Supplementary Fig. 1C, D; Fig. 2C). Moreover, MeRIP-qPCR analyses indicated that the knockdown of METTL3 or METTL14 substantially diminished m6A methylation of BARX2 mRNA in IL-1β-induced human chondrocytes (Fig. 2D). Guided by the m6A modification sites predicted by RM2Target software (Supplementary Fig. 1E), we generated dual luciferase reporter plasmids harboring wild-type and mutant sequences of the m6A modification sites in BARX2 mRNA and conducted assays in C-28/I2 cells. The findings indicated that the knockdown of METTL3 or METTL14 influenced expression through the motif sequence at the recognition site 682 (Fig. 2E). Subsequently, human chondrocytes with knockdown of METTL3 or METTL14 were treated with the transcription inhibitor actinomycin D at specified time points. Our findings revealed that the knockdown of either METTL3 or METTL14 substantially decelerated the degradation of BARX2 mRNA (Fig. 2F). Furthermore, the expression mRNA and protein levels of BARX2 as well as the m6A methylation status of BARX2 mRNA were assessed following the transfection of sh-METTL14 into METTL3-overexpressing human chondrocytes. Our results demonstrated that silencing METTL14 abrogated the suppressive effects of METTL3 overexpression, restoring the mRNA and protein levels of BARX2 and m6A methylation levels of BARX2 mRNA (Fig. 2G, H and I; Supplementary Fig. 1F). Once mRNA is modified by the m6A “writer,” the m6A “reader” recognizes the methylation to modulate mRNA levels. YTHDF2, an m6A reader, is known to facilitate the degradation of target mRNAs by recognizing m6A modification sites21. To explore the putative regulatory function of YTHDF2 in relation to BARX2, we utilized an adenoviral vector to infect human chondrocytes, aiming to downregulate YTHDF2 and consequently inhibit its expression (Supplementary Fig. 1G). RT-qPCR and western blot analyses revealed that the knockdown of YTHDF2 significantly reversed the inhibitory effect of IL-1β on BARX2 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 2J, K; Supplementary Fig. 1H). Additionally, RIP assays confirmed the interaction between YTHDF2 and BARX2 in human chondrocytes, an interaction that was markedly reduced upon the knockdown of METTL3 or METTL14 (Fig. 2L). The actinomycin D decay assay indicated that YTHDF2 knockdown significantly slowed the degradation of BARX2 mRNA, while overexpression of METTL3 and METTL14 did not significantly influence this effect (Fig. 2M). In summary, the METTL3/METTL14 complex-mediated m6A modification diminishes BARX2 mRNA stability via YTHDF2-dependent mRNA degradation, consequently repressing BARX2 expression in OA.

Involvement of BARX2 in IL-1β-induced osteoarthritic chondrocyte injury

To elucidate the biological role of BARX2 in human chondrocytes, we constructed adenoviruses for both BARX2 overexpression (BARX2) and knockdown (shBARX2). RT-qPCR and western blot analyses revealed that IL-1β treatment substantially suppressed BARX2 mRNA and protein expression in human chondrocytes, and this suppression was significantly mitigated by BARX2 overexpression and exacerbated by BARX2 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 2A–D). Further, CCK-8 and EdU assays indicated that BARX2 overexpression significantly counteracted the inhibitory effect of IL-1β on chondrocyte proliferation, while BARX2 knockdown intensified this inhibitory effect (Fig. 3A, B). In addition, western blot results demonstrated that overexpression of BARX2 significantly increased the expression of Cyclin E and decreased the expression of CDK inhibitors p21 and p27 in IL-1β-induced human chondrocytes. On the contrary, knockdown of BARX2 significantly reduced the expression of Cyclin E and increased the expression of CDK inhibitors p21 and p27 in IL-1β-induced human chondrocytes (Supplementary Fig. 2E). Flow cytometry and western blot analyses were employed to assess the impact of BARX2 on apoptosis in IL-1β-induced chondrocyte injury. Flow cytometry results showed that BARX2 overexpression significantly reduced IL-1β-induced apoptosis in human chondrocytes, and knockdown of BARX2 significantly increased it (Fig. 3C). Consistent with the results of flow cytometry, the result from western blot demonstrated that IL-1β treatment markedly increased Bax protein expression and decreased Bcl-2 protein expression in human chondrocytes, effects that were attenuated by BARX2 overexpression (Fig. 3D, E), indicating that BARX2 overexpression significantly suppresses the IL-1β-induced apoptosis in chondrocyte. In contrast, BARX2 knockdown obviously intensified the IL-1β-induced apoptosis (Fig. 3G, H). Furthermore, given that the degradation of articular cartilage is a key pathological hallmark of OA, we examined the impact of BARX2 on the expression of extracellular matrix (ECM)-associated proteins, including MMP-13, ADAMTS-5, Collagen II, and Aggrecan, in human chondrocytes stimulated by IL-1β using Western blot analysis. Our results revealed that overexpression of BARX2 markedly suppressed the protein levels of cartilage-degrading enzymes (MMP-13 and ADAMTS-5) and enhanced the protein levels of chondrogenic markers (Collagen II and Aggrecan) in IL-1β-stimulated human chondrocytes (Fig. 3D, F), indicating that BARX2 overexpression mitigates IL-1β-induced ECM degradation in human chondrocytes. Conversely, knocking down BARX2 significantly exacerbated ECM degradation induced by IL-1β (Fig. 3G, I). Knockdown of BARX2 exacerbated IL-1β-induced osteoarthritic chondrocyte injury by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway

Current research indicates that the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is crucial in the pathogenesis and progression of OA22, and targeted modulation of this pathway is considered one of the most promising therapeutic strategies for OA treatment23,24. Our study revealed that treatment with IL-1β upregulated the protein expression levels of Wnt1, total β-catenin, nuclear β-catenin, and downstream target genes of the β-catenin pathway (c-MYC and cyclinD1) in human chondrocytes, these effects were significantly mitigated by the overexpression of BARX2 (Fig. 4A), indicating that BARX2 overexpression suppresses IL-1β-induced activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in human chondrocytes. Conversely, BARX2 knockdown significantly enhanced the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway induced by IL-1β in human chondrocytes (Fig. 4B). Therefore, we hypothesize that BARX2 may modulate cartilage damage in OA by regulating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. To elucidate the potential regulatory role of BARX2 via the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, we performed rescue experiments using XAV-939, an inhibitor of this pathway. CCK-8 assay results demonstrated that BARX2 knockdown markedly suppressed the proliferation of human chondrocytes treated with IL-1β, and this inhibitory effect was significantly counteracted by XAV-939 (Fig. 4C). Flow cytometry results revealed that BARX2 knockdown significantly enhanced IL-1β-induced apoptosis in human chondrocytes, an effect that was mitigated by XAV-939 treatment (Fig. 4D). In line with the flow cytometry findings, BARX2 knockdown led to elevated Bax and reduced Bcl-2 expression in IL-1β-stimulated human chondrocytes, changes that were reversed by XAV-939 treatment (Fig. 4E, F). Additionally, western blot analysis disclosed that BARX2 knockdown markedly increased the protein expression of cartilage-degrading enzymes (MMP-13 and ADAMTS-5) and significantly decreased the protein expression of chondrogenic markers (Collagen II and Aggrecan) in IL-1β-induced human chondrocytes, effects that were attenuated by XAV-939 treatment (Fig. 4E, G). Discussion

Emerging evidence indicates that the dysregulation of TFs is associated with the pathogenesis of OA25–27, but the biological functions of most TFs in OA remain poorly understood and warrant further investigation. In this study, we demonstrated that BARX2 is involved in OA progression, with low expression in osteoarthritic human chondrocytes resulting from the degradation of BARX2 mRNA through m6A modification mediated by the YTHDF2 recognition of the METTL3/METTL14 complex. More importantly, our study shows that BARX2 ameliorates OA chondrocyte injury by regulating the TOP2A-Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting OA progression. This research reveals a novel mechanism in OA pathogenesis and identifies a promising therapeutic target for the disease.

Previous studies have classified BARX2 as a homeobox transcription factor within the Bar class, which plays a role in cell signal transduction across various developmental contexts28,29. For example, BARX2 regulates chondrogenesis in conjunction with Sox proteins through the BMP signaling pathway in limb development30. However, the biological function of BARX2 in OA has been scarcely documented. Herein, our research evidence has indicated that knockdown of BARX2 exacerbates IL-1β-induced human chondrocytes damage. On the contrary, overexpression of BARX2 counteracts IL-1β-induced human chondrocytes damage and improves cartilage damage in OA mice. In cell cycle regulation, Cyclin D1 binds to cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) 4/6, forming a complex that phosphorylates the retinoblastoma (Rb) protein and releases E2F transcription factors, driving cells from the G1 phase into the S phase. Typically, increased Cyclin D1 levels promote cell proliferation. However, our study observed that BARX2 overexpression reduced Cyclin D1 protein levels while enhancing chondrocyte proliferative capacity. This seemingly paradoxical finding can be explained by considering broader cell cycle regulation. While Cyclin D1 is pivotal for the G1/S transition, other cyclins and CDKs also govern cell cycle progression. Our data show that BARX2 overexpression significantly upregulates Cyclin E and downregulates CDK inhibitors p21 and p27. This suggests BARX2 may compensate for Cyclin D1 loss by promoting Cyclin E upregulation and directly activating CDK2, bypassing Cyclin D1-CDK4/6 signaling requirements. Additionally, p21/p27 inhibition releases residual CDK2 and CDK4/6 activity, enabling S-phase entry despite reduced Cyclin D1 levels. This mechanism aligns with previous studies where Cyclin E overexpression rescued proliferation in Cyclin D1-deficient models31. These phenomena reflect the complexity of cell cycle regulation and indicate that cell proliferation mechanisms should be analyzed by considering multiple interacting factors rather than single ones.

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, playing a crucial role in osteoblast maturation and bone formation, has emerged as a hot area of research in OA32. Previous studies have demonstrated that the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is associated with chondrocyte-mediated cartilage degradation and the subsequent progression of OA33. Therefore, modulation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, either directly or indirectly, could serve as an effective strategy for the amelioration of OA. Wu et al. found that platelet-rich plasma protects chondrocytes activated by IL-1β by inhibiting the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway34. Sun et al. reported that C-X3-C motif chemokine receptor 1 (CX3CR1) knockout ameliorates OA by regulating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway32. In this study, we observed that inhibiting the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway promoted cell proliferation and inhibited apoptosis and ECM degradation in IL-1β-induced human chondrocytes, while activation of the pathway exerted opposite effects. These findings suggest that the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is implicated in OA progression. Moreover, our data further confirmed that the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is negatively regulated by BARX2, a finding consistent with previous results in cancer research35,36. Importantly, our findings reveal that the suppression of BARX2 triggers the WNT signaling cascade within osteoarthritic human chondrocytes, thereby exacerbating chondrocyte damage. Nonetheless, the precise regulatory mechanisms of WNT signaling by BARX2 are yet to be fully elucidated.

The gene encoding human DNA topoisomerase II α, TOP2A, is situated on chromosome 17 and plays a pivotal role in regulating DNA topology, facilitating chromosome segregation, and governing the cell cycle37,38. Currently, the TOP2A study mainly focuses on cancer39,40, but its role in the progression of OA has rarely been reported. Our research revealed that elevated TOP2A levels substantially inhibited chondrocyte proliferation, enhanced apoptotic processes, and led to the degradation of the ECM, concurrently activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascade in a chondrocyte injury model triggered by IL-1β. Additionally, our study introduces the novel finding that TOP2A is a downstream target of BARX2, which exerts a suppressive effect on its expression. Lastly, we have shown that BARX2 mediates the suppression of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, thereby mitigating OA progression by reducing TOP2A transcription, as evidenced in both cellular and animal models.

Recent research has underscored the significance of m6A-mediated mRNA regulation in the pathogenesis and progression of OA41,42. Nevertheless, the interaction between BARX2 and m6A modification had yet to be elucidated. Our findings reveal a pronounced upregulation of m6A modification on BARX2 mRNA in human chondrocytes stimulated by IL-1β and within osteoarthritic cartilage, an effect orchestrated by the METTL3-METTL14 writer complex, which is central to nuclear RNA N6-adenosine methylation43. Additionally, we have demonstrated that cytosolic YTHDF2, known for its ability to bind and recognize m6A, is instrumental in promoting mRNA decay. Our study suggests that m6A modification, mediated by the METTL3/METTL14 complex, enhances BARX2 mRNA decay in a YTHDF2-dependent manner.

While our findings advance the understanding of BARX2 in OA pathogenesis, several limitations merit consideration to guide future research. Firstly, we did not delve deeper into the impact of the METTL3/METTL14 complex-mediated m6A modification on the BARX2-TOP2A-Wnt/β-catenin pathway in chondrocytes exposed to IL-1β or in the cartilage tissues of DMM-induced OA mice. Secondly, Although BARX2 modulates TOP2A-Wnt/β-catenin signaling, its broader regulatory functions in OA-relevant processes—including chondrocyte senescence, synovial angiogenesis, and subchondral bone remodeling—remain unexplored. Spatial transcriptomics or BARX2 conditional knockout models could delineate its tissue-specific roles across joint compartments. Thirdly, m6A modification contributes to BARX2 suppression, but alternative mechanisms (e.g., DNA methylation, histone modifications, or non-coding RNA interactions) likely synergize to regulate its expression. Integrated multi-omics analyses (methylation arrays, ATAC-seq, and RNA interactome profiling) are warranted to dissect this regulatory hierarchy in further. Fourthly, our conclusions derive from a model utilizing young adult mice (initially 9-week-old at DMM surgery). While this model effectively captures synchronized molecular responses during post-traumatic OA progression, it does not recapitulate the complex pathophysiology of age-related human OA, such as senescence-associated secretory phenotype or systemic low-grade inflammation. As our observations were made in adult mice (approximately 17-week-old at analysis) rather than in truly aged mice (>1 year old), the findings primarily reflect surgically induced OA mechanisms in non-aged adults. Therefore, caution is warranted when extrapolating these results to aged or chronic spontaneous OA contexts. Future studies should validate the role of BARX2 in two complementary directions: one using genuine aged mouse cohorts (>1 year old) to assess its interplay with aging-related processes such as cellular senescence and metabolic dysregulation; and the other employing 12-week-old mice to establish whether the functional role of BARX2 in OA pathogenesis remains consistent across different stages of adulthood. Fifthly, our findings reflect pooled responses from male and female donors. Given established sex differences in OA44,45, the lack of gender-specific analysis is a limitation. In future experimental designs, we will employ a gender stratified approach to clarify the potential differences in treatment responses. Lastly, our conclusions are drawn from preclinical data, and preclinical findings necessitate validation in a large cohort of patients with OA. Correlative studies assessing BARX2 expression levels, m6A signatures, and TOP2A-Wnt/β-catenin activity in patient-derived cartilage samples stratified by OA severity could strengthen clinical relevance.

Taken together, we have demonstrated that BARX2 plays a crucial role in safeguarding cartilage from damage in OA. Mechanistically, YTHDF2 initiates the degradation of BARX2 mRNA by recognizing the m6A modification mediated by the METTL3/METTL14 complex, this reduction in BARX2 levels subsequently triggers the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway through the transcriptional activation of TOP2A mRNA, accelerating the progression of OA. In contrast, elevated BARX2 levels inhibit the Wnt/β-catenin signaling, thereby mitigating OA progression by downregulating TOP2A transcription, as evidenced in both cellular models and animal studies. Our research uncovers the BARX2-TOP2A-Wnt/β-catenin axis as an innovative pathway in combating cartilage degeneration in OA, presenting a potential therapeutic target for the clinical management of this condition.

Methods

Data collection and analysis

We analyzed transcriptomic data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE113825 and GSE16464) using R language packages to identify differentially expressed TFs in cartilage tissues or chondrocyte of patients with or without OA. The threshold for significance was set at | log (fold change) | > 1 and P-values < 0.05. To predict the potential target genes of BARX2, we utilized the hTFtarget database available at HumanTFDB (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/HumanTFDB/#!/search).These predicted targets were then intersected with the differentially regulated genes associated with OA identified from GSE113825 and GSE16464. Additionally, we employed the SRAMP and RM2Target (http://rm2target.canceromics.org) to analyze the m6A modification of BARX2 transcripts and to identify the m6A “writers” that regulate these modifications, respectively.

Clinical samples

This study was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of the Shenzhen Second People’s Hospital. Informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to the study’s commencement. Human articular cartilage tissues were collected aseptically from patients undergoing total knee replacement surgery due to end-stage knee OA or traumatic amputation. The diagnosis of OA in these patients was made in accordance with the classification criteria established by The American College of Rheumatology for diagnosing OA46. All specimens were graded according to the International Cartilage Regeneration & Joint Preservation Society (ICRS) scoring system, and the clinical characteristics of the patients are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Primary culture of articular human chondrocytes and establishment of an in vitro OA human chondrocyte injury model

Human articular chondrocytes were derived from 6 osteoarthritic cartilage specimens (2 males, 4 females; age range 55–65 years), employing a protocol analogous to prior descriptions with minor modifications. Briefly, the cartilage tissue was sectioned into fragments and subjected to enzymatic digestion with 0.25% trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37°C for 30 minutes. This was followed by treatment with 2 mg/mL collagenase type II (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37°C for 4 hours to further break down the tissue. A 100 μm cell strainer was utilized to separate the digested material, and the human chondrocytes were collected through centrifugation at 1000 g for 10 minutes. Post-washing with PBS, the isolated human chondrocytes (designated as passage 0, P0) were resuspended and plated in six-well plates with α-MEM medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific), enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% double antibody (Sigma-Aldrich). After reaching confluence, P0 cells were subcultured once (1:2 ratio) to generate passage 1 (P1) cultures. A portion of the P1 cells was utilized for identification, and following successful confirmation, the remaining P1 chondrocytes were directly used in experiments without further passaging to prevent the potential interference of dedifferentiated cells. Additionally, to mimic the injury in human osteoarthritic chondrocytes, the cells were treated with interleukin-1beta (IL-1β) at concentrations of 0, 10, 20, and 40 ng/mL (Sigma-Aldrich) for 24-hour.

Protein extraction and western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from the specified samples using pre-chilled radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Roche). Next, protein concentrations were determined using the Enhanced Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following quantification and denaturation, equal amounts of protein were resolved and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). After being blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 hour, the PVDF membranes were incubated with the respective primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Thereafter, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam, ab6721, 1:5000) for 2 hours at room temperature. Protein bands were detected and analyzed using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (DINGGUO Biology) on a gel documentation system (GE Healthcare), and the intensity of bands was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.46r, U.S. National Institutes of Health). All primary antibodies listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Cell proliferation assessment via CCK-8 and EdU assays

To evaluate cell proliferation, we employed the cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) and the 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) assays. Human chondrocytes were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5×104 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight prior to treatment cells per well. After treatment, the cells were collected for CCK-8 and EdU analysis. For the CCK-8 assay, treated with the Enhanced CCK-8 solution (Beyotime, catalog number C0042) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Finally, the absorbance of the samples was recorded at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan, model F50). For the EdU assay, cells were processed with the BeyoClick™ EdU Cell Proliferation Kit conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (Beyotime, catalog number C0071S) following the provided guidelines. Nuclear counterstaining was carried out using Hoechst 33342. Subsequently, images were captured and analyzed using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Mshot, model MF52) and ImageJ software (version 1.46r, U.S. National Institutes of Health).

Assessment of human chondrocyte apoptosis via flow cytometry

Apoptosis in human chondrocytes was quantified using the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime, catalog number C1062S), following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. Human chondrocytes were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 8×10⁵ cells per well. After adherence and treatment, cells were harvested and then stained with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) staining solution. After a gentle mix, the cells were incubated for 20 minutes at room temperature in the absence of light. The apoptotic rate was subsequently determined and analyzed using the BD FACSVerse™ Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences).

m6A RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP) assay

The m6A RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP) assay was conducted using the Magna MeRIP m6A kit (Sigma, catalog number 17-10499-2) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Initially, total RNA was extracted from the human chondrocytes and cartilage tissue using the TaKaRa MiniBEST Universal RNA Extraction Kit (Takara, catalog number 9763). Subsequently, the RNA was purified with the Dynabeads mRNA Purification Kit (Invitrogen, catalog number 61006). Following fragmentation using RNA fragmentation reagent (Invitrogen, catalog number AM8740), immunoprecipitation was carried out with an anti-m6A antibody (Abcam, ab258077, 6 μg/mL). Both the input and immunoprecipitated RNA samples were subsequently analyzed via RT-qPCR.

RNA decay assays

The human chondrocytes were treated with 5 μg/mL Actinomycin D (Sigma, A9415) for various durations (0 h, 3 h, 6 h, and 12 h) to evaluate BARX2 mRNA stability. At each time point, cells were harvested, and total RNA was extracted using the TaKaRa MiniBEST Universal RNA Extraction Kit (9763, Takara) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted RNA was then subjected to RT-qPCR analysis to quantify the remaining BARX2 levels.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

The RIP assay was conducted using BeyoRIP™ RIP Assay Kit (Beyotime, P1801S) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, human chondrocytes with knockdown of METT14 or METTL3 were lysed with RIP lysis buffer. The lysates were then incubated with magnetic beads conjugated to either anti-YTHDF2 antibody (Abcam, ab220163, 1:30) or anti-IgG antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 2729, 1:50) for 6 h at 4°C. Subsequently, the beads were treated with proteinase K and shaken to elute the proteins. Ultimately, the co-precipitated RNAs were purified using the TaKaRa MiniBEST Universal RNA Extraction Kit (catalog number 9763, Takara) and subjected to RT-qPCR analysis.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

The ChIP assay was performed by the ChIP assay kit (Abcam, ab500) in compliance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. Briefly, the nuclei of designated chondrocyte were extracted after cross-linking with 1% paraformaldehyde to obtain chromatin-protein complexes. Subsequently, chromatin was cleaved into 400 to 800 fragments by sonication, and then incubated with anti-BARX2 antibody (SCBT, sc-53177, 1:50) and anti-IgG antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 2729, 1:50) at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubated with the coated magnetic beads for 4 h at 4 °C. After the beads were washed, bound chromatin was eluted using ChIP elution buffer and then cross-linking was relieved. Finally, the immunoprecipitated DNA was purified using the Wizard® Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega, UAS), and the relative enrichment levels of TOP2A promoter were evaluated using RT-qPCR with specific primers (Forward primer of TOP2A promoter, AAGAAAAAAAAAATTACAT and Reverse primer of TOP2A promoter, AAGAAAAAAAAAATTACAT).

Dual- luciferase reporter assay

To investigate the effect of BARX2 binding on TOP2A promoter on transcriptional activity of TOP2A, we generated luciferase reporter constructs containing the wild-type (pro-TOP2A-WT) and mutant (pro-TOP2A-Mut) sequences of the TOP2A promoter region, which include the binding sites for BARX2. Additionally, to determine the role of METTL3 and METTL14 in m6A modification and its effect on BARX2 expression, we created luciferase reporter vectors with m6A modifications at specific sites (701 A-G, 862 A-G, and 1204 A-G) within the BARX2 mRNA sequence. Subsequently, these vectors were transfected into the human chondrocyte cell line C-28/I2 cells (BFB Life Sciences, http://www.bluefcell.com/sy, BFN60803901, Shanghai, China), which was cultured in a Dmem/High Glucose medium (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich) under conditions of 5% CO2 and 37°C. After 48 h of transfection, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega, USA). The relative luciferase activity was calculated by normalizing the Renilla luciferase signal to that of Firefly luciferase.

Establishment of a mouse model of OA through destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM)

Male C57BL/6 mice (SPF, 8-weeks old) purchased from Laboratory Animal Center of Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China) were house in the institution’s animal facility under optimal husbandry conditions for the duration of the study. Following a one-week acclimation period, 64 mice were randomized to the DMM (n = 48) and sham (n = 16) cohorts. Anesthetize mice using the isoflurane vaporization system (RWD Life Sciences Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). Briefly, anesthesia was induced by placing the mouse into an induction chamber with 5% isoflurane delivered in 100% oxygen at a flow rate of 0.3 L/min. When the plantar reflex disappears, was transferred to a nose cone for maintenance anesthesia with 2% isoflurane in 100% oxygen at a flow rate of 0.3 L/min. Under the maintenance anesthesia, destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) was performed on the right hind knee using established methods48. Briefly, the right knee and lower leg of the mice in DMM cohorts underwent hair removal and disinfection with 70% ethanol following anesthesia with isoflurane. Next, a skin incision was made from the distal patella to the proximal tibial plateau. Subsequently, the joint capsule medial to the patellar tendon was incised, and the tendon was retracted to expose the medial meniscus craniomeniscotibial ligament (MMTL) via blunt dissection of the fat pad. The MMTL was transected using micro-scissors, followed by wound closure with 6-0 vicryl rapide sutures. The mice in sham cohorts (n = 16) were received identical surgery without MMTL transection. One-week post-surgery, the DMM mice (n = 48) were re-randomized into three treatment groups (n = 16/group): DMM + adeno-associated virus (AAV) - negative control (NC) group, DMM + AAV-Barx2 group, and DMM + AAV-Barx2 + AAV-Top2a group, and a single intra-articular injection of the corresponding AAV (10 μL, 1×1013 vg/mL) into the right knee joint space under ultrasound guidance. Meanwhile, the mice in the sham cohorts were also given the same solvent-containing AAV-NC through single intra-articular injection. The AAV vectors, including AAV-NC, AAV-Barx2, and AAV-Top2a, were procured from AIji Biotechnology (Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). Eight weeks after surgery, the mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide (CO₂) inhalation. The right hind limb was then removed, and the muscles and soft tissues attached around the knee joint were carefully dissected. Subsequently, the knee joint samples from 5 mice per group were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight for histopathological analysis. And the remaining knee joint samples were immediately separated from the femoral condyles and tibial platforms to obtain the articular cartilage tissues, which were then placed in liquid nitrogen for molecular biological analysis. The expression efficiency of AAV in murine knee cartilage tissues was assessed using RT-qPCR and western blot analyses.

All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Shenzhen Second People’s Hospital (Approved number: 202400114) and in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Histological analysis

The harvested knee joints were fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution, followed by decalcification, paraffin embedding, and sectioning for further analysis. Following dewaxing in xylene and a series of graded ethanol dehydrations, the tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (C0105S, Beyotime) and Safranin O & fast green (G1371, Solarbio) for histological assessment. The stained sections were examined and photographed using an orthotopic light microscope (Leica, model DMI1). Histological grades of OA were then evaluated using the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) scoring system, as detailed in a previous publication49, and the higher scores indicates more degeneration and destruction of articular cartilage.

Immunohistochemistry analysis

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were sequentially processed through deparaffinization with xylene and rehydration through a graded ethanol series. Following heat-mediated antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0), endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by treating the sections with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 minutes at room temperature. Non-specific binding sites were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were then incubated with primary anti-Barx2 antibody (Invitrogen, MA1-27164, 1:2) at 4 °C overnight in a humidified chamber. After three washes with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (PBST), the samples were treated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (Invitrogen, G-21040, 1:500) for 2 h at room temperature. Diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogenic development was performed using a commercial HRP substrate kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with reaction termination monitored under light microscopy. All stained sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated through an ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam.

For quantitative analysis of Barx2 protein expression levels in mouse cartilage tissue, we performed immunohistochemical image analysis based on optical density measurements. Firstly, an inverted fluorescence microscope (Mshot MF52) was used to randomly capture five non-overlapping high-power fields in the articular cartilage region of each section under identical imaging parameters. Subsequently, all images were converted to grayscale and processed with a uniform threshold using ImageJ software (version 1.46r, National Institutes of Health, USA) to measure the integrated optical density (IOD) and Area for each field of view. Finally, the relative expression level of Barx2 protein was calculated as the average optical density (AOD) using the formula AOD = IOD / Area. To ensure objectivity, all analyses were performed independently by two investigators blinded to the experimental groups, and the results were averaged for statistical analysis.

Statistics and reproducibility

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses and graph plotting were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad, USA). The differences between the two groups and more than two groups were confirmed by Unpaired t test and ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, respectively. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All experiments were conducted in triplicate at a minimum. ‘
