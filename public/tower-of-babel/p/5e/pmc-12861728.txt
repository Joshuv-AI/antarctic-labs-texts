Thirty-three years of editorial work. A lifetime adventure
Authors: Rose-Marie Bluthe
Published: 2025

While working as a senior scientist at the Laboratory of Psychobiology of Adaptive Behavior in Bordeaux, France, in the early 1990s, I was asked by Robert Dantzer, who was the head of the research group where I was employed, whether I would accept serving as an Editorial Assistant to help him with his new role as the European Editor of Psychoneuroendocrinology (PNEC). I had no experience in this type of work, but I accepted enthusiastically. PNEC was then published by Pergamon, and Dr. Ned Kalin (University of Wisconsin at Madison) had agreed to be the Editor for North America. I was appointed on May 1st, 1992.

The journal experienced several major changes during the long period I worked for it. Elsevier took over from Pergamon on January 1, 1998. Dr. Kalin kept his editor position until 2018 [1] and was succeeded by Dr. Isabella Heuser (Charité, Berlin, Germany) from 2019 to 2021. At that point, the distribution of work between the two Editors shifted from the initial geographic focus of the authors to the order in which manuscripts were submitted.

After 30 years as Editor-in-Chief of PNEC, Robert Dantzer decided it was time to step down from this role and pass the baton to another Editor [2]. He was replaced by Dr. Elizabeth Shirtcliff (University of Oregon at Portland) [3], and the journal's management was modified with the appointment of four Associate Editors to handle better the diverse topics the journal covers. I became the journal manager. Meanwhile, Robert Dantzer had launched another journal called Comprehensive Psychoneuroendocrinology (CPNEC) to complement PNEC. I continued serving as an editorial assistant for this new journal. Our investment in the editorial processing of manuscripts for PNEC paid off, as its impact factor steadily increased to 5.8 in 2011. This was accompanied by an increase in the number of manuscripts submitted to the journal which reached a record number of 1087 in 2018. The journal was published in print until volume 149 in 2023, after which publication became fully digital. On its side, CPNEC did very well, as its first impact factor obtained in 2023, after three years of existence, reached 2.1 and moved up to 2.5 in 2024.

What I remember most from all these years is, first and foremost, the evolution of manuscript processing for PNEC. I directly experienced the shift from mail to electronic publishing during the 1990s, and I had to navigate through various publishing software programs tried with varying success by Elsevier, including its own, before switching to Editorial Manager in June 2016. The main advantage of moving to electronic journals was that it significantly improved the dialogue with authors and reviewers and reduced the time for editorial processing, from several months initially to just a few weeks. However, the transition was not easy, as with each new editorial software adopted by Elsevier, I had to learn how the system operated and help our authors and reviewers manage it.

All of this took up more of my time than I initially expected. I used to handle everything related to my role as an Editorial Assistant outside of my working hours when I was still doing research in Bordeaux. This was made easier because Robert Dantzer was still there and easily accessible. I thought I could do the same work at my own pace after I decided to retire in 2008. But that was not the case. Working as an Editorial Assistant requires interacting not only with authors and reviewers but also with the publishing office of the journal, which is located in the Netherlands for the administrative tasks and in India for the journal's production. This directly affects my working hours. I now live in Toulouse, France. Therefore, my communications with India occur from 4:00 a.m. to 10:00 a.m. The editors of PNEC and CPNEC are both based in the United States, meaning I work with them from 4:00 p.m. onward. Since the PNEC editor is in the USA, our exchanges mostly happen between 9:00 pm and 4:00 am the next day. This daily workload distribution leaves me some time to perform the checks needed when we receive new or revised manuscripts before passing them to the relevant editors, whether associate or guest editors. In addition to this routine work, I also check submission deadlines for articles under review and send reminders to reviewers when deadlines have passed.

My responsibilities don't end there, as I must answer specific questions from editors, authors, and reviewers. Additionally, I often need to assist authors or reviewers who are having trouble connecting to Editorial Manager or who have lost their login and password. This means I constantly monitor everything, a task that never stops. On the production side of the journal, I verify the publication order within a given volume and the titles for each section.

The year 2025 saw an increase in manuscript submissions for PNEC, approaching 800 submissions at the time of writing. CPNEC also saw a rise in submissions, from a few dozen to 150. Much of this increase is due to the rise in submissions from China for both journals (37.6% of PNEC submission originate from China), while submissions from North America have decreased for both journals. Articles from China are not without problems, due to the excessive use of artificial intelligence tools to process big data repositories, often without much consideration of the scientific relevance of the question the Authors address.

Overall, I can say that although my job is demanding and leaves little time for vacation, it has provided me with great satisfaction. Not all authors who publish in PNEC and CPNEC are members of the International Society of Psychoneuroendocrinology or attend its annual meetings, but it has been interesting to meet them there and see them from a different perspective compared to when they are just authors. Several of them have become friends, and I enjoy seeing them at the annual ISPNE meeting.

Robert Dantzer has decided to step down as Editor-in-Chief of CPNEC at the end of December 2025. He will pass the baton to Dr. Julie Lasselin. She is a psychoneuroimmunologist focused on better understanding how inflammation influences behavior and the factors underlying individual differences in sickness behavior. Her research closely relates to mine, which has been a focus throughout my career, and reconnects with Robert Dantzer's original work. My task is not finished as I will work with her and Birdie for the next year.

In summary, I can say that being an Editorial Assistant is a challenging but fascinating task that requires a lot of dedication, patience, and tolerance.
