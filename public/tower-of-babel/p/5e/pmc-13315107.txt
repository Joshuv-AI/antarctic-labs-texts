DMXAA accelerates orthodontic tooth movement via macrophage-mediated Rab13-enriched extracellular vesicle release and chemokine secretion
Authors: Yan Wang, Kejia Zhang, Xueyu Sun, Hao Xu, Heng Gu, Zhichun Jin, Linlin Zhu, Hanwen Zhang, Bin Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOrthodontic tooth movement (OTM) is a complex process involving periodontal tissue remodeling, and safe strategies to accelerate OTM remain a research focus. DMXAA (Vadimezan), a non-toxic immune-modulating drug with established clinical trial data, stimulates the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines potentially relevant to OTM, which involves immune-mediated bone remodeling.MethodologyA murine OTM model was established in C57BL/6 mice, receiving local subperiosteal injections of DMXAA. Tooth movement distance, bone volume fraction, and osteoclast parameters were quantified via Micro-CT and histology. The mechanism was dissected using RNA-sequencing of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs), sEV proteomics, and functional osteoclastogenesis assays. Macrophage depletion and Rab13 knockdown models were employed to verify specificity.ResultsLocal DMXAA administration significantly enhance orthodontic tooth movement and rate in mice under orthodontic force application, accompanied by reduced alveolar bone volume and increased osteoclast numbers on the compressive force side. DMXAA induced macrophage accumulation at the force-applied site, polarizing them toward M1-like pro-inflammatory phenotypes characterized by high secretion of CCL5 and CXCL10. Furthermore, small extracellular vesicles derived from DMXAA-treated macrophages, which were enriched with Rab13 protein, contributed to osteoclast fusion and maturation, thereby accelerating OTM.ConclusionsDMXAA accelerates OTM through a dual mechanism: chemokine-driven osteoclast differentiation and sEV-Rab13-mediated osteoclast fusion. These findings highlight the Rab13-sEV axis as a novel therapeutic target for modulating the periodontal microenvironment and enhancing orthodontic efficiency. Our results elucidated a novel mechanism by which DMXAA accelerated orthodontic tooth movement and suggested it as a potential therapeutic agent to optimize orthodontic treatment.

Background

Orthodontic tooth movement (OTM) is a complex biological process driven by mechanical forces applied during orthodontic treatment. This process involves remodeling of the periodontal tissues, including alveolar bone resorption and formation, primarily mediated by osteoclasts and osteoblasts [1, 2]. Clinically, the efficiency of OTM directly affects treatment duration, patient compliance, and the risk of complications such as root resorption or periodontal tissue damage [3]. Thus, identifying safe and effective strategies to accelerate OTM while maintaining bone homeostasis remains a key focus in orthodontic research.

In recent years, researchers have investigated new techniques combined with orthodontic treatment to accelerate tooth movement. These techniques can be categorized as invasive (surgical) and non-invasive (non-surgical) techniques or methods [4, 5]. Evidence showed that corticotomy is effective and safe for accelerating OTM [6, 7]. However, few patients can accept this surgical intervention to accelerate tooth movement due to its aggressive and invasive nature. The non-surgical approaches include low-intensity laser therapy, pulsed electromagnetic fields, photobiomodulation, electric currents and pharmacological interventions [8–10]. Local pharmacological interventions would be an excellent replacement if their efficiency is proven, especially if they are not accompanied by any side effects [11, 12]. However, existing research describing the effects of pharmacological agents on OTM such as vitamin D, prostaglandins, parathyroid hormone and cytokines were based on animal subjects and lack precise molecular mechanisms regarding intercellular communication [13]. Given this need, selecting effective drugs that can accelerate orthodontic tooth movement from the drugs already in clinical trials could be a promising strategy. In fact, OTM is considered a good reflection of osteoimmunology since it involves immune response and bone remodeling [10, 14]. Activated immune cells such as macrophages have been found to play a crucial role in orthodontic treatment [6]. Therefore, various factors that affect the immune microenvironment around the teeth or alter the immune response in this area can, theoretically, influence orthodontic tooth movement.

DMXAA (5,6-dimethylxanthenone-4-acetic acid, Vadimezan), a flavone-acetic acid-based drug, has been reported to modulate immune responses and inflammatory pathways in various disease models [15, 16]. The induction of the innate immune system by DMXAA has been observed to stimulate the production of inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin 6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor (TNF), granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (GCSF), and macrophage inflammatory protein 1α (MIP-1α), Those pro-inflammatory cytokines may contribute to accelerating the OTM process [15, 17]. The databases of drug clinical studies in ClinicalTrials.gov (https://clinicaltrials.gov) and the EU Clinical Trials Register (https://www.clinicaltrialsregister.eu/) showed 14 and 5 clinical trial items in phases I/II/III for this drug, respectively. Importantly, DMXAA exhibits no significant toxicity and its pharmacokinetics has been completely characterized. Although DMXAA can cause specific and irreversible destruction of established tumor vessels, it does not harm healthy vascular tissues [15, 16, 18]. Therefore, investigating whether DMXAA enhances OTM and its underlying biological mechanisms could be a worthy study.

In this study, we demonstrated that DMXAA induced macrophage accumulation at the site of force application and drove their differentiation into M1-like pro-inflammatory phenotypes characterized by high secretion of CCL5 and CXCL10. Furthermore, small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) derived from DMXAA-treated macrophages contributed to osteoclast fusion through the Rab13-dependent pathway. Our results elucidated a novel mechanism by which DMXAA accelerated orthodontic tooth movement and suggested it as a potential therapeutic agent to optimize orthodontic treatment.

Results

DMXAA treatment accelerated the orthodontic tooth movement of mice

To investigate whether DMXAA affect tooth movement under orthodontic force, we subjected mice to orthodontic force application and injected DMXAA subperiosteally around the moving teeth every three days for a 14-day observation period (Fig. 1A). Then we measured tooth movement via Micro-CT at days 3, 7, and 14 post force application. Our results showed that the tooth movement distance in the DMXAA-injected mice was significantly greater on days 7 and 14 compared to that in PBS-injected mice (vehicle) (Fig. 1B and C). Moreover, the rate of tooth movement in the DMXAA-injected mice was significantly higher on day 7 (Fig. 1D), indicating that DMXAA contributes to accelerating tooth movement under orthodontic force. The day-7 time point corresponds to the peak rate of OTM acceleration, suggesting that this time point is optimal for detecting drug-induced bone metabolism response, consistent with previous reports [6, 19, 20]. Furthermore, Micro-CT analysis showed that the BV/TV of alveolar bone in the interradicular region of the loaded tooth was significantly decreased after DMXAA injection compared with that in the vehicle group (Fig. 1E and F), suggesting a decrease in bone quality and that this decrease may due to increased osteoclast activity. Histological analyses using H&E and TRAP staining revealed that the TRAP-positive surface and the number of osteoclasts per unit area on the compressive force side of loaded alveolar bone increased significantly under orthodontic force on day 7, and these increases were further augmented in the DMXAA-treated mice (Fig. 1G-I). These findings implied that DMXAA treatment was associated with enhanced osteoclast activity, which facilitated the acceleration of orthodontic tooth movement. Taken together, our results demonstrated that DMXAA could enhance orthodontic tooth movement in mice under orthodontic force application.

DMXAA treatment promoted macrophage recruitment in the loaded alveolar bone region

Macrophages play a crucial role in regulating bone remodeling during orthodontic tooth movement (OTM) [6, 21]. To explore the mechanism of DMXAA-induced acceleration of OTM, we detected the number of macrophages in the loaded alveolar bone region. Immunofluorescence analysis of alveolar bone revealed that the number of macrophages labeled with F4/80 was markedly increased in DMXAA-treated mice when compared to mice treated with PBS (vehicle) (Fig. 2A and B). Similarly, flow cytometry analysis of macrophages in the loaded alveolar bone showed that the number of F4/80- and CD11b-positive macrophages was significantly greater following DMXAA administration under mechanical force compared to that in the PBS-treated group (vehicle) (Fig. 2C and D). To further determine whether the macrophages were involved in DMXAA-induced orthodontic tooth movement, we injected clodronate liposomes (Clo-Lip), a well-established macrophage depletion agent, into the alveolar bone of mice followed by DMXAA treatment. As shown in Fig. 2E and F, the tooth movement distance measured by Micro-CT revealed that macrophage depletion significantly inhibited the orthodontic tooth movement induced by DMXXA, suggesting that the accumulation of macrophages contributed to DMXAA-induced tooth movement.

DMXAA induced pro-inflammatory macrophages characterized by high expression levels of CCL5 and CXCL10

To further explore the characteristics of macrophages induced by DMXAA during OTM, we applied compressive force (CF) to bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) to simulate the phases of orthodontic treatment in vitro according to a well-established model. CF was applied to BMDMs using glass disks in combination with DMXAA stimulation. Cells without drug treatment served as controls. The expression profile of cytokines, as detected using RNA sequencing, is shown in Fig. 3A and B. Our analysis revealed a significant upregulation of the expression of most classical M1 markers, while a significant decrease in the expression of M2 markers was observed. This result suggested that DMXAA treatment drove macrophage differentiation to be skewed toward the M1 phenotype with a pro-inflammatory response. Notably, we observed that among the cytokines that showed significant alterations, CCL5 and CXCL10 showed prominent upregulation in DMXAA-treated BMDMs (Fig. 3C). To further validate this result, we employed RT-qPCR to assess the expression levels of CCL5 and CXCL10 in BMDMs subjected to CF, in the presence or absence of DMXAA treatment. As shown in Fig. 3D, under CF conditions, the expression levels of both CCL5 and CXCL10 were significantly elevated in DMXAA-treated cells compared to cells subjected to CF but without DMXAA treatment. In further support, the levels of CCL5 and CXCL10 proteins secreted into the culture medium from DMXAA-treated BMDM were higher compared to those in the medium from untreated BMDMs (Fig. 3E and F). We also analyzed the changes in these cytokines after DMXAA stimulation of THP-1 cells, a human monocyte/macrophage cell line. Similar to BMDMs, the levels of both CCL5 and CXCL10 in THP-1 cells increased significantly after DMXAA stimulation compared with those in untreated cells under CF (Fig. 3G). In addition, we observed that the cell morphology of DMXAA-stimulated BMDMs was distinct from that of classic M1 macrophages, featuring shorter pseudopodia and larger cell size (Fig. 3H). To determine the expression of CCL5 and CXCL10 in alveolar bone under orthodontic force in vivo, with or without DMXAA treatment, we subjected mice to orthodontic traction and administered DMXAA subperiosteally around the moving teeth, with two injections given at three-day intervals (Fig. 3I). As shown in Fig. 3J and K, the expression levels of CCL5 and CXCL10 in the loaded alveolar bone exhibited a significant increase after DMXAA treatment compared to those in the non-treated group. However, the increase in the expression of CCL5 and CXCL10 induced by DMXAA was compromised when macrophages were depleted using clodronate liposomes, suggesting that macrophages are an important cellular source contributing to DMXAA-induced CCL5 and CXCL10 expression. Altogether, these results demonstrated that DMXAA stimulation drove macrophages to differentiate into the pro-inflammatory cells characterized by high expression of CCL5 and CXCL10.

Secretion of CCL5 and CXCL10 induced by DMXAA contributed to orthodontic tooth movement

Next, we explored whether the CCL5 and CXCL10 induced by DMXAA contributed in accelerating OTM. After CF was applied to BMDMs in combination with DMXAA treatment for 24 h, the culture supernatant (SN) of BMDMs were treated with or without specific antibodies against CCL5 and CXCL10 and then used to incubate immature osteoclasts (Fig. 4A). The number of cells differentiating into tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP)-positive multinuclear osteoclasts after incubation with supernatant (SN) without CCL5 and CXCL10 antibodies (Ab) was significantly greater than that of cells incubated with SN containing the antibodies (Fig. 4B and C). This suggests that neutralizing CCL5 and CXCL10 with these antibodies suppressed the differentiation of immature osteoclasts into multinucleated TRAP-positive cells. Moreover, the F-actin ring formation assay showed that the SN (without antibodies) promoted more efficient F-actin ring formation in immature osteoclasts compared with the SN containing the antibodies (Fig. 4D and E). To further evaluate the role of CCL5 and CXCL10 in accelerating OTM in vivo, we subjected mice to orthodontic traction and injected recombinant CCL5 and CXCL10 subperiosteally around the moving teeth. Our results showed that the tooth movement distance measured via Micro-CT on day 7 in the cytokines-injected mice was significantly greater than that in PBS-injected mice (Fig. 4F and G), but was less than that in DMXAA-injected mice. This result indicating that while CCL5 and CXCL10 were involved in accelerating OTM, additional DMXAA-driven mechanisms, such as small extracellular vesicles (sEVs)-mediated acceleration, may also contribute substantially.

Small extracellular vesicles derived from DMXAA-treated macrophages have a functional role in accelerating OTM

Recent studies have revealed that small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) or exosomes are involved in orthodontic tooth movement [22, 23].To explore whether sEVs derived from DMXAA-treated macrophages participate in OTM, we isolated sEVs from BMDM subjected to CF with or without DMXAA treatment. Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA) showed that sEVs from both DMXAA-treated (CF+DMXAA) and non-treated BMDMs (CF) had a similar particle size to those from mock-treated BMDMs (negative control, NC), suggesting that DMXAA did not impact the size of sEVs released from BMDMs (Fig. 5A). Moreover, compared to mock-treated BMDMs, we found no significant alteration in sEV secretion when BMDMs were subjected to CF or treated in combination with DMXAA, as determined by NTA (Fig. 5B), indicating that DMXAA did not affect the release of sEVs from BMDM. To further investigate the role of sEVs from DMXAA-treated BMDMs in OTM, GW4869, a widely used sEV/exosome secretion inhibitor, was added to the culture medium to suppress sEV secretion by BMDMs. Then the medium was collected and used to incubate immature osteoclasts (Fig. 5C). As shown in Fig. 5D and E, the levels of CCL5 and CXCL10 in the medium from DMXAA-treated BMDM (SN) were similar to those in the medium from BMDMs treated with both DMXAA and GW4869 (SNGW4869). However, although the number of TRAP-positive multinuclear osteoclasts after incubation with SNGW4869 or SN was similar (Fig. 5F and G), the size of TRAP-positive osteoclasts was much smaller after incubation with SNGW4869 than that after incubation with SN (Fig. 5G and H). To further evaluate the role of sEVs derived from DMXAA-treated BMDMs in accelerating OTM in vivo, we subjected mice to orthodontic traction and injected DMXAA either alone or in combination with GW4869 around the moving teeth. Our results showed that the tooth movement distance measured via Micro-CT on day 7 in the mice injected with DMXAA in combination with GW4869 (TM + GW4869 + DMXAA) was significantly less than that in the DMXAA-injected mice (Fig. 5I and J). In addition, the tooth movement distance in mice that received GW4869 alone (TM+GW4869) showed no significant alteration compared to that in non-treated (TM), suggesting that GW4869 alone had no impact on tooth movement under orthodontic traction. Taken together, our results demonstrated that sEVs released from DMXAA-treated macrophages contribute to the acceleration of OTM, primarily by enhancing osteoclast fusion and maturation, in complement to chemokine-driven effects on osteoclast differentiation. Rab13-enriched sEVs derived from DMXAA-stimulated macrophages promoted osteoclast fusion thereby facilitating their maturation

To further determine the mechanism of sEVs derived from DMXAA-treated BMDMs in accelerating orthodontic tooth movement, we performed quantitative proteomics analysis on sEVs from DMXAA-treated BMDMs versus the vesicles from non-treated cells under CF conditions. Quantitative proteomic analysis identified Rab13 as one of the most prominently enriched proteins in sEVs from DMXAA-treated BMDMs compared with control sEVs(Fig. 6A), and western blot analysis confirmed that Rab13 levels were higher in DMXAA-treated sEVs compared to non-treated controls, with no changes in the levels of standard sEV markers such as Alix, TSG101, and CD63 (Fig. 6B). To further investigate whether Rab13 enriched in sEVs contributes to osteoclast behavior, BMDMs were transfected with specific siRNA targeting Rab13 transcripts and then treated with DMXAA under CF conditions. The culture supernatants from transfected (SN-Rab13KD) or non-transfected (SN) BMDMs were collected and used to incubate immature osteoclasts (Fig. 6C). Upon incubation with sEVs from Rab13 knockdown BMDMs (SN-Rab13KD), the number of TRAP-positive multinucleated osteoclasts was comparable to the control (SN), but the size of the osteoclasts was significantly reduced (Fig. 6D, F). This suggested that Rab13 played an important role in the fusion and maturation of osteoclasts, yet it did not affect their initial differentiation. Furthermore, we also isolated the sEV fractions from either wild-type (sEVs) or Rab13 knockdown (sEVs-Rab13KD) BMDM treated with DMXAA. These sEV fractions were then added to immature osteoclasts in combination with CCL5 and CXCL10 (Fig. 6G). Western blot analysis revealed a decrease in the level of Rab13 in sEVs from DMXAA-treated BMDMs with Rab13 knockdown (Fig. 6H). When osteoclasts were incubated with sEVs from Rab13 knockdown BMDMs (sEVs-Rab13KD) and CCL5/CXCL10, their size was significantly smaller than those treated with wild-type sEVs and CCL5/CXCL10 (Fig. 6I, J), confirming that Rab13-enriched sEVs are crucial for osteoclast fusion and maturation. This result suggested that Rab13 in sEVs derived from DMXAA-treated BMDMs had a key role in regulating the fusion of osteoclasts. To confirm Rab13’s role in osteoclast fusion, we co-cultured equal numbers of BMDMs, labeled with DiO (green) or DiI (red) lipophilic dyes, and induced osteoclastogenesis with M-CSF and RANKL. The fused osteoclasts, visible as yellow/orange cells, were significantly larger in the presence of wild-type sEVs compared to those treated with sEVs-Rab13KD (Fig. 6K, L). As shown in Fig. 6L, the size of osteoclasts was significantly larger after incubation with sEVs combined with CCL5 and CXCL10 than that after incubation with sEVs-Rab13KD combined with CCL5 and CXCL10. Collectively, our results demonstrate that Rab13-enriched sEVs from DMXAA-stimulated BMDMs are essential for osteoclast fusion and maturation, but not for their initial differentiation. Rab13 mediates the functional role of sEVs in promoting osteoclast activity, enhancing the overall orthodontic tooth movement.

Discussion

The efficiency of OTM directly influences clinical outcomes. However, existing strategies for accelerating OTM have their limitations. Surgical approaches, such as corticotomy, are invasive and not well-accepted by patients [24, 25]. In contrast, non-surgical methods, like low-intensity laser therapy, lack consistent mechanistic evidence to support their efficacy [4]. Pharmacological interventions, especially those with well-established clinical safety profiles, present a promising alternative [1, 26, 27]. In this study, we demonstrated that DMXAA accelerated OTM through a multi-layered mechanism involving macrophage polarization, chemokine secretion, and small extracellular vesicle (sEV)-mediated osteoclast fusion (Fig. 7). Our findings highlight DMXAA as a promising non-invasive candidate for optimizing orthodontic treatment.

Schematic of DMXAA accelerated OTM by boosting macrophage-derived pro-inflammatory chemokines and Rab-13 enriched small extracellular vesicles. DMXAA accelerated OTM by recruiting macrophages, inducing a pro-inflammatory state with high CCL5 and CXCL10 secretion, and releasing Rab13 enriched sEVs that drove osteoclast fusion

The ability of DMXAA to enhance OTM is closely linked to its role in modulating immune responses [16]. The core mechanism of OTM involves dynamic coordination between osteoclast-mediated bone resorption and osteoblast-driven bone formation, which is closely related to the inflammatory microenvironment within periodontal ligament tissue. Our study suggested that through promoting the accumulation of macrophages at the force application site, DMXAA redirected these cells toward a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, characterized by the upregulated expression of CCL5 and CXCL10. Importantly, the initial inflammatory response is of crucial significance in launching OTM. DMXAA accelerates the process of bone remodeling by enhancing early inflammatory signaling to establish inflammatory microenvironment. Additionally, a primary consideration regarding the induction of M1-like polarization is the potential for periodontal tissue destruction. In our study, the local administration of DMXAA fusogenic licensing factor (administered every three days) promoted bone turnover without eliciting macroscopic root resorption or alveolar bone loss, as demonstrated by the intact root surface in Micro-CT analysis. This suggests that a transient, controlled amplification of the catabolic phase can enhance tooth movement efficiency while remaining within the physiological tolerance of the periodontal tissues. Nevertheless, long-term safety studies and an assessment of the dose - dependent manner are necessary for further evaluation.

Depletion of macrophages using clodronate abolished the acceleratory effect inducing by DMXAA, suggesting that macrophages are the key target cells for DMXAA to accelerate OTM. Recently, macrophages have come into prominence with the accumulated understanding of their multi-layered contributions to OTM. Previous research has established that macrophage polarization, cytokine secretion, their potential to differentiate into osteoclasts, and their crosstalk with osteoblasts play an integral role in OTM [21, 28, 29]. Therefore, macrophages represent a compelling therapeutic target due to their role as key regulatory cells. This provides a clear motivation for the development of various drugs to modulate this autologous force that impacts the rate of tooth movement. However, the drugs studied at this stage are mainly anti-inflammatory drugs, which play an important role in a small percentage of teeth that do not need to be moved clinically and function as a branch resistance, but even have a reverse effect in most of the teeth that need to be moved rapidly [30, 31]. Our study confirmed that local injection of DMXAA not only recruited an increased number of macrophages but also skewed macrophage polarization toward an M1-like proinflammatory phenotype by producing high levels of proinflammatory factors, particularly CCL5 and CXCL10.

CCL5 is known for their role in recruiting various immune cells to sites of inflammation. With respect to bone remodeling, CCL5 has been shown to induce chemotaxis of osteoclasts and osteoblasts in vitro, thereby suggesting a critical role in bone remodeling [32, 33]. Importantly, CCL5-deficient mice displayed decreased bone formation [34]. Moreover, the expression of CCL5 and its receptor CCR5, were observed to increase in response to compression or the application of compression or tension force during OTM [35]. Blockade of CCL5 receptor CCR5 using specific antibodies impaired osteoclast function in vitro. Mice deficient in CCR5 exhibited dysfunctional osteoclasts and were resistant to osteoporosis induced by RANKL [36]. These studies, combined with our results, revealed that CCR5-CCR5 ligands axis has an essential role in bone remodeling through the functional regulation of osteoclasts. Although there is a lack of evidence regarding the function role of chemokines CXCL10 in tooth movement, previous study found that CXCL10 could promote osteoclast differentiation and osteolytic bone metastasis in a mouse model of rheumatoid arthritis [37], suggesting CXCL10 was involved in in bone destruction.

In addition to soluble cytokines, sEVs derived from immune cells have been reported to play important roles in bone metabolism [38]. We observed that sEVs from DMXAA-treated macrophages did not alter osteoclast formation but significantly promoted osteoclast fusion, an effect dependent on Rab13 enrichment. This mechanism is particularly relevant given that osteoclast size correlates with resorptive activity [39]. Rab13, recognized for its function in membrane trafficking and vesicle tethering, has been shown to regulate the actin cytoskeleton by promoting actin polymerization [40–42]. Interestingly, several studies have observed that the expression of RAB13 increases during osteoclast differentiation, although its function during bone resorption remains unclear [43, 44]. Our study demonstrated that the sEVs enriched with Rab13 contributed to osteoclast fusion rather than to their formation. This study is among the first to identify sEV-delivered Rab13 functions not as a differentiation factor but as a fusogenic licensing factor. Additionally, it has been reported that Rab13 can control the formation of membrane protrusions, which contributes to cell migration and scattering [40, 45–47]. Base on those findings, it is not surprising that sEV carrying Rab13 was implicated in promoting osteoclast fusion, although the detailed molecular mechanisms in this process remain unclear. Our study suggested that DMXAA-induced sEVs complement cytokine-mediated osteoclastogenesis by enhancing cell fusion and maturation. This dual mechanism highlights the complex interplay between inflammatory signaling and vesicle-mediated communication in regulating bone remodeling during OTM. Future studies may explore whether macrophage-derived vesicle pathways can be safely and selectively manipulated to regulate orthodontic bone remodeling.

DMXAA has indeed been reported in previous studies as a vascular disrupting agent (VDA), particularly in tumor models, where it can induce vascular collapse and hemorrhagic necrosis within tumor tissues through activation of innate immune signaling pathways [15, 18]. In the context of OTM, vascular changes are also an integral component of periodontal tissue remodeling. Mechanical force applied during orthodontic treatment leads to vascular compression on the pressure side and vascular dilation on the tension side, which subsequently triggers inflammatory cell recruitment and osteoclast-mediated bone resorption [48, 49]. In our study, although we did not directly assess vascular density or endothelial responses, the enhanced recruitment of macrophages and the increased inflammatory microenvironment observed after DMXAA administration suggest that vascular-related inflammatory signaling may contribute to the observed biological effects. It is important to note that the vascular-disrupting activity of DMXAA reported in tumor models primarily affects abnormal tumor vasculature, whereas normal tissues are generally less susceptible to this effect. Consistent with this notion, in our study no obvious structural damage to the alveolar bone or tooth roots was observed in the Micro-CT analysis, suggesting that the local administration of DMXAA at the selected dose mainly modulated the immune microenvironment rather than causing detrimental vascular injury.

The capacity of DMXAA to accelerate OTM via immune modulation has important clinical implications. However, it is essential to recognize that DMXAA exerts its immunomodulatory effects predominantly via the binding of murine STING (mSTING), a structural interaction not conserved across human STING variants. This species-specific characteristic may account for the discontinuation of DMXAA as a human chemotherapeutic agent [50]. Nevertheless, within the framework of our study, DMXAA functions as a valuable pharmacological tool to illustrate that the downstream biological consequences, specifically, M1 polarization and the secretion of Rab13-enriched small extracellular vesicle, are effective mechanisms for accelerating OTM. Future translational strategies should strive to reproduce this downstream effect by employing human- compatible STING agonists (such as cyclic dinucleotides) or by directly utilizing Rab13-enriched sEVs as cell-free therapeutics.

Conclusions

Our findings elucidated that DMXAA accelerated OTM by recruiting macrophages, inducing a pro-inflammatory state with high CCL5/CXCL10 secretion, and releasing Rab13-enriched sEVs that drove osteoclast fusion, positioning DMXAA as a promising non-invasive therapeutic candidate for optimizing orthodontic treatment. However, further studies are required to evaluate long-term periodontal safety, dose dependency, and translational applicability in human orthodontic treatment.

Methods

Application of orthodontic devices and local injection

Eight-week-old C57BL/6J mice (male, 20–25 g) were used in this study. At this age, mice are skeletally mature but still exhibit active bone remodeling, making them suitable for orthodontic tooth movement studies. For the orthodontic device application, a nickel–titanium (NiTi) closed-coil spring (0.008 inch) were ligated between the left maxillary first molar and maxillary incisors of mice to induce mesial movement of molars. The orthodontic force generated by the NiTi coil spring was approximately 30 g, which is widely used in murine orthodontic tooth movement models [21, 51, 52]. The appliances were checked without anesthesia every 24 h and reinstalled immediately if they fell off. The mice were allowed free access to water and soft food after installation to relieve discomfort. For drug treatment, the concentration of DMXAA [53, 54] and GW4869 [22, 55] was selected based on previous studies evaluating its activity in murine models and our preliminary dose-optimization experiments. Each mouse was injected with 10 µL DMXAA (2.5 mg/mL, # HY-10964, MCE, China) or GW4869 (0.5 mg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) into the palatal gingiva of the loaded first maxillary molar utilizing a 33-gauge needle Hamilton syringe (Hamilton Company, USA) to ensure accurate delivery of the small injection volume. The injection site and volume were standardized for all animals and performed by the same operator to minimize experimental variability. For depletion of macrophages, mice received intravenous injection of liposome-encapsulated clodronate (200 µL/mouse, 5 mg/mL; Liposoma B.V., Netherlands). For cytokine treatment, each mouse was injected with 10 µL CCL5 (10 ng/mL, Peprotech, USA) and CXCL10 (10 ng/mL, Peprotech, USA) into the palatal gingiva of the loaded first maxillary molar. The same volume of PBS was injected as that for the controls. The mice were sacrificed for following experiments. The sample size was determined using power analysis (G*Power 3.1), indicating that n = 6 mice per group provides more than 80% statistical power (α = 0.05) to detect significant differences in tooth movement distance.

Micro-computed tomography scanning and 3D reconstruction

Mice maxillae were collected from each group and fixed in 4% para-formaldehyde for 2 days. Then maxillae were scanned using a Micro-CT scanner (vivaCT80, Switzerland) with a standard acquisition protocol (55 kV, 72 µA, and 15.6 μm voxel size). The Micro-CT data were imported into InVivoDental to carry out 3D reconstruction or were imported into CTAn v1.15.4.0 (Bruker) to perform quantitative analysis. The orthodontic tooth movement distance was determined by the most mesial point of the second molar crown and the most distal point of the first molar crown.

Histologic and immunofluorescent staining

Maxillae collected from sacrificed mice were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution (PFA) and then decalcified in 10% EDTA for 28 days. The dehydrated maxillae were embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 4 μm thick slices from the middle part (between the double black bars). Subsequently, the paraffin sections were stained using H&E staining for routine histology or tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining (#BC5400, Solarbio, China). Osteoclast activity was identified by TRAP-positive signs.

For tissue immunofluorescent staining, the samples were embedded in Tissue-Tek O.C.T and sectioned into 8 μm sections. Sections were washed with PBS for 30 min and blocked with 5% BSA for 30 min at room temperature, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies against F4/80 (#ab300421, Abcam, 1;500) overnight at 4 °C. Then the sections were washed with PBS and incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 555) secondary antibody (#ab150078, Abcam, 1:1000) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Beyotime, China). All images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany) and the representative images were selected randomly from each group.

Culture and treatment of bone marrow–derived macrophages

Bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated and cultured as described [7]. Briefly, mouse bone marrow cells were flushed out from the bone marrow cavity of femurs and tibias. The cells were then washed, resuspended, and seeded into culture plates in α-MEM medium added with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA), and 20 ng/ml M-CSF (Peprotech, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 5 days to generate BMDMs. For application of compressive force on cells, a sterile glass disk (17.1 g, 2 g/cm2) onto BMDMs growing adherently at 70% confluence in 6-well plate. This magnitude of compressive force has been widely used in in vitro orthodontic force models to mimic the pressure microenvironment during orthodontic tooth movement [56, 57]. Then BMDMs were treated with DMXAA at 10 µg/mL in combine with or without GW4869 at 10 µM for 24 h. Cells and the corresponding culture supernatants were collected for RNA expression analysis, ELISA or small EV isolation.

Small EV isolation and purification

Small EVs (sEVs) were isolated and purified from BMDM culture supernatants following previously established procedure with minor revised [58]. Briefly, cell supernatants were serially centrifuged at 2000 g for 20 min to remove dead cells and cellular debris, 10,000 g for 30 min to remove large vesicles or apoptotic bodies. Then, the supernatants were subjected to filter twice through 0.2 μm pore PES filter (Millipore, USA). The filtrated supernatant was subsequently ultracentrifuged at 100,000 g for 90 min to pellet the crude sEV fraction using Optima L-100 XP (Beckman Coulter). The crude sEV fractions were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and centrifuged again at 100,000 g for 90 min at 4 °C to remove soluble factors. Finally, the purified sEV pellets were resuspended in PBS for next analysis.

RNA sequencing and RT-qPCR

BMDM under compressive force with or without DMXAA treatment suspended in TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) for total RNA isolation following the manufacturer’s instructions. For RNA sequencing, the concentration, purity and integrity of the RNA are gauged by a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). A total amount of 2 µg RNA per sample was used as input material for the RNA sample preparations. Sequencing libraries were generated using VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (#NR604-01/02, Illumina) following the manufacture’s recommendations and index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. First strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primer and RNase H. Second strand cDNA synthesis was subsequently performed using buffer, dNTPs, DNA polymerase I and RNase H. And then, the double stranded cDNA was purified by AMPure P beads. The purified double stranded cDNA was repaired at the end, added a tail and connected to the sequencing connector, then the fragment size was selected, and finally the final cDNA library was obtained by PCR enrichment. The RNA libraries were sequenced on the performed on Novaseq 6000 S4 platform (Illumina) using NovaSeq 6000 S4 Reagent kit V1.5. DESeq2 v1.6.3 was designed for differential gene expression analysis between two samples. Genes with q ≤ 0.05 and |log2_ratio|≥1 are identified as differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

For RT-qPCR, the cDNA was reversely transcribed with the application of PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Q711-02; Vazyme, China) and subjected for quantitative real-time PCR on an ABI QuantStudio7 (Applied Biosystems). All reactions were carried out in triplicate and analysis was carried out using 2−ΔΔCt method. The sequences of the forward and reverse primers are listed following:

Proteomic analysis of sEVs

Small EVs were isolated from BMDM supernatants using the differential centrifugation procedure outlined previously. The protein concentration was quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (#23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteomic analysis for sEVs was carried out as previously described [59].

F-actin ring staining

For F-actin ring staining, osteoclast differentiation was induced using M-CSF and RANKL for 5 days in α-MEM medium. as described above. Culture supernatants from DMXAA-treated BMDM with or without containing antibodies of CCL5 (concentration: 0.2 µg/mL, #AF478, R&D systems) and CXCL10 (concentration: 0.5 µg/mL, #AF-466-NA, R&D systems) were incubated osteoclasts for 3 days. Then osteoclasts were fixed with 4% formaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X‐100. Cells were then incubated with 2 U/mL rhodamine‐phalloidin (Molecular Probes) at room temperature for 20 min. After washing with PBS, the cells were treated with DAPI and then photographed under a fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany).

Osteoclast differentiation and TRAP staining

Osteoclasts were differentiated by incubating BMDM with 20 ng/ml M-CSF (Peprotech, USA) and 35 ng/ml RANKL (Peprotech, USA) for 5 days in α-MEM medium. After various treatments, the cells were washed with PBS, fixed for 10 min with 4% paraformaldehyde. Osteoclast differentiation was measured by TRAP staining with the corresponding kit (#BC5400, Solarbio, China). TRAP-positive cells containing more than three nuclei (> 3) were termed as mature osteoclasts, and counted under a light microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

Small sEVs were measured using a Nano NS300 instrument (Malvern Instruments, UK) to track the size and number of sEVs. Samples were resuspended in PBS to reach an appropriate concentration range, and then manually pushed into the sample chamber. Laser light scattering was performed to visualize particles in collected samples using the conventional optical microscope perpendicular to the beam axis. The particles were recorded and analyzed based on Brownian motion and the diffusion coefficient. For each sample, three separate 60-second video recordings were acquired. These video data were then processed using NTA 2.3 software to compute the size of particles. The camera sensitivity was maintained consistent across all measurements to ensure data uniformity.

Western blot

Total protein of small EV fractions from various treatment of BMDMs were obtained using RIPA lysis buffer (Santa Cruz, USA) for 20 min on ice and then subjected to a centrifugation at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. Lysates were quantified by BCA assay (#23225,Thermo Fisher Scientific), boiled in 5×sample loading buffer (Beyotime, China), separated by 10% SDS-PAGE gels (Vazyme, China), and transferred onto PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk in TBST and then incubated with appropriate primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubating with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Santa Cruz, USA) for 1 h at room temperature and exposed using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS (Thermo Fisher scientific). Primary antibodies included the following: Alix (#ab186429, Abcam,1:1000), CD63(#ab134045, Abcam,1:1000), TSG101(#28283-1-AP, Proteintech,1:500), Rab13 (#PA5-13621, Thermo Fisher scientific, 1:500).

Cell labeling for fusion assay

Adherent BMDMs were removed from the dish by incubating with 1 mM EDTA in PBS, and the recovered cells were labeled with fluorescent dyes including lipophilic tracers DiI (1 µM, Sigma-Aldrich) or DiO (1 µM, Sigma-Aldrich). Equal number of DiI- and DiO-labeled cells were mixed and seeded on 12-well plates and cultured with RANKL and M-CSF. In the meantime, the cells were incubated with sEVs from DMXAA-treated BMDMs or Rab13 knockdown BMDMs at a final concentration of 100 ug/mL for 8 days. Cell fixed with 3.7% formaldehyde for confocal microscopy. The efficiency of multinucleation (syncytium formation) was analyzed under a fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany).

RNA interference

For siRNA transfection, BMDMs were transfected with Rab 13 siRNA (50 nM, #180513, Thermo Fisher scientific) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) for 48 h according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The DMXAA treatment was carried out 48 h after the cell transfection.

Flow cytometry

Mice tissues were minced using a gentleMACS Dissociator (gentle MACS) in ice-cold PBS containing 1% FBS, and then filtered through a 200-mesh nylon grid. Cells were subsequently washed with PBS and resuspended in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 10 min. Fixed cells were washed several times with PBS, followed by incubation in blocking buffer. Then cells were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against F4/80 (#123135; BioLegend,1:100) and CD11b (#17-0112-82; eBioscience,1:100) for 1 h at room temperature. After staining, cells were washed in PBS with EDTA. Flow cytometry were acquired by FACSAria II (BD Biosciences).

ELISA

The concentrations of CCL5 and CXCL10 in the culture supernatants from BMDM were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent asay (ELISA) kits (Elabscience Biotechnology Ltd., Co, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Replicate of each sample was measured and three independent experiments were performed. Optical density was measured at 450 nm.

Statistical analyses

All data were analyzed and expressed as mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego CA).

Two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to compare differences between two groups, while analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons between groups. The criterion of P < 0.05 was defined as the threshold for statistical significance, indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.
