Distinct genomic trajectory among invasive Salmonella Typhimurium ST313 infections
Authors: Chenghao Jia, Haiyang Zhou, Qianzhe Cao, Chenghu Huang, Hongli An, Yan Li, Lefu Lan, Min Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInvasive non-typhoidal Salmonella (iNTS) disease, primarily manifesting as bloodstream infections and dominated by Salmonella Typhimurium sequence type 313 (ST313), represents a major global health challenge, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. However, the transmission landscape and evolutionary driver of ST313 remain incompletely understood. Here, we compile the global dataset to date, comprising 11,361 human-derived iNTS genomes and 3115 ST313 genomes from diverse sources, enabling identification of a highly antimicrobial-resistant sublineage 2.4 and an emerging lineage 4. Phylogenomic analyses reveal patterns consistent with intercontinental dissemination of ST313, which may be associated with modernized human biomass movement. Sublineage 2.4, an antimicrobial-resistant offshoot that displaced lineage 1, harbors extended-spectrum β-lactamase genes, including blaOXA-1, blaCTX-M-15, often linked to mobilome, suggesting a potential trend of increasing resistance within lineage 2. Furthermore, mobilome-driven alterations in antimicrobial-resistant gene carriage coincide with the post-2006 decline of lineage 1 in Africa. Additional genetic changes may enhance invasiveness and competitive fitness , including enrichment of pseudogenes and positively selected functional genes, which may have facilitated replacement by newly identified ST313 clone. Together, Our findings highlight lineage-specific selection pressures shaped by anthropogenic factors, such as antimicrobial use and stewardship, that modulate ST313 evolution and underscore the need for enhanced surveillance.Subject terms: Bacteria, Genomics, Bacterial infection, Population genetics

Introduction

Invasive bacterial bloodstream infections pose a significant threat to global health, particularly in resource-limited settings. The World Health Organization (WHO) prioritizes Salmonella species among the top pathogens posing significant risks for gastrointestinal and systemic disorders in humans1–3. Invasive non-typhoidal Salmonella (iNTS) diseases, defined as infections that invade normally sterile sites such as blood or cerebrospinal fluid (excluding fecal sources), are especially alarming due to their mimicry of typhoid fever and high case fatality rates4,5. Recent estimates from the Global Burden of Disease 2019 study indicate that iNTS causes ~594,000 cases (95% uncertainty interval [UI] 486,000–718,000) annually worldwide, leading to 79,000 deaths (95% UI 43,000–124,000) and 6.11 million disability-adjusted life years (95% UI 3.32–9.71)6. Notably, Incidence rates often exceed 100 cases per 100,000 person-years among high-risk populations, including people living with HIV, those with malnutrition, and individuals at the extremes of age (<5 years and ≥70 years)4,7. Despite this substantial burden, the overall global landscape of iNTS remains incompletely understood, hindered by limited surveillance across regions.

Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium (S. Typhimurium) emerges as a primary etiological agent of iNTS disease8. In sub-Saharan Africa, the S. Typhimurium sequence type 313 (ST313) clade predominates, accounting for a substantial proportion of cases and being associated with severe manifestations such as septicemia9,10. Genome-based studies have identified at least four major lineages within ST313—lineage 1 (L1), lineage 2 (L2) with sublineages 2.0–2.3, lineage 3 (L3), and a UK-associated lineage—highlighting ongoing diversification11–14. Nevertheless, the complete global population structure, intercontinental dissemination patterns, and the potential for zoonotic or cross-host transmission remain only partially known.

Emergence of dominant bacterial variants, including within ST313, occurs through mutational processes, horizontal gene transfer, and gene loss, often under selective pressures favoring traits like increased transmissibility, virulence, or immune evasion15. Prior research underscores the pivotal role of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), mediated by acquired resistance genes (ARGs), in lineage dynamics and replacement within ST31316. For instance, chloramphenicol resistance, largely restricted to L2, likely facilitated L1 displacement by L2 around 200117. Since 2005, amid rising use of third-generation cephalosporins and azithromycin, ST313 isolates in Africa—particularly L2 strains—have exhibited adaptive resistance signatures to first-line antibiotics18,19. Beyond AMR, progressive genome degradation—evidenced by the accumulation of pseudogenes and alterations in functional gene expression—has been implicated in enhancing the invasive potential of ST31320–22. These findings highlight existing knowledge gaps in constructing a comprehensive genomic landscape of ST313 to understand its evolutionary trajectory, the driving forces behind its adaptation and the design of precise disease interventions.

To address these gaps, we curated the largest global iNTS genomic dataset to date, comprising 11,361 human-derived genomes from non-fecal clinical sources, and in parallel compiled a partially overlapping collection of 3,115 ST313 isolates collected between 1966 and 2023. Leveraging this unique resource, we aimed to: (1) delineate the global landscape of iNTS, with a particular focus on ST313; (2) characterize its population structure, patterns of intercontinental dissemination, and evidence for cross-host transmission at single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) resolution; and (3) investigate the roles of AMR, mobilome dynamics, and other genomic factors in driving lineage replacement within ST313.

Results

The global landscape of the iNTS collection

Analysis of 11,361 publicly available iNTS genomes (Supplementary Data 1) identifies Africa as the predominant region of iNTS sampling (n = 4741; 41.7%), followed by North America (n = 4351; 38.3%). Although Asia accounts for fewer samples (n = 1350; 11.9%), these were primarily from China (1173 of 1350; 86.9%) (Figure. S1a). Calculations of the sampling rates revealed an overall upward trend across countries, with the largest increase observed in South Africa (Figure. S1b). Serovar classification underscored the predominance of S. Typhimurium (n = 3260, 28.7%) and S. Enteritidis (n = 3785, 33.3%). Interestingly, in our dataset, S. Typhimurium samples predominated before 2015, but after 2015, S. Enteritidis became predominant (Figure. S1c). Next, to examine the details of this shift within S. Typhimurium, we used multilocus sequence typing (MLST) to identify three major circulating sequence types (STs): ST313 (n = 1763), ST19 (n = 1140), and ST34 (n = 237). Among these, the temporal changes in the available ST313 genomes from public databases closely mirrored the post-2015 decline in S. Typhimurium sampling (Figure. S1d–e).

Next, by assembling all publicly available ST313 genomic data, we constructed the most extensive global dataset of ST313 genomes to date, comprising 3115 isolates (Supplementary Data 2). Our results show that, geographically, ST313 predominated among isolates sampled in Africa (n = 2266; 72.7%), with a growing but still limited sampling presence in Europe (n = 260; 8.3%) (Fig. 1a, b). From a host perspective, most isolates were human-derived (n = 2027, 65.1%), with rare detections in animals (n = 15, 0.5%) and food (n = 8, 0.3%) (Fig. 1c). Notably, among human isolates, blood samples were the primary source (1702/2027, 84%), far exceeding fecal isolates (96/2027, 4.7%) (Fig. 1d). Only a small number of ST313 isolates contained sex information (n = 440), of which more than 99% were reported to have been collected at Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital (Blantyre, Malawi)14. Overall, the sex distribution was balanced, with slightly more male cases (n = 226) than female (n = 214) (Fig. 1e).

Global population diversity of ST313 uncovers new lineages

Lineage assignment, based on previous studies13,14, identified five primary lineages: L1 (296/3115), L2 (2273/3115), L3 (164/3115), L4 (230/3115), and L5 (152/3115) (Fig. 2a). Among these, L1 and L2 predominated in Africa. Within L2, phylogenetic analysis revealed a novel sublineage beyond 2.0–2.3, designated 2.4 (61/3115). The majority of isolates in sublineage 2.4 originated from Kenya (58/61, 95.1%), with two isolates also from the UK (Fig. 2b). Nearly half of L3 genomes (79/164, 48%) were from the Americas. L4, a previously unreported and newly defined lineage here, was concentrated in Africa (159/230, 69.1%). Interestingly, L5, previously referred to as the UK-isolated lineage, included isolates from Australia and Japan. To further assess whether the newly identified sublineage 2.4 and L4 represent single clonal expansion events, we calculated their within-lineage genetic diversity. Pairwise SNP distances within sublineage 2.4 ranged from 0–50 nucleotides (mean: 19.1), whereas L4 showed substantially greater divergence, ranging from 0 to 510 nucleotides (mean: 173.4). These findings indicate that neither sublineage 2.4 nor L4 arose from a single clonal expansion event (Figure. S2).

Inference of historical changes in the effective population sizes (Ne) showed distinct declines for L1, L2, L3, and L5. Specifically, L1 exhibited a sharp reduction around the year 2000 (Figure. S3b), while declines in L2 and L3 occurred later, approximately around 2010 (Figure. S4b and Figure. S5b), and L5 around 2017. By contrast, the newly characterized sublineage 2.4 has expanded continuously since its emergence, while L4 has maintained relatively high Ne since 2000 (Fig. 2e).

Phylogeographic analysis suggested that the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of L1 and L2 originated in Africa, approximately around 1950 (95% highest posterior density [HPD]: 1922–1966) and 1960 (95% HPD: 1955–1965), respectively (Figure. S3a and Figure. S4a). The earliest inferred potential transcontinental transmission event for L2 occurred around 1996 (95% HPD: 1995–1998), while the earliest inferred potential transcontinental transmission event for L1 occurred slightly later, around 2006 (95% HPD: 2003–2009). The earliest transcontinental transmission events of both lineages were potentially associated with Africa and Europe (Figure. S3c and Figure. S4c).

The MRCA of L3, L4, and L5 originated outside Africa. Specifically, the MRCA of L4 is estimated to have appeared around 1731 (95% HPD: 1575–1859) in Asia (Fig. 2c). In contrast, the MRCA of L3 emerged slightly later, around 1874 (95% HPD: 1787–1932) in Asia (Figure. S5a). The MRCA of L5 likely appeared around 1965 (95% HPD: 1943–1982) in Europe (Fig. 2d). The earliest potential transcontinental transmission events of these lineages appear to have occurred around 1899 (95% HPD: 1839–1941) in L3 strains, between Europe and Asia (Figure. S5c). The earliest potential transcontinental transmission events for the newly defined L4 occurred around 1908 (95% HPD: 1864–1946), possibly between Asia and Africa geographically. Finally, the earliest potential transcontinental transmission events for L5 occurred the latest, around 1993 (95% HPD: 1984–2002) (Fig. 2f).

Human-borne ST313 transmission predominates in lineage 2

To identify potential recent transmission events of ST313, we conducted an SNP distance analysis using a 3-SNP threshold as the optimal cutoff (see the “Methods” section; Fig. 3a and Figure. S6). This identified 1,599 clusters with pairwise distances below the cutoff; 454 clusters (1,970/3,115 isolates) with ≥2 isolates (Fig. 3b). Four clusters (Clusters 357, 342, 131, and 49) showed potential intercontinental transmission events (Fig. 3c). The primary potential recent intercontinental transmission events were concentrated within cluster 357, predominantly associated with lineage 2. These potential transmission events (n = 8) occurred between the Americas (United States) and Africa (Congo), with the estimated timeframe spanning 2019 to 2020. These potential events involved six isolates in total: two from the United States and four from Congo. The geographic information for the Congo isolates was cross-validated against published literature, whereas the isolates from the United States have not yet been reported (Supplementary Data 3). Fewer potential transmission events occurred between Europe (UK) and Africa (Nigeria, n = 3) (Fig. 3d), involving four isolates with well-documented sampling backgrounds (Supplementary Data 3). Considering that these isolates belong to the same cluster and were collected within a relatively short time frame (less than three years), we speculate that the putative recent transmission events represented by these ST313 strains may be associated with international travel; however, alternative exposure routes, such as foodborne transmission through consumption of contaminated food products, cannot be excluded.

Although ST313 has been considered a lineage restricted to human-to-human transmission23, analysis of 454 clusters identified a potential cross-host transmission event within a single cluster (Cluster 453), possibly involving human–canine transmission in the UK (Figure. S7). Despite this observation, potential cross-host transmission events remain extremely rare, accounting for only a single case in our dataset.

Mobilome differences distinguishing ST313 lineages

To characterize genomic differences—particularly within the accessory genome—among the newly identified ST313 lineages, we examined mobile genetic elements (MGEs) previously reported to be carried by ST313, including plasmids (pBT1–pBT3, pSLT-BT, pSLT, pAnK), prophages (BTP1, BTP3, BTP5, BTP6, Gifsy-1, Gifsy-2), transposons (Tn21), and Salmonella pathogenicity islands (SPIs; see “Methods”). The pSLT was highly conserved across all lineages, consistent with previous reports14. In the newly discovered L4, we observed high carriage rates of pSLT (221/230, 96.1%) and pBT2 (186/230, 80.9%), similar to those in L5. Additionally, sublineage 2.4 exhibited high carriage rates of pSLT (57/61, 93.4%), pBT2 (60/61, 98.4%), and pBT3 (61/61, 100%) (Fig. 4a).

Prophage BTP1, BTP3, BTP5, BTP6, Gifsy-1, and Gifsy-2 are prevalent in the newly discovered L4 and sublineage 2.4. Furthermore, these prophages appear to be lineage-specific within ST313. For example, the carriage rate of BTP3 in the L1 population is only 11.8% (35/296), whereas L3 and L5 rarely carry BTP1 and BTP5. Regarding SPIs, only SPI-5 and SPI-6 are lineage-specific within the ST313 population: SPI-5 is highly prevalent in L1, whereas the carriage rate of SPI-6 in L1 is relatively low (48.3%, 143/296). Previous studies have shown that Tn21 confers the ability to acquire additional antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) upon ST313, and our results indicate that Tn21 is primarily carried by L1 (93%, 275/296) and L2 (72%, 1635/2273), which aligns with prior reports14 (Fig. 4a).

To further investigate the presence of other potentially prevalent MGEs among ST313, we performed sequence alignments between the ST313 genome and a larger-scale MGE database (Figure. S8-S10; Supplementary Data 4). This analysis revealed additional lineage-specific MGEs that were previously unreported (Figure. S11). Specifically, Tn6205 (92%, 2091/2273), Tn6292 (90.1%, 2,047/2273), and Tn9 (84.4%, 1919/2273) were highly prevalent in L2, whereas sublineage 2.4 additionally carried Tn602 (100%, 61/61), Tn2012 (96.7%, 59/61), Tn5057 (96.7%, 59/61), and Tn5718 (63.9%, 39/61). Integron In498 exhibited a carriage rate exceeding 60% exclusively in L1 and sublineage 2.4. Notably, prophage 118970_sal3 was highly prevalent across all ST313 lineages, and plasmid classification by replicon type indicated that IncHI2A/rep_cluster_1088-type plasmids were highly prevalent in sublineage 2.4.

Changes in AMR levels are associated with the ST313 lineage shift

AMR could influence the dynamics of bacterial population structure. Our identification of AMR determinants in ST313 revealed that both ARGs and AMR-associated point mutations predominantly occurred in L1 and L2 (Figure. S12; Supplementary Data 5). Sublineage 2.1 is reported as a multidrug-resistance (MDR) branch, conferring ARGs with extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) production and azithromycin resistance. Unlike sublineage 2.1, sublineage 2.4 additionally carries blaOXA-1, blaCTXM-15, and tet(A), indicating ongoing AMR evolution in L2. Moreover, a lineage-specific preference for certain ARGs was observed. For instance, the dfrA23 gene conferring trimethoprim resistance was more frequently carried by L1 than L224,25. In contrast, sul2 (sulfonamide resistance gene) and catA1 (chloramphenicol resistance gene) had higher carriage rates in L226,27. The occurrence of AMR-associated point mutations in ST313 is relatively low, with gyrA being slightly more common than gyrB, but overall occurring in only 4.9% (n = 153) of isolates.

The overall MDR rate among ST313 is 63.76% (1986/3115), primarily in L1 (34/296, 11.49%) and L2 (1949/2273, 85.75%), with the highest prevalence in Africa (1542/1986, 77.64%). Notably, the emergence of MDR varies significantly. Unlike L2, which exhibited MDR almost concurrently with its initial emergence, L1 appeared to acquire MDR only post-2000 in Africa (Figure. S3a and Figure. S4a).

A prior study reported that the cat gene was one of the key drivers behind the replacement of L1 by L2 around 2001, which led to the subsequent successful expansion of L2 in Africa17. We also observed a strong concordance between the prevalence of ARGs within L1 and its population dynamics (Spearman ρ = 0.74, p < 0.05, Fig. 5a). To investigate whether the decline of L1 after 2006 was also associated with changes in ARG carriage, we analyzed the temporal trends of 16 types of ARGs carried by L1. Each ARG had a carriage count exceeding 15 (Figure. S13). Further analysis identified six key ARGs, including mcr-9, tet(D), aph(3’)-Ia, sul2, aph(6)-Id, and aph(3”)-Ib, correlating with post-2006 decline (correlation coefficients > 0.6, p < 0.05, Fig. 5, c and d).

To further test our hypothesis that the decline in AMR may have contributed to the disappearance of L1 post-2006, we focused on Malawi, the central source region for L1, and modeled the frequency of ARG transfer. Horizontal ARG transfer frequency modeling showed high rates pre-2000 ( ~ 5.4), rising to 2004 amid L1 expansion, then declining post-2005 to half pre-2000 levels by 2010 (Fig. 5b).

Mobilome links with AMR changes in L1 and L2

Our recent study has demonstrated that the mobilome drivers play a critical role in ARG dynamics in Salmonella28. To explore this in ST313, we found that the capacity of the mobilome to contain ARGs varies significantly. Specifically, plasmids exhibit the highest average number of ARGs (n = 5.77), followed by transposons (n = 5.32), integrons (n = 4.23), and prophages (n = 0.02) (Fig. 4b). L2 mobilomes carried more ARGs than L1; sublineage 2.4 plasmids averaged 12.3 ARGs versus 7.3 in other L2 (Fig. 4, c and d; Supplementary Data 6).

The three highly prevalent ARGs—blaTEM-1B, sul1, and ant(3”)-Ia—detected in both L1 and L2, blaTEM-1B was found to be primarily associated with IncFIB/IncFII-type and IncFIB/IncFII/IncQ1-type plasmids. The sul1 is distributed across diverse MGEs, including In1012, In240, Tn6205, IncFIB/IncFII/IncQ1-type plasmids, IncFIB/IncFII-type plasmids, and various prophages (e.g., PWH3a_P1, BcepMu, Escher_520873). Interestingly, ant(3″)-Ia is co-located on In1012, In240, and Tn6205, highlighting the pivotal roles of these MGEs in shaping AMR dynamics in ST313 (Fig. 4 e–h).

Additionally, we investigated the six key ARGs (mcr-9, tet(D), aph(3’)-Ia, sul2, aph(6)-Id, and aph(3”)-Ib) associated with the post-2006 decline of L1 and three key ARGs (blaOXA-1, blaCTX-M-15, and tet(A)) potentially linked to sublineage 2.4 expansions, highlighting IncHI2A/rep_cluster_1088-type resistance plasmids as the key driver (Figure. S14a). Meanwhile, the carriage rates of IncHI2A/rep_cluster_1088-type resistance plasmids in L1 and sublineage 2.4 exhibited consistency with population dynamics (Figure. S14b). By comparing these plasmids from Kenyan isolates with previously reported IncHI2A plasmids in ST313, we found that the IncHI2A/rep_cluster_1088-type resistance plasmids from Kenyan isolates additionally carried an insertion sequence—likely mediated by Tn21—containing blaOXA-1, blaCTX-M-15, and tet(A) (Figure. S15).

Gene gain-or-loss might reshape distinct pathways in ST313 lineages

Although AMR correlated with L1 and L2 shifts, this was less evident in lineages L3-L5, which harbor few ARGs. Bacterial virulence, calculated as invasiveness based on genome degradation, may be involved in this process. To evaluate this, we examined the invasiveness index and found a progressive increase in scores from L1 to L3 (L1: 0.128, L2: 0.132, L3: 0.184), then decreased to L4 (0.131) and L5 (0.129) (Fig. 6a; Supplementary Data 7), corroborated by pseudogene-associated SNPs across different lineages (Figure. S16; Supplementary Data 8). Invasiveness correlated with annual prevalence in L4 (Spearman ρ = 0.67, p < 0.05) and L5 (Spearman ρ = 0.81, p < 0.05) (Fig. 6b).

To explore other genetic determinants for the association study, we conducted a pan-genome analysis and found that genes located in the accessory regions exhibited different patterns (Fig. 6c). Specifically, we identified the largest number of differentially associated genes in L2 (n = 1526), followed by L1 (n = 982), L4 (n = 710), L3 (n = 689), and L5 (n = 623). To confirm the association between these differential genes and the observed population dynamics, we performed a correlation analysis on individual genes. Our results revealed that most significantly correlated genes exhibit positive correlations (Spearman ρ > 0.6, p < 0.05, Fig. 6d). To elucidate the biological functions of these positively correlated genes, we performed functional enrichment analysis Fig. 6e). The findings indicated that the positive correlation genes enriched in L2 predominantly belonged to pathways associated with replication, recombination, and repair (Supplementary Data 9). In particular, intragenic segments associated with the transposase gene tnpR, which is related to transposons such as Tn2129, have been identified. Additionally, the rusA gene, located within a genomic island, has been shown to enhance Salmonella survival through parenteral transmission30. These determinants might collectively drive ST313 lineage shifts.

Discussion

A comprehensive understanding of the epidemiological trends and evolutionary drivers of iNTS disease is essential for developing targeted prevention and control strategies. In this study, we assembled the global dataset of iNTS genomes to date (n = 11,361), using isolation-source information and established epidemiological definitions of iNTS to delineate global patterns. However, it is important to note that our global iNTS genomic collection included only isolates with clearly defined sources; thus, some potential iNTS strains that were not explicitly reported may have been overlooked. This likely reflects variations in clinical reporting and sampling practices across different regions. Our analysis suggests that the currently available iNTS genomes predominantly originate from Africa and North America, with rapid expansion observed between 2000 and 2017. S. Typhimurium and S. Enteritidis may have emerged as the leading serovars, with ST313 of S. Typhimurium potentially accounting for most cases prior 2015. Given the limited capacity for pathogen isolation and identification in resource-constrained settings, the actual global burden of iNTS infections is likely underestimated31.

Next, we assembled a focused dataset of 3,115 rigorously quality-controlled ST313 genomes spanning diverse geographic and temporal origins. In contrast to the wider iNTS landscape, ST313 infections appear restricted to sub-Saharan Africa, with sporadic detections elsewhere potentially attributable to human-mediated dispersal. Among isolates with annotated sources, 84% were recovered from human blood, highlighting ST313’s pronounced invasive potential and association with bacteremia and other systemic infections—a profile distinct from gastroenteritis-associated sequence types like ST19 and ST34. Although rare (0.5% from animals and 0.3% from food sources), non-human ST313 isolations were detected. Refined SNP-distance–based tracing that incorporated isolation time as epidemiological support (≤3 SNPs and ≤3 years apart) suggested potential cross-host transmission within the UK-associated cluster 353. Such patterns are compatible with infrequent reverse zoonosis, including possible human–canine transmission. However, sampling bias should be considered, as current ST313 collections are predominantly derived from human clinical samples, and its prevalence in sick animals has received insufficient attention. This raises the possibility that ST313 may possess a broader host range than previously recognized, underscoring the need for further investigation from a One Health perspective.

Prior phylogenomic classifications delineated ST313 into lineages L1, L2 (with sublineages 2.0–2.3), L3, and a UK-associated lineage, often based on geographically limited collections32. Expanding on this, our analysis unveils two novel variants: the emerging lineage L4 and the MDR sublineage 2.4 within L2. Bayesian phylodynamic modeling, estimates L4’s MRCA origin around 1731 (95% HPD: 1575–1859) in Asia. Interestingly, the earliest potential transcontinental transmission events for L4 might have occurred around 1908 (95% HPD: 1864–1946). Compared to the MRCA divergence time, L4 appears to have persisted in Asia as a lineage with limited prevalence for a considerable period. Unlike lineages showing recent contractions, L4 maintained elevated effective population sizes in Africa post-1980–2000 expansion, signaling successful adaptation and necessitating vigilant monitoring. These discoveries advance our grasp of ST313’s diversification and underscore the role of global connectivity in pathogen evolution.

AMR exerts a profound selective pressure on ST313 evolution, as evidenced by historical lineage replacements14. Chloramphenicol, which served as an empirical therapy for ST313 in Africa until its replacement around 200117, promoted resistance acquisition—predominantly in L2 via ARGs such as catA1 (88% carriage rate)—facilitating lineage displacement from L1 to L2. Our AMR profiling, stratified by year of isolation, demonstrates a robust correlation between the post-2006 contraction of L1 and reduced carriage of ARGs such as mcr-9, tet(D), aph(3’)-Ia, sul2, aph(6)-Id, and aph(3”)-Ib. This pattern is consistent with the decline in ARG horizontal transfer frequencies observed among L1 isolates from Malawi. We further show that loss of key AMR drivers, such as Tn6205 and IncHI2A/rep_cluster_1088-type plasmids, is a major contributor to this phenomenon. Interestingly, this temporal alignment with Malawi’s post-2005 shift in clinical antimicrobial policies supports AMR modulation as a driver of lineage turnover, extending the evidence for resistance-fueled expansions.

Conversely, L4 and L5 harbored very few ARGs, and their population dynamics may instead be linked to increased invasiveness driven by genome degradation. In addition, unbiased pan-genome profiling revealed lineage-specific accessory genes—particularly those involved in post-translational modification, protein turnover, and chaperone functions—that may enhance the persistence of L4. Pathways related to energy production and conversion, as well as cell cycle control, cell division, and chromosome partitioning, may in turn support the persistence of L5. Future functional validation will be essential to elucidate these adaptive mechanisms in ST313.

This study has limitations. First, disparities in surveillance and reporting are likely to bias prevalence estimates toward specific regions. Because the available data are derived from WGS rather than reported case counts, this issue becomes even more apparent when assembling a global iNTS genomic dataset comprising 11,361 isolates. To quantify this sampling bias, we examined the relationship between a country’s economic level (measured as GDP per capita) and its corresponding sample count (Figure. S17). The results indicate that countries with greater public health infrastructure and resources—particularly the USA, Canada, and China—appear to have higher recorded incidences than those with more limited resources. At the same time, regions with fewer resources—such as Malawi, Congo, and South Africa—also seem to show higher incidences. This pattern reflects existing large, well-funded studies conducted specifically to investigate the burden of iNTS in selected African countries. In addition, outbreak-driven sampling and unsampled intermediate transmission chains may introduce similar sampling biases. Second, our focus on genome-based transmission features excluded host factors such as HIV status or other comorbidities33,34. Finally, genomic evidence suggesting potential intercontinental transmission of ST313 may also suggest the role of human travel or the global distribution of food products, rather than direct bacterial dissemination35. Genome-based links alone cannot definitively distinguish person-to-person transmission from human-mediated dispersal across continents. Incomplete sampling may lead to the omission of intermediate transmission processes (e.g., one or more transmission steps, repeated introductions from a shared reservoir, or shared exposure). Therefore, epidemiological linkage data will be essential for inferring discrete transmission events in future studies. Nevertheless, by harnessing the most comprehensive iNTS and ST313 genomic collections to date, we deliver an integrated spatiotemporal framework, uncover novel lineages, and illuminate evolutionary pressures shaping this pathogen. These findings advocate for expanded, multisectoral surveillance encompassing non-African regions and animal reservoirs to capture emerging threats. Ultimately, such insights pave the way for refined global strategies to mitigate iNTS morbidity and mortality.

Methods

Assembly of the global iNTS genomic dataset

In this genomic epidemiology study, iNTS was defined as non-typhoidal and non-paratyphoidal Salmonella isolates obtained from human specimens of non-fecal origin, including blood, urine, abscesses, and other sterile sites, as previously defined in the literature4. We subsequently retrieved all available Salmonella genomes from EnteroBase and GenBank (data cutoff: March 1, 2024; total number: 488,232). For metadata, when BioSample information was available in EnteroBase, we obtained the corresponding metadata directly from the associated BioSample record, unless the isolate was deposited exclusively in EnteroBase without a linked BioSample entry. We excluded all strains with ambiguous isolation sources—for example, those labeled only as “clinical” or “human”—and retained only those isolates with clearly defined sources that met the above criteria for iNTS. Information on the country and date of isolation was also collected. This process resulted in a final dataset of 11,361 iNTS genomes (Supplementary Data 1).

For the global iNTS genomic dataset constructed above, we further confirmed the species identity as Salmonella using KmerFinder v3.0.236, and serovar determination was performed with SISTR v1.1.237 and SeqSero2 v1.3.138. In terms of isolation sources, most isolates were recovered from blood (n = 7922; 69.7%) and urine (n = 2971; 26.2%). Additional isolates primarily originated from abscesses (n = 116), wounds (n = 81), cerebrospinal fluid (n = 74), the respiratory tract (n = 36), and synovial fluid (n = 30) (Figure. S18).

Assembly of the ST313 genomic dataset

Next, we constructed a global dataset comprising 3,115 ST313 genomes (Supplementary Data 2). We not only included human-derived ST313 data but also incorporated isolates from animals, plants, and those with an “Unknown” source label. Through extensive literature searches, we confirmed that 1584 of the 3115 ST313 isolates have been explicitly reported in 20 previously published studies (Figure. S19). Genome quality was assessed using the European Reference Laboratory’s Salmonella genomic quality control standards39. A Python3 script was employed to exclude genomes with over 500 contigs and an N50 value less than 30,000, as well as those with a GC content outside the 51%–53% range or a genome size between 4.2 and 5.5 MB.

Furthermore, we verified the completeness and coverage of each ST313 genome. Specifically, genome coverage was assessed using QUAST v5.3.040. The tool was run with the fully sequenced D23580 genome as the reference to compute alignment-based metrics, including genome fraction, which quantifies the proportion of the reference genome aligned by the assembly contigs. Genome completeness was evaluated using BUSCO v6.041, which assesses the presence of near-universal single-copy orthologs in lineage-specific datasets. Analyses were performed in genome mode for prokaryotic sequences, using Prodigal for gene prediction. The enterobacteriaceae_odb dataset, comprising 874 marker genes, was selected to ensure taxon-specific accuracy for the bacterial group under study. To ensure the reliability of downstream analyses, including those involving MGE detection, we retained only genomes with both coverage and completeness exceeding 95% (Figure. S20).

Next, we used SISTR v1.1.2 and SeqSero2 v1.3.1 to determine the serovar of all 3,115 genomes, ensuring they were classified as S. Typhimurium. Additionally, MLST was performed using MLST v2.22 with the senterica_achtman_2 scheme42. Only strains identified as ST313 were included in subsequent analyses. Geographic visualization was performed using the online platform MapChart (https://www.mapchart.net).

Phylogenetic and population structure analyses

Core-genome single-nucleotide polymorphisms (cgSNPs) were identified among 3,115 ST313 isolates using Snippy v4.4.5. To maintain consistency with established practices in ST313 population structure definitions, the D23580 strain (ERS2705687) was utilized as the reference genome14. Then, Gubbins v2.3.443 was used to remove cgSNPs located within recombination regions from the aligned cgSNPs file, which contained a total of 9238 loci. After filtering, 8902 high-quality cgSNPs were retained for phylogenetic analysis. The optimal model (TVM + F + ASC + R2) was determined and used to construct the maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree using IQ-TREE v1.6.244 with 1000 bootstraps. The final phylogenetic tree was annotated and visualized using iTOL v6.045.

A total of 3115 global assemblies of ST313 underwent population structure analysis using RhierBAPS v1.1.446, based on the Hierarchical Bayesian clustering algorithm and employing aligned cgSNPs as input. The calculation of population structure utilized R package v4.3.1, supplemented by the R packages “phytools” v2.0.3, “ape” v5.7.1, and “rhierbaps” v1.1.4. Specifically, the aligned ST313 cgSNP sequences were processed with the “hierBAPS” function, with the parameters set to a maximum depth of 2 (max.depth = 2) and an initial number of populations of 20 (n.pops = 20). This hierarchical approach partitions the dataset into nested clusters, with level 1 representing major lineages and level 2 defining sublineages.

Temporal signal and randomization test

We assessed the presence of significant temporal signals in the different ST313 lineages using date-randomization tests (DRT). First, recombination regions were removed from each lineage’s genome using Gubbins v2.3. To identify and exclude isolates with inconsistent sampling dates, we applied TreeTime v0.1147 to perform root-to-tip regression analysis (strains whose sampling dates deviated substantially from the regression line, indicating discrepancies between genetic divergence and sampling time). These outliers were excluded before conducting the formal Bayesian Evolutionary Analysis of Sampled Trees (BEAST)48 to ensure robustness (Figure. S21). Subsequently, we used BEAUti to generate XML-formatted input files for DRT and the R package TipDatingBeast49 to randomize tip dates, producing 20 randomized replicates alongside the original dataset. Each of the 21 datasets was analyzed in BEAST2 using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) with 1,000,000 iterations and sampling every 1000 steps. Substitution rates were estimated from all runs using BactDating v1.150. A significant temporal signal was inferred if the substitution rate from the original dataset did not overlap with those from the randomized datasets. Significant temporal signals were detected in all lineages.

Population dynamics and phylogeographical analysis of ST313

The strains in the dataset were subjected to phylogeographical reconstruction and inference of effective population sizes using BEAST v2.5. To determine the optimal model, we tested a total of six combinations in the initial phase of our study. These combinations included the strict clock, relaxed lognormal clock, and three population models (Bayesian SkyGrid, Bayesian Skyline, and Constant Size). Before conducting the full BEAST analysis, we evaluated each combination using a MCMC analysis with a total chain length of 100 million and sampling every 10,000 iterations. We then summarized the results using NSLogAnalyser and determined the optimal model based on the marginal likelihood value for each combination. The results indicated that the model incorporating the Bayesian Skyline and the relaxed lognormal clock yielded the highest marginal likelihood value in our sample. Consequently, we performed a time-calibrated Bayesian phylogenetic inference analysis for each lineage. The following settings were configured: the “GTR” substitution model, “4 gamma categories”, the “Relaxed Clock Log Normal” model, and the “Coalescent Bayesian Skyline” tree prior. Convergence was evaluated using Tracer v1.7.2, confirming that all parameters achieved effective sample sizes (ESS) greater than 200. Maximum clade credibility trees were generated using TreeAnnotator v2.6.7. Finally, key divergence time points (with 95% HPD) were estimated and the phylogenetic tree was visualized using Figtree v1.4.3. To mitigate the artefactual downward bias often observed in Bayesian skyline plots toward the end of the inferred demographic trajectory, particularly when estimating effective population size51, we limited our skyline analysis to the period preceding 2020.

SNP-distance-based transmission tracing

To minimize potential bias introduced by the choice of reference during SNP-distance calculation, we selected one complete ST313 genome assembly from each of the five lineages as the corresponding lineage-specific reference. Specifically, strain A130 was used as the reference for lineage 1, D23580 for lineage 2, BKQZM9 for lineage 3, B3589 for lineage 4, and U2 for lineage 514,52. Using the alignment method described above, we obtained cgSNP alignments, and then we calculated pairwise SNP distances between ST313 isolates with SNP-dists v0.8.2.

Furthermore, we performed complete-linkage hierarchical clustering under the optimal SNP threshold (SNP threshold = 3, as described in the “Methods” section below). This step was implemented in SciPy v1.14.1, ensuring that the SNP distances among isolates within each cluster did not exceed three SNPs. We also incorporated sampling time as additional epidemiological information by restricting the isolation dates of potentially related strains to within three years, a timeframe consistent with the estimated period required for the accumulation of three SNPs in ST313. Two ST313 isolates within the same cluster and collected within a three-year interval were considered to represent a potential transmission event. However, it is important to note that, given the epidemiological resolution achievable by this method, it is used as a pragmatic heuristic to summarize genetic proximity. To facilitate this analysis, we developed a custom script to count transmission events.

Determination of the optimal SNP distance threshold

We first estimated the evolutionary rate of ST313 using the methodology described by Arthur W. Pightling et al.53. Specifically, The number of new SNPs arising per year was determined by multiplying the evolutionary rate estimated with BEAST by the number of core SNP sites identified in the alignments. The results indicated that the overall evolutionary rate of ST313 is ~0.97 SNPs per genome per year (95% HPD: 0.75–1.53). By incorporating evolutionary rates, we estimated the number of new SNPs that may arise per year for each lineage: L1: 1.85 (95% HPD: 1.62–2.09), L2: 1.37 (95% HPD: 1.26–1.56), L3: 0.9 (95% HPD: 0.6–1.19), L4: 0.95 (95% HPD: 0.69–1.22), L5: 1.79 (95% HPD: 1.46–2.09). As shown, the annual number of new SNPs in ST313 is highly consistent across lineages, with differences of less than one SNP per year.

Furthermore, we evaluated the source-tracing performance under different SNP thresholds. Considering that an appropriate SNP distance threshold is generally assumed to correspond to the number of SNPs accumulated over ~2–3 years, we conducted a more detailed comparison of source-tracing performance using SNP thresholds of 1–5. The underlying assumptions were as follows: (i) the optimal threshold should approximate the number of SNPs expected to accumulate over 2–3 years in ST313; and (ii) when the threshold is set to 1 SNP, inference of potential transmission between isolates is maximally stringent, and the number of inferred potential transmission events should not increase substantially when moving to the optimal SNP threshold.

We found that as the SNP threshold increased, the number of clusters decreased, while the total number of inferred potential transmission events increased. We next compared, for thresholds of 1–5 SNPs, the mean number of potential transmission events per cluster (Figure. S6a). Notably, potential transmission events can be identified only in clusters containing 2 or more isolates; therefore, clusters comprising a single isolate were excluded. Our results show that when the threshold was set to 4 SNPs, the mean number of potential transmission events per cluster was significantly higher than with a threshold of 1 SNP (Mann–Whitney U test, FDR-corrected; p = 0.002).

We also compared, across different SNP thresholds, the mean number of within-country and between-country potential transmission events per cluster. In both cases, the mean number of potential transmission events per cluster increased with increasing SNP threshold. When we examined within-country potential transmission events across clusters, the mean number of events per cluster was significantly higher at a threshold of 4 SNPs than at 1 SNP (Mann–Whitney U test, FDR-corrected; p = 0.03, Figure. S6b). In contrast, for between-country potential transmission events, the mean number of events per cluster did not differ significantly between thresholds of 4 and 1 SNP (Mann–Whitney U test, FDR-corrected; p = 0.83). Taken together, these observations support the use of a 3-SNP threshold for the final source attribution analyses of ST313.

Construction of the SPI and plasmid database carried by ST313

A literature review was conducted to identify known plasmids and Salmonella pathogenicity Island (SPI) carried by ST31314,54–56. The plasmid sequences pBT1–pBT3 and pSLT-BT were derived from the ST313 reference strain D23580, with GenBank accession numbers LS997975.1, LS997976.1, LS997977.1, and LS997974.1. The lineage-specific pSLT plasmid reference sequence was obtained from GenBank (accession number CP060170), and the reference sequence for pAnK was NC_010896.1. All SPI reference sequences (SPI-1, SPI-2, SPI-3, SPI-4, SPI-5, SPI-6, SPI-9, SPI-11, SPI-12, SPI-13, SPI-14, SPI-16) were extracted from the D23580 reference genome (GCA_900538085.1).

Mobile genetic element detecting

Four classes of mobile genetic elements were further identified. Specifically, plasmid sequences carried by each ST313 genome were reconstructed and typed using MOB-suite v3.1.957. Then, BacAnt v3.4.058 was used to detect integrons and transposons in the genomes. Only integrons or transposons with a similarity of more than 60% and a coverage greater than 60% will be identified. The prophages were detected using the Phastest pipeline59 with default parameters.

Detection and co-localization of ARG determinants with MGE

We employed Abricate v1.0.1 with the ResFinder database60 to detect ARGs, setting minimum sequence similarity and coverage thresholds of 80% and 90%, respectively. AMR-associated point mutations were identified using Staramr v0.10.0 with default parameters61. Based on a previous study62, MDR strains are defined as those harboring AMR determinants for chloramphenicol, ampicillin, and co-trimoxazole. The resistance profiles associated with each AMR determinant were retrieved from the Card Database63. Furthermore, ARGs were considered co-localized with plasmids or prophages if they were located within their respective sequence boundaries. For integrons and transposons, given their structural characteristics and detection methods, ARGs located within 5 kb of integrase or transposase genes were also considered potentially co-localized with these MGEs28,64.

Invasiveness index calculation and degradation gene analysis

The invasiveness index was calculated following previously established methods67. Specifically, the ability of ST313 to cause intestinal or extraintestinal infections in hosts correlates with the degree of degradation of the top 196 predictor genes. To identify these genes, we employed a machine-learning approach that leveraged a pre-trained random forest classifier and delta-bitscore functional variant-calling. To further validate the presence of these 196 predictor genes, which were confirmed to be associated with invasiveness, we performed BLAST analyses for each gene. This analysis produced a presence/absence matrix derived from the alignment results. Concurrently, predictor genes identified as having undergone mutations were annotated as “degradation”.

Identification of pseudogene-associated SNPs

We employed Snippy v4.4.5 to identify single-nucleotide polymorphisms in all ST313 strains. SNPs predicted to cause “Stop Gained” or “Frameshift Variant” mutations were initially classified as pseudogene-associated SNPs. To minimize potential overestimation, we further examined the positional context of each mutation within its corresponding gene. Following the conservative criterion described by Ochman et al., mutations located within the first 80% of a gene were considered likely to disrupt function, whereas those occurring in the terminal 20% were not classified as pseudogene-associated68.

Pan-genome analysis with functional annotation

For the pan-genome analysis, genome annotation was performed using Prokka v1.14.669 with a minimum contig size of 200 bp and the kingdom parameter set to Bacteria. Pan-genome construction was then carried out using Panaroo v1.5.170 with default parameters. The resulting binary presence/absence matrix, indicating gene presence across strains, was analyzed using Scoary v1.6.1671 to identify lineage-specific differentially present genes. Functional annotation of key differentially present genes was conducted using eggNOG-mapper v2.1.11 with the eggNOG 5 database72. We identified a total of 30,061 coding sequences (CDS) annotated within the ST313 population. Based on gene prevalence, these CDS were categorized as core (3,608 genes), soft-core (562 genes), shell (745 genes), and cloud genes (25,146 genes) (Figure. S22).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SciPy v1.14.1 to evaluate various genomic features. To assess the correlation between the data, we first determined whether the data conformed to a normal distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The results indicated that none of the pairwise comparisons in this study followed a normal distribution. For pairwise comparisons, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied to determine the significance of differences between groups or conditions, and the resulting p values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Consequently, the Spearman correlation test was applied to examine the association between the prevalence of specific genes and population shifts across ST313 lineages. Additionally, in the pan-genome analysis, Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p values were employed by Scoary to identify genes that were overrepresented in specific lineages. In all methods, statistical significance was determined based on a p value threshold of 0.05.
