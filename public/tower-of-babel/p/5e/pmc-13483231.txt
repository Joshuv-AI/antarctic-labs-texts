Polyvinyl chloride promotes radioresistance in hepatocellular carcinoma by inhibiting radiotherapy-induced CD8⁺ T cell differentiation
Authors: Haibo Zhang, Yanwei Lu, Hao Xiong, Lei Shi, Hongen Xu, Yongshi Jia, Jun Zhao, Hailong Sheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Radiotherapy is standard-of-care treatment for intermediate and advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC); however, resistance remains widespread. Microplastics are detectable in various tumors and may disrupt immune responses, but evidence regarding the role of microplastics in radiotherapy is scarce. Here we show that various types of microplastics are detectable in 52 of the 72 HCC samples we examine, but only polyvinyl chloride (PVC) impairs radiotherapy efficacy. Mechanistically, irradiation enhances histone lactylation, which promotes transcription of HMG-CoA reductase in HCC cells, thereby facilitating cholesterol synthesis and reinforcing CD8+ T cell stemness. PVC alters cholesterol localization and affects CD8⁺ T cell differentiation, which results in the maintenance of an immunologically cold tumor immune microenvironment. A high-cholesterol diet restores CD8+ T-cell stemness and improves therapeutic response to radiotherapy in PVC-infiltrated HCC. Targeting cholesterol metabolism may represent a potential combinatorial strategy to enhance the efficacy of radiotherapy in PVC-infiltrated HCC.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a common malignancy characterized by insidious early symptoms and rapid progression. Approximately 85% of patients are diagnosed at an intermediate or advanced stage, losing the opportunity for surgical intervention, resulting in a 5-year survival rate of only 14–18%1. With the rapid advancement of radiotherapy techniques, such as stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), radiotherapy has emerged as one of the primary treatment modalities for intermediate and advanced HCC2. However, radiotherapy resistance remains widespread, and understanding the factors that induce resistance is crucial to improving the efficacy of radiotherapy in HCC.

The efficacy of radiotherapy in HCC depends on ionizing radiation (IR) damage to tumor cells and its ability to activate the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), thereby eliciting immune responses that synergistically target the tumor3. Dr. Llovet proposed that the HCC TIME exhibits a “hot” versus “cold” state. Radiotherapy can activate the TIME by promoting the exposure of tumor-associated antigens and the release of damage-associated molecular patterns, converting the TIME from a “cold” to a “hot” state and enhancing radiotherapy efficacy4. Conversely, factors such as β-catenin mutations or defects in DNA damage repair reverse the radiotherapy -induced TIME activation5–7. Failure of radiotherapy to activate the TIME is considered a key contributor to radiotherapy resistance in HCC. Abnormal substance deposition in the TIME may impair radiotherapy-mediated TIME activation. Microplastic defined as plastic particles <5 mm, arise from the degradation of plastics via physical, chemical, and biological processes. As pervasive micro- or nanoscale pollutants, microplastics have recently been identified in multiple human tissues, including blood, lungs, liver, atherosclerotic plaques, and various tumor tissues8–12. Microplastic have been shown to modulate the local immune microenvironment13. In the murine intestine, microplastic can induce lysosomal damage in macrophages, leading to IL-1β secretion and subsequent Treg differentiation, ultimately reshaping the local immune microenvironment and promoting the formation of a “cold” TIME that favors colorectal tumor progression14. Polystyrene (PS) can activate PF4+ macrophages in the kidney, promoting renal fibrosis and a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment15. Compared with normal tissue, the TIME in irradiated HCC is highly dynamic, and the presence of microplastic may trigger complex and profound immunological effects. However, to date, no study has systematically investigated the impact of microplastic on the TIME and radiotherapy efficacy in HCC.

To investigate the impact of microplastic on tumors and their treatment, we use HCC tissues and preclinical models, and demonstrate that PVC plays a critical role in the radioresistance of HCC. Mechanistically, radiotherapy induces CD8+ T cell stem-like differentiation and activates the TIME. Whereas PVC is phagocytosed by HCC cells, it alters cholesterol distribution, suppresses CD8⁺ T cell stemness, and maintains a “cold” TIME. This study highlights the potential adverse impact of microplastic on the efficacy of radiotherapy and presents possible combination strategies.

Results

PVC impairs the efficacy of radiotherapy in HCC

We analyzed tumor tissues from 72 patients with locally advanced HCC using pyrolyzer-gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (Py-GCMS) (baseline characteristics of the 72 patients are presented in Supplementary Table 1). We found that 72% (52/72) of patients with HCC exhibited the presence of microplastics in TIME. Several microplastics, including PVC, polyethylene (PE), and polystyrene (PS), were detected in 52 HCC samples (Fig. 1A, B). The participants were stratified into PS, PVC, and PE-infiltrated groups. All patients received radiotherapy in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors and bevacizumab. Treatment responses were evaluated based on the modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST). Patients in the PVC-infiltrated group had decreased rates of complete response (CR) and partial response (PR), as well as increased proportions of stable disease (SD) and progressive disease (PD), compared with the PVC-free group. This trend was more pronounced with higher levels of intratumoral PVC infiltration. In contrast, PS and PE infiltration did not affect patient response to therapy (Fig. 1D). Progression-free survival (PFS) analysis revealed that those with PVC-infiltrated tumors exhibited a poorer response to radiotherapy than the patients with PVC-free HCC. This effect was more pronounced in patients with high intratumoral PVC levels (10–500 ng/g). No significant difference in PFS was observed between patients exposed to PS and PE (Fig. 1E). Cox regression analysis demonstrated that PVC burden was an independent predictor of outcomes after adjustment for major confounders (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2). This suggests that PVC may be associated with radiotherapy resistance in HCC.

PVC may impair the efficacy of radiotherapy by modulating the TIME in HCC

We established C57BL/6 orthotopic HCC models using Hepa1-6 cells to determine whether microplastics affect the efficacy of radiotherapy in HCC. To mimic the intratumoral exposure of microplastic in patients, mice were treated with PS, PVC, and PE at doses of 2, 20, or 200 μg/d for two weeks. In the 2 μg/d groups, intratumoral levels of PS, PVC, and PE were mostly below the detection limit, whereas the 200 μg/d groups exhibited levels far exceeding those observed in human HCC tissues. In contrast, the 20 μg/d groups closely recapitulated the microplastic burden detected in human HCC (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Following the two-week administration period, microplastics were withdrawn, and we observed that a substantial amount of microplastics remained within the orthotopic HCC lesions during the third and fourth weeks, indicating that microplastic can persist and accumulate within the tumor microenvironment (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Mice were treated with PS, PVC, PE, radiotherapy, or radiotherapy plus PS, PVC, or PE (Fig. 2A). We found that PVC impaired the effects of radiotherapy. However, PVC treatment alone did not promote HCC progression (Fig. 2B). Subsequently, we co-cultured Hepa1-6 cells with PVC and found that PVC inhibited the proliferation of Hepa1-6 cells (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, PVC did not affect the radiosensitivity of Hepa1-6 cells (Fig. 2D). Consistent results were obtained using the Hep53.4 orthotopic HCC model, where PVC diminished the efficacy of radiotherapy without affecting Hep53.4 cell proliferation and radiosensitivity (Supplementary Fig 4A–C). Previous studies have shown that microplastics can disrupt or even reshape the TIME10,11. Therefore, we hypothesized that PVC might impair the efficacy of radiotherapy by suppressing radiotherapy-induced activation of TIME11,15. We established an orthotopic Hepa1-6 tumor model in BALB/c nude mice and subjected the mice to radiotherapy or radiotherapy T combined with PVC treatment. We found that, under immunodeficient conditions, PVC no longer promoted radiotherapy resistance in HCC (Supplementary Fig. 4D). We analyzed Hepa1-6 orthotopic tumors treated with radiotherapy or radiotherapy + PVC using mass cytometry (CyTOF). The results revealed a reduction in intratumoral CD8+ T-cell infiltration in the radiotherapy + PVC group (Fig. 2E, F). Flow cytometry further confirmed that PVC reduced CD8⁺ T cell infiltration following radiotherapy, whereas the combination of PS and PE did not affect CD8⁺ T cell levels after radiotherapy (Fig. 2G). Given that CD8+ T cells are the main effector cells that mediate radiotherapy-induced TIME activation, they may play a key role in the effects of PVC. Accordingly, the depletion of CD8+ T cells in vivo attenuated the suppressive effect of PVC on radiotherapy (Fig. 2H). Collectively, these results suggested that PVC may impair radiotherapy efficacy by modulating the TIME in HCC, with CD8+ T cells playing a key role.

PVC impairs the efficacy of radiotherapy by suppressing the stemness of CD8⁺ T cells

We conducted in-depth analyses of intratumoral CD8+ T cells in the two treatment groups to further elucidate the effect of PVC on CD8+ T cells after radiotherapy. The CD8+ T cells in the radiotherapy + PVC group exhibited impaired stemness and a tendency toward exhaustion (Fig. 3A). Immunofluorescence analysis of orthotopic Hepa1-6 tumors further confirmed that PVC suppressed the stemness of CD8+ T cells after radiotherapy (Fig. 3B). CD8⁺ T cell differentiation occurs in several stages, including naïve T cells (TCF1⁺ PD-1⁻ Tim-3⁻), precursor exhausted T cells (Tpex; TCF1⁺ PD-1⁺ Tim-3⁻), and terminally exhausted T cells (Tex; TCF1⁻ PD-1⁺ Tim-3⁺). The Tpex subtype exhibits stem-like characteristics and represents a key effector population responsible for post- radiotherapy clonal expansion and antitumor activity. In contrast, the Tex subtype is characterized by the high expression of multiple exhaustion markers and shows poor responsiveness to tumors. Consequently, the differentiation of CD8+ T cells from the Tpex to the Tex is considered a major factor contributing to the failure of TIME activation after radiotherapy16,17. We confirmed that radiotherapy induced the differentiation of CD8+ T cells toward the Tpex subset in the Hepa1-6 orthotopic HCC model. PVC alone had no effect on CD8⁺ T cell differentiation, however, when combined with radiotherapy, it suppressed the radiotherapy -induced differentiation of CD8⁺ T cells into a stem-like phenotype (Fig. 3C). Additionally, CD62L, co-expression of IFN-γ and TNF-α, as well as granzyme B expression, can also serve as markers of CD8⁺ T cell stemness18. Accordingly, we evaluated the expression of these markers and confirmed that radiotherapy promoted CD8⁺ T cell stemness, whereas co-treatment with PVC impaired this effect (Fig. 3D, E). Meanwhile, radiotherapy enhanced CD8⁺ T cell stemness in the Hep53.4 orthotopic HCC model, which was attenuated by PVC (Supplementary Fig 5A–C). We further evaluated the effects of PS and PE on radiotherapy-induced CD8⁺ T cell stemness in the Hepa1-6 orthotopic HCC model and found that the combination of PS and PE did not suppress radiotherapy-induced CD8⁺ T cell stemness (Supplementary Fig. 5D–F). Moreover, CD8⁺ T cells exhibited elevated expression of multiple exhaustion markers (PD-1, Tim-3, LAG-3, and TIGIT) and reduced expression of cytotoxic and proliferative markers (CD28, CD69, and Ki67) in the radiotherapy + PVC group (Fig. 3F, G).Fig. 3PVC inhibits radiotherapy-induced CD8⁺ T cell stemness in the Hepa1-6 orthotopic HCC model.A CD8⁺ T cell stemness proportion analyzed by CyTOF in the Hepa1-6 orthotopic C57BL/6J model (n = 5 mice/group). Subsequently, we compared the developmental trajectories of CD8+ T cells between the two groups to further investigate the impact of PVC on their stem-like differentiation. Nearly all Tpex subpopulations—including CCR7⁺, CXCR5⁺, CX3CR1⁺, Ki67⁺ CXCR6⁺, Ki67⁺ CXCR6⁻, and Ki67⁻ CXCR6⁺ subsets—were reduced in the radiotherapy + PVC group, indicating that Tpex cells underwent accelerated differentiation toward the Tex subtype (Fig. 3H). These findings suggest that radiotherapy promotes TIME activation by enhancing CD8⁺ T cell stemness, whereas PVC inhibits the radiotherapy -induced stem-like differentiation of CD8⁺ T cells. Thus, PVC contributes to the maintenance of a “cold” TIME, thereby promoting resistance to radiotherapy in HCC.

PVC impairs radiotherapy-induced stem-like CD8⁺ T cells through HCC cells

We co-cultured CD8⁺ T cells with PVC to elucidate the mechanism by which PVC inhibits radiotherapy-induced CD8⁺ T-cell stemness and found that PVC did not impair CD8+ T-cell stemness (Fig. 4A). Therefore, we treated orthotopic HCC mice with FITC-labeled PVC and examined their localization in the TIME. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that PVC were phagocytosed by HCC cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells (DC) (Fig. 4B). Subsequently, we treated Hepa1-6 cells, macrophages, and DCs with control, IR, PVC, and IR + PVC and collected the supernatants for co-culture with CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 4C). Supernatants from irradiated Hepa1-6 cells promoted CD8⁺ T cell stemness, which was diminished when Hepa1-6 cells were co-treated with PVC (Fig. 4D–G). A similar pattern was observed in the Hep53.4 co-culture system (Supplementary Fig. 6). Furthermore, when Hepa1-6 cells were treated with the combination of PS and PE, we observed that PS and PE did not affect IR-induced CD8⁺ T cell stemness (Supplementary Fig. 7). Primary HCC cells were isolated from three PVC-free patients. Cells were treated with control, PVC, IR, or IR + PVC. Culture supernatants were collected and co-cultured with autologous peripheral blood-derived CD8⁺ T cells for 72 hours. Supernatants from irradiated primary HCC cells promoted CD8⁺ T cell stemness, whereas the addition of PVC attenuated this effect, suppressing T cell stemness in the IR + PVC group (Fig. 4H). These findings suggest that radiotherapy promotes CD8⁺ T cell stem-like differentiation by modulating tumor cell–derived factors. PVC impairs the stem-like CD8⁺ T cells by disrupting radiotherapy-induced cholesterol synthesis

Therefore, we performed mRNA sequencing of the control and irradiated Hepa1-6 cells. Enrichment analysis revealed the upregulation of the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway in irradiated cells (Fig. 5A). qPCR confirmed that HMGCR, the key enzyme in cholesterol synthesis, was upregulated in irradiated Hepa1-6 cells (Fig. 5B). Cholesterol plays a critical role in the regulation of CD8⁺ T cell activation and function. Additionally, it is closely involved in T cell receptor signaling, immune synapse formation, as well as the phosphorylation/dephosphorylation of downstream kinases that drive T cell activation19,20. Therefore, we treated Hepa1-6 cells with control, atorvastatin (statin), IR, or IR + statin and collected the supernatants for co-culture with CD8⁺ T cells. The results showed that IR + statin treatment suppressed CD8⁺ T cell stemness (Fig. 5D, E). These results suggest that radiotherapy promotes CD8⁺ T cell stemness by inducing cholesterol biosynthesis in HCC cells.

Thereafter, Hepa1-6 cells were treated with IR, IR + PVC, IR + PS, or IR + PE to investigate whether PVC suppresses CD8⁺ T cell stemness by interfering with IR-induced cholesterol biosynthesis. The results showed that cholesterol levels were reduced in the IR + PVC group compared to that in the IR group, whereas the combination of PS and PE did not affect cholesterol content (Fig. 5F). We then treated Hepa1-6 cells with IR, cholesterol, IR + PVC, and IR + PVC + cholesterol and collected the supernatants for co-culture with CD8⁺ T cells. Cholesterol supplementation reversed the PVC-mediated suppression of CD8⁺ T cell stemness (Fig. 5G, H). Similar results were observed in the Hep53.4 co-culture system, where exogenous cholesterol restored CD8⁺ T cell stemness (Supplementary Fig 8A). In the orthotopic HCC model, a cholesterol diet did not enhance the efficacy of radiotherapy; however, the combination of the cholesterol diet reversed the inhibitory effect of PVC on radiotherapy (Fig. 5I, Supplementary Fig. 8B). We isolated primary HCC cells from three PVC-negative patients and treated them with IR, cholesterol, IR + PVC, or IR + PVC + cholesterol. The supernatants were collected and co-cultured with autologous peripheral blood-derived CD8⁺ T cells for 72 hours (Fig. 5J). The results showed that the combined treatment with cholesterol improved the stemness of CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 5K–M). We then stratified 37 patients with PVC-infiltrated HCC based on their pretreatment blood cholesterol levels into high-cholesterol (>6.2 mmol/L) and low-cholesterol (<6.2 mmol/L) groups. Patients in the high-cholesterol group exhibited better outcomes after radiotherapy compared to those in the low-cholesterol group (Fig. 5N, O). These findings suggest that PVC suppresses radiotherapy efficacy in HCC by inhibiting CD8⁺ T cell stemness via the disruption of tumor-derived cholesterol. However, dietary cholesterol supplementation reversed this inhibitory effect.

Radiotherapy promotes cholesterol biosynthesis by histone lactylation-mediated regulation of HMGCR transcription

We investigated whether radiotherapy regulated HMGCR via the classical SREBP-2 pathway. As a transcription factor, SREBP-2 serves as a key upstream regulator in cholesterol metabolism. SREBP-2 binds to sterol regulatory elements within the promoters of genes encoding cholesterol biosynthetic enzymes, thereby promoting HMGCR transcription21. We found that IR upregulated HMGCR mRNA expression in Hepa1-6 cells, whereas co-treatment with fatostatin, an SREBP-2 inhibitor, did not affect the IR-induced increase in HMGCR mRNA levels (Fig. 6A). PVC promotes radiotherapy resistance by interacting with radiotherapy-induced cholesterol

Next, we investigated the mechanism by which PVC interferes with radiotherapy-induced cholesterol secretion. The preliminary results indicated that among the three types of microplastics, only PVC caused this phenomenon, suggesting that its mechanism may be closely related to its unique structure. PS has been reported to bind specifically to the Tim4 receptor on the cell surface, facilitating cellular uptake24, and can also interact with intestinal lipases, altering their enzymatic activity25. We investigated whether PVC affects cholesterol distribution and function by binding to cholesterol. A stable multilayer PVC model was established using molecular dynamics simulations to better mimic real-world conditions (Fig. 7A). Molecular docking simulations between cholesterol and the PVC model, combined with binding site analysis, indicated that the hydrophobic portion of cholesterol is inserted into cavities on the surface of the PVC polymer, with the hydroxyl group oriented toward the exterior of the PVC surface. Consequently, extensive hydrophobic interactions were observed, allowing cholesterol to stably bind within the PVC surface cavity, with a binding energy of –9.243 kcal/mol, indicating strong binding affinity (Fig. 7B). We also established PS and PE models and performed molecular docking with cholesterol. The results showed that the binding energies of the two were –4.071 and –2.185 kcal/mol, respectively, indicating that their binding affinity was weak. (Supplementary Fig. 9). Subsequently, we co-cultured PVC labeled with PE fluorescence and cholesterol labeled with FITC with Hepa1-6 cells to confirm that PVC can bind cholesterol. We found that PVC could bind to substantial amounts of cholesterol in the intracellular and extracellular compartments (Fig. 7C). Using the Hepa1-6 model, we treated mice with radiotherapy + PS, radiotherapy + PE, or radiotherapy + PVC, collected intratumoral microplastics, and measured the cholesterol bound to them. We found that radiotherapy increased cholesterol levels in CD8⁺ T cells, whereas the combination with PVC reduced cholesterol content in CD8⁺ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 10A, B). Moreover, a cholesterol-enriched diet was able to reverse the PVC-mediated suppression of CD8⁺ T cell stemness in the Hepa1-6 model (Fig. 7F–J). Similarly, in the Hep53.4 orthotopic tumor model, the combination of a cholesterol diet enhanced CD8⁺ T cell stemness in the radiotherapy + PVC group (Supplementary Fig. 10C-F). Discussion

Radiotherapy resistance has emerged as a major clinical challenge in HCC treatment, and evidence supports that TIME is a key determinant of radiotherapy efficacy in HCC. However, the effects of microplastics on radiotherapy -mediated TIME activation and their therapeutic efficacy remain unclear. Herein, we demonstrate that PVC in HCC suppresses the radiotherapy -induced histone lactylation–HMGCR–cholesterol–CD8⁺ T cell stemness axis, and maintains a cold TIME, thereby promoting radiotherapy resistance.

Previous studies have detected microplastic in a variety of human tumors, including lung, prostate, gastric, and colorectal cancers10,26. Microplastics exhibit heterogeneous distribution patterns across different tumor types, presenting as either “enriched” or “desert” phenotypes. For instance, multiple microplastics (PS/PVC/PE) were found in 80% of patients with lung cancer (8/10), whereas none were detected in nine patients with esophageal cancer. Moreover, tumor affinity for specific microplastic types also varies; PS and PE are most common in lung cancer, whereas PS and PVC are predominant in pancreatic cancer10. In this regard, our findings suggest that HCC represents a microplastic-enriched tumor, with multiple microplastics (PS/PVC/PE) detectable in approximately two-thirds of the cases and a high affinity for PS and PVC. The liver, the primary site for processing blood from the digestive tract, plays a central role in the accumulation and metabolism of environmental pollutants, which may render it particularly prone to microplastic deposition. In this study, the vast majority of patients shared a similar ethnic background and primarily resided in East Asia. Approximately 80% of the cohort had HBV-related HCC. This relative homogeneity of the study population limits our ability to evaluate potential associations between the high prevalence of microplastic in HCC and factors such as ethnicity, geographic location, dietary habits, or viral etiology. Additionally, the high prevalence of HBV in this cohort reflects the epidemiological characteristics of HCC in East Asia rather than the global patient population. This predominance of HBV-related cases may limit the generalizability of our findings to HCC populations with other etiologies, such as alcohol-related liver disease or nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, as well as to patients from other geographic regions. Larger, multi-regional international cohorts are warranted to further investigate these relationships. Despite the widespread detection of microplastic in HCC, there is substantial inter-patient variability in microplastic burden. This variation may reflect differences in environmental microplastic exposure, individual dietary and lifestyle patterns, or genetic factors influencing microplastic transport and clearance. These observations warrant further investigation to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

Recently, multiple studies have reported the impact of various microplastics on the TIME. PS exposure reshaped renal mononuclear phagocytes, activating the PI3K-Akt and IL-17 signaling pathways and promoting tissue-resident M2-like PF4⁺ macrophages15. In pancreatic cancer, microplastics reduce the abundance of CD8⁺ T and natural killer cells within the TIME10. However, the role of microplastics in TIME remodeling during radiotherapy remains unclear. In this regard, our study demonstrates that PVC can induce a “cold” TIME by inhibiting radiotherapy-induced CD8⁺ T cell stemness differentiation. Notably, PVC alone did not affect CD8⁺ T cell stemness, suggesting that PVC interferes with a critical step in radiotherapy-induced CD8⁺ T cell stemness development.

Radiotherapy influences the Tpex subset of CD8⁺ T cells via multiple mechanisms. For instance, activation of the cGAS/STING pathway by radiotherapy enhances Tpex CD8⁺ T cells27. Alternatively, low-dose fractionated radiotherapy could expand CD8⁺ Tpex and transitional Tpex-like (TCF1⁻ TIM3⁺ CD101⁻ PD1⁺) cells through improved DC cross-presentation and elevated tumor-associated high endothelial venule density28. Additionally, low-dose radiotherapy recruits CD8⁺ Tpex cells from draining lymph nodes to the irradiated tumor via the CXCL10/CXCR3 axis29,30. SBRT was employed in this study to better replicate the clinical radiotherapy setting for HCC. We found that SBRT promotes the expansion of CD8⁺ Tpex cells by enhancing the lactylation–cholesterol metabolic pathway in HCC.

The effect of cholesterol on CD8⁺ T cell stemness is still not fully understood. Cholesterol is crucial for CD8⁺ T cell activation and clonal expansion, with the low-density lipoprotein receptor facilitating cholesterol transport and interacting with the TCR complex to regulate signaling and immune synapse formation, thereby enhancing CTL function19,20. Conversely, cholesterol overload in CD8⁺ T cells impairs their stemness and promotes exhaustion31. In the TIME, cholesterol induces immune checkpoint expression in CD8⁺ T cells32. Moreover, tumor-associated cholesterol can trigger a cascade of effects. For instance, colorectal cancer cells can take up cholesterol, leading to its accumulation in the endoplasmic reticulum and promotion of the formation of 24-hydroxycholesterol, which in turn suppresses CD8⁺ T cell stemness33. In this regard, our results suggest that radiotherapy promotes cholesterol synthesis in HCC, thereby enhancing CD8⁺ T cell stemness within the TIME. Cholesterol may exert distinct or even opposing biological effects depending on its presence at different time points during therapy or within different spatial compartments of the tumor microenvironment. This could be one of the key factors contributing to the inconsistencies observed in previous studies regarding the impact of cholesterol on T cell stemness. In this study, cholesterol accumulation occurred after radiotherapy, which coincides with a critical window for CD8⁺ T cell activation. At this time point, the induced cholesterol meets the demand of CD8⁺ T cells and supports their stemness. In contrast, prior to radiotherapy, CD8⁺ T cells have little requirement for cholesterol; under these conditions, we speculate that exogenous cholesterol may be taken up by tumor cells and metabolized into products such as 4-hydroxycholesterol, which may subsequently impair CD8⁺ T cell function. It remains unclear how differences in cholesterol metabolism among HCCs of various etiologies affect radiotherapy efficacy. For example, steatosis-associated HCC can exhibit excessive intrahepatic cholesterol accumulation. In this study, most of the collected clinical cases were hepatitis B virus–related HCC, and the Hepa1-6 and Hep53.4 cell–derived orthotopic liver tumors used in our experiments did not accumulate cholesterol. Therefore, our findings may not be applicable to HCC associated with cholesterol accumulation.

HMGCR is transcriptionally regulated through several mechanisms, including the canonical SREBP pathway. Additionally, the role of histone lactylation in cholesterol metabolism has been increasingly recognized34,35. Radiotherapy upregulates HMGCR at the transcriptional level via histone lactylation. Although the precise mechanism remains unclear, intratumoral lactate accumulation is a common consequence of radiotherapy36. Within tumor cells, lactate influences signaling pathways, enhances lactate shuttling, and promotes lactylation37, suggesting that lactate may serve as a key metabolite driving histone lactylation following radiotherapy.

As foreign particles, PVCs are generally thought to be primarily phagocytosed by macrophages and DCs, which subsequently trigger a series of immune responses. Phagocytosis of nano-microplastic altered the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and TNF) and anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10) in monocytes, with PVC exerting a greater effect on cytokine production than did PS13. In a C57BL/6 model, a significant increase in the death of DCs and macrophages phagocytosing nano-PS in the liver was observed, suggesting that the accumulation of nano-PS exerts cytotoxic effects on these cells38. In primary cells derived from human peripheral blood, the expression of maturation and activation markers on DCs, such as CD83, CD80, and CD86, was downregulated upon exposure to PS. Additionally, PS was capable of inducing M1 polarization in macrophages39. Collectively, these data indicate that microplastics exert a non-negligible impact on DCs and macrophages. In our study, macrophages and DCs also phagocytosed PVC. We did not observe an effect of PVC-phagocytosing macrophages and DCs on CD8⁺ T cell stemness. However, internalized PVC may influence the secretion, activation, and other immune functions of macrophages and DCs within the TIME, potentially interfering with tumor antigen presentation and consequently affecting T cell activation and function. The impact of these factors on the efficacy of radiotherapy in HCC warrants further investigation. Notably, we found that HCC cells internalized PVCs. Studies on microplastics in tumor cells have focused on their effects on tumor proliferation, invasion, and other intrinsic properties; however, their role in modulating TIME remains unexplored40. In this regard, our results demonstrate that PVC regulates the TIME by modulating CD8+ T cell stemness via its interaction with HCC cells.

Recently, several studies have investigated the intracellular binding of microplastic to macromolecules, such as proteins, leading to subsequent biological effects. Some of these effects arise from alterations in protein activity or structure upon binding. For example, PS binds to intestinal lipase and reduces its activity by modifying its secondary structure and inducing an open conformation25. In brain tissue, microplastics exhibit high-affinity interactions with α-synuclein domains, promoting the formation and propagation of α-synuclein fibrils41. We found that PVC binds to cholesterol inside and outside HCC cells, which does not affect cholesterol synthesis but alters its spatial distribution, thereby eliciting biological effects. This mode of action provides insights into the biological toxicity of microplastics.

Nonetheless, this study has some limitations. First, the detection of 11 common microplastics using PY-GCMS may have overlooked other microplastic species present in HCC. Second, the PVC used in this study consisted of standardized spherical particles, whereas in real-world scenarios, microplastics exhibit diverse shapes and weathering states, which could produce different biological effects and warrant further investigation42. Third, the HCC patients included in this study did not receive radiotherapy alone; all patients concurrently underwent systemic targeted therapy combined with immunotherapy. Therefore, long-term, large-scale cohorts of HCC patients receiving RT are required to further evaluate the impact of PVC on radiotherapy, highlighting the need for further clinical studies.

Taken together, our results indicate that radiotherapy promotes the formation of a “hot” TIME by enhancing CD8⁺ T cell stemness, whereas PVC maintains a “cold” TIME by altering the spatial localization of cholesterol and suppressing T cell stemness. As a key factor in radiotherapy resistance in HCC, our results highlight cholesterol metabolism as a potential therapeutic target and provide a theoretical basis for developing combination treatment strategies for PVC-enriched HCC.

Methods

Study approval

All human studies complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and Istanbul. Animal experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital (No. 20240705132506932609) and followed relevant guidelines. All uses of human material and clinical data use was approved by the Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital Ethics Committee (No. KY2024122). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients diagnosed with HCC before their participation.

Clinical data

The research was conducted in full compliance with the principles outlined in the 2013 Declaration of Helsinki and 2018 Declaration of Istanbul. This study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital (Approval No. KY2024122). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients diagnosed with HCC prior to their participation. Participants did not receive any compensation. Patients aged over 18 years with biopsy-confirmed locally advanced, unresectable HCC were enrolled. A total of 72 participants were enrolled. Eligible participants had not undergone prior systemic therapy and presented with measurable lesions. Liver function was categorized as Child-Pugh class A-B. Exclusion criteria comprised untreated or inadequately managed esophagogastric varices or a history of autoimmune disorders. All patients received local SBRT targeting hepatic lesions, combined with at least two cycles of immunotherapy (PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors) plus bevacizumab. The median total dose administered to patients was 45–60 Gy, with a dose per fraction of 5–10 Gy. The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS), assessed according to the HCC-specific modified RECIST (mRECIST) criteria. PFS was defined as the time from completion of treatment to radiological or clinical disease progression or death. Participants’ sex, age, ethnicity and sample size are reported in the Supplementary Table 1. Sex of participants was determined based on self-report.

Quantitative and qualitative analysis of microplastics in HCC

HCC specimens were obtained through percutaneous biopsy, then promptly transferred into pre-conditioned glass vials. The tissues were preserved at –80°C. All procedures were performed within a laminar flow bench. To minimize the potential for secondary contamination, operators wore cotton-based laboratory coats and non-plastic gloves throughout sample handling. Prior to use, all glassware and metal instruments were thoroughly rinsed three times with deionized water that had been passed through a 1.6 μm pore-size glass fiber membrane filter (Whatman GF/A, UK). HCC samples were weighed and digested in a 10% potassium hydroxide (KOH) solution—prepared from analytical-grade KOH pellets (484016, Sigma) and similarly filtered—at a solution-to-tissue volume ratio of 40:1. The digestion process was conducted in sealed glass vessels at 25 °C for 14 days. The digested solutions were passed through 1.6 μm pore-size glass fiber filters. Subsequently, the membranes were gently rinsed with deionized water and left to dry at 25 °C. A parallel blank control group was processed using identical procedures to monitor potential background contamination by microplastics introduced during sample handling.

Cell culture

Hepal-6 (SCSP-512) and HepG2 (SCSP-510) cell lines were sourced from the Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology, Shanghai, China. Additionally, Hep53.4 cells were obtained from CLS Cell Lines Service GmbH (400200, Dr.-Eckener-Str. 8, 69214 Eppelheim, Germany). Hepal-6-luc and Hep53.4-luc cell lines were established by transducing the parental cells with Ubi-MCS-firefly Luciferase-IRES-Puromycin lentiviral vectors, followed by selection using puromycin. All cell lines, including Hepal-6, Hepal-6-luc, Hep53.4, Hep53.4-luc, and HepG2, were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA). Atorvastatin (HY-N0322, Sigma-Aldrich) was used at a final concentration of 0.6 μM. Cholesterol (HY-B0589, Sigma-Aldrich) was applied at a concentration of 20 μM. 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG, D6134, Sigma-Aldrich) was applied at a concentration of 20 mM. Fatostatin (S8284, Selleck) was used at a concentration of 2.5 μM.

Establishment of an orthotopic HCC model

C57BL/6 J (Female, 6-8 weeks, JAX stock: 000664) and BALB/c nude mice (Female, 6-8 weeks, Shanghai Model Organisms Center stock: SM-014) were sourced from the Experimental Animal Center of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital. Both strains were maintained on defined genetic backgrounds as specified by the supplier. 1 × 10^6 Hepa1-6-luc cells were injected beneath the liver capsule. Likewise, the Hep53.4-luc orthotopic tumor model was established by administering 1 × 10^6 Hep53.4-luc cells in the same manner. Tumor development was tracked via bioluminescence imaging performed in vivo.

Mouse treatments

On day 5 post-tumor implantation, mice were ear-tagged and randomly allocated to different treatment arms. Experimental groups received either radiotherapy or daily oral administration of 1 μm microplastics, including PVC (ZKKY-PVC-0100, Zhongke Keyou Company), PE-labeled PVC (ZKKY-PVC-1-01, Zhongke Keyou Company), PS (6-1-0060, BaseLine ChromTech), FITC-labeled PS (7-3-0100, BaseLine ChromTech), PE (W532194, BaseLine ChromTech), or FITC-labeled PE (ZKKY-PE-1-01, Zhongke Keyou Company) at a dose of 2,20,200 μg/mouse/day. In both the Hepa1-6 and Hep53.4 models, a total radiotherapy dose was delivered in three fractions of 6 Gy each on days 1, 3, and 5. The radiotherapy was delivered at a dose rate of 5 Gy/min. Lead shielding blocks were used to ensure targeted irradiation of the localized liver tumor while protecting surrounding normal organs. Radiotherapy was carried out using an X-ray linear accelerator (Infinity, Elekta). Tumor progression was monitored at five-day intervals. To deplete CD8+ T cells, mice received intraperitoneal injections of 200 µg anti-CD8 antibodies (clone 2.43, Bio X Cell), starting three days prior to radiotherapy and continuing twice weekly thereafter. On day 5 after tumor establishment, mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of 2-DG at a dose of 20 mg/mouse. For high-cholesterol dietary intervention, mice were switched from the first day of treatment to a low-fat (4% fat) and high-cholesterol (1% cholesterol, w/w) diet to simulate cholesterol metabolism modulation. Mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide inhalation. Humane endpoints for euthanasia were defined in accordance with institutional animal care and use guidelines, including tumor size did not exceed 1500 mm3, body weight loss exceeding 15%, reduced mobility, abdominal distension, and signs of pain or distress.

Bioluminescence imaging

Hepa1-6 and Hep53.4 cells were stably engineered to express firefly luciferase, and orthotopic tumor models were generated as previously described. For bioluminescence imaging, mice were administered D-Luciferin potassium salt (150 mg/kg; Beyotime) via intraperitoneal injection 15 minutes before imaging using the Spectral Instruments imaging platform.

Cell proliferation and radiosensitivity Assays

Hepa1-6 and Hepa53.4 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 96-well plates and co-cultured with 1 μm PVC (1, 10, and 100 μg/mL) for 7 days. 190 μL of serum-free medium and 10 μL of CCK-8 were added and incubated for 2 hours. The absorbance was measured at 490 nm. To evaluate radiosensitivity, Hepa1-6 and Hepa53.4 cells were treated with PVC for 7 days. After irradiation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (MedChemExpress). Colonies containing more than 50 cells were identified.

Mass cytometry

On day 15, orthotopic Hepa1-6 tumors were excised and transferred into cryovials containing tissue preservation buffer for subsequent single-cell suspension preparation. Purified antibodies were sourced from BioLegend, eBioscience, BioXcell, R&D Systems, and Bio-Rad. Antibodies were conjugated with metal isotopes using the MaxPAR Antibody Labeling Kit (Fluidigm). Prior to use, metal-conjugated antibodies were titrated to determine the optimal staining concentration. The list of antibodies is provided in Supplementary Table 2. Cells were washed once with 1× PBS and then incubated on ice for 5 minutes with 100 μL of 250 nM cisplatin (Fluidigm) to exclude dead cells. Afterward, cells were incubated in Fc receptor blocking solution, followed by staining with a cocktail of surface antibodies on ice for 30 minutes. Cells were then washed with FACS buffer (1× PBS + 0.5% BSA) and fixed overnight in 200 μL of Maxpar Fix and Perm Buffer (Fluidigm), which contained 250 nM 191/193Ir intercalator. After fixation, cells were washed with FACS buffer and permeabilization buffer (eBioscience), then stained on ice for 30 minutes with a cocktail of intracellular antibodies. Cells were subsequently washed with deionized water, resuspended, and supplemented with 20% EQ Four Element Calibration Beads (Fluidigm). Data acquisition was performed using a Helios mass cytometer (Fluidigm). CyTOF analysis was conducted at the laboratory of Plttech Technology Company (Supplementary Table 2).

Flow cytometric analyses

Tumor-bearing mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and orthotopic liver tumors were harvested. Tumor tissues were cut into small pieces and enzymatically digested with 0.5 mg/ml type IV collagenase and 0.1 mg/ml DNase I (Solarbio Life Science) at 37 °C for 40 minutes. The resulting suspension was filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer (Corning) to generate a single-cell suspension. Red blood cells were then removed by treatment with 2 mL of RBC lysis buffer (150 mM NH₄Cl, 10 mM NaHCO₃, 0.1 mM EDTA) for 1 minute at 25 °C. Cells were counted using a Cellometer Auto T4 (Nexcelom), and 1 × 10⁶ cells were collected for subsequent experiments. For cytokine analysis, Cells were incubated at 37°C for 4 hours with Leukocyte Activation Cocktail containing GolgiPlug (550583, BD Biosciences), followed by 2-hour culture period to facilitate intracellular detection of IFN-γ, TNF-a and Granzyme B. Cells were incubated with surface-staining antibodies in Buffer II (1× PBS supplemented with 1 g/ml BSA and 0.5 g/ml NaN₃) for 30 minutes at 4 °C in the dark. They were then fixed and permeabilized using a transcription factor buffer set (eBioscience) for 1 hour at 4 °C in the dark, followed by intracellular protein staining for 1 hour at 4 °C in 1× Perm/Wash buffer (BD Biosciences). Samples were analyzed with a Multidimensional High-Definition Flow Cytometer (LSRFortessa X-20, BD). Data acquisition, instrument settings, and analysis were performed on a BD LSRFortessa™ flow cytometer controlled by BD FACSDiva™ Software. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo v10.4.0 software. Splenocytes were used as compensation controls. Supplementary Fig. 1 illustrates a representative gating strategy employed for flow cytometry analysis.

Immunofluorescence staining

Tumor specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for immunohistochemical analysis. CD8⁺ T cells were identified using anti-CD8 antibody (EPR21769, Abcam) and anti-TCF-1 antibody (2203 T, CST), followed by incubation with secondary antibodies. Nuclear counterstaining was performed using DAPI. CD8⁺ T cells were assessed in five randomly selected fields under a 10× magnification lens. Quantification was conducted using ImageJ software.

Isolation and differentiation of bone marrow–derived macrophages and bone marrow–derived dendritic cells

C57BL/6 mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the femurs and tibias were harvested. Bone marrow was flushed from the marrow cavities using PBS and filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. Bone Marrow–Derived Macrophages (BMDM) were cultured for 7 days in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 20 ng/mL M-CSF, and 1 μg/mL PVC. On day 7, cells were exposed to 6 Gy of irradiation. After 72 hours, culture supernatants were collected for co-culture with CD8+ T cells. Bone marrow–derived dendritic cells were cultured with 15 ng/mL GM-CSF, 10 ng/mL IL-4, and 1 μg/mL PVC for 7 days. On day 7, the cells were irradiated with 6 Gy and the culture supernatants were harvested after 72 hours for CD8+ T cell co-culture.

Isolation and culture of mouse CD8⁺ T Cells

Single-cell suspensions were generated from spleens of C57BL/6 mice. Naïve CD8⁺ T cells were purified using the Mouse CD8⁺ T Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec). Cells were maintained in culture medium supplemented with 55 μM β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME; Sigma-Aldrich) and 5 ng/mL recombinant murine IL-2 (PeproTech). The medium was refreshed every 48 hours. On day 1, supernatants derived from pre-established BMDMs and BMDCs cultures were added.

Co-culture of HCC cells and mouse CD8⁺ T Cells

Hepa1-6 and Hepa53.4 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 96-well plates and co-cultured with 1 μm PS, PVC, and PE (1 μg/mL) for 7 days. On day 7, cells were exposed to 6 Gy of irradiation. After 72 hours, culture supernatants were collected for co-culture with CD8+ T cells.

Isolation and culture of human CD8⁺ T Cells and primary HCC cells

Naïve human CD8⁺ T cells were purified using the Human CD8⁺ T Cell Isolation Kit (HY-K0351, MCE). Cells were stimulated with anti-CD3/CD28 magnetic beads (Cat11161D, Thermo Fisher) and cultured in the presence of human IL-2 (PeproTech, Cat# 200-02) for 7 days. On day 1, supernatant from primary human HCC cell culture was added to the system.

HCC biopsy specimens were minced and digested with a collagenase VI solution at 37 °C for 30 minutes. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through sterile gauze and centrifuged twice at 200 × g for 5 minutes, the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was then subjected to two additional centrifugation steps at 150 × g for 4 minutes, with the supernatant discarded each time. The final cell pellet was resuspended in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Cells were seeded into 25 mL culture flasks at a density of 1×10⁶ cells/mL. After 24 hours, the medium was replaced to remove non-adherent cells. ToProCult™-Hepatoma Medium (TC200, Biotech) was then added. The medium was changed every 3 days. Fibroblasts were removed using repeated differential adhesion and mechanical scraping methods, resulting in the purification of primary HCC cells. The purified primary HCC cells were cultured in ToProCult™-Hepatoma Medium (TC200, Biotech) for 7 days, and the culture supernatant was collected on day 7. On day 1 of culture, primary HCC cells were exposed to 6 Gy irradiation using an X-ray linear accelerator (LINAC). Co-culture with PVC was performed at a concentration of 1 μg/mL. Cholesterol (HY-B0589, Sigma-Aldrich) was applied at a concentration of 20 μM, and 2-DG was administered at a concentration of 20 mM.

RNA-seq

Hepa1-6 cells were divided into control and IR groups. After 7 days of culture post-irradiation, cells were collected for transcriptome sequencing analysis, which was performed by Beijing Biomics Technology Co., Ltd.

PCR

Hepa1-6 cells were divided into control and IR groups. After 7 days of culture post-irradiation, total RNA was isolated. cDNA synthesis was carried out using the PrimeScript Reverse Transcription Kit (Takara). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis was performed with SYBR Premix Ex Taq™ (Takara) on a Roche LightCycler 480 system. Primers were designed based on the target sequences using Primer3 and synthesized by Beijing Qinkuo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Lyophilized primers were dissolved in nuclease-free water to a stock concentration of 100 µM and stored at –20 °C. Working solutions were prepared by diluting the stock to 10 µM with nuclease-free water. The primer sequences are as follows: Srebf2-F1: GCAGCAACGGGACCATTCT; Srebf2-R1: CCCCATGACTAAGTCCTTCAACT; Hmgcr-F1: TGTTCACCGGCAACAACAAGA; Hmgcr-R1: CCGCGTTATCGTCAGGATGA; Sqle-F1: AGTTCGCTGCCTGCCTTCTCGGATA; Sqle-R1: GCTCC TGTTAATGTCGTTTCTGA; Lss-F1: CGGATCGTGAAGACATCAACTC; Lss-R1: CGCCCAATGCAATCATAGGAA.

Cholesterol assay

After 7 days of culture, the supernatants from Hepa1-6 cells were collected. Cholesterol was measured using a mouse automated biochemical analyzer.

Public data processing and analysis

ChIP-seq data (GSE156674) and RNA-seq data (GSE156675) were obtained from the GEO database. The Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) was used to analyze whether the promoter region of HMGCR is regulated by histone H3K18 lactylation. Additionally, the effects of 2-DG and oxamate on HMGCR mRNA levels were evaluated.

Western blotting

Hepa1-6 cells were lysed with RIPA protein extraction buffer. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk (Bio-Rad, USA) for 1 hour, then washed with TBST and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-HMGCR (GB15003-100, Diagbio), anti-H3K18 lactylation (H3K18la; PTM-1406RM, Jinjie), and anti-β-actin (GB15003-100, Servicebio). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies for 90 minutes. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using a gel imaging system and analyzed for relative band density with Fusion software. For full scanned images of the blots, please refer to the source data file.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing

Chromatin immunoprecipitation was performed by the Pierce™ Magnetic ChIP Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #26157) following the manufacturer’s instructions. HepG2 cells were cross-linked with formaldehyde and lysed to obtain soluble DNA–protein complexes. The chromatin was then sheared into fragments, and immunoprecipitation was carried out to enrich for target protein–DNA complexes. The precipitated complexes were subsequently washed and eluted. DNA was purified and used for library preparation. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform.

Molecular Dynamics (MD) Simulation of the PVC Model

MD simulations were performed using GROMACS 2022.1 under constant temperature and pressure conditions with periodic boundary conditions. During the MD simulations, all bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm, with an integration time step of 2 fs. Electrostatic interactions were calculated using the Particle Mesh Ewald method. A cutoff distance of 10 Å was applied for non-bonded interactions, which were updated every 10 steps. Temperature was maintained at 298.15 K using the V-rescale thermostat, and pressure was controlled at 1 bar using the Parrinello-Rahman barostat. Initially, energy minimization of both systems was performed using the steepest descent method to eliminate any close atomic contacts. This was followed by 1 ns each of NVT and NPT equilibration simulations at 298.15 K. Finally, production MD simulations were run for 50 ns for both systems, with conformations saved every 10 ps. Visualization of the simulation results was conducted using built-in GROMACS tools and VMD. One hundred PVC, PS, PE polymer chains were each arranged in a layered structure with dimensions of 1 × 4 × 25 along the X, Y, and Z axes, respectively. A 50 ns MD simulation was performed on the entire layered polymer system to promote the aggregation of the initially dispersed layers, resulting in a more stable layered PVC, PS, PE polymer structure. This enhanced stability is favorable for the binding of protein molecules on its surface. Conformations were extracted before and after the MD simulation. After 50 ns, the initially dispersed PVC, PS, PE chains aggregated to form a more compact and stable layered polymer. Solvent-accessible surface area analysis indicated that the entire layered composite system reached equilibrium around 10 ns.

Molecular docking between the PVC model and cholesterol

The molecular structure of cholesterol was obtained from the PubChem database and optimized using the MOPAC program. Partial atomic charges were calculated using the PM3 semi-empirical method. Ligand preparation was performed using AutoDock Tools 1.5.6. The docking grid box was centered on the PVC, PS, PE model, encompassing the entire polymer structure, with grid dimensions set to 100 × 100 × 100 points along the X, Y, and Z axes. The number of docking runs was set to 50, while all other parameters were kept at default values. Due to possible steric clashes and unfavorable atomic contacts in the initial docking poses, energy minimization was carried out to relieve strain and optimize the binding conformation. The Amber14 force field was used for this process. Energy minimization was performed in two steps: an initial 1000 steps of the steepest descent method followed by 5,000 steps of the conjugate gradient method. The final optimized structure was used for subsequent analyses. The binding energy between cholesterol and the PVC, PS, PE model was predicted using AutoDock software.

Intracellular binding of PVC and cholesterol

Hepa1-6 cells (1 × 10⁵) were co-culture with 1 μg/mL FITC-labeled cholesterol (X-DC096305, New Weichuang Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) and 1 μg/mL PE-labeled PVC for 3 days. The co-localization of cholesterol and PVC within cells was observed in three randomly selected fields using a 10 × objective lens. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Isolation and quantification of cholesterol associated with intratumoral microplastics

Hepa1-6 orthotopic tumor models were established and treated with radiotherapy + PS, radiotherapy + PE, or radiotherapy + PVC. After 15 days, hepatic orthotopic tumors were harvested and quantified. Tumor tissues were digested using deoxyribonuclease type I (0.1 mg/mL, Solarbio Life Sciences) and collagenase type IV (0.5 mg/mL, Solarbio Life Sciences) for 1 hour. The microplastic was filtered by a 0.45 μm strainer (Corning). The microplastic retained on the filter was washed three times with deionized water and collected. Ethanol was added to the microplastic, followed by ultrasonic extraction for 30 minutes (temperature <40 °C). The mixture was centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 minutes, and the supernatant was collected. The extraction was repeated 3 times, and the supernatants were combined. Ethanol was removed by vacuum centrifugation to dryness. The dried material was resuspended in a diluent (PBS + 0.5% Tween-20), and cholesterol content was quantified using a biochemical analyzer.

Statistics

Comparisons between two groups were assessed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, while one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test was applied for comparisons among three or more groups. For subcutaneous tumor volume, a mixed-effects model was employed and followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons. When data failed to meet normality assumptions, non-parametric tests with Welch’s correction were used. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method, with group differences evaluated by the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. For baseline patient characteristics, categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test, while continuous variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Hazard ratios (HRs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals were estimated using a Cox proportional hazards model. To avoid model instability, variables with extremely low frequencies were excluded from the multivariable Cox regression analysis. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data visualization and statistical calculations were carried out using GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, USA).
