Dialysis for Parathyroid Tumor Crises to Combat Ventricular Arrhythmia Risk: A Report of 2 Cases
Authors: Qi Yang Damien Qi, Joanna Y Gong, Michelle So, Christopher J Yates, Spiros Fourlanos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA parathyroid crisis is a rare endocrine emergency characterized by life-threatening hypercalcemia, often due to delayed presentations with severe primary hyperparathyroidism. Prompt identification and management are crucial to mitigate potentially fatal complications such as cardiac arrhythmias. We report 2 cases of severe hypercalcemia secondary to parathyroid tumors managed with dialysis prior to definitive surgery. The first involved a 41-year-old man with severe hypercalcemia (serum calcium 20.6 mg/dL [SI: 5.14 mmol/L]) and parathyroid carcinoma, complicated by ventricular arrhythmias requiring cardioversion. Despite aggressive medical therapy, dialysis was initiated, but persistent arrhythmias necessitated emergency surgery. The second case involved a 30-year-old woman with significant hypercalcemia (serum calcium 20.2 mg/dL [SI: 5.04 mmol/L]) and an atypical parathyroid adenoma, who was stabilized preoperatively using continuous dialysis with a low calcium dialysate and regional citrate anticoagulation. Both patients underwent successful parathyroidectomy with subsequent normalization of calcium levels. These cases highlight the critical role of dialysis in stabilizing severe hypercalcemia prior to surgical intervention, particularly when arrhythmia risk is of concern. Given the possibility of cardiac complications, early consideration of dialysis should be considered in the management of parathyroid crisis.

Introduction

A parathyroid crisis is a rare but important endocrine emergency, with potentially fatal complications. It is most commonly due to primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) with a reported incidence of 1.6% to 6% among patients with hyperparathyroidism [1]. Autonomous overproduction of parathyroid hormone (PTH) can result in severe hypercalcemia with end-organ dysfunction, including cardiac instability and acute kidney injury (AKI) [2]. Management requires prompt identification of the autonomous parathyroid gland(s), intensive multimodal presurgical medical optimization, followed by definitive parathyroidectomy [3]. Early use of dialysis to lower plasma calcium is an important management consideration in cases of severe hypercalcemia, given potentially fatal outcomes of hypercalcemia-induced ventricular arrhythmias [4]. We present 2 cases of severe PHPT secondary to parathyroid tumors where dialysis was utilized.

Case Presentation

Case 1

A 41-year-old man presented with 3 days of lower abdominal pain associated with constipation and arthralgias of the knee and ankle. There were no neurological or urinary symptoms. A goiter with a large, hard, left-sided irregular mass was palpable. There was no family history of endocrine tumors.

Notably, he had presented to an overseas hospital approximately 6 months prior with marked hypercalcemia and hyperparathyroidism (serum calcium 13.91 mg/dL [SI: 3.47 mmol/L]) (reference range, 8.6-10.2 mg/dL [SI: 2.15-2.55 mmol/L]) and PTH 829.8 pg/mL (SI: 88 pmol/L) (reference range, 16.0-94.3 pg/mL [SI: 1.7-10.0 pmol/L]). A computed tomography (CT) scan of the neck demonstrated a multinodular goiter with a dominant right-sided nodule and a 3- to 4-cm mass behind the lower pole of the left thyroid. Fine needle aspiration of the lesions was performed during this index presentation with cytology of the left lower mass consistent with a parathyroid lesion. Unfortunately, he was lost to follow-up prior to definitive parathyroidectomy.

Case 2

A 30-year-old woman presented with 2 weeks of nausea, vomiting, dizziness, fatigue, and significant muscular cramps involving both lower limbs. Neck examination revealed a large palpable neck mass. There was no family history of endocrine tumors.

Diagnostic Assessment

Case 1

Serum calcium was 20.6 mg/dL (SI: 5.14 mmol/L) with a plasma ionized calcium level of 10.3 mg/dL (SI: 2.57 mmol/L) (reference range, 4.53-5.29 mg/dL [SI: 1.13-1.32 mmol/L]) and PTH of 2850.7 pg/mL (SI: 302.3 pmol/L). Associated biochemistry included an AKI (serum creatinine 4.96 mg/dL [SI: 439 umol/L]) (reference range, 0.74-1.35 mg/dL [SI: 65-119 umol/L]), hyperphosphatemia (serum phosphate 6.35 mg/dL [SI: 2.05 mmol/L]) (reference range, 2.69-4.49 mg/dL [SI: 0.87-1.45 mmol/L]), and a raised alkaline phosphatase of 682 U/L (30-130 U/L). An electrocardiogram (ECG) showed QTc shortening of 300 msec and changes mimicking ST elevation (Fig. 1). Imaging of the thyroid identified a large left lower pole heterogeneous solid mass measuring 5 cm in size (Fig. 2).

Case 2

Initial serum calcium was 20.2 mg/dL (SI: 5.04 mmol/L) (reference range, 8.4-10.42 mg/dL [SI: 2.10-2.60 mmol/L]), with an ionized calcium level of 10.42 mg/dL (SI: 2.60 mmol/L) (reference range, 4.49-5.21 mg/dL [SI: 1.12-1.30 mmol/L]) and PTH of 2526.3 pg/mL (SI: 267.9 pmol/L). Associated biochemical abnormalities included hypokalemia (serum potassium 2.2 mmol/L) (reference range, 3.5-5.2 mmol/L), AKI (serum creatinine 2.67 mg/dL [SI: 236 umol/L]) (reference range, 0.6-1.1 mg/dL [SI: 53.0-97.3 umol/L]), and raised alkaline phosphatase of 263 U/L. ECG demonstrated normal QT interval of 440 msec and ST changes consistent with severe hypokalemia (Fig. 1).

Neck imaging identified a large cystic mass abutting the inferior aspect of the right thyroid gland, with associated retrosternal extension, measuring 5.7 cm in size (Fig. 3).

Treatment

Case 1

The patient was treated with aggressive intravenous (IV) therapy with 0.9% saline and subcutaneous salmon calcitonin (100 IUs) every 6 hours. Antiresorptive therapy was deferred due to the severity of AKI. Despite medical therapy, the serum calcium continued to rise with a peak plasma ionized calcium of 12.02 mg/dL (SI: 3.0 mmol/L) and serum calcium 22.0 mg/dL (SI: 5.49 mmol/L). The institution of dialysis was delayed and while being prepared for dialysis, the patient experienced 3 episodes of ventricular fibrillation requiring cardioversion. Due to recurrent malignant arrhythmias and severe persistent hypercalcemia despite subsequently commencing dialysis in the form of hemofiltration (ionized calcium nadir of 10.1 mg/dL (SI: 2.52 mmol/L) during dialysis), an expedited left hemithyroidectomy and parathyroidectomy was performed.

Case 2

Initial management consisted of aggressive IV fluids with 0.9% saline, 100 IUs subcutaneously of salmon calcitonin every 8 hours, and IV pamidronate 60 mg (due to denosumab unavailability). Given the hypercalcemia persisted with an ionized calcium level above 8.02 mg/dL (SI: 2.0 mmol/L) despite multimodal medical therapy, dialysis using a low calcium dialysate and regional citrate anticoagulation was performed over a 12-hour period to achieve a lower plasma calcium, with myocardial and clinical stabilization prior to definitive parathyroidectomy. Dialysis lowered the plasma ionized calcium to 5.97 mg/dL (SI: 1.49 mmol/L) and serum calcium to 11.62 mg/dL (SI: 2.90 mmol/L) prior to surgery with initial shortening of the QTc interval following correction of severe hypokalemia, but normalization of the QTc interval with gradual correction of hypercalcemia (QTc 382 msec). There were no perioperative complications.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Case 1

The patient recovered and was discharged, requiring a short period of oral calcium supplementation to maintain normal serum calcium levels. His renal function, however, remained impaired, with permanent stage 4 chronic kidney disease. Histopathology revealed a parathyroid carcinoma weighing 70 g, with a Ki-67 index of up to 20% and associated histological changes, including broad fibrous bands, nuclear atypia, punctate foci of necrosis, and mitotic activity. Immunohistochemistry was positive for parafibromin and deemed negative for protein gene product (PGP) 9.5 with focal staining of less than 50% of cells.

Case 2

Transient IV and oral calcium supplementation was required postoperatively. Histopathology identified an atypical large parathyroid adenoma weighing 44 g with mostly monotonous tumor cells, a Ki-67 index of 1% to 2%, and immunohistochemistry positive for parafibromin and negative for PGP9.5. Genetic testing subsequently returned showing no abnormalities. Following parathyroidectomy, the serum calcium (9.66 mg/dL [SI: 2.41 mmol/L]) and PTH (78.3 pg/mL [SI: 8.3 pmol/L]) normalized.

Discussion

Calcium plays a critical role in cardiac conduction, such that disorders of calcium homeostasis can cause arrhythmias. Characteristic ECG changes of severe hypercalcemia include prolongation of the PR and QRS intervals and shortening of the QT interval and ST segment [5] via effects on the length of cardiac cell depolarization, and Osborn waves, depicted by a notch at the end of the QRS complex. PTH itself has been shown in animal models to have positive inotropic and chronotropic effects on cardiac cells [6], where complications of aberrant cardiac conduction are relatively common in this setting. Given possible occurrence of such severe complications, prompt reduction in serum calcium in severe hypercalcemia is critical.

Management of the parathyroid crisis must involve multiple simultaneous investigative and management strategies. As definitive parathyroidectomy is the only curative intervention for individuals with the parathyroid crisis, rapid localization of the culprit lesion is crucial. Four-dimensional CT is now established as the most effective imaging study for PHPT [7]; however, in the setting of AKI commonly encountered in association with the parathyroid crisis, the contrast load associated with the scan may not be appropriate. Noncontrast CT imaging or neck ultrasound may therefore be sufficient for surgical planning. Functional imaging is not necessary and should not delay surgery. Fine needle aspiration, as was done in case 1, is not recommended if there is concern regarding the possibility of parathyroid carcinoma given the risk of possible seeding of malignant cells on retraction of the biopsy needle, and subsequent parathyromatosis [8].

While definitive management requires surgical resection of the parathyroid tumor, intensive medical therapy is necessary to stabilize the hypercalcemia preoperatively and reduce the risk of any perioperative complications, including malignant cardiac arrhythmias. Rapid IV volume expansion with calcium-free solutions and administration of agents that inhibit osteoclast-mediated bone resorption (salmon calcitonin and bisphosphonates) are well established interventions in the management of severe hypercalcemia [3]. More recently, the calcimimetic cinacalcet has been identified as an effective therapy. Calcimimetics increase the sensitivity of the calcium sensing receptor to calcium, where cinacalcet is a second-generation agent known to induce calcium and PTH lowering effects in the setting of hyperparathyroidism. Peak plasma concentration occurs within 2 to 6 hours following administration with improved exposure to drug when administered with or following a meal. Its efficacy in the management of hypercalcemia due to hyperparathyroidism first emerged over 2 decades ago, with recent systematic review data confirming its beneficial role for PTH-mediated hypercalcemia [9]. Although prompt access for administration in emergency situations is often limited, its use is increasingly recommended in parallel with dialysis. Despite the availability of these therapies, we propose early dialysis be strongly considered to achieve rapid calcium normalization where appropriate. Although a number of case reports and case series [4, 10-28] have been published that describe the successful use of dialysis for rapid medical stabilization in the management of parathyroid crisis, and some suggesting it should be used as first-line treatment in all individuals with ECG abnormalities secondary to severe hypercalcemia [4], its use remains variable. Table 1 describes the demographic and clinical characteristics of this population, where notably this group often present with marked hypercalcemia (median serum calcium 19.8 mg/dL [SI: 4.94 mmol/L]) and significant end-organ manifestations, most commonly AKI (16 of 23) and cardiac manifestations (9 of 23). Regarding dialysis modality, low calcium or calcium-free dialysates, using both intermittent and continuous forms of dialysis have been described. Existing reports suggest significant flux in plasma calcium may induce intra-dialytic hemodynamic instability [16], and as such continuous dialysis using a low calcium dialysate is the most favored modality to reduce this likelihood. Additionally, recent reports have utilized regional citrate anticoagulation [18, 24, 27]. This form of anticoagulation is known to induce less inflammation in the dialysis circuit [29] and is increasingly used for other indications of dialysis. Use in the setting of the parathyroid crisis may further assist with calcium clearance given citrate's inherent ability to chelate plasma calcium, rendering it physiologically inactive.

Our case series supports the proactive use of dialysis in cases of severe hypercalcemia with signs of electrocardiac instability, given potential significant complications of delayed initiation. Case 2 highlights the utility of continuous dialysis using a low calcium dialysate, with regional citrate anticoagulation to avoid the development of malignant cardiac arrhythmias. While there is no consensus, this is likely the modality of choice to achieve optimal clearance of calcium while minimizing the risk of intra-dialytic hemodynamic instability, to facilitate rapid definitive parathyroidectomy.

Other considerations in the management of the parathyroid crisis include the use of antiresorptive therapy.

Antiresorptive agents exert effects on calcium homeostasis by inhibiting osteoclast-mediated calcium and phosphate liberation from bone via osteolysis and include bisphosphonates and the Receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL)-inhibiting monoclonal antibody, denosumab. Due to their delayed onset of 48 to 72 hours, additional rapid-acting agents are required to promptly lower serum calcium. They also have additional risks in the setting of severe AKI with the bisphosphonates theoretically bestowing nephrotoxicity risk and both bisphosphonates and RANKL-inhibiting monoclonal antibody therapies also bestowing risk for hypocalcemia following definitive surgical parathyroid treatment due to transient postoperative hypoparathyroidism.

These cases of parathyroid tumor crises, although rare, highlight the need for rapid intervention with treatments such as dialysis and expediting to parathyroidectomy to prevent life-threatening cardiac complications. With the increasing availability of dialysis and experience to support its use in this context, its prompt utilization should be considered for managing parathyroid crises.
