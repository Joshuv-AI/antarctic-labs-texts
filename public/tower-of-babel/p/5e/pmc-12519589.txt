Aspirin use is associated with attenuated risk of severe acute kidney injury in septic patients: a dual-center retrospective analysis from MIMIC-IV and eICU cohorts
Authors: Yaru Luo, Hongyi Li, Jian Li, Danyang Li, Zhen Qiu, Shaoqing Lei, Qiaochu Fu, Zhongyuan Xia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSepsis is a systemic inflammatory response syndrome caused by infection, and sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (AKI) markedly increases mortality. Although aspirin’s anti-inflammatory properties show therapeutic promise in sepsis, its specific renal protective effects in septic patients remain underexplored. This study investigated the association between aspirin exposure and severe acute kidney injury in septic patients using two databases: MIMIC-IV (73,181 ICU stays, 2008–2022), and eICU (200,859 ICU stays, 2014–2015). Among 45,562 septic patients, cohorts were stratified by aspirin exposure, and outcome variables were compared using multiple statistical adjustment methods including multivariable regression and propensity score analysis. The primary outcome was severe AKI incidence, with secondary outcomes including overall AKI, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), and mortality. Our study suggests that aspirin exposure was associated with significantly lower severe AKI incidence in both databases (adjusted OR 0.35 in MIMIC-IV; 0.84 in eICU), representing risk reductions ranging from 16% to 65%. Secondary outcomes showed that aspirin exposure was associated with reduced kidney injury incidence, mortality rates and continuous renal replacement therapy requirements. These protective associations were consistent with sensitivity analyses and subgroup analyses. Furthermore, these protective effects were observed across different aspirin doses and formulations. However, aspirin may also increase the risk of thrombocytopenia and gastrointestinal bleeding. Our findings suggest that aspirin may be associated with reduced risk of sepsis-related kidney injury and mortality. Nevertheless, prospective randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these associations, and individualized risk-benefit assessments remain essential before clinical application.Keywords: Aspirin, sepsis, acute kidney injury, renoprotection, intensive care unit

Introduction

On a global scale, sepsis ranks among the principal causes of mortality in critically ill populations, with yearly death tolls approaching 11 million and constituting beyond 20% of worldwide fatalities [1]. During the course of sepsis, the incidence of acute kidney injury (AKI) reaches 50%–60%, which is associated with a 2–3fold increase in hospital mortality. Approximately 15%–20% of these patients require renal replacement therapy (RRT), significantly increasing the healthcare burden [2]. The pathogenesis of AKI involves the interaction between inflammatory storm and coagulation disorders, where platelet activation-induced microthrombus formation may exacerbate renal ischemic injury [3], thus prompting researchers to explore the potential therapeutic value of antiplatelet agents.

Aspirin, as a classic cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) inhibitor, exerts antiplatelet effect by blocking the production of thromboxane A2 (TXA2) [4]. Additionally, it can regulate inflammation by inhibiting the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway, activating AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), and promoting the release of lipoxin A4 [5–7]. Evidence from several observational analyses indicates that employing aspirin treatment can considerably lower mortality outcomes in sepsis cases [8,9]; similarly, a retrospective cohort study found that aspirin-treated patients had a lower risk of requiring mechanical ventilation, intensive care unit admission, and mortality during hospitalization for coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) [10]. However, there are some controversies regarding aspirin’s renal safety profile. On one hand, aspirin may reduce renal blood flow by inhibiting prostaglandin synthesis, potentially inducing AKI, especially in high-risk populations. Clinical investigations indicate that aspirin administration in severely ill patients with chest trauma correlates with heightened kidney injury risk [11]. On the other hand, studies related to sepsis have presented contradictory results, with several animal experiments indicating that aspirin can actually alleviate sepsis-associated kidney injury [12–14]. To examine the potential link between aspirin and the incidence of severe AKI in patients suffering from sepsis, this study primarily investigated the hypothesis that aspirin exposure is associated with lower incidence of severe AKI in septic patients. Furthermore, we investigated the associations between aspirin exposure—including its different dosages and formulations—and the risks of severe sepsis-associated AKI, any AKI, the requirement for continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), and in-hospital mortality, as well as the associated risks of thrombocytopenia and gastrointestinal bleeding.

Materials and methods

Research design

This retrospective cohort study analyzed data obtained from two publicly available critical care databases: MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD. Our analysis included 14,777 septic patients from the MIMIC-IV database (version 3.0), which contains records of 73,181 ICU admissions at a single center (Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, Boston) spanning 2008–2022 [15]. We also analyzed 28,089 sepsis cases from the eICU database (version 2.0), which includes 200,859 critical care admissions across 208 healthcare institutions in the continental United States over a two-year span (2014–2015) [16]. We obtained permission to use these databases (Record ID 61878473). Given the preexisting anonymization of protected health information, institutional review boards at both participating centers granted informed consent exemption for this secondary data analysis. In presenting our findings, We used the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) cohort checklist when writing our report [17].

Study population and data sources

Both repositories underwent standardized eligibility assessment for ICU-admitted septic patients. Inclusion criteria are patients above 18 years old who have been diagnosed with sepsis [18]. Exclusion criteria included: (1) patients with dialysis prior to sepsis; (2) patients with missing creatinine (Cr) data; (3) preexisting AKI prior to sepsis onset; (4) patients with chronic kidney disease. For individuals with repeated ICU admissions, the initial episode was retained for analysis.

Data collection and definitions

Data retrieval from both repositories (MIMIC-IV/eICU-CRD) was executed through structured query language (SQL) programming. The dataset encompassed: (1) Demographics: age, sex, body mass index; (2) Hematological parameters: leukocyte/erythrocyte counts, renal biomarkers (blood urea nitrogen/creatinine); (3) Hemodynamic monitoring: blood pressure recordings; (4) Interventions: ventilation parameters (initiation/duration), renal replacement modalities (CRRT or dialysis application and temporal metrics); (5) Renal trajectory: AKI incidence, staging criteria, onset timing; (6) Comorbidities: cardiometabolic disorders (HTN/DM/hyperlipidemia), cardiorespiratory dysfunction (CHF/COPD), cerebrovascular events; (7) Pharmacotherapy: aspirin exposure, dosage, and formulation. Aspirin exposure was defined as aspirin administration prior to the onset of AKI. In our preliminary analysis, patients who received aspirin only after the occurrence of AKI were considered nonexposed group. Dosage Stratification: Aspirin dosage was categorized into two groups: doses of 300 mg/day or less (low-dose group) and doses greater than 300 mg/day (high-dose group). Formulation Type: Aspirin was also classified by its formulation as either enteric-coated or plain non-enteric-coated tablets. The data on aspirin formulation were exclusively available within the MIMIC-IV database and is not available in the eICU database; (8) Prognostic endpoints: 28-day mortality; (9) Validated critical care scoring systems were utilized, including the Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS) II and the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) IV, with cross-validation using the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) criteria. (10) Thrombocytopenia and gastrointestinal hemorrhage: Our study identified two key adverse events: thrombocytopenia and gastrointestinal hemorrhage. Patients with platelet counts < 100 × 109/L were classified as having thrombocytopenia. Gastrointestinal hemorrhage was operationally defined as any instance where ICD-9 or ICD-10 bleeding-related diagnostic codes appeared in the patient’s medical records. Variables with over 20% missing data were excluded from the analysis. Missing values in continuous variables were imputed using group means if normally distributed, or medians if skewed. There were no missing values among binary variables. (Tables S1–S2).

Outcome variables

The primary outcome was defined as the development of severe acute kidney injury—classified as stage 2 or 3 according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria [19]—in patients diagnosed with sepsis. Our secondary outcomes encompassed development of any stage of AKI (stages 1–3), 28-day in-hospital mortality, the need for CRRT or dialysis following admission, thrombocytopenia and gastrointestinal hemorrhage.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using R 4.3.3. Continuous variables underwent normality assessment via Kolmogorov-Smirnov testing. Parametric data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), non-parametric data as median with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. We reported categorical variables as both absolute frequencies and relative percentages. Intergroup baseline comparisons employed parametric/nonparametric tests (tableone package). Survival differences were assessed using the cumulative event incidence curves and compared with the log-rank test, implemented via the survminer package. Three Cox regression tiers examined aspirin’s therapeutic effect: crude model, demographic-adjusted model (sex/age), and comprehensive model incorporating covariates altering effect estimates > 10% via change in estimate (CIE) methodology (chest package). To assess the impact of unmeasured confounding factors, E-values were calculated (using the EValue package).

Propensity score matching employed nearest-neighbor approach (1:1; caliper = 0.05) via MatchIt package, incorporating all baseline confounders. Post-matching intergroup comparisons utilized parametric/nonparametric assessments. Outcome analyses involved: (1) crude Cox regression, (2) propensity score-adjusted Cox model (CAPS), and (3) propensity-based weighting methods (inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW): aspirin = 1/PS vs non-aspirin = 1/(1 − PS); standardized morbidity ratio weighting (SMRW): aspirin = 1 vs non-aspirin = PS/(1 − PS)) with survey package-implemented weighted regressions.

Stratified Cox regression analyses (using the publish package) were conducted with adjustment for the same confounding factors as in adjusted model II, and interaction effects were evaluated using likelihood ratio tests, with forest plots generated using the forestplot package. The stratification variables were defined as follows: Weight tertile groups < 70.0 kg, ≥ 70.0 kg and < 87.8 kg, ≥ 87.8 kg; BMI ≤ 23.9, > 23.9; SOFA tertile groups ≤ 1, > 1 and ≤ 4, > 4; SAPS II tertile groups < 31, ≥ 31 and < 42, ≥ 42; SIRS tertile groups < 3, = 3, > 3; APS IV tertile groups ≤ 40, > 40 and ≤ 59, > 59; APACHE IV tertile groups ≤ 53, > 53 and ≤ 74, > 74; and RBC count normal value (females ≥ 3.5 × 1012/L or males ≥ 4.0 × 1012/L) versus low value (females < 3.5 × 1012/L or males < 4.0 × 1012/L).

Adjusted covariates encompassed: (1) Biometrics - age, sex, BMI; (2) Hematological indices - white blood cell (WBC)/red blood cell (RBC); (3) Hemodynamic parameters - MBP; (4) Validated critical care scores - SOFA, SAPS II, SIRS, APACHE IV (incorporating APS IV); (5) Interventions - mechanical ventilation; (6) Comorbid spectrum - cardiometabolic disorders (hypertension/diabetes), cardiorespiratory pathologies (CHF/COPD), cerebrovascular events.

Dichotomous outcomes, including both primary and secondary endpoints, were evaluated using odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A p-value threshold of 0.05 was applied to determine statistical significance.

Results

Study participants

The MIMIC-IV and eICU databases yielded 14,777 and 30,785 sepsis cases respectively through criteria-based selection (Figure 1). Demographic and outcome distributions are systematically detailed in Tables S3 and S5. AKI prevalence demonstrated significant inter-database variation, with MIMIC-IV exhibiting 69.99% (10,343 of 14,777 patients) versus eICU 48.45% (13,608 of 28,089 patients). When applying KDIGO diagnostic thresholds (serum creatinine/urine output), severe AKI (stage 2–3) incidence rates were 14.62% and 14.87% across respective cohorts.

Primary outcomes

In the MIMIC-IV cohort, patients exposed to aspirin had a significantly lower incidence of sepsis-related severe AKI (8%) compared to those not exposed to aspirin (18.37%) (Table S3). Similarly, in the eICU database, sepsis patients exposed to aspirin (13.23%) had a lower incidence of severe AKI than those not exposed to aspirin (15.28%) (Table S5). Furthermore, both databases demonstrated attenuated AKI incidence in aspirin-administered versus non-administered cohorts through adjusted analyses (MIMIC-IV: 64.87% vs. 72.89% and eICU: 45.47% vs. 49.18%) (Tables S3 and S5, Figures 2 and 3).

When controlling for clinically pertinent variables, patients receiving aspirin had substantially lower rates of severe acute kidney injury across both critical care datasets. The MIMIC-IV population demonstrated an adjusted odds ratio of 0.35 (95% CI: 0.31–0.39), indicating statistical significance (p < 0.001). Similarly, the eICU cohort showed a significant protective association with an adjusted odds ratio of 0.84 (95% CI: 0.77–0.91, p < 0.001) (Tables 1 and 2).

Univariate Cox regression, CAPS, IPTW and SMRW analyses revealed aspirin exposure significantly correlated with decreased severe AKI incidence in both cohorts. In the MIMIC-IV dataset, the adjusted odds ratio was 0.44 (95% confidence interval: 0.38–0.51) with high statistical significance (p < 0.001). The eICU group similarly showed a protective relationship with an adjusted odds ratio of 0.87 (95% confidence interval: 0.77–0.99) and statistical significance (p = 0.031). (Tables S4 and S6). Univariate Cox regression analysis for AKI of any stage yielded results consistent with the primary outcome analysis.

We also conducted the dose-stratified analysis regarding the severe AKI, the analysis suggested a significant association with lower risk of severe AKI at both low and high doses across the two databases. This effect was particularly pronounced in the MIMIC-IV cohort, where adjusted analyses suggested that low-dose aspirin was associated with an adjusted HR of 0.49 (95% CI: 0.43–0.55), and high-dose aspirin with 0.58 (95% CI: 0.46–0.75) for severe AKI, respectively (both p < 0.001). This association was also observed in the eICU cohort, though with a more modest effect; both low and high doses aspirin showed a similar degree of protection, with a same adjusted HR of 0.85, and both were statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Figure 4 and Table S7). Besides, our results demonstrate that both enteric-coated and plain tablets of aspirin are associated with reduced incidence of severe acute AKI in the MIMIC-IV database. The enteric-coated tablets were associated with lower risk compared to the plain tablets. The adjusted hazard ratios were 0.35 for enteric-coated aspirin and 0.63 for plain tablets, with both results achieving statistical significance (p < 0.05) (Figure S9 and Table S9).

Secondary outcomes

Multivariate regression analysis controlling for relevant covariates revealed that aspirin exposure was associated with significantly decreased rates of AKI, with this association maintained independently across both the MIMIC-IV and eICU datasets (Adjusted OR in MIMIC-IV: 0.67 [95% CI, 0.64–0.70], p < 0.001; and eICU Adjusted OR: 0.83 [95% CI, 0.79–0.86], p < 0.001) (Tables 1 and 2, Figures 2 and 3).

After adjustment for covariates, multivariable regression models demonstrated significant associations between aspirin use and CRRT requirements (p < 0.001) alongside reduced 28-day hospital mortality (both p < 0.001) in MIMIC-IV (Table 1, Figure 2). In the eICU dataset, aspirin exposure was linked to lower in-hospital mortality; however, it showed no statistically significant relationship with the proportion of patients requiring dialysis (Table 2, Figure 3).

For the risk of AKI, CRRT, and in-hospital mortality, different aspirin dosages exhibited a clear difference in their effects. Regarding the risk of AKI, both low and high doses demonstrated a consistent and significant protective effect across both databases. For the outcome of in-hospital mortality, however, a clear benefit was observed exclusively with low-dose aspirin, whereas high-dose aspirin failed to show improvement in survival in either database. The association with CRRT was observed only for the low-dose group within the MIMIC-IV cohort (Figure 4 and Table S7).

In the MIMIC-IV database, different formulations of aspirin demonstrated a generally consistent protective association with the risks of AKI, CRRT, and in-hospital mortality. Specifically, for the enteric-coated aspirin formulation, the Hazard Ratios (HR) for AKI and 28 day in-hospital mortality were 0.66 (p < 0.001) and 0.20 (p < 0.001), respectively, indicating a significant protective effect. However, the association with CRRT was not statistically significant (HR = 0.70, p = 0.16). For plain tablets of aspirin, a statistically significant protective association was observed across all three outcomes, with HR values of 0.56 for AKI, 0.62 for CRRT, and 0.86 for in-hospital mortality (p < 0.05) (Figure S9 and Table S9).

Following propensity score matching, a separate analysis indicated that aspirin use was associated with an increased risk of thrombocytopenia and gastrointestinal bleeding. In the MIMIC-IV database, the OR for thrombocytopenia was 1.66 (p < 0.001) and for gastrointestinal bleeding was 1.94 (p < 0.001). These findings were corroborated by data from the eICU database, which showed similarly increased risks, with an OR of 1.32 for thrombocytopenia (p < 0.001) and 1.96 for gastrointestinal bleeding (p < 0.001) (Table S8).

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted sensitivity analyses using various statistical models, including: post-matching univariate regression models (Figure S8), CAPS models, IPTW models adjusting for all covariates, SMRW models adjusting for all covariates, and univariate Cox regression models. As shown in Tables 1, 2 and S4, S6, all models yielded P-values < 0.001, highlighting the robustness of our findings.

In this study, the E-value evaluation method was further used to analyze the sensitivity of the influence effect of potential confounding variables, so as to provide a methodological basis for the robustness of the research conclusions. In the MIMIC database, the E-values for severe AKI, any-stage AKI, 28-day hospital mortality, and need for CRRT were 3.52, 2.34, 2.89, and 3.59, respectively. In the eICU database, the E-values for severe AKI, any-stage AKI, and in-hospital mortality were 1.68, 1.54, and 1.92, respectively. These values indicate that to significantly affect our conclusions, unmeasured confounding factors would need to have an effect size larger than these E-values. Therefore, it can be inferred that unknown or unmeasured confounders are unlikely to exert a statistically significant influence on our outcomes when compared to established risk factors.

Subgroup analysis

Studies from both the MIMIC and eICU databases showed that aspirin was associated with a reduced incidence of severe AKI, overall AKI incidence, CRRT and in-hospital mortality (Figure S1-S7). In the MIMIC database, the protective effect of aspirin was generally consistent across all subgroups, including various demographic characteristics, disease severity scores, comorbidities, and treatment modalities. For subgroups requiring CRRT, some results did not reach statistical significance, possibly due to insufficient sample size (Figure S3). In the eICU database, the protective effect of aspirin against severe AKI and in-hospital mortality was also consistent across most subgroups (Figure S5, S7). However, this protective effect was not significant in certain specific subgroups, such as patients aged < 60 years, those with BMI ≤ 23.9, specific blood pressure ranges, certain disease severity scores, and patients with hypertension comorbidity. The association between aspirin and AKI incidence was significant across all subgroups except for those with hypertension comorbidity (Figure S6). Notably, there was no significant association between aspirin use and the requirement for dialysis. Overall, despite some differences in subgroup analysis results between the two databases, the protective relationship between aspirin administration and decreased risk of both acute kidney injury and mortality during hospitalization showed notable consistency across different patient subgroups, supporting the conclusion that aspirin may have potential protective effects. However, results from certain subgroup analyses in the eICU database suggest that there may be heterogeneity in the protective effects of aspirin.

Discussion

Our study is the first to identify an association linking aspirin use to lower severe AKI incidence in sepsis patients, demonstrating robustness in sensitivity assessments and multiple stratification models. All sensitivity analyses and the majority of subgroup evaluations demonstrated consistency in the observed findings. Furthermore, our analysis revealed a correlation between aspirin administration and lower probabilities of AKI, CRRT requirement, and mortality in septic patients, which is consistent with evidence from several animal experimental studies [12–14]. The results for different aspirin dosages and formulations were consistent with the primary analysis of overall aspirin exposure. A notable exception was that high-dose aspirin failed to show an improvement in 28-day in-hospital mortality. This may be attributable to insufficient statistical power, residual confounding factors, or a less favorable risk-benefit profile at higher doses. Concurrently, our results indicate that aspirin use is associated with an increased risk of both thrombocytopenia and gastrointestinal bleeding.

In our study, we observed significant heterogeneity in the strength of aspirin’s protective association between the two cohorts. While aspirin use was associated with 65% lower odds of severe AKI in MIMIC-IV (Adjusted OR 0.35), the effect was more modest in eICU, with only a 16% risk reduction (Adjusted OR 0.84). This discrepancy may arise from the different characteristics of the two databases, such as variations in patient populations, data collection periods, standards of care across different hospitals, differences in aspirin indications and dosing, and variations in the diagnosis and treatment of sepsis and AKI [15,16].

Furthermore, our subgroup analyses, while reinforcing our primary findings, also revealed some notable heterogeneity. For instance, aspirin was associated with a significantly lower rate for CRRT in the MIMIC-IV cohort, whereas the association between aspirin and the requirement for dialysis was not significant in the eICU cohort. It may be attributable to several factors: (1) compared to the single-center setting of MIMIC-IV, there are differences in the thresholds for initiating renal replacement therapy (RRT) among hospitals in the eICU; (2) there is significant inter-hospital variability in the availability and clinical practice patterns of RRT among the participating eICU institutions; and (3) potential under-recording or coding inconsistencies in dialysis-related variables in the eICU database, which could attenuate observed associations. These factors may have limited the statistical power to detect a modest protective effect.

Additionally, the association between aspirin and lower AKI risk was less evident in certain low-risk subgroups. In the eICU cohort, for instance, the association between aspirin and a reduced incidence of severe AKI did not reach statistical significance for patients who were younger (< 60 years) or had a low BMI (≤ 23.9 kg/m2). This is maybe because the baseline event risk in these subgroups is inherently low, leading to a smaller risk reduction and, consequently, insufficient statistical power in a sample-size-limited analysis. In contrast, the protective effect of aspirin on AKI was also non-significant in the high-risk subgroup of patients with hypertension. This particular finding may be related to complex unmeasured confounding factors, such as the specifics of antihypertensive drug regimens, their therapeutic efficacy, and their potential interactions with aspirin.

The mechanism of sepsis-associated AKI encompasses multiple pathological processes, including inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, microvascular dysfunction, and microthrombus formation [19–21]. We suspect that aspirin may exerts renoprotective effects through several complementary approaches. First, aspirin inhibits COX-1, and TXA2 synthesis [22], thereby reducing platelet aggregation and preventing renal microthrombosis. Second, aspirin downregulates Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) expression [23] and suppresses NF-κB [24] and NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation [25,26], resulting in decreased inflammatory cytokines release [13]. Third, aspirin promotes the biosynthesis of specialized pro-resolving mediators, including lipoxin A4 and resolvin D1, which accelerate inflammation resolution [13,14]. Fourth, aspirin preserves vascular endothelial integrity, maintaining microcirculatory function [27]. Finally, the AMPK-PGC-1α pathway is upregulated by aspirin treatment, leading to the mitigation of oxidative stress and prevention of mitochondrial impairment [28]. The combined effect of these complementary mechanisms may explain the observed relationship between aspirin exposure and reduced incidence of sepsis-related acute kidney injury. The multifaceted nature of these pathways suggests that aspirin’s renoprotective benefits extend beyond its traditional antiplatelet properties, encompassing broader anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective functions that address the complex pathophysiology of sepsis-induced renal impairment.

This research also has some important limitations that must be considered. First, the databases did not provide detailed information regarding the specific clinical indications for aspirin use. Due to the lack of access to patients’ pre-admission aspirin medication information and pre-admission disease status, we were unable to distinguish between chronic, pre-admission aspirin users and patients acutely initiated on aspirin in-hospital prior to AKI onset. We were also unable to determine the accurate duration of aspirin use and the timing of aspirin use relative to sepsis onset, which may have introduced indication bias into our analyses. Second, despite employing propensity score matching to control for measured confounders, unmeasured confounding variables such as renal perfusion pressure and tissue oxygenation indices, and concurrent use of other nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or nephrotoxic medications could still influence the outcomes. Third, our analysis did not differentiate between patients at various stages of sepsis progression or distinguish between chronic aspirin users and those receiving acute treatment. This limitation is particularly significant given that aspirin’s therapeutic effects may vary dynamically throughout the course of disease progression. Fourth, our findings may have limited applicability to wider clinical groups because patients with chronic kidney disease were not included in the study population. Finally, the eICU database contained substantial proportions of missing data across key variables, and our imputation methods may have introduced systematic bias into the results. These methodological considerations should be taken into account when interpreting our findings and planning future investigations in this area.

The evaluation of aspirin’s potential benefits must be carefully balanced against its associated safety risks. Our propensity score matching analysis demonstrated that aspirin exposure was significantly associated with increased risks of thrombocytopenia and gastrointestinal bleeding. Therefore, aspirin use in sepsis should be carefully considered in the context of individual bleeding risk profiles, baseline platelet counts, and gastrointestinal comorbidities. In clinical decision-making, aspirin may be most appropriate for septic patients without active bleeding, severe thrombocytopenia, or high bleeding propensity. Furthermore, this study cannot exclude the potential nephrotoxic effects of high-dose aspirin therapy. The observed net protective effect may mask potential harm from higher dosage regimens.

This study provides important theoretical foundations for reassessing the role of aspirin in preventing sepsis-associated AKI. However, the inherent limitations of observational studies preclude the definitive establishment of causality. Therefore, prospective, randomized controlled trials are essential to verify these findings.

Conclusion

Based on combined analyses of two large critical care databases, we found that aspirin exposure was significantly associated with reduced risk of severe AKI and mortality in patients with sepsis. This observed association may be related to aspirin’s unique antithrombotic properties, anti-inflammatory effects, promoting the resolution of inflammation and protecting vascular endothelial cells. Although our study has certain limitations, these findings provide important evidence for the potential protective role of aspirin in sepsis-related kidney injury. Further research, especially through prospective randomized clinical studies, remains necessary to confirm the validity of our findings.
