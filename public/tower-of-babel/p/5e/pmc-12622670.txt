Diagnostic discordances in multiparametric prostate magnetic resonance imaging: A retrospective analysis of PI-RADS Scores and follow-up imaging
Authors: Rabia Mihriban Kılınç, Başak Akbay, Faruk Bulut
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study was aimed to evaluate the frequency of false-positive and false-negative results in multiparametric prostate magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI), to investigate the potential contributing factors, and to analyze the clinical variables that may affect the diagnostic accuracy of mpMRI. This retrospective study included 1269 patients who underwent mpMRI between 2018 and 2024, followed by prostate biopsy within 3 months. Patients with Prostate Imaging-Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) 1 to 3 lesions but positive biopsy results were classified as “false-negative,” whereas those with PI-RADS 4 to 5 lesions and negative biopsy results were considered “false-positive.” mpMRI findings were compared with clinical parameters such as prostate-specific antigen (PSA), PSA density, and age. Intra-observer consistency was assessed using a 2nd mpMRI reading performed by the same experienced radiologist. mpMRI may yield false results, particularly in cases involving anterior/apical lesions or suboptimal image quality. Although the number of false-negative cases was low (n = 21), the possibility of missing clinically significant prostate cancers (ISUP Grade Group ≥ 2, Gleason ≥ 3 + 4) remains an important concern due to the potential for delayed diagnosis and management. reevaluation by experienced radiologists significantly may enhance diagnostic precision. Incorporating PSA density and standardized imaging quality criteria into routine practice may improve the overall diagnostic performance of mpMRI. A total of 203 patients (15.99%) were identified as having discordant results: 1.65% (n = 21) were false-negatives, and 14.34% (n = 182) were false-positives. Among the 182 index false-positive cases (defined as PI-RADS 4–5 with a negative biopsy within 3 months), 78 patients had visible lesions on mpMRI. During limited follow-up, 7 of these were later diagnosed with prostate cancer on repeat biopsy, including 4 with clinically significant disease. As repeat biopsies were not routinely performed, the number of false-positive cases at index evaluation remained 182. PSA density, advanced age, and lesion location (particularly the anterior and apical zones) were identified as potential contributors to false results. Intra-observer agreement for mpMRI interpretation was low, with a Cohen kappa coefficient of 0.099, indicating significant discordance.

1. Introduction

Prostate cancer is one of the most commonly diagnosed malignancies among men, and early diagnosis and accurate risk stratification play a critical role in its effective management. Multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI) has emerged as a pivotal diagnostic tool, that offer high accuracy in the detection of prostate cancer and clinically significant lesions.[1,2] The Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) has further enhanced the diagnostic utility of mpMRI by standardizing reporting protocols and improving interobserver consistency, thereby supporting clinical decision-making processes.[3]

However, the diagnostic performance of mpMRI may be influenced by various factors, potentially leading to false-negative outcomes. Such outcomes can result in missed diagnoses of clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa; Gleason score of ≥ 3 + 4, ISUP Grade Group ≥ 2) and subsequent delays in treatment, ultimately affecting the patient prognosis. According to the literature, the reported false-negative rates of mpMRI range between 10% to 20%.[1,4]

Factors contributing to false-negative mpMRI results include poor image quality, anatomical location of lesions, and the radiologist’s level of experience.[2,5] In particular, image quality assessment systems such as the prostate imaging quality (PI-QUAL) score, have been shown to significantly affect diagnostic accuracy. Studies have demonstrated that lower PI-QUAL scores are associated with a higher likelihood of missing csPCa.[5,6]

Conversely, false-positive findings may occur due to benign pathologies (such as prostatitis, stromal hyperplasia, hemorrhage, or scar tissue) mimicking the signal characteristics of malignant lesions on mpMRI. These scenarios can lead to unnecessary biopsies, increased patient anxiety, and inefficient use of healthcare resources.[7,8]

In this context, identifying the factors contributing to both false-negative and false-positive mpMRI results is crucial for optimizing diagnostic workflows and improving patient management.

This article aims to systematically analyze the major causes of false-positive and false-negative interpretations in mpMRI based on current literature, assess their clinical implications, and offer strategies to enhance diagnostic accuracy.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Patient selection

This retrospective, single-center study included patients who underwent mpMRI at our hospital between 2018 and 2024. All magnetic resonance images were retrieved from the institutional Picture Archiving and Communication System maintained by our hospital. This study was approved by the Muğla University Faculty of Medicine Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval Number: 155, Date: August 07, 2025).

Patients who had undergone prostate biopsy within 3 months of their mpMRI scan, had complete clinical and radiological data available, and had at least 1 lesion scoring PI-RADS ≥ 1 were included in the study. Exclusion criteria included a prior diagnosis or treatment of prostate cancer (n = 52), unavailable biopsy results (n = 32), missing prostate-specific antigen (PSA) values or imaging data (n = 17), and not reported due to poor image quality on mpMRI (e.g., severe rectal gas artifacts, artifacts from orthopedic devices, and movement artifacts, n = 18). Non-evaluable mpMRI examinations were repeated, but 18 patients could not be contacted for repeat imaging.

2.2. Imaging protocol

All mpMRI examinations were performed using 3.0 Tesla MRI scanners (MAGNETOM Skyra, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Imaging protocols were standardized in accordance with the PI-RADS v2.1 guidelines.[3] Patients were instructed to follow a 12-hour fluid-restricted diet, received rectal preparation with enema, and were administered 20 mg of intravenous hyoscine-N-butylbromide (Buscopan) to reduce bowel motility. During dynamic contrast-enhanced imaging, 0.1 mmol/kg of a gadolinium-based contrast agent was injected intravenously. The protocol included high-resolution T2-weighted turbo spin-echo sequences in the axial, sagittal, and coronal planes, diffusion-weighted imaging using multiple b-values (e.g., 400, 800, and 1500 s/mm²), calculated apparent diffusion coefficient maps, and dynamic contrast-enhanced T1-weighted 3D gradient-echo sequences.

After applying the exclusion criteria, 1269 patients were included in the final analysis. The mpMRI findings were compared with the histopathological results. Patients were classified as having false-negative results if mpMRI was reported as PI-RADS 1 to 3 but biopsy revealed clinically significant or insignificant prostate cancer. Conversely, patients with PI-RADS 4 to 5 findings and negative biopsy results were classified as having false-positive mpMRI results. These cases have been documented in separate tables. Clinical and imaging variables including biopsy findings, PSA values, patient age, prostate volume, lesion location, lesion size, and PI-RADS v2.1 scores were recorded. Prostate-specific antigen density (PSAD; ng/mL³) was calculated for each patient.

2.3. Study design

In total, 203 discordant cases were identified. All mpMRI scans from these cases were reevaluated by the same radiologist who originally interpreted them: an expert with 20 years of experience, including 10 years of urogenital imaging. Based on this 2nd review, false-positive patients were reclassified as having either “lesion present” or “lesion absent.” The original and revised PI-RADS v2.1 scores were compared to assess intra-observer agreement.

To minimize selection bias, all eligible patients within the defined time frame were included in the study, thereby reducing sampling bias. Strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were consistently applied. Regarding information bias, patients whose electronic medical records were inconsistent with biopsy results were identified and documented by researchers independent of the radiologist who evaluated the mpMRI examinations. Imaging evaluations were performed by a single radiologist who was blinded to the subgroup classification (i.e., false-negative or false-positive). This ensured that the radiologist who reviewed the images had no access to patients’ information (including previous interpretations, biopsy results, and patient identifiers). The comparison of 2nd evaluations with the initial assessments was conducted by different investigators independent from the radiologist.

The biopsy specimens were reviewed by institutional pathologists. Cases with a Gleason score of ≥ 3 + 4 (ISUP grade Group ≥ 2) were considered csPCa. However, both clinically significant and insignificant cancers were included in the false-negative and false-positive group classifications, respectively.

2.4. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) based on data distribution, which was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using the independent samples t test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the chi-square or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Cohen kappa coefficient was used to evaluate intra-observer agreement in mpMRI interpretation. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

3. Results

A total of 1269 patients who underwent mpMRI between 2018 and 2024 were included in the study (Patient Flow Diagram is shown in Fig. 1). Comparison of mpMRI and biopsy results revealed that 203 patients had discordant findings, either false-positive or false-negative. Of these, 21 were classified as false-negatives and 182 as false-positives. The false-negative rate was 1.65% (n = 21), while the false-positive rate was 14.34% (n = 182), with a total discordance rate of 15.99% (n = 203). To assess intra-observer consistency, a 2nd radiological evaluation was conducted by the same radiologist. Cohen kappa coefficient was calculated as 0.099, indicating poor agreement. A chi-square test confirmed that the discrepancy between the 2 readings was statistically significant (χ² = 6.09, P = .014). In the 1st reading, 104 cases were assessed as having lesions, whereas in the 2nd reading, these were classified as ‘lesion-absent’. The 2nd evaluation was consistent with the biopsy results in approximately 52% of cases.

4. Discussion

In this study, false-positive or false-negative results were identified in 203 of the 1269 patients who underwent mpMRI. The false-negative rate was 1.65%, while the false-positive rate was 14.34%. The total discordance rate was calculated as 15.99%. In the literature, the reported false-negative rate of mpMRI ranges between 5% and 15%, and the false-positive rate between 10% and 30%.[3,9] Salka et al reported false-positive and false-negative rates of 52% and 15%, respectively. However, unlike our study, they considered PI-RADS 3 lesions positive.[2] Although our false-positive rate was similar, our false-negative rate is lower. These differences may be attributed to the experience of the interpreting radiologist, which has been shown to influence the concordance between MRI and biopsy findings. In our study, all imaging was evaluated by a single experienced radiologist, and a reevaluation after 6 years led to a 52% correction of previously discordant results. This finding supports the notion that reader experience plays a crucial role in the diagnostic accuracy of mpMRI.

Another factor contributing to the discordant results is image quality. Artifacts from rectal gas or patient motion and orthopedic implants in the pelvic region may render diffusion-weighted images nondiagnostic. Recent studies have suggested that implementing visual image quality assessment tools such as the PI-QUAL system can improve the reliability of mpMRI and help determine which scans are suitable for interpretation.[10] Indeed, it has been shown that a PI-QUAL score ≤ 3 significantly increases the false-negative rate and decreases inter-reader agreement on PI-RADS assessments.[11] Although our study did not utilize formal PI-QUAL scoring, we attempted to mitigate the impact of poor image quality by excluding scans explicitly reported as nondiagnostic because of artifacts. Our relatively low false-negative and comparable false-positive rates suggest that this strategy is reasonably effective. Future studies should routinely include PI-QUAL scoring to improve interobserver agreement and overall diagnostic reliability.

Among the 182 patients initially classified as false-positive, 104 had no visible lesions upon reevaluation, while 78 retained visible lesions. Of these 78, 7 were later diagnosed with cancer upon repeat biopsy, including 4 cases of csPCa. These lesions were located either apically or anteriorly, and all lesions were ≥ 10 mm. Despite being categorized as false-positives, these findings indicate that approximately 5% of the patients in this group may harbor previously undetected tumors, particularly in older patients. High PI-RADS score lesions with initially negative biopsies should be closely followed up, as sampling error or lesion location (especially in difficult-to-access anterior zones) may lead to missed diagnoses. Stanzione et al[12] reported that 24% of patients with PI-RADS ≥ 4 lesions and a negative initial biopsy were diagnosed with csPCa upon repeat biopsy within 1 year. Similarly, the PRECISION trial conducted by Rouvière et al[13] found that 17% of these patients were later found to have csPCa. Bertolo et al[14] also observed that 10 to 25% of patients who underwent radical prostatectomy after a negative biopsy were diagnosed with prostate cancer, and some with clinically significant tumors. These findings caution against dismissing lesions as false-positives. In particular, Washino et al[15] showed that patients with PSA density > 0.15 ng/mL3 had a 32% rate of csPCa upon re-biopsy, despite negative mpMRI and initial biopsy. Our findings showed no statistically significant differences in PSA, PSA density, or prostate volume between patients with and without csPCa, possibly due to the limited sample size (only 4 cancer cases among 78). However, all the subsequently detected tumors were located anteriorly or apically. These findings highlight the need for vigilant follow-up of older patients with lesions at these locations. Elevated PSA density remains an important consideration.

Early precursor lesions such as ASAP or prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia significantly increase the long-term risk of cancer development.[16] Although these were not present in our patients who later developed tumors, 10 false-positive cases had ASAP on biopsy. These patients were followed up based on PSA levels, but no repeat biopsies were available at our institution. Epstein and Herawi[16] report a 40% malignancy risk in such cases, suggesting that PSA surveillance alone may be insufficient. Biochemical markers, such as PSA density, along with careful histopathological evaluation, are important, particularly in older patients or those with difficult lesion locations. Targeted re-biopsies may be warranted to avoid missing clinically significant tumors.

After excluding 7 cancer cases and 10 ASAP cases among the 78 patients with visible lesions, 20 of the remaining 61 patients were diagnosed with prostatitis. Chronic prostatitis can mimic tumor characteristics on mpMRI, leading to PI-RADS ≥ 3 scores.[17–19] In our cohort, chronic prostatitis was the most common benign finding among the false-positives. Prostate biopsy of inflamed tissue may increase the risk of complications such as sepsis, making it crucial to differentiate prostatitis from true malignancy. In the prostatitis group, 7 patients had solitary lesions between 4 to 7 mm, while the remaining 13 had lesions > 11 mm. This raises the question of whether small true tumors are under-sampled. These patients were younger than those who were later diagnosed with cancer, although PSA levels and PSA density did not differ significantly. While age appears to be relevant, quantitative imaging biomarkers such as apparent diffusion coefficient values, k-trans/kep ratios, and contrast washout kinetics are being investigated for their potential to differentiate prostatitis from cancer.[20–22]

Among the 21 false-negative cases, only 3 showed lesions upon reevaluation, and only 1 of these cases was found to have csPCa. In total, 6 of the 21 patients had csPCa, supporting the high negative predictive value of mpMRI. The literature reports negative predictive values above 90% for PI-RADS ≤ 2 cases.[23,24] This also suggests that reader experience may have less impact on false-negative cases than on false-positives, given that only 3 results were revised among 21 false-negatives, compared to 104 revisions among 182 false-positives. It is likely that the absence of a lesion on a high-quality mpMRI reliably excludes csPCa, while the interpretation of suspicious findings remains more challenging. Therefore, future studies should focus on improving our ability to distinguish between suspicious but benign lesions and true malignancies.

There were no statistically significant differences in age, PSA level, or PSA density between the false-positive and false-negative groups. However, within the false-positive group, patients with visible lesions were significantly older (P = .036). Although PSA density has been reported as a useful predictor of prostate cancer in previous studies, our findings did not show a statistically significant difference between the false-positive and false-negative groups.[1,2,4,15,25]

Intra-observer agreement analysis of mpMRI interpretations yielded a Cohen kappa value of 0.099, indicating substantial inconsistency. The chi-square test confirmed that this difference was statistically significant (P = .014). Notably, a 2nd review by the same radiologist 6 years later led to a 52% reduction in discordant results. Prior literature reports moderate interobserver variability (kappa ≈ 0.4–0.6) and notes that agreement tends to diminish with lower reader experience.[26] To improve diagnostic reliability, standard training, AI-assisted interpretation, and multi-center reviews are recommended.

This study has certain limitations. The retrospective, single-center design limits the generalizability of the findings. All mpMRI scans were interpreted by a single experienced radiologist, which enabled intra-observer analysis but precluded evaluation of interobserver variability. Another potential limitation is the possibility of hindsight bias during the reevaluation process. Although the radiologist was blinded to biopsy results and subgroup classifications, awareness of the study purpose may have subconsciously influenced interpretation during the 2nd reading. Additionally, the study did not employ formal PI-QUAL scoring, relying instead on subjective image quality assessment. This limits deeper analysis of how image quality affects false-positive and false-negative outcomes.

Despite these limitations, our findings contribute valuable insights regarding mpMRI false-result rates and highlight the influence of radiologist experience on diagnostic performance.

5. Conclusion

This study demonstrates that mpMRI is a highly valuable modality for the detection and characterization of prostate cancer, but also highlights several inherent limitations. Among the 1269 patients evaluated, the false-negative rate was found to be low (1.65%), and the false-positive rate was moderate (14.34%), consistent with previously reported values in the literature. Notably, cases in which the initial biopsy failed to detect cancer but subsequent biopsies revealed clinically significant tumors suggesting that lesions initially categorized as false-positives on mpMRI warrant close surveillance. This phenomenon appears to be more common in older patients and in anatomically challenging regions such as the anterior and apical zones of the prostate, where biopsy sampling is more difficult.

The study suggests that radiologist experience plays an important role in diagnostic accuracy. A 2nd evaluation conducted by the same radiologist 6 years later was associated with a 52% reduction in false interpretations, which may reflect the combined effects of accumulated experience, standardized image review, and improved awareness of lesion-related factors in mpMRI interpretation.

Although the PI-QUAL scoring system was not implemented in this study, which is a methodological limitation: examinations with poor image quality were excluded to mitigate this bias. Nevertheless, inflammatory conditions such as prostatitis have emerged as a significant contributor to false-positive findings due to their mpMRI appearance mimicking that of malignancy. While PSA density did not reach statistical significance in our cohort, the existing literature supports its utility as a predictive marker for lesion visibility and malignancy potential.

Overall, mpMRI findings should be interpreted with caution within the clinical context. In patients with negative biopsies but elevated risk features, follow-up imaging and, when necessary, repeat targeted biopsies should be considered. Enhancing image quality standardization, reducing interobserver variability, and integrating quantitative imaging parameters may further improve the diagnostic performance of mpMRI.
