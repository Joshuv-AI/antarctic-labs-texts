Assessing cancer risk due to exposure to radionuclides in soil dust in mining areas of South Africa
Authors: Violet Patricia Dudu, Masengo Ilunga, Dunisani Thomas Chabalala, Manny Mathuthu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDespite the environmental significance of airborne particulates generated from mine tailings, there is limited information on exposure of people living in the neighbourhoods adjacent to such sites. The main objective of the study was to assess cancer risk due to enhanced radionuclides present in dust samples and to evaluate the exposure risk of populations living in three mining areas in the Free State and West Rand in South Africa. Soil and dust samples were collected between January 2016 and March 2018 in different seasons for determination of the level of naturally occurring radioactive materials in the samples. The samples were prepared and sealed for 4 weeks to attain secular equilibrium of the 238U and 232Th and their respective progenies, and thereafter analysed using gamma spectroscopy. Radiological parameters were calculated from the activity of the radionuclides to estimate exposure risk. The absorbed dose rate in air ranged from 76 to 137, 50 to 133 and 19 to 75 (nGy hr−1) for areas A, B and C, respectively. The mean excess lifetime cancer risk in all three mines was lower than the world average of 1.45 × 10−3; accordingly, the cancer risk was found to be negligible. The findings revealed that most radiological parameters were within the accepted international values; thus the soils in the areas could be considered to be safe for the humans living there.Keywords: radionuclides, cancer risk, gamma dose, dust

INTRODUCTION

Five to eight million people globally have been impacted negatively by radionuclides, while 3.3 million people are at risk in the areas assessed [1]. Since 1960, human exposure to natural radionuclides (naturally occurring radioactive materials (NORMs)) has increased by two or three orders of magnitude [2]. A study on the effects of changes in the environment concluded that a quarter of the global burden of diseases can be attributed to these changes [3]. Water pollution for example, from anthropogenic radionuclides such as 137Cs and natural radionuclides such as 210Po can lead to human health risks from the consumption of marine food. In other uranium-producing countries such as Canada, Australia and the USA, most mining activities are done in sparsely populated areas or desert-like areas. However, in South Africa, most mining legacy sites are in densely populated areas [4]. This poses a great risk of contamination through inhaling tailings dust. Human activities result in environmental problems which can pose a threat to the health of those individuals who are occupationally exposed to them and to members of the public who live in the vicinity of, for example, mining areas. Some communities in the West Rand live along gold dumps and these include Kagiso, Krugersdorp, Davidsonville and Victory Park [5, 6]. Usually, the communities living close to mine dumps are of lower socioeconomic status. Epidemiological studies have shown that people living near mine dumps are at risk of exposure to different pollutants via different pathways [6, 7]. The routes for uptake of metals and radionuclides are ingestion and inhalation [8–10]. Exposure to radiation can damage the cells of an organism, which can result in cancer or even death when there are high radiation doses.

Cancer risk is defined as the incremental probability that an individual will develop cancer during their lifetime [11]. Determining cancer risk from different radionuclides is important as it helps to ensure that the health of individuals is protected. The adverse effects of radionuclides in the human body emanate from the deposition of energy in the tissues as a result of radioactive decay. Hence, ionizing radiation is a well-known risk factor for human cancer [12]. Damage to the body by ionizing radiation is related to the exposure dose and dose rate. Radiological parameters are very important as they can help in assessing the health effects emanating from exposure to ionizing radiation. Some of these parameters include absorbed gamma dose rates and effective dose rates. The most important radionuclides in the environment that may be of significance for radiological analysis are 238U and 232Th and their decay series, and 40K [13]. In some instances, informal houses have been built onto old slimes dams, exposing the populace to the different health risks arising from the inhalation of radon and dust-borne radionuclides [7, 14]. Given the absence of proper waste management techniques in most areas, radionuclides can end up contaminating adjacent territories and transboundary areas due to transport by prevailing wind, river and ground water flows [15]. A few studies have focussed on radionuclides in dust from the soil and related sources, such as mine tailings in the West Rand mining area [16–18]. Not many of these studies used a holistic approach to understanding dust from the mining operations, including the radionuclide composition.

To our knowledge the number of studies in South Africa that have investigated radioactive contamination from dust particulates in mining areas is limited. In this study radiological parameters were evaluated from the activity of radionuclides to estimate the exposure risk to populations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Description of the study areas

The study was carried out in the West Rand mining area and the Free State area where there are uranium and gold mines. The locations of the study areas and surrounding communities are shown on the map in Fig. 1. Mine A is in the Free State area where gold and uranium mining is done, and mines B and C are located in Carletonville where there is gold mining. However, since uranium usually occurs together with gold, uranium is also extracted as a byproduct in the two mines B and C. The West Rand area (Highveld) has short, cold winters and long, hot summers. Rainfall occurs mostly in summer and the annual rainfall ranges from 600 to 732 mm. The period October–March is often characterized by heavy storms and the average annual temperature is 16°C [19, 20]. The area has a well-defined surface drainage system, which includes streams, dams and wetlands. In the Free State province, the area is characterized by a continental climate with warm to hot summers and cool to cold winters. Precipitation mainly occurs in summer, with the highest rainfall recorded in the month of January. Rainfall ranges from 400 to 600 mm per annum, with the lowest rainfall recorded in July. The background area the Welgegund research station was selected since there are no mining activities taking place there and hence there are no direct impacts from pollution sources in close proximity. It is considered to be a regionally representative background site. The location of the Welgegund research station is at 1480 m above sea level, at 26°34′10″S latitude and 26°56′21″E longitude [21].

Sample collection

Two areas in the West Rand mining area in Gauteng Province and Odendaalsrus area in the Free State Province were selected conveniently targeting where gold and uranium mines exist or have existed. Soil and dust samples were randomly collected from different positions in the different study areas for determination of the level of NORMs and heavy metals in the samples. The samples were collected in summer and in winter to cater for seasonal variations. Collection of the samples was done from January 2016 to March 2018 in the different study areas.

Soil and dust sampling

This was done using the method utilized in previous studies [22, 23]. Composite surface soil samples were collected at different points from mine tailings, roadsides and settlements. From the tailings, the top layer 2–5 cm was scrapped using a scrapping metal. For the villages, roadsides and farm lands, the collection was done by gently sweeping the sampling area with a soft plastic brush. Multiple subsamples were collected around a geo-referenced point. Subsamples were mixed into one composite sample for each soil. One kilogram of each sample was collected. These samples were packed into neat polyethylene bags, marked for later identification and finally transported to the laboratory for preparation and analysis. At the Welgegund monitoring station (background area), dust samples were collected on 47 mm filters and with pore size of 10 μm using a Partisol sequential air sampler. The flow rates of air sampling were set at an initial value of 16 l min−1. The sampler’s flow rate was calibrated before each sampling cycle and monitored periodically using the sampler’s built-in flow control system. The soil samples were then sealed in labelled airtight vials to prevent the escape of radiogenic gases, radon (222Rn) and thoron (220Rn) [25]. Before measurements, the vials were kept sealed for 4 weeks in order to achieve secular equilibrium of the 238U and 232Th and their respective progenies. Secular equilibrium is attained when the activity of the progeny is equal to the activity of the parent radionuclide.

Filters with dust were placed in vials and sealed for 4 weeks to attain secular equilibrium. After attainment of secular equilibrium, the activity concentrations were determined by gamma spectroscopy. Measurement of these samples was done at the Analytical Laboratory.

The radius of the vial (‘R’ big radius and ‘r’ small radius) and height were measured using a vernier calliper. The measurements recorded were R = 7.25 mm, r = 6 mm and height = 40 mm. These were used to calculate the surface area of the vial using the formula 2π (R2 − r2) + 2πh (R + r). The area of the filter paper onto which the dust collected was determined using the formula A = πr2. The volume of the vial was determined using the formula V = πr2h. The height considered was up to the mark where the filter would fit in the vial when folded. The density of dust was then determined using the mass of the dust collected divided by the volume. Gamma spectroscopy

Energy and efficiency calibration was done using the following radioactive sources obtained from Eckert & Ziegler: 238U, 241Am and 152Eu. These sources were used to prepare a mixed standard solution. The activity concentrations of 238U and 232Th were determined by measuring the counts of γ-ray energies of daughter nuclides assuming secular equilibrium exists in the decay chain. The 238U content was determined assuming secular equilibrium with 226Ra; hence, the daughter nuclides at the following energies were used: 214Pb 351.9 and 295 keV and 214Bi 609.2, 1764.5 and 1120.29 keV. For 232Th, the following daughter nuclides and their energies were used: 212Pb 238.6 keV and 228Ac 911.60 and 968.9 keV. The activity concentrations of 40K and 137Cs were quantified using their characteristic γ-ray energies of 1460.81 keV and 661.65 keV respectively. The time for the determination was 12 hr per sample.

DISCUSSION

The results in Table 1 show the gamma dose rates in air at 1 m above the ground. Of the three areas, mine C recorded the lowest values for the ADRA, while mine A recorded the highest doses. Mine A and mine C had mean values that were above and below the world average of 51 nGy hr−1, respectively [37]. The order of increasing gamma dose rates in air in terms of mining areas is C < B < A. Differences in dose rates could be attributed to geological differences, with mine A in the Free State having the highest dose rates. The Free State goldfields around Welkom are known for gold deposits closely associated with uranium-bearing minerals in the Witwatersrand Supergroup. Uranium, a significant gamma emitter, increases absorbed dose rates in the air. Mines B and C in Carletonville are also in the Witwatersrand Basin, mainly focused on gold mining and with generally a lower uranium content compared to Free State deposits. However, some gold reefs may still contain trace uranium or radioactive decay products, leading to moderate dose rates at mine B. Mine C likely has even lower uranium content, or a different host rock, for example, less radioactive conglomerates, resulting in the lowest dose rates. There is a high degree of variability in contribution to the dose rate by the radionuclides, as shown in Fig. 3. Appendix 1 shows the descriptive statistics from SPSS of the activity concentrations of the radionuclides in the study areas. The worldwide average values for contributions to gamma dose rates in air have been reported by UNSCEAR to be 25%, 40% and 35% for 238U (226Ra), 232Th and 40K, respectively [27, 35]. The average value of the AEDE was found to be higher in mine A, followed by mine B, and mine C recorded the least value. The average value of the AEDE was found to be 130Sv (or ~0.13 mSv y−1) in mine A and 95Sv (or ~0.095 mSv y−1) in mine B. The acceptable levels for existing exposure scenarios for the general public are 1–20 mSv y−1, preferably <10 mSv y−1 [38, 39]. For planned exposures, the dose limit for occupationally exposed workers is 20 mSv y−1, while the annual dose limit for the public is 1 mSv. The mean ELCR in all three mines is lower than the world average of 1.45 × 10−3; accordingly the cancer risk was found to be negligible[40].

As shown in Table 3, the radioactivity in terrestrial soils varies from country to country. The results from this study lie well within the mean values for studies in Nigeria, Botswana and Cameroon given in Table 3. However, when looking at the ADRA range, this study had the maximum value of 137 nGy hr−1 in mine A compared to 316 nGy hr−1, the maximum reached in a study in Malnichera, Bangladesh. The authors attributed the high doses to excess use of agricultural fertilizers and pesticides, since the soils were obtained from a tea garden[32]. In this study, the values for annual effective doses in all three study areas are lower than the recommended limit by ICRP of 1 mSv y−1 for the public[36, 38]. They are in agreement with mean values of effective doses from Cameroon, India, Nigeria and Botswana as shown in Table 3 [33–36]. The cancer risk values calculated in this study ranged from 8.00 × 10−5 to 6.04 × 10−4. The values are lower than the calculated values from India and Turkey [31, 34]. Figure 4 shows the measured dust concentrations in the air in the background area (Welgegund). The sources of dust in this area could be activities such as industry, agriculture and fugitive dust from roads. A significant local source of wind-blown dust in this area could be farming activities within the 60 km radius of Welgegund [41]. The dust concentration was high during the month of October 2017 (4.65 × 10−3 mg m−3) and lowest in February and March 2018, with a value of 2.23 × 10−3 mg m−3. The climate and weather of the background area, just like that of South Africa, is characterized by wet and dry seasons, which influence the concentrations of dust. The period August to November is characterized by increases in wind speeds; therefore, the high dust concentrations can be attributed to the wind generation [23]. The period January to March has lower dust concentrations due to the wet season, so the dust was reduced.

The background NORM activity levels were also measured and presented (Appendix 2). The activity concentration for 40K ranged from 2.98 × 10−5 to 6.00 × 10−5 Bq m−3, whereas those for 214Pb (238U) and 214Bi (238U) had a minimum of 7.11 × 10−6 and 1.96 × 10−5 Bq m−3 and a maximum of 2.16 × 10−5 and 3.60 × 10−5 Bq m−3, respectively. The world averages for the mean atmospheric activity concentrations of 232Th and 238U associated with dust are 0.5 and 1.0 μBq m−3, respectively [42]. In the background area, the activity concentrations of 214Pb and 214Bi, which are decay products of 238U, were above the world average. It cannot be disputed that there is anthropogenic influence from industries from pollutant source regions whereby air masses can carry these radionuclides, thereby impacting the background area. However, 40K had an activity concentration that was slightly above the world average value of 2.2 × 10−5 Bq m−3 [43]. The 40K range was 2.98 × 10−5–6.00 × 10−5 Bq m−3. The difference in the activity value for 40K from the world average might be due to plant residues in soils from the area. Moreover, the soils might have been fertilized with inorganic fertilizers and contain animal residues since the area is surrounded by farms. Hence, the background area as expected exhibited low levels of activity concentrations of NORMs, some of which were not even detected. This therefore implies that the health risk to the public from the ionizing radiation is low.

CONCLUSION

Most of the radiological parameters of the study were within the accepted international values, meaning the soils in the areas could be considered to be safe for the humans living there. The results show that the mean ELCRs were 4.50 × 10−4, 3.34 × 10−4 and 1.74 × 10−4Sv in the study areas at mines A, B and C, respectively. Lower dust concentrations were recorded and the radionuclides detected in the background area (Welgegund monitoring station) had low activity concentrations. However, abandoned and active gold mines require continuous monitoring and caution should be taken against long-term effects.
