Predictive Value of Referral Concerns for Autism Spectrum Disorder and Global Developmental Delay: A Retrospective Audit of a Tertiary Paediatric Developmental Service
Authors: O Hamed, S Shrestha, H Bhurawala, A Poulton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo examine the predictive value of specific concerns documented in paediatric referral letters for subsequent diagnoses of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and global developmental delay (GDD) within a tertiary child development clinic.MethodsA retrospective audit was conducted of consecutive children assessed at the Nepean Hospital Child Development Clinic between 2015 and 2019. Referral letters, supporting documents, and medical records were reviewed to identify referral concerns and diagnostic outcomes. Chi‐square analyses were used to explore associations between referral concerns and final diagnoses.ResultsAmong 161 children assessed, 68% were under 5 years of age, and 78% were male. Speech delay (80%) and social interaction concerns (78%) were the most frequently documented referral issues. Following multidisciplinary assessment, 51% of children referred for ASD were diagnosed with ASD, and 57% referred for developmental concerns were diagnosed with GDD. Speech delay was the only referral concern significantly associated with an ASD diagnosis (p = 0.002). Behavioural concerns predicted attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and GDD (p < 0.001), while motor delay predicted GDD (p < 0.05). ADHD, although not a primary focus of the clinic during the study period, emerged as a clinically relevant co‐occurring outcome. Ten percent of children received no neurodevelopmental diagnosis.ConclusionsReferral concerns varied widely in their diagnostic utility. Speech delay demonstrated the strongest predictive value for ASD, while motor delay and behavioural problems were associated with GDD. Other commonly cited concerns lacked diagnostic specificity. These findings highlight the clinical value of detailed symptom‐based referral information and support the role of early speech pathology assessment in improving triage and prioritisation for ASD within tertiary developmental services.Keywords: autism spectrum disorder, developmental assessment, global developmental delay, paediatrics, predictive value, referral

Introduction

Background/Rationale

Multidisciplinary child development clinics play a critical role in the assessment of children with complex neurodevelopmental concerns [1, 2]. These services provide comprehensive developmental surveillance, structured assessment, and coordinated recommendations for intervention. In Australia, demand for developmental assessment, particularly for autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and global developmental delay (GDD), has increased substantially over the past decade, driven by heightened community awareness, improved early identification, and the expansion of the National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS) [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. As a result, waiting times for diagnostic assessment in public services frequently exceed recommended timeframes, creating barriers to timely diagnosis and early intervention [1, 3, 8].

Accurate and detailed referral information is essential for effective triage within these high‐demand services [9, 10]. Referral letters often represent the first point of clinical information available to tertiary teams, yet the quality and specificity of referral content vary widely. International studies suggest that certain presenting concerns, such as language delay, social communication difficulties, or behavioural problems, may be associated with neurodevelopmental diagnoses [10, 11, 12]. However, Australian data examining the predictive value of specific referral concerns remains limited, particularly within hospital‐based developmental clinics [9, 13]. While ASD has been the focus of most research in this area, GDD is a diagnostic outcome of at least equal importance in early childhood, with significant implications for developmental support and long‐term functioning [1, 2, 5].

The Nepean Hospital Child Development Clinic is a tertiary service, accepting referrals only from specialists. This pathway is intentional: referring paediatricians are expected to initiate preliminary assessment, management, and early intervention, ensuring that children seen in the clinic have already undergone initial evaluation and that service demand remains manageable. Similar referral models are used in other tertiary developmental services, including in Western Sydney. Identifying referral concerns that predict specific diagnoses helps optimise triage, prioritise children likely to have ASD or GDD, and inform referrers about useful details for developmental assessment.

Screening tools such as the Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers (M‐CHAT‐R), Adult ADHD Self‐Report Scale (ASRS) or Vanderbilt scales are sometimes included in referral documentation, but these instruments identify risk rather than providing a diagnosis [9, 13]. Distinguishing between screening results, provisional impressions, and formal diagnostic outcomes is therefore critical. Attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) was not a primary focus of the clinic during the study period, as ADHD diagnosis and treatment generally occurred in private paediatric practice at the time of the study. However, ADHD is an important neurodevelopmental condition and is increasingly managed in primary care settings due to the recent changes in general practitioner prescribing [1, 5, 10]. Understanding the behavioural concerns that predict an ADHD diagnosis has relevance for triaging and recommendation of more appropriate pathways for diagnosis and management.

This study aims to examine the predictive value of specific concerns documented in paediatric referral letters for subsequent diagnoses of ASD, GDD, and likely ADHD within a tertiary child development clinic.

Methods

Design and Setting

This retrospective cross‐sectional audit included consecutive children assessed at the Nepean Hospital Child Development Clinic between January 2015 and January 2019. The clinic functions as a tertiary‐level developmental assessment service within Western Sydney. Ethics approval was obtained from the Nepean Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee (Apollo 18 54).

Clinic Context and Referral Pathway

The clinic provides multidisciplinary assessment for children with complex neurodevelopmental presentations, including suspected ASD and GDD. The clinic services individuals residing in the Nepean Blue Mountains Local Health District. Referrals typically consist of free‐text letters supplemented by supporting documents such as speech pathology reports, occupational therapy notes, preschool reports, or parent‐completed questionnaires.

Participants

All children aged 0–18 years who underwent a full multidisciplinary diagnostic assessment during the study period were eligible. Children were excluded only if the referral letter was unavailable. For school‐aged children, prior psychometric assessment, such as the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children Fourth Edition (WISC‐IV), Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence Fourth Edition (WPPSI), Adaptive Behaviour Assessment System, Third Edition (ABAS‐3) and Vineland‐3, was required. The clinic did not accept referrals solely for review of a diagnosis of ASD made elsewhere. However, for children already identified as having GDD, multidisciplinary assessment was considered appropriate for accurate prioritisation of their ongoing developmental needs.

Triage Process

All referrals were reviewed by a developmental paediatrician, paediatric registrar, and clinical psychologist. Triage decisions were based on referral concerns, developmental history, supporting documentation, and prior assessments or screening results. Triage determined the assessment pathway, including the need for developmental testing, cognitive assessment, and adaptive behaviour assessment.

Diagnostic Assessment

Each child underwent detailed developmental and medical history, observation of free play and social communication, physical and neurological examination and standardised developmental or cognitive testing as appropriate. This was then followed by multidisciplinary case discussion. Diagnoses of ASD and GDD were made according to the DSM‐5 criteria. GDD was defined as significant delay in at least two developmental domains in children under 5 years as per the DSM‐5 criteria [2]. Formal assessment tools, such as Autism Diagnostic Interview‐Revised (ADI‐R), Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS), Griffiths Mental Developmental Assessment (GMDS) III, were used in diagnostic assessment based on individual patient needs. ADHD was sometimes included as a secondary diagnostic outcome, but in many cases, an impression of ADHD was not followed through with formal diagnostic assessment.

Data Collection

Data were extracted from medical records and referral documentation and entered into a spreadsheet. Extracted variables included demographic characteristics, referral source, source of documented concern, specific referral concerns, prior assessments or screening results, and diagnostic outcomes, as seen in Table 1.

Coding of Referral Concerns

Referral concerns were coded into predefined categories based on common neurodevelopmental presentations and refined after initial review. Coding was performed by a paediatric registrar and checked by a supervising developmental paediatrician. A random subset of records was independently re‐coded to confirm consistency. Only concerns clearly documented in referral letters or accompanying reports were coded.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 31). Continuous variables were summarised using means and ranges; categorical variables using frequencies and percentages. Associations between referral concerns and diagnostic outcomes were examined using chi‐square tests, with significance set at p < 0.05. No multivariable modelling was performed due to sample size constraints. Cases with missing referral data (< 2%) were excluded.

Results

A total of 163 children were assessed during the study period. Two were excluded due to missing referral documentation, leaving 161 records for analysis (Figure 1).

Demographic Characteristics

Of the 161 children assessed, 109 (68%) were under 5 years of age and 128 (78%) were male. The mean waiting time from referral to assessment was 10 months.

Prior Assessment and Screening

Thirty‐eight children (24%) had undergone developmental screening or preliminary assessment before referral. Screening tools included the M‐CHAT‐R, ASRS for ASD, and Vanderbilt scales. These instruments identify developmental risk but do not provide a formal diagnosis. Positive screening results were therefore recorded as indicators of concern rather than pre‐existing diagnoses.

Among children with positive ASD screening results, a proportion were later diagnosed with ASD, with the M‐CHAT‐R showing the strongest alignment. All children previously identified as having developmental delays through community assessments were confirmed to meet criteria for GDD following formal multidisciplinary evaluation. Two of three children with behavioural screening suggestive of ADHD were subsequently diagnosed with ADHD. This is depicted in Table 2.

Referral Source

Most referrals originated from general paediatricians (95%), with the remainder from developmental paediatricians (n = 2), neurologists (n = 4) and a geneticist (n = 1).

Reason for Referral

Referral reasons included ASD assessment alone (40%), developmental assessment alone (10%), and combined ASD and developmental concerns (50%). This translated to 148 children referred for ASD assessment and 96 children referred with developmental concerns, with 81 children referred with both.

Source of Documented Concern(s)

Neurodevelopmental concerns most commonly originated from paediatricians (98%), but some referrals reported concerns expressed by parents (53%), speech pathologists (26%), preschool teachers (19%), occupational therapists (14%) and early intervention teams (12%), as portrayed in Figure 2. ASD

Speech delay was the only referral concern significantly associated with an ASD diagnosis (p = 0.002). Other commonly cited concerns, such as poor eye contact and sensory behaviours, were not significant predictors (Table 4).

Discussion

This study examined the predictive value of specific concerns documented in paediatric referral letters for subsequent diagnoses of ASD and GDD within a tertiary child development clinic. By focusing on symptom‐level predictors rather than referral labels, the findings provide practical insights into how referral information can support more accurate triage and prioritisation in high‐demand developmental services.

Principal Findings

Speech delay emerged as the only referral concern significantly associated with an eventual ASD diagnosis. This finding is consistent with international and Australian literature showing that language delay is one of the earliest and most observable developmental ‘red flags’, often prompting referral even before ASD is explicitly suspected [5, 10, 12]. This may be due to speech delay being easily recognised by parents. Delayed milestones for speech development can be documented accurately in referral letters. In contrast, social communication differences, such as reduced eye contact, limited reciprocal interaction, or repetitive behaviours, tend to be more subjective and inconsistently reported and may fluctuate across settings [2, 11] hence their limited predictive value in this cohort.

Motor delay and behavioural concerns were more strongly associated with GDD, reflecting the broader developmental impact of GDD across multiple domains. Motor delay is a core feature of GDD and aligns with DSM‐5 criteria requiring significant delay in at least two developmental domains [2]. Behavioural concerns, including dysregulation, inattention, or oppositional behaviours, were associated with both GDD and ADHD. This is consistent with evidence that behavioural difficulties in early childhood are transdiagnostic and may reflect underlying cognitive, adaptive, or communication challenges rather than a specific neurodevelopmental condition [11]. The identification of ADHD in a substantial proportion of children, despite ADHD not being a primary focus of the clinic during the study period, underscores the importance of considering the indicators of ADHD and the possible need for structured ADHD assessment in referral pathways [9, 10].

Importantly, 10% of children did not receive any neurodevelopmental diagnosis following multidisciplinary assessment. This highlights the challenges faced by referrers in distinguishing between developmental variation, isolated delays, and neurodevelopmental disorders, particularly in younger children, where developmental trajectories are highly variable [9, 11, 12].

Interpretation in the Context of Existing Literature

The prominence of speech delay as a predictor of ASD aligns with previous studies showing that language delay is one of the most common presenting concerns in children later diagnosed with ASD [5, 10, 12]. In contrast, social communication differences may be more subtle, context‐dependent, or misinterpreted, reducing their predictive value when documented in isolation [2].

The association between motor delay and GDD is consistent with the developmental profile of GDD, with delays across multiple domains [2]. Motor delay may therefore serve as an early indicator of broader developmental deficits. Similarly, the association between behavioural concerns and both GDD and ADHD reflects the non‐specific nature of behavioural difficulties in early childhood. Behavioural dysregulation may arise from frustration, sensory processing differences, communication challenges, or emerging attentional difficulties, making it an imprecise predictor of any single diagnosis [11].

The findings also reinforce the limitations of relying on screening tools alone. Consistent with Australian data, children with positive screening results did not all receive corresponding diagnoses, highlighting the limitations of screening instruments [9, 13]. Multidisciplinary assessment remains important for accurate diagnosis and identification of areas of development requiring ongoing support, particularly in complex presentations.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this study is the use of real‐world clinical data from a tertiary developmental clinic. The wide range of presenting symptoms increases the potential for identifying the predictive value of referral concerns.

At the same time, several limitations should be considered. As the study is retrospective, there was limited control over the consistency of referral information and variability in the quality of the documentation. However, variability in clinical documentation could be representative of real‐world practice at the time of the study.

Detailed multivariable analyses were not possible due to the limited sample size. It is possible that with a larger sample and more consistent reporting, some of the more subtle social and sensory concerns might have had some predictive value for a diagnosis of ASD.

It is also worth noting that the cohort predates the COVID‐19 pandemic. Given the documented changes in paediatric healthcare utilisation and the well‐being of children with neurodevelopmental conditions during this period, referral patterns may have shifted in ways not captured here [14, 15, 16, 17]. In particular, developmental assessment of children in their home environment using video consultation can provide different insights. However, the predictive diagnostic value of speech development is likely to remain.

Finally, referrals in this study originated from paediatricians, which may limit generalisability to settings where a wider range of professionals contribute to referral pathways. Even so, this model is not uncommon in tertiary services and may still be representative.

Clinical Implications

These findings have several implications for clinical practice. First, detailed documentation of speech and language development appears particularly valuable for triage, given its predictive association with ASD and GDD. Early referral to speech pathology may therefore support both timely intervention and more accurate prioritisation for tertiary assessment. Second, behavioural concerns should prompt consideration of GDD or ADHD. The results also highlight the continued need for strong referral processes to ensure appropriate triaging. The findings suggest that the inclusion of developmental milestones and the integration of speech pathology assessment may enhance the quality of the information available at triage. Given the high demand for developmental services, this information may facilitate the prioritisation of children most likely to have ASD or GDD. This in turn may help optimise resource allocation and reduce delays for those with the greatest need. There may also be a case for a policy specifying initial assessment for ADHD prior to clinic referral, particularly for children principally presenting with behavioural concerns.

Future Research

Future research should examine combinations or clusters of referral concerns, as single symptoms may lack diagnostic specificity [11]. Longitudinal studies could explore whether children who do not initially meet criteria for ASD or GDD may later receive diagnoses, providing insight into developmental trajectories. Multicentre studies with larger samples would enable more robust modelling of predictors and improve generalisability. Finally, evaluating the impact of structured referral tools, early speech pathology assessment, and allied health‐led triage models may inform service redesign and policy development.

Conclusion

This study shows that specific referral concerns differ markedly in their diagnostic value within a tertiary child development clinic. Speech delay was the strongest predictor of ASD, while motor and behavioural concerns were more closely associated with GDD. Other commonly reported concerns lacked diagnostic specificity. These findings highlight the importance of detailed, symptom‐focused referral information to enhance triage accuracy. Additionally, early involvement of allied health professionals, particularly speech pathologists, may enhance triage accuracy and provide an opportunity for early intervention while children await formal multidisciplinary assessment.
