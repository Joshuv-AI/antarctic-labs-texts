Single‐Photon Emission From TMDCs through Strong Coupling to EQ Plasmon Modes of an Au Metasurface
Authors: Andergachew Mekonnen Berhe, Haroldo T Hattori, Andrey E Miroshnichenko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn this study, we theoretically demonstrate that a strongly coupled hybrid platform consisting of a compact gold plasmonic metamaterial (metasurface) and a monolayer of transition‐metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs) enables scalable single‐photon emission (SPE) at room temperature. The Au metasurface is designed to support electric‐quadrupole (EQ) resonance that efficiently couples to excitons in tungsten diselenide (WSe2), molybdenum disulfide (MoS2), and molybdenum diselenide (MoSe2). The planar metasurface geometry enables in‐plane field overlap with the TMDC exciton, yielding a pronounced coupling strength with a vacuum Rabi splitting exceeding 100 meV. To connect this enhanced coupling to our investigation of SPE purity, we first realized conventional photon blockade (CPB) in an Au–WSe2 hybrid under weak coherent driving, achieving g(2)(0)=4×10−4. We then demonstrated unconventional photon blockade (UnPB) in Au–WSe2, Au–MoS2, and Au–MoSe2 using a two‐tone coherent drive to induce destructive interference between two‐photon excitation pathways, resulting in very high purity. Additionally, pure dephasing of the emitters up to Γϕ=40 meV was considered, and antibunching was preserved. These results establish a scalable route to room‐temperature, high‐purity SPE for quantum communication and integrated quantum photonics.Keywords: antibunching, coupling strength, electric quadrupole, photon blockade, single photon purity

Introduction

Quantum nanophotonics provides a versatile platform for confining and coherently controlling individual quanta of light and matter at deeply sub‐wavelength scales, enabling deterministic single‐photon generation and on‐chip quantum functionality [1, 2]. In this context, single photons become a universal resource for encoding qubits across quantum communication and photonic quantum computing [3, 4, 5]. In quantum computing [6], photonic qubits support superposition and parallel processing, whereas in quantum communication [7], deterministic single‐photon sources (SPSs) underpin quantum key distribution and related cryptographic protocols with provable security. Subsequently, over the past decade, solid‐state platforms ranging from defect centers in diamond to semiconductor quantum dots (QDs), hexagonal boron nitride (hBN) defect emitters, and colloidal/perovskite QDs have been pursued as SPSs [8]. However, hBN defect emitters still face challenges associated with spectral diffusion, blinking, emitter‐to‐emitter variability, and deterministic placement of optically active defects, which can limit reproducibility [9]. Additionally, hBN defect emitters exhibit lower oscillator strengths compared with TMDCs, resulting in weaker light–matter coupling and lower achievable Rabi splittings in cavity‐coupled geometries [10]. Additionally, III–V epitaxial QDs generally require cryogenic operation and are sensitive to charge noise and phonon sidebands, forcing trade‐offs between brightness and indistinguishability unless complex cavity architectures are employed [11]. Besides, although these QDs have been integrated with optical metasurfaces to control emission directionality, spin state, and photon out‐coupling [12], they still rely on epitaxially embedded emitters and post‐growth nanophotonic patterning. In contrast, TMDC monolayers offer an atomically thin van der Waals emitter platform that can be dry‐transferred onto prefabricated photonic or plasmonic metasurface cavities, enabling flexible heterogeneous integration and strain‐defined localization. Moreover, colloidal perovskite QDs also suffer from limited photostability and environmental robustness, as well as concerns about material toxicity, despite recent advances in surface passivation [13]. Additionally, despite notable progress in TMDC‐based SPE, the operating temperature remains limited to below 150 K [14]. Thus, existing quantum light sources still face limited integrability, restricted spectral and spatial tunability, and inefficiencies in achieving deterministic on‐demand single‐photon generation [15].

To develop scalable, integrable SP emitters and operate them at room temperature, they should exhibit high emission efficiency and purity [16]. The purity of these photons can be measured through the second‐order correlation function at zero delay, g(2)(0), with an ideal value of g(2)(0)=0, corresponding to unity purity defined as P=1−g(2)(0) [17]. As a result, one promising route to single‐photon generation with high brightness and purity is through cavity quantum electrodynamics (cQED) [3]. For instance, He et al. demonstrated the possibility of SPE from a bare WSe2 monolayer under cryogenic conditions, achieving g(2)(0)=0.21±0.06 [18]. Beyond WSe2, other monolayer TMDCs such as MoSe2 and MoS2 have also emerged as promising quantum emitters, exhibiting localized SPE from strain‐ or defect‐induced potential wells. Site‐controlled quantum emitters in monolayer MoSe2 have been realized through nanoscale strain confinement [19], whereas defect‐engineered MoS2 emitters created through focused helium‐ion irradiation and encapsulated in hBN have shown stable photon antibunching [20]. However, challenges in bare monolayer TMDCs at room temperature include strong exciton–phonon interactions and inefficient light extraction. At 300 K, the exciton linewidth in TMDC monolayers broadens to tens of meV due to phonon‐induced dephasing and scattering, washing out the zero‐phonon line and severely limiting the coherence time of the exciton transition [21]. In a bare monolayer, spontaneous emission is also not directed into a specific optical mode, so most photons radiate into a wide solid angle, resulting in low collection efficiency. To improve this, it has been demonstrated that by deterministically coupling WSe2 emitters to gold gap‐plasmon nanocavities, the measured cavity‐enhanced quantum yield jumps from ∼1% to as high as 65% (average 44%) [22]. Even with cavity‐enhanced quantum yields up to 65%, the emission remains limited to cryogenic temperatures, as nonradiative channels and spectral diffusion dominate at higher temperatures. Moreover, it should be noted that despite the enhancement in spontaneous emission rates facilitated by QE–cavity systems, a significant challenge arises from photon bunching. This phenomenon can occur in the absence of strong photon blockade, when the cavity mode allows multiphoton occupancy, thereby increasing the probability of multiphoton emission [8]. To mitigate photon bunching and promote SPE with high purity, spatially aligning quantum emitters, such as TMDC monolayers, with photonic/plasmonic cavities can enable strong light–matter coupling, leading to photon blockade [23].

Despite advances, realizing room‐temperature SPSs with high purity remains an outstanding challenge, motivating novel approaches in materials and device design. More recently, to address the operating temperature issue, Do et al. demonstrated room‐temperature strong coupling between carbon‐based SPEs in a few‐layer hBN film and a high‐Q bound‐state‐in‐the‐continuum (BIC) cavity. This platform achieved a Rabi splitting of ∼4meV with g(2)(0)=0.28, resulting in an emission purity of 1−g(2)(0)≈72% [10]. Although the photon antibunching was confirmed in the strong coupling regime at room temperature, the oscillator strength of hBN resulted in a much lower Rabi splitting. This, in turn, led to reduced anharmonicity and decreased photon purity. Additionally, another research group has sought to demonstrate antibunching in photons emitted from a strongly coupled single‐QD‐pillar microcavity system at low temperature [24]. However, due to the relatively small Purcell factor of 61±7, the measured g(2)(0) was =0.18. Therefore, it is evident that the challenges in developing a compact and “ideal” SPE are mainly due to the lack of effective coupling between the emitter and the cavity to enhance anharmonicity and emit photons on demand. In parallel, dielectric metasurfaces and photonic crystals have emerged as a complementary route to SPE because they can support low‐loss and higher Q‐factor than plasmonic nanostructures. These features can reduce dissipative broadening and help preserve optical coherence when coupled with emitters [16, 25, 26]. However, in many dielectric resonators, the optical field is concentrated within the high‐index resonator rather than in air, where the TMDC monolayers can be placed. This can reduce exciton–photon overlap, leading to reduced light–matter interaction in the hybrid structures. In this regard, our previous work demonstrated a lithium niobate metasurface with guided‐mode‐resonance platforms coupled to TMDC monolayers, where the Rabi splitting was lower than that achieved with plasmonic metasurface‐TMDC platforms [27, 28].

To address this question, we designed and theoretically investigated a miniaturized SPS by coupling TMDC monolayer emitters to a subwavelength plasmonic Au metasurface. Additionally, the resonant Au metasurface exhibits a tunable Fano‐shaped resonance that can be adjusted by varying its design parameters, which is crucial for aligning the plasmonic mode with the exciton mode of WSe2 and other TMDC monolayers [28]. The designed Au metasurface, hereafter referred to as the Au cavity, supports an electric quadrupole (EQ) mode with a higher Q‐factor, facilitating strong coupling with MoS2, WSe2, and MoSe2 monolayers at room temperature. These components are essential for realizing ultra‐compact SPS devices operating in the strong coupling regime, as they enhance the system's anharmonicity. This anharmonicity can be exploited to achieve the photon‐blockade regime, thereby enabling precise on‐demand control over the number of photons transmitted under resonant excitation. As a result, we initially examined the optical properties of the Au metasurface using multipole analysis and discussed its applications after coupling to WSe2, MoS2, and MoSe2. Strong coupling was systematically investigated by varying metasurface thickness, air‐gap width (gap distance), and periodicity [27, 29].

The results indicate that significant Rabi splitting, exceeding 110 meV, can be achieved in Au‐TMDC hybrid nanostructures by optimizing the Au nanostructure parameters. This substantial Rabi splitting is attributed to the higher Q‐factor of the EQ mode induced by the Au cavity. This strong coupling regime at room temperature induces pronounced anharmonicity, resulting in high‐purity SPE from the Au–WSe2 system with g(2)(0)=4.0×10−4 through CPB. Furthermore, applying the UnPB to the strongly coupled Au–TMDC systems at a drive amplitude of Ωp=0.5meV yields even higher photon purity, with g(2)(0)=1.64×10−4 for Au–WSe2, 2.30×10−4 for Au–MoS2, and 1.59×10−4 for Au–MoSe2). The purity of the emitted photons in these hybrid systems increases (i.e., the g(2)(0) value decreases) as the drive strength is reduced. Comparing our results with bare TMDCs emitters, TMDC‐coupled to dielectric microcavities, and plasmonic nanocavity‐WSe2, our design offers a scalable field enhancement, provides improved TMDC emitter‐cavity overlap, and enables efficient photon extraction [22]. Our results highlight the potential of Au cavity‐TMDCs to realize scalable room‐temperature SPSs with near‐unity purity, thereby opening avenues for integrated quantum photonic devices, including on‐chip quantum networks and secure quantum communications.

Result and Discussion

Multipole Analysis of Au Metasurface

To reveal the physical nature of the designed Au metasurface (cavity), multipole decompositions were performed across a range of design parameters. Subsequently, to analyze the light scattering from the metasurface illustrated in Figure 1a, a Cartesian multipole decomposition was applied. The scattered power was obtained from the Cartesian multipole expansion by integrating the current–density distribution J within the metasurface unit cell (see Figure 1b and Supporting Information S1: Section S1) [36, 37]. As can be seen from Figure 1d–f, the incident electric field excites an electric–dipole (ED) mode, producing a broadband scattering spectrum centered near 500nm. Because ED modes couple efficiently to the far field, they exhibit intrinsically low quality (Q) factors and therefore broad absorption bands. Additionally, this ED response diminishes, and a dominant electric–quadrupole (EQ) resonance emerges at longer wavelengths (660–730nm). Regardless of thickness and/or slit gap, the EQ mode becomes predominant, whereas the contributions from magnetic dipole (MD), magnetic quadrupole (MQ), and toroidal dipole (TD) remain minimal. Near–field intensity maps (Figure 1c) verify that EQ‐dominant regimes confine energy within the meta–atom, suppressing radiative leakage and leading to narrow high‐Q absorption features. Because quadrupolar radiation is two orders higher in the multipole hierarchy, it couples only weakly to the far field, further reducing radiation losses. This suppression enhances the Q‐factor, which could compensate for Ohmic losses in gold by prolonging the dwell time of energy in near‐field hotspots. As shown in Figure 1g–i, the resulting EQ‐dominated absorption spectra provide clear advantages for light–matter interaction.

Photon‐Blockade in Au‐WSe2

The demonstrated strong coupling in the Au cavity–WSe2 monolayer, with large Rabi splitting shown in Figure 3, reveals that the system is highly anharmonic [42]. Hence, the strongly coupled Au cavity interacting with a monolayer WSe2 exciton can give rise to pronounced quantum nonlinearities, enabling conventional photon blockade (CPB). To implement and dynamically manipulate PB in this strongly coupled system, we apply weak driving fields to the cavity in Equation (6).

CPB is a quantum nonlinear effect in which the absorption of a single photon by an Au cavity prevents the entry of a second. This phenomenon originates from the anharmonicity of the JCs energy ladder in a strongly coupled Au–WSe2 system. As can be seen from Figure 4a, once the first rung is occupied, the higher rungs are energetically shifted by an amount set by g, preventing resonant re‐excitation and giving rise to CPB. When the first photon is resonantly absorbed, it shifts the effective resonance frequency for the second photon, effectively blocking the cavity and enforcing the emission of photons one by one (see Figure 3a). This is a direct consequence of a two‐level system strongly coupled to a cavity, which gives rise to polaritonic dressed states and the photon blockade effect [43]. This, in turn, results in strongly antibunched emission.

Subsequently, the proposed hybrid system, an Au cavity strongly coupled to a monolayer of WSe2, is exceptionally well suited for realizing CPB at room temperature. The key figure of merit here is the light–matter coupling strength g, which in our system reaches tens of meV. This leads to a high cooperativity C=4g2/ΓPΓX>11, placing the system firmly in the strong coupling regime required for CPB. Numerical demonstrations of Rabi splittings exceeding 100 meV in similar plasmon–TMDC systems [28] confirm that robust room‐temperature operation is feasible. Moreover, the giant optical nonlinearities in WSe2 directly strengthen the intrinsic nonlinearity that drives the blockade.

To quantitatively describe CPB, we numerically solve the dissipative JCs Hamiltonian [Equation 16]. When the cavity is resonantly driven at the lower polariton frequency ωp≈ωc−g, the system exhibits a pronounced photon‐blockade signature. This effect arises from the anharmonicity of the JCs ladder, Δblk=(2−2)g (see Supporting Information S1: Section S2 for detailed procedures) [44]. In this regime, the single‐photon transition |g,0〉→|1,−〉 is resonant, whereas the two‐photon transition |1,−〉→|2,−〉 is detuned by Δblk, thereby suppressing double excitation and yielding high‐purity antibunched emission (see Figure 4a). As shown in Figure 4b, the anharmonic energy spacing forcefully suppresses the two‐photon probability, producing a deep minimum of g(2)(0)=1.4×10−3 at a drive amplitude of Ωp=0.002meV. This pronounced dip near the lower polariton detuning marks the regime of highest single‐photon purity, arising from photon blockade in the strong coupling regime. Here, the anharmonic energy ladder of the hybrid exciton–photon system prevents simultaneous occupation of multiple cavity photons, yielding distinctly sub‐Poissonian statistics. At resonance with the lower polariton, the exciton–photon coupling is maximized, enabling efficient SPE.

As demonstrated in Figure 3e, increasing the Au cavity thickness causes a decrease in the Rabi splitting [28]. This, in turn, reduces the anharmonicity of the hybrid system, resulting in larger g(2)(0) and lower purity (see Figure 4c). Figure 4c,d further illustrate the dependence of CPB on coupling strength and drive amplitude. In Figure 4c, g(2)(0) decreases systematically with increasing g, because the larger anharmonicity Δblk increasingly detunes the two‐photon transition and strengthens the blockade. At a coupling strength of g=99.2meV and under weak driving, where Ωp is much smaller than both Δblk and the dissipation rates, the system exhibits nearly ideal antibunching with g(2)(0)≈10−4, corresponding to near‐unity single‐photon purity [45]. As the drive amplitude increases (red and blue curves), power broadening partially lifts the blockade, enabling excitation of higher JCs rungs and raising g(2)(0) toward the classical limit. The complementary behavior is seen in Figure 4d, where the mean intracavity photon number 〈n〉 is plotted as a function of Ωp for various g. Stronger coupling enhances both the light–matter hybridization and the nonlinear energy shift, resulting in a higher 〈n〉 and improved antibunching at the same drive strength. The demonstrated g(2)(0) in strongly coupled Au–WSe2 is significantly lower than in previously reported weakly coupled systems of gold nanocubes on a sapphire substrate supporting monolayer WSe2 (WSe2/Au/Sapp on an Au mirror), where g(2)(0)=0.15±0.03 was obtained at low temperature [22].

As a result, the results presented above confirm that the strongly coupled Au–WSe2 platform exhibits sufficient intrinsic nonlinearity to generate quantum light through CPB at room temperature. Furthermore, the extracted purity, 1−g(2)(0), is already competitive with leading cryogenic implementations, underscoring the promise of plasmon–exciton hybrid states.

Together with the steady‐state statistics, these results highlight the advantages of the Au–WSe2 platform, which combines interference‐based UnPB with a genuine two‐level nonlinearity at room‐temperature‐compatible parameters, thereby simultaneously suppressing two‐photon and higher‐order multiphoton coincidences and providing a practical route toward integrated SPSs.

In addition to investigating the single‐photon purity through g(2)(0) in the UnPB regime, we further evaluate higher‐order correlations to assess multiphoton suppression in the two‐drive JCs system. Figure 5f–h show the higher‐order equal‐time correlation functions g(3)(0), g(4)(0), and g(5)(0) as functions of the light–matter coupling strength g and the cavity‐drive amplitude Ωp for the two‐drive JCs system. These quantities extend the standard equal‐time second‐order correlation g(2)(0) to probe multiphoton correlations beyond simple antibunching. In a true SPS, all orders of the correlation functions should satisfy g(n)(0)≪1, indicating a negligible probability of emitting n or more photons at once. The nearly uniform dark regions in Figure 5f–h confirm that, in the two‐drive configuration, multiphoton events up to fifth order are coherently suppressed across a broad parameter range. This behavior originates from destructive quantum interference between the cavity and emitter excitation pathways, thereby eliminating higher‐order photon resonances. As the drive amplitude Ωp increases, partial breakdown of this interference gradually raises g(n)(0), although the suppression remains robust for moderate Ωp and large g. Evaluating higher‐order correlations up to n=5 thus provides a stringent benchmark for verifying genuine SPE, because a low g(2)(0) alone may not rule out residual three‐ or four‐photon bursts. The consistently small values of g(3)(0), g(4)(0), and g(5)(0) shown in Figure 5f–h demonstrates that the two‐drive scheme achieves near‐ideal single‐photon purity, extending photon blockade to higher orders of the correlation hierarchy.

Antibunching in Strongly Coupled Au–MoS2 and Au‐MoSe2 System

Building on our Au–WSe2 baseline for room‐temperature SPE in the strong coupling regime shown in Figures 4, 5, we now examine Au–MoS2 and Au–MoSe2, whose tightly bound A excitons at visible energies can be spectrally matched to tunable Au plasmonic modes to form a plasmon–exciton hybrid state satisfying g≳ΓP+ΓX/4. To enhance the coupling strength and nonlinearity, the plasmon resonance was tuned by varying the design parameters shown in Figure 6a to match the excitons of MoS2 and MoSe2 monolayers. As shown in Figure 6b–c, the plasmonic EQ mode is tunable by varying the Au‐cavity thickness. This tunability is a prerequisite for achieving and quantifying strong coupling in Au–MoS2 and Au–MoSe2, and it reveals clear anticrossing with Rabi splittings that persist to room temperature (see Figure 6d–e). The extracted Rabi splitting of Au–MoS2 heterostructures is 112.2 meV (near zero detuning), and the resulting polariton hybrid states exhibit pronounced anharmonicity, supporting high‐purity and offering a route to efficient out‐coupling.

As a result, the formation of exciton–plasmon strong coupling at room temperature in the Au–MoS2 system enables pronounced photon blockade: excitation of a single polariton suppresses subsequent excitation because the polaritonic ladder is anharmonic. To further enhance antibunching, the simultaneous driving of the cavity and exciton modes was optimized (see Equation 23) to achieve an UnPB condition through destructive interference between the two excitation pathways. Under this two‐tone excitation, the system yields the extremely low g(2)(0) values observed in Figure 6f–g. The robustness of the photon blockade in this system results in an ultra‐low g(2)(0) value of 2.30×10−4 at a cavity drive of Ωp=0.5 meV and a coupling strength of 56.11 meV. Such deep antibunching indicates near‐perfect SPE, representing a roughly three‐order‐of‐magnitude improvement in multiphoton suppression over typical uncoupled MoS2 quantum emitters [46]. Additionally, embedding the monolayer in an Au metasurface yields large Rabi splittings and tight confinement, enabling blockade dominated by cavity anharmonicity rather than localized‐defect nonlinearity, thereby suppressing multiphoton probability even under modest drive.

Furthermore, under identical coherent driving conditions, the designed Au–MoSe2 hybrid structure was also investigated for SPE applications. Unlike quantum emission from monolayer MoSe2 at cryogenic temperatures [19], here, we study operation at room temperature. Tuning the Au‐cavity thickness allows spectral matching to the MoSe2 monolayer exciton. This system exhibits strong coupling at room temperature with a large Rabi splitting exceeding 135 meV (see Figure 6h). This Rabi splitting increases as the Au thickness decreases, consistent with enhanced field confinement and a smaller mode volume in thinner and narrower cavities [28]. Subsequently, as shown in Figure 6i the induced enhanced nonlinear response with moderate radiative damping in Au‐ MoSe2, making it suitable for photon blockade under the same dual‐drive configuration. This results in an ultra‐low g(2)(0) value of 1.59×10−4 at Ωp=0.5 meV and a coupling strength of 67.5 meV, with purity increasing as the drive amplitude Ωp is reduced.

As shown in Figure 6i, the g(2)(τ) reveals how the single‐photon character evolves with delay. For all drive amplitudes Ωp, the correlations exhibit a deep antibunching dip at zero delay, g(2)(0)≪1, confirming strong suppression of simultaneous two‐photon emission due to destructive interference between the cavity‐ and exciton‐driven excitation pathways. At longer timescales, g(2)(τ) rises above unity and shows a modest overshoot before relaxing to g(2)(τ)→1, reflecting re‐excitation dynamics and damped Rabi oscillations of the coupled exciton–plasmon system. Increasing the drive from Ωp=1 to 10meV gradually fills in the antibunching dip and enhances the overshoot, indicating power broadening and an increased probability of populating higher excitation manifolds. Nevertheless, the curves retain clear antibunching at τ=0, demonstrating that UnPB remains effective over a broad range of drive strengths in Au–MoSe2.

Together, Au–MoS2 and Au–MoSe2 complement Au–WSe2 by spanning different exciton energies and dephasing environments, enabling a comparative Au‐metasurface–tunable platform in which detuning, Rabi splitting, and radiative channels can be co‐optimized to realize scalable, on‐chip SP emission with engineered g(2)(0) and directional out‐coupling.

Antibunching in Weakly Coupled Au‐WSe2

Finally, to validate our model for SPS purity in the strong coupling regime, we transitioned to the weak‐coupling regime by decreasing the Q‐factor and field enhancement of the Au cavity (see Figure 7a). Here, the emitter is placed in the near field of the plasmonic structure, allowing spontaneous emission to couple into plasmonic modes. The design parameters of the Au cavity were set to (W1=96 nm, W2=90 nm, h=86.5 nm, g=86.5 nm, α=348 nm, t=80 nm, and Px=Py=420 nm) to match the exciton resonance of WSe2. The optical response of the designed metasurface is strongly dependent on the cavity gap width [28]. As shown in Figure 7b–c, the bare metasurface exhibits an electric field enhancement of E/E02=106 and a broad, low‐Q absorption spectrum. Although the field enhancement of the Au cavity is lower than that of Figure 1c (with narrower slit gaps), it still reaches E/E02=191 when hybridized with WSe2. Despite this enhancement, distinct polariton branches are not observed due to the reduced local density of optical states (LDOS) induced by the Au cavity, which prevents hybrid state formation. The coupling strength scales as g∝Q/V, where Q is the cavity quality factor and V its optical mode volume. Therefore, before simulating g(2)(0) for the WSe2 emitter coupled to the Au cavity, we first verified the coupling strength. According to Equation (15), if the calculated Rabi splitting in Au–WSe2 does not satisfy both criteria, the system lies in the weak‐coupling regime [41]. By substituting ΓP=125.9 meV from Figure 7 into Equation (15), both conditions are unmet, confirming weak coupling. As shown in Figure 7f, this regime yields a Purcell factor FP that quantifies the enhancement of the WSe2 emission rate ΓX through the plasmonic cavity, expressed as γ=1+FPΓX, where FP∝(λ/n)3(Q/V), indicating that strong field confinement enhances spontaneous emission. The calculated FP at a 10 nm emitter–cavity separation is 1310, which exceeds the 15±3 reported for WSe2 in a plasmonic slot waveguide, resulting in g(2)(0)=0.42, consistent with weaker field confinement in our metasurface [47]. Notably, FP=1310 is moderate compared to FP>105 achievable when the dipole emitter is closely aligned with the local field maximum in the WSe2 (see Supporting Information S1: Figure S4).

Effect of Dielectric Spacer on Photon Purity in TMDCs/Au Metasurface/SiO2

To further assess the experimental feasibility of the proposed device, we rigorously investigated the influence of the spacer within the numerical simulations. Because the present work is primarily based on simulations and theoretical modeling, these additional calculations were performed to bring the design closer to realistic fabrication conditions. In the proposed structure, a thin alumina (Al2O3) dielectric spacer with a refractive index of n=1.6 is introduced between the MoSe2 and WSe2 monolayers to prevent direct metal‐induced quenching (see Supporting Information S1: Figures S5 and S6 in the supplementary information). As a result, first the design parameters of the Au metasurface shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure S5a were varied to match the exciton of MoSe2. As shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure S5c, the plasmon resonance of the Au metasurface/SiO2 matches the MoSe2 resonances at a thickness of t=54 nm. Subsequently, Al2O3 varied from 2 to 3 nm in 1L‐MoSe2/Al2O3/Au metasurface/SiO2 substrate, as shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure S5d [49]. In addition, we evaluated the influence of the Al2O3 spacer on the Rabi splitting. As shown in Supporting Information S1: Figures S5e and S6b in the supplementary information, the Rabi splitting is only weakly affected by the presence of the dielectric spacer, confirming that a few‐nanometer‐thick Al2O3 layer can effectively suppress metal‐induced quenching without significantly degrading the coupling strength. Using the extracted plasmon linewidth κP, exciton linewidth ΓX, and coupling strength estimated from the Rabi splitting in Supporting Information S1: Figure S5e, the calculated g(2)(0) exhibits strong antibunching at a drive strength of 1 meV (see Supporting Information S1: Figure S5f in the supplementary information). Furthermore, to account for defect‐induced decoherence that may arise during MoSe2 monolayer transfer, we introduced a pure‐dephasing term Γϕ into Equation (24). As shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure S5f, g(2)(0) remains as low as 0.02 even in the presence of pure dephasing, corresponding to a high single‐photon purity. Additionally, these results confirm that the proposed 1L‐WSe2/spacer/Au metasurface platform shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure S6 can also retain high‐purity antibunched emission under realistic experimental conditions. Moreover, the designed Au metasurfaces, shown in Figure 7b and Supporting Information S1: Figure S5b in the supplementary information, exhibits strong field confinement and forms deterministic plasmonic hot spots across the array. When a TMDC monolayer is transferred onto these structures, strain‐localized emitter states positioned near these gaps can efficiently couple to the enhanced local optical field. As a result, this could enable cavity‐assisted deterministic SPE with enhanced both the radiative emission rate and quantum yield [22].

Furthermore, the proposed design fabrication process can be carried out using conventional electron‐beam lithography (EBL) and lift‐off techniques. First, a resist layer can be patterned on the SiO2 substrate by EBL, followed by development and surface cleaning. A thin Ti adhesion layer is then deposited by electron‐beam evaporation under high‐vacuum conditions, followed by deposition of the Au layer. After lift‐off, a thin Al2O3 dielectric spacer of 2–3 nm is deposited by atomic layer deposition (ALD). Alternatively, a thin hexagonal boron nitride (hBN) flake may be transferred as an atomically flat dielectric spacer to prevent direct contact between the TMDC monolayers and the Au metasurface. Finally, a crystalline WSe2 (including other TMDC) monolayers can be transferred onto the spacer‐coated Au metasurface to evaluate the resulting strong coupling and SPE performance.

Conclusions

Solid‐state single‐photon sources that operate at room temperature and are compatible with planar nanophotonic integration remain a key unmet need for scalable quantum photonic technologies. In this study, we theoretically demonstrate room‐temperature SPE in TMDC monolayers by strongly coupling them to an Au plasmonic metasurface. First, the Au metasurface was characterized through multipole decomposition, and strong coupling was established by fitting a coupled‐oscillator model to the polariton dispersion, which reproduces the pronounced anticrossing and yields vacuum Rabi splittings exceeding 100 meV at room temperature. This coupling rate is enabled by the metasurface's high‐Q EQ mode and deep‐subwavelength mode confinement, which enhances Q/V and thus exciton–plasmon coupling strength g. Across the three hybrids considered here, Au–WSe2, Au–MoS2, and Au–MoSe2, we obtain coupling strengths g in the tens of meV, corresponding to high cooperativities in Au–TMDC heterostructures. This quantum nanoplasmonic design, which engineers intense, localized near fields at exciton resonances, enables robust strong coupling and single‐photon operation at room temperature despite substantial dephasing in TMDC monolayers. Building on this strong‐coupling platform, we simulated deterministic single‐photon generation using both CPB (single‐drive) and UnPB (interference‐assisted, two‐drive) within a JCs master‐equation framework. For Au–WSe2 in the CPB regime, resonant driving at the lower polariton yields deep antibunching with g(2)(0)≈1.4×10−3 at Ωp=0.002 meV, and even lower values approaching 10−4 under optimal weak‐drive conditions. In the UnPB configuration, the two‐drive scheme further suppresses multiphoton events, as confirmed by higher‐order correlations g(n)(0) with n=3–5, which remain ≪1 over a broad range of g,Ωp. Extending this strategy to Au–MoS2 and Au–MoSe2 under the same dual‐drive protocol, we obtain ultra‐low of g(2)(0)=2.30×10−4 at Ωp=0.5 meV and g=56.11 meV for Au–MoS2, and g(2)(0)=1.59×10−4 at Ωp=0.5 meV and g=67.5 meV for Au–MoSe2. These values correspond to single‐photon purities 1−g(2)(0) exceeding 99.98% at room temperature. Thus, the strongly coupled Au–TMDC heterostructures yield a two‐to three‐orders of magnitude reduction in g(2)(0) at room temperature compared with weak coupling operation, indicating substantial suppression of multiphoton contributions. Importantly, the high purity is maintained at non‐negligible intracavity populations: for comparable 〈n〉, the antibunching dip in g(2)(0) remains pronounced. Taken together, Au–WSe2, Au–MoS2, and Au–MoSe2 form a tunable quantum nanophotonic platform in which exciton energy, coupling strength, dephasing environment, and drive configuration can be co‐engineered to realize scalable on‐chip SP emitters with scalable g(2)(0) and directional out‐coupling. In this way, our room‐temperature Au metasurface–TMDC platform could overcome the traditional reliance on cryogenic TMDC monolayer SPSs and establish a viable route toward integrated quantum light sources for quantum key distribution and low‐power photonic quantum interconnects.
