Local Recurrent Melanoma Treated with BRAF and MEK Inhibitors Despite Adjuvant Anti-Programmed Cell Death Protein 1 Antibody Therapy
Authors: Hye Won Hwang, Seon Bok Lee, Hyun-Tae Shin, Jeonghyun Shin, Gwang Seong Choi, Ji Won Byun
Published: 2023

Dear Editor:

The current standard therapies for patients with advanced melanoma include immunotherapy with an anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (anti-PD-1) antibody1. Anti-PD-1 antibodies prolong recurrence-free survival in high-risk melanoma patients, but some patients experience recurrence within 1 year. One study reported that patients who experienced disease recurrence during adjuvant anti-PD-1 antibody therapy had a minimal response to anti-PD-1 monotherapy but BRAF and MEK (BRAF/MEK) inhibitors showed clinical utility. However, these patients were only followed up for a few months2.

A 73-year-old female was diagnosed with nodular melanoma on her calf. The lesion was surgically removed, which led to a diagnosis of stage IIB melanoma without distant metastasis (Fig. 1). The first regional in-transit metastases were noticed 18 months after surgical removal along with the elevation of serum lactate dehydrogenase, so the patient was treated with an anti-PD-1 antibody along with resection of all recurrent tumors (Fig. 2A). There was still no evidence of distant metastasis. Another in-transit lesion was detected 2 years after the first in-transit metastasis, so real-time PCR examination for BRAF mutation in the originally resected tumor was performed (Fig. 2B). The tumor indeed had a BRAFV600E mutation, so combination therapy with BRAF/MEK inhibitors was initiated. The lesion had shrunk significantly after one month (Fig. 2C). The patient has continued taking BRAF/MEK inhibitors for 20 months and has shown no evidence of disease progression thus far. We received the patient’s consent form about publishing all photographic materials.

(A) Primary nodular melanoma developed on the medial side of the left calf of a 73-year-old female. She had a medical history of stomach cancer 8 years prior. (B) The primary lesion was resected with a 2-cm safety margin, and her sentinel lymph node biopsy findings were negative.

(A) Several in-transit metastases (white arrows) were detected 18 months after the surgical resection of the primary tumor. The recurrent lesion was confirmed by biopsy, and all observed lesions were excised. There was still no evidence of distant metastasis on fluorodeoxyglucose-positron emission tomography or computed tomography. (B) Another in-transit lesion was noted during anti-PD-1 antibody (pembrolizumab) therapy at 2 years after the first in-transit metastases detectection, and adjuvant radiotherapy was administered but was ineffective. (C) The lesion showed significant shrinkage at one month after treatment with dabrafenib (150 mg twice daily) combined with trametinib (2 mg once daily).

PD-1 blockade prolongs the overall survival of melanoma patients, and those with negative programmed cell death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression still respond to this treatment, so testing for PD-L1 expression is not required1,3. However, 20% to 30% of patients experience recurrence. One study reported that BRAF/MEK inhibitors have clinical utility for these patients2. Our patient was administered anti-PD-1 antibody therapy for 2 years without testing for PD-L1 expression, but secondary metastases developed. Therefore, we concluded that anti-PD-1 antibodies had no effect and searched for another treatment approach by testing for BRAF mutation, which showed a BRAFV600E mutation.

BRAF is a serine/threonine protein kinase that activates the MAPK signaling pathway. This signaling pathway drives the growth of melanoma cells through the upregulation of cyclin D1 expression and the downregulation of cell cycle inhibitors. The most frequent BRAF mutation in melanoma is substitution at the second position of codon 600, which results in an amino acid change from valine to glutamic acid (p.V600E), creating an active protein that binds MEK4. BRAF/MEK inhibitors, which block the MAPK pathway at two nodes rather than one, decrease the risk of progression without an increase in overall toxicity5. The adverse events of the regimen are cutaneous and gastrointestinal events.

In one study of four patients who experienced recurrence after surgical resection and treatment with BRAF/MEK inhibitors, all 4 patients showed no signs of recurrence after a median follow-up of 6.5 months2. Our patient had no recurrence for 20 months, which is the longest follow-up period among the reported cases. Our findings reveal that genetic studies should be considered when treating patients with recurrent melanoma who are refractory to adjuvant anti-PD-1 antibody therapy.
