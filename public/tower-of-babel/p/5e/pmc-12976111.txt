Integrative spatial profiling pipeline for determining TME architectures in archival clinical specimens using CmTSA superplex technology
Authors: Chaoxin Xiao, Ruihan Zhou, Qin Chen, Wanting Hou, Yulin Wang, Lu Liu, Huanhuan Wang, Xiaohong Yao, Rui Zhu, Zirui Wang, Leyi Yao, Ouying Yan, Xiaoying Li, Tongtong Xu, Fujun Cao, Banglei Yin, Na Xiao, Lili Jiang, Wei Wang, Dan Cao, Chengjian Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe tumor microenvironment (TME) comprises diverse cellular components that spatially interact to form distinct functional niches (FNs). Profiling these TME spatial features has proven to be a critical approach for correlating tumor progression and therapeutic response. However, RNA stability limitations constrain the broad clinical implementation of spatial transcriptomics, while high background noise and low signal resolution compromise the accuracy of direct labeling-based spatial proteomic approaches in clinical specimens. To overcome these constraints in archival samples, we developed hybrid optochemical fluorescence depletion (HOC-FD) technology that integrates autofluorescence quenching with cyclic multiplex tyramide signal amplification (CmTSA) for formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues. This unified platform enables the concurrent labeling of 30–60 biomarkers with ultrahigh signal-to-noise ratios while maintaining cost efficiency and compatibility with high-throughput processing of archival FFPE specimens. While superplex imaging captures multidimensional TME data, extracting spatial features from raw pixel-level outputs remains technically challenging. To resolve this problem, we implemented a computer vision pipeline beginning with deep learning-based cellular segmentation and phenotype classification under predefined biomarker annotation rules. Using single-cell spatial mapping of human colon and cervical cancer specimens, we systematically evaluated and selected radius-constrained neighborhood network (RNN) analysis to define functional niches, validating their accuracy and reliability in generating spatially coherent FNs with biological and prognostic relevance. In summary, the CmTSA platform combined with RNN-based spatial profiling provides an integrated framework for visualizing and quantifying multicellular functional states within architectures of the TME, potentially enhancing tumor immunology investigations and precision immunotherapies.Subject terms: Cancer microenvironment, Cancer immunotherapy

Introduction

Tumor persistence, dissemination, and therapeutic response are influenced not only by the intrinsic properties of malignant cells but also by their interactions with diverse nonmalignant components within the microenvironment, including immune, fibroblast, endothelial, and other stromal cells1. A comprehensive understanding of cancer and effective treatment development requires an investigation of the complex, multidirectional interactions and signaling networks between tumor cells and their surrounding microenvironment. In the past decade, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has been extensively used to map the gene expression profiles of tumor, immune, and stromal cells across various cancers2. However, these techniques lack critical spatial information. Tumors function as ecosystems of heterogeneous cell types that dynamically interact, exchange information, and influence disease progression and treatment outcomes3–5. Traditional tissue architecture analysis using Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining, while crucial for identifying malignancy, often overlooks spatial interactions between tumor cells and other cell types. Therefore, molecular studies that preserve and reveal the spatial features of tumors at the single-cell level are essential for understanding how intercellular communication affects tumor growth and therapeutic response.

Profiling the spatial features of patients’ tumor ecosystems begins with identifying cell phenotypes in situ. There are two mainstream technologies that can be used for this purpose: spatial transcriptomics and spatial proteomics6,7. Although spatial transcriptomics may offer more intracellular molecular information, its limited throughput makes assessing and comparing spatial interactions among various cell types in the TME at the clinical cohort level challenging8,9. Additionally, mRNA-based detection methods face practical challenges when applied to routine clinical samples because of the unstable nature of mRNA. Therefore, proteomics based on single-cell-resolved superplex protein imaging is more suited for this purpose. It is compatible with routine clinical samples from large cohorts and can distinguish dozens of markers ( > 30 biomarkers), adequately characterizing cellular phenotypes and statuses within a tumor ecosystem. Spatial proteomic technologies can be grouped into two classes on the basis of readout signaling: mass spectrometry (IMC and MIBI–TOF) and fluorescence or chromogenic imaging (CODEX, CycIF, and multiplex IHC)3,10–13. Each method offers unique benefits and drawbacks in terms of resolution, multiplexing capacity, efficiency, and signal amplification. However, when applied to clinical samples, current technologies face significant challenges, including high background noise, low throughput, low signal intensity, high cost, and incompatibility with conventional primary antibodies. These issues limit their effectiveness and accessibility in clinical settings and hinder their application in research involving large cohorts14. There is an urgent need to develop new methods to overcome these challenges, enhancing the reliability, efficiency, and affordability of TME spatial profiling processes.

Spatial proteomic technologies enable the visualization of dozens to hundreds of marker proteins across up to millions of cells within a tissue slide. This vast pixel dataset presents multidimensional information that characterizes the patient’s TME15. This dataset includes multiple layers of information, such as single-cell phenotypes, single-cell morphology, the composition of cells in the tissue, cell‒cell interactions, and their arrangements into multicellular niches or larger anatomical/functional structures16. However, extracting these features from the raw pixel data is not straightforward and requires dedicated analysis methods, bridging the worlds of microscopy, pathology and computer science. Machine learning models (such as Cellpose and StarDist) enable accurate cell segmentation and classification to translate pixel-based high-plex imaging data into digitized, matrix-based spatial data17–19. These computer-vision based methods can perform as accurately as humans when compared with the manual segmentation of ground truth data. However, cells within the TME often contain densely packed cells, exhibit irregular shapes and express multiple cell markers. The segmentation and cellular phenotyping workflow therefore requires meticulous implementation of standardized threshold-driven gating processes integrated with rigorous annotation rules.

In addition, deriving biological and clinical insights from this novel data type remains an ongoing research challenge. To characterize the spatial interactions between cell types within tumors, many computational platforms have been introduced3,8,20,21. Some cellular spatial analysis platforms focus on pairwise associations between cell types on the basis of the frequency of spatial co-occurrence, spatial correlation, or nearest distances between individual cells. Other platforms use machine learning (e.g., K-means) or topic modeling to define tissue cellular niches with consistent cell type compositions3,16. These platforms focus mainly on localized regions (small regions from tissue microarrays (TMAs) or specific regions of interest (ROIs)) rather than whole-mount pathological slides and lack strategies to comprehensively understand the spatial characteristics of the patient’s immune microenvironment. Additionally, the behavior and spatial interactions of immune cells within the tumor immune microenvironment are complex but have inherent patterns. Subtypes, abundance, and spatial interactions with other immune cells within the microenvironment are meaningful features that collectively define the immune function of the individual microenvironment16. However, the commonly used spatial analyses are rather fragmented and lack logically interconnected systematic approaches, making it difficult to form an effective chain to dissect the spatial characteristics of the immune microenvironment at the whole-slide level22. This limitation hinders the comprehensive and systematic evaluation of the immune status of individuals.

We developed cyclic multiplex tyramide signal amplification (CmTSA), a high-throughput superplex staining platform optimized for clinical FFPE specimens that enables the simultaneous detection of 30–60 biomarkers across multislide cohorts. This methodology specifically addresses critical limitations in current superplex techniques for archival samples: (1) hybrid opto-chemical fluorescence depletion (HOC-FD) enables complete elimination of autofluorescence in human FFPE sections; (2) enzymatic signal amplification achieves a 5–100-fold increase in sensitivity for low-abundance proteins; and (3) it is compatible with conventional IHC-grade antibodies and IHC workflows, thereby reducing the cost of reagents and equipment. Additionally, we introduce a computer vision AI-assisted cell segmentation model and a cell type annotation rule. These tools efficiently and accurately translate pixel-based superplex images into spatial matrix data, containing cell types and spatial positions of all segmented cells, and can handle millions of cells. On the basis of these spatial matrix data, we provide a comprehensive RNN workflow for dissection of the spatial organization and cell‒cell interactions within patients’ TMEs at the whole-mount slide level.

In summary, we developed (1) a high-quality, low-cost superplex staining platform (CmTSA); (2) a computer vision-assisted superplex imaging digitization method; and (3) a streamlined analytical pipeline for spatial feature profiling of the TME at the whole-FFPE slide scale. This integrated framework demonstrates proof-of-concept utility in deriving actionable insights for prognostic stratification and personalized immunotherapy strategies (Fig. 1a).

Results

HOC-FD integrates autofluorescence quenching and CmTSA staining for archival FFPE sections

Conventional FFPE sections exhibit substantial autofluorescence across broad emission spectra (400–600 nm), originating from collagen fibers, elastin, erythrocytes, and aldehyde fixatives (formaldehyde/formalin)23. The emission spectra of these autofluorescent molecules overlap with those of commonly used fluorophores (e.g., AF488 and AF568), severely compromising detection specificity (Supplementary Fig. S1a). To address this, we developed HOC-FD — a method combining specific photobleaching (using a spectrum adjustable high-intensity LED, 400–600 nm) and chemical quenching. With background fluorescence elimination, deparaffinized FFPE sections are uniformly irradiated with a high-intensity refrigerated LED light source (with a continuous emitting spectrum, 400–600 nm, covering most autofluorescence excitation ranges) while submerged in a solution containing 4% hydrogen peroxide. The LED light excites background fluorescent molecules, and the hydrogen peroxide undergoes photolysis to generate hydroxyl radicals (·OH)24. These radicals engage in redox reactions with excited fluorophores, altering their molecular structures and quenching fluorescence via disrupted energy transitions (Supplementary Fig. S1b)25. An 8-min HOC-FD treatment eliminated >90% of the visible-range background fluorescence in FFPE sections without impairing antigen detection by immunofluorescence (IF) (Fig. 1b, c). Further evaluations confirmed the compatibility with subsequent IF and covalent TSA staining (Fig. 1d), achieving a 50%–80% increase in the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) for FFPE-based fluorescence workflows (Fig. 1e; Supplementary Fig. S2).

Spectral crowding remains a critical bottleneck in superplex fluorescent labeling ( >30 markers). Current methodologies predominantly employ cyclic direct labeling approaches using preconjugated primary antibodies, such as DNA-barcoded antibodies in CODEX (PhenoCycler), metal-tagged antibodies in imaging mass cytometry (IMC), and fluorophore-conjugated antibodies in cyclic immunofluorescence (CycIF). While achieving superplexing ( > 20–40 markers), these techniques necessitate elevated primary antibody concentrations because secondary amplification cascades are absent. This inherent limitation exacerbates background fluorescence and reduces specificity thresholds, particularly when low-abundance intracellular antigens are detected or when human FFPE archival samples are analyzed. In contrast, tyramide signal amplification (TSA) — a catalytic labeling method leveraging the tyramide–HRP reaction — enables highly specific fluorophore deposition with minimal antibody binding while amplifying signals 5–100× over conventional immunofluorescence under identical conditions (Fig. 3b)26. TSA effectively circumvents weak-labeling artifacts through enzymatic signal enhancement; however, its multiplexing potential remains constrained by conventional spectral demixing algorithms’ limited discriminative bandwidths ( < 9 channels). To overcome these technical constraints while preserving the supermultiplex capability for archival FFPE tissue phenotyping, we developed an integrated framework that synergizes HOC-FD-mediated iterative spectral imaging with multiplex TSA codetection. This approach enables theoretically unlimited multiplexing through three cyclically applied steps: First, spectral optimization of a refrigerated LED light source (matched to TSA fluorophores: cyclic-480, cyclic-550, and cyclic-630) achieved 99% fluorescence quenching within 10 min while preserving DAPI nuclear signals (Fig. 1f, g; Supplementary Fig. S1c). Next, each staining cycle generated triplex fluorescence images with DAPI as an invariant fiducial marker for registration (Supplementary Fig. S5a). Finally, the serial images were computationally fused using scale-invariant feature transform (SIFT) keys27,28 to achieve pixel-level alignment precision (Supplementary Fig. S5b). Validation across 20 quenching cycles confirmed that no significant antigenicity loss occurred (Supplementary Fig. S1d).

Generation of single-cell spatial maps from high-quality superplex images of FFPE sections

In CmTSA workflows, fluorophores are covalently anchored to antigens via tyramide deposition, while antibodies bind cooperatively through noncovalent forces such as hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, and van der Waals forces29. This covalent vs noncovalent binding dichotomy enables selective antibody stripping while retaining fluorophores — a critical advantage for iterative staining in TSA. However, conventional stripping methods (e.g., microwave heating in EDTA buffer at > 95 °C) pose risks of antigen epitope denaturation and tissue morphology disruption, thereby limiting iterative staining to fewer than 10 markers (Supplementary Fig. S3b). To minimize damage while enhancing colabeling throughput, we developed a standardized screening workflow (Supplementary Fig. S3a) to screen antibodies with high specificity and low affinity. These screening antibodies permit precise TSA labeling and gentle removal using ionic buffers (Supplementary Figs. S3c, S4). Validated through epitope stability, cellular morphology, and tissue integrity assessments, this approach supports the colabeling of at least 40 biomarkers on a single FFPE section, e.g., colon tissue (Fig. 2a). Theoretically, the CmTSA platform enables ≥ 20 iterative staining cycles (triple-plex/DAPI per cycle), yielding 60-channel superplex images at single-cell resolution across whole-FFPE slides. However, manual analysis of such datasets is operationally infeasible, necessitating the implementation of an AI-driven computational pipeline. The first essential task is accurate cell segmentation. We tested and applied multiple deep learning models, such as StarDist and CellPose, for this purpose (Supplementary Fig. S7)30. StarDist represents cells using star-convex polygons, approximating each cell’s shape with multiple rays extending from a central point to delineate the cell’s boundary27. This model is particularly suitable for segmenting morphologically diverse cells in complex TMEs, where cells are densely arranged and frequently overlap. We implemented a StarDist pretrained model for nuclear segmentation of stained human FFPE tissue section images, achieving satisfactory segmentation accuracy within the QuPath analytical platform.

Following cell segmentation, cell type annotation involves two major steps, which we refer to as the cell gating strategy: first, the expression levels of cellular markers in individual cells are determined, and single positively marked cells are obtained. The expression patterns of cell markers, as revealed through fluorescent signals, vary. Several key issues must be carefully considered when this methodology is applied. Some markers are expressed on the cell membrane (e.g., CD8 and CD4), some are expressed in the cytoplasm (e.g., panCK and CD68), and others are expressed in the nucleus (e.g., FOXP3 and Ki67). Different signal extraction methods must be used carefully on the basis of the specific expression patterns of individual markers (Supplementary Fig. S6). Second, an annotation rule is designed to gate cellular types or subtypes. Since certain antigen markers are shared by multiple cell types and some cell types require multiple markers to define, an annotation rule is essential for ensuring consistent, accurate, and automated classification of cellular subtypes. For instance, regulatory T cells (Tregs) were operationally defined as those in which FOXP3⁺ nuclei colocalized with CD4⁺ membrane signals (Supplementary Fig. S6).

To benchmark how the cell gating strategy performs, we annotated a superplex image of a human colon section that was stained by CmTSA (Fig. 2b). A panel of lineage-defining markers and cell type annotation rules (based on typical lineage markers, expected population abundance, and staining quality supervision) was used to define the cell types within the superplex image (Supplementary Fig. S8). We observed strong agreement between the ground truth CmTSA superplex image and the resulted single-cell spatial proteomic map (Fig. 2c), demonstrating that an appropriate cell gating strategy enables accurate classification and mapping of the diverse cells present in the whole-FFPE slide.

Comparison and integration of CmTSA with other highplex immunostaining methods

In recent years, single-cell spatial proteomic technologies, such as CODEX, MICS, and IMC, have been widely applied in cancer and biological research. However, these technologies encounter notable challenges when applied to analyze clinical FFPE samples, particularly those with high background noise and low signal intensity. CmTSA implements significant optimizations specifically to address these limitations and improve the technology’s applicability in clinical settings.

Conventional multiplex staining technologies, such as the use of metal-labeled antibodies (IMC), oligonucleotide-tagged antibodies (CODEX), and fluorophore-conjugated antibodies (MICS), utilize directly labeled primary antibodies to achieve high efficiency and throughput. However, the signal intensity in direct detection methods relies entirely on antigen expression levels, which can vary hundreds of times across and within samples. For low-abundance antigens, such as immune checkpoints and transcription factors, this approach may result in false-negatives or inefficient detection because of the lack of signal amplification (Fig. 3a). To address this limitation, CmTSA employs TSA, offering significant advantages. TSA ensures accurate antigen detection at the single-cell level while enhancing the signal intensity (5–100×) (Fig. 3b). This approach not only improves the detection of low-abundance antigens but also enhances imaging quality (Fig. 3c; Supplementary Fig. S9).

Second, multiplex fluorescence-based imaging technologies (e.g., CODEX/MICS), lacking signal amplification, rely on increased exposure time and detector sensitivity to detect directly labeled fluorescent signals. However, this approach simultaneously amplifies background signals, reducing the overall signal-to-noise ratio and image quality. This issue is particularly pronounced in clinical FFPE sections because of the presence of abundant autofluorescent molecules, such as fibrotic collagen, blood cells, pigments, and other unknown autofluorescent compounds (Fig. 3d). To address this challenge, CmTSA incorporates HOC-FD background removal pretreatment, which effectively eliminates prominent visible-range background fluorescence in FFPE sections (Fig. 1d), significantly enhancing the specificity and imaging quality of fluorescent labels, including directly labeled signals (Fig. 3e).

Third, multiplex antigen-based staining serves as the foundation for single-cell spatial proteomics, aiming to capture the spatial features of tissue microenvironments. Current research shows that these spatial characteristics (e.g., in TMEs) exist at multiple scales — from microscopic cell–cell interactions to macroscopic functional niches involving heterogeneous cell populations. Whole-slide analysis is crucial for accurately resolving microenvironmental heterogeneity in clinical samples. However, mass spectrometry-based multiplex techniques such as IMC face inherent speed limitations. Their reliance on laser ablation (1 μm spot size) makes whole-slide analysis impractical. In contrast, CmTSA employs widefield fluorescence imaging similar to other fluorescence-based platforms, enabling centimeter-scale detection that readily accommodates entire clinical pathology sections while maintaining single-cell resolution (Fig. 3f, g).

Finally, the amplified fluorescent multiplex technology of CmTSA enables sensitive low-abundance antigen detection with enhanced imaging quality, whereas directly labeled fluorescent multiplex staining offers operational simplicity and high multiplexing capacity ( > 100 antigens), making it ideal for high-abundance targets. Both approaches utilize iterative fluorescent labeling and are fundamentally compatible. Their combined use facilitates rapid multiplex staining (50–100 markers) while ensuring reliable detection of both high- and low-abundance antigens (Fig. 3h, i).

RNN defines multicellular functional niches and reveals biologically coherent tissue architectures in whole-slide spatial omics

Following whole-slide cellular segmentation and annotation, each section yields a spatially resolved atlas of 105–106 cells exhibiting heterogeneous density distributions across the 2D histoarchitecture. The conserved spatial organization of immune, stromal, and parenchymal cells into discrete structural–functional niches with defined cellular compositions reflects fundamental microenvironmental programming. The compositional diversity and spatial architecture of multicellular niches collectively orchestrate tissue-specific physiological homeostasis and immunological responses. Previous studies have proposed terms such as “neighborhood,” “community,” and “milieu” to describe these niches31–33, with core mechanisms relying on spatially regulated intercellular communication mediated by ligand–receptor interactions (e.g., MHC-II/MHC-I–TCR, CD80/CD86–CD28/CTLA4, and PD-L1–PD-1)34–37 and cytokine signaling (e.g., IL-12, IFN-γ, IL-10, and TGF-β)38,39. While ligand–receptor binding predominantly occurs between directly adjacent cells, paracrine factors (e.g., IL-2) can propagate signals across multiple cell layers. However, their effective range is dynamically shaped by microenvironmental constraints, such as IL-2 depletion via high-affinity uptake by Tregs2,40.

In this study, by leveraging single-cell spatial proteomic mapping of human colon tissue, we systematically compared two spatial clustering strategies for delineating multicellular functional niches within whole-slide tissue microenvironments: k-nearest neighbors (KNN) and RNN (Supplementary Figs. S10, S11). Our analysis revealed that both methods achieved comparable spatial clustering outcomes when biologically calibrated parameters (radius r = 100 μm for RNN; cell number k = 283 for KNN) were used, as determined by evaluating cellular community patterns (cell type composition and proportional distributions) (Fig. 4a–d). Both algorithms successfully identified key histological structures, including mucosal tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs), the mucosal epithelium, lamina propria, muscularis mucosae, submucosa, muscular arteries, inflamed veins, and muscularis propria. However, a detailed evaluation revealed that KNN introduced spurious clustering artifacts in heterogeneous regions. Specifically, it erroneously incorporated distant cells in low-density zones (e.g., submucosa) while omitting critical multicellular niches in high-density areas (e.g., mucosal layers) (Fig. 4e). This observation aligns with biological principles: since functional niche formation relies on distance-regulated physicochemical processes, compared with KNN, RNN demonstrates greater biological relevance.

At the algorithmic level, both methods detect functional niches by statistically differentiating cellular compositions, but their foundational definitions of spatial neighborhoods fundamentally diverge. KNN selects k nearest cells on the basis of Euclidean proximity, generating density-adaptive neighborhoods that dynamically expand or contract with local cell density. In contrast, RNN enforces fixed-radius boundaries (e.g., 100 μm), creating spatially uniform neighborhoods aligned with biologically meaningful interaction ranges. Consequently, the adaptive sizing of KNN risks the merging of functionally distinct compartments in dense regions and the assimilation of extraneous cells in sparse zones. Conversely, the absolute spatial constraints of RNN preserve structural continuity by restricting neighborhoods to empirically validated interaction distances.

Dissection of the spatial architecture of the TME by CmTSA and RNN in cervical cancer patients with different prognoses

The TME is a complex ecosystem comprising INs, mesenchymal stromal compartments, and malignant cell populations. Whole-slide spatial mapping of INs and other functional niches enables the systematic dissection of immune activity dynamics and functional states within patient-specific TMEs. Such spatial profiling provides a robust framework for quantifying interpatient functional heterogeneity and its clinical relevance to prognosis and therapeutic response16,41–43. However, correlating long-term clinical outcomes with spatial omic signatures remains a persistent technical challenge. Archival FFPE sections — particularly years-old human tumor samples — exhibit pronounced autofluorescence artifacts and epitope degradation because of prolonged fixation/storage conditions. To test the reliability of the CmTSA platform in this context, we implemented CmTSA staining coupled with RNN-based spatial analysis on several FFPE sections from International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage-matched cervical cancer patients exhibiting divergent prognostic outcomes (Fig. 5a; Supplementary Table S2).

First, through analysis of previously published scRNA datasets (accession number HRA004971) encompassing 20 clinically stage-matched cervical cancer samples, we identified the major cellular components of the TME, including diverse immune subsets, stromal populations, and malignant epithelial cells (Fig. 5b; Supplementary Fig. S12). We subsequently developed a 20-marker CmTSA staining panel to spatially resolve these scRNA-derived cellular identities across archival FFPE sections (Fig. 5c, d). Staining validation demonstrated that despite prolonged archival storage (3–5 years), inherent tissue autofluorescence did not compromise CmTSA-based protein marker specificity, confirming the robustness of the technique for application to FFPE specimens after long-term preservation (Fig. 5e). Following cell type annotation across patient samples and RNN-based spatial clustering analysis of TMEs from four representative cases, 14 discrete INs characterized by unique cellular compositions were identified (Fig. 6a, b; Supplementary Fig. S13). These INs can be broadly categorized into four types: the immune-active cluster, mixed tumor-immune cluster, fibroblast-enriched cluster and tumor-dominant cluster (Fig. 6b).

Deconvolution of the cell‒cell interaction network within prognosis-stratified functional niches of cervical cancer TMEs

The spatiotemporal heterogeneity of FN organization may serve as a surrogate for interpatient variability in immune–tumor crosstalk, potentially stratifying distinct clinical trajectories, including progression propensity and therapeutic response. To evaluate this hypothesis, we performed a systematic quantification of FN compositional proportions across all four patient cohorts. Prognostically stratified analysis revealed distinct functional niche enrichment patterns: specific functional niches were preferentially associated with either adverse (progression prone) or favorable clinical outcomes. For instance, the immune-active cluster was enriched in patients with a good prognosis (P1 and P2), whereas FN1 — which is primarily composed of fibrotic and tumor-suppressive components — was significantly overrepresented in patients with a poor prognosis (P3 and P4) (Fig. 7a, b). RNN-based spatial clustering delineated prognostically distinct FNs (located at the tumor–stromal interface) between cervical cancer patients, revealing substantial spatial heterogeneity in stromal–immune niche organization. At the microarchitectural level, the tumor–stromal interface exhibited concentric stratification into spatially coherent functional zones — either continuous or segmented in distribution (tumor core (Cluster2 and Cluster4) with FN-7/11/1 annotation) — composed of heterogeneous immunostromal assemblies, including Th/B_cells/Treg-enriched lymphoid aggregates, cytotoxic CD8⁺ T lymphocyte clusters, and cancer-associated fibroblasts (Fig. 7c).

This spatial architecture holds prognostic potential. Lymphocyte-enriched niches (FN-7 and FN-11) were predominantly localized at the tumor–stromal interface in patients with a good prognosis (P1 and P2). Further analysis of cellular abundance and spatial intercellular interactions revealed that FN7 was dominated by Th cells, with coexisting fibroblasts, B cells, and Tregs, indicating prominent Th–Treg and Th–B cell interactions (Fig. 7d, e). In contrast, the FN11 niches demonstrated complex coordination among CD8 T cells, Th cells, and B cells through intensive pairwise spatial crosstalk, indicating robust adaptive immune activity (Fig. 7f, g).

Conversely, patients with a poor prognosis (P3 and P4) exhibited continuous stromal barrier formation (FN1) encapsulating tumor masses, characterized by fibroblast-dominated architectures intermixed with malignant cells and sparse Th/CD8_T infiltrates (Fig. 7h, i). Fibroblasts spatially segregated Th–CD8_T clusters from tumor cells through dual interactions: forming cohesive networks with malignant cells while physically insulating cytotoxic lymphocytes. This spatial configuration suggests that fibroblast-mediated immunosuppression is a mechanism underlying treatment resistance, indicating the need for combination therapies targeting both PD-1/PD-L1 and stromal components.

Our panoramic spatial niche profiling framework (CmTSA + RNN-based spatial FNs and interactions) enables the mechanistic stratification of spatially compartmentalized immune effector states across the TME landscape, providing actionable insights for prognostic prediction and personalized immunotherapy. However, clinical translation requires rigorous validation in large cohorts.

Discussion

In recent years, it has become increasingly appreciated that the features of individual TMEs are closely associated with tumor progression and response to treatment44. The TME mainly regarded to comprise different cell types, their spatial distribution, cell‒cell interactions and spatially structured arrangements (cellular niches or neighborhoods)45,46. To capture the complexity of the TME, a suite of spatial profiling technologies have been developed, which can be roughly divided into two categories: (1) spatial transcriptomics (10× Visium and Slide-seq) and 2) spatial proteomics (CODEX, MICS, MIBI–TOF and IMC)3,10,13,47,48. The application of these technologies has helped elucidate the structure and function of the TME, but several challenges need to be overcome to unlock their full potential.

One of the greatest challenges associated with current spatial profiling technologies is the limited throughput for parallel sample testing14. Cancer-related studies require the evaluation or comparison of cohort samples (generally containing several to dozens or even hundreds of samples in each cohort) to overcome intertumor and intratumor heterogeneity and obtain meaningful results49. This is true for studies using animal models and is dramatically exacerbated in studies with patient samples, where increased biological and treatment heterogeneity is compounded. The number of samples that can be uploaded during each experiment using current commercially available technologies is generally limited to just a few, typically 1–2 tissue slides per test50. Here, we developed the CmTSA platform, which is compatible with a high-throughput automated workflow. First, antibodies and fluorophores are applied to samples using standard automated IHC equipment. Next, the stained FFPE sections, up to dozens at a time, undergo imaging scanning using a standard fluorescence scanner. After fluorophore quenching and antibody wash-off, the samples are subjected to an additional round of TSA labeling. Finally, serial images from iterative staining are registered into a cohesive dataset for analysis. This CmTSA workflow simplifies the experiment, increases the batch size, and enhances the consistency and processing speed, resulting in reliable and high-throughput outcomes for superplex immunostaining for FFPE cohorts.

As previously mentioned, the complexity of the TME arises from the diversity of cell types, their spatial distribution, cell‒cell interactions, and their structured spatial arrangements1,45,51. Identifying cell identity and states within the TME using phenotype markers and coexpressed functional antigens is a fundamental step in spatial profiling analysis based on superplex immunostaining. However, most microscopy-based superplex spatial proteomic methods ( ≥ 30 biomarkers) primarily use prelabeled primary antibodies with fluorophores or DNA oligos52. The lack of a signal amplification step can result in dim fluorescence signals for cell markers, especially those with low expression levels, such as PD-1, CTLA-4, TIM-3, and FOXP3. TSA can amplify the signal of primary antibodies by catalyzing the deposition of tyramide-bound labels (such as fluorophores or enzymes) at the antigen site, which is particularly useful for detecting low-abundance targets12. Here, we provide the HOC-FD quenching system, capable of completely erasing background autofluorescence and TSA fluorescence signals within minutes. Thus, unlike other microscopy-based spatial proteomic platforms, CmTSA represents a superplex iterative staining method that is particularly suitable for detecting low-abundance antigens and rare cell types within human FFPE samples.

Another major limitation of superplex immunostaining is its high cost. This expense stems primarily from the instruments and reagents involved. To achieve full automation and lower technical barriers, manufacturers have integrated immunostaining, fluorophore signal recycling, and imaging scanning into a single machine. However, this integrated design strategy not only increases equipment costs but also reduces testing throughput because of hardware limitations10,12,53. In contrast, the use of CmTSA is compatible with both automated and manual workflows and involves separating the superplex staining process into distinct steps: TSA staining, scanning, fluorophore quenching, and registration. Each step can be executed either manually or via specific automated instruments, most of which are commonly available in pathology labs. This significantly reduces the cost of instruments. Moreover, because the CmTSA workflow is based on the TSA staining method, it is compatible with conventional IHC antibodies (i.e., passes the wash-off test).

Most critically, the CmTSA platform achieves superplex colabeling (30–60 biomarkers) with exceptional signal-to-noise ratios, enabling the simultaneous detection of 30+ cell types within clinical archival whole-FFPE slides. This vast pixel dataset presents multidimensional information that characterizes the patient’s TME. It includes multiple layers of information, such as cell phenotypes, cell morphology, the composition of cell types within the tissue, cell‒cell interactions, and their arrangements into multicellular niches or larger anatomical/functional structures49. In this work, we presented a solution in the form of an analysis pipeline to elucidate the spatial information embedded in the raw pixel data. This pipeline is logical and straightforward, starting with deep learning-based computer vision algorithms to segment individual cells from a large pixel-based image. Next, a rational cell type annotation rule is used to classify cells into different types. On the basis of the spatial matrix, cell‒cell interactions and multicellular functional units can be easily dissected and evaluated. This can significantly aid in basic research related to tumor immunology and in the analysis of immune microenvironment status for drug development and precision medicine.

While comprehensive spatial profiling of the TME is valuable and informative, it is essential to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the formation of spatial interactions and organizational patterns, as well as to elucidate their functions and clinical implications. To address these questions, it is necessary to integrate spatial profiling technologies with multiomic data, carefully annotated patient cohorts, and additional well-controlled clinical data. This integrated approach provides deeper insights into the TME and its role in disease progression and treatment outcomes.

While automated classification frameworks such as MAPs, STELLAR, and AnnoSpat have recently advanced the computational annotation of multiplex imaging data, their performance often depends on dataset-specific retraining and may not be generalizable across staining batches or tissue types. Given the signal heterogeneity introduced by iterative TSA amplification and photochemical quenching, we adopted a rule-based gating strategy in this study to ensure transparent, reproducible, and cross-sample–consistent lineage identification. Nevertheless, we envision that hybrid frameworks integrating manual gating priors with probabilistic or deep-learning classifiers (e.g., Nimbus and Pixie) will provide more scalable and generalizable solutions for future highplex spatial proteomic studies.

Although direct computational integration between the scRNA-seq and CmTSA datasets was not performed in this pilot cohort, our study established an indirect cross-modality linkage by designing the CmTSA antibody panel based on scRNA-defined cellular lineages. The consistent marker expression patterns between the two modalities support the reliability of CmTSA-based phenotyping. In future work, the integration of spatial proteomic data with single-cell transcriptomic profiles using multimodal frameworks such as Seurat v5, MISTy, or Tangram will further enable the quantitative mapping of transcriptional states within spatial contexts, thereby providing mechanistic insights into tumor–immune–stromal interactions.

Materials and methods

Clinical samples

All clinical FFPE section samples, including those from cancer of the tonsils, lungs, colon, spleen, pancreas, stomach, kidneys, liver, and cervix, were obtained from the Department of Pathology at Sichuan University West China Hospital. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (Approval No. 1027), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Animal samples for CmTSA

Adult C57 mice housed in an SPF-grade animal facility were anesthetized and perfused with 50 mL of PBS via the left ventricle, allowing blood to exit from the right atrium. Afterward, the mice were perfused with 50 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) tissue fixative. Various organs (brain, heart, liver and spleen) were then collected and immersed in 25 mL of 4% PFA for fixation. After 48 h of fixation, the tissues were dehydrated and embedded. Tissue sections 4 μm in thickness were prepared using a paraffin microtome, floated on 45 °C warm water to flatten, and then carefully transferred to the center of the adhesive glass slides. The sections were baked at 60 °C for one hour to ensure firm adherence to the slides.

Cell line and mouse primary neuronal cells

HeLa cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin. The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For the preparation of coverslips, cells were seeded onto sterilized glass coverslips placed in 24-well culture plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h prior to further experimental procedures.

For mouse primary neuronal cells, subventricular zone (SVZ) neurons were extracted from postnatal day 8 (P8) mouse pups using a previously established protocol. Briefly, the brains were harvested, and the SVZ was isolated, followed by enzymatic dissociation with accutase and mechanical trituration to obtain a single-cell suspension. The cells were then cultured in neurobasal medium supplemented with 2% B27, 0.5 mM l-glutamine, and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin. The culture conditions were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator. Similar to the HeLa cells, the primary neuronal cells were seeded onto sterilized glass coverslips in 24-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h before the subsequent experiments were performed.

Deparaffinization and antigen retrieval

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were incubated in a thermostatic incubator at 65 °C overnight. The sections were then sequentially immersed in staining jars containing xylene (three changes), with each immersion lasting 5 min. Afterward, the sections were gradually rehydrated by immersion in staining jars containing absolute ethanol (three changes), 95% ethanol, 85% ethanol, and 75% ethanol, each for 3 min. Finally, the sections were placed in a staining jar with purified water and rinsed three times for 3 min each. For antigen retrieval, the sections were placed in a staining jar containing 1× EDTA antigen retrieval solution and heated in a microwave until small bubbles formed in the solution. The jar was then transferred to a preheated water bath set at 95 °C, where the sections were incubated for 30 min. Afterward, the sections were allowed to cool to room temperature naturally. The sections were then washed with PBS three times for 2 min each.

H&E staining

After deparaffinization and antigen retrieval, the tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin, which binds to nucleic acids and stains the cell nuclei blue. Next, the sections underwent bluing, where they were treated with a weak alkaline solution to intensify the blue color of the hematoxylin. Afterward, the sections were stained with eosin, which binds to cytoplasmic and extracellular proteins, providing a pink-to-red contrast. The sections were then dehydrated through a series of graded ethanol solutions, finishing with xylene. Finally, the stained sections were mounted with a coverslip using permanent mounting medium. This process resulted in tissue sections with nuclei stained blue and cytoplasmic components stained in various shades of pink, allowing for detailed histological examination.

CmTSA workflow

Background fluorescence quenching

After deparaffinization and antigen retrieval, the tissue sections were placed in the LUMINIRIS Fluorescence Quenching System (#MH030101), incubated with IRISKit® HyperView Quench Buffer (#MH900102), and subjected to LED-based fluorescence quenching at a power level of 5 for 8 min. During quenching, the integrated LED illumination intensity was set between 220 and 510 mW/cm² and adjusted according to the selected wavelength setting. The sections were then washed with PBS.

CmTSA

Before staining, a hydrophobic barrier was drawn around the tissue sections using an immunohistochemistry pen. An IRISKit® HyperView multiplex immunostaining kit (#MH900205) was used for antigen labeling. The sequential labeling was performed with primary antibody for 20 min, HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 20 min, and fluorescent dye for 5–10 min per antibody. After labeling with each antibody, the IRISKit® HyperView Advanced Ab Stripping Kit was used for antibody stripping. Different tyramide-based fluorescent dyes, specifically cyclic-480 (EX: 465–495; EM: 512–558), cyclic-550 (EX: 540–560; EM: 575–595), and cyclic-630 (EX: 620–640; EM: > 665), were used for antibody staining. These dyes are small-molecule organic fluorophores derived from fluorescein or cyanine dye structures. Their spectral properties align with those of common imaging channels: cyclic-480 is suitable for the green channel (analogous to FITC/Alexa 488), cyclic-550 is suitable for the orange/red channel (comparable to Cy3/TRITC), and cyclic-630 is suitable for the far-red channel (similar to Cy5/Alexa Fluor 647). After completing the three antibody labeling steps, DAPI was used for nuclear staining. After being washed with different reagents, the sections were washed twice with PBS, with each wash lasting for 3 min. Finally, the sections were mounted with mounting medium, and whole-slide imaging was performed using an EVIDENT VS200 microscope with a 20× objective lens. The image resolution was 0.345 μm/px.

Differential quenching of fluorescent molecules

After imaging was completed, the specific staining fluorescence was quenched, while ensuring that DAPI remained unquenched to serve as coordinates for image registration. The procedure and background fluorescence quenching were performed in the same manner. Once quenching was complete, the samples were subjected to the next round of staining using three antibodies.

Image registration

After images were acquired from multiple rounds of imaging, an algorithm automatically identified approximate feature points in the DAPI channel. A rigid transformation (translation and rotation) was then applied to find an optimal overlapping method that minimized errors, ensuring the maximum overlap of all feature points. The images were subsequently standardized by converting the bit depth to a uniform 8-bit format with a grayscale range of 0–255. This standardization allows the images to be properly displayed on standard monitors, facilitating subsequent registration, analysis, and other operations.

With respect to the workflow, staining of 40 channels usually required 13 iterative cycles (averaging three markers per cycle). Each cycle (antibody incubation, TSA reaction, imaging, and mild stripping) took approximately 6–8 h, resulting in a total experimental time of ~4–5 days for completing the 40-plex staining on a single FFPE section.

IF staining of FFPE sections

After antigen retrieval on the paraffin sections was complete, the background fluorescence was quenched for 8 min, followed by a 3 min PBS wash, which was repeated twice. The sections were incubated with the appropriate primary antibody at 37 °C for 1 h. Afterward, the sections were washed with PBS for 3 min twice, after which the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibody was added, and the sections were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Then, the sections were washed with PBS for 3 min twice, and the nuclei were stained with DAPI diluted 1:500 for 5 min. The samples were washed again with PBS for 3 min twice, and the sections were mounted using suitable mounting medium. Finally, whole-slide imaging was performed using an EVIDENT VS200 microscope with a 20× objective lens.

CmTSA staining of cultured cells

Healthy HeLa cells or neuron-differentiated cells were grown in a 12-well plate. The cells were then washed with PBS for 3 min twice. The cells were fixed with 4% PFA at 4 °C for 30 min. After fixation, the cells were washed again with PBS for 3 min twice. A sufficient amount of IRISKit® HyperView Quench Buffer (#MH900102) was added to cover the cells, and the cells were incubated for 5 min. The antibody staining steps were performed according to the CmTSA protocol used for FFPE samples. Finally, imaging was performed using an EVIDENT SpinSR microscope with a 100× objective lens.

Antibody screening and stripping efficiency validation

To verify whether antibodies are suitable for multiple rounds of CmTSA staining, we established a standardized screening workflow. In brief, antibodies were first applied to FFPE tissue sections for labeling and TSA visualization. After color development, the slides were incubated at 60 °C for 20 min in IRISKit® HyperView Advanced Ab Stripping Buffer (6 M urea, 62.5 mM Tris–HCl, pH 6.8, 100 mM β-mercaptoethanol) to remove bound antibodies. A second TSA reaction with a distinct fluorophore was subsequently performed to detect any residual antibodies. If the fluorescent signals remained at the same sites, this result indicated that the antibody possessed excessively high affinity and was not completely stripped, suggesting potential cross-reactivity during multicycle CmTSA staining. Conversely, the complete disappearance of signals indicated low affinity and high specificity, making the antibody suitable for iterative CmTSA applications. Representative results are shown in Supplementary Fig. S3a.

Cell segmentation

The StarDist model is a powerful tool for accurately segmenting cell nuclei in microscopy images. We imported the pretrained StarDist model into QuPath for cell segmentation in multicolor images. The preprocessed DAPI channel image was then passed through the StarDist model, which employs a star–convex polygon representation for precise nuclear segmentation. Simultaneously, we obtained the mean fluorescence intensity for each channel across different cellular structures, including the nucleus, cytoplasm, membrane, and whole cell, enabling us to filter marker positivity effectively. Additionally, we extracted the x and y coordinates of each cell’s nuclear centroid, which was used to calculate spatial relationships between cells.

The key StarDist script parameters used in QuPath were validated across multiple large FFPE tissue sections, as follows: def stardist = StarDist2D.builder(modelPath).channels(‘DAPI’).normalizePercentiles(1,99).threshold(0.5).pixelSize(0.4).cellExpansion(1).measureShape().measureIntensity().build()

Cell type thresholds for marker positivity and lineage assignment

We identified the threshold for each marker channel individually in every multiplex image of FFPE sections. First, we selected the channel for which the threshold needed to be set. Then, we chose the specific cellular location where the marker was expressed, such as the nucleus or membrane. A threshold was set within the 0–255 range, where cells with values equal to or above the threshold were classified as marker positive, and those below the threshold were classified as marker negative. This process was manually supervised, with marker-positive cells displayed on the stained image using a predefined mask color. Since certain antigen markers are shared by multiple cell types and some cell types require multiple markers for accurate identification, an annotation rule is essential for ensuring consistent, accurate, and automated classification of cellular subtypes. To assign cellular phenotypes, we established a method based on typical lineage markers, expected population abundance, and staining quality supervision.

Background fluorescence quenching efficiency

Before and after quenching, the same imaging parameters were used on the same section. Six non-overlapping ROIs were randomly selected, and the fluorescence intensity of the three imaging channels was measured using QuPath.

Specific marker quenching efficiency

For five strictly consecutive sections, triple staining was performed using the CmTSA method, with DAPI applied as the nuclear counterstain. After staining, whole-slide scanning was performed under identical imaging conditions. The first section was not quenched, the second was quenched for 3 min, the third for 6 min, the fourth for 9 min, and the fifth for 12 min. After quenching, whole-slide scanning was performed again under identical imaging conditions. At the same location on all five consecutive sections, six nonoverlapping ROIs were randomly selected, and the fluorescence intensity in the stained channels was measured using QuPath.

Assessment of antibody stripping efficiency

Antibody stripping efficiency was assessed to ensure complete removal of antibodies between staining rounds. After the tissue was stained with the first antibody (using the cyclic-480 fluorophore) and antibody stripping was completed, the effectiveness of the stripping was tested by reincubating the tissue with the secondary antibody for the first antigen for 20 min, followed by visualization with the cyclic-550 fluorophore. After imaging, six nonoverlapping ROIs were randomly selected in QuPath to measure the fluorescence intensity of cyclic-480 and cyclic-550.

Spatial interaction analysis

To identify significant interactions and separations between cell types within functional niches, we adapted the spatial neighborhood analysis method from the SOAPy package by rewriting it in R to suit the needs of this study. All calculations and plotting were performed in the R (v4.3.1) environment. The core of this method involves calculating the number of edges between each pair of cell types in the network and comparing it with the sum of the edges between the paired cell types and other cells. During the calculation, a permutation test was introduced to assess the specificity of the distribution in the real data.
