Regularity and correlation analysis of regional lymph node metastasis in nonoperative patients with non-small cell lung cancer based on positron emission tomography/computed tomography images
Authors: Feifan Sun, Zhiming Chen, Daijun Zhou, Zhihui Li, Haoyang Wang, Rong Zhao, Jing Xian, Jingjing Peng, Xingchen Peng, Chaoyang Jiang, Mei Shi, Dong Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDefinitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) is the standard treatment for locally advanced, inoperable non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Previous studies have mainly focused on examining local failure and recurrence patterns after surgery and the principles of lymph node metastasis (LNM) in surgical candidates with NSCLC. However, these studies were just only able to guide postoperative radiotherapy (PORT) and the patterns of LNM in patients with resected NSCLC was inadequate to represent that in locally advanced inoperable NSCLC patients for guiding target volume delineation of CCRT. In this study, we aimed to analyze the metastasis regularities and establish the correlations between different lymph node levels in NSCLC patients without any intervention using positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) images.MethodsOverall, 358 patients with N1–N3 NSCLC admitted in our hospital between 2018 and 2022 were retrospectively analyzed. The diagnosis of metastatic lymph nodes was reviewed and determined using the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer standard and the standardized value of the PET/CT examination. Univariate and multivariate analysis were performed to investigate the correlations between the different levels were evaluated by using of the chi-square test and logistic regression model.ResultsThe lymph nodes with the highest metastasis rates in patients with left lung cancer were in order as follows: 10L, 4L, 5, 4R, and 7; while in those with right lung cancer they were 10R, 4R, 7, 2R, and 1R. Notably, we found left lung patients were more likely to have contralateral hilar, mediastinal and supraclavicular lymph nodes involved, and the right lung group exhibited a higher propensity for ipsilateral mediastinum and supraclavicular lymph node invasion. Furthermore, correlation analysis revealed there were significant correlative patterns in the LNM across different levels.ConclusionsThis study elucidated the patterns of primary LNM in patients with NSCLC who had not undergone surgery (without any treatment interventions) and the correlations between lymph node levels. These findings were expected to provide useful reference for target volume delineation in definitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy in locally advanced NSCLC patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13014-024-02523-5.

Introduction

Lung cancer poses a major global health threat with high prevalence and fatality rates worldwide in 2020 - there were 2.2 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths reported globally; China accounted for the highest proportion at 37.0% and 39.8%, respectively [1–3]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), which comprises about 85% of all cases, often leads to advanced-stage diagnoses [4, 5], with only a minority (20–25%) eligible for surgical resection; even after successful surgery, recurrence affects up to half of these patients leading to mortality [6–8]. Regional lymph node metastasis (LNM) significantly contributes to this outcome.

Definitive concurrent or sequential chemoradiotherapy remains the standard treatment for locally advanced, inoperable NSCLC, even in the era of immunotherapy [9]. The 2018 European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology (ESTRO)-Advisory Committee on Radiation Oncology Practice (ACROP) guidelines provide recommendations on target volume delineation for standard clinical scenarios in definitive (chemo)radiotherapy (RT) for locally advanced NSCLC. These guidelines are generally accepted and applied in clinical practice. The delineation of the clinical target volume (CTV) is inherently complex and often sparks controversy. Additionally, these recommendations are generally based on empirical considerations and are selected depending on the volumes of target areas, dose limitations of organs at risk, patient tolerance, etc.

The delineation of the CTV typically relies on the principles of LNM and the patterns of failure after surgery. This is particularly relevant in postoperative radiotherapy (PORT) as previous studies predominantly focused on examining the role of NSCLC lymphatic metastasis in postoperative recurrence patients [10–12]. However, surgery disrupts the lymphatic drainage pathway, and findings from studies on postoperative recurrence patients may not accurately represent the actual LNM patterns of NSCLC without any intervention. Furthermore, some studies retrospectively investigated patterns of LNM which were confirmed by postoperative pathological microscopy in NSCLC patients who had undergone thoracotomy [13–15]. Admittedly, pathological microscopy is gold standard to judge the LNM. However, patients undergoing radical surgery usually have earlier stages and less LNM compared to local advanced inoperable patients. For example, locally advanced NSCLC patients with multiply mediastinal LNM (multiply station N2) or supraclavicular LNM (N3) are recognized to be unfit for operation in principle, which could just exactly be major candidates for concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT). Meanwhile, systematic lymphadenectomy in NSCLC surgery generally do not extend to lymph nodes in supraclavicular fossa so that it is difficult to obtain the LNM information of these regions by postoperative pathological findings. Therefore, studies in patients with resected NSCLC were inadequate to interpret the inherent lymph node metastatic patterns of the locally advanced inoperable NSCLC patients to provide the guidance for target volume delineation of CCRT. Taken together, the inherent LNM patterns of NSCLC should be explored to guide the delineation of the CTV in definitive or sequential chemoradiotherapy. Nevertheless, it still lacks of this study at present.

The determination of the metastatic lymph node N stage still poses a critical challenge for tumor staging and the delineation of targets in definitive or sequential chemoradiotherapy of patients with NSCLC. It is unrealistic to pathologically confirm all metastatic lymph nodes by endobronchial ultrasound sampling (EBUS) or mediastinoscopy. Currently, imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and positron emission tomography/CT (PET/CT) are employed to identify metastatic lymph nodes at the clinical N stage prior to pathological biopsy. PET/CT is a well-established technique for lung cancer staging that integrates morphological CT data with metabolic data on the cellular uptake of 18 F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG). It has demonstrated superior accuracy compared with CT and MRI in distinguishing LNM and is strongly recommended in the ESTRO ACROP guidelines for defining target volumes in the treatment of locally advanced NSCLC [16, 17]. Furthermore, the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) has achieved consensus on the utilization of PET/CT imaging for delineating the target volume for curative radiotherapy of NSCLC. In cases where FDG-PET indicates positive lymph nodes in the mediastinum without support from CT imaging, it is advisable to encompass them in the GTV when diagnosis is uncertain. Lymph nodes that are CT-positive but PET-negative are generally excluded from the GTV, underscoring the significance of PET/CT imaging in delineating the target volume [18]. However, the majority of previous studies focusing on the patterns of LNM in postoperative patients who developed lung cancer recurrence have detected metastatic lymph nodes using CT and MRI, potentially influencing the precision of assessments [10–14].

In this study, PET/CT was used to explore the inherent patterns and correlations of regional LNM in patients with NSCLC without any intervention. This study was expected to offer insights for optimizing target volume delineation during radiotherapy in patients with locally advanced NSCLC.

Materials and methods

Patient population

Following approval from the Ethics Committee of the General Hospital of Western Theater Command PLA, we retrospectively enrolled data from 358 patients with N1–N3 stage NSCLC in our hospital from January 2018 to December 2022. 1,852 radiologically metastatic lymph node levels were retrospectively analyzed. Patients with N0 stage NSCLC were excluded from the study. The 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC)/Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM staging system was used for tumor staging. The regional lymph nodes detected in patients with NSCLC were classified according to the 2009 International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) Staging Project. The retrospective data collected in this study did not include any therapeutic interventions, and all patients underwent whole-body PET/CT examination prior to treatment.

Diagnostic criteria for regional LNM in NSCLC

A Siemens Biograph Sensation 16 PET/CT imaging instrument was used to scan patients following a fasting period of > 6 h and with fasting blood glucose levels controlled under 10.0 mmol/L. A 7.4 MBq/kg dose of 18 F-FDG was injected intravenously through the elbow. Following a 60-min rest, whole-body CT and PET images were obtained from the top of the skull to the upper thigh. The obtained images were sent to a Wizard workstation for automatic image fusion.

Two experienced nuclear medicine physicians with backgrounds in diagnostic radiology independently conducted a double-blind examination of the images to determine the presence of LNM in the hilar, mediastinal, and supraclavicular regions of lung cancer patients. The N stage was recorded based on their findings. In cases of disagreement, a senior physician guided the image reading process to reach a consensus.

The interpretation of the PET/CT images involved a combination of visual and semiquantitative analyses. The software on the workstation was used in selecting the regions of interest and measuring the maximum standardized value (SUVmax) of the lesions. The criteria for diagnosing LNM in lung cancer using PET/CT were as follows: lymph nodes with a minor diameter of ≥ 1.0 cm, SUVmax ≥ 2.5, and the absence of plaque calcification. Lymph nodes detected by CT alone, even those with a short diameter of ≥ 1.0 cm, were deemed negative for LNM if no visible increase was observed in the radioactive uptake.

Statistical analysis

A univariate analysis of 358 patients was conducted to compare different lymph node levels using a chi-square test. Multivariate analysis was performed using a binary logistic regression model to examine the correlation between metastatic lymph node levels. The goodness of fit in the logistic regression model was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, where a P-value of > 0.05 indicated a well-estimated logistic model. Statistical significance was defined as a P-value of < 0.05. Data analysis was conducted using the SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Baseline data of 358 patients included in the retrospective analysis

This retrospective study included 358 patients (N1: 9.5%, N2: 32.1%, and N3: 58.4%) with confirmed NSCLC. The majority of patients were men (67.1%), and the average age was 63.2 years (range, 27–96 years). Of the total patients, 230 (64.2%) were aged ≥ 60 years. Primary lesions developed in the left lung in 45% of the patients (upper lobe, 28.8%; lower lobe, 16.2%) and the right lung in 55% of the patients (upper lobe, 26.3%; middle lobe, 5.8%; lower lobe 22.9%). The most common types of lung cancer were adenocarcinoma (62%) and squamous cell carcinoma (33.3%). The average maximum tumor diameter was 4.59 cm (range, 0.9–16 cm). Local invasion was observed in 233 patients (65.1%), with visceral pleura being the most common site. Distant metastases occurred in 55.6% of patients, most commonly affecting the bones and non-regional lymph nodes. The proportion of patients with T stage was as follows: T1a (0.3%), T1b (8.4%), T1c (8.1%), T2a (19.5%), T2b (9.5%), T3 (20.4%), and T4 (33.5%). One patient had an indeterminate T stage due to the challenges in measuring tumor size. The proportion of patients according to TNM staging was as follows: IIB (2.8%), IIIA (12%), IIIB (16.2%), IIIB (13.4%), IVA (31%), and IVB (24.6%). Variations in the distribution of metastatic lymph nodes in different regions

In the present study, we investigated the distribution of LNM in patients with primary lung tumors in different regions. The findings showed variations in the levels of lymph node involvement based on tumor location.

In patients with tumors in the right upper lung, the most common LNM sites were the 10R, 4R, 2R, 7, and 1R. In patients with tumors in the right middle lung, the top five LNM sites were 10R, 4R, 2R, 7, and 1R. In patients with tumors in the right lower lung, the most frequent sites of LNM were 10R, 7, 4R, 2R, and 8. In patients with tumors in the left upper lung, the top five LNM sites were 10L, 4L, 5, 4R, and 6. Lastly, in patients with tumors in the left lower lung, the most common sites of LNM were 10L, 7, 4L, 4R, 5, and 8. Overall, the location of the lung tumor influenced the LNM patterns. The left lung had a higher likelihood of contralateral hilar and contralateral supraclavicular LNM compared with the right lung. Additionally, metastases to the opposite mediastinal lymph nodes were more common in the left than in the right lung. However, no significant difference was found in the prevalence of ipsilateral hilar LNM between the two groups. All P-values were < 0.05. Discussion

We retrospectively analyzed the LNM rates in 358 patients with N1–N3 stage and explored the actual patterns and correlation of LNM in patients with locally advanced NSCLC at first diagnosis without any intervention (no surgery, drug therapy, radiotherapy, etc.) based on the PET/CT imaging results. Currently, the delineation of the lymph node target volume in patients with locally advanced NSCLC undergoing postoperative or definitive radiotherapy is controversial due to variations in the experience of radiation oncologists. Although these guidelines offer multiple options for delineating the target volume, a unified standard has not been established [19]. Previously, our research team reported LNM patterns in nasopharyngeal carcinoma [20]. However, lung cancer exhibits distinct characteristics compared with nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and primary tumors located in different lobes demonstrate varying patterns and correlations of LNM [21, 22]. We aimed to investigate the patterns and correlations of the inherent LNM in patients with locally advanced NSCLC and provide initial ideas for radiotherapy target optimization in our center.

In terms of the incidence of LNM at various levels, we found that the five levels most susceptible to LNM in patients with NSCLC, with tumors originating in the right lung, were all in the ipsilateral hilum, mediastinal and supraclavicular regions (10R, 4R, 7, 2R, and 1R). In addition to LNM in the ipsilateral hilum, mediastinum (10L, 4L, 5, and 7), patients with NSCLC whose tumors originated in the left lung were also prone to LNM in the 4R level of the contralateral mediastinum. Feng et al. discovered that patients with NSCLC experienced local recurrence after surgery and identified the following sites of invasion in patients with left lung cancer: 4R was the most common site, followed by 7, 4L, 6, 10L, and 5. In patients with right lung cancer, the most frequent site was 2R, followed by 10R, 4R, and 7 [23]. This implies that there may be certain similarities between the initial regional LNM patterns and postoperative recurrence patterns in patients with NSCLC. Moreover, patients with left lung cancer were more likely to have contralateral hilar, contralateral mediastinum, and contralateral supraclavicular lymph nodes than those with right lung cancer. By contrast, right lung patients were more likely to have an invasion of the ipsilateral mediastinum and ipsilateral supraclavicular lymph nodes than those with left lung cancer. Kelsey et al. found that among patients with postoperative local recurrence of NSCLC, contralateral mediastinal LNM was more prevalent in patients with left lung cancer than in those with right lung cancer [24]. Thus, this study also has some implications for target volume delineation during PORT.

According to the ESTRO guidelines [19], for patients with locally advanced NSCLC, the GTV should include lymph nodes that have been confirmed to be metastatic by imaging or biopsy. The CTV should expand beyond the GTV margins and may encompass selective hilar and/or adjacent lymph nodes. In PORT for locally advanced NSCLC, the CTV should include the resected mediastinal lymph nodes, bronchial stump, ipsilateral hilum, 4 level, and 7 level. The delineation of the GTV in the Chinese radiotherapy guidelines is similar to that recommended by ESTRO. For patients undergoing definitive radiotherapy, the CTV should be expanded based on the GTV; however, additional negative lymph should not be included. In patients with right-sided lung cancer with N2 involvement, the CTV should comprise the bronchial stump, right hilar and subcarinal lymph nodes (including 4R when subcarinal lymph nodes are positive), and the ipsilateral mediastinal lymph nodes (including 4R and 2R). If the pathological test confirms the involvement of 2R after surgery in patients with right-sided lung cancer, the inclusion of 1R in the CTV is recommended. For PORT targeting left-sided lung cancer, the CTV should consist of a bronchial stump (for central tumors), left hilar and subcarinal lymph nodes (with recommendations to include both 4R and 4L when the subcarinal lymph nodes are positive), and ipsilateral mediastinal lymph nodes (including 2L, 4L, 5, and 6). The inclusion of 1L in the CTV remains controversial if there is pathologically confirmed involvement of 2L after surgery. Similarly, for N2 patients with left-sided lung cancer undergoing PORT, the inclusion of 2R and 4R remains controversial. The findings of this study may offer a theoretical foundation for resolving certain disputes within established guidelines.

In this study, the correlation between various metastatic lymph node levels was initially verified by univariate analysis. Following the univariate analysis, a subsequent multivariate analysis was conducted to discern the fundamental patterns of metastasis among different lymph node levels. For example, in patients with primary tumors in the left lung, we found the high incidence of 10L metastasis (left hilum) was closely correlated with level 7 (subcarinal region), the high incidence of 4L metastasis (ipsilateral mediastinum) was closely correlated with 4R (contralateral mediastinum) and the supraclavicular lymph nodes (1L and 1R), and level 7 was correlated with 4R, 8, and 9. In addition, 1L and 2L, 1R and 2R, 1L and 1R were also correlated. Therefore, when determining the GTV based on imaging or histological results in patients with left lung cancer undergoing definitive radiotherapy, lymph node levels that are closely associated with positive lymph node stations identified through imaging and/or histology might be considered. The radiation-related toxicity of the target organ should also be taken into consideration [25]. The next step involves conducting a prospective study to further validate the efficacy of the recommended radiotherapy strategy.

In our study, we observed a relatively high proportion of patients with an N3 stage, which may be related to the fact that patients with left lung cancer were prone to contralateral mediastinal LNM, while those with right lung cancer were prone to metastasis to 1R. In the analysis of risk factors (Supplementary Table 5), adenocarcinoma and distant metastasis (DM) were identified as risk factors for the N stage. Patients with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and NSCLC with DM were more prone to having an N3 stage, which might be related to the biological characteristics of adenocarcinoma cells. Several studies have consistently demonstrated that LUAD exhibits a propensity for LNM even in its early stages. Furthermore, compared with lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), LUAD displays a higher susceptibility to LNM due to various underlying mechanisms, such as macrophages, tumor-infiltrating T lymphocytes, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and non-coding ribonucleic acid [26–29]. Another study indicated that NSCLC patients with a more advanced N stage at the time of diagnosis exhibited an increased likelihood of developing DM [30]. Additionally, LNM in melanoma facilitates the initiation and progression of DM [31]. The results of our study reveal a higher prevalence of N3 disease in patients with DM. This implies a potential interrelationship between LNM and DM and suggests that they may serve as mutually associated risk factors. Clinical observations also note a higher likelihood of LNM and DM in patients with LUAD compared to those with LUSC.

Our study has several limitations. When discussing the rules and correlations of LNM, the patients were divided into left and right lungs; however, the right upper, right middle, right lower, left upper, and left lower lobes were not further separated to explore the inherent rules of LNM. In future studies, a larger sample size should be used to facilitate a more precise stratified analysis. In addition, this study was retrospective in nature and lacked follow-up data to validate the identified patterns. Nonetheless, the outcomes might offer a theoretical foundation for prospective studies in the future.

Conclusion

Based on PET/CT findings, we report the inherent patterns and correlations of regional LNM in patients with NSCLC without any intervention. Based on our findings and aligning with the ESTRO and Chinese radiotherapy guidelines, we propose preliminary recommendations from our center to enhance the optimization of target volume delineation for definitive RT and PORT in patients with locally advanced NSCLC.
