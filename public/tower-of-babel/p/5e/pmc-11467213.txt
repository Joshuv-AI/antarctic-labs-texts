Bending properties and damage evolution of fiber-reinforced aeolian sand backfill materials
Authors: Yanan Sun, Bing Liang, Tao Liu, Mingxu Li, Zhifa Qin, Shenghao Zuo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackfilling is a promising measure for controlling surface subsidence in mined-out areas and disposing solid wastes from the mineral processing, there are increasing demands of enhanced toughness and anti-cracking properties of backfill materials to prolong the service life under the complex loads. In this study, polypropylene (PP) fibers were employed to improve performance of backfills, four-point bending and uniaxial compression tests were conducted to investigate the failure process and damage evolution of fiber-reinforced aeolian-sand backfill materials (FABs) using Digital Image Correlation (DIC) and Acoustic Emission (AE) techniques. The results show that the compressive strength tends to decrease with the increasing fiber volume fractions, while the four-point bending strength shows a tendency to increase initially and then decrease, and the optimum volume fraction of PP fiber is 0.6%. At the optimal fiber volume fraction, PP fibers with lengths of 3 mm and 9 mm resulted in a 65.25% and 81.62% increase in the four-point bending strength of FABs, respectively. As indicated by DIC measurements, PP fibers with a length of 9 mm were more effective in controlling the horizontal and vertical displacements of FABs under four-point bending loads than that of 3 mm, and the cracks developed more slowly at the same deflection. In addition, PP fibers with a length of 9 mm have stronger crack extension delay characteristics due to longer effective anchorage distance, as evidenced by more frequent acoustic emission ringing counts and higher cumulative ringing counts. The results of the study may provide a theoretical basis for the application of FABs materials in backfilling.Keywords: Solid waste-based backfill, Bending performance, Damage evolution, Digital image correlation, Acoustic emissionSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Engineering

Introduction

The use of cemented backfills composed of cemented material and solid waste to backfill the mine-out area can effectively control the surface subsidence of the mine-out area1,2. Traditional backfills composing of mining solid waste and low-content cementitious materials essentially have characteristics of high porosity and low strength3,4. However, in regions of southwestern and northern China where seismic frequency is relatively high, there are elevated demands for the toughness, strength, and other properties of backfill materials5,6. Once backfill materials crack, their strength and durability will sharply decrease with the cracks propagate7. Therefore, effectively controlling the formation and propagation of cracks in backfilled materials and improving their toughness are essential requirements for achieving safe backfill mining operations.

In recent years, fiber-reinforced materials have been widely applied in the field of civil engineering8,9. The incorporation of fibers into the matrix can significantly enhance the mechanical properties, toughness, and crack resistance of cementitious materials10, which has promoted the applications in areas such as structural reinforcement and construction materials11. Introducing a high-performance and cost-effective fiber into the backfill material can achieve a balance between enhancing material performance and controlling backfill costs. Polypropylene (PP) fibers are synthetic fibers made from polypropylene resin and have advantages of lightweight, high strength, corrosion resistance, and non-flammability12,13. The incorporation of polypropylene fibers into cement-based materials has been demonstrated to enhance the material’s tensile strength, impact resistance, durability, crack resistance, and overall performance. Additionally, polypropylene fibers offer the advantage of low cost compared to other types of synthetic fibers14,15. The incorporation of PP fibers significantly improves the mechanical performance of the cementitious materials, especially the early strain hardening and softening behavior16. Acoustic emission techniques detected that the introduction of PP fibers altered the damage and destruction process of cemented tailings backfill under loading17. In addition, the length and volume of PP fibers used have two sides on the flow and mechanical properties of the mortar, which depends on the distribution and potential agglomeration of fibers18–20.

The PP fibers enhance the crack and impact resistance and toughness of traditional cementitious materials through actions such as bridging and crack blocking, the effect is closely related to the interaction between the fibers and the cement matrix, the key influencing factors are fiber length and volume fraction. However, backfill materials have significantly different flow and mechanical properties, which may further affect fiber dispersion and pull-out behavior, the effect of different parameters of fibers on the flexural and damage resistance of backfill remains unclear. This study explores the failure process under four-point bending and the damage evolution under uniaxial compression of fiber-reinforced aeolian-sand backfill materials (FABs) using Digital Image Correlation (DIC) and Acoustic Emission (AE) techniques. The study evaluates the impact of different parameters of PP fibers on the performance of FABs, providing reference for the application of this novel backfill material in backfill mining operations.

To achieve cleaner production of backfill materials, fly ash (FA) and aeolian sand (AS) were also utilized. Specimen preparation

In this study, OPC and FA serving as cementitious materials and AS as aggregate were used to design the mixture, with the mix proportions shown in Table 4. The incorporation content of FA was fixed at 30% of the total mass of binder, the solid mass fraction was 65%, and the water-binder ratio (W/B) was 0.6. To enhance the bending performance of FABs, PP fibers was introduced at volume fractions of 0.4, 0.6, and 0.8%. Before specimen preparation, the raw materials were preconditioned at 20 ± 2 °C for 24 h. First, OPC, FA, AS, and PP fibers were dry mixed at low speed (150 rpm) for 1.5 min using a planetary mixer. Then, mixing water was added into the mixture and mixed at low speed (150 rpm) for 1.5 min, followed by high speed (300 rpm) mixing for 2.5 min, and finally low speed (150 rpm) mixing for another 1 min. Before casting into the molds, the fresh mixtures were manually mixed to ensure uniformity. Then fresh FABs were poured into molds with dimensions of 40 × 40 × 40 mm³ and 40 × 40 × 160 mm³, and the specimens were covered with plastic film and cured at 20 ± 2 °C. After curing for 48 h, the specimens were demolded and further cured in the standard curing condition (20 ± 2 °C, 98% RH) until the specified ages of experiments. Specimens were named according to the naming convention PxLy, where x represents the fiber volume fraction, and y represents the fiber length.

Test methods

Mechanical property tests

The compressive strength of hardened specimens at 28 days (40 × 40 × 40 mm³) was measured using a hydraulic servo testing machine. The loading rate for compressive strength tests was set at 2400 ± 200 N/s, following the guidelines specified in Chinese standard GB/T 17,671 − 2021.

For the four-point bending strength test of FABs, the procedures recommended by ASTM C1609/C1609M-12 were adopted. The specimens were placed on two support pins at the bottom with a fixed distance of 120 mm, and two loading pins were positioned equidistantly with a distance of 20 mm on the top surface, as shown in Fig. 1. Digital image correlation (DIC) tests

DIC has extensive application prospects in the detection of concrete and mortar for the high-precision and non-contact optical characteristics. In this study, the first step involves uniformly applying a layer of white matte coating to the surface of the specimen, then unordered point calibrations are sprayed on the surface using a black calibration pen to ensure sufficient contrast during the entire loading process of the specimen. After specimen pretreatment, four-point bending loading is conducted. Image acquisition is performed using a 3 million high-definition color industrial camera equipped with a CW05100-AUSB high-definition industrial lens and supplementary light source. The capture frequency is set to 1 frame per second. The camera is adjusted until the system captures the first image clearly showing speckle patterns on the specimen surface. VIC-2D developed by Correlated Solutions is employed in this study to achieve high-precision measurement of material surface deformation and strain. By analyzing continuous image sequences, displacement and strain information at various points on the exposing surface can be obtained, enabling precise quantification of material deformation behavior. The DIC experimental system is illustrated in Fig. 2a.

DIC and AE tests system.

Acoustic emission (AE) monitoring

AE system (Ds5, China) was employed for the synchronous acquisition of AE signals during the loading process of FABs samples. Two sensors were affixed at the equidistant, central positions on either side of the sample using an adhesive tape. Vaseline was used as the coupling agent to enhance signal transmission between sample and sensors. The acquisition frequency was set to 3 MHz, and the acquired signals were amplified by 40 dB. A threshold amplitude of 45 dB was used to eliminate background noise. Details of the testing system are depicted in Fig. 2b.

Microstructure characterizations

After a four-point bending strength test, thin slices of samples measuring approximately 5 × 5 × 2 mm³ were selected. Samples of these thin sections were placed in glass tubes filled with isopropyl alcohol to stop hydration, the isopropyl alcohol being renewed within the first 3 days. After 7 days of immersion, these samples were transferred to a desiccator for 7 days to completely evaporate the isopropanol. The microstructure of the FABs was observed for 28 days using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, TESCAN MIRA LMS) with an accelerating voltage of 15.0 kV.

Results and discussion

Mechanical properties of FABs

Results of compressive and four-point bending strength of FABs are shown in Fig. 3. As shown in Fig. 3a, with the increasing volume fraction of PP fibers, the compressive strength of FABs samples shows a gradually decreasing trend. PP fibers embedded in hardened backfill matrix are influenced by the hydration of OPC and FA particles and the distribution of AS. On the one hand, the hydration products dominate the pull-out behavior of PP fibers from the matrix, thus affecting the reinforcement effect; on the other hand, the dispersion of PP fibers is also affected by the fiber volume fraction incorporated, which has a significant impact on the mechanical strength.

Four-point bending strength of FABs at 28 days of age.

The four-point bending strength of FABs containing PP fibers at 28 days is shown in Fig. 3b. As shown, the bending strength of FABs containing PP fibers with lengths of 3 mm and 9 mm exhibits a similar trend, which initially increases and then decreases with the increasing fiber volume fractions, the four-point bending strength reaches the maximum value at a fiber volume fraction of 0.6%. For PP fibers with a length of 3 mm, the incorporation of 0.4 and 0.6% by volume increases the four-point bending strength of FABs s by 30.9 and 65.2%, respectively. However, when the volume fraction increases to 0.8%, the bending strength decreases by 24.8% in comparison with that containing 0.6% fiber. For PP fibers with a length of 9 mm, the incorporation of 0.4 and 0.6% by volume increases the four-point bending strength of FABs by 51.7 and 81.6%, respectively. Similarly, when the volume fraction increases to 0.8%, there is a notable decrease in the bending strength, which is even close to the control group.

The observed phenomenon can be attributed to the dispersion of PP fibers within the backfill matrix, at the appropriate volume fraction, PP fibers is uniformly dispersed, forming a three-dimensional network structure in the matrix. At this point, PP fibers plays a full “bridging” role, and the load is transmitted from the matrix to the PP fibers, greatly enhancing the four-point bending strength of FBs. However, as the PP fibers volume fraction increases, the agglomeration of PP fibers occurs, which result in unevenly dispersion of PP fibers in FABs. This can lead to local stress concentration and higher local porosities, thereby reducing the overall strength of FABs. When the fiber volume fraction is 0.8%, the effective anchorage distance of PP fibers with a length of 9 mm is higher than that of PP fibers with a length of 3 mm, resulting in greater four-point bending strength of P6L9 group than that of P6L3.

Evolution of the failure process of FABs

According to the recommendations of ASTM C160921 and22, four representative deflection values, including points of L/600, , L/150 and L/100, were selected from the full load-deflection curves under four-point bending load in this study, as shown in Fig. 4. The points on the L-D curves corresponding to the deflection values L/600, , L/150, and L/100 are A, B, C, and D, respectively, which together form a sequence. The scattered images were processed using VIC-2D software, and DIC images corresponding to four points were selected to analyze the changes in horizontal displacement, vertical displacement, and crack development.

Load-deflection curve.

Horizontal displacement from DIC images

Based on the aforementioned results, among FABs containing PP fibers with different lengths, the group with the highest four-point bending strength was selected for DIC analysis to further reveal the failure process and the differences in the development of the main cracks of the FABs under four-point bending loads. Using the VIC-2D software, the scatter images of four points A, B, C, and D of specimens P6L3 and P6L9 were analyzed to generate contour plots of the horizontal displacements during loading, as shown in Fig. 5.

Evolution of horizontal displacement of specimen in four-point bending strength test.

Figure 5 illustrates the evolution of horizontal displacement during four-point bending loading on the specimens. At point A, both P6L3 and P6L9 exhibit different “hill-like” distributions of horizontal displacement. In the initial stage, as the load increases, stress concentration zones within the specimens begin to appear continuously. The “hills” in P6L9 are denser than those in P6L3, which is attributed to the fact that the incorporation of long PP fibers lead to the formation of more internal defects and voids, resulting in more stress concentration areas. After reaching the peak load at point B, horizontal displacement gradually increases with load, and the displacement distributes more uniformly. As shown in the figure, there is a noticeable difference in displacement between the left and right sides of the specimen, indicating significant stress concentration in the middle of the bottom surface. At this stage, micro-cracks gradually appear and the load approaches to its maximum value. As the bending load continuously increases, PP fibers gradually share the load from the matrix. The stress concentration in the middle of the bottom surface of P6L9 is less pronounced than that of P6L3, indicating lower levels of crack opening in P6L9. Then, the curve after point B exhibits post-peak residual stress characteristics.

At point C, the noticeable stress concentration appears on the specimens. The contour map shows significant displacement stratification, notable differences of displacement on the same horizontal plane are observed. At this stage, significant cracks have already opened at the middle part, and PP fibers bear the main load. Owing to the different reinforcing and toughening effect of fibers with varying lengths, P6L3 exhibits more pronounced horizontal displacement changes than P6L9. At point D, cracks in the specimens further propagate, and displacement continues to accumulate. However, due to the bridging effect and energy dissipation of PP fibers embedded into the matrix, specimens still maintain a certain load-bearing capacity at this stage.

Vertical displacement from DIC images

Figure 6 presents the evolution of vertical displacement during loading. As shown in the figure, at point A from the load-deflection curve, the matrix starts to bear the stress, resulting in the change of vertical displacement contour plot. As the load increases, the vertical displacement on the specimen shows a trend of stripe-like distribution along the vertical direction. At point B, the contour map of P6L3 show more obvious change, indicating the more effective role of 9 mm fibers in resisting the deformation of the matrix than that of 3 mm fibers. During the four-point bending experiment, the longer anchorage length of 9 mm PP fibers bridges the cracks and prevents the propagation of tiny cracks more effectively under the external load, thereby reducing the vertical displacement deflection.

Evolution of vertical displacement of specimen in four-point bending strength test.

When the deflection value reaches point C at L/150, obvious macroscopic cracks are observed on the specimen. At this point, the vertical displacement of P6L3 appears in a more obviously stripe-like manner, which indicates that P6L3 exhibits more pronounced macroscopic cracks and less residual stresses than P6L9. At point D, the axial deformation further increases, the vertical displacement distribution of P6L3 is more regularly arranged, indicating that it almost completely loses its anti-bending capacity. However, the good crack resistance and toughening effect of the PP fibers still maintain certain bending resistance of FAB specimens. By analyzing the vertical displacements of P6L3 and P6L9, fibers with a length of 9 mm showed a better effect to hold the vertical displacements of the FABs under the same deflection conditions.

Main crack development in FABs

Using the DIC technique, the evolution of the main crack in FABs under four-point bending load was obtained and shown in Fig. 7. As shown in the figure, at point A, no cracks appear on the specimen and the matrix bears the main bending load. The incorporated fibers form a three-dimensional network structure, which enhances the stability of matrix under four-point bending load. As the load approaches to the maximum load at point B, tiny cracks gradually appear in the stress concentration area at the bottom center. When the deflection reaches point C, the crack has developed to the center of the specimen, the crack size in P6L3 is larger than that of P6L9 at the same deflection. As the bending load continues to increase, the macroscopic cracks become more pronounced at point D. The main crack of P6L3 almost penetrates the entire specimen, while the crack size of P6L9 is only two thirds of the specimen, the better resistance to crack extension is attributed to the longer effective anchorage distance using longer PP fibers with a length of 9 mm.

Analysis of the damage mechanism of FABs based on the AE technique

Acoustic emission (AE) phenomena are caused by the formation and propagation of microcracks in solid materials23, AE technology has been developed as one of the effective methods monitoring the damage of cement-based materials under loading. The associated AE behavior can be used to qualitatively characterize the damage process inside the backfill specimen. In this study, the damage process of FABs was qualitatively characterized by AE technique23, AE results from four groups FABs, namely P0L3, P0L9, P6L3, and P6L9, were selected for analysis.

The damage and deformation processes of FABs specimens were analyzed using two AE parameters including ringing counts and cumulative ringing counts, the relationship between AE parameters and stress development is shown in Fig. 8. As illustrated in the figures, the peak values of ringing counts are closely correlated with the peak stress values, indicating that the specimens underwent damage under load24. However, there were significant differences in the ringing counts and cumulative ringing counts for FAB specimens containing different dosages and lengths of PP fibers. Under the action of load, the incorporation of PP fibers resulted in an increase in the ringing counts and cumulative ringing counts, as shown in Fig. 8. The addition of PP fibers allowed the formation of multiple cracking in the matrix, and the formation of microcracks produced more ringing responses during loading. The presence of PP fibers effectively inhibits the propagation of cracks, which in turn increases cumulative acoustic emission counts. As shown in Fig. 8b,d, the cumulative ringing counts of P6L9 are higher than those of P6L3, which can be attributed to the stronger crack propagation delay properties of P6L9. For PP fibers with a length of 9 mm, the effective anchorage length is much longer than that of 3 mm fiber, thereby enhancing the friction and interface bonding strength of fibers embedding with the matrix. When the interface between the PP fibers and matrix slips or tears under stress, energy is released, resulting in acoustic emission. Thus, the incorporation of PP fibers with a length of 9 mm are more efficient in terms of energy absorption and release, which correspondingly increases the acoustic emission count.

Microstructure characterization

The crucial role of embedded fibers in fiber-reinforced cementitious composites lies in the interface between fibers and the matrix, where the mutual bonding enhances toughness and stands as one of the key determinants of the mechanical performances of composites25. Microscopic images captured by SEM (Fig. 9) reveal various states of fibers embedded in the matrix and the formation of ettringite and C-S-H gels near the bonding interface, as depicted in Fig. 9e,f the observation also manifests the well-bonded interface between PP fibers and the matrix. As the bending load on FABs increases, the embedded PP fibers gradually bear the main load. When the stress on fibers exceeds the interfacial bonding strength between fibers and the matrix, fiber debonding and sliding occur, then fibers continue to bear loads through the frictional resistance, further promoting the deformation capacity until fibers were ultimately fractured or pulled out from the matrix. As illustrated in Fig. 9c, fiber failure is evident under high bending loads. Additionally, the increasing volume fraction of PP fibers, as shown in Fig. 9a,b leads to excessive agglomeration, forming localized weak zones and reducing overall structural stability and compressive strength under the external loads. Following the application of force, fibers are easily pulled out from void defects. With the increase of fiber length, longer fibers (9 mm) start to exhibit parallel alignment, as shown in Fig. 9d.

The SEM microscopic image results of FABs after curing for 28 days.

Enhancement mechanism

The reinforcement mechanism of PP fibers in FABs is a complex and multifaceted process. For PP fibers with various lengths of 3 mm and 9 mm, their reinforcement mechanisms in FABs exhibit slight differences. As depicted in Fig. 10, 3 mm PP fibers can form a bridging effect in FABs. When FABs are subjected to external stresses resulting in microcracks, these short fibers can form effective bridges over cracks, thereby preventing further crack propagation and enhancing the toughness and tensile strength. With the increase in PP fibers volume fraction, PP fibers can bridge more cracks. Additionally, as the PP fibers volume fraction increases, more fibers can interact with each other, forming a denser network structure and strengthening the crack bridging effect, which further enhance the tensile strength and toughness of FABs26. However, when the fiber volume fraction exceeds the optimal value, fiber agglomeration begins to occur, leading to the formation of weak zones and localized stress concentration in FABs during the loading process, resulting in weak planes27. The incorporation of 9 mm-long PP fibers into FABs has been demonstrated to enhance bending performance at low volume fractions, a phenomenon attributed to the bridging effect. However, it is worth noting that long fibers are more prone to agglomerate at higher volume fractions, leading to unevenly distributed bending performance in different directions when FABs are subjected to bending loads. Despite the length of fibers, agglomeration phenomenon is more likely to occur at high volume fractions, which may lead to voids formation or uneven fiber distribution within the matrix of FABs, thus affecting its uniformity and overall performance.

Conclusions

This study focuses on the bending performance, failure behavior, and reinforcement mechanism of FABs by means of DIC AE, and SEM analyses. The key findings of this study can be summarized as follows:

Incorporating PP fibers into the backfill material results in a decrease in compressive strength with the increasing volume fraction. The four-point bending strength initially increases and then decreases with increasing fiber volume fraction. At the optimal fiber volume fraction (0.6%), PP fibers with lengths of 3 mm and 9 mm resulted in a 65.25% and 81.62% increase in the four-point bending strength of FABs, respectively.

Incorporating PP fibers into the backfill material results in a decrease in compressive strength with the increasing volume fraction. The four-point bending strength initially increases and then decreases with increasing fiber volume fraction. At the optimal fiber volume fraction (0.6%), PP fibers with lengths of 3 mm and 9 mm resulted in a 65.25% and 81.62% increase in the four-point bending strength of FABs, respectively.

The introduction of PP fibers increased the acoustic emission response of the backfill material, both in terms of ringing counts and cumulative ringing counts.
