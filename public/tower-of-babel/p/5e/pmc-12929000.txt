Single-Fraction Stereotactic Radiosurgery for Benign Perioptic Meningiomas: A Strategy for Long-Term Tumor Control and Radiation-Induced Optic Neuropathy Avoidance With a 10-Year Follow-up
Authors: Eun Jung Lee, Hyun-Tai Chung, Jin-Wook Kim, Dong Gyu Kim, Sun Ha Paek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLower doses of single-fraction stereotactic radiosurgery (SF-SRS) are typically prescribed for perioptic meningiomas (POMs) due to concerns about radiation-induced optic neuropathy (RION). However, comprehensive long-term follow-up data on both tumor control and RION occurrence are currently lacking. This study endeavored to formulate safe and practical strategies for the implementation of SF-SRS in POMs, utilizing extensive ten-year follow-up data.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed patients who underwent SF-SRS for POMs (< 2 mm from the optic apparatus [OA]) and who were followed up for more than ten years at our institution. Correlations between diverse dosimetric factors irradiated to the tumor and the OA and their respective impacts on tumor control and RION were examined.ResultsThirty patients were included (a median follow-up: 152 months). A dose of 12.7 ± 3.1 Gy was delivered to the gross tumor volume of 4.8 ± 5.1 cm3 with a coverage rate of 76.7 ± 19.1%, yielding a D98% of 7.0 ± 4.7 Gy. The actuarial 5- and 10-year progression-free survival rates were 90% and 70%, respectively (overall tumor control in 57%). Treatment failure occurred in a delayed fashion at 107 ± 55 (20–200) months after stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS). Coverage was associated with long-term tumor control (P = 0.004, cutoff value: 81%). Among patients who retained valid vision, the maximum dose to the OA was 7.2 ± 1.6 Gy (4.8–12.9) Gy, and visual decline attributed to tumor progression manifested in two patients at 116 months and 103 months post-SRS, with no occurrence of RION.ConclusionInadequate tumor coverage near the OA during SF-SRS for POMs, driven by concerns about RION, resulted in a significant incidence of long-term tumor progression and subsequent visual deterioration. Clarifying the optimal dose range is crucial for achieving sustained tumor control and preventing RION following SF-SRS.

INTRODUCTION

Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) is an effective alternative to microsurgery for benign meningiomas; however, its application near the optic apparatus (OA) within 3 mm poses challenges due to the risk of radiation-induced optic neuropathy (RION).123 This forces restrictions on dose to the tumor during SRS, potentially leading to treatment failure. Gamma knife surgery (GKS) offers a steep dose gradient between the target and surrounding normal tissue, minimizing irradiation to normal tissue.4 Nevertheless, in cases where the tumors are in direct contact with the OA, achieving a safe dose gradient becomes arduous. Surgical decompression is the primary approach in such cases, but residual tumors remaining after resection due to the risk posed by intricate surrounding neurovascular structures require SRS.13 Additionally, there is a demand for SRS as a first-line treatment for patients preferring less invasive options or with contraindications for open surgery. Studies have reported that single-fraction stereotactic radiosurgery (SF-SRS) for perioptic meningiomas (POMs) can achieve favourable tumor control with lower radiation doses and acceptable visual outcomes125; however, they often reported only intermediate follow-up data. Our study evaluated long-term outcomes of SF-GKS for POMs using a strict criterion of < 2 mm from the OA, with data collected beyond 10 years. We analyzed factors affecting long-term tumor control and RION to establish strategies for effective SF-SRS to POMs in the era of fractionated SRS.

METHODS

Patients

Among forty-seven patients who underwent GKS for POMs at our institution between January 1998 and April 2010 and for whom the OA was within 2 mm, the data of thirty patients who were followed up for more than ten years were retrospectively analyzed. The patients visited the clinic with brain magnetic resonance (MR) imaging and ophthalmologic examination at 6 months, 1 year, and then biannually after GKS.

GKS

Information on treatment planning and GKS procedures are described in previously published articles.46 As an organ at risk, the right and left OAs were separately contoured, referring to three-dimensional volume isotropic turbo spin–echo acquisition sequence images, from the optic nerve to the optic tract, with the half-optic chiasm included for each side. We determined the prescription dose, accounting for the gross tumor volume (GTV), the distance of the tumor from the OA, and the maximum dose to the optic apparatus (Dmax_OA). When vision remained functional, we prescribed a dose to the tumor to ensure that Dmax_OA remains below 8 Gy as a rule; however, we administered a sufficient dose to the tumor regardless of Dmax_OA when the affected eye was already blind. The prescription doses were usually delivered at the 50% isodose line.

Evaluation of the factors associated with long-term tumor control

MR images of the patients were obtained six months, one year, and then biannually after GKS. Tumor progression was defined as a volume increase greater than 20% compared to the baseline. Tumor volumes were measured at baseline and at each follow-up observation time point using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) to assess the presence or absence of progression. We revised the tumor contour to establish a new GTV in cases where the dural rail and certain tumor portions were initially omitted from the GTV delineation during GKS. Dosimetric parameters were derived from the dose-volume histogram using the newly established GTV. For clarity, the newly defined GTV was denoted as GTV, while the original GTV was designated as GTV′ throughout this study.

Evaluation of the factors associated with RION

Ophthalmological examination was performed in all patients before GKS, 6 months after GKS, and annually thereafter with a median duration of 152 months (range, 120–283 months). RION was defined as either a decrease in visual acuity by ≥ 2 lines in the Snellen visual acuity test or deterioration of visual field defects by ≥ 2 dB in the Swedish interactive thresholding algorithm without evidence of tumor progression. Visual outcomes were evaluated in eyes displaying functional acuity, which was operationally defined as quantifiable acuity by the Snellen vision test prior to GKS. Notably, cases of blindness, light perception, and finger count vision were excluded from the analysis.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), with P < 0.05 indicating significance. Continuous variable differences were assessed using the Mann-Whitney test, while categorical variables utilized the χ2 and Fisher’s exact tests. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses identified factors affecting tumor control and RION, employing stepwise selection (P < 0.05 for entry, P > 0.10 for removal). Cut-off values were determined via maximally selected rank statistics.

Ethics statement

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Seoul National University Hospital waived the requirement for patient consent and approved the study protocol (IRB No. 2101-076-1187).

RESULTS

Tumor characteristics, GKS dosimetry, and long-term tumor control

The follow-up duration was 163 ± 45 months (median, 152; range, 120–283). Clinoidal meningiomas were most common (n = 10, 33.3%), followed by tuberculum sellae (n = 6, 20%), optic nerve sheath (n = 5, 16.7%), cavernous sinus (n = 5), and meningiomas in other locations (n = 4). The GTV was 4.8 ± 5.1 (0.2–22.6) cm3, while the treated target volume was 3.8 ± 4.3 (0.05–19.7) cm3, with some tumor portions near the OA excluded from the prescription isodose volume (PIV), resulting in coverage rates of 76.7 ± 19.1% (17.6–97.9%). The prescription dose to the tumor was 12.7 ± 3.1 (median, 13; range 7–20) Gy, while the Dmax_OA was 9.3 ± 5.6 (4–25.9) Gy. Thirteen tumors (43.3%) showed local progression at 107 ± 55 (median, 103; range 20–200) months post-GKS. The 5-, 10-, and 15-year actuarial progression-free survival (PFS) rates were 90%, 70%, and 43%, respectively (Fig. 1A), with a notable one-third of progression occurring ten years after GKS. Progression was observed in areas outside the radiation field, either through intensional exclusion of tumor portion adjacent to the OA to mitigate RION risk or due to delineation errors. Factors associated with long-term tumor control in POMs

According to the univariate Cox regression analysis, the GTV-GTV′ (P = 0.029), coverage (%) (P = 0.004), Dmin (P = 0.007), D98% (P = 0.008), Dmean (P = 0.035), and Paddick conformity index (P = 0.006) were significantly associated with long-term tumor control (Table 2). In the multivariate Cox regression analysis, coverage (%) was a dosimetric factor associated with long-term tumor control in POMs (hazard ratio [HR], 0.96; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.94–0.99; P = 0.004), with a cut-off value of 81% (HR, 0.10; 95% CI, 0.02–0.40; P = 0.004).

Vision outcomes of GKS for POMs

Eighty percent (n = 24) of the tumors were in contact with either the right or left hemi-OA. Specifically, two tumors abutted the OA, eight compressed or displaced it, and fourteen encased it. In the remaining six patients, the shortest distance between the tumor and the OA was 0.88 ± 0.50 (0.4–1.8). Before GKS, vision was normal in 38 eyes, partially compromised in 11, and blind in 11 due to tumors. Only functioning and relevant eyes were analyzed for vision outcomes, excluding 17 nonfunctioning eyes and 21 eyes with visual pathways ≥ 2 mm from the tumor. A total of 22 eyes from 19 patients were included in the analysis, with 16 eyes having tumors with a 0 mm distance to the OA. The Dmax_OA was 7.2 ± 1.6 (median, 7.3; 4.8–12.9) Gy. The volumes of OAs exposed to doses of ≥ 10 Gy, ≥ 11 Gy, and ≥ 12 Gy were measured as 0.01 cm3, 0.003 cm3, and 0.001 cm3, respectively, in one eye, whereas the others showed no exposure. The most eyes (n = 18, 81.8%) maintained stable vision after GKS, while two (9.1%) improved. Vision declined in two other cases, associated with tumor progression in both patients (Fig. 2). This deterioration became evident at 116 and 103 months post-GKS in patients who received the prescription doses of 8 Gy and 9 Gy, respectively, for tuberculum sellae meningiomas. Notably, no instances of RION were observed.

DISCUSSION

We evaluated the long-term (> 10 years) tumor control efficacy of SF-GKS for benign POMs located within 2 mm from the anterior OA and meticulously analyzed the associated dosimetric factors. With a mean prescription dose of 12.7 Gy, the 5-year PFS rate was 90%. However, this rate decreased significantly to 73% at 10 years and 46% at 15 years. In general, perioptic tumors typically receive lower doses than meningiomas in other locations due to the need to limit radiation near the OA. The mean prescription dose for POMs is around 12 Gy,125 while it exceeds 14 Gy for non-POMs.6789 A reduced radiation dose affects the long-term effectiveness of SRS (Fig. 2). The environment from which tumors originate may influence biological features, such as growth potential and radiation response, with the efficacy of SRS for benign meningiomas varying by location.6 POMs, including tuberculum sellae and cavernous sinus meningiomas, grow slowly and are sensitive to radiation. While studies on skull base meningiomas reported post-SRS 5- and 10-year PFS rates of 92–100% and 83–97%,10111213 respectively, studies specifically on POMs showed rates of 90 to 96% and 69 to 89%, respectively (Table 3).25141516 This underscores that long-term control of POMs post-SRS significantly declines over time. Our study found tumor control rates lower than in previous studies. This could be attributed to a longer median follow-up of 152 months, compared to earlier studies averaging around 60 months.1215 As the follow-up duration increases, treatment failures typically rise. In our study, tumors progressed with a median interval of 107 months post-GKS. A recent multicenter study on SRS for 438 POMs reported a median progression time of 94 months, ranging from 12 to 233 months. Given the inherently slow growth of benign POMs, treatment failures tend to occur in a delayed fashion, emphasizing the importance of using long-term follow-up data to assess the efficacy of SRS for these tumors.

Most studies on SRS for meningiomas have predominantly analyzed the correlation between tumor control and the prescription dose, often using an arbitrarily selected dose. A recent international multicenter study involving 438 POMs reported that a prescription dose of 12 Gy or higher improved tumor control.2 Another study suggested a prescription dose above 12 Gy for POMs with volumes ≥ 10 cm3.5 The prescription dose refers to the theoretical dosage at the prescription isodose line rather than the actual dose received by tumor cells; thus, when coverage is inadequate, a discernible discrepancy between the prescription dose and Dmin may manifest. In our prior study on benign supratentorial nonskull base nonperioptic meningiomas, we found a significant correlation between D98%, the minimal dose received by tumor cells in volumetric units, and long-term tumor control over a decade, indicating that a D98% of ≥ 11 Gy is essential for achieving durable tumor control.6 In our current study, we observed a significant correlation between coverage (%) and long-term control of POMs. Notably, 80% of the tumors in our study exhibited contact with the OA. To mitigate RION risk, we excluded part of the tumor adjacent to the OA from the PIV, thereby aligning with the acceptable dose threshold for the OA (Fig. 3). Therefore, we aimed to administer an effective radiation dose to the larger tumor volume while strategically reducing the dosage near the OA to achieve both treatment efficacy and safety in POMs. This approach differs from administering a reduced prescription dosage with broader coverage. Consequently, we noted considerable variability in coverage (%) among tumors correlating with long-term control. Similar to the prescription dose, the coverage (%) is also theoretical notion. The coverage and prescription dose in isolation do not provide comprehensive information regarding the actual radiation dose received by tumor cells. In contrast, D98% represent the absolute parameter that specifies the minimum dose delivered to the tumor in a volume unit. The disparity between D98% and the prescription dose decreases with higher coverage (%).46 In the current study, D98% ≥ 9 Gy significantly improved PFS (log-rank test, P = 0.017), though it did not emerge as significant in multivariate analysis due to a limited sample size (Fig. 1B). We propose that D98% may serve as a key indicator for effective planning, distinct from coverage alone. In summary, our findings indicate that achieving adequate coverage (95–100% of the GTV) while delivering a prescription dose at or above the minimum required D98% is more effective than employing a high prescription dose with inadequate coverage (i.e., insufficient D98%). Further research is necessary to establish the minimum D98% required for sustained tumor control.

In an international multicenter study on SRS for POMs near the OA, vision deterioration occurred in 10% of patients over a median follow-up of 51 months, with visual decline rates of 9% and 21% at 5 years and 10 years, respectively.2 Tumor progression accounted for 42% of this decline, while the rest was linked to RION, with a Dmax_OA of ≥ 10 Gy identified as a key predictor. Another recent study involving 328 POM cases in direct contact with the OA found a visual decline of 9.6% during a median follow-up of 55 months.1 A total of 41.7% of the decline (4% of the entire cohort) was attributed to tumor progression, while the remaining cases were presumed to be associated with RION. Therefore, it is essential to establish a dose range that balances effective tumor control and RION prevention. Historically, a Dmax_OA of < 8 Gy was recommended17; however, recent studies indicate favorable outcomes with higher Dmax_OA level. Hasegawa et al.18 demonstrated no RION when the Dmax_OA was below 15 Gy in 100 craniopharyngiomas cases, except for one previously treated with radiation therapy. Conversely, Leber et al.19 observed 27% RION occurrences in patients with Dmax_OA between 10 and 15 Gy, while no RION was noted in those with Dmax_OA < 10 Gy. Some studies suggested that a Dmax_OA of 12 Gy could present a risk of RION under 1%.202122 The challenge of determining an appropriate Dmax_OA threshold is ongoing, with 10 Gy frequently advised.24519 Additionally, high-dose irradiated volume of OA may influence RION risk.18 In our study, we noted no RION but identified visual decline in 9.1% of cases, primarily due to tumor progression. This resulted from intentionally avoiding radiation to tumors near the OA due to RION concerns. Of the 22 eyes with valid vision prior to GKS, only one eye received a Dmax_OA of 12.9 Gy, while others received < 10 Gy. Further investigation on acceptable Dmax_OA limits and irradiated volumes remains necessary.

In SRS for POMs, it is crucial to adequately cover the tumor area around the OA with the appropriate dose to ensure effective tumor control while preventing RION. Current literature and our findings suggest that SF-SRS for POMs can be safely and effectively administered, provided that the appropriate dose window is achievable. Specifically, this involves delivering a minimum dose of 11 Gy to the tumor for long-term control while ensuring that the dose to the OA does not exceed 12 Gy to mitigate the risk of RION. However, achieving a suitable dose gradient in a single fraction can be challenging for tumors in direct contact with the OA for both long-term tumor control and visual preservation. Multi-fraction stereotactic radiosurgery (MF-SRS) may be an effective alternative, as it helps balance the doses required for long-term tumor control and RION protection (Table 4),32324 enhancing the radiation tolerance of the OA.252627 A cumulative Dmax_OA ≤ 25 Gy over five fractions is generally considered safe, with less than 1% incidence of RION.21 Delivering 25 Gy in five fractions is approximately equivalent to 12.7 Gy for benign meningiomas, providing durable local control in intricate perioptic tumors.32829 Additionally, fractionated SRS serves as a valuable salvage option for patients with progressive POMs after initial SRS (Fig. 3), promoting visual preservation and tumor decompression. The study retrospectively analyzed data from a single institution with a small sample size. Including only patients with over ten years of follow-up may have introduced selection bias, potentially overrepresenting treatment failures. In contrast, among the 17 patients excluded from this study due to loss to follow-up within 10 years, only one showed tumor progression at 106 months post-GKS, resulting in 5-year, 10-year, and 15-year PFS rates of 93.5%, 76.3%, and 47.6%, respectively, in the overall cohort of patients with POMs (n = 47) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Including patients lost to follow-up early decreased in the treatment failure rate in comparison to the long-term follow-up cohort, from 43.3% (13 of 30) to 29.8% (14 of 47). However, since the primary goal of GKS in benign meningioma is to achieve long-term tumor control rather than focusing on mid-term outcomes, incorporating patients lost to follow-up after a short- to medium-term period may overestimate the treatment effect. Therefore, these findings need to be validated and generalized through further studies that utilize large, prospectively collected data with long-term follow-up of the majority of patients in the entire cohort.

In summary, SF-SRS for POMs exhibited a tendency towards tumor progression over long-term, predominantly attributable to inadequate coverage near the OA driven by RION concerns. Tumor progression near the OA is a primary contributor to visual deterioration, underscoring the need for adequate tumor coverage with an appropriate radiation dose. We recommend hypofractionated SRS for large tumors in direct contact with the OA.
