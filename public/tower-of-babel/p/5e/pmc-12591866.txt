Dual modulation of atherosclerosis by exercise and metformin: convergent pathways, divergent outcomes, and therapeutic potential
Authors: Hongpeng Li, Ziyi Zhang, Manqi Sun, Shousheng Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Atherosclerosis remains a major contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, characterized by endothelial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation. Both exercise and metformin have demonstrated cardiovascular benefits through overlapping molecular mechanisms, notably involving AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activation, mitochondrial biogenesis, anti-inflammatory pathways, and autophagy regulation. This review synthesizes current evidence on how these 2 interventions individually and jointly modulate vascular remodeling and atherogenesis. We critically examine their synergistic effects and potential conflicts, particularly regarding AMPK signaling intensity, tissue-specific responsiveness, and the influence of intervention timing, dose, and host metabolic state. We also explore how exercise and metformin interact dynamically across key molecular networks, including the M3–calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase kinase beta–AMPK axis and downstream effectors such as sirtuin 1 and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha. While emerging data suggest potential benefits from the combined intervention in attenuating vascular aging and plaque formation, evidence remains mixed, and context-dependent responses are increasingly recognized. This review highlights the need for individualized intervention strategies and proposes mechanistic models to guide future research. Overall, a deeper understanding of the dynamic crosstalk between exercise and metformin may enhance the development of personalized therapies for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.

INTRODUCTION

Atherosclerosis (AS) remains the principal pathological substrate underlying cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases and accounts for a major share of global morbidity and mortality [12]. Once regarded as an irreversible degenerative process, AS is now recognized as a dynamic immunometabolic disease, driven by lipid dysregulation and chronic low-grade inflammation [1].

Recent insights have elucidated a multifaceted pathophysiology involving foam cell formation, macrophage polarization imbalance, endothelial dysfunction, and vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) phenotypic switching [3456]. Although statins have substantially reduced low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, residual cardiovascular risk persists, underscoring the need for more comprehensive therapeutic paradigms [7].

Within this broader framework, lifestyle interventions—especially regular exercise—have emerged as potent modulators of atherosclerotic progression. Evidence from both preclinical and clinical studies shows that aerobic training can attenuate plaque growth, enhance plaque stability, and reduce cardiovascular event rates [89].

Concurrently, the antidiabetic agent metformin (MET), long prescribed for glycemic control, has gained attention for its pleiotropic cardiovascular benefits. Beyond its insulin-sensitizing effects, MET has been associated with improved endothelial function, reduced oxidative stress, and anti-inflammatory modulation, rendering it a candidate for repurposing in cardiometabolic disease [10]. However, a growing body of literature has uncovered a striking paradox: in certain populations, particularly metabolically healthy individuals, MET may blunt the beneficial effects of exercise on mitochondrial biogenesis, maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max) improvement, and skeletal muscle remodeling [1112]. This so-called synergy paradox challenges conventional assumptions about the additive benefits of drug-exercise co-intervention and points toward an underlying complexity in the temporal, spatial, and mechanistic integration of these 2 modalities.

This review explores 3 key questions at the intersection of pharmacology and physiology:

1) Do exercise and MET engage complementary or competing molecular pathways in the reversal of AS?

2) Is the observed paradox contingent upon specific host metabolic states, intervention timing, or dose thresholds?

3) Can we redefine individualized co-intervention strategies based on mechanistic alignment rather than additive logic?

By synthesizing findings from mechanistic and interventional studies, we propose a conceptual framework for decoding the paradox of exercise–MET synergy. We further discuss its translational implications for tailoring integrated therapeutic strategies against AS.

CONVERGENT PATHWAYS AND DIVERGENT OUTCOMES: THE AMPK AXIS AS A THERAPEUTIC NEXUS

Convergence on AMPK: a shared metabolic switch with distinct inputs

5′-AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is a central regulator of cellular energy homeostasis. Upon energy depletion, elevated adenosine monophosphate/adenosine triphosphate (AMP/ATP) ratios activate AMPK, which in turn initiates downstream processes such as mitochondrial biogenesis, autophagy, lipid oxidation, and suppression of inflammation and mTOR signaling [131415].

Both exercise and MET converge on AMPK as a central signaling hub [1920]. Exercise induces AMPK activation primarily through elevated mechanical and metabolic demand. In skeletal muscle, cardiac tissue, and vascular endothelium, increases in intracellular AMP and Ca2+ during contraction drive AMPK phosphorylation, leading to enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis, nitric oxide (NO) synthesis, and lipid catabolism [2122].

By contrast, MET activates AMPK via a mechanistically distinct route. The predominant hypothesis suggests that MET inhibits mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I, lowering ATP production and secondarily raising AMP levels [232425], In addition, some studies have identified a more direct mechanism in which hepatic AMPK is activated through liver kinase B1 (LKB1)-mediated phosphorylation of the AMPK α-subunit [232425].

Despite this shared endpoint, the 2 interventions diverge markedly in their initiating stimuli, tissue specificity, and metabolic consequences. Exercise-induced AMPK activation is acute and peripheral, favoring adaptations in muscle and vasculature. In contrast, MET’s effects are more chronic and centralized, targeting hepatic and intestinal tissues [26]. These mechanistic disparities define a double-edged relationship: on the one hand, they create opportunities for pathway complementarity in combined interventions; on the other, they pose the risk of signal interference, especially when timing and tissue targeting are misaligned. This duality provides a molecular rationale for the observed inconsistencies in exercise–MET synergy.

Anti-inflammatory convergence: disrupting the self-perpetuating cycle

Chronic vascular inflammation is now recognized as a central driver of AS progression [27]. Classical risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and smoking initiate endothelial injury, leading to the generation of ROS and pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α. These mediators activate the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) pathway [28], upregulating death receptors including Fas and TNF receptor 1, thereby promoting mitochondrial and DNA damage, and triggering apoptosis through caspase-9-dependent mechanisms [529], This cascade amplifies the inflammatory milieu, perpetuating endothelial dysfunction and advancing lesion formation [3031]. A feed-forward loop ensues, where ROS, cytokines, foam cells [32], and infiltrating immune cells create a self-sustaining, pro-atherogenic microenvironment [3334]. AMPK activation provides a multifaceted countermeasure to this loop. Exercise-induced AMPK signaling enhances endothelial NO synthase (eNOS) phosphorylation and NO bioavailability [2122], improves endothelial function, and suppresses expression of adhesion molecules such as VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 [3536], thereby attenuating monocyte adhesion and transmigration.

MET, beyond its AMPK-dependent effects, exerts additional anti-inflammatory actions via AMPK-independent mechanisms. These include modulation of intracellular calcium fluxes through calcium release-activated channels, resulting in suppression of IL-6 production [337]; and inhibition of mitochondrial complex I to reduce ROS generation [3]. Concurrently, MET downregulates NF-κB and inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome assembly, leading to decreased IL-1β and TNF-α release [38]. While both interventions target overlapping inflammatory nodes, their distinct molecular mechanisms offer potential for synergy—particularly when dosing and timing are optimized to avoid pathway redundancy or interference. The convergence on inflammation thus represents a promising axis for combined atheroprotection.

Suppressing foam cell formation and restoring autophagy: dual clearance of lipid and organelle burden

Foam cell formation—an early yet persistent feature of atherosclerotic lesions—arises from chronic inflammatory stimulation and dysregulated macrophage plasticity. Under sustained exposure to oxLDL, macrophages undergo M1 polarization driven by activation of TLR4/6 and CD40/CD40L signaling. This pro-inflammatory phenotype increases the release of ROS, IL-1, and IL-6, which collectively exacerbate endothelial cell apoptosis and upregulate adhesion molecules such as VCAM-1 and ICAM-1. These changes, in turn, enhance monocyte recruitment and promote the transition toward foam cell formation (Fig. 1). A 2021 study by Seneviratne et al. [16] showed that MET attenuated AS in normoglycemic mice by activating the AMPK–ATF1–M2 pathway in macrophages, “This effect was dependent on AMPK signaling in hematopoietic cells, highlighting the tissue-specific nature of MET action and the concept of AMPK compartmentalization.” In advanced plaques, many foam cells are derived from VSMCs, which exhibit intrinsic defects in autophagy that impair cholesterol ester clearance [39], AMPK reactivates the Beclin1–LC3 autophagy pathway, enhancing lipid degradation and promoting reverse cholesterol transport to HDL [1718].

Exercise training has also been shown to induce selective autophagy in endothelial cells and mitochondria, preserving cardiovascular cellular homeostasis. MET, similarly, stimulates autophagy in foam cells—especially those of VSMC origin—promoting lipid clearance and lesion regression [40]. These processes converge on the AMPK–ULK1 signaling axis, forming a coordinated “clearance pathway” that mitigates advanced plaque pathology and may serve as a mechanistic fulcrum for lesion reversal [4142].

Anti-aging synergy: preserving endothelial function through metabolic rejuvenation

Aging is an irreversible biological process characterized by progressive loss of cellular proliferative capacity, accompanied by structural, functional, and metabolic deterioration [43], Vascular aging—manifesting as endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and arterial stiffness—is increasingly recognized as a fundamental contributor to atherosclerotic disease progression [44].

At the cellular level, endothelial senescence involves hallmark features such as telomere attrition, downregulation of sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), upregulation of cell cycle inhibitors (p16^INK4a^ and p21^CIP1^), and widespread epigenetic drift [45]. AMPK acts as a negative regulator of these aging signatures by activating the SIRT1–peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) axis, inhibiting mTOR signaling, improving mitochondrial function, and promoting DNA repair mechanisms [4647].

Exercise confers anti-senescence effects by activating Nrf2-dependent antioxidant pathways [48], enhancing telomerase activity, and mobilizing vascular progenitor cells, thereby restoring endothelial regenerative capacity. In parallel, MET exerts pleiotropic anti-aging actions by modulating telomeric repeat-containing RNA (TERRA) transcription, attenuating the accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), and upregulating ATM—a key DNA damage response protein [4950]. Together, exercise and MET converge on the AMPK–SIRT1–mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) axis to form a dual-pronged strategy for vascular rejuvenation. This coordinated targeting of molecular aging pathways provides a mechanistic rationale for their combined application in attenuating endothelial decline and delaying the onset of atherosclerotic events.

In the AMPK–inflammation axis, AMPK inhibits NF-κB activity, enhances eNOS phosphorylation, and upregulates nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (NRF2) [48], thereby reducing NLRP3 inflammasome activation, ROS production, and pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) [337]. These anti-inflammatory effects help attenuate endothelial injury and limit plaque development.

In the AMPK–autophagy axis, AMPK promotes autophagic clearance and mitochondrial turnover in foam cells by activating Beclin-1 and LC3, while concurrently inhibiting mTOR signaling. This enhances vascular cell homeostasis and supports plaque regression through improved lysosomal function and lipid efflux.

In the AMPK–aging axis, AMPK signaling reduces advanced glycation end-product accumulation, upregulates TERRA [4647], and activates the ATM–NRF2–DNA repair cascade. These responses help maintain chromosomal stability and endothelial integrity, delaying vascular senescence [40].

Exercise stimulates AMPK primarily via increased ATP consumption and elevated AMP/ATP ratio in skeletal muscle. In contrast, MET activates AMPK through mitochondrial complex I inhibition and modulation of intracellular calcium via CRAC channels, with cellular entry mediated by organic cation transporter 1 (OCT1). Together, these inputs form a coordinated but non-identical AMPK activation landscape [1718].

By integrating complementary but temporally distinct signals, exercise and MET jointly modulate glucose metabolism, lipid homeostasis, and vascular remodeling—contributing to glycemic control, lipid lowering, plaque stabilization, and delayed vascular aging.

CONVERGING BENEFITS: INDEPENDENT EFFICACY OF EXERCISE AND MET IN CARDIOVASCULAR PROTECTION

Mechanistic overlaps between exercise and MET, particularly through AMPK activation, support the rationale for their combined use in AS (Fig. 2). Yet clinical findings have not uniformly confirmed this synergy. Some studies report that MET may blunt key exercise-induced adaptations—such as improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness and mitochondrial biogenesis—especially in metabolically healthy individuals [11]. This paradox challenges the translational generalizability of their joint application.

Independent efficacy: robust evidence supporting exercise and MET across cardiometabolic domains

A substantial body of evidence supports the independent cardioprotective effects of both exercise and MET. For the general population, the benefits of exercise stem not only from its physiological enhancements but also from its ability to counteract the deleterious effects of sedentary behavior—a leading global risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and all-cause mortality [89], Sedentary time, particularly screen-based and deskbound activities expending less than 1.5 metabolic equivalent of tasks, is independently associated with increased cardiovascular and overall mortality, regardless of physical activity levels [5152535455]. Reducing sedentary time is thus a foundational priority in CVD prevention [565758]. Notably, Ornish et al. [59] demonstrated as early as 1990 that comprehensive lifestyle modification—including structured exercise—could partially reverse atherosclerotic plaque burden within a year. Beyond structural regression, habitual physical activity improves VO2max, enhances insulin sensitivity, increases vascular compliance, delays arterial calcification, and improves endothelial function—benefits observed across diverse populations.

MET, long recognized for its glycemic benefits, has also demonstrated cardiovascular protection independent of glucose control. Early studies showed that MET therapy was associated with significantly lower cardiovascular mortality in patients with type 2 diabetes compared to sulfonylureas or insulin [6061]. More recent trials have expanded its relevance to non-diabetic individuals at heightened cardiovascular risk. MET has been shown to improve metabolic profiles, reduce systemic inflammation, and preserve cognitive function in obese and prediabetic populations [626364]. A large NHANES-based analysis further reported that MET use was associated with improved cardiovascular outcomes in specific subgroups, including younger, married, and alcohol-consuming individuals [65].

Together, these findings make a compelling case for the broader application of MET beyond glycemic control. Mechanistically, its combination with exercise appears rational for the reversal of AS, for several reasons:

1) Tissue-target complementarity: MET primarily targets hepatic and intestinal metabolism, whereas exercise exerts systemic effects, particularly on skeletal muscle and the vasculature.

2) Shared AMPK axis: both activate AMPK to regulate inflammation, autophagy, and vascular aging—albeit via distinct upstream mechanisms.

3) Multi-level synergy: both interventions modulate inflammation, lipid clearance, and vascular remodeling through partially overlapping mechanisms.

4) Systemic metabolic reprogramming: each modulates whole-body energy balance, offering coordinated support for vascular and metabolic health.

Given these mechanistic synergies, the combined use of MET and exercise has been proposed as a “complementary strategy,” particularly relevant for individuals in the "cardiometabolic gray zone"—those at elevated risk who do not yet meet pharmacological treatment thresholds.

Contradictory evidence: is the benefit of exercise blunted by MET co-administration?

Despite compelling mechanistic overlaps, emerging human studies have raised concerns that MET may attenuate key physiological adaptations to exercise, particularly in insulin-resistant individuals. In a landmark study by Sharoff et al. [11] in 2010, it was shown that the addition of MET to aerobic training blunted the improvement in insulin sensitivity among non-diabetic individuals with insulin resistance. Subsequent work by Malin et al. [66] confirmed that MET significantly diminished the gains in VO2max induced by aerobic exercise. Later trials extended these observations: in a 12-week intervention involving prediabetic adults, both MET and exercise independently improved insulin sensitivity, yet their combination offered no additive benefit and potentially obscured the full metabolic impact of training [67]. These findings were independently reproduced by Konopka et al. [68], further corroborating the antagonistic interaction between MET and exercise-induced adaptations.

Mechanistically, this paradox may be rooted in mitochondrial interference. MET exerts its antihyperglycemic effect in part by inhibiting complex I of the mitochondrial electron transport chain—specifically the NADH-to-CoQ10 electron transfer [6970]. While effective for glycemic control, this mechanism may counteract the mitochondrial biogenesis and respiratory enhancements typically stimulated by endurance training. As exercise draws heavily on mitochondrial reserve capacity, particularly in large muscle groups, complex I inhibition may constrain this adaptation. This suppression has been linked to small but significant reductions in VO2max among MET users undergoing exercise programs [12]. Further stratified analyses reveal that this inhibitory effect is not uniform. In a human study, individuals with lower fasting glucose and higher baseline complex I activity experienced greater blunting of insulin sensitivity gains when MET was co-administered [68]. The MASTERS trial similarly reported attenuated hypertrophic and strength responses to resistance training among older adults receiving MET, including reductions in muscle cross-sectional area and lean mass accrual [71]. These converging lines of evidence frame a core paradox: while MET and exercise share upstream targets such as AMPK, their downstream effects may diverge or even conflict when co-administered. This paradox—where upstream synergy masks downstream antagonism—poses a challenge to clinical translation and underscores the need to optimize timing, dosage, and patient selection.

Sources of discrepancy: the intertwined modulation of dose, intensity, and individual variability

Exercise dose and intensity as pivotal—but controversial—modifiers

Among the many variables modulating the interaction between exercise and MET, exercise intensity and dosage emerge as the most contentious and consequential. Although regular physical activity is widely recognized as protective against CVD, the dose-response curve is far from linear, and the definition of “optimal” remains elusive. Even adherence to the World Health Organization’s minimal physical activity guidelines—150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week—may fail to fully counteract the detrimental effects of sedentary behavior [72]. Recent data suggest that a higher daily activity dose (30–40 minutes/day) may be necessary to confer robust cardioprotection [73].

Yet paradoxically, several large-scale studies have reported that individuals engaging in long-term endurance exercise exhibit a higher prevalence and burden of coronary AS, as reflected in elevated coronary artery calcium (CAC) scores [747576]. Endurance athletes consistently show higher CAC scores compared to the general population [747778]. This effect is particularly pronounced in older athletes, where the detection rate of atherosclerotic plaques and overall CAC burden are significantly elevated [79]. The underlying mechanisms remain speculative but may involve exaggerated mechanical stress, repetitive inflammatory stimuli, elevated ROS, and increased parathyroid hormone levels during intense exertion [80], Fortunately, the majority of these "athlete plaques" are highly calcified and morphologically stable, as opposed to rupture-prone mixed or lipid-rich plaques [81]. Consequently, while anatomical indicators suggest an increased plaque load, clinical outcomes do not reflect a corresponding rise in cardiovascular events [8283]. Indeed, the apparent paradox is resolved by recognizing that regular high-volume exercise likely improves vascular function and systemic metabolism, mitigating the risk posed by stable calcified plaques. A meta-analysis of 35,920 athletes reported a 27% reduction in all-cause mortality compared to the general population [848586]. Notably, even individuals engaging in extreme activity levels—up to 5,000 metabolic equivalent of task·minute/week—did not exhibit increased cardiovascular risk [87], Rather, high-volume activity remained associated with lower cardiovascular mortality. Thus, current guidelines emphasize progressive, regular training over unsupervised high-intensity regimens to avoid potential maladaptations [88]. Collectively, these findings underscore a U- or J-shaped relationship between exercise intensity and cardiovascular health. This nonlinearity profoundly complicates efforts to evaluate drug–exercise interactions. In particular, it suggests that exercise dosage can fundamentally reshape the physiological context in which MET exerts its effects—potentially shifting synergy toward interference when intensity thresholds are exceeded. Any attempt to translate combined interventions into clinical protocols must therefore account for exercise intensity not as a background variable, but as a central mechanistic determinant (Table 1).

From synergy to interference: exercise intensity dictates the direction of interaction

Beyond determining health benefits, exercise intensity also critically modulates how MET exerts its metabolic effects. In a 2018 study, Das et al. [95] argued that prior findings of MET-induced reductions in VO2max may have overlooked an essential detail: such interference manifests predominantly at high or near-maximal exercise intensities, with little to no effect observed under moderate or low training loads. A synthesis of recent animal and clinical trials (summarized in Table 1) strongly supports this dose–response interaction model.

Multiple studies have shown that under low-to-moderate intensity training, exercise and MET can act synergistically. In diabetic and atherosclerotic mouse models, Yu et al. [93] and Zhao et al. [94] reported enhanced upregulation of glucose transporter type 4 (GLUT4), SIRT1, and PGC-1α in combination therapy groups, with greater improvements in glucose metabolism than with either intervention alone. Similarly, Fitch et al. [90] demonstrated that lifestyle modification combined with MET significantly improved insulin resistance in human immunodeficiency virus-associated metabolic syndrome, suggesting a mutually reinforcing effect in moderately trained populations.

However, under high-intensity protocols—such as HIIT or prolonged endurance training—this synergy may shift toward interference. Malin et al. [66] and Linden et al. [96] found that in individuals with impaired glucose tolerance or prediabetic obesity, MET not only failed to amplify the benefits of exercise but also blunted mitochondrial adaptations in skeletal muscle. These inhibitory effects included downregulation of PGC-1α, suppression of oxidative phosphorylation genes, and reduced gains in VO2max. Likewise, Alivaisi et al. [89] reported that despite upstream AMPK activation, downstream adaptations—such as GLUT4 expression—were paradoxically impaired in diabetic models receiving high-intensity exercise plus MET, suggesting that pathway saturation or desynchronization may blunt expected benefits. Notably, findings under moderate intensity training remain contradictory. For example, Chen et al. [65] observed sustained synergy between exercise and MET, while Malin et al. [66] reported mitochondrial suppression under similar training conditions. This inconsistency hints at a hidden moderator behind intensity itself—namely, the metabolic state of the individual.

Relative intensity: individual capacity shapes the boundary between synergy and interference

The core issue behind these discrepancies may not lie in the intervention per se, but in how the same absolute exercise intensity is perceived differently across individuals. In people with impaired baseline metabolism or low cardiopulmonary fitness—such as those with diabetes, obesity, or aging-related decline—moderate-intensity training (e.g., 60% VO2max) may represent a disproportionately high physiological stress. In these contexts, cellular pathways typically implicated in synergy—such as mitochondrial biogenesis and inflammatory regulation—may become overstimulated, triggering compensatory suppression rather than adaptive remodeling.

In other words, physiological load is not absolute, but relative. The body’s response to training depends not only on what is prescribed but on who receives it. The mitochondrial impairments observed by Malin et al. [66] and Linden et al. [96] may reflect not inherently excessive training loads, but training that exceeded the participants’ adaptive thresholds. Conversely, Clarson et al. [91] found sustained synergistic effects with long-term intervention, likely because improved baseline fitness shifted training intensity back into a physiological “sweet spot.”

This concept of “relative intensity threshold” helps explain the heterogeneity across studies. Only when exercise load aligns with an individual’s metabolic capacity can synergy with MET be preserved. If the threshold is exceeded, pathway interference mediated by signal overload, desynchronization, or compensatory suppression—becomes more likely.

Therefore, the outcome of combined interventions is not determined by exercise intensity alone, but by its dynamic interaction with the individual's physiological context. The shift from synergy to interference is not binary, but a continuum shaped by relative load. Future studies must integrate this dimension as a core analytic and modeling parameter. Both mechanistic and translational research should adopt frameworks that dynamically couple intervention dose with baseline physiological status to prevent maladaptive responses and optimize combined efficacy.

MECHANISTIC DECONSTRUCTION: DIVERGENT PATHWAYS UNDERLYING SYNERGY AND ANTAGONISM

Timing and individual context: a dual-axis threshold for synergistic activation

The heterogeneous outcomes observed in exercise–MET co-intervention studies are often attributed to differences in drug dosage or training intensity. However, deeper analysis suggests that the outcomes are governed by the interplay of intervention timing and individual metabolic state—together defining a personalized synergy threshold.

Exercise induces a rapid, high-amplitude activation of AMPK via acute ATP depletion and Ca2+ signaling, peaking during the later phases of physical exertion. This transient surge triggers PGC-1α upregulation, mitochondrial biogenesis, and lipid oxidation [212266]. By contrast, MET initiates a slower, more sustained AMPK activation through complex I inhibition and is centered primarily in hepatic tissues [232425]. These kinetic and spatial mismatches imply that concurrent administration may cause signal saturation or negative feedback, dampening training-induced adaptations.

In older adults, Konopka et al. observed that MET blunted the increase in mitochondrial respiration and PGC-1α expression following exercise [71]. This supports the feedback inhibition hypothesis: premature AMPK activation may trigger suppressive regulatory loops that limit subsequent remodeling that limit downstream remodeling. Accordingly, staggered protocols—exercise-first or time-separated delivery—have been proposed to preserve synergy [91].

On the individual level, the same absolute training load can represent drastically different physiological stimuli. In metabolically impaired individuals, moderate-intensity exercise may evoke high relative stress, mimicking high-intensity effects. Malin et al. [66] found that in such individuals, MET suppressed exercise-induced improvements in hepatic fat reduction and insulin sensitivity, an effect not seen in metabolically healthy counterparts [12]. This suggests that the metabolic state dictates whether MET amplifies or interferes with exercise adaptations. Crucially, the interaction follows a threshold model: below a personalized threshold, MET acts as a metabolic enhancer; above it, it functions as a buffer, flattening stress signals and hindering adaptation. This phenomenon has been repeatedly documented across animal and human studies [899697].

Thus, temporal misalignment and metabolic mismatch jointly determine the trajectory of combined intervention. While current mechanistic insights—mainly derived from animal models and cellular signaling analyses—support the relevance of timing, the translational implications remain uncertain. Further pharmacological studies, including those addressing drug metabolism, pharmacokinetics, and individualized response dynamics in humans, are needed to clarify how timing and dosage influence clinical outcomes.

Divergent AMPK activation: shared target, split outcomes

Although AMPK is a shared molecular hub for both interventions, emerging data show that simultaneous activation can yield opposite physiological effects. This divergence stems from the distinct upstream triggers and tissue distributions of AMPK activation.

Exercise activates AMPK via rapid ATP consumption and Ca2+ influx, with LKB1 and CaMKKβ driving phosphorylation in skeletal muscle. The resulting cascade boosts PGC-1α, mitochondrial remodeling, and systemic metabolic flexibility [6697], These responses are temporally pulsatile and spatially localized, particularly in muscle and myocardium.

MET, however, activates AMPK through a subtler route: mild inhibition of complex I decreases ATP synthesis, indirectly elevating AMP levels. In the liver, AMPK is also phosphorylated via LKB1 upon MET uptake through OCT1 transporters [232425]. This slow, hepatic-centric activation prioritizes homeostatic regulation over structural remodeling.

Thus, although the 2 interventions converge on AMPK, they differ in signal initiation, temporal rhythm, tissue specificity, and downstream outcomes. Exercise promotes dynamic remodeling; MET maintains metabolic steadiness. Without synchronization, their effects may counteract each other. When co-administered without synchrony, their effects may conflict rather than complement.

This conflict is not intrinsic to AMPK but arises from upstream and downstream discord. For example, exercise-induced PGC-1α expression depends on ROS bursts and NAD+/NADH oscillations, both of which are attenuated by MET’s suppression of complex I and ROS generation [2324254647]. key mitochondrial biogenesis pathways may be disrupted, hindering mitochondrial adaptation [667196].

Tissue-specific isoforms of AMPK further compound this divergence. In muscle, AMPK-α2 is critical for enhancing VO2max and lipid oxidation, yet MET has minimal effect on this isoform and may even blunt its activation via energy buffering [6696].

In sum, AMPK is not a monolithic synergy switch, but a context-sensitive signaling node. True synergy emerges only when activation mode, tissue target, and feedback balance are aligned.

Tissue-specific partitioning: muscle interference vs. hepatic support

The paradox of exercise–MET co-intervention is further complicated by organ-level divergences. While AMPK is a common node, its physiological effects are highly tissue-dependent.

In skeletal muscle, exercise-induced AMPK activation enhances mitochondrial capacity, fatty acid oxidation, and glucose uptake—critical for VO2max improvement and metabolic remodeling [66]. Yet MET has been shown to blunt these responses. Malin et al. [66] and Linden et al. [96] independently reported that combined therapy reduced PGC-1α expression and VO2max gains in prediabetic populations. Konopka found similar impairments in mitochondrial respiration and lean mass accrual among older adults [1271]. These findings suggest that MET’s role as an energetic buffer may dull the adaptive stress signals necessary for muscular plasticity.

In contrast, hepatic responses to MET are more uniformly beneficial. The drug suppresses gluconeogenesis, inhibits lipogenesis, and enhances insulin signaling—all effects complementary to those of exercise [26]. AMPK activation in the liver reduces sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1c activity and 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase expression, limiting hepatic lipid accumulation and improving glycemic control [171840]. While exercise also supports hepatic function, its effects are less pronounced than MET’s in this context.

These tissue-specific contrasts result in a functional decoupling: the same co-intervention can yield hepatic synergy and muscular antagonism. This dichotomy explains why some studies observe improved lipid profiles or plaque regression, yet diminished cardiorespiratory outcomes.

Moreover, the divergence extends to other metabolic tissues. In adipose tissue, some evidence suggests additive benefits in reducing visceral fat and inflammatory markers [91], In vascular endothelium, however, benefits hinge on sustained NO availability via eNOS activation—a process driven more reliably by exercise than by MET [3536].

Recognizing these differential effects is key to optimizing intervention strategies. MET may benefit individuals with hepatic insulin resistance but hinder those seeking muscular or aerobic enhancements. Tissue-specific targeting and outcome alignment are essential to avoid one-size-fits-all pitfalls.

Reframing combination therapy: from linear addition to dynamic coupling

Mechanistic inspection reveals a central truth: synergy and interference are not opposing outcomes, but conditional possibilities governed by context. Combined exercise–MET interventions do not follow linear or additive logic. Their effects reflect a nonlinear, dynamic interplay shaped by time, intensity, individual physiology, and tissue response.

As discussed, AMPK activation can emerge through oscillatory stress (exercise) or metabolic damping (MET). These modes differ in rhythm, magnitude, and anatomical focus. When synchronized, they can reinforce systemic adaptation. When misaligned, they may induce pathway saturation or signal inversion, particularly in high-load conditions [6696].

The "energy-buffering hypothesis" posits that MET tempers the acute energy fluctuations and ROS signals critical for exercise-induced remodeling. While this may support metabolic stability in low-stress settings, it compromises adaptive signaling under intense exertion [97].

Hence, synergy is not guaranteed but must be engineered. Effective combination therapy demands recognition of mechanistic plasticity and strategic calibration across 4 axes:

Temporal alignment: staggered administration avoids signal conflict;

Load matching: training intensity must fit metabolic capacity;

Tissue targeting: interventions should align with intended organ-level outcomes; Physiological profiling: baseline health determines benefit vs. interference.

Rather than asking whether MET and exercise synergize, the critical question becomes: under what conditions, for whom, and in what configuration? This paradigm shift moves beyond additive logic toward individualized, context-responsive intervention design, offering a path to unlock their full therapeutic potential.

Patients with impaired metabolism and low exercise capacity

1) Profile

VO2max < 20–25 mL·kg−1·min−1; HbA1c > 6.5%; hepatic/systemic metabolic stress common in atherosclerotic multimorbidity. Exercise improves vascular function yet may be constrained by achievable intensity/volume; MET can complement via hepatic AMPK activation, suppression of gluconeogenesis, and anti-inflammatory signaling.

2) Testable hypothesis

Compared with pre-exercise dosing, MET taken ≥ 2–3 hours after exercise preserves skeletal-muscle mitochondrial/training adaptations while maintaining glycemic benefit and improving vascular surrogates.

3) Pragmatic protocol cues

Training: moderate continuous exercise 3–5×/week at 60–70% VO2reserve (≈60–70% heart rate reserve [HRR]); gradual volume progression.

Dosing: use clinician-directed dosing per label/guidelines, titrated to renal/hepatic function and tolerability; when feasible, maintain a ≥ 12–24 hours pre-exercise washout to protect muscle signaling; record exercise → dose and dose → next-exercise intervals and total daily dose actually used.

Endpoints: primary—carotid intima-media thickness (IMT) or magnetic resonance imaging-based plaque composition; secondary—VO2peak, heart rate variability (HRV), flow-mediated dilation (FMD), carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV), lipid subfractions, inflammatory markers, and AMPK–PGC-1α surrogates.

Note. Combination therapy is reasonable when liver function and drug tolerance are acceptable; timing is prioritized to preserve training responsiveness.

Prediabetic patients with intact cardiorespiratory function

1) Profile

HbA1c 5.7–6.4%; VO2max ≥ 30 mL·kg−1·min−1; early/subclinical AS. Moderate exercise alone often improves glycemic and endothelial function; peri-exercise MET may blunt mitochondrial adaptations if dosed too close to workouts.

2) Testable hypothesis

Additive benefits emerge when MET is separated from exercise sessions (post-exercise or on non-training days), whereas immediate pre-exercise dosing attenuates gains in VO2peak and mitochondrial markers.

3) Pragmatic protocol cues

Training: moderate-to-vigorous continuous or interval programs targeted to the putative “synergy window” of 60–75% VO2reserve (≥ 75% for vigorous bouts); report weekly time in zone.

Design features: pre-specify subgrouping by homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance, age, and baseline fitness; test treatment × intensity × timing interactions.

Endpoints: co-primary—endothelial function (FMD) and plaque composition; secondary—VO2peak, cfPWV, lipid/inflammatory panels, and transcriptomic readouts (AMPK–PGC-1α gene sets).

Note. Net interaction is timing- and intensity-dependent; explicit reporting of exercise→dose and dose→next-exercise intervals is recommended.

Physiologically healthy individuals aiming for performance optimization

1) Profile

Normal metabolism; VO2max > 40 mL·kg−1·min−1; prevention-oriented participation with performance rather than metabolic correction as the primary goal. In metabolically healthy or athletic contexts, indiscriminate addition of MET has been associated with smaller VO2max gains and muted muscle adaptations.

2) Testable hypothesis

Withholding MET around key training days (≥ 24 hours washout) produces superior improvements in VO2peak, muscle oxidative capacity, and performance compared with routine peri-exercise dosing.

3) Pragmatic protocol cues

Training: periodized programs prioritizing exercise-first strategies; if MET is required for other indications, separate dosing from high-quality training sessions or cycle usage; dosing remains clinician-guided with documentation of actual total daily dose and timing relative to sessions.

Endpoints: performance (VO2peak, power/velocity thresholds), muscle-adaptation surrogates (e.g., blood-based p-AMPK/PGC-1α signatures where feasible), and vascular surrogates (FMD/cfPWV).

Note. When no metabolic dysfunction is present, routine MET is unlikely to add benefit and may dampen training effects.

MET-intolerant or hepatically compromised patients

1) Profile

Liver insufficiency, alcohol-related liver disease, or intolerance to MET. Pharmacological AMPK activation may be contraindicated; structured exercise retains anti-inflammatory, endothelial, and mitochondrial benefits.

2) Testable hypothesis

In patients unsuitable for MET, a periodized program (moderate base with progressive intervals) yields vascular and mitochondrial improvements comparable to combination therapy in tolerant patients matched for baseline risk.

3) Pragmatic protocol cues

Training: emphasize moderate-to-vigorous exercise within safety limits; monitor symptoms and workload progression; standardize intensity with %VO2reserve/%HRR and weekly time in zone.

Safety/adhesion: track adherence, adverse events (particularly gastrointestinal intolerance), and liver panels; when MET is used in comparator cohorts, document guideline-based dosing and timing to permit indirect comparison.

Endpoints: use the same vascular imaging outcomes (carotid IMT, plaque composition) plus HRV/FMD/cfPWV to maintain comparability.

Note. Exercise remains the primary modality when MET is contraindicated; standardized reporting improves reproducibility and cross-study synthesis.

CONCLUSION

Exercise and MET each offer unique mechanisms to reverse AS: the former via hemodynamic stress and muscular adaptation, the latter through hepatic AMPK signaling and metabolic homeostasis. In theory, their convergence on shared molecular targets suggests a synergistic paradigm. Yet clinical observations reveal a paradox: MET often blunts the very mitochondrial and cardiorespiratory benefits that exercise confers.

This review proposes that such contradictions stem not from mechanistic incompatibility, but from mismatch across temporal, tissue, and individual dimensions. We identify 3 mechanistic axes for rethinking synergy:

1) Rhythmic resonance: effective co-intervention requires alignment between the acute stress-induced signaling of exercise and the chronic stabilizing effects of MET;

2) Pathway redundancy and saturation: overlapping activation of AMPK–macrophage polarization–cholesterol efflux axes may trigger compensatory dampening;

3) Organ-specific timing mismatches: muscle and liver respond to stimuli on different temporal scales; simultaneous intervention may blunt net gain.

From these insights, we advocate a dynamic, stratified framework for future implementation:

In performance-oriented or metabolically healthy individuals, avoid concurrent high-intensity exercise and MET; In insulin-resistant individuals with hepatic dysfunction, prioritize pharmacologic control complemented by moderate exercise.

Across all contexts, customize dose, timing, and organ targeting according to physiological profiling. The resolution of the synergy paradox lies not in stronger interventions, but in smarter alignment. Precision in timing, intensity, and individual context will define the next frontier in drug–behavior combination therapy for AS.
