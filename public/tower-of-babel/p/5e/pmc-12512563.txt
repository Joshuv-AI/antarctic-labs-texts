Clinical features, treatments and prognosis of COVID-19 (Omicron) and hemorrhagic colitis: a case series study
Authors: Jie Li, Chao Xu, Kebing Chen, Qing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to evaluate the gastrointestinal implications of the Omicron variant, focusing on intestinal injury.MethodsThis retrospective, single-center study analyzed a case series of 12 Omicron patients with hemorrhagic colitis at the First Hospital of Yangtze University in December 2022.ResultsThe study involved 12 Omicron patients with hemorrhagic colitis, aged 52 to 72 years. The primary clinical symptoms included abdominal pain and hematochezia. Laboratory findings in some patients showed decreased hemoglobin, elevated D-dimer, and increased C-reactive protein levels. Abdominal CT demonstrated intestinal wall thickening and exudation. Colonoscopy revealed intestinal damage characterized by varying degrees of erythema, erosion, and ulceration. The most common site of lesions was the sigmoid colon, followed by the transverse colon and descending colon. Histopathological examination revealed a decrease in the glands within the lamina propria of the intestinal mucosa, along with focal infiltration of neutrophils or lymphocytes and interstitial hemorrhage. Following the treatment, 11 patients improved significantly, while one patient died from multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), secondary to severe COVID-19 pneumonia.ConclusionThe Omicron may be associated with intestinal injury. Most patients could recover after receiving conservative treatment.

Introduction

The global COVID-19 pandemic is caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) [1]. Although SARS-CoV-2 primarily causes respiratory symptoms, its extrapulmonary manifestations—particularly in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract—are gaining increasing attention. Xiao et al. demonstrated that 53.4% of patients (39/73) tested positive for viral RNA in fecal sample, including 17 cases where stool remained positive despite respiratory sample clearance. Histopathological analysis showed occasional lymphocytic infiltration in the esophageal squamous epithelium, and marked infiltration of plasma cells and lymphocytes in the lamina propria of the stomach, duodenum, and rectum, accompanied by stromal edema. The viral entry receptor ACE2 was mainly expressed in the cytoplasm of gastrointestinal epithelial cells. Immunofluorescence detected viral nucleocapsid protein in the cytoplasm of glandular epithelial cells in the stomach, duodenum, and rectum [2]. In a small follow-up study involving 14 individuals, conducted at a mean of 4 months (range: 2.8–5.7 months) after acute SARS-CoV-2 infection, viral antigens (nucleocapsid protein) were detected in the intestinal epithelial cells of five patients (35.7%) [3]. These findings indicate that SARS-CoV-2 may replicate in the gastrointestinal tract and cause mucosal injury.

Moreover, GI manifestations of COVID-19 have been increasingly reported. A meta-analysis of 60 studies involving 4,243 patients revealed that 17.6% of COVID-19 patients presented with GI symptoms, including diarrhea (12.5%), nausea and vomiting (10.2%), and abdominal pain or discomfort (9.2%) [4]. In a Hong Kong cohort of 59 patients, diarrhea (22%) was the most common GI symptom [4], while in other studies, loss of appetite, nausea, and vomiting were more prominent [5]. Notably, different studies have reported varying predominant GI symptoms, suggesting possible regional, demographic, or methodological differences. Furthermore, in addition to common GI symptoms, COVID-19 may also cause other uncommon manifestations [6]. Some studies have found a potential association between COVID-19 and acute hemorrhagic colitis or GI bleeding. Carvalho et al. reported a case which in a SARS-CoV-2 gastrointestinal infection resulted in acute hemorrhagic colitis. Endoscopic examination revealed colonic damage with patchy focal erythema visible in the descending colon, sigmoid colon, and rectum [7]. A systematic study in Western countries reported a GI bleeding (GIB) incidence of 0.06% among 808 COVID-19 patients, similar to that in the general population. However, the mortality rate was significantly higher in GIB patients than in non-GIB patients (25.4% vs. 16.4%). Upper GIB accounted for 66% of cases, with melena (47.5%) and hematemesis (16.7%) as the predominant presentations. Endoscopic findings included peptic ulcers (48.4%), followed by esophagitis, and erosive gastritis. Lower GIB primarily manifested as hematochezia (37.8%), with endoscopic findings of rectal ulcers (36.4%), colitis (30.7%), and colonic ischemia (29.8%) [8].

The Omicron variant has attracted widespread global attention, since it was first identified in November 2021 [9]. Although most Omicron cases present with mild symptoms, a small number of patients may develop severe complications [10].This study focuses on the rare occurrence of hemorrhagic colitis observed in patients infected with the Omicron variant. We aim to provide further insights into the relationship between the Omicron and intestinal lesions, and to explore the potential underlying mechanisms.

Methods

The single-center retrospective study collected data from 12 Omicron patients with hemorrhagic colitis at the First Hospital of Yangtze University in December 2022. COVID-19 was confirmed by a positive PCR test, and the viral gene sequencing results of the patient’s nasopharyngeal swab samples, tested at the Jingzhou City Disease Control Center laboratory, identified the variant as Omicron [11]. The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Jingzhou Public Health Clinical Center (KY2025-056-01).

Patients’ data collection included general information (age, comorbidities, etc.), vital signs (oxygen saturation, blood pressure, etc.), clinical manifestations (fever, abdominal pain, hematochezia, etc.), laboratory tests (hemoglobin, C-reactive protein (CRP), D-dimer, etc.), abdominal computed tomography (CT), colonoscopy, pathological examination, and therapeutic medications.

Patient demographics and medical history

All patients tested positive for COVID-19. The mean age of the patients was 65 years. Comorbidities included hypertension (6/12), diabetes mellitus (2/12), cardiovascular disease (1/12), cerebral infarction (1/12), bronchitis (1/12), and malignancies (2/12). Five patients had a history of using non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or antiplatelet agents (e.g., ibuprofen, aspirin, clopidogrel). No patient had a prior history of gastrointestinal bleeding or known intestinal pathology.

Clinical manifestations

The most common clinical manifestations were hematochezia (12/12), abdominal pain (10/12), fever (5/12), and diarrhea (3/12).

All patients had normal oxygen saturation. Four patients had abdominal tenderness, primarily in the lower abdomen or periumbilical region.

Laboratory and imaging findings

Only two patients had hemoglobin levels below the lower limit of normal, without meeting the diagnostic criteria for mild anemia. Elevated D-dimer levels were observed in three patients, and elevated CRP levels in six patients.

Abdominal CT revealed segmental intestinal wall thickening and exudation in five patients. In one patient, non-calcified plaques were observed in the proximal segment of the superior mesenteric artery (SMA), accompanied by mild luminal narrowing (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Colonoscopy

Colonoscopy revealed mucosal injury characterized by varying degrees of hyperemia, erosion, and ulceration, with features resembling ischemic colitis (Table 1; Fig. 2). The sigmoid colon (11/12, 91.7%) was the most frequently affected site, followed by the transverse colon (5/12, 41.7%) and descending colon (5/12, 41.7%). Fewer cases involved the cecum, ascending colon, and rectum (Table 1).

Pathological examination

Ten patients (10/12) underwent colonic biopsy. Histopathological examination revealed inflammatory cell infiltration, vascular injury, and disruption of mucosal barrier (Fig. 3). Specific findings included neutrophilic infiltration (8/10), lymphocytic infiltration (4/10), glandular atrophy (5/10), and interstitial hemorrhage (2/10).

Clinical outcomes

Eleven patients (11/12) showed clinical improvement with medical treatment. In one patient, follow-up colonoscopy at 6 months demonstrated complete mucosal healing (Fig. 4). One patient developed intestinal obstruction requiring emergency surgery, was admitted to the intensive care unit with mechanical ventilation, and subsequently died from multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), secondary to severe COVID-19 pneumonia.

Discussion

Potential mechanisms of intestinal injury

Hemorrhagic colitis is an extremely rare manifestation in COVID-19 patients [6]. In this study, we conducted an in-depth analysis of 12 Omicron-infected patients presenting with hemorrhagic colitis, examining clinical manifestations, abdominal CT imaging findings, colonoscopy, and pathological features. Notably, all patients were older, than 50 years, and primarily presented with bloody stools or abdominal pain. The intestines of these patients showed varying degrees of erythema, erosion, and ulceration, resembling findings in ischemic bowel injury. Histopathological examination revealed acute active changes in the intestinal mucosa, characterized by focal neutrophilic infiltration and hemorrhage in the submucosal layer. Abdominal CT imaging and colonoscopy further confirmed the intestinal injury, with the sigmoid colon, descending colon, and transverse colon being the most commonly affected sites. These results indicated that the Omicron variant may have a direct or indirect destructive effect on the gastrointestinal tract.

Studies have reported varying rates of positive SARS-CoV-2 detection in fecal specimens, emphasizing its presence and potential replication in the gastrointestinal tract. For instance, one study detected the virus in 29% of fecal specimens (44/153) [12], whereas another reported an even higher rate of 53.42% among hospitalized COVID-19 patients [2]. The potential gastrointestinal involvement is thought to be mediated by viral entry into host cells via the attachment of the SARS-CoV-2 spike (S) protein to the ACE2 receptor on the cell surface, the primary entry route for the virus [13]. Single-cell transcriptomic analyses revealed high levels of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 co-expression in gastrointestinal epithelial cells, particularly in the ileum and colon, suggesting that these cells are highly susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection [14]. Following viral entry into intestinal epithelial cells, disruption of the paracellular barrier may occur through altered expression and function of tight junction (TJ)-associated proteins, initiating intestinal injury. Furthermore, binding of SARS-CoV-2 to ACE2 impairs its physiological function, resulting in elevated levels of angiotensin II (Ang II), which promotes thrombogenesis, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses. These pathological changes may further impair capillary function, leading to tissue hypoxia, which in turn stimulates cytokine release and perpetuates microcirculatory disturbances in a positive feedback loopt [15]. Lehmann et al. demonstrated a significant increase in apoptosis in intestinal epithelial cells of COVID-19 patients, as evidenced by immunohistochemical detection of cleaved caspase-3 [16]. Additionally, SARS-CoV-2 infection in the lungs and intestinal epithelium could trigger a massive release of pro-inflammatory mediators, including chemokine-1, TGF-β1, TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6, leading to a cytokine storm that further aggravates small intestinal mucosal injury and disrupts gastrointestinal barrier integrity [17]. Notably, reduced expression of vascular endothelial cadherin (VE-cadherin, VE-cad) and increased phosphorylation at tyrosine residue 731 (Tyr731) have also been observed in the intestinal vasculature of COVID-19 patients, indicating potential damage to endothelial barrier function [18].

In the regulation of inflammation, ACE2 plays a role in maintaining amino acid homeostasis, promoting antimicrobial peptide expression, and preserving the ecological balance of the gut microbiota [13].Binding of SARS-CoV-2 to the ACE2 receptor could block the uptake of tryptophan and inhibit the B0AT1/ACE2 transport pathway. This interference affects mTOR activation, reducting antimicrobial peptide synthesis. The consequent disruption of antimicrobial peptide expression could alter the gut microbiota, thereby promoting Th17 cells polarization and amplifying inflammation. Reduced ACE2 expression might further exacerbate these processes [13, 19].

COVID-19-associated coagulopathy (CAC) is characterized by hypercoagulability and an increased risk of thrombotic complications [20]. Conway et al. systematically described the coagulation abnormalities in COVID-19 patients as being associated with endothelial cell injury, the procoagulant effects of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), imbalances in the coagulation-fibrinolysis system, and other contributing factors [21]. Notably, D-dimer levels are markedly elevated in COVID-19 patients (median 4,336 ng/mL in severe cases vs. less than 500 ng/mL in healthy individuals), accompanied by increased resistance to fibrinolysis, as evidenced by a 1.5-fold upregulation of plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) expression [22]. In this study, some patients showed elevated D-dimer levels. Previous research indicated that patients with gastrointestinal symptoms have significantly longer prothrombin time (13.1 s) compared to those without such symptoms (12.5 s), while no significant differences were found in other coagulation parameters [23]. A radiological review of ischemic bowel disease reported on 31 COVID-19 patients, nearly half of whom had thrombosis in the mesenteric veins or arteries [24]. Our study identified one patient with non-calcified plaques in the mesenteric vessels, raising suspicion of a possible thrombotic event, which may warrant consideration of acute mesenteric ischemia in similar scenarios.

Microbiota dysbiosis and immune activation

It is widely recognized that COVID-19 infection could potentially trigger systemic inflammatory and immune responses [13]. This is supported by the elevated levels of CRP observed in some patients in our study. The virus infects epithelial cells, triggering acute intestinal inflammation characterized by infiltration of neutrophils, macrophages, and T cells, driven by the release of various cytokine and chemokines [25]. Confirming this, an Austrian study revealed the activation of an intestinal inflammatory response in COVID-19 patients, demonstrated by symptoms such as diarrhea, increased fecal calprotectin (mainly from neutrophils granulocytes), and systemic IL-6 response [26]. Pathological reports from various studies have shown that patients with COVID-19 infection exhibit plasma cell and lymphocyte-rich infiltration in the lamina propria of the stomach, duodenum, and rectum, along with interstitial edema [2]. In our study, pathological examination revealed acute active changes in the intestinal mucosa, localized increase of neutrophils, and evident hemorrhage in the interstitial spaces. Together, these findings indicated that Omicron-infected patients may exhibit an inflammatory response in the intestines.

An early study using a mouse model to study viral infections found an association between Th17 cells and virus-induced intestinal immune damage. In the small intestine of those mice, increased expression of Th17-specific transcription factor RORγt and IL-17 A was detected, which promoted immune-mediated intestinal damage and subsequently disrupted intestinal microbiota homeostasis [19]. Similar alterations in gut microbiota composition have been reported in COVID-19 patients. Zuo et al. utilized genomic sequencing technology to assessed the fecal microbiome of COVID-19 patients. They reported an increase in opportunistic pathogens, such as Clostridium hathewayi, Actinomyces viscosus, and Rothia mucilaginosa, along with a decline in beneficial symbionts, including members of the Lachnospiraceae family, Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, bacteroides massiliensis, and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii [27]. Similarly, Mizutani et al. found a decrease abundance of Clostridia in COVID-19 patients, which was negative correlation with interferon (IFN)-γ and IL-28 A/IFN-λ2 levels. Overall, these alterations in gut microbiota composition correlated with elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine levels [28].

Comparison with previous studies

Only a few clinical case reports have suggested a potential association between COVID-19 and intestinal injury. Norsa et al. reported ischemic bowel disease in seven COVID-19 patients involving either the small intestine or either colon, with three patients ultimately dying from severe complications [29]. However, studies describing the endoscopic features of intestinal injury remain relatively scarce. In our cohort, the observed endoscopic findings were partially consistent with those reported by Norsa and Carvalho [7, 29]. Nevertheless, although an association between Omicron infection and hemorrhagic colitis was identified, a definitive cause-and-effect relationship could not be established. The observed association may be attributed to multiple mechanisms, including direct viral injury to intestinal tissue, systemic inflammatory responses, vascular endothelial damage, coagulation abnormalities, and immune-mediated mechanisms [13].

The research study by Vuille-dit-Bille et al. demonstrated correlations between ACE2 gene expression and age. Analysis of duodenal biopsy tissues from 43 healthy adults revealed higher intestinal ACE2 mRNA expression in older individuals, potentially increasing their susceptibility to gastrointestinal manifestations [30]. Our study aligns with these findings, with the mean age of patients with intestinal injury being 67 years. This association suggested a potential age-dependent susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 gastrointestinal infection. We postulated that the Omicron, through its affinity for ACE2 receptors and other binding sites on the intestinal mucosa, might invade intestinal epithelial cells, triggering inflammation. This inflammatory response may compromise the intestinal barrier, exacerbating mucosal injury and resulting in clinical symptoms such as hematochezia. Elevated CRP levels in some patients further support the presence of a systemic inflammatory response.

Furthermore, some patients had a history of long-term anticoagulant use. In a matched case-control study, Martin et al. reported that 39% of patients in the GLB group received therapeutic-dose anticoagulation, compared with 27% in the non-bleeding group; although this difference did not reach statistical significance -likely due to the limited sample size-anticoagulant therapy may still increase the risk of GLB [31]. Therefore, anticoagulant use may also be a potential risk factor for GIB in COVID-19 patients in our study.

Clinical implications and treatment outcomes

In this study, patients received symptomatic treatments, including proton pump inhibitors, glutamine, and probiotics. 11 patients showed significant improved, with one showing complete mucosal healing on follow-up after six months. One patient, whose pulmonary infection worsened, eventually died from MODS. Thus, symptomatic treatment could effectively alleviate the symptoms of the majority of patients. Notably, Hashimoto et al. found that ACE2 inhibitors could regulate intestinal amino acid metabolism and maintain a balanced microbiota, thereby improving intestinal inflammation in mice. This finding provides a novel approach for identifying effective drugs to treat the gastrointestinal symptoms of COVID-19. The development of drugs targeting the ACE2 receptor holds promise for achieving improved therapeutic outcomes [32].

Limitations

This study has several important limitations that may affect the generalizability of its findings. First, the small sample size (n = 12) means that it is primarily descriptive in nature. Second, as a single-center retrospective study, there is potential for selection bias. Participants may have had more severe gastrointestinal symptoms or undergone endoscopic evaluation, limiting the representativeness of the broader COVID-19 population. Third, different physicians may apply varying thresholds for performing biopsies based on their experience and assessment of potential risks and benefits. Consequently, not all patients underwent histopathological examination, which may affect the completeness of pathological characterization. Fourth, the absence of enteric virus testing weakens the ability to directly attribution of gastrointestinal injury to SARS-CoV-2. Therefore, further confirmation may require multicenter retrospective studies or long-term follow-up of past cases, given the evolving context of the pandemic.

Conclusion

In summary, this study suggests a possible association between the Omicron variant of SARS-CoV-2 and hemorrhagic colitis, particularly in older adults.

The condition was mainly characterized by abdominal pain and hematochezia, with some patients showing elevated CRP and D-dimer levels. Colonoscopy showed intestinal injury with varying degrees of erythema, erosions, and ulcerations.

The pathogenesis may involve direct viral injury, immune-mediated inflammation, and coagulopathy. Although a causal link cannot be definitively established, symptomatic treatment proved effective in most cases. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these findings and clarify underlying mechanisms.
