Use of robotics may facilitate earlier functional recovery and reduce overcorrection compared to conventional implantation techniques in total knee arthroplasty: a single-surgeon cohort study
Authors: Ali Ghossein, Matthias Gatz, Achim Dohmen, Frank Hildebrand, Ulf Krister Hofmann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTotal Knee Arthroplasty (TKA) is a highly successful procedure to treat osteoarthritis. Yet a relevant number of patients complain about persisting pain. In the present study, patient satisfaction, functional outcomes, and radiological parameters were evaluated in a retrospective single-surgeon analysis. Conventional implantation was compared with a robotic system that uses computed tomography (CT)-based planning for haptically guided bone preparation (MAKO/Stryker), hypothesizing that the use of robotics would lead to improved early function and potentially different radiological alignment.MethodsThe first 150 consecutive robotic-TKAs of one surgeon were compared with the manually operated TKAs number 8000–8150 in a retrospective cohort study using the same implanting philosophy and pre-, intra-, and postoperative conditions and workflows with exception of the robotic-assisted component. Patients undergoing primary TKA for osteoarthritis resistant to conservative treatment were included; exclusion criteria were age < 18 years, rheumatic disease, or complex preoperative traumatic injury. Both groups shared the same implanting philosophy and perioperative workflows, with exception of the robotic assistance. Key outcomes included the Hospital for Special Surgery (HSS) score, functional recovery milestones, and radiological parameters.ResultsFunctionally, patients in the robotic TKA groups reached 90° of knee flexion much earlier with a medium of 5 (2–10) days postoperatively when compared to the manually operated group (8 [5–12] days (p < 0.001). Pain at discharge was slightly higher in this group with median NRS values of 2 (1–7) versus 1 (1–4) in the manually operated group (p < 0.001) with patients being discharged, however, three days earlier in the robotic group. Interestingly, mechanical axis deviation showed a greater variation in the robotic TKA group than in the manual TKA group with the median being more in varus (-6 [-20–30] mm vs. -1 [-19–17] mm, p < 0.001). One year postoperatively, the HSS Score was marginally higher in the robotic group with (73 (45–83) vs. 70 (58–70), p < 0.001), however, still below the minimal clinically important difference.ConclusionThe data from this study suggest that patients operated with such a robotic system may be mobilized earlier and also show less overcorrection compared to those operated by a conventional technique. The long-term benefit remains, however, still unclear.

Introduction

The Global Burden of Disease study described hip and knee osteoarthritis (OA) as the 11th contributor to global disability [1, 2]. If conservative treatment of knee OA fails, partial or total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is performed. Although this standardized technique works solidly for the majority of cases, the satisfaction rates are known to be lower than for total hip arthroplasty [3]. Next to complications such as infection or fracture, malalignment can occur in or after TKA [4]. Between 10–20% of patients complain about persistent knee pain and functional limitations even in cases of a radiologically well aligned and uninfected implant. The reasons for such persistent knee pain often remain unclear [5–8]. Factors such as imperfect soft tissue balancing or altered kinematics are implicated in these suboptimal outcomes. To address these challenges and improve outcomes, various surgical adjuncts, including navigation-assisted and robotic-assisted techniques, have been developed. These technologies aim to enhance implantation precision and reliability.

The MAKO®-system from Stryker (Kalamazoo, Michigan, U.S.A.) was launched in 2016. It matches preoperative CT-data with intraoperative findings to generate an in-situ 3D reconstruction which recreates the patients'native knee anatomy. This patient-specific model is then used to calculate a window for resection levels and to select implant size and position for the desired postoperative bone coverage. Intraoperatively, the system gives audio, visual and tactile feedback to guide the bone resection which is itself still performed by the surgeon within a corridor provided by the robotic arm. Dynamic balancing is used to assess intraoperative flexion and extension gaps, joint stability, range of motion and limb alignment.

While several studies suggest robotic-assisted TKA, including the MAKO® system, can offer improved component positioning, reduced soft tissue injury, and potentially accelerated early recovery [9–14], it could be demonstrated that high implantation precision is achievable. However, what still remains uncertain is the translation of these technical advantages into superior long-term patient-reported outcomes, functional benefits, and overall satisfaction across diverse surgical practices. The impact of specific alignment philosophies achievable with robotic assistance on these outcomes also warrants further investigation. In the present study, patient satisfaction and radiological outcome after TKA was evaluated in a retrospective single-surgeon analysis. Results were compared of patients operated with the robotic MAKO®-system with those from a collective operated by the same surgeon with a conventional surgical technique including intramedullary femoral and extramedullary tibial rods.

The rationale for this study was to rigorously compare these two techniques within the practice of a single, high-volume surgeon, thereby minimizing surgeon-dependent variability and focusing on the impact of the technology itself on patient outcomes and radiological parameters. We hypothesized that: (1) robotic-assisted TKA would lead to earlier achievement of postoperative functional milestones and a shorter inpatient stay; (2) radiological outcomes, particularly mechanical axis alignment, would differ between the groups due to the robotic system's planning capabilities and the surgeon's application of a more precise restricted kinematic alignment strategy; and (3) patient-reported outcomes at one year would be comparable or potentially favor the robotic group.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

All patients operated with TKA for high grade symptomatic primary OA of the knee joint resistant to conservative treatment between July 2020 and July 2022 were included. Exclusion criteria were age < 18 years, ligament instability, rheumatic disease, or complex preoperative traumatic injury. Full departmental, institutional and local ethical committee approval were obtained before commencement of the study. This retrospective cohort study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines.

Study design and outcome parameters

A flow chart of the study design is displayed in Fig. 1. Using a retrospective cohort study design, n = 150 consecutive manually operated TKAs and n = 150 consecutive robotic-TKAs were included and analyzed. The robotic group registration was started after the device had been implemented in the hospital and the operating surgeon had gained experience from n = 30 cases with the robotic system. All surgeries were performed by the same person (A.D.), head of department of a certified arthroplasty center, with a personal experience of more than n = 8000 performed knee arthroplasties. For this reason, the same implanting philosophy and pre-, intra-, and postoperative conditions and workflows were applied for both surgical techniques with exception of the robotic-assisted component.Fig. 1Study flowchart. In the robotic group, CT scans were performed around three weeks prior to surgery. For the robotic procedure, planning was performed with the software provided by the company. For the conventional operating technique 2D planning was performed with the software mediCAD® (Hectec GmbH, Version 6.5, Altdorf/Landshut, Germany).

As demographic parameters sex, age, ASA-score, and BMI were registered. All patients were interviewed pre-operatively, and 1 year after surgery using the Ranawat and Shine Hospital for special Knee Surgery (HSS) Score [15]. During the inpatient stay, the following additional parameters were monitored: operation time, pain as reported on the numeric rating scale (NRS) for pain, range of motion, time to 90 degrees of flexion and time to walk stairs, duration of inpatient stay, rate of blood transfusions, and complications. Standing long-film radiographs and lateral knee views were obtained from patients before and 5 days after surgery. Radiographic analysis on the pre- and postoperative X-rays was performed with Sectra-IDS7 (Germany, Austria, Switzerland). The following parameters were measured and analyzed: Kellgren-Lawrence grade [9], mechanical axis deviation (MAD), notching, tibial oversizing, tibial under sizing, femoral oversizing, femoral under sizing. A-p X-rays were taken in weight bearing position. For a detailed description of the radiographic measurement techniques for each of these parameters please see Table 1.

Postoperative care

Postoperatively, patients received opioids (oxycodone) in combination with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (ibuprofen) if no contraindications were present. The analgesic therapy was reduced according to the evaluated pain levels of the patients. The first physiotherapy session was already performed at the day of surgery (approximately 8 h after surgery) and all patients underwent a structured rehabilitation program.

Surgical technique and treatment protocol

All patients received general anesthesia without regional anesthesia. The operation was performed without tourniquet. The patients were positioned supine with a lateral thigh and foot bolster. When possible, diathermy was used. A medial parapatellar approach was used. In both groups, a restricted kinematic alignment strategy with symmetric balance in extension and flexion was performed. All components were cemented. In the Robotic group, the Triathlon Total Knee System (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA), a cruciate-retaining (CR) design, was used. In the conventional group, the Attune Knee System (DePuy Synthes, Warsaw, IN, USA), also a CR design, was utilized. All implants in both groups were CR and featured a single radius femoral component design. Routine patellar resurfacing was not performed in either group; the patella was preserved unless significant chondral damage necessitated resurfacing, which was not necessary in this cohort. At the end of surgery, local intraoperative anesthesia was performed infiltrating the posterior capsule with a 20 ml mixture of ropivacaine/adrenaline (100 mg/0.005 mg) using a 20G 0.9 × 70 mm hypodermic needle, the medial and lateral capsule with 20 ml ropivacaine/adrenaline (100 mg/0.005 mg), and after closure of the capsule 20 ml intraarticular infiltration with a mixture of tranexamic acid/ropivacaine (3 g/100 mg Ropivacaine).

Statistical analysis

An a priori power analysis was not conducted for this retrospective study, as the sample size was determined by the consecutive inclusion of 150 patients in each group within the defined study period. The sample size of 132 matched pairs was considered substantial and comparable to similar studies in the field for detecting clinically meaningful differences [2, 16].

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Version 29 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). Individual matching was performed based on the degree of OA as stratified by the Kellgren-Lawrence grade [10] and those cases without a match were excluded. Distributions of variables within the groups were assessed by histograms. Continuous variables are presented as medians (interquartile range). In cases where the low rate of values different from zero led to a little meaningful median, the means and standard deviation are additionally reported to better illustrate variation of the data. Data are presented as boxplots or bar diagram. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies. Comparison between groups of continuous variables was performed by Mann–Whitney U test or t-test as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-squared test. Results

No statistically different characteristics were found for age (robotic TKA 68 [43–87] years vs. manual TKA 69 [46–90] years, p = 0.818), sex (p = 0.300) and ASA-score (p = 0.800). Radiological outcome

The manual TKA group presented a neutral median alignment (0 [−9–7]) (p = < 0.001). Of note, posterior condylar offset was significantly greater (28 [18–34] mm vs. [17–33] mm, p < 0.002) in the robotic group (Fig. 3.2). Tibial oversizing showed no difference in both groups. Tibial undersizing in the robotic group (mean 0.41 [1.21] mm) was significantly lower than in the manual TKA (mean 1.55 [3.79] mm, p = 0.001). Discussion

In this retrospective single-surgeon cohort study, patient satisfaction and radiological differences between a TKA group operated with help of the robotic MAKO®-system and a group operated with conventional intramedullary femoral and extramedullary tibial navigation were compared. Both groups showed good results in the HSS score one year postoperatively, with an average improvement in both groups above 25 points. While the authors are not aware of any study defining the substantial clinical benefit [11] for the HSS score in patients with TKA, the magnitude of the improvement would correspond to those necessary in the KOOS to be classified as a substantial improvement [12]. In a direct comparison, the robotic TKA group presented with a score slightly higher by around 3 points which is, however, still below the minimal clinically important difference of 5.41 reported for the HSS score [13]. In a similar study design of a demographically matched retrospective analysis of a single high-volume surgeon with n = 148 robotic TKA cases and n = 159 conventional guided TKAs patients, Wang et al. also found comparable results in both groups at 1 year postoperatively [14]. This aligns with several other investigations suggesting that while robotic systems offer precision, the translation to superior mid-term PROMs is not always evident when compared to experienced conventional surgeons. However, some studies do report modest benefits in PROMs at one year or beyond with robotic TKA, particularly in specific patient subgroups or when certain alignment protocols are followed [17]. Functionally, patients in the robotic TKA group reached two important postoperative landmarks (90° of knee flexion and walking stairs) three days earlier than did their counterparts in the manually operated group (both p < 0.001). This directly translated into a discharge from hospital also being three days earlier (robotic TKA 7 [4–15] days vs. manual TKA 10 [7–17] days) (p < 0.001). The slightly increased values on the NRS at discharge can thus possibly be explained by the earlier time-point of registration. These improved early functional results are also in line with those reported by Mitchell et al., 2021 [16]. Conversely, other research, such as the study by Muster et al. [18], did not find significant differences in early pain or opioid consumption between robotic and conventional TKA, highlighting the variability in reported outcomes which may depend on perioperative protocols and patient populations.

One possible important factor for earlier mobilization and increased flexion in the early postoperative period might be the significantly increased posterior condylar offset in the robotic TKA group with a median of 28 mm when compared with the manually operated group with a median of only 24 mm (p = 0.002). Indeed, a reduction of the posterior condylar offset by only 3 mm can already reduce free flexion before the occurrence of tibiofemoral impingement by 10 degrees [19]. While many patients still manage to further increase their maximum flexion in the long-run despite posterior tibiofemoral impingement by lengthening the ventral joint capsule, this phenomenon is not possible in the early postoperative period where only tension free flexion will be permitted. Another possible explanation for the early remobilization and good early flexion could be the seemingly"worse"alignment in the robotic group. Historically, for a long-time mechanical alignment has been considered the standard of care aiming for a postoperative neutral mechanical axis going centrally trough the knee joint [20]. Over the past two decades and accompanied by several improvements in the implanted materials and fixation techniques, this concept has been increasingly questioned. Presently, several competing alignment philosophies are discussed and implemented such as kinematic alignment, restricted kinematic alignment, mechanical alignment, adjusted mechanical alignment, or anatomic alignment [21, 22]. The hope associated with these new alignment strategies is to improve functional outcome due to a more ligament-oriented implantation technique. This technique shall reduce postoperative over- or under tensioning and in such postoperative pain and instability. The physician operating the patients of the present study applied a restricted kinematic alignment strategy. Since knee OA is frequently associated with a varus deformity, the aim for implantation was in most cases a slight varus-undercorrection to improve ligament balancing. While such an undercorrection was also striven for in the manually operated group, for fear of exceeding the allowed tolerance levels—of for example 5° of varus/valgus positioning of the tibial component [14], the median MAD in this group was only 1 mm and median femoro-tibial alignment exactly 0°. In the robotic TKA group, however, the surgeon felt confident to perform undercorrection up to 5° safely without exceeding such delineated safety margins. And indeed, the femoro-tibial axis presented with a median of −2° with a range of only 11°, while the range in the manually operated group was 16°. The greater postoperative MAD is thus explained by an assumed higher implantation precision respecting restriction margin in the process of undercorrection. One major group of patients dissatisfied with their postoperative result are those with patella maltracking [9, 10, 13]. Next to better ligament balancing in varus OA, a slight varus undercorrection also decreases the Q-angle with consecutively an improved patella tracking [23]. The ability to achieve these targeted, sometimes non-neutral, alignments with high precision is a key purported advantage of robotic systems, though the long-term biomechanical consequences of such alignments, particularly increased varus, warrant continued study [24].

Tibial oversizing was not a relevant issue in both groups. Interestingly, tibial oversizing occurred rather in the coronal plane in the manually operated group and oversizing in the sagittal plane in the robotic group. Tibial undersizing was also comparable in both groups. Extreme values with a size mismatch of up to 4 mm were, however, only present in the manually operated group with maximum values in the robotic group of only 2 mm. While tibial oversizing is associated with pain and impaired functionality, tibial undersizing is related to bone resorption and early component loosening [25]. In a recent study analyzing cementless knee arthroplasty results, tibial undersizing was associated with a higher risk of implant loosening already within the first two postoperative years [26]. Of note, a higher risk of loosening was also observed in cases with lacking posterior bone support [25]. Using the robotic system in the present study rather led to a proud sagittal size than in the manually operated group, thus ensuring also posterior cortical bone contact.

Notching was also not significantly different between groups, with maximum values again obtained in the manually operated group (2 mm). Even these maxima remained, however, still within grade I of the Tayside classification [27]. Yet, the data presented here with respect to notching must be interpreted with care, since the geometry of the Stryker® Triathlon® knee used in this study does not possess a ventral femoral cut perpendicular to the distal femoral cut. The ventral bone resection is rather performed tilted ventrally at a 6° angle which only allows classical femoral notching in implants small in size in relation to the distal femur. In all other cases it leads to the proximal apex of the femoral shield being free from bone contact—a phenomenon also termed"airballing".

Summary

Robotic knee replacements are seen as expensive and complex procedures but early recovery has been shown in several studies [2, 14, 23]. Reasons for this seeming superiority in the early postoperative phase might be a more ligament-oriented implantation technique, improved posterior condylar offset and improved patella tracking. As discussed, while our study supports these early benefits, the one-year functional advantage was not sustained, a finding consistent showed for example. by Wang et al., [28], Whether the observed differences in alignment and implantation precision translate into improved or also impaired long-term implant survival remains a critical question. While our study focuses on TKA, findings from related fields, such as unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA), provide some context. For instance, Maritan et al. (2023) reported similar 5-year survivorship comparing robotic-assisted and conventional lateral UKA [17]. Yet 5-years might still be too short a time period to evaluate the effect of planned undercorrection on long-term wear patterns and loosening rates in TKA. Rigorous, long-term follow-up through large registry data will be essential to elucidate these potential effects.

Limitations

While this study follows a coherent design with a relevant number of patients being consecutively operated by the same surgeon, it is still a retrospective design which does not provide the same level of evidence as a prospective study. The surgical protocol was the same in all patient in both groups except for the use of two implants (because of the consecutive inclusion to avoid errors by learning curve). Moreover, the follow-up of one year allows a good estimate of the steady-state achieved by the patients postoperatively. It does not, however, permit any statements regarding the longevity of the implanted prostheses. The technique used was restricted kinematic alignment where in robotic techniques personalized alignment is expected. However, all different alignment strategies can be used.

Clinical implications

The findings of this study carry several clinical implications. The earlier functional recovery and discharge observed in the robotic TKA group suggest that this technology can support enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols, potentially reducing healthcare costs associated with prolonged hospital stays. For surgeons, the ability of the robotic system to reliably achieve specific, sometimes non-neutral, alignment targets, as seen with the varus undercorrection in our cohort using a restricted kinematic approach, offers greater control in personalizing TKA to individual patient anatomy and ligament balance. However, the increased variation in MAD and the tendency towards varus highlight the importance of careful preoperative planning and understanding the long-term biomechanical consequences of such alignments. Patients should be counseled that while robotic TKA may offer a faster initial recovery, one-year functional outcomes and satisfaction levels may be similar to those achieved with conventional techniques by an experienced surgeon. The slightly higher pain at discharge in the robotic group, likely due to earlier discharge, also warrants consideration in postoperative pain management strategies.

Conclusion

Using a restricted kinematic alignment strategy, the robotic system led to an increased variability in mechanical axis deviation with most knees being in slight varus as a result of the targeted undercorrection of the preoperative varus deformity. Posterior condylar offset was also significantly increased by a median of 4 mm. Both factors might have contributed to the fact that patients from the robotic group reached 90° of flexion and walking stairs three days earlier than did their conventionally operated counterparts. This early mobilization also allowed to discharge patients from the robotic group from hospital three days earlier. One year postoperatively, this initial functional superiority was no longer visible. If in the long run the altered implantation technique allowed through the robotic precision leads to beneficial or detrimental results with respect to implant survival future studies will still have to demonstrate.
