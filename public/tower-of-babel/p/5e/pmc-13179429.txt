Protected Engagement Time and Related Interventions: A Scoping Review of Experience and Outcomes, and Relevance to the Australian Context
Authors: Donna Hristodoulidis, Brent Hayward, Adam Searby
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEngagement between mental health nurses (MHNs) and older adults on acute mental health units is often hindered by age‐related factors such as cognitive decline, frailty and sensory impairment, as well as psychosocial challenges including bereavement, social isolation and loss of identity. This makes therapeutic engagement essential on older adult mental health units. However, high workloads, administrative demands and the dominance of risk management often limit meaningful interactions. Protected engagement time (PET), originally introduced in the United Kingdom, was designed to preserve dedicated, interruption‐free time for MHNs to engage therapeutically with consumers. This scoping review aimed to map existing literature on PET and to examine its relevance to the Australian older adult acute mental health context. The review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and reported using the PRISMA‐ScR checklist. After screening, eight studies met the inclusion criteria, with none conducted in Australia, and only two focused specifically on older adult units. Data analysis revealed four overarching categories: relational engagement and therapeutic presence, individualised and meaningful engagement, staffing and organisational structure, and outcomes and broader impacts. This scoping review suggests that PET can positively influence engagement between mental health consumers and MHNs, with benefits extending beyond the sessions themselves. However, the review highlights the need to evaluate how this approach may be integrated in older adult mental health units to enhance the therapeutic relationships, promote recovery and uphold dignity to some of the most vulnerable consumers in mental health care.

Introduction

Older adults, defined as those aged over 65 years, who are admitted to acute mental health units often present with co‐morbidities, frailty, sensory impairment and cognitive decline. These factors may hinder communication and limit the ease with which therapeutic engagement can occur (World Health Organisation 2025). Mental health later in life is often influenced by bereavement, shifts in identity associated with retirement, financial strain and disruptions to long‐standing social roles (Sayer and Cotton 2014). Within Australian public mental health services, older adults account for approximately 8.9% of all admissions, with nearly half (48%) being new consumers to mental health services (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2025b). Their average length of stay (36.8 days) and 28‐day readmission rate (7.8%) reflect the complexity of needs (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2025b). Experiences of isolation and loneliness are also prevalent in this population and up to 15% have experienced some type of abuse, with the most prevalent being psychological abuse, neglect and financial abuse (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2025a). These vulnerabilities may be intensified during hospitalisation, where unfamiliar environments, disrupted routines and separation from social supports can heighten distress and disengagement (Clissett et al. 2013).

At the same time, longer admissions in older adult units present valuable opportunities for therapeutic engagement, relationship building and activities that promote connection and meaning. Yet despite this potential, a persistent gap exists in the application of humanistic principles within acute mental health nursing for older adults. Acute mental health units are dominated by biomedical and risk‐management frameworks, often prioritising symptom reductions at the expense of person‐centred, relational care (Abt et al. 2022; Glantz et al. 2023; Hristodoulidis et al. 2022). Humanistic care that centres on dignity, authentic connection and the lived experience of the person offers a meaningful framework to address this gap (Kogstad et al. 2011; Taghinezhad et al. 2022). Nevertheless, its application within older adult acute mental health units remains underexplored, and how nurses enact humanistic care in this context warrants further investigation.

Humanistic mental health nursing provides a useful foundation for understanding why time for relational engagement is essential in older adult mental health units. Humanistic practice is characterised by attention to biological, psychological, social, behavioural and spiritual dimensions of the individual, ensuring that nursing interventions remain responsive to personal values, wishes and expectations (Sanz‐Osorio et al. 2023). Rather than being defined by cure, this approach highlights dignity, authenticity and mutual presence, recognising the consumer as an active participant in their own care (Nwozichi et al. 2019). In older adult acute mental health units, where cognitive and functional changes may constrain autonomy, humanistic nursing emphasises the importance of sustained interpersonal connection, familiarity and understanding the person (Gjernes and Måseide 2020). These principles of dignity, authenticity and mutual presence, combined with consumers participating in their own care, thereby create a clear rationale for approaches that intentionally protect time and space for meaningful nurse–consumer engagement within busy acute in‐patient units.

The therapeutic significance of ordinariness, understood as the taken‐for‐granted routines, rhythms and relational aspects of everyday life been highlighted within humanistic care has been higlighted within humanistic care and extends beyond the completion of daily activities (Deveau 2024). While terms such as ‘everyday activities’ are often used, the concept of ordinariness is adopted here to emphasise the relational, experiential and taken‐for‐granted qualities of daily life that extend beyond discrete activities. It refers not simply to engaging in activities such as socialising, working or having a cup of tea, but to the familiar and often unnoticed flow of ordinary moments and interactions that structure a person's sense of self and belonging (Deveau and Bradshaw 2025). However, when people are experiencing acute mental health conditions, it is this ordinariness that is often disrupted, often by compulsory admission to mental health facilities under mental health legislation (Deveau 2024). Humanistic care recognises that the therapeutic value lies not in the performance of tasks, but in the relational and experiential qualities of everyday life, such as shared conversation, being present with others or participating in informal, routine moments (Gjernes and Måseide 2020; Schrems 2025). These ordinary, shared experiences can support the re‐establishment of continuity and belonging. Therefore, ordinariness is understood as relational and contextual, distinct from task‐based assistance with activities of daily living, for example, assistance with personal hygiene, and central to recovery‐oriented, humanistic care (Kogstad et al. 2011).

Despite the centrality of humanistic care and the therapeutic value of ordinariness, mental health nurses (MHNs) on acute mental health units report challenges to meaningful engagement with consumers. Previous research has indicated that high workloads, increasing administrative tasks and the dominance of risk management impede the opportunity for therapeutic connection (Hurley et al. 2022). Previous time and motion studies indicate that MHNs spend as little as 17% of a shift in direct consumer care, and this time is predominantly occupied with medication administration (Abt et al. 2022; Glantz et al. 2023; Goulter et al. 2015). This gap between the ideals of humanistic care and the reality of practice highlights the need for approaches that preserve time for ordinary, relational moments that are central to recovery‐oriented care.

In response to these challenges, the concept of protected engagement time (PET) was introduced in the United Kingdom (UK) in 2006 following the recommendation of the Chief Nursing Officer's review of mental health nursing (Department of Health 2006). Designed to ensure that there was dedicated time allocated on acute mental health units exclusively for MHNs to engage with consumers, during PET, the unit is closed to visitors, and MHNs refrain from administrative tasks (Kent 2005). This arrangement enables MHN to engage actively with consumers through one‐to‐one interactions, group activities, walks or shared meals (Dodd et al. 2018). Thus, PET represents a structured attempt to preserve time for meaningful therapeutic interaction and to promote the principles of humanistic care in acute mental health units. There is a paucity of research on PET as a mental health nursing intervention in Australian healthcare settings, with fewer studies specifically exploring older adult mental health units. While there is a scarcity of literature pertaining to PET, there is a need to map the little that is there to determine PET's relevance to acute older adult mental health units.

Aims

This scoping review aimed to map existing literature on PET and to examine its relevance to the Australian older adult acute mental health context. To achieve this aim, this review was guided by three key questions: To what extent does the evidence show the effectiveness of PET in enhancing therapeutic engagement, safety and quality of care?To what extent are there gaps in current literature concerning the application and relevance of PET to Australian older adult acute mental health units?To what extent are there other documented interventions that share characteristics of PET, namely the provision of dedicated, uninterrupted time for MHNs to engage meaningfully with consumers on acute units?

A scoping review was chosen to map the extent and depth of the existing literature on PET and identify comparable interventions with similar characteristics. That is, the provision of dedicated, uninterrupted time for MHN to engage with consumers. The review also aimed to identify gaps in knowledge relevant to the implementation of PET within Australian older adult acute mental health settings. Scoping reviews do not seek to critically appraise or synthesise the quality of the included studies, instead providing a structured framework for identifying, categorising and summarising existing evidence to inform future research (Arksey and O'Malley 2005). The scoping review approach is particularly well‐suited to areas of inquiry that are in the early stages of development. In the case of PET, a scoping review enables exploration of the extent and nature of available evidence, clarifying definitions and identifying areas where further research is needed.

Methods

This review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews (Peters et al. 2020) and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items Systematic reviews and Meta‐Analysis extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) checklist (Tricco et al. 2018), to ensure a transparent and replicable approach.

Steps of the Review

This scoping review follows the methodology guidance as outlined by (Peters et al. 2020) identify a clearly defined review question, establish inclusion criteria using the population, concept and context (PCC) framework, develop and execute a comprehensive search strategy, select relevant studies through a structured screening process, extract and chart data using a standardised approach, analyse the data and summarise and report the findings in relation to review objectives. This approach enables a rigorous and transparent mapping of existing evidence on PET within acute mental health settings, while identifying knowledge gaps to inform future research. Inclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria were empirical qualitative, quantitative or mixed methods studies that examine the intervention of PET or a similar intervention. To ensure a broad and comprehensive understanding of the literature, there were no limits placed on publication date or language. However, no studies published in languages other than English were identified.

Population

This review included MHNs working in acute mental health units and consumers receiving or having received care in such units. Studies including other health care professionals were only included if MHNs were directly involved in the intervention.

Concept

PET or interventions that have a similar structure: that is, scheduled uninterrupted time dedicated for MHN to therapeutically engage, that are distinct from routine clinical tasks with consumers.

Context

Studies were conducted on acute adult and older adult inpatient mental health units. International studies were included, though the primary focus was on the application to the Australian context.

Exclusion Criteria

Studies not addressing PET or comparable interventions, and those conducted in non‐acute or community mental health settings. Studies that solely focused on healthcare professionals other than MHN.

Search Strategy

An exploratory, pilot search was conducted in CINAHL and Google Scholar in February 2025 to identify relevant peer‐reviewed articles. The pilot search was structured around three key concepts: (1) protected engagement time, (2) mental health nursing and (3) engagement. The concepts were operationalised by using the Boolean operators ‘AND’ and truncated to capture variations in terminology. Keywords searched were ‘protected engagement time’ AND ‘mental health’ AND nurs* AND engagement. Keywords and index terms were extracted from the title, abstract and subject headings to build a comprehensive search strategy. During this pilot search, an intervention called ‘Time Together’ (Molin et al. 2017) was identified as being identical to PET and therefore was included in the search terms, as outlined in Table 1. Following the pilot search, the proposed search terms were discussed and refined collaboratively among the authors and a university librarian experienced in systematic searching. The final comprehensive search strategy, based on the concepts of MHNs, engagement and outcomes, which is detailed in Table 1, employed Boolean operators and was applied across multiple databases selected for their relevance to mental health, psychiatry and nursing, including PsycINFO, PubMed, Medline, Emcare and CINHAL. A search was also conducted in Google Scholar. These searches were conducted in April and May 2025.

Selection of Sources of Evidence

All identified citations were imported into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, 2025), and duplicate records were removed. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts against the inclusion and exclusion criteria, with disagreements resolved through consensus by the whole research team. Full‐text articles of studies included during the title and abstract search were then retrieved and assessed for inclusion, with two independent reviewers required for consensus. Where discrepancies were encountered, they were resolved through discussion with the third reviewer not involved in the original screening decision. Reference lists of included studies were examined to identify additional relevant studies using the Scopus journal database. Two further studies were found using this method, and one extra study was found by conducting a Google Scholar search. Results

Following the removal of duplicates, a total of 2064 articles were identified through the initial database search. Title and abstract screening resulted in 43 articles being retained for full‐text review. Of these, eight studies met the inclusion criteria and were extracted (Figure 2).

Of the eight studies included, three were conducted in the UK (Dodd et al. 2018; Smith et al. 2018; Thomson and Hamilton 2012), three in Sweden (Molin et al. 2018; Molin et al. 2019; Molin et al. 2020) and two in Spain (Moreno‐Poyato, El Abidi, et al. 2021; Moreno‐Poyato, Rodríguez‐Nogueira, et al. 2021). Only two focused specifically on PET (Dodd et al. 2018; Smith et al. 2018). Three studies referred to the intervention as Time Together (TT) (Molin et al. 2018; Molin et al. 2019; Molin et al. 2020), two as Reserved Therapeutic Space (RTS) (Moreno‐Poyato, El Abidi, et al. 2021; Moreno‐Poyato, Rodríguez‐Nogueira, et al. 2021) and one called the intervention Protected Therapeutic Time (Thomson and Hamilton 2012). Data Extraction

A data extraction form was developed in Microsoft Excel to capture relevant data from each of the included studies, with two reviewers independently extracting data and all members of the research team discussing the extraction to obtain consensus. Each journal article was reviewed in its entirety, and all content relating directly to the process and outcomes of PET was extracted for analysis.

Data Analysis

Content analysis was conducted to map the literature. Included studies were read and re‐read to ensure thorough familiarisation with the data. Inductive open coding was then applied to identify and extract content relevant to the scoping review questions. Codes were subsequently grouped into categories based on shared characteristics that were refined for consistency across the dataset.

Four categories, each containing two sub‐categories, were identified across the studies; these categories are presented in Table 3. Category 1: Relational engagement and therapeutic presence encompasses the relational skills and interpersonal relationships that underpin therapeutic communication. Category 2: Individualised and meaningful engagement emphasises the importance of consumer participation, agency and decision‐making in consumer‐centred personalised engagement. Category 3: Staffing and organisational structure addresses the influence of staffing attitudes, availability and organisational factors that may impact PET implementation. Category 4: Outcomes and broader impacts explores the environmental outcomes and indirect effects of PET on the unit environment, including its indirect effects on staff and consumers.

Category 1: Relational Engagement and Therapeutic Presence

The first category is concerned with the interpersonal foundations that meaningful therapeutic encounters have enabled. Interpersonal connection and the therapeutic presence of MHNs were portrayed as central to the effectiveness of PET. Two sub‐categories are presented: the first addresses the interpersonal behaviours and qualities that therapeutic engagement has fostered. The second highlights the intentional use of self, where personal attributes and authentic expression have been drawn upon to support connection. Together, these sub‐categories show how PET has been understood to strengthen relational engagement and person‐centred interaction.

Sub‐Category 1.1: Relational Therapeutic Engagement

Across the majority of the studies, MHNs emphasised that PET facilitated meaningful contact with consumers and created an atmosphere conducive to engagement (Dodd et al. 2018; Molin et al. 2019; Molin et al. 2020). MHNs expressed a desire to use PET effectively and described it as a valuable intervention. As one MHN reflected, ‘You want to engage; it's like we can engage in a brief period of time. So, for me we cannot waste this precious time’ (Dodd et al. 2018, 613).

Engagement was often supported through activities that were seen to promote therapeutic connection and professional nursing practice (Molin et al. 2018; Molin et al. 2019; Moreno‐Poyato, El Abidi, et al. 2021). MHNs framed PET as integral to their care, emphasising its alignment with core aspects of mental health nursing and being present for consumers (Molin et al. 2020). Four studies reported that PET contributed to strengthening the therapeutic relationship between MHN and consumers (Dodd et al. 2018; Molin et al. 2018; Moreno‐Poyato, El Abidi, et al. 2021; Moreno‐Poyato, Rodríguez‐Nogueira, et al. 2021). As Moreno‐Poyato, El Abidi, et al. 2021 found: ‘The participants confirmed the importance and usefulness of the intervention for the improvement of the therapeutic relationship between nurses and patients in hospitalization [sic] units’ (p. 788).

Sub‐Category 1.2: Using Self as a Therapeutic Tool

Molin et al. (2020) reported that PET created a space for MHNs to share aspects of themselves, enhancing the therapeutic relationship between MHNs and consumers. From a consumer perspective, MHNs were seen as less of clinicians concerned with illness and more as people engaged in authentic connection (Molin et al. 2019; Molin et al. 2020). MHNs similarly reflected consumers as more visible and acknowledged during PET (Molin et al. 2020). Non‐verbal presence was expressed as important to the therapeutic engagement, ‘… they may not talk to you, but even sitting next to them, it does make a difference in somebody's life’ (Dodd et al. 2018, 614).

Category 2: Individualised and Meaningful Engagement

This category concerns the ways that PET supports person‐centred interaction through activities that were shaped by consumers' preferences, strengths and choices. Individualisation and meaningfulness were portrayed as central to engagement, with participation strengthened when activities aligned with what consumers wanted. Two sub‐categories are presented; the first focuses on activity design and the importance of tailoring interventions to each individual. The second addresses empowerment and involvement, where consumer agency, choice and decision making were enabled. Together, these sub‐categories illustrate how PET has been understood to promote autonomy, reduce hierarchical distance and enhance the therapeutic relationship.

Sub‐Category 2.1: Activity Design and Meaningfulness

Evidence consistently highlighted that PET was most effective when activities were tailored to the preferences and strengths of consumers (Dodd et al. 2018; Molin et al. 2018). A standardised approach was found to be inappropriate, with consumers reluctant to participate in activities that did not align with their interests (Dodd et al. 2018; Smith et al. 2018). Activity selection was therefore identified as a key determinant of PET effectiveness (Thomson and Hamilton 2012). To design activities that were both individualised and appropriate, MHNs required knowledge of consumers' life stories, strengths and interests (Dodd et al. 2018). This person‐centred approach enabled engagement that built on consumers' capabilities rather than deficits associated with mental health difficulties. Further, as the MHN became familiar with consumers, PET activities could be tailored to accommodate individual preferences (Dodd et al. 2018). Molin et al. (2020) provided examples of activities perceived as meaningful, including multi‐sensory and social options. ‘Baking, cooking, making coffee, and preparing popcorn’, were described as enjoyable and valued by consumers; ‘card/board games and bingo’, supported socialisation and ‘outdoor walks were meaningful for those who preferred exercise’ (p. 1195).

Sub‐Category 2.2: Empowerment and Involvement

Empowerment and involvement were reflected in consumer choice, participation and decision‐making within therapeutic activities. Input from consumers was routinely sought in planning PET activities (Molin et al. 2019). PET was described to be more meaningful when it could be adapted to individual needs and cognitive abilities, highlighting the value placed upon personalised engagement (Moreno‐Poyato, Rodríguez‐Nogueira, et al. 2021; Dodd et al. 2018). The need for flexibility and choice was further emphasised by MHNs, especially when a person was acutely unwell (Molin et al. 2020).

Although choice was considered important, consumers felt supported when pressure to join PET activities was not applied. This was illustrated in consumer observation ‘patient wants to go out for a smoke first. The staff member answers: “Please come and join us after your smoke”, still friendly in her voice’ (Molin et al. 2018), 1703. Further, it was noted that choosing not to join the structured activity on offer but to sit with the group and participate in conversation during PET was found to be beneficial (Molin et al. 2018).

Category 3: Staffing and Organisational Structure

The third category encompasses the organisational conditions and workforce factors that shaped the feasibility and quality of PET delivery. Staff attitudes, readiness and availability were shown to influence whether PET could be enacted as intended, while structural features of the unit determined the extent to which protected time was supported or disrupted. The first sub‐category addresses staff perspectives; workload pressures and relational readiness required for meaningful engagement. The second sub‐category examines organisational routines, hierarchies and procedural arrangements that either enabled or constrained PET. Together, these sub‐categories illustrate how PET implementation was contingent on both individual and systemic conditions within acute mental health settings.

Sub‐Category 3.1: Staff Attitudes, Availability and Readiness

Staff attitudes, availability and readiness were identified as influential in the delivery of PET. The perceived value of PET shaped MHNs' willingness to engage, with some studies noting that PET was regarded as burdensome (Dodd et al. 2018; Molin et al. 2020), while others described it as rewarding and worthwhile (Moreno‐Poyato, Rodríguez‐Nogueira, et al. 2021; Thomson and Hamilton 2012). The relational stance of MHNs was critical to PET; one study found that listening and being perceived as available to consumers was valued more highly than the specific activities offered (Moreno‐Poyato, El Abidi, et al. 2021).

The approach taken by MHNs towards consumers was also found to influence participation. Proactive engagement was considered essential for consumers who were acutely unwell (Molin et al. 2020). While task‐focused practices limited opportunities, as MHNs were often unwilling to put aside their usual tasks and participate in PET because they felt unproductive (Molin et al. 2019; Molin et al. 2020). Some MHNs perceived PET activities as incongruent with their professional identity, expressing discomfort with tasks they considered to fall outside of the role. For example, some MHNs reported activities such as colouring in as felt inappropriate and ‘likening it to kindergarten’ (Molin et al. 2020, 1196). This contrast suggests that the relational value of PET may be shaped by staff perceptions of its legitimacy within routine care. Similarly, stress and fatigue following difficult shifts could reduce MHNs’ willingness to engage (Dodd et al. 2018). Conversely, when MHNs were actively engaged, PET was described as satisfying and a rewarding component of their role (Thomson and Hamilton 2012).

Workforce capacity was another determining factor of PET implementation. Short staffing and competing clinical demands consistently constrained PET delivery (Dodd et al. 2018; Molin et al. 2020; Smith et al. 2018; Thomson and Hamilton 2012). In some settings, this meant that only a limited number of MHNs could participate, and PET was deprioritised when clinical pressures escalated (Thomson and Hamilton 2012). Concerns were also raised about the potential impact on consumers not involved in PET, as reduced staffing could increase risk elsewhere on the unit (Smith et al. 2018). There was a division of labour noted, with some suggesting PET should be the responsibility of less qualified nurses, while the more highly trained specialised nurses were primarily engaged in containment and risk management (Molin et al. 2020).

Preparation and active facilitation were viewed as essential for meaningful PET. Poorly prepared sessions or interruptions, such as medication rounds, undermined the quality of PET (Molin et al. 2018). Consumer participation was further discouraged when MHNs adopted a passive role. Molin et al. (2019) observed that physical separation between MHN and consumers created distance, while Molin et al. (2018) reported that staff conversations directed towards one another, rather than consumers, prompted disengagement. Such attitudes had a contagion effect, with one study reporting that a single negative staff approach could affect the mood of the entire unit (Molin et al. 2019).

Sub‐Category 3.2: Structural and Organisational Factors

Structural and organisational factors address systemic, procedural and cultural elements that enable or hinder PET delivery. Although PET was designed to protect time for MHNs to engage with consumers, its boundaries were not respected by other professionals. One participant noted that ‘… breakfast will run from about 8 in the morning until 10. I've got doctors on the ward at 9 o'clock and I've got a cleaner’ (Dodd et al. 2018, 616). Similarly, psychiatrists were reported to schedule consumer meetings during PET, disregarding established unit routines (Molin et al. 2020). These examples suggest that PET was often considered lower status than medical or operational tasks, limiting the credibility of PET.

Ambiguity about the responsibility further complicated PET delivery. At times, MHNs deferred to other professions such as occupational therapists, physiotherapists or art therapists to provide structured activities during PET (Dodd et al. 2018). Where PET was introduced without consultation or clear operational guidance, its implementation was inconsistent and vulnerable to disruption (Dodd et al. 2018). By contrast, when leadership roles were designated, PET was more effective. MHNs’ leaders would motivate MHNs and consumers who did not want to attend PET (Molin et al. 2020). Also, prior planning of PET sessions and clear communication of any changes or cancellations were essential to consumer participation (Molin et al. 2020).

Category 4: Outcomes and Broader Impacts

The final category explores the wider effects of PET on the ward environment and on consumer experiences beyond the immediate activity itself. PET was found to influence the wider unit mood, interpersonal dynamics and therapeutic climate, while also contributing to individual shifts in emotion, coping and recovery. Two sub‐categories are presented: the first changes to the atmosphere and relational tone within the unit. The second examines the broader impacts of PET on consumer well‐being engagement and patterns of care. Together, these sub‐categories demonstrate how PET was understood to shape both environmental conditions and individual outcomes in subtle yet meaningful ways.

Subcategory 4.1: Unit Mood, Atmosphere and Environment

Unit mood, atmosphere and the broader environment were reported to be positively influenced by PET. Participants in one study described PET as being associated with a calmer unit atmosphere (Dodd et al. 2018). MHNs described that the time intentionally engaging with consumers contributed to a sense of cohesion within the unit (Dodd et al. 2018). The influence of PET was perceived to extend beyond the designated session, with a general calming effect on the unit (Molin et al. 2019; Molin et al. 2020). Consumers reported that the PET sessions added enjoyment to the unit and alleviated boredom, and their positive effect extended to others not directly involved in the activities, enhancing the overall unit environment (Molin et al. 2019; Molin et al. 2020). This is in contrast to before PET, where consumers stated that they would spend time in their bedroom with nothing to do, and the only interaction with MHNs was when administering medications (Dodd et al. 2018; Molin et al. 2018).

Studies included in this review suggest that PET created a sense of trust and improved the therapeutic relationship between MHNs and consumers. PET was viewed as reducing hierarchy and fostering a more collaborative stance between MHNs and consumers that enhanced trust (Molin et al. 2019; Molin et al. 2020; Thomson and Hamilton 2012). Professional boundaries were described as becoming less rigid, supporting a greater sense of mutual understanding (Molin et al. 2019; Molin et al. 2020). MHNs reported less tension in interactions, and consumers had a greater appreciation of their role. Overall, PET was perceived to enhance nurse–consumer relationships (Molin et al. 2020; Thomson and Hamilton 2012). MHNs also reported that PET contributed to risk reduction (Thomson and Hamilton 2012). However, Smith et al. (2018) found that there was no significant difference in the adverse events on older adult acute mental health units implementing PET.

Sub‐Category 4.2: Outcomes and Impact of PET

The outcomes and impact of PET were described as both subtle and profound. One study found that PET contributed to building trust, and MHNs were able to elicit emotional responses from consumers as consumers were able to speak freely with the MHNs (Dodd et al. 2018). Further, PET was described as facilitating the development of new coping strategies and enabling consumers to develop a sense of pride in themselves, contributing to consumers’ personal recovery (Molin et al. 2020). Molin et al. (2019) found that PET added structure to consumers' day and provided a reminder to consumers that returning home was achievable.

The included studies suggested that PET assisted in reducing stigma around mental illness. PET created an opportunity for conversations to shift from a consumer's mental health to topics of ordinary daily life (Molin et al. 2019). Consumers reported ‘feeling relieved from shame, guilt and feeling sensitive or stupid’ (Molin et al. 2019, 555) while developing social skills within a supportive environment (Molin et al. 2020). Additionally, in the studies included in this review, PET appeared to reduce the use of PRN medication. In one included study, MHNs reported that consumers were less likely to request medication since the introduction of PET (Molin et al. 2020). Another study noted that consumers reported that MHNs were less likely to offer medication as first‐line therapy when consumers experienced distress or became overwhelmed, instead encouraging consumers to engage in some form of activity (Molin et al. 2019).

Discussion

This scoping review aimed to map existing literature on PET and to examine its relevance to the Australian older adult acute mental health context. The findings of this review suggest that PET and similar interventions, such as TT and RTS, extend beyond a structural mechanism for MHN‐consumer engagement. PET may be understood as a relational model of care consistent with humanistic care principles, operational through the integration of person‐centred care and recovery‐oriented care. By preserving time for interpersonal engagement, PET enables individual interactions that represent consumer preferences (person‐centred) and fosters agency and meaningful participation (recovery‐oriented).

Humanistic Care

The concept of humanistic care has been described as the integration of ethical awareness, professional knowledge and emotional engagement that together enable individual and compassionate care (Zhang et al. 2021). Similarly, Taghinezhad et al. (2022), through an evolutionary concept analysis, identified nine defining attributes of humanistic care, including:… excellence in clinical literacy, creating a healing environment [a] comprehensive and unique viewpoint, contribution to clients' adaptation and flourishing of their talents, unrequited love and affection [nurse is empathetic, compassionate and altruistic], preservation of human dignity, real presence, constructive dynamic interaction and nurse's self‐care (p. 88).These qualities are consistent with the interpersonal and environmental conditions fostered by PET, suggesting that the intervention provides a structured mechanism through which humanistic nursing care can be realised. However, Kogstad et al. (2011) found that although consumers desire care grounded in humanistic principles, such an approach is in jeopardy of being lost in the biomedical model of mental health, indicating a need for a paradigm shift to ensure that humanistic care is sustained.

… excellence in clinical literacy, creating a healing environment [a] comprehensive and unique viewpoint, contribution to clients' adaptation and flourishing of their talents, unrequited love and affection [nurse is empathetic, compassionate and altruistic], preservation of human dignity, real presence, constructive dynamic interaction and nurse's self‐care (p. 88).

This review demonstrates that when time is intentionally preserved for MHN‐consumer engagement, the quality of the interactions on the acute mental health unit improved. Consumers reported feeling seen, heard and acknowledged, while MHNs described a greater understanding of the consumer's lived experience (Molin et al. 2020; Moreno‐Poyato, El Abidi, et al. 2021). These findings highlight that PET's therapeutic benefit arises from relational presence rather than procedural intervention. This aligns with Parse's Theory of Humanbecoming that places consumers at the centre of care. Humanbecoming emphasises MHNs’ need to be actively engaged in fostering trust so that consumers are empowered to be active participants in their own care (Lalwani and Jan 2023). Moreno‐Poyato, El Abidi, et al. (2021) reported that PET improved the therapeutic relationship between MHN and consumer, and Molin et al. (2020) found that this improved therapeutic relationship created a sense of calm on the unit. Together, these findings indicate that humanistic care functions through authentic connection, where time, presence and mutual recognition underpin therapeutic engagement rather than task‐based interactions.

PET may, therefore, be interpreted as a corrective mechanism within acute mental health units, where staff shortages, administrative tasks and the dominance of risk management have been seen as barriers to humanistic care. This interpretation is consistent with Wand et al. (2022), who posited that the future focus of mental health nursing should be on therapeutic engagement, collaboration and trauma‐informed care over custodial and pharmacologic approaches. Similarly, Hurley et al. (2022) identified that while MHNs possess sophisticated relational and emotional competencies, these skills are frequently underutilised due to institutional constraints that focus on safety and procedural compliance to be enacted within acute care contexts.

PET protects time for interpersonal engagement by shielding it from competing clinical priorities. In doing so, it creates a dedicated opportunity for MHNs to engage in conversation, shared activity and relational presence. Conceptually PET may be situated alongside activities such as Nursing Observations Through Engagement (New South Wales Government 2017; State of Victoria 2013), Hourly Rounding (Roustaei et al. 2023) or Protected Meal Times (Porter et al. 2017) that preserve therapeutic engagement; however, unlike PET, these approaches are not distinct from routine clinical tasks; rather, they embed engagement into routine clinical tasks. Nevertheless, these interventions share a common objective: to ensure that therapeutic interactions are not treated as incidental but are integral to nursing practice. Hurley et al. (2022) further emphasised that the underutilisation of MHNs arises from systematic and identity‐based constraints rather than a lack of capability. Embedding PET into routine practice can serve to integrate the therapeutic nature of MHN identity.

Ordinariness

The therapeutic value of PET can be seen through the lens of ordinariness. Ordinariness refers to the familiar aspects of daily life, those daily routines and social connections that structure the rhythm of daily life (Deveau 2024; Gjernes and Måseide 2020; Schrems 2025). During admission to acute mental health units, these ordinary rhythms are disrupted as consumers are separated from the familiar environments and their social connections (Deveau 2024). Through PET, elements of ordinariness are reintroduced into the clinical space. Activities such as sharing a cup of coffee, playing cards or going for a walk may appear mundane, yet can have a profound therapeutic significance (Deveau 2024; Schrems 2025). These actions reaffirm identity and reduce stigma associated with acute mental distress (Molin et al. 2020; Moreno‐Poyato, El Abidi, et al. 2021). Further, consumers in the studies included in this review reported that PET offered hope for the future, suggesting ordinariness provides a context for recovery.

This reconnection between the ordinary and the therapeutic echoes Jackson and Stevenson's (2000) grounded theory of ‘knowing you, knowing me’ that describes how MHNs continuously navigate between professional, engineered and ordinary modes of being. The authors describe that therapeutic care often arises from the ‘ordinary‐me’ stance, acts of friendliness, reciprocity and everyday humanity, rather than from formalised procedures. PET operationalises ‘ordinary‐me’ as a deliberate therapeutic stance that legitimises human connection through the ordinary.

Relational engagement was identified as fundamental to the therapeutic value of PET. Its effectiveness was found to depend largely on the relational skill of the MHN, including the therapeutic use of self. Consumers valued authenticity and reciprocity in interactions, while MHNs reported enhanced understanding of consumers’ experiences (Molin et al. 2019; Moreno‐Poyato, El Abidi, et al. 2021). When consumers were invited to choose or design activities, they experienced greater agency and involvement in their care. This participatory approach emphasises the humanistic aspect of respect for personal values and preferences (Dodd et al. 2018; Molin et al. 2019). Even small choices, such as whether to participate in PET or what activity to undertake, restored a degree of control, often diminished on acute mental health units. These findings support Jackson and Stevenson's (2000) position that anticipating and responding to individual needs with authenticity is central to therapeutic effectiveness.

Challenges

Despite these benefits, several challenges to the implementation and sustainability of PET were identified. Organisational and cultural factors emerged as a major barrier to PET, including competing clinical priorities, inadequate staffing and lack of managerial support. PET was viewed as a low priority, particularly when other disciplines or tasks encroached on scheduled PET (Dodd et al. 2018; Molin et al. 2020). Furthermore, some MHNs viewed activities offered during PET, such as colouring and playing cards, as contrary to their professional identity (Molin et al. 2020).

Leadership and role modelling were critical to the success of PET. This was also highlighted by Dodd et al. (2018), who found that some MHNs would look to other disciplines to provide structured activities during PET. While such collaboration could broaden activity options, it risks reinforcing the perception that PET was ancillary to MHNs' core work rather than a central component to therapeutic care. Mental health units where PET was advocated by senior nurses and embedded into routine reported more consistent engagement (Molin et al. 2020). Preparation, communication and flexibility were also critical in providing quality engagement. Therefore, PET should be understood both as an interpersonal and organisational intervention, requiring commitment on multiple levels. Despite these findings, it is clear that further research is required to identify the barriers and facilitators to PET implementation, including resource (staff) shortages and the imminent care needs of consumers that may compete with PET.

Relevance to Australia

Although no studies were identified from the Australian setting, the principles underpinning PET have relevance to local practice. PET has been found to reduce stigma, strengthen therapeutic relationships and develop a collaborative partnership between MHNs and consumers. These outcomes correspond with the Australian College of Mental Health Nurses' (2010) Standards of Practice, particularly Standard 2: Facilitating consumer participation in all aspects of care, Standard 3: developing a therapeutic relationship grounded in respect, hope and strengths and Standard 6: promoting social inclusion and actively addressing stigma. PET operationalises person‐centred and recovery‐focused care, supporting the workforce characteristics as outlined in the National Mental Health Workforce Strategy (Commonwealth of Australia 2022). Additionally, PET addresses findings from the Royal Commission into Victoria's Mental Health System (State of Victoria 2021) regarding the over‐reliance on pharmacological interventions and limited therapeutic engagement. PET also aligns with the Australian States and Territories Mental Health Acts, which emphasise autonomy, dignity and human rights (The Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Psychiatrists 2017). By structuring time for meaningful engagement, PET offers a practical mechanism to realise these legislative and policy aspirations in the Australian acute mental health setting.

Older Adult Mental Health Acute Units

Only two studies were found to refer to older adult mental health acute units (Dodd et al. 2018; Smith et al. 2018). Dodd et al. (2018) highlighted the need to adapt PET activities to individual cognitive and functional abilities and needs, reporting improvement in mood and reduction in anxiety. In contrast, Smith et al.'s (2018) quasi‐experimental study found no significant difference in adverse events between older adult mental health units that had implemented PET and those that had not. However, data regarding PET frequency, duration, attendance or activity type were not reported, limiting interpretation. This limited evidence underscores the need for further research examining PET within older adult acute mental health units.

Limitations

Although this is the first scoping review exploring PET and with relevance to the Australian context, there are several limitations across the studies included in this review. Implementation fidelity was rarely reported, and variations in how PET was delivered across settings were not consistently documented. Quantitative studies generally lacked control groups and relied on self‐reported data, which may have introduced measurement bias. Given the inconsistent terminology used to describe PET, related interventions may have resulted in the omission of relevant literature.

In terms of the review itself, the transferability of findings may be limited to the geographic regions, healthcare practices and cultural differences of the setting where studies were conducted, highlighting the need for further research into the applicability of PET to Australian settings. The limited volume of existing research on PET in mental health acute units reflects the broader gap in the literature and further work is needed to strengthen the evidence base.

Conclusion

This scoping review examined whether PET is a relevant evidence‐based intervention for Australian older adult acute mental health units. Eight international studies were identified, with none conducted in Australia. Only two studies were conducted in older adult mental health: one indicating that PET created a therapeutic relationship between MHN and consumer when implemented in a flexible, person‐centred manner, while the other found no significant difference in adverse events between those that introduced PET and those that did not. The findings suggest that PET offers a structured intervention where humanistic care may be realised. Humanistic care is a holistic, individualised practice that is responsive to the needs, wishes and expectations of the consumer. It values ordinariness, alongside meaningful interactions and empathetic, respectful and authentic care as a way of supporting recovery. PET has been demonstrated to support these values in acute mental health units by protecting time for relational and therapeutic engagement.

This is particularly important for the older adult population, who often are admitted to acute mental health units with experiences of bereavement, financial strain and diminished sense of purpose associated with retirement. PET has the potential to affirm older adults' identities by engaging with them as individuals. More than a scheduled activity, PET embodies humanistic care enacted through ordinary moments of presence and connection. By structuring time for relational engagement, PET provides a practical framework where Australian policy objectives may be realised in the older adult acute mental health units. Evaluation of PET within the Australian older adult mental health settings is warranted to determine how this approach could be integrated to enhance the therapeutic relationships, promote recovery and uphold dignity to some of the most vulnerable consumers in mental health care.

Relevance for Clinical Practice

The results of this scoping review indicate that PET is an MHN approach with the potential to enhance humanistic care in older adult acute mental health units. It facilitates a stronger therapeutic relationship between MHN and the consumer and supports consumers’ recovery. PET provides an uninterrupted period of time where the MHN can engage meaningfully with the consumer. These findings demonstrate that activities during PET need to be flexible and person‐centred. However, the evidence also suggests that MHNs lack structured guidance for implementing PET, and its significance may not be fully recognised by other professions, limiting the effectiveness of the intervention. These findings emphasise the importance of developing a PET model tailored specifically for the Australian older adult mental health context.
