Automatic diagnosis of osteonecrosis of the femoral head and segmentation of necrotic regions using deep learning at computed tomography: a real-world multicenter study
Authors: Hao Chen, Yi Zhou, Sisi Gao, Chenglin Liu, Hongzhong Xi, Changyuan Gu, Shaowei Liu, Peng Xue, Chunsheng Zhang, Yang Chen, Guangquan Sun, Xin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComputed tomography (CT) is essential for diagnosing osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) due to its superior visualization of osseous structures, yet CT-based deep learning (DL) models remain underexplored in real-world settings.MethodsThis retrospective multicenter study developed a two-stage DL model integrating Vision Transformer (ViT) and Swin Transformer (SwinT) for ONFH diagnosis and necrotic segmentation, using coronal CT images from 2,312 patients (53,828 images) across four hospitals from February 2023 to June 2024. The data from three hospitals were split 7:1:2 for training, validation, and internal testing, with the fourth serving as the external test set. Diagnostic performance was evaluated at both the slice and patient levels using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, and negative predictive value (NPV) while segmentation performance was assessed using the Dice coefficient and HD95. Human–computer comparison trials were conducted against three orthopaedic surgeons.ResultsAt the slice level, the model achieved robust performance with strong results in internal test set (AUC = 0.98, sensitivity = 98.91%, NPV = 98.66%, Dice = 72.64%, HD95 = 4.51 mm) and external test set (AUC = 0.97, sensitivity = 99.61%, NPV = 99.63%, Dice = 69.11%, HD95 = 5.27 mm). At the patient level, diagnostic performance remained high, with AUCs of 0.98 and 0.97 in the internal and external test sets, respectively. It outperformed surgeons in diagnosis and matched a 10-year-experienced attending in segmentation.ConclusionOur CT-based two-stage DL model exhibits high accuracy and strong generalizability for ONFH diagnosis and necrotic segmentation, providing a technically robust foundational framework with promising clinical translational potential for ONFH management.Trial registrationThis study was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry chictr.org.cn (Registration ID: ChiCTR2300067945, 01/02/2023).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10113-7.Keywords: Osteonecrosis of the femoral head, Computed tomography, Necrotic regions segmentation, Deep learning, Real-world

Introduction

Osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) is a debilitating disease characterized by osteocyte death due to reduced or interrupted blood supply to the femoral head, eventually leading to femoral head collapse and secondary osteoarthritis [1, 2]. ONFH has an insidious onset and progresses rapidly, primarily affecting young and middle-aged adults [3–6]. Its causes vary and may include trauma (such as fractures or dislocations), long-term use of corticosteroids [7], excessive alcohol intake [8], and certain medical conditions (such as sickle cell disease, lupus, or Gaucher's disease [9]). As its incidence continues to rise, ONFH is increasingly imposing a significant economic and social burden on healthcare systems worldwide [3–6, 10].

Treatment options for ONFH vary based on the extent of necrosis regions and the condition of affected femoral head. In the early to mid-stages, treatments includes protective weight-bearing, physical therapy, drug or surgical treatment to preserve hip [9, 11, 12]. In the advanced stage of ONFH, total hip arthroplasty (THA) is often the primary option [9]. Of note, despite THA is reliable option for end-stage osteoarthritis resulting from ONFH, complications of THA and the deficiencies of prosthesis durability remain challenging to address [13–16]. Early and appropriate treatment before femoral head collapse may slow ONFH progression and reduce the future need for THA [12, 15, 17]. Therefore, early screening and diagnosis of ONFH [18, 19], along with accurate evaluation of the extent of necrotic regions [20–23] to employ appropriate treatment strategies are crucial for the prevention and treatment of ONFH. However, this task is challenging and requiring extensive of training and practice. Necrotic regions can exhibit substantial variability in shape and appearance, and inter- and intra-observer variability can affect assessment reliability. Therefore, to improve diagnostic accuracy and precisely assess the extent of necrosis regions in ONFH, an effective and robust approach is needed.

Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning (DL), has recently attracted considerable attention in the field of ONFH. A variety of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based DL models have been developed for diagnosis [18, 19, 24–26], classification [21–23], and necrotic regions segmentation [20, 27], demonstrating promising potential for clinical application. MRI is the preferred diagnostic imaging modality for ONFH, and its high sensitivity for detecting early-stage, non-osseous changes, such as edema and inflammatory exudates in the femoral head. However, MRI has limitations in revealing bony structural changes such as regions of osteosclerosis surrounding the necrotic bone and repaired bone, as well as subchondral bone fracture, which are closely related to the prognosis of ONFH, and are key indicators for assessing ONFH severity and formulating treatment strategies [9, 12, 15].

Computed tomography (CT) is also widely used to diagnose and evaluate ONFH [15, 28, 29], which offers advantages of convenience, affordability, and accessibility, especially in remote areas. In particular, CT perfectly compensates for the shortcomings of MRI in the detailed visualization of osseous changes (e.g., osteosclerosis, subchondral fractures), which are critical for assessing ONFH severity and treatment planning [15, 30–33]. Among multi-planar CT reconstructions, the coronal sequences are clinically prioritized for ONFH evaluation, as it directly visualizes the femoral head’s weight-bearing zone (anterior–superior quadrant) and the spatial relationship between necrotic lesions and subchondral bone, which are two core factors for guiding clinical decision-making [17, 32]. For the purpose of efficient ONFH screening and preliminary necrotic region assessment, coronal 2D CT images provide sufficient anatomical information while minimizing multi-center protocol variability and annotation complexity, making it feasible for large-scale real-world validation. However, existing DL models for ONFH are almost exclusively MRI-based. To the best of our knowledge, there is no CT-based models for ONFH diagnostic, and only few DL models attempt to visualize or ambiguously localize necrotic regions in ONFH [28, 29], which are limited to single-center with a small dataset, limiting their adaptability in real-world clinical practice.

To address these challenges, we developed a fully automated, CT-based, two-stage DL model for diagnosing ONFH and segmenting necrotic regions. This model integrates two networks, Vision Transformer [34] (ViT) and Swin Transformer [35] (SwinT) for ONFH diagnosis and necrotic regions segmentation, respectively, as illustrated in Fig. 1. This model was trained on real-world CT datasets from different institutions across China, and we critically evaluated the model’s performance for ONFH diagnosis and segmenting necrotic regions on both internal and external test sets. Additionally, we conducted human–computer comparison trials to compare the model’s performance against that of orthopaedic surgeons to preliminarily assess its clinical application potential.

Materials and methods

Datasets

This study utilized hip CT images and clinical information from patients at four different hospitals in China: Hospital #1 (Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine), Hospital #2 (The Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine), Hospital #3 (Nanjing Hospital of Chinese Medicine), and Hospital #4 (Nanchang Hongdu Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine). From February 2023 to June 2024, data were collected from patients over 18 years with hip pain or undergoing routine medical examination. Hip CT data from Hospitals #1, #2, and #3 were used for model training, validation, and internal testing, while an external test set of hip CT data from Hospital #4 was used to evaluate the model’s performance. All patients were positioned supine with toes close together and internally rotated, and scanned with a length of approximately 400 mm, extending from 10 mm above the iliac crest to the middle femoral, to ensure complete visualization of the femoral head. The specific scanning parameters in participated hospitals are provided in Table S1.

Only coronal sequences were selected for analysis. Patients with low-quality hip CT scans or missing coronal sequences were excluded from the study. All CT sequences from patients with a definitive ONFH diagnosis were included in the ONFH dataset, whereas CT sequences from patients with hip tumors, implant-related surgeries, femoral head bone marrow oedema, hip fractures, and normal hips were included in the non-ONFH dataset.

Data preprocessing and ground truth

All hip CT data were exported from the PACS system in dicom format and desensitized. Due to the variation in CT imaging parameters across participating hospitals, all CT data were Z-score normalized and resampled with a layer thickness and spacing of 1 mm to improve model generalization. After preprocessing, the hip CT data in dicom format were imported into ITK-SNAP software (http://www.itksnap.org, V4.2.0). A radiologist (Shaowei Liu) with 12 years of experience in musculoskeletal radiology and a deputy chief orthopaedic surgeon (Guangquan Sun) with 15-year experience in ONFH independently performed ONFH diagnosis and annotated necrotic regions on coronal CT sequences. An orthopaedic specialist (Bin Du) with over 30 years of experience in ONFH reviewed and refined these annotations with reference to each hospital’s diagnostic reports. The finalized, specialist-reviewed sequences served as ground truth and were converted as CT coronal images in png format, along with the original hip coronal sequences, for use in training and evaluating the two-stage DL model.

Development and training of the DL model

The internal dataset consisting of data from Hospitals #1, #2, and #3 was randomly divided into training, validation, and internal test in a 7:1:2 ratio, with data split at the patient level to prevent data leakage.

The DL model is composed of two networks in a two-stage diagnostic and segmentation process (Fig. 2). In the first stage, a CT image is processed by a diagnostic network to classify it as ONFH or non-ONFH using thresholded probabilities from a SoftMax activation layer. If ONFH is detected, the second stage applies a segmentation network to delineate necrotic regions by thresholding pixel probabilities.

Network architectures of the DL model. a The architectures of Vision Transformer and b the architectures of Swin Transformer

The diagnostic stage is based on ViT and the segmentation stage is based on SwinT, both implemented in Pytorch (version 2.3.1, Compute Unified Device Architecture [CUDA] 12.1) on one 24-GB GeForce RTX 4090 (version 537.42; NVIDIA). Both models are initialized using the pretrained weights provided by the respective official GitHub repositories with default configurations (ViT: patch size 16 × 16; Swin Transformer: window size 7 × 7 with 4-stage hierarchy). For classification, input images were resized to 512 × 512 pixels, while for segmentation, models were trained on randomly cropped 512 × 512 patches. Training was conducted for 200 epochs (classification) and 300 epochs (segmentation). Cross entropy was used as the loss function for the diagnostic stage and binary cross entropy was used as the loss function for the segmentation stage. We used AdamW optimizer (learning rate = 0.0001, weight decay = 0.01) with cosine annealing schedule, batch sizes of 8, and standard data augmentation including random horizontal/vertical flipping (probability = 0.5), random rotation (± 15°), and brightness/contrast adjustment (± 0.2) was applied during two training stages to enhance model robustness. The best models were selected based on the best validated F-score and Dice coefficient (Dice) for the diagnostic and segmentation stages, respectively.

Human–computer comparison trials

To evaluate the performance of the model in diagnosis of ONFH and necrotic regions segmentation in a clinical context, human–computer comparison trials were conducted. The best-performing model from cross-test run was selected for final evaluation on the internal and external test sets. For comparison, three orthopaedic surgeons with different levels of experience (Chenglin Liu, an intern in residency training; Changyuan Gu, a resident with 5 years of experience; Xin Liu, an attending with 10 years of experience) independently diagnosed and segmented necrotic regions on coronal CT images from the internal and external test sets. Participants were only given access to coronal CT images and were blinded to other imaging modalities and clinical findings.

Data analysis

Model metrics were calculated using Scikit-learn (version 1.5.1; https://scikit-learn.org/stable/index.html). Diagnostic performance in the first stage was evaluated by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with the DeLong test used for statistical comparison of AUC values and bootstrapping applied for calculating 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Negative predictive value (NPV), sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1-score, all of which are proportional metrics derived from confusion matrices, complement the evaluation of diagnostic performance alongside AUC. The 95% CIs for these proportional metrics were calculated using the Wilson score interval method, a robust approach for binomial proportion estimation suitable for clinical diagnostic performance analysis. Diagnostic performance was assessed at both the slice level and the patient level. For patient-level evaluation, predictions were aggregated across all slices of each patient using a maximum probability strategy, where a patient was classified as ONFH-positive if the maximum slice-level probability exceeded the optimal threshold determined from the validation set.

For the second segmentation stage, performance was evaluated using the Dice coefficient (Dice), precision, recall, and the 95th percentile Hausdorff Distance (HD95). Physical scale calibration was performed based on the pixel spacing (mm/pixel) information embedded in dicom files to ensure clinical relevance, where HD95 was calculated in millimeters (mm) to reflect the actual spatial discrepancy between segmentation boundaries and ground truth. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to statistically compare Dice values. P < 0.05 was considered indicative of a statistically significant difference in both stages.

Subgroup analysis

To evaluate the model’s robustness across different clinical populations, we performed subgroup analyses based on sex (male vs. female) and age (< 40, 40–60, and > 60 years). Diagnostic metrics (AUC, sensitivity, specificity) and segmentation metrics (Dice, HD95) were calculated separately for each cohort to ensure the model’s equitable performance across diverse patient profiles.

Results

Data set characteristics

The patient inclusion process is shown in Fig. 3. Between February 2023 and June 2024, a total of 2,312 patients with 53,828 coronal CT images from four hospitals were finally included in this multicenter study. Among these patients, 1,189 (24,534 coronal CT images) were diagnosed as ONFH and 1,123 (29,294 coronal CT images) were non-ONFH cases. Table S2 summarizes the baseline clinical characteristics of each group.

Multicenter data composition diagram

Table S3 presents the demographics details for patients in both diagnostic and segmentation stages. In particular, segmentation was performed exclusively on definitive ONFH cases from the diagnostic dataset. Patient characteristics, including age, sex, Japanese Investigation Committee (JIC) classification, and Association Research Circulation Osseous (ARCO) stage, showed no statistically significant differences among the four datasets (all p > 0.05), confirming balanced allocation of cases with diverse clinical features. Specifically, each dataset comprehensively encompasses low-to-moderate collapse-risk cases (JIC A/B/C1) and high-risk cases (JIC C2), as well as early-stage (ARCO Ⅰ-Ⅱ) and advanced-stage (ARCO Ⅲ-Ⅳ) ONFH, ensuring the model was trained and validated on a fully representative cohort. Of note, the above ARCO stage and JIC classification data were only used to describe the balance of clinical characteristics across cohorts, and were not used to validate the model against clinical staging, prognostic stratification or collapse risk assessment in this study.

Performance of the DL model

In the diagnostic stage, the model’s gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) confirmed that ViT accurately targeted necrotic regions within the femoral head (Fig. 4). As shown in the ROC curves (Fig. 5) and detailed metrics (Table 1 and Table S4), the model achieved robust performance. The internal test set yielded an AUC of 0.98 (95% CI: 0.96–1.00), sensitivity of 98.91% (95% CI: 97.93–99.89%), and NPV of 98.66% (95% CI: 97.58–99.74%), while the external test set maintained an AUC of 0.97 (95% CI: 0.95–0.99), sensitivity of 99.61% (95% CI: 99.22–100%), and NPV of 99.63% (95% CI: 99.25–100.00).

Performance of the DL model in diagnosis stage and segmentation stage

At the patient level, the model maintained high diagnostic accuracy (Table S5), achieving an AUC of 0.98 (95% CI: 0.96–1.00) in the internal test set and 0.97 (95% CI: 0.95–0.99) in the external test set, with sensitivities of 99.21% and 99.70%, and NPVs of 99.10% and 99.80%, respectively (Fig. 5d–f).

In the segmentation stage, the model demonstrated satisfactory capability in delineating necrotic regions (Fig. 6), with results showing good alignment with ground truth annotations. The added transparent overlay visualizations further aid in illustrating boundary accuracy and spatial correspondence between segmentation outputs and references. Core metrics (Table 1) demonstrated favorable performance: the internal test set achieved a Dice coefficient of 72.64% (95% CI: 64.22–81.35%) and HD95 of 4.51 mm, while the external test set had a Dice coefficient of 69.11% (95% CI: 62.88–75.30%) and HD95 of 5.27 mm. Training and validation loss curves (Figure S1) for both diagnostic and segmentation stages confirm stable convergence with no obvious overfitting.

Performance of the model in different subgroups

To evaluate the model’s robustness across clinically meaningful populations, we conducted subgroup analysis based on sex and age. The model exhibited consistent and reliable performance across male and female patients, with key metrics showing no substantial discrepancies (Table 2 and Table S6): male and female patients had diagnostic AUCs ≥ 0.97 and segmentation Dice coefficients ≥ 68.72% in both test sets, and female patients’ diagnostic performance in the external test set remained stable and aligned with that of male patients. For age subgroups (< 40, 40–60, > 60 years), the model retained stable diagnostic accuracy (AUC ≥ 0.96 in both test sets, Table 3 and Table S7), with minor variations in precision and NPV aligning with clinical ONFH risk distribution but not compromising utility. In segmentation, Dice coefficients remained above 68.32% across all age groups, with HD95 values within clinically acceptable ranges (≤ 5.54 mm).

Patient-level subgroup analyses further confirmed these findings. Human–computer comparison trials with orthopaedic surgeons of varying experience levels

To verify clinical applicability, we compared the model’s performance against three orthopaedic surgeons with distinct experience levels. In the diagnostic stage, the model consistently delivered superior performance across core metrics (Table 4): its AUC (0.98) and sensitivity (98.91%) in the internal test set outperformed the 10-year experience attending physician (AUC: 0.91; sensitivity: 96.20%), resident (AUC: 0.88; sensitivity: 91.30%), and intern (AUC: 0.83; sensitivity: 82.61%), with detailed classification data in Table S10 supporting its reliable case identification and low false-negative rates. In the segmentation stage, the model’s Dice coefficient (72.64% in internal test set) was comparable to the attending physician (70.82%) and significantly higher than the resident (67.68%) and intern (59.57%), with core metrics reflecting favorable region overlap and boundary accuracy (Table 5).

Discussion

Definitive diagnosis and accurate evaluation of the extent of necrotic regions are important prerequisites for guiding ONFH treatment [9, 12, 13, 15, 17]. This study is the first to integrate two DL networks, ViT and SwinT, to construct a fully automatic, CT-based, two-stage DL model for ONFH diagnosis and necrotic regions segmentation. The model demonstrated reliable overall performance, consistent utility across key clinical subgroups, and favorable performance compared to orthopaedic surgeons, underscoring its technically robust performance and promising clinical translational value as a foundational framework for ONFH imaging assessment.

Previous studies [18, 23, 25–27] focused on MRI-based DL models to assist in the diagnosis, segmentation, and classification or grading of ONFH. Despite the effectiveness of these MRI-based DL models, a critical consideration that ONFH is an orthopaedic disease intricately linked to changes in bony structures is overlooked. The degree of high pressure and edema within the femoral head, although important, is not a determining factor in the prognosis of ONFH. While MRI is sensitive for detecting ONFH and illustrating the extent of inflammatory edema in the necrotic regions well, it often overestimates the extent of necrosis, which potentially leads to treatment misdirection due to a deviation between model predictions and actual necrotic regions.

CT imaging with clear visualization of bony structures is increasingly significant in the diagnosis of ONFH and assisting decision-making for treatment [28, 29]. In this study, we chose coronal 2D CT images as model input, a choice that balances clinical relevance and real-world feasibility. Firstly, for the core goal of ONFH screening and preliminary necrotic region evaluation, the coronal sequences directly visualize the femoral head’s weight-bearing zone (the most common site of ONFH necrosis) and the spatial relationship between necrotic lesions and subchondral bone, both of which are critical factors guiding clinical triage and initial treatment decisions [15, 17]. Secondly, this choice is further supported by practical considerations. Coronal reconstruction is a routine clinical post-processing step, minimizing image orientation variability across heterogeneous multicenter CT scanners and ensuring protocol standardization. Compared with time-consuming 3D volumetric annotation, coronal 2D annotation significantly reduces workload while maintaining reliability through multi-level expert review, making large-scale multicenter data collection feasible. Additionally, the model mimics clinical radiologists’ reading habits of prioritizing coronal images for rapid assessment, which enhances its acceptance in routine practice.

In this study, ViT and SwinT were applied for ONFH diagnosis and necrotic regions segmentation, respectively. In contrast to convolutional neural networks (CNN) limited to local feature capture, ViT leverages global relationship modelling while integrating CNN-derived architectural and training strengths (e.g., pyramid structure, residual connections, knowledge distillation) to expand receptive fields and capture richer feature information, enabling superior diagnostic performance [34]. For segmentation, SwinT fuses CNN and transformer merits through a hierarchical architecture and shifted window self-attention, reducing computational complexity while efficiently capturing fine details and overall structure which is ideal for necrotic region delineation [35–38]. Complemented by a dataset covering diverse clinical characteristics, strict data splitting to avoid leakage, subgroup analysis confirming cross-population robustness, human–computer comparison validating clinical utility, and training curves demonstrating stable convergence, the model maintains excellent performance across heterogeneous multi-center CT equipment and scanning parameters. Despite relying on 2D coronal images, this approach exhibits important clinical application potential by enabling high-efficiency ONFH screening in resource-limited settings via accessible coronal CT, minimizing false negatives critical for early detection, providing reliable preliminary necrotic region assessment to guide further management, reducing experience-dependent interpretation variability thereby narrowing the diagnostic gap between medical institutions and laying a technical foundation for subsequent real-world clinical translation.

This study has several limitations. First, the single-sequence 2D CT input fails to capture the 3D spatial characteristics of necrotic lesions. Notably, the current study does not derive necrotic volume, area ratio, weight-bearing zone involvement, or collapse-risk indicators from the segmentation outputs, which are key clinically actionable variables for treatment decision-making [12, 17]. Second, the retrospective design and external validation restricted to a single additional hospital compromise the verification of real-world performance. To address these shortcomings, we will develop a multi-view fusion model integrating coronal, axial, and sagittal 2D CT images via cross-attention mechanisms, and construct a 3D volumetric segmentation model based on 3D CNNs or 3D ViT for accurate necrotic volume measurement and lesion characterization. Beyond algorithmic development, translating these segmentation masks into actionable clinical variables is the next essential step. Precise automated segmentation is the fundamental prerequisite for calculating necrotic area ratios or determining weight-bearing zone involvement, critical factors for deciding between hip-preserving surgery and total hip arthroplasty. Future work will integrate these quantitative surrogates, combined with radiomics and clinical data, to build an AI-assisted tool for predicting treatment outcomes and guiding ONFH therapy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we successfully developed a fully automatic, CT-based, two-stage DL model for diagnosing ONFH and segmenting necrotic regions. Rigorously validated on a large-scale, real-world multicenter CT dataset, the model demonstrated outstanding diagnostic and segmentation performance, comparable to or surpassing that of an attending orthopaedic surgeon. Crucially, its robust performance across diverse clinical settings confirms strong generalizability. This model has the potential to improve ONFH diagnostic accuracy, streamline clinical workflow, and optimize hospital resource allocation for ONFH imaging screening. The accurate necrotic region segmentation provided by the model lays a solid technical foundation for subsequent clinical endpoint validation and treatment decision support research, making it a promising foundational framework with broad translational potential in ONFH management.
