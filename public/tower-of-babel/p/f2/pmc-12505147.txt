Ethnic disparities in prevalence and screening rates of sexually transmitted infections
Authors: Perla Schmidt Henriquez, Markus M Heimesaat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSexually transmitted infections (STIs) represent a major global health concern, with certain ethnic communities being disproportionately affected. This review summarizes recent evidence of ethnic disparities in STI prevalence and screening rates, with most data originating from the United States. The findings confirm the fact that certain ethnic communities such as Black and Hispanic display higher prevalence rates of STIs. Yet the data on screening behavior reveal a striking contradiction. While some studies report lower testing rates among these communities, others demonstrate that Black and Hispanic individuals reported the highest testing engagement compared to other ethnic groups. If testing behavior alone explained these disparities, a clearer correlation between screening and infection rates would be expected. Instead, that paradox might suggest a complex interplay with additional factors included in this review such as, for example, institutional racism, socioeconomic inequalities, differences in sexual and healthcare-seeking behavior, traumatic experiences, and limited access to healthcare. In conclusion, given the global burden caused by STIs, the ongoing health disparities in many parts of the world indicate an urgent need for further investigations into the mechanisms behind these disparities and to identify factors that yet may have been overlooked.Keywords: sexually transmitted infections (STIs), sexually transmitted diseases, STI screening, ethnic disparities, infection prevention and intervention strategies

Introduction

Race and ethnicity

In this paper, the term ethnicity is used instead of race since the idea that there are separate human races has been widely rejected by genetic and anthropological research [1]. Human genetic variation does not suit the definition and traditional classification of races. Since race in humans has no biological basis, it is more a socially constructed concept. Since most publications use the term race where the term ethnicity is used in this review, it refers to the definition of race provided by the original papers that is understood as perceived physical traits, ancestry, and lived experiences of discrimination or inequality. The continued use of race as a descriptive category bears the risk of reinforcing outdated and scientifically invalid ideas of human difference [1].

Global extent of sexually transmitted infections (STIs)

Worldwide, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infections, viral hepatitis, and other STIs are responsible for over 2.3 million deaths annually. Moreover, these infections lead to more than 1 million new cases each day and contribute to 1.2 million new cancer diagnoses each year [2]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) estimates from 2020, there were around 374 million new cases of four major STIs, namely 156 million infections caused by Trichomonas vaginalis; 129 million by Chlamydia trachomatis; 82 million by Neisseria gonorrhoeae, and 7.1 million by Treponema pallidum (syphilis). In the same year, it was estimated that over 520 million individuals were living with genital herpes, while approximately 300 million women were infected with the human papillomavirus (HPV) [3].

More than 30 different types of bacteria, viruses, and parasites can be sexually transmitted following vaginal, anal, and oral intercourse. Some infections can also be passed from mother to child during pregnancy, childbirth, and breastfeeding. Among these, eight pathogens cause the majority of STI cases with hepatitis B virus, herpes simplex virus (HSV), HIV, and HPV as viral infectious agents. Four of them, namely T. pallidum, N. gonorrhoeae, C. trachomatis, and T. vaginalis are currently curable. The infections bring further complications: Herpes, N. gonorrhoeae, and T. pallidum, for instance, can increase the risk of a HIV infection. The transmission of some STIs from mother to child can increase the risk of various perinatal complications, stillbirth, and neonatal death. C. trachomatis and N. gonorrhoeae, for instance, can lead to pelvic inflammatory disease, which can result in infertility. Furthermore, HPV infection can cause cervical cancer, while hepatitis B accounted for 1.1 million deaths mostly from cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma in 2022 [3].

Disparities in STI distribution and screening

It is evident that certain human population groups are particularly vulnerable and disproportionately affected by STIs. Low- and middle-income countries experience the highest STI burden worldwide. Particularly women, adolescents, marginalized and underserved populations are disproportionately affected [4]. 48.2% of cases of C. trachomatis, N. gonorrhoeae, and T. pallidum infections reported in 2023 were among adolescents and young adults (15–24 years of age) [5], while young Black women had almost four times the risk of having STIs compared to young White women [6]. Even though in 2023 non-Hispanic Black or African American people made up only 12.6% of the U.S. population, they represented 32.4% of all reported cases of C. trachomatis, N. gonorrhoeae, and T. pallidum [5]. This highlights relevant ethnic disparities, that were also discussed in the United Nations High Level Meeting on HIV in 2020. As a result, addressing these inequalities contributing to the high prevalence must become a central priority [2].

Viral hepatitis also disproportionately affects economically disadvantaged individuals, displaced populations, migrants, and rural communities [2, 4]. Additional groups facing higher risks of acquiring STIs including hepatitis are men who have sex with men (MSM), people who inject drugs, sex workers, transgender individuals, and people in prison. When including their partners, these groups collectively account for approximately 62% of new HIV infections [2].

The high prevalence and disparities among these groups are not a coincidence and the unexplained mechanisms leave room for the assumption of ethnic disparities. The unequal distribution of infection rates, particularly among some ethnic populations reflect deeper, unmet structural and social needs. It is therefore even more important to shed light on the epidemiological dynamics which involve multiple parameters that must be carefully examined to reduce the risk of infection for marginalized and vulnerable groups. One parameter is STI screening/testing, which, however, is not practiced equally across all groups. Testing practices often vary depending on ethnicity, reinforcing persistent inequalities in infection rates [7–11].

Objectives

This review aims to provide recent evidence for ethnic disparities in the prevalence and screening rates of STIs. Our work explores the fundamental causes of unequal prevalence rates and the multiple social and structural levels that enable and sustain these disparities and influence the screening rates in some ethnic communities. Given that most available data originate from the United States (U.S.), this review will primarily focus on the U.S., although it is important to note that STIs represent a significant global health issue.

Methods

The search for literature was made from June 10th to June 15th, 2025, using the search engine “PubMed” by the United States National Library of Medicine. The search term racial “AND” ethnic “AND” disparities “AND” STI screening “NOT” review provided 28 results.

After careful screening of the literature, all review articles were excluded using the search term “NOT” review to ensure only original studies were included. Additionally, studies that did not focus on STIs, addressed other topics or did not collect data related to ethnic disparities in STI screening were excluded. This process led to the exclusion of 6 studies, resulting in a total of 22 studies that were included in this review.

Results

Prevalence of STIs among ethnic populations

Between 2019 and 2021, a study was conducted in Baltimore, Maryland, investigating female individuals aged 13–25 years with pelvic inflammatory disease, that mostly were African American and from low-income households. The baseline STI rates the authors registered were relatively high with 45% caused by Mycoplasma genitalium; 31% by C. trachomatis; 25% by N. gonorrhoeae; and 11% by T. vaginalis [12]. According to a national survey from 2008 to 2019, Black and Hispanic women were more likely to report C. trachomatis and N. gonorrhoeae than White women, whereas 4.8% of women reported C. trachomatis and 3.1% N. gonorrhoeae, with the highest rates among Black women reported between 2017 and 2019 [13]. In that context, C. trachomatis infections were among the most frequently reported infectious diseases in the U.S. [14], with young Black men (15–26 years) being disproportionately affected [15]. The overall prevalence of C. trachomatis infections in Baltimore (U.S.) was 3.9% among 15–35-year-olds, according to data from 2006–2009 [13]. Also in this survey, almost all C. trachomatis positive cases were found among Black participants. Whereas non-Black study participants showed a 0.7% positivity rate, Black participants displayed a rate as high as 5.8%. After adjusting for sociodemographic, behavioral, and health-related factors, Black ethnicity remained a strong predictor of infection, with Black participants having a 4.8 times higher risk of infection compared to non-Black individuals [14]. A similar pattern was observed in England, where test results were obtained from women aged 16–24 between 2012 and 2019. Here, also Black women showed higher test positivity rates if compared to White women (i.e., 1.24 times and 1.92 times in case of C. trachomatis and N. gonorrhoeae infections, respectively) [16].

One of the most common infectious agents causing STIs in the U.S., besides C. trachomatis, is herpes simplex virus type 2 (HSV-2), also being most prevalent among Blacks [17]. Furthermore, a study published in 2008 examined women at the ages of 13–35 years who were considered at high risk for STIs and investigated if the association between bacterial vaginosis and the likelihood of acquiring a STI differed by ethnicity. At the start of the study, 33% of African American women, 31% of Hispanic women, and 24 % of White women suffered from bacterial vaginosis. To compare how much more likely women acquired STI depending on their bacterial vaginosis status and ethnicity, the researchers calculated the adjusted hazard ratio (aHR). White women without bacterial vaginosis were the reference group, with a risk set to 1.0. With a bacterial vaginosis-positive status, the patients had an aHR of 0.59 (indicating that the STI risk was a bit lower). African American women, however, displayed an almost doubled risk as indicated by an aHR of 1.96. When suffering from bacterial vaginosis the aHR increased up to 2.86 making them nearly three times more likely to get a STI than White women without bacterial vaginosis [18].

An additional hazard among STIs affecting women is HPV causing more then 99% of cervical cancers. Potential prognostic factors for cervical cancer patients also here include ethnicity along with a disproportional higher mortality rate in Blacks, Hispanics, and women in low-resource areas [19].

In addition, states of emergency such as COVID-19 have shown to contribute to increased outbreak risks among vulnerable groups, further amplifying existing STI-related health disparities [15].

HIV prevalence and distribution among ethnic populations

From 2010 to 2018, a significant decrease in HIV incidence among MSM was observed in New York. While the incidence was 2.82 per 100 person-years in 2010, it dropped to 0.82 in 2018. Among White MSM, incidences declined from 1.73 to 0.31, and among Black MSM, from 3.79 to 2.19. Even though this might indicate a progress, the ethnic disparities worsened. A clear increase in ethnic disparities is observed when comparing adjusted incidence rate ratios (aIRRs) over the time. Between 2010 and 2012, Black and Latino MSM were 1.8 and 1.4 times more likely to be HIV infected than White MSM. By 2016–2018 the disparities had almost tripled, with aIRRs increasing from 1.8 to 6.0 for Black MSM and from 1.4 to 4.0 for Latino MSM, if compared to White MSM [19]. From 2010 to 2012, a similar epidemiological distribution of HIV infection rates was observed in Atlanta. During that period, 43% of the Black MSM tested were HIV positive, compared to 13% among white MSM. This trend applies not only to HIV, but also other STIs like syphilis as well as rectal C. trachomatis and N. gonorrhoeae infections in which Black MSM showed a 3–4 times higher risk as compared to White MSM [21]. According to Canadian research from 2019, men subjected to syphilis, genital warts or genital ulcers had a higher chance of being HIV positive [22]. The study found that the prevalence of HIV (9.2%), T. pallidum (7.2%), hepatitis B (2.7%), and high-risk type HPV infections, both penile (21.3%) and anal (8.4%), was notably high among Black men. These infection rates were even higher among MSM compared to those who had sex with women only. Both, T. pallidum and high-risk type HPV infections were more common among HIV positive men indicating an association of past STI diagnoses and HIV infections. Specifically, men with a syphilis history were 6.48 times more likely to be HIV-positive than men without such a history [22]. Hence, these data show that individuals from certain ethnic backgrounds, those with co-infections, and MSM represent vulnerable groups at increased risk of acquiring HIV and other STIs.

However, vulnerability to STIs including HIV is not limited to these groups, since in 2014 women formed 19% of new HIV diagnoses in the U.S., with African American and Hispanic women representing a disproportionate burden [23]. A community-based analysis in San Diego from 2008–2014 included data from women with a median age of 32 years of whom 39.4% were identified as non-Hispanic White, 36.3% as Hispanic, and 11.8% as Black. Despite relatively low overall HIV prevalence in the general female population in San Diego (i.e., 0.09%), the study found a nearly eight times higher prevalence (0.7%) among the women tested, indicating that these females represented a high-risk group for HIV infection. Although Hispanic women made up only 36.3% of the tested population, they accounted for 82.0% of new HIV diagnoses constituting a statistically significant overrepresentation (P = 0.008) [23].

Ethnic disparities in STI screening rates

Screening is a key method representing one of the most effective ways to reduce the prevalence of STIs [13]. Therefore, it is intriguing to explore whether the disparities in STI prevalence between different ethnic groups are also reflected by their testing behavior. If certain groups were less likely to get tested, that might at least partly explain why some ethnic communities show higher infection rates. Indeed, several studies support this assumption. Research done in Atlanta between 2010 and 2012, for example, revealed that 89.2% of Black MSM had ever been tested for HIV in their lifetime as compared to 94.2% of White MSM. Such a difference was also found in recent testing since only 66.3% of Black MSM had been tested within the past 12 months versus 73.6% of White MSM [21]. Patterns of STI testing varying by ethnicity have also been observed in women, showing that Black and Asian women had decreased odds of using frequent STI testing as a protective strategy as indicated by an adjusted odds ratio (aOR) for Black women of 0.43 (confidence interval: 0.25–0.75) and an aOR of 0.53 for Asian women (confidence interval: 0.30–0.92) compared to White women [22].

During the COVID-19 pandemic, STI screening rates among adolescents dropped by 28 percentage points when the pandemic started, but overall screening rates returned to pre-pandemic levels by early 2021, but except for non-Hispanic Black individuals among whom screening rates remained lower, with a 3.5–3.6 percentage point decline compared to pre-pandemic values. The decrease in screening rates (i.e., 9.9 percentage points) was, however, most pronounced in adolescents living in socioeconomically and educationally disadvantaged neighborhoods [25].

Interestingly, other studies present a different picture, with evidence suggesting that ethnic minorities may be tested more frequently than White individuals [13, 16, 26–28]. According to an American National Survey from 2008–2019 [13], Black females consistently reported the highest rates of STI testing compared to other ethnic groups. From 2011–2013, 55.3% of Black females reported having been tested for STIs in the past 12 months, compared to 34.2% of Hispanic females and 27.2% of White females. By 2017–2019, testing rates had increased to 58% for Black females, 35.4% for Hispanic females, and 30.5% for White females. Among men, a similar pattern was observed, although with lower overall testing rates. From 2011 to 2013, 33.9% of non-Hispanic Black men reported STI testing in the past year, compared to 18.9% of Hispanic men and 13.3% of non-Hispanic White men. By 2017–2019, testing rates slightly declined across all groups, with 32.5% for non-Hispanic Black men, 15.4% for Hispanic men, and only 10.1% for non-Hispanic White men. These findings highlight that Black individuals consistently had the highest engagement in testing [13].

A study examining disparities in sexual and reproductive health service receipt by sex of sexual contacts and ethnicity among women aged 15–19 in the U.S. between 2011–2019, showed that Black adolescents were 1.44 times more likely to receive STI testing and 2.13 times more likely to receive HIV testing than White adolescents. When looking at ethnic differences in access to contraception, the disparities were alarming. The study found that Black and Hispanic adolescent girls were significantly less likely to receive a contraceptive method and counseling than White girls. 58.2% of non-Hispanic White girls received a contraceptive method in the last year, whereas in contrast, only 41.7% of Black girls and just 33.9% of Hispanic girls received one [27]. In that context other data showed that White women had nearly three times the odds of reporting that a healthcare provider spoke to them about condoms [24]. A separate study that examined predictors of STI testing among adolescent females presenting to an urban pediatric emergency department with symptoms potentially suggestive of an STI, also found that African American ethnicity was a consistent factor associated with higher STI testing. Despite U.S. Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidelines that recommend testing for all patients with STI-related symptoms, a major number of symptomatic adolescents were not tested. Whereas only 10% of Black or African American patients were not tested, more than 30% of White patients were missed. The authors concluded that Black adolescents may be more likely to receive appropriate testing in this clinical setting but on the other hand, missing such a large percentage of potentially symptomatic patients had to be regarded as quite concerning [28].

Factors influencing ethnic disparities in STI screening rates

Several factors have been identified as influencing screening behavior, including ethnicity, a lifetime history of STI diagnosis, health insurance status, education level, and employment [26]. Some suggest that the higher STI-testing behavior might be due to ethnical and xenophobic stereotypes among healthcare providers and institutions or due to a history of public health hyper-surveillance of Black and Latinx communities [26]. On the other hand, Black communities may avoid STI screening due to fear of stigmatization, discrimination, homophobia, judgement or legal prosecution under HIV non-disclosure laws, which results in low screening rates among these individuals [22].

Factors influencing ethnic disparities in STI prevalence

Some studies tried to investigate structural mechanisms contributing to and fundamental reasons behind the observed ethnic disparities in STI prevalence. In fact, several factors could be identified. One of them is risky sexual behavior (RSB). For example, 48.4% of Black respondents reported having multiple sexual partners in the past year, compared to 18.0% of non-Black respondents. Importantly, even after adjusting for other factors such as age, gender, education, sexual behavior, and health status these disparities persisted. Black ethnicity stayed a strong independent predictor of infection, with Black participants having 4.8 times higher odds of STIs compared to non-Black participants. The study also underlines that individual behaviors alone do not fully explain these ethnic disparities. Structural factors, such as “ethnically” segregated partner selection contribute to the ongoing inequities [14]. A study investigating RSB among Hispanic women revealed that Hispanic women were more likely than other women to be diagnosed with HIV despite significantly lower rates of RSB [23]. Yet, they were more likely to report sex with an HIV-infected partner, which may explain their increased infection rates [23]. A study titled “Gender and race informed pathways from childhood sexual abuse to sexually transmitted infections” used data from a nationally representative U.S. sample and found that childhood sexual abuse (CSA) increases the risk of STIs, making CSA a direct predictor of STIs (P < 0.01) [29]. This relationship was partly mediated by RSB given that individuals with a history of CSA were more likely to engage in sexual behavior, which in turn elevated their STI risk. This mediation pathway, however, varied by ethnicity and gender. It was statistically significant for Black females (CI: 0.01–0.03), non-Black females (CI: 0.02–0.05), and non-Black males (CI: 0.01–0.02), but not for Black males (CI: 0.00–0.01). Although Black and Latinx female participants reported lower average levels of RSB compared to other groups, Black females showed the strongest pathway from CSA through RSB to STI. This indicates that, despite engaging in less RSB on average, Black females were disproportionately more affected. This pattern suggests a vulnerability in Black females, where even moderate levels of RSB were more likely to result in STIs. Furthermore, CSA prevalence was highest among Latinx females (P < 0.001), making them another group at elevated risk for STI through different but equally concerning mechanisms. In addition, depressive symptoms and non-medical prescription drug use also mediated the CSA–RSB–STI pathway. To conclude, being female was associated with a stronger effect of RSB on STI outcomes, while being Black increased the strength of the RSB–STI link [29].

Another important factor that needs to be taken into consideration is income. Data showed that individuals with unmet needs were more likely to report RBS such as unprotected anal or vaginal sex, especially applying to women [30]. Similarly, other studies found that both ethnicity and income were independent predictors of STI risk [20, 30, 31], but ethnic disparities were considered more prominent than income disparities. The poorest income quintile had 83% higher odds of STI diagnosis compared to the richest. Furthermore, income had a weaker effect on STI diagnosis among Whites than among other ethnic groups. Gender differences were also found since the income gradient was steeper for women, particularly Black women, than for men [31].

Not only is testing itself important to prevent infections, but also how well individuals are connected to the healthcare system besides the quality of the care they receive. One study from 2009 examined how quickly individuals diagnosed with HIV across 18 major U.S. cities were linked to medical care within three months which is a key goal of the U.S. National HIV/AIDS Strategy [32]. Overall, 71% were linked to HIV/AIDS care, with major differences between cities (48.5% in Miami versus 92.5% in Denver). Younger individuals (13–24 years of age) exhibited the lowest linkage rates (64%), while those diagnosed at an advanced disease stage (i.e., AIDS) had the highest (98%). The study further found significant ethnic disparities since White individuals had a median linkage rate of 79%, compared to 76% in Latinos and 67% in Black individuals. Among MSM, 82% of White MSM were linked to care, compared to 76% of Latino and 70% of Black MSM. These findings show persistent inequities in access to HIV/AIDS care, particularly affecting Black communities [32].

Also, many prevention programs overlook Black heterosexual men, even though some (especially individuals with a syphilis infection history) had an increased HIV infection risk. The high prevalence of vaccine-preventable infections such as hepatitis B virus and HPV indicates missed opportunities for early intervention since HPV vaccine coverage is insufficient in Black communities, especially among men [22].

Furthermore, there is growing recognition that discrimination is a major factor influencing health outcomes and seems impossible to ignore when trying to explain health disparities in the U.S. [33]. Racism constitutes a long-term source of stress that negatively affects both mental and physical health. For ethnic minorities, this can increase their vulnerability and make it more difficult to access healthcare.

Preventions and intervention strategies

It is important to examine effective prevention methods to specifically reduce disparities and infection rates. One concept is called “Design of the Technology Enhanced Community Health Precision Nursing” (TECH-PN). The model is designed to improve care for adolescents and young adults with pelvic inflammatory disease and work within the real-life challenges faced by many low-income, urban, African American young women (e.g., limited healthcare access, mistrust in systems, social instability). The TECH-PN approach provides individualized care, mobile community health nursing and digital support tools to facilitate access to individualized STI prevention and treatment. By using real-time diagnostic data, such as STI test results and antimicrobial resistance profiles, providers can offer immediate and tailored treatment. Nurses deliver care directly in patients' homes, which helps to overcome common barriers such as transportation issues, inflexible clinic hours or stigma. Early results show high participation and acceptance, yet there is no published data on STI reduction. The goal was to show that in the long term, personalized care can lead to fewer new infections. Nevertheless, the intervention has strong potential to reduce ethnic health disparities by offering more accessible, personalized, and timely care in underserved communities [12]. Another approach in New Orleans aimed to improve STI screening and treatment among young Black men. Since traditional outreach methods often fail within this population, the study tried a community-based approach that included peer engagement and small financial rewards. To better reach young Black men, the study used a method called Incentivized Peer Referral. Starting in July 2020, participants were given $5 for every friend or peer they brought into the program. This helped to increase participation, especially during the time of the COVID-19 pandemic, when many people avoided clinics. Before the Incentivized Peer Referral was introduced, only about 20% of participants were referred by peers. After the Incentivized Peer Referral had begun, the number rose to nearly 46%. The study also found that men who joined through peer referral were more likely to be tested C. trachomatis positive and to have more sexual partners meaning the strategy helped to reach those at higher risk of infection. This shows that peer referral, even with a small reward, can be a useful and low-cost way to build trust and involve people in STI screening, especially those who usually rather avoid medical/clinical settings or feel unsure about the healthcare system [15].

Discussion

Summary, conclusion, and perspectives

Our review revealed that the literature on ethnic disparities in STI screening is marked by significant contradictions. Overall, Black and Hispanic individuals showed much higher prevalences of STIs if compared to White subjects. While some studies reported lower testing rates among certain ethnic communities [17, 21, 24, 25], others showed the exact opposite [26–28]. One possible interpretation of that paradox is that both findings support the fundamental idea that STI disparities are not driven by testing behavior alone, but by a more complex and intertwined set of influencing factors.

If testing were the only key variable, the link between testing and STI rate would be more consistent. The fact that there can be found such conflicting findings suggests that the issue cannot be understood through a single lens. Instead, the inconsistency between the studies is probably also influenced by the role of structural, social, and systemic influences such as differences in healthcare-seeking behavior, traumatic experiences, institutional racism, socioeconomic inequalities, and limited access to healthcare [14, 20, 22, 23, 26, 29–33]. That there is no clear agreement also shows that there is an urgent need to better understand these underlying mechanisms and to conduct more focused research in this area. Only by disentangling how these factors interact, and which factors maybe haven't been considered yet will set the path to develop effective and sustainable solutions. Given the global burden of STIs and the ongoing health disparities in many parts of the world, it is important that research is not only done in countries like the U.S., but also in a global setting. Addressing these issues is not just a local or national matter; in fact, it is a global public health priority.

Even more alarming in that context are the current developments in the U.S. The 2026 federal budget eliminated the HIV prevention program of the CDC, that builds efforts to reduce new infections and support communities at risk [34–36]. Global HIV efforts have also been hit hard since massive budget cuts for “The U.S. President's Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief” and USAID, putting millions of people at risk. Furthermore, the administration shut down the CDC's only laboratory dedicated to molecular STI surveillance and T. pallidum PCR testing. This weakened the national capacity to monitor and respond to outbreaks [37–40]. Broader healthcare initiatives, including funding for reproductive health, mental health services, and harm reduction programs, have also seen significant setbacks, leaving underserved populations with even fewer options for care [41–43]. In the context of rising STI rates and persistent health inequities, these developments mark a profound setback for public health, both in the U.S. and globally.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, most of the included studies are based on data from the U.S., which may limit how well the findings apply to other countries or healthcare systems. Second, the studies were selected using only PubMed and based on specific search terms. It is likely that other relevant studies exist but were not included because they used different keywords or were found in other databases. Finally, the search and selection process were done by a single investigator. Although great care was taken, mistakes in the search process cannot be completely ruled out.
