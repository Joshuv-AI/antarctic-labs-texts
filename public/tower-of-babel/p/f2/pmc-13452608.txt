Anaphylactic transfusion reaction to group B platelets related to alpha‐gal syndrome: A case report
Authors: Oscar Andre Hinojosa, Amitava Dasgupta, Zhan Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlpha‐gal syndrome (AGS), a distinct form of IgE‐mediated hypersensitivity to the carbohydrate galactose‐α‐1,3‐galactose (α‐Gal), typically occurs after repeated tick bites and leads to allergic reactions after ingestion of mammalian meat. Patients with AGS may experience a broad spectrum of allergic reactions, from pruritus and urticaria to angioedema and anaphylaxis. Although classically associated with food‐triggered reactions, emerging reports describe AGS‐related anaphylaxis following exposure to group B blood products, including cases without accompanying gastrointestinal symptoms, highlighting an underrecognized clinical presentation.Case ReportA 29‐year‐old group O male with chronic myelogenous leukemia, status post stem cell transplant from group A donor, with extensive pancytopenia, received multiple blood products, including red blood cells and platelets. Although most of the transfusions were uneventful, he developed allergic reactions repeatedly, from mild to anaphylactic reactions during the transfusion of group AB or B platelets. A history of tick bites was confirmed without establishing an association between his symptoms and meat consumption. A mildly elevated galactose‐α‐1,3‐galactose IgE was detected. No additional allergic reactions were observed after strict use of non‐B blood products was implemented.ConclusionsThis case demonstrates a possible clinical association between AGS and anaphylactic transfusion reaction to group B blood products in non‐B recipients. Early laboratory workups for AGS antibodies are recommended in non‐B patients with a history of tick exposure and severe allergic reactions to meat consumption or transfusion of group B blood products. Meanwhile, transfusion of group B blood products should be avoided once AGS is suspected clinically in non‐B patients.

INTRODUCTION

Anaphylaxis is an acute, potentially fatal systemic hypersensitivity reaction caused by a sudden release of mast cell and basophil mediators into the bloodstream. It often results from an immune reaction to foods, insect allergens, medications, etc. Anaphylaxis has traditionally been conceptualized as an IgE‐mediated event, although other mechanisms are possible. 1

Alpha‐gal syndrome (AGS) is an immunoglobin (Ig) E‐mediated hypersensitivity reaction characterized by the development of pruritus, urticaria, angioedema, anaphylaxis, and a variety of gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms after meat ingestion. 2 This IgE antibody recognizes the oligosaccharide chain galactose‐α‐1,3‐galactose (α‐Gal), present in the glycoproteins and glycolipids of non‐primate vertebral tissues. 2 , 3 Sensitization to this carbohydrate requires exposure to repeated tick bites; several species have been identified, including Amblyomma americanum, Amblyomma sculptum, Ixodes holocyclus, Ixodes ricinus, etc. 3

Although there are no specific diagnostic criteria for AGS, a compatible clinical history and serologic testing with an adequate dietary response are used to diagnose it. 4 However, a few cases of AGS have presented an anaphylactic response to group B blood products, with some of these patients lacking GI symptoms. 5 , 6

CASE PRESENTATION

A 29‐year‐old male presented to our institution with a history of 2 days of several episodes of melena and fatigue. The patient had a history of chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML), diagnosed 5 years ago. His CML had a complex genetic profile, characterized by a translocation 9:22 (t9:22), considered a high‐risk cytogenic abnormality, and mutations in the BCR/ABL gene (T315I and F317L), which conferred resistance to several tyrosine kinase inhibitors. He underwent an unrelated allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (SCT) 6 months before. The patient's donor was group A and Rh positive, while the patient was O with Rh positive. His blood type was never converted after the transplant. Despite the myeloablative treatment, the patient's CML recurred, and he was started on azacitidine, venetoclax, and asciminib approximately 1 month prior to presentation. His case was also complicated by graft versus host disease (GVHD) manifested as intermittent diarrhea.

On physical examination, the patient had skin pallor and mild tachycardia at rest with stable blood pressure and oxygen saturation. The patient's complete blood count showed a hemoglobin of 4.5 g/dL, white blood cell count of 0.5 K/μL, absolute neutrophil count of 0 K/μL, and platelet count of 3 K/μL. The remaining laboratory results, including coagulation profile and chemistry, did not reveal any significant abnormalities (Table 1). An esophagogastroduodenoscopy and a colonoscopy were performed, which did not reveal significant lesions, but biopsies were compatible with known GVHD.

During his hospitalization, the patient required several supportive blood product transfusions, including leukoreduced and irradiated red blood cell (RBC) and apheresis platelet units. The threshold for platelet transfusion was 10,000/μL. Most platelet transfusions were O‐positive and without side effects. Due to his severe thrombocytopenia (platelet counts of 5000/μL) with suspected GI bleeding and blood product constraints, the patient received one B‐positive platelet unit. Approximately 35 min after the transfusion started and with the administration of 80 cc of product, the patient developed signs and symptoms of anaphylaxis. The patient developed voice hoarseness, stridor, wheezing, lip and perioral edema, mild tachycardia (heart rate: 100–108 beats/min), and tachypnea (respiratory rate: 20 respirations/min). The patient received epinephrine 0.3 mg intramuscular injection twice, diphenhydramine 50 mg and methylprednisolone 62.5 mg intravenous injection twice, with clinical improvement. The transfusion reaction investigation was negative for hemolysis. Pre‐transfusion IgA level was normal and anti‐IgA test was negative.

On retrospective examination, the patient had previously developed a mild allergic reaction to an AB‐negative platelet unit, with hives and itching, and then a moderate allergic reaction with mild throat tightness and wheezing to a B‐positive platelet unit on the same day during a previous admission. The first reaction was controlled by diphenhydramine only. Methylprednisolone and famotidine were administered after the second reaction and symptoms resolved quickly. Premedication of diphenhydramine and methylprednisolone was required for further transfusions. He received 6 units of platelets between this event and the current anaphylactic reaction. No allergic reactions occurred and all the 6 units were O‐positive or A‐positive platelet.

On further questioning, the patient disclosed that he lived in a semi‐rural area and was frequently outdoors, having been exposed to ticks multiple times. The history of tick exposure and severe allergic reaction to group B blood product was suggestive of an underlying case of AGS. The patient had intermittent diarrhea, attributed to GVHD, but his diet also included several types of meat; the patient was unable to establish a timeline between meat consumption and symptom development. A subsequent evaluation revealed a mildly elevated galactose‐α‐1,3‐galactose IgE of 0.1 KU/L (Table 1).

Due to probable AGS associated allergic reactions, a strict requirement for non‐B platelet transfusions was implemented. No further allergic reactions occurred during the remainder of this admission and during subsequent admissions over a 18‐month follow‐up period.

DISCUSSION

The α‐Gal core structure is composed of a disaccharide galactose‐α‐1,3‐galactose (Gal‐α‐1,3‐Gal) linked to a N‐acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc). This epitope is then expressed on glycoproteins and glycolipids. All vertebrates possess the gene for the enzyme α‐1,3‐galactosyltransferase (α‐1,3GT), required for the synthesis of these carbohydrates. The exceptions are the so‐called old‐world monkeys, apes, and Homo sapiens. Specifically in humans, a pseudogene is present, leading to an inactive α‐1,3GT. 7

Although α‐Gal is absent in humans, it is chemically very similar to the blood group B antigen. Only deferring by the presence of a fucose attached to the subterminal galactose in the group B antigen. 3 A decreased reaction of IgG against α‐gal has been detected in individuals with the group B and AB antigens. 8 This could explain a possible correlation of group B antigen and AGS.

The vast majority of AGS cases present with urticaria (89%–93%), anaphylactic symptoms (49%–62%), GI symptoms (64%–66%), and only GI symptoms (4%) after ingestion of meat. 9 Our case exemplifies a potential subgroup of patients whose AGS manifests anaphylactic reactions to transfusion of B‐group blood products instead of the typical meat ingestion, which has been rarely reported. 5 , 6 It also suggests a possible etiology for severe allergic/anaphylactic reactions in group O individuals who receive platelet transfusions from group B donors.

The platelet transfusion guidelines of our institution favor compatible platelet transfusion as the first line. However, minor and major incompatible platelet units can be administered if transfusion is indicated and there is product constraint. This is compatible with current evidence. Based on the database of the Recipient Epidemiology and Donor Evaluation Study‐III, comprising 21,176 patients, there is no significant association between ABO‐nonidentical platelet transfusion and mortality, except in the subgroup of patients with hematologic/oncologic diagnoses among blood group A and B recipients. The incidence of allergic/anaphylactic reactions was not reported. 10

Therefore, prevention of transfusion‐related AGS can be challenging. In our patient, the risk went unrecognized because he had no known history of tick bites, and his GI symptoms were attributed to GVHD rather than to meat consumption. Given the rarity of this condition, we did not initially associate his reactions with blood type, particularly because they were mild or could be mitigated with pre‐medication. Increasing awareness of this uncommon entity, obtaining a thorough clinical history, and carefully reviewing transfusion records will be essential for early recognition and prevention.

It is not clear whether our patient's complicated medical conditions, for example, relapsing CML, failure of RBC engraftment, GVHD and chemotherapy, contributed to the severity of the reactions. There are several risk factors documented that increase the severity or incidence of allergic reactions in AGS patients. These factors include indolent systemic mastocytosis, atopy, and cofactors that lower the threshold for allergic symptoms such as exercise, alcohol ingestion, menstruation and nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) use. 11 , 12 Some therapeutic monoclonal antibodies, for example, Cetuximab and Infliximab, are reported to cause anaphylaxis in association with IgE antibodies against α‐gal. 13 , 14 Our patient received vedolizumab to manage GVHD. Unlike rabbit antithymocyte globulin, vedolizumab is produced in Chinese hamster ovary cells which lack the enzyme to synthesize α‐gal and is considered safe to patients with AGS.

Available literature does not identify the direct association between the status of leukemia, immunosuppression, or SCT and the severity of AGS allergic reactions. The ongoing collaborative study of transfusion‐related AGS may provide more evidence to reveal the impact of coexisting medical conditions on the severity of AGS reactions. 15

CONCLUSIONS

Based on the few published cases, there is not enough evidence to attribute causation, but there is a possible association between AGS and anaphylactic reactions to transfusion of group B blood products. Therefore, it is advisable to avoid transfusion of group B blood products to patients with AGS. Another possible recommendation would be to consider workup for AGS in patients who present with anaphylactic transfusion reactions to group B blood products in the appropriate clinical context.
