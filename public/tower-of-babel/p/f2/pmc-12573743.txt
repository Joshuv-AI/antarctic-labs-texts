Are We Witnessing a Speciation Continuum? Evidence From Current and Past Gene Flow in the Genus Oritrophium s.s. (Asteraceae) From the Tropical High Andes
Authors: Juan Manuel Gorospe, Luciana Salomón, Jorge Armijos‐Barros, Adam Kantor, Filip Kolář, Itziar Arnelas, Petr Sklenář, Roswitha Schmickl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDetermining species boundaries is key for appropriately assessing biodiversity. However, the continuity of the speciation process makes delimiting species a difficult task, especially for recently diverged taxa. Furthermore, past introgression may leave traces that result in reticulate evolutionary patterns, challenging the estimation of species relationships. The fastest‐evolving biodiversity hotspot on Earth is the Páramo. Its flora in the tropical high Andes is known for extraordinarily high species richness and endemism. However, the recent origin, fast diversification and complex taxonomy of many genera challenge species delimitation and phylogenetic reconstruction. In this study, we reconstructed phylogenetic relationships and addressed the role of introgression in the diversification of Oritrophium s.s. (Asteraceae) based on phylogenomic data. We combined genomic, phenotypic and ecological data to test species boundaries and compared trajectories across the speciation continuum within the taxonomically complex ‘ O. peruvianum group’. We found that historical introgression played an important role in the evolution of Oritrophium s.s., and many of the taxa within the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ are at various stages of speciation. These results highlight the importance of testing for introgression to understand the diversification of recently evolved groups. Likewise, they suggest that heterogeneous speciation trajectories associated with geographic isolation and secondary contact, possibly during the Pleistocene, contributed to plant diversity in the tropical high Andes.Keywords: ddRADseq, ecological niche, historical introgression, Hyb‐seq, morphometrics, reproductive isolation, speciation continuum

Introduction

Delimitation of species is important for accurately assessing biodiversity, which can be hampered by a lack of consensus on species concepts (Stankowski and Ravinet 2021b). For instance, species could be defined as entities capable of interbreeding (biological concept), as identifiable genotypic clusters (genetic concept), as those forming distinctive phenotypic clusters (phenetic concept), or as those that occupy a distinct adaptive zone (ecological concept). The consideration of species as separately evolving metapopulations through time brought together alternative species definitions under a unified species concept (de Queiroz 2007). This concept aims to apply integrative approaches using species definitions as combined criteria to determine whether taxa are independently evolving entities (Dayrat 2005; Padial et al. 2010; Schlick‐Steiner et al. 2010). The integrative concept of species implies that speciation is a continuous process in which isolation, namely reproductive isolation, between entities can be bidirectional and occur at different rates (Stankowski and Ravinet 2021a). Even though speciation has occasionally been evidenced to be relatively instantaneous, via chromosomal inversions leading to reproductive and adaptive divergence (Wellenreuther and Bernatchez 2018), speciation usually involves numerous factors that shape the strength of reproductive isolation (Lowry 2012; Oh and Shaw 2013; Riesch et al. 2016; Sobel et al. 2010). Incorporating different trait dimensions and comparing speciation trajectories (i.e., when different processes drive differentiation and cause populations to follow distinct pathways towards speciation) to study speciation across the speciation continuum is possible through multivariate approaches such as the “speciation cube” (Dieckmann et al. 2004) or its extension, the “speciation hypercube” (Bolnick et al. 2023). For that, multiple pairs of taxa and measures of divergence across different traits (e.g., genotypic, phenotypic, ecological and, if feasible, reproductive isolation) can be combined to reconstruct the speciation process at varying degrees of divergence (Bolnick et al. 2023). Estimating the strength of reproductive isolation entails tedious experimental work (White et al. 2020), which is often not feasible for non‐model organisms (Stankowski and Ravinet 2021a). In such cases, estimating the extent of gene flow between populations based on population genetic data could provide a proxy for the strength of reproductive isolation (Westram et al. 2022), although this approximation will be affected by barriers to gene flow other than reproductive isolation. Overall, these multivariate approaches (the speciation cube or hypercube) provide a unified framework, applicable across study systems, to assess the relative importance of factors involved in speciation (Johannesson et al. 2024).

Gene flow barriers may be absent or weak at the onset of differentiation, and their strength increases along the speciation continuum (Bolnick et al. 2023; Stankowski and Ravinet 2021a). When recurrent gene flow occurs among populations, alleles from one population may be incorporated into another through introgressive hybridisation (Rieseberg et al. 2007; Suarez‐Gonzalez et al. 2018). Especially at later stages of the speciation continuum, introgressive hybridisation can play an ambiguous role in population differentiation, as it may reduce differentiation and prevent divergence, or it may, on the contrary, reinforce reproductive barriers (Abbott et al. 2013; Barton 2001; Soltis and Soltis 2009). The accumulation of barriers across the speciation continuum may reach a point when speciation is irreversible, and then speciation is complete (Seehausen et al. 2014; Stankowski and Ravinet 2021a). But even at this stage, although species are distinct, determining macroevolutionary relationships is often challenging because of incongruent traces derived from the process of speciation (Li et al. 2018). For instance, detecting and accounting for signals of introgressive hybridisation at deep evolutionary scales (historical introgression hereafter) helped to elucidate the reticulate evolution across the tree of life (Mallet et al. 2016; Schley et al. 2021). Historical introgression can be detected by analysing patterns of phylogenetic incongruence between gene trees or polymorphic sites (Malinsky et al. 2021; Suvorov et al. 2022), assuming that introgression makes one incongruent topology more common than the rest, while the stochastic inheritance of alleles, i.e., incomplete lineage sorting (ILS), makes incongruent topologies equally common (Doyle 1992; Durand et al. 2011; Green et al. 2010). These analytical approaches provide a great opportunity to study the impact of introgressive hybridisation on current plant diversity, and while its role is well studied in temperate floras (Mallet 2005; Marques et al. 2018; Whitney et al. 2010), it is rather unexplored in notoriously species‐rich areas such as the Neotropics (Schley et al. 2021).

Mountainous regions within the Neotropics are among the most species‐rich areas in the world (Meyers et al. 2020; Rahbek, Borregaard, Colwell, et al. 2019). The Páramo flora, found in the tropical Andes from ca. 3000 to 4500 m, is particularly high in species richness, with over 3500 species of vascular plants (Luteyn et al. 1999). This high species richness has been suggested to be the outcome of rapid radiations (Hughes and Eastwood 2006), and the Páramo has been estimated as the fastest‐evolving biodiversity hotspot (Madriñán et al. 2013). Adaptation to new habitats and allopatric speciation, as a result of mountain uplift (10–5 million years ago, Mya; Garzione et al. 2008) and the emergence of the Páramo ecosystem (5–3 Mya; van der Hammen and Cleef 1986), have been hypothesised among the main processes that promoted the origin and diversification of the Páramo flora (Bell and Donoghue 2005; Hughes and Eastwood 2006; Salomón et al. 2022; Sklenář et al. 2011). Similar to other mountainous regions, the Páramo ecosystem is characterised by a high habitat heterogeneity, with abrupt changes in topography, soil properties and water availability, as well as an extreme climate, with abrupt daily temperature changes (Cortés and Wheeler 2018; Rahbek, Borregaard, Antonelli, et al. 2019; Rundel 1994; Sarmiento 1986; Sklenář et al. 2010). These environmental conditions have the potential to take populations through different evolutionary pathways along ecological and phenotypic traits, resulting in a diversity of speciation trajectories across the speciation continuum. The Andean cordilleras also show a higher continuity and potentially higher connectivity between alpine vegetation belts compared to other mountain systems within the tropics (Kandziora et al. 2024; Sklenář et al. 2014), providing ample opportunities for gene flow through plant migration and hybridisation after secondary contact. Moreover, during Pleistocene glacial cycles, populations may have repeatedly migrated downwards and expanded during cold periods, to later migrate back upwards and contract during warm periods (Flantua and Hooghiemstra 2018; Hooghiemstra et al. 2006; Hooghiemstra and Van der Hammen 2004). These population dynamics changed habitat connectivity across and within mountain ranges (Flantua et al. 2019), resulting in heterogeneous speciation trajectories across different traits. For instance, populations may have differentiated along genetic traits, via recurrent isolation and secondary contact cycles, and/or across ecological traits, through niche shifts due to adaptation and establishment in newly available habitats. Given the recent diversification of plant groups and the complex habitat and population dynamics, the tropical high Andes are a well‐suited model ecosystem to address the speciation continuum and test for signals of introgression. It can be hypothesised that for some high Andean plants the speciation continuum has not yet reached the extreme of complete reproductive isolation.

The Asteraceae family is among the most species‐ and endemic‐rich plant groups in the Páramo (Luteyn et al. 1999). Studies on Andean Asteraceae have highlighted the importance of morphological evolution, geographic isolation and hybridisation in their diversification (Diazgranados and Barber 2017; Dušková et al. 2017; Kandziora et al. 2022; Pouchon et al. 2018; Vargas et al. 2017; Salomón et al. 2025). However, the history of speciation, under the speciation continuum framework, and the patterns of hybridisation among Andean Asteraceae have not been fully explored. Within the Asteraceae, the genus Oritrophium s.s. (Kunth) Cuatrec. comprises small, perennial, herbaceous plants forming basal rosettes with solitary capitula (Cuatrecasas 1997). The genus displays a disjunct distribution pattern, with the majority of its members inhabiting the Northern and Central Andes and two species growing in the high‐elevation grasslands in Mexico (Aranguren et al. 2008; Cuatrecasas 1997). Most representatives grow in wet habitats like swamps within the Páramo (Salomón et al. 2022), and while two species are widespread along the Northern and Central Andes (Oritrophium peruvianum (Lam.) Cuatrec. and O. limnophilum (Sch. Bip.) Cuatrec.), many species show very restricted distributions (e.g., Oritrophium llanganatense Sklenář & H. Rob., O. ollgaardii Cuatrec.). The origin of Oritrophium s.s. has been estimated in the Central Andes during the Early Pliocene (ca. 4.19 Mya; Salomón et al. 2022), when the Páramo ecosystem emerged. Subsequently, the population dynamics during the Pleistocene might have structured the diversity within the genus (Salomón et al. 2022). Oritrophium s.s. is considered to be diploid, with a base chromosome number n = 9 (Turner et al. 1967), and it comprises ca. 18 species (Arnelas et al. 2017; Calvo et al. 2024; Cuatrecasas 1997; Nesom 1992, 1998; Salomón et al. 2022; Sklenář and Robinson 2000). The diversity within Oritrophium s.s. is unclear, especially within a taxonomically complex group formed by O. peruvianum and morphologically close species (the O. peruvianum group; Arnelas et al. 2020; Cuatrecasas 1961; Salomón et al. 2022). This group, which comprises at least six described species, shows overlapping variation in morphological characters (Arnelas et al. 2020; Cuatrecasas 1997) and a lack of support for the described species based on molecular markers (Salomón et al. 2022). This suggests that the described species may be at different stages of the speciation continuum.

In this study, we estimated the role of gene flow throughout the evolutionary history of Oritrophium s.s. Specifically, (1) at a deep scale, we test for signals of historical introgression across Oritrophium s.s. and between Oritrophium s.s. and closely related species from other genera using Hyb‐Seq data, and (2) at a shallow scale, we test if the described species within the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ are supported based on genetic (ddRADseq), ecological and phenotypic criteria, or alternatively, if they represent different speciation stages and trajectories across the speciation continuum.

Materials and Methods

Sampling, Library Preparation and Sequencing

Plant material from 15 described species and six morphologically intermediate individuals was selected for phylogenomic analyses. The selected samples cover most of the distribution of the genus and represent ca. 83% of currently described species. In addition, one sample of the monotypic genus Novenia acaulis (Benth. & Hook. f. ex B.D. Jacks.) S.E. Freire & F.H. Hellw (Freire 1986; Freire and Hellwig 1990) was included based on previous phylogenetic results (Brouillet et al. 2009), and 17 closely related species, including former members of Oritrophium s.s. (O. blepharophyllum (S.F. Blake) Cuatrec., O. figueirasii Cuatrec., O. hirtopilosum (Hieron.) Cuatrec., O. nevadense (Wedd.) Cuatrec., O. venezuelense (Steyerm.) Cuatrec.; Salomón et al. 2022), were included as outgroup taxa. The majority of the plant material was available from field expeditions to the Andes in Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru and Bolivia between 2007 and 2022. Representative herbarium vouchers from these collections were deposited at the BOL, F, HUT, HSP, HUTPL, KUELAP, PRC, QCA and VEN herbaria. In a few cases, plant material was taken from herbarium vouchers at the B, F, LPB, MA, NY, PRC, SI and US herbaria, or data was downloaded from the Sequence Read Archive (Table S1).

Extraction of DNA, genomic library preparation and bait hybridisation to generate targeted nuclear and off‐target plastid sequence data followed the methodology of Gizaw et al. (2022); see Supplementary Text for details. Targeted nuclear loci correspond to the Asteraceae conserved orthologous set (Compositae1061; Mandel et al. 2014). The majority of samples were sequenced on an Illumina (San Diego, California, USA) NovaSeq 6000 SP at IAB (Olomouc, Czech Republic), and some on an Illumina NextSeq at the Genomics Core Facility of CEITEC (Brno, Czech Republic).

For the purpose of inferring genetic differentiation among members of the ‘ O. peruvianum group’, we selected a total of 235 samples from 38 populations (Table S2), covering the distribution range of the species and including narrow endemics. Libraries for ddRAD sequencing were prepared following the methodology of Gorospe et al. (2025). We used a combination of two Type II restriction endonucleases, SphI and MluCI, and chose a narrow fragment size selection of 450–550 base pairs (bp). ddRADseq libraries of 241 samples, including technical replicates, were pooled for sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq X at Macrogen Europe (Amsterdam, Netherlands) and an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus at NovoGene (Cambridge, UK).

Phylogenomic Analyses and Divergence Time Estimation Based on Hyb‐Seq Data

We processed and analysed targeted nuclear and off‐target plastid DNA data following the methodology of Gizaw et al. (2022). Given the paleopolyploid origin of the Asteraceae (Barker et al. 2008, 2016), we considered it important to account for paralogy in the analysis of the targeted nuclear loci. Paralogy of these loci was analysed using ParalogWizard (Ufimov et al. 2022), establishing a minimum and maximum pairwise sequence divergence of 9.57% and 22.76%, respectively, as thresholds for paralog detection. The HybPhyloMaker workflow (Fér and Schmickl 2018) was used to reconstruct species trees for both nuclear (nrDNA) and plastid DNA (cpDNA) data, allowing only ≤ 50% missing data per accession and ≤ 25% missing accessions per locus. The complete chloroplast genome of Oritrophium peruvianum (accession number KX063861) was retrieved from GenBank and used as a reference for cpDNA read mapping. Gene trees based on nrDNA were estimated using RAxML v.8.4.2 (Stamatakis 2014) with the general‐time reversible (GTR) substitution model with a gamma‐distributed rate variation among sites “GTRGAMMA” and 500 bootstrap replicates. We generated a coalescent‐based nrDNA phylogeny using ASTRAL‐III v.5.7.4 (Zhang et al. 2018) and concatenated maximum likelihood phylogenies for both nrDNA and cpDNA using RAxML‐NG v.8 (Kozlov et al. 2019). The cpDNA phylogeny was based on coding and non‐coding regions (introns and intergenic spacers). Phylogenetic reconstruction was performed, excluding seven accessions which showed introgression across numerous accessions (see Introgression analyses based on Hyb‐Seq data section). This was done to meet the assumptions of concatenation and coalescent‐based methods and avoid inaccurate estimations derived from incongruences resulting from gene flow.

Divergence time estimation was performed based on penalised likelihood using treePL v.1.0 (Sanderson 2002; Smith and O'Meara 2012) and following the guidelines of Maurin (2020). We employed one secondary calibration point based on the 95% confidence interval of the highest posterior density (HPD) values used by Salomón et al. (2022) for the crown age of Erigeron–Diplostephium–Linochilus (22.06–7.7 Mya). Parameter optimisation and cross‐validation analyses were performed using the script ‘treepl_wrapper.sh’ (available at https://github.com/tongjial/treepl_wrapper) and the nrDNA concatenated maximum likelihood phylogeny without accessions showing signals of historical introgression. Confidence intervals for node ages were inferred from 300 nrDNA bootstrapped phylogenies using the nrDNA concatenated phylogeny as topological constraint and the secondary calibration. The optimisation and cross‐validation values previously calculated were used. The resulting dated trees were combined using TreeAnnotator v.2.7.7 available in BEAST v.2.7.7 (Bouckaert et al. 2019). To account for incongruences between nrDNA topologies based on coalescence and maximum likelihood, the divergence time estimation analysis was replicated using the coalescent‐based phylogeny as a topological constraint.

Introgression Analyses Based on Hyb‐Seq Data

For the analysis of introgression, we constructed distance‐based networks using Neighbour‐Net (Bryant and Moulton 2004) available in SplitsTree v.4.12.6 (Huson et al. 2008) to visualise reticulation and potential hybridisation within the Hyb‐Seq dataset. We estimated historical introgression employing a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)‐based approach using Dsuite v.0.5 (Malinsky et al. 2021) and a gene tree‐based approach using the Discordant Count Test and the Branch Length Test (DCT‐BLT; Suvorov et al. 2022). We selected Oritrophium s.s. accessions and included Novenia acaulis, based on our phylogenomic results. For the SNP‐based analysis, nrDNA loci were concatenated for variant calling using snp‐sites v.2.3.3 (Page et al. 2016), employing O. orizabense G.L. Nesom as reference. We filtered SNPs using bcftools v.1.17 (Li 2011), setting 20% as the missing data threshold and taking the first SNP in each locus to retain unlinked SNPs. The resulting VCF file comprised 1082 SNPs and was used to estimate the D statistic (Patterson et al. 2012) using Dsuite. We controlled for the family‐wise error rate by adjusting p‐values using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) method (Jafari and Ansari‐Pour 2019) available in the stats v.3.6.3 package in R v.4.1 (R Core Team 2018). An adjusted p‐value below 0.01 was used to determine significance based on the value established in the description of Dsuite. A heatmap showing significant results was plotted using the script ‘plot_d.rb’ (available at: https://github.com/mmatschiner/tutorials/tree/master/analysis_of_introgression_with_snp_data/src). For the gene tree‐based analysis, we employed the coalescent‐based phylogeny, selecting O. orizabense as outgroup and 642 gene trees, because the method does not allow missing accessions in gene trees. The p‐value was adjusted using the FDR method in R, and a value below 0.05 was used to determine significance based on the description of DCT‐BLT. We estimated the introgression proportion for triplets showing significance according to DCT‐BLT. Additionally, we estimated historical introgression between Oritrophium s.s. and closely related species from other genera (Linochilus rupestris (Kunth) Saldivia & O.M. Vargas and former Oritrophium s.s. species, O. figueirasii, O. venezuelense and O. blepharophyllum) by analysing 1097 SNPs using Dsuite and 646 gene trees using DCT‐BLT. The methodology described above was replicated using Linochilus floribundus Benth. as a reference/outgroup.

ddRADseq Raw Read Processing (O. peruvianum group)

The processing of raw reads and selection of the optimal set of parameters was performed using the Stacks v.2.5 pipeline (Catchen et al. 2011; Rochette et al. 2019) and following the methodology of Gorospe et al. (2025). We removed 30 low‐quality accessions and 6 that were used as technical replicates; a total of 36 populations remained. After the optimisation step, the minimum depth of coverage to create a stack (m) and the distance parameters (M and n) were set to 3, allowing a maximum nucleotide mismatch (N) of M + 2. With these settings the ‘populations’ pipeline within Stacks was used to perform de novo assembly of loci and generate a raw VCF file. The resulting VCF file was thinned, keeping one random SNP per locus to avoid linked SNPs and filtered using a 25% threshold for the minimum percentage of individuals in a population (r). The maximum threshold for observed heterozygosity was set to 0.65 to prevent the presence of repetitive or paralogous loci. Using vcftools v.1.6 (Danecek et al. 2011), rare variants were removed, establishing a minimum minor allele frequency threshold (MAF) of 0.05, the maximum proportion of missing data per site was set to 0.50, and the minimum and maximum depth coverage over all individuals was set to 5 and 50, respectively. The resulting VCF file comprised 2574 SNPs and included all representatives from the ‘ O. peruvianum group’.

Genetic Differentiation Based on ddRADseq Data (O. peruvianum group)

Genetically homogenous groups were identified using the model‐based clustering analysis approach implemented in ADMIXTURE v.1.3.0 (Alexander et al. 2009) to test whether described species within the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ are supported based on genetic criteria. The VCF file including all representatives from the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ was used to perform analyses ranging from K = 1 to K = 36 with 200 bootstrap resamplings and the default optimisation method. The most probable value of K was determined based on the lowest cross‐validation error value (Alexander and Lange 2011). Accessions with > 25% of ancestry among clusters were considered genetically admixed. We constructed distance‐based networks using Neighbour‐Net to visualise relationships and remaining reticulation among genetic groups. Additionally, we estimated mean observed heterozygosity (HO), mean expected heterozygosity (H E), mean inbreeding coefficient (F IS), mean nucleotide diversity (π) and population differentiation (F ST) using the ‘populations’ pipeline in Stacks. Rare variants were kept for estimating nucleotide diversity.

Gene Flow Based on ddRADseq Data as Proxy for Reproductive Isolation (O. peruvianum group)

Estimating the strength of reproductive barriers through reciprocal crosses and fitness experiments under controlled conditions or in the field was not feasible given the large distribution of the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ and the difficulty to replicate the environmental conditions of the tropical high Andes. Thus, to obtain a measure of differentiation, current gene flow was estimated and used as a proxy for reproductive isolation between population pairs, even though limited signals of gene flow can be due to isolation factors other than solely reproductive barriers (e.g., geographic, climatic). Estimates of migration rates between 13 populations, representing the distribution, morphological and genetic variability in the group, were obtained using the Bayesian approach implemented in BA3‐SNPs (Mussmann et al. 2019; Wilson and Rannala 2003). The program BA3‐SNPs‐autotune was used to search for the optimal mixing parameter value for migration rates (deltaM), allele frequencies (deltaA) and inbreeding coefficients (deltaF), employing 10 exploratory analyses, each with 100,000 Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) generations, discarding 10% of the generations as burn‐in and sampling every 100th generation. Migration rates between populations were estimated using the optimal mixing parameter values (deltaM = 0.2125, deltaA = 0.9991, deltaF = 0.2125) and employing 90 million MCMC generations, discarding 10% of the generations as burn‐in, and sampling every 10,000th generation. The convergence and effective sample size (ESS ≥ 200) value of all parameters was assessed using Tracer v.1.7.2 (Rambaut et al. 2018). The mean migration rate for each population pair was calculated (BA3‐SNPs estimates bidirectional migration rates), and reproductive isolation was considered complete (RI proxy = 1) for estimates of absence of gene flow (mean migration rate = 0) and incomplete (RI proxy = 0) in the opposite case (mean migration rate = 1).

Phenotypic Differentiation Based on Morphological Data (O. peruvianum group)

Morphological measurements were taken from 54 herbarium specimens, comprising 379 individuals, selected from different localities across the distribution range of the ‘ O. peruvianum group’. These included 274 individuals identified as O. peruvianum , 44 as O. crocifolium (Kunth) Cuatrec., 41 as O. tergoalbum (Cuatrec.) Cuatrec., 9 as O. llanganatense, 4 as O. callacallense Cuatrec., 2 as O. acerosum J. Calvo & Sklenář and 5 specimens were not assigned to species level due to intermediate characters. A total of 55 characters were measured, including 14 vegetative and 41 reproductive (Table S3). Categorical characters were transformed to numerical values, except for three characters (leaf.C, abaxInd.C, bracSha.C), which had to be excluded, together with characters and samples with high amounts of missing data (Table S4). Spearman correlation coefficients revealed that none of the remaining character pairs were highly correlated (ρ > 0.9). We performed a principal component analysis (PCA) using 362 individuals and 50 characters to test if species within the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ were supported based on phenotypic criteria. Remaining missing values were handled using a single imputation approach that employs an iterative PCA algorithm (Josse and Husson 2012) available in the package missMDA v.1.19 (Husson and Josse 2023) in R. The optimal number of dimensions used to impute the data was selected using the K‐fold method for cross‐validation, testing up to 25 dimensions. An alternative PCA including 362 individuals and 41 characters was generated to evaluate the consistency of our results. In this case, characters with moderate amounts of missing data were removed, and remaining missing values were replaced by population means.

Ecological Differentiation Based on Environmental Data ( O. peruvianum group)

Occurrence points for members of the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ from our collections were combined with those available in the GBIF database (GBIF.org). Only records identified by taxonomists with expertise in Asteraceae and in the flora of the tropical high Andes were retained to avoid misidentifications. We filtered occurrences based on the known distribution of the group (Arnelas et al. 2020; Calvo et al. 2024; Cuatrecasas 1997; Sklenář and Robinson 2000) and using a minimum distance threshold between conspecific occurrences of 5 km for widespread species and 1 km for endemic species. This way autocorrelation between occurrences was avoided, and sample sizes between species were balanced while still representing the distribution of widespread species. A total of 194 occurrences remained, 164 corresponding to O. peruvianum , 23 to O. crocifolium, 4 to O. tergoalbum, 3 to O. callacallense and single occurrences for O. acerosum, O. llanganatense and a specimen not assigned to any species. A set of environmental variables, 19 climatic (CHELSA v.2.1; Karger et al. 2017) and 13 edaphic (SoilGrids v.2.0; Poggio et al. 2021), were downloaded at a resolution of 1 km2 (Table S5). The environmental data for each occurrence (Table S6) was extracted using QGIS v.3.38 (QGIS.org 2024). We obtained linearly uncorrelated variables by performing PCA, and the three most important components, which explained 65% of the data variability, were used to estimate a multidimensional ecological niche hypervolume using the package hypervolume v.3.1.4 (Blonder et al. 2014) in R. Taxa with single occurrences could not be used for subsequent analyses. Niche hypervolumes were constructed following the methodology of Gorospe et al. (2025). We estimated the overlap between ecological niche pairs using the Sørensen similarity index (Sørensen 1948) to test if species within the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ were supported based on ecological criteria.

Multidimensional Differentiation ( O. peruvianum group)

We applied the hypercube approach to population pairs from the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ to compare differentiation trajectories according to reproductive isolation as well as differentiation measures based on genetic, phenotypic, climatic, edaphic and geographic traits. We included 13 populations covering the morphological and genetic diversity within the group and spanning most of its geographic distribution. Reproductive isolation and genetic differentiation between pairs of populations were based on current gene flow and F ST values, respectively. Phenotypic differentiation between pairs of populations was estimated based on the imputed PCA by calculating the centroid value for each population across the 16 optimal dimensions. The Euclidean distance between the centroid of each population pair was taken as the phenotypic differentiation value. After performing a separate PCA for climatic and edaphic data, the Euclidean distance between each population pair was taken as the divergence value according to each criterion. The geographic distance between population pairs was estimated using the geodesic method available in the package geodist v.0.1.0 (Padgham et al. 2024) in R. When necessary, values were normalised in order that values of zero represent identical populations, and values of 1 represent distinct populations across all measures. Following the approach described by Bolnick et al. (2023), we generated a PCA across all measures of divergence. Three uncorrelated measures, according to the PCA, were selected to generate a speciation cube based on reproductive isolation, genetic and phenotypic differentiation. Alternatively, as reproductive isolation was not directly measured, a speciation cube was generated using genetic, phenotypic and geographic differentiation. Finally, population pairs along the continuum were selected to illustrate the process of speciation within the group.

Results

Phylogenomic Analyses and Divergence Time Estimation Based on Hyb‐Seq Data

In both nrDNA phylogenies, coalescent‐based and concatenated maximum likelihood‐based, most of the nodes along the backbone were fully supported (local posterior probability (LPP) = 1 and bootstrap support (BS) = 100%). Oritrophium s.s. was estimated as polyphyletic because Novenia was placed (with low support, LPP = 0.59) within Oritrophium s.s. at the base of the tree together with the Mexican endemic species O. orizabense. Besides that, the majority of described species were fully or strongly supported (LPP ≥ 0.95, BS ≥ 95%), with the exception of those within the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ (Figure 1; Figures S1 and S2). The position of O. ollgaardii varied depending on the approach used, and it remained unclear whether it forms a sister lineage to the clade comprising O. aciculifolium Cuatrec., O. repens and O. yacuriense Arnelas & J. Calvo (BS = 63%) or, alternatively, is sister to the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ (LPP = 0.51). The cpDNA phylogeny again estimated Oritrophium s.s. as polyphyletic due to the placement of Novenia, and it showed strong discordance with the nrDNA phylogenies, in particular for O. ollgaardii, O. limnophilum, the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ and one O. repens accession (Figure S3). Our divergence time estimation based on both nrDNA topologies showed similar results. According to the coalescent‐based topology, Oritrophium s.s. (including Novenia) diverged from Linochilus and former Oritrophium s.s. species 6.87 Mya (HPD: 6.63–8.48 Mya; Figure 1; Figure S4). The diversification of Oritrophium s.s. (including Novenia) started 4.75 Mya (HPD: 6.20–4.56 Mya), while members of Oritrophium s.s. from the tropical high Andes began to diversify 3.30 Mya (HPD: 4.43–3.16 Mya). Within this Andean diversification, the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ started to differentiate 1.35 Mya (HPD: 1.84–1.27 Mya).

The distance‐based network generated from Hyb‐Seq data showed reticulation and potential hybridisation between early diverging Oritrophium s.s. accessions and closely related outgroup taxa (Figures S5 and S6). Two ambiguously identified accessions (ORx53 and ORx047xA) showed intermediate positions in the distance‐based network and in a preliminary phylogeny (results not shown).

In the analyses including Oritrophium s.s. and closely related species from other genera, historical introgression was estimated between members of Oritrophium s.s. and close relatives (Linochilus rupestris and former Oritrophium species O. figueirasii, O. venezuelense and O. blepharophyllum) in both approaches. The gene tree‐based approach detected the latter signal according to BLT but not DCT (results not shown).

The SNP‐based analysis, for the same dataset, showed strong signals of introgression for the same three accessions as well as one accession from the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ (ORx026xx; Figure S8; Table S10). Most of these accessions showed potential hybridisation in the distance‐based network.

Genetic Differentiation Based on ddRADseq Data ( O. peruvianum group)

The population genomic analyses of the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ estimated an optimal number of clusters of K = 20 based on cross‐validation values. Our clustering analyses showed that at different values of K, populations often clustered according to geography; thus, the described species were not well supported (Figure S9). Species with a very narrow distribution (O. llanganatense, O. callacallense and O. acerosum) showed high proportions of admixture or were estimated to be distinct depending on the value of K. The distance‐based network showed a high amount of reticulation, which was reduced after removing admixed accessions (Figure 2a; Figure S10). Relationships among these 20 genetic groups based on this filtered distance‐based network were congruent with the geographic distribution of populations, with the exception of a group formed by populations from Venezuela, Colombia and Bolivia (Figure 2a). Also, these results showed the presence of several distinct groups within Ecuador. Estimates of HO were generally low, ranging from 0.02 to 0.11, but were similar to H E, which ranged between 0.01 and 0.13. Estimates of π were low, with values between 0.01 and 0.05, while F IS was generally high, ranging from −0.03 to 0.10 (Table S11). F ST varied from 0.13 to 0.83 (Table S12).

Estimation of Current Gene Flow as a Proxy of Reproductive Isolation Based on ddRADseq Data ( O. peruvianum group)

The estimation of current gene flow revealed low migration rate estimates, ranging from 0.015 to 0.033, which resulted in overall high RI proxy values, ranging from 0.967 to 0.985 (Table S13). Estimates were generally congruent with geographic distribution, with population pairs from geographically proximate localities showing slightly lower RI proxy values than those from more distant localities.

Phenotypic Differentiation Based on Morphological Data ( O. peruvianum group)

Phenotypic differentiation based on PCA showed that the majority of specimens formed a single cluster according to the first three dimensions, which, respectively, explained 28.9%, 9.7% and 7.0% of the variability (Figure 2b). Specimens assigned to O. llanganatense showed some differentiation along the third dimension. Contrarily, specimens assigned to O. tergoalbum showed slight differentiation along the second dimension. The PCA based on the removal of characters with moderate amounts of missing data and replacing missing values with population means showed similar results (Figure S11).

Ecological Differentiation Based on Environmental Data ( O. peruvianum group)

Ecological differentiation based on PCA revealed a single continuous cluster of points (Figure S12). Ecological niche differentiation based on the hypervolume analysis estimated a high to intermediate degree of overlap between the four species analysed, with similarity index values ranging from 0.65 to 0.38 (Figure 2c; Table S14). The highest overlap was estimated between O. crocifolium and O. callacallense, and the lowest overlap values were obtained between O. tergoalbum and both O. peruvianum and O. crocifolium. Besides the overall high to intermediate degree of niche overlap, the niches of O. peruvianum and O. tergoalbum were estimated to be unique to some extent, with average values of 67% and 56%, respectively.

Multidimensional Differentiation ( O. peruvianum group)

Our multidimensional analysis showed that differentiation within the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ likely followed different trajectories along a speciation continuum (Figure 3; Table S15). The first two major axes explained 33.8% and 23.3% of the variation of divergence, respectively, and showed eigenvalues exceeding those expected based on a broken‐stick null model (Figure 3b). The first major axis represented positively correlated measures of edaphic, climatic and genetic differentiation, while the second major axis constituted positively correlated measures of reproductive isolation and geographic differentiation, both being slightly negatively correlated with phenotypic differentiation. Speciation cubes showed population pairs at heterogeneous stages within the speciation continuum (Figure 3a; Figure S13). Discussion

Historical Introgression as a Potential Driver of Diversification in Oritrophium s.s

The acquisition of novel genetic variation via introgression can be advantageous and play an important role in adaptation, particularly when species expand their ranges or are exposed to changing environments (Edelman and Mallet 2021). Introgression is therefore a major driver of biodiversity, especially in plants (Short and Streisfeld 2023; Taylor and Larson 2019), and our results highlight the importance of accounting for introgressive hybridisation in studies on speciation. The transfer of genetic variation through introgression between Oritrophium s.s. and closely related outgroup taxa (Linochilus and former Oritrophium s.s. species) may have played an important role in their adaptation and diversification into high‐elevation ecosystems, as they diverged during the Late Miocene when the Andean orogeny reached its current elevation and the Páramo ecosystem emerged (Garzione et al. 2008; van der Hammen and Cleef 1986). However, an insufficient number of SNPs or the limited variability of the conserved Hyb‐Seq loci could have restricted our inference of introgression. A dataset comprising sufficient genetic variation across groups and specific analyses aimed at detecting introgressed loci and their possible role in adaptation are needed to elucidate this (Folk et al. 2018; Twyford and Ennos 2012). Besides this, the signals of historical introgression detected are consistent with those detected between Diplostephium/Linochilus and closely related genera within Astereae (Baccharis, Blakiella, Heterothalamus, Laennecia, Vargas et al. 2017). Similarly, introgressive hybridisation has been detected across plant lineages from high elevation areas in the Andes, especially in the Páramo (Diplostephium/Linochilus, Vargas et al. 2017; Lachemilla, Morales‐Briones et al. 2018; Lupinus, Nevado et al. 2018; Espeletia, Pouchon et al. 2018; Loricaria, Kandziora et al. 2022; Senecio ser. Culcitium, Salomón et al. 2025). All together, these results suggest that gene flow might have played a major role in the adaptation and diversification of lineages in the tropical high Andes. In fact, introgression between Carex curvula All. ecotypes potentially promoted novel ecological adaptations within high‐elevation grasslands in the Alps (Choler et al. 2004). Likewise, interspecific introgression likely promoted the adaptation and range expansion of Cupressus duclouxiana B. Hickel into high‐elevation habitats in the Qinghai‐Tibet Plateau (Ma et al. 2019).

In addition to adaptive introgression, hybridisation has the potential to generate novel genotypes/phenotypes that eventually may become established lineages through hybrid speciation (Feder et al. 2013; Soltis and Soltis 2009). Heterogeneous and fluctuating environments may promote increased reproductive isolation and differentiation (Abbott et al. 2013; Barton 2001), thus contributing to hybrid speciation. Ecogeographical displacement between hybrid and parental lineages is recurrent in cases of hybrid speciation (Kadereit 2015), and mountainous regions, particularly the Andean Páramo, have the potential to enhance hybrid speciation through cycles of evolutionary processes such as fragmentation, dispersal and intermixing (Flantua and Hooghiemstra 2018). A specimen of Andean Oritrophium s.s. with linear/oblong involucral phyllaries resembling O. limnophilum and with dentate leaf margins resembling O. hieracioides (Cuatrecasas 1997), showed consistent signals of introgression with both potential parental species, O. limnophilum and O. hieracioides. Similar evidence of hybrid origin as seen for this specimen could also be argued for the origin of O. cocuyense and a specimen identified as O. cf. mucidum, but in these cases clear morphological evidence is lacking. These findings suggest that hybridisation contributed to lineage differentiation and possibly diversification within Oritrophium s.s. Given that Andean lineages originated from the Late Pliocene onwards, climatic change during the Pleistocene could have enhanced this process. Furthermore, examples of hybridogenous taxa have been suggested in different Páramo plant groups (Espeletia, Diazgranados 2012; Senecio ser. Culcitium, Dušková et al. 2017). However, studies testing whether these hybrid taxa are genetically, morphologically and ecologically distinct from their parental species (Nieto Feliner et al. 2017) are needed to elucidate the role of hybrid speciation in mountain plant diversification.

Consistent with Brouillet et al. (2009) Oritrophium s.s. was found to be polyphyletic, with Novenia being placed within Oritrophium s.s. However, this is not supported based on morphological evidence. Novenia shares some morphological similarities with Oritrophium s.s., but the presence of several sessile capitula in the centre of the rosette clearly differentiates Novenia from Oritrophium s.s. (Arnelas et al. 2020; Freire 1986; Freire and Hellwig 1990; Nesom 1992). While historical introgression could be behind the polyphyly of Oritrophium s.s., the inconsistency of signals with Novenia does not allow us to rule out ILS. Distinguishing among signatures of hybridisation and ILS can be especially problematic in cases where the divergence between taxa and subsequent hybridisation occurs in a short time interval (Yu et al. 2011). These signals of either ILS or hybridisation prevented us from confirming if Oritrophium s.s. originated in high mountainous regions in Mexico and subsequently reached the tropical high Andes via long‐distance dispersal, or if it instead originated in the Central Andes to later disperse to Mexico, as previously suggested by Salomón et al. (2022). Analyses including the second species from Mexico (O. durangense G.L. Nesom) could help to elucidate the origin and diversity within Oritrophium s.s. Likewise, while some signals of introgression were detected in O. ollgaardii, the inconsistency among approaches leaves the origin of this species unclear. Oritrophium ollgaardii is clearly differentiated based on the presence of malpighiaceous trichomes on the leaves and the scape, a character that is absent in other Oritrophium s.s. species (Cuatrecasas 1985, 1997). Further analyses employing a population‐level sampling that covers the genetic variability within species could help to resolve the origin of Novenia and O. ollgaardii.

Oritrophium peruvianum group: Distinct Species or Different Speciation Stages and Trajectories Across a Continuum?

Our delimitation analyses based on genetic, phenotypic and ecological criteria failed to find consistent support for the majority of species described within the ‘ O. peruvianum group’. According to the morphological traits measured, we found differentiation, although weak, only in O. llanganatense, pointed out as a distinct lineage in previous phylogenetic work (Salomón et al. 2022), and in O. tergoalbum. This overall lack of differentiation agrees with the complex taxonomy reported for this group and reflects that some of the differences among species rely on overlapping taxonomic features (mainly leaf characters, Arnelas et al. 2020; Cuatrecasas 1997). Similarly, our analyses support that, independently of species assignment, geographic isolation contributed to shaping genetic differentiation within the group. This pattern is consistent with studies that highlighted geography as one of the major drivers of diversification in plant groups from the Páramo (Linochilus, Vargas et al. 2023; Espeletia, Diazgranados and Barber 2017; Senecio ser. Culcitium, Dušková et al. 2017). At the same time, high‐elevation habitats are a mosaic of micro‐environments (Cortés and Wheeler 2018), which may drive local adaptation (Gonzalo‐Turpin and Hazard 2009), ecological divergence (Cortés et al. 2018) or ecological speciation (Gorospe et al. 2025). However, the ecological niche overlap within the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ was high, even in the case of the widespread species O. peruvianum , which appears to have a broad and continuous niche that overlaps with the rest of the described species. These findings might to some extent be the result of a lack of resolution of the global environmental data that we used, but they could also reflect a pattern of niche conservatism, in which taxa tend to retain their niches and related traits over time (Peterson 2011). Overall, the lack of support according to genetic, phenotypic and ecological criteria for species within the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ suggests that the diversity within the group should be assessed from the perspective of the speciation continuum.

A multivariate view of speciation as a continuum enables us to account for different processes and trace distinct routes to speciation among pairs of diverging taxa (Bolnick et al. 2023). Our analysis, including six measures of divergence, suggests that these populations are at different speciation stages and likely followed heterogeneous speciation trajectories. Various degrees of divergence across the speciation continuum, as a result of different evolutionary trajectories, have also been detected in marine snails (Littorina, Johannesson et al. 2024) and cyanobacteria (Microcoleus, Stanojković et al. 2024). As research focusing on a multivariate view of the speciation continuum has only recently been intensified, it can be expected that more examples will be found in other groups of organisms. In the ‘ O. peruvianum group’, one example of different evolutionary trajectories is illustrated in the negative correlation between geographic and phenotypic differentiation; this negative correlation is probably reflecting the high phenotypic variability found in southern Ecuador, which harbours the majority of diversity (ca. 83%) within the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ (Arnelas et al. 2020; Salomón et al. 2022). We obtained high estimates of reproductive isolation across the speciation continuum, likely as a result of the use of current gene flow as a proxy for reproductive isolation. Given that the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ started to diversify 1.35 Mya, our reproductive isolation measures could be an overestimate, as the low migration rates estimated likely reflect reproductive as well as geographic barriers. Despite the limitations of the use of a reproductive isolation proxy, the combination of the differentiation measures we estimated still reflects a speciation continuum with incomplete reproductive isolation at lower values and complete or near‐complete reproductive isolation at higher values. Notably, some population pairs deviate from this trend and have lower phenotypic differentiation in relation to other measures such as geographic, climatic and genetic differentiation. A similar pattern of heterogeneous divergence trends across populations has been observed in marine snails (Johannesson et al. 2024). In our study, this pattern could be the result of stabilising selective pressure acting on particular morphological traits that are maintained despite divergence across other traits (Grenier et al. 2016), or by other processes such as secondary contact followed by reinforcement or another period of isolation (Bolnick et al. 2023). While the stabilising selective pressure scenario seems unlikely, given the past environmental changes hypothesised for the Andean Páramo (Flantua and Hooghiemstra 2018; Hooghiemstra and Van der Hammen 2004), scenarios of secondary contact with subsequent geographic isolation have been proposed as drivers of diversity in the Páramo (Flantua et al. 2019; Nevado et al. 2018). Besides this, we cannot rule out that high phenotypic variability between individuals within a population decreases differentiation among populations, but these cases are rare in our dataset.

Within the ‘ O. peruvianum group’, at one end of the speciation continuum we find population pairs that are morphologically and genetically similar, likely due to weak reproductive barriers among them, while at the opposite end we find strongly differentiated populations that might even be reproductively isolated from each other. Strikingly, some population pairs from geographically close localities (OR_56–OR_15 and OR_55–OR_33) show similar reproductive isolation and lower genetic and phenotypic differentiation than pairs from sympatric or almost sympatric localities (OR_15–OR_33 and OR_55–OR_56). These values of reproductive isolation and differentiation could be explained by past geographic isolation in combination with other barriers, such as distinct ecological pressures and/or non‐random mating derived from differences in flower phenology and pollinator visitation. Divergent selection coupled with some extent of geographic isolation is an important driver of differentiation at the lower end of the speciation continuum in sticklebacks (Gasterosteus, Hendry et al. 2009). Examples of geographic isolation and ecological factors driving divergence in mountainous environments have been reported in plant groups from the Andean Páramo (Espeletia, Cortés et al. 2018; Pouchon et al. 2021; Lupinus, Nevado et al. 2018; and Lobelioideae, Lagomarsino et al. 2016), the Afroalpine region (giant lobelias, Brochmann et al. 2022; Knox and Palmer 1998; and Dendrosenecio, Gorospe et al. 2025), and the Himalaya‐Hengduan Mountains (Taxus, Liu et al. 2013). In the middle of the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ continuum, pairs of populations tend to have intermediate differentiation values which, in most cases, seem to be the result of geographic isolation. Similarly, populations that diverged mainly in parapatry or allopatry, in some cases followed by secondary contact, often occur at intermediate stages of the speciation continuum in sticklebacks (Hendry et al. 2009). Lastly, at the higher end of differentiation, population pairs representing O. llanganatense and other species like O. acerosum or O. peruvianum (Figure S14) show high differentiation across all measures, suggesting that speciation is near or past completion. Confirmation of whether these taxa are different species would require experimental work to estimate the strength of reproductive barriers, ecological adaptations and phenotypic plasticity (Lascoux et al. 2016; Scheiner 2002; Sobel and Chen 2014).

In conclusion, our results suggest that historical introgression played an important role in the evolution of Oritrophium s.s. and close relatives, highlighting the importance of testing for introgression to disentangle the complex reticulate evolution of recently diverged groups from mountainous environments, like those from the Páramo flora. Currently described species within the ‘ O. peruvianum group’ were not fully supported, likely due to heterogeneous speciation stages and trajectories associated with periods of geographic isolation and secondary contact possibly resulting from Pleistocene climatic oscillations.
