Onlay repair for abdominal wall hernias: a valid alternative. Systematic review and comparison with international standards
Authors: Allison Cascante-Gutiérrez, Jesús Martínez-Hoed, José Angel Ortiz-Cubero, Bueno-Lledó José, Salvador Pous-Serrano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ventral hernias are a common surgical challenge for general surgeons and several techniques have been described to repair it. The mesh positioning for the repair is a factor frequently mentioned as a major contributor to the outcome of the operation. Although onlay mesh repair is considered the simplest approach for ventral hernia repair, recent studies have led to its stigmatization. Even so, it could still be a reasonable option. Objective: to evaluate the complications and recurrences documented with this technique and compare them to international quality standards for ventral hernia repair outcomes. Method: Systematic review of the literature using PubMed, Cochrane, and EMBASE, following PRISMA criteria. Search terms “onlay hernia repair”, from January 2020 to October 2024. The results were evaluated and compared to standard international results. The following data were extracted: MINORS criteria, number of patients, age, body mass index, comorbidities, variables related to hernia characteristics, including prior surgical repair, defect diameter, classification, surgical complications, including general complications, surgical site infections, seromas, hematomas, death, recurrence, and the follow-up period. Results: Ten papers were analyzed, including 910 patients. The average age among the patients was 55.6 years, and 39.4% of the sample population was male. Their mean BMI was 31.4, and 36% had been diagnosed with cardiovascular disease. The incisional hernias were 74.6%, with 285 patients with midline hernias. The hernia defect diameter was 6.4 cm. The surgical complications: infections 10.1%, seroma 11.8%, hematoma 2,8%, wound dehiscence 4.4%, mortality 0.78%, recurrence 5.4% with median follow-up of 12 months. Conclusions: The results of this review compared to international standards for hernia repair, show that onlay mesh appears is a suitable option for ventral hernia surgery, with low recurrence, complications and mortality rates. However, there is missing information in patient’s comorbidities, hernia characteristics, and complications, emphasize the importance of structured and systematic methodologies in future research.

Introduction

Ventral hernias are a common surgical challenge for general surgeons. These hernias include acquired incisional defects and primary defects in the abdominal fascia as umbilical, paraumbilical, epigastric, and Spigelian hernias [1]. Several techniques have been described to repair ventral hernias ranging from open to laparoscopic repair. While the surgical approach also plays a role, the main difference is the mesh positioning, a factor frequently mentioned as a major contributor to the outcome of the operation [2, 3]. Onlay mesh repair is considered the simplest way to address ventral hernias, given that dissection is performed on the surface of the anterior abdominal fascia, where the anatomic landmarks to fix the mesh are simple [2]. However, this technique involves extensive dissection, and large cutaneous flaps to develop that plane, predisposing patients to skin necrosis, seroma, surgical site infections, and infection of prosthetic material [3].

Recent studies have stigmatized onlay mesh repair [4, 5], but it could still be an acceptable option for abdominal wall surgery. Our goal was to evaluate the incidence of complications and recurrences documented with this technique by systematically reviewing available literature. In addition, we aimed to compare the outcomes to international quality standards for ventral hernia repair outcomes, to identify whether its application is still appropriate.

Methods

The databases PubMed, Cochrane, and EMBASE were systematically reviewed following PRISMA criteria (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses) [6], using the search terms “onlay hernia repair”, from January 2020 to October 2024, by three of the authors [ACG, JMH, JAOC]. The findings were evaluated and compared against internationally recognized standards.

This review included articles published in English or Spanish, during the selected time period, including series and patient reports with ventral hernia who underwent onlay hernia repair. The exclusion criteria were titles related to laparoscopic hernia repair, diastasis recti without hernia, prophylactic mesh use, and the combination of onlay mesh repair in conjunction with other techniques during the same surgery, for example component separation.

The following data were extracted from the selected articles: MINORS criteria (Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies) [7], number of patients with onlay mesh repair, age, body mass index, respiratory and cardiovascular comorbidities, diabetes, immunosuppression, and chronic renal disease. In addition, variables related to hernia characteristics, including prior surgical repair, defect diameter, classification as primary or secondary hernias, and localization details (midline or lateral) for incisional hernias, were included.

Surgical complications, including general complications, surgical site infections, seromas, hematomas, fistulae, intestinal obstruction, recurrence, and death, were analyzed. These data were arranged in tables, incorporating the total number of occurrences, averages, mean deviations, and ranges. The follow-up period in which these complications were recorded was also considered.

Desirable outcomes in hernia surgery according to international guidelines were extracted from the available literature in terms of surgical complications, surgical site infection, and recurrence [6–11]. This information was used as a comparison point for the outcomes reported in the systematic review.

Results

The initial search identified 298 publications. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria following the PRISMA phases, 10 articles were selected for data extraction. Figure 1 illustrates the PRISMA phases of article selection.

Following abstract screening, 12 studies were excluded: 1 narrative review, 4 systematic reviews, 1 meta-analysis, 2 studies lacking patient samples, 1 commentary, and 3 articles unrelated to the study topic (2 addressing prophylaxis and 1 on component separation). The full texts of 14 articles were then assessed. Of these, 3 were excluded due to insufficient data and 1 for combining onlay mesh with component separation. This process resulted in 10 articles for data analysis. All the articles included to be analyzed were written in English.

The average MINORS score across studies was 19. Only one article was non-comparative, scoring 13 out of a maximum of 16 points. The remaining nine studies were comparative, with a maximum possible score of 24, at least four of which achieved a score of ≥ 20 points.

Population

A total of 910 patients who underwent onlay repair were reported in the analyzed articles. The average age was 55.6 years (range 39–66), with 327 (39.40%) male patients. The average BMI was 31.4% (range 26–42), although only 6 publications reported it. The following comorbidities were observed: pulmonary disease (15%), cardiovascular disease (36%), diabetes (13%), smoking (21%), neoplasia (16%), chronic renal disease (5.7%), and 28% presenting other risk factors. Hernia characteristics

Most hernias reported in this review were incisional hernias (74.6%), whereas only 25.4% were primary hernias. We classified the incisional hernias according to their location, into midline and lateral hernias, given the heterogeneous distribution. Midline hernias accounted for 285 patients; 4 studies did not provide information. Lateral hernias were reported in 35 patients, and 2 articles did not report this data. Complications and recurrence

The complications and follow-up times are summarized in Table 3. General wound complications were recorded in 159 patients, although five studies did not provide these data. Surgical site infections were reported in 89 patients (10.1%). One study, which included 28 patients, did not provide data on this complication. Seroma occurred in 105 patients (11.8%), one study of 17 patients did not provide this information. The recurrence rate was 5.4%. Other complications were infrequent, including hematoma (2.8%), dehiscence (4.4%), and fistula (1 patient). Mortality was rare, with only four deaths (0.78%) reported in the five studies providing these data. The median postoperative follow-up was 12 months (R 1–60), two articles do not report follow up.

International standards and results in abdominal wall hernia repair

International hernia societies have set goals regarding surgical outcomes to certify Abdominal Wall Surgery Units as high-quality centers of reference. These values are ideal for making comparisons; although specific targets may differ across organizations, they serve as reference benchmarks [17, 18].

Postoperative complications within 30 days after elective ventral hernia repair differ across registries. According to the Danish national Database, the 30-day readmission rate following primary umbilical or epigastric hernia repair is 5%, primarily due to wound infection, hematoma, seroma, and pain, with a reoperation rate of 0.3% and a mortality rate of 0.2%. In contrast, for elective incisional hernia repair, the same registry reports 30-day readmission, reoperation, and mortality rates of 13.3%, 2.2%, and 0.5%, respectively [17, 19].

Additionally, 31,664 patients with ventral hernias were enrolled in the Herniamed Hernia Registry between 2009 and 2013. Data on surgical techniques, postoperative complication rates, and 1-year follow-up outcomes were analyzed, revealing postoperative surgical complication rates of 3.2% for umbilical hernias, 3.5% for epigastric hernias, and 9.2% for incisional hernias. The corresponding reoperation rates were 1.0%, 1.2%, and 4.2%, respectively [17, 20].

The role of accreditation programs is equally significant. The accreditation program for hernia centers by the German Hernia Society and the German Society for General and Visceral Surgery, requires infection and reintervention rates < 10% for open incisional hernia repair [17]. Similarly, the Italian certification program describes relevant targets depending on the complexity of the surgery. The mortality rate within 30 days of surgery should be < 1% for simple abdominal wall reconstruction and < 5% for complex surgery. Similarly, morbidity in simple surgery must be less than 30% and < 50% for complex surgery. In addition, the acceptable rates of surgical site infection are < 10% in simple surgery, and < 30% in complex surgery. The same program stipulates recurrence rates of < 5% at annual follow-ups, and < 10% at three years for simple surgery, whereas for complex surgery, the rates are 5% at one year and 20% at three years [21].

According to the ACS Risk Calculator, the estimated risk of complications from ventral hernia surgery includes: wound infection during open surgery 5.8%, reintervention 3.7%, pneumonia 0.8%, urinary tract infection 0.7%, deep vein thrombosis 0.5%, cardiovascular complications less than 1%, renal failure less than 1%, and death less than 1%. The risk of any complication for open ventral hernia repair is 10.1% [22].

The international standards used for comparisons in this review are an overall wound complication rate < 10%, a surgical site infection rate < 10%, a recurrence rate < 5%, and surgery-related mortality < 1%.

Discussion

The repair of abdominal wall hernias is a surgical challenge that can be addressed through multiple approaches. The European Hernia Society (EHS) and Americas Hernia Society (AHS) have established treatment guidelines for ventral hernias, recommending mesh-based repair for midline incisional defects [23]. For umbilical or epigastric hernias with defects measuring 0 to 1 cm, either sutured or mesh repair is suitable, whereas larger defects require mesh reinforcement [24].

A propensity score-matched cohort study was recently published that compared the outcomes of open mesh repair and suture repair for hernias of less than 1 cm, with 590 patients in each group. Both techniques are considered effective and safe, with the mesh group demonstrating lower recurrence rates after 2 years and no significant differences in postoperative pain [25]. Additionally, a European Hernia Society (EHS) revision found that mesh repair was associated with a significantly lower risk of recurrence compared to suture repair. When studies were pooled by mesh position (onlay or retrorectus), both mesh positions demonstrated significantly lower recurrence rates than suture repair [23].

This systematic review analyzed 10 medium-to-high quality studies, with an average MINORS score of 19, encompassing 910 patients who underwent onlay mesh repair. The recurrence rate was 5.4%, not only consistent with the international standard (< 5%), but even lower than the 7.3% reported by the European Hernia Society (EHS). As part of the development of EHS guidelines, systematic literature searches were conducted to identify all relevant clinical and health evidence by addressing predefined key questions. Five randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were reviewed to compare the outcomes of mesh versus suture repair for incisional hernias. Among them, three specifically evaluated onlay mesh repair, involving 237 patients, with a recurrence rate of 7.3% (9/124) [23]. Given the heterogeneity of the reviewed studies, a meta-analytic approach was not feasible.

In addition to the surgical technique, there are several other factors that affect recurrence after a hernia repair. Common risk factors are age, high BMI, chronic illnesses, diabetes mellitus, size of the defect, wound infection and early physical exertion following surgery [2]. In this review, the most frequently reported comorbidity was cardiovascular disease, followed by smoking and respiratory disease. However, not all authors reported the same comorbidities. One of the studies with 50 patients even listed usual comorbidities as exclusion criteria: obesity, recurrent incisional hernia repair, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cardiovascular disease, being immunocompromised, having a history of immunosuppressive drug intake, or a history of any malignancy [11]. Furthermore, the characteristics of the hernia defects differed significantly among the selected articles in this review. This variation makes direct comparisons challenging. For example, Fonseca et al. focused exclusively on a group of female patients with small hernia defects, whereas Korkut et al. reports an average defect diameter of 13 cm, which is indicative of more complex hernia repairs [13, 14].

For incisional hernias, a substantial percentage of recurrences occur within the first year (35.19%), progressively declining over time: 20.53% in the second year, 9.98% in the third, 6.14% in the fourth, and 7.02% in the fifth year [26]. The median follow-up time in this review was 12 months, which is sufficient to capture a meaningful proportion of recurrences. However, it is important to note that two of the reviewed studies did not report follow-up data, while three documented follow-up periods ranging from 1 to 6 months.

According to the literature, when performing onlay mesh repair, since the mesh is placed more superficially, it increases the risk of seroma formation, as well as the risk of surgical site infection [2]. However, in this study we reported an incidence of 10,1% for SSI, which is comparable to international standards. These results are compatible with those of another systematic review including 6 RCT that reported 27 out of 268 patients (10.07%) with reported surgical site infection after onlay mesh repair [2].

The incidence of seroma in our review was only 11.8%, which was lower than that reported in previous systematic reviews. For example, Pereira et al., in a systematic review in 2023 published findings of seroma in 72 of 268 patients (26.8%) [2].

On the other hand, SSO was reported in 159 patients in this revision. Nonetheless, it is not representative, given that only 5 of the 10 articles analyzed offered data. In addition, Bot Ianu et al., recorded only complications that required treatment in the operating room were recorded, leaving out other minor SSOs [10].

The mortality of hernia repairs is typically low in the literature; it is usually less than 1% according to international standards. Similarly, only four deaths were reported (0.78%) from the five papers that provided this information.

Limitations

This review is subject to several limitations that must be considered when interpreting the results. Only 10 studies met the inclusion criteria, and the patient populations within them are highly heterogeneous in terms of demographics, hernia type (e.g., primary ventral, incisional, midline, and lateral), and hernia size. These clinical and methodological variables are known to influence postoperative outcomes following hernia repair. Moreover, due to the lack of stratification in the published data, it is not feasible to reliably differentiate cases based on hernia type or complexity.

Additionally, key parameters are inconsistently reported across studies, making it difficult to conduct significant direct comparisons or meta-analyses. For example, minor surgical site occurrences (SSOs) were underreported due to study protocols that recorded only major complications requiring reintervention. Further limitations include variability in follow-up duration, with two studies omitting this information. Such inconsistencies weaken the overall strength and generalizability of the conclusions.

Taken together, the limited number of studies, high heterogeneity, lack of stratified data, and incomplete reporting significantly limit the strength of the conclusions that can be drawn. These challenges complicate the overall assessment and underscore the need for more rigorous methodological frameworks in future research.

Conclusions

When the results of this review are compared to international standards for hernia repair, onlay mesh appears to be a suitable option for ventral hernia surgery, demonstrating low recurrence and mortality rates that align with international benchmarks. However, there is currently insufficient evidence to extend recommendations regarding which specific subgroups of patients might particularly benefit from this intervention, primarily owing to inconsistencies in data reporting. Variations in patients’ comorbidities and hernia characteristics, along with differences in reported complications, emphasize the importance of structured and systematic methodologies in future research.
