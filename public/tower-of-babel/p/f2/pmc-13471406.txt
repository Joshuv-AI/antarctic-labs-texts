Blood pressure screening event at hispanic community organization yields positive screenings and links to primary care
Authors: Adwait Chafale, Thomas B. Morland, Deborah Mills, Rossanna Gabriel, George Ruiz, Alexander Chang, Caroline deRichemond, Michael Kovalick, Kathryn Hall, Tracey Leitzel, Bobbie L. Johannes, Katrina Romagnoli, Maria Welch, Mallory Hammer, Allison Hess, H. Lester Kirchner, Annemarie G. Hirsch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertension affects nearly half of U.S. adults and drives adverse cardiovascular and renal outcomes. Hispanic populations have similar prevalence to the general population but lower control. Targeted interventions leveraging community partnerships could mitigate this problem.ObjectiveThis study evaluated a hypertension screening event in Hazleton, PA tied to a monthly community dinner, aiming to increase hypertension awareness, identify uncontrolled hypertension, address health-related social needs, and link patients to a federally-qualified health center look-alike (FQHC).MethodsA hypertension screening event was conducted at a community dinner, with patients offered education and FQHC appointments. Staffing included 22 FQHC employees, a health plan wellness associate, 7 community organization members, and the research team. The event was advertised on local Spanish-language radio and publications. The menu included low-sodium options, developed in consultation with the FQHC dietician. Bilingual screening and materials were available. A concurrent mixed-methods evaluation was conducted using the RE-AIM framework. Quantitative measures included demographics, blood pressure, primary care status, and scheduling status with the FQHC. Qualitative interviews to assess the event were conducted with community members, community organization staff, and healthcare team members during and after the event to understand their experiences.ResultsAmong 145 individuals screened, 140 completed the survey. Seventy-two (72) participants (51%) reported age > 50 years, 102 (73%) reported female sex, 54 (39%) did not have primary care, 58 (41%) reported diagnoses of hypertension. Fifty-nine (59) participants (42%) had blood pressure in the hypertensive range, including 24 (17%) who had never been diagnosed with hypertension. Twenty-six (26) individuals scheduled with the FQHC. Surveys showed high acceptability, though participants requested more interpreters and expanded screenings (e.g., diabetes).ConclusionThe event engaged the Hispanic community, identified undiagnosed hypertension, and connected participants to care. Similar efforts could advance chronic disease management in other underserved populations and conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28023-7.

Introduction

Nearly half of United States (U.S.) adults have hypertension, a leading contributor to cardiovascular disease and stroke [1, 2]. Although Hispanic Americans overall have similar prevalence of hypertension to the general U.S. population, those of Caribbean origin (e.g., Dominican Republic, Puerto Rico, Cuba) have higher incidence of hypertension compared to other Hispanic populations [2, 3, 4]. Overall, Hispanic patients with hypertension have lower rates of awareness, antihypertensive treatment, and blood pressure control compared to other groups [5]. Barriers to blood pressure control in the Hispanic population include healthcare and insurance access, language, and other cultural and socioeconomic factors [6, 7]. Strategies are therefore needed to identify and better manage hypertension among Hispanic communities, especially those of Caribbean origin.

Community-clinical linkage is a promising strategy to improve population health among disadvantaged populations, which has been promoted by public health leaders and has shown early promise for improving cardiometabolic risk factors, especially weight and glycemic control [8, 9]. However, efforts to control hypertension through community-clinical links have struggled with issues including low enrollment, barriers to establishing community-clinical linkages, and difficulty tracking and improving blood pressure [9, 10]. Indeed, a recent evaluation of community-clinical linkages through the National Hypertension Control Initiative highlighted that not all projects were successful in establishing such links, and even successful examples struggled with enrollment. The authors noted multiple barriers, including inconsistencies in community-based organizations’ capabilities and the challenges they experienced developing new capabilities on tight timelines. Careful evaluation of capabilities of both community-based organizations and clinical partners was recommended to design more effective linkages [10].

An implementation science approach could help understand and overcome barriers to successful establishment of community-clinical linkages to address hypertension in underserved populations. In this article, we present a concurrent mixed methods evaluation of a hypertension screening and clinical linkage event conducted by a federally qualified health center (FQHC) look-alike and a community organization serving a largely Hispanic population of mostly Caribbean origin, utilizing the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance (RE-AIM) framework [11]. The event was designed to leverage the community-based organization’s relationships with community members and the healthcare capabilities of the FQHC look-alike. The event was hosted by the Hazleton Integration Project (HIP), a community organization, and integrated into a widely-attended monthly community dinner. Blood pressure screening, education, and scheduling of follow-up care were performed at HIP’s site by visiting staff from the FQHC (CommunityCare, affiliated with Geisinger) and the wellness division of its affiliated health plan (Geisinger Health Plan).

We tested the hypothesis that bringing clinical capabilities to the gathering place of a community expected to have high rates of undiagnosed and/or uncontrolled hypertension would identify cases of hypertension and facilitate links to clinical care. Concurrent qualitative analysis sought to characterize stakeholders’ (i.e., community members’, community organization personnel’s, and healthcare workers’) experiences with the event and explain the quantitative results. The event was held in Hazleton, PA, which is 63% Hispanic according to United States Census, with a 27% overall poverty rate [12]. Hazleton’s Hispanic population is mostly of Caribbean origin and underserved, making it an ideal place to implement and assess a novel strategy to create community-clinical linkages as a strategy to improve hypertension identification and treatment in an at-risk population.

Methods

Population and intervention

A four-hour blood pressure screening event for adults, 18 years and older, was conducted associated with a standing monthly community dinner hosted by the HIP. HIP is housed in a retired Catholic school in Hazleton, PA and provides a range of services to a mostly (but not exclusively) Hispanic community, including childcare, food bank services, science enrichment for high school students, English classes, music classes, art programs, citizenship classes, and a food prescription program. The community dinner itself is a free two-hour event from 4 PM to 6 PM in the former school’s cafeteria and is attended by 300–600 community members monthly. The screening event was held in the former gymnasium next to the entrance and on the way to the cafeteria, with HIP staff encouraging community members to stop for blood pressure screening on their way to dinner.

The blood pressure event was advertised on Spanish-language radio, in both Spanish- and English-language newspapers, and through flyers distributed throughout the community along with other HIP services. Recognizing that activities related to the dinner typically begin before the dinner itself, the blood pressure event began two hours before the dinner began and continued until the dinner ended.

The event was organized by staff from the Department of Population Health Sciences of Geisinger Health System with support by a learning collaborative with representatives of local government, Geisinger Health Plan, academic, and community organizations, as well as a network of Geisinger-affiliated FQHC look-alike clinics. This learning collaborative was developed over the course of two years through the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)’s Innovative Cardiovascular Health Initiative, which provides competitive grants to such learning collaboratives to identify novel collaborative approaches to achieving improved cardiovascular population health. The CDC grant funds a core research team in Geisinger’s Department of Population Health Sciences, which holds quarterly “all hands” meetings with representatives of each learning collaborative member and individual meetings with stakeholders as needed for specific initiatives, including the blood pressure screening event. The learning collaborative enjoys support from senior leadership at each organization, who attend meetings and events personally and/or appoint high-ranking delegates to ensure the collaborative’s success. Non-Geisinger attendees at “all hands” meetings are offered modest honoraria to support their time.

Screening activities were conducted primarily by 22 FQHC look-alike employees, including nine nurses (some bilingual), two physicians, three schedulers, and two certified medical interpreters. Additional interpretation services were provided by HIP volunteers. Medically-trained research staff contributed as needed in addition to evaluating the event. Participants were invited to an education area where tables included a nutritionist from CommunityCare, representatives of Geisinger cardiology, and wellness/community engagement staff from Geisinger Health Plan (bilingual). Bilingual hypertension education materials were from the American Heart Association (AHA) were distributed with AHA consent.

Individuals undergoing screening were asked to complete a brief, anonymous survey, described below. They then underwent screening using validated, automated blood pressure cuffs, with attention to best practices in accurate blood pressure management (e.g., correct cuff size, feet flat on floor, repeating blood pressure check after sitting for several minutes if initial pressure was high). Participants were informed of their results and provided with the AHA Blood Pressure Categories graphic in their preferred languages, [13, 14] with their ranges (including normal range, if applicable) circled. Participants were then directed to the education tables and FQHC look-alike schedulers.

Evaluation

Design

A concurrent mixed methods design was utilized within a RE-AIM framework and elements of the Quality Enhancement Research Initiative (QUERI) Roadmap [11, 15]. The QUERI Roadmap was used as a step-by-step implementation science guide in developing the program to ensure that best practices were considered in the design of the event and its evaluation. RE-AIM was the primary evaluation framework, although patient-centered outcomes referenced in the QUERI Roadmap (e.g., acceptability, timeliness, and safety) were incorporated into the evaluation to maximize the breadth of the patient perspective incorporated into the evaluation. The quantitative components sought to understand the reach and yield of the screening event, whereas the qualitative component sought to understand the experiences of key stakeholder groups (participants, healthcare workers, and community organization members) and to explain how stakeholders’ experiences with the event and its procedures impacted reach and yield. The mixing strategy utilized the qualitative results to explain the quantitative results and gain insights into how the event’s model could be improved, scaled, or adapted in the future.

Data collection

Quantitative clinical data collection

All individuals who were screened were asked to complete a demographic questionnaire in their preferred languages, English or Spanish. The questionnaire was developed specifically for this event and is available for review in the Supplemental Materials (see Intake Form). Questionnaires were administered verbally based upon paper guides prepared in English and Spanish in advance and provided to evaluation staff members. Each participant’s responses were written on a paper printout of the intake form, along with the blood pressure obtained in their screening. Key quantitative outcomes included the measured blood pressures, basic patient demographics, whether each patient had a primary care provider (PCP), and whether each patient had been told they had high blood pressure in the past.

Mixed methods questionnaire data collection

Sampling: Purposive sampling was utilized to the degree possible with a small event, attempting to understand the views of screened participants, as well as individuals with various roles on the healthcare and community organization teams. A target of at least five individuals from each group (participants, community organization staff, and healthcare workers) was set. Recruitment of screened individuals was facilitated by the HIP staff and by research staff inviting participation at the event. For Spanish-speaking participants, interpreters were available to assist with survey interpretation if the interviewer did not themselves speak Spanish.

Questionnaire Completion: Three follow-up questionnaires consisting of interview guides, capturing quantitative and qualitative data, were developed for screened individuals, healthcare workers, and HIP representatives, respectively (see Supplemental Materials). The questionnaires asked participants to rate various aspects of the event on a 7-point Likert scale ranged from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 4 (“neutral”) to 7 (“strongly agree”). Open-ended questions were asked to explain these ratings. See Follow-up Questionnaires section in the supplemental materials. These questionnaires, hereafter termed “follow-up questionnaires” were conducted in-person by research team staff as participants finished their screenings and continued during event wrap-up (e.g., clean-up), with additional questionnaires (particularly of healthcare team members and community organization members) administered by telephone and/or email up to 3 months after the event.

Analysis

A concurrent mixed methods analysis sought to understand both the quantitative yield of positive hypertension screens and FQHC appointments scheduled at the event, and what factors led to the event’s quantitative outcomes, according to the key RE-AIM constructs of reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, and maintenance [11]. The primary research team consisted of a physician undergoing implementation science training, a medical student, a leader of the community organization with a background in qualitative research, with support from an epidemiologist, biostatisticians, primary care, cardiology and nephrology experts, qualitative research methodologists, and a human-centered design expert.

To evaluate reach, the research team generated summary statistics on the served population, based upon questionnaire responses. The yield of the event was evaluated by tallying the number of participants with blood pressures in each blood pressure category (normal, elevated, stage I hypertension, stage II hypertension, hypertensive urgency/emergency) per the 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association Task Force Guidelines [16]. Blood pressure results were reported overall and by whether or not individuals reported an existing hypertension diagnosis and by whether they reported having a PCP. The number of patients in each category who established care with the FQHC look-alike was computed.

Questionnaire/interview coding and analysis

Mixed methods questionnaires/interviews were analyzed and coded by three investigators (A.C., T.B.M., D.M.) utilizing Miro software (a virtual whiteboard; free online version, available at miro.com). A mix of inductive and deductive reasoning approaches was used, with team members collectively grouping data deductively mapped to a structured format corresponding to RE-AIM domains, and incorporating patient-centered QUERI Roadmap concepts as above. Within this structure, thematic analysis searching for themes within and across grouped data utilized a consensus-driven inductive approach. As such, no formal codebook was developed or used. Coding disagreements did not occur, because all data sorting and analysis was done collectively by the group in real time using the Miro collaboration software, following the pre-specified structure of the questionnaires and interview guides.

The mixing analysis was primarily convergent, as the majority of qualitative interviews were conducted during the event itself, before quantitative results were available. That said, there were features of a sequential explanatory design, because the primary intent of the qualitative interviews was to explain the quantitative results and some of the qualitative interviews were conducted after the event. The mixing strategy involved discussion of each RE-AIM domain at the group-level among evaluation team members, FQHC leaders, and community organization leaders regarding the meaning of quantitative results and the explanatory insights gained by thematic analyses. Evaluation of the event itself focused on reach and effectiveness. Findings related to adoption, implementation, and maintenance were incorporated into planning discussions of the learning collaborative, which supported this event and its evaluation and will seek to implement sustainable community-clinical links to address hypertension and other cardiovascular health determinants among underserved patients in the region.

Qualitative interview themes

Community partners thought that the event should be repeated because it identified and partially met a community need, but there is more work to do. Partners indicated the event provided “access to healthcare,” “some place to go for help”, and “good conversation” that served many community members while ”550 meals”1 were provided at the same time, acknowledging that the gap between the number of screenings and meals may be since people were “fearful or did not hear [about the event] in time.” Participants expressed diverse opinions on the location of the event. Participants did indicate that the event should be expanded in other location but felt it should also be repeated at HIP.

1Includes both children and adults.

Additional findings

Most participants reported no financial cost to themselves other than effort. Most participants walked to the event or got a ride, with some using personal automobiles and some taking taxis. Most participants traveled less than 2 miles, though some travelled up to 20 miles to attend. Asked to suggest improvements, participants suggested more advertising, more screenings, and adding screening locations.

Barriers

Participants reported few barriers to participating, although one participant indicated childcare was a barrier. Healthcare workers noted challenges finding the right cuff size (although four cuff sizes were provided) and community partners noted how noisy the event was and that room layout prevented some participants from visiting certain stations.

Facilitators

Participants came for a variety of reasons. Key themes included being at HIP/dinner anyway, social invitation, and personal history. Multiple participants noted that they attended the blood pressure event, in part, because it was integrated into their routine of attending the monthly dinner. Specifically, participants cited the dinner as a facilitator indicating that it was “convenient after eating” and part of “normal routine.” Others noted “friend” as a motivator. Other participants cited “takes [blood pressure measurements] regularly” as a reason to attend the event.

Discussion

The present study evaluated a blood pressure screening event run by FQHC employees embedded in a monthly community dinner run by a community organization. Key findings included a high participation rate, high yield of positive screens for high blood pressure, a high rate of scheduling primary care appointments at the FQHC, and a general consensus among stakeholders that the event succeeded in creating a functional community-clinical linkage to address hypertension in its target population. The overall experience of the screening event supported the hypothesis that an effective community clinical linkage could be created by building upon the core strengths of each partner, namely the ability of the community organization to draw a large crowd open to receiving healthcare services and the ability of the FQHC look-alike team to implement healthcare tasks at the community organization site quickly and effectively. This study provides early proof of concept for an approach to building community-clinical linkages for identification and management hypertension. This success creating a community-clinical linkage – a task which has proven challenging in past attempts – may reflect application of best practices identified in the National Hypertension Control Initiative [10]. In particular, the current initiative overcame the previously observed pitfall of attempting to develop clinical capabilities among community organizations, especially on short timelines. Instead, a linkage was created by bringing healthcare personnel from an established clinic to the community organization site at the time of a regular gathering. The event may have been particularly effective because it leveraged entire working units from each participating organization, with the community organization, the FQHC look-alike, and the health plan all bringing both leaders and team members experienced in their assigned tasks to the event. Although this required a significant investment of time and effort from each stakeholder during the event itself, it was likely both more efficient and more effective than trying to build healthcare delivery capabilities from scratch. High rates of high blood pressure observed in this hypertension screening event were consistent, if not exceeding, high rates of hypertension reported in the literature among Hispanic adults of Caribbean origin [3, 4]. 41.4% of participants reported having hypertension before this screening event, comparable to CDC’s report of a 43.7% rate of hypertension among Hispanic adults overall. [2] An additional 29.3% of participants screened positive for high blood pressure, having not been told they had high blood pressure before. Although we did not capture country (or territory) of origin among screening participants, the event was held in a community whose Hispanic population is predominantly Dominican origin [12]. Literature suggests Hispanic adults of Caribbean origin have approximately 30–50% higher incidence of hypertension compared to those of Mexican origin [3]. Although blood pressure measurements at this event represented screening results rather than confirmed diagnoses, high rates of positive screens do support the hypothesis that this community has both high rates of hypertension and a substantial number of undiagnosed cases, as would be expected from the published literature [5]. These findings lend support to the potential for future targeted community-based screening events targeting populations likely to have high rates of disease and/or low rates of diagnosis based upon existing literature.

A key finding of this event was that members of this community were broadly interested in receiving healthcare services. Whereas event planners sought to deliver a focused and feasible screening intervention by focusing on hypertension among adults, community members requested expansion of the event to include both children and additional healthcare services. This finding supports our hypothesis that a well-respected community organization could translate its engagement with its community into high participation in healthcare activities and that this phenomenon may be generalizable beyond hypertension screening among adults.

Limitations

This was an evaluation of a single blood pressure screening event hosted by a well-known Hispanic community organization, which regularly hosts a large community dinner in a community known to have high rates of hypertension. It is not clear whether similar events held elsewhere or for different health issues would have similar yields. This event relied upon more than 20 staff members from an FQHC attending an off-site event during business hours. Such an investment may not be replicable or sustainable in all settings. Although 26 participants scheduled follow-up, the study team did not collect data to link appointments made with outcomes, such as attendance versus no-show of scheduled visits or whether blood pressure was controlled effectively.

A key missed opportunity at this event was engagement of youth. Event staff prepared screening for adults only and were surprised that children and teenagers expressed interest in blood pressure screening and education. Because data was not collected on the ages of community dinner participants, it is not clear how many of the 405 attendees who did not participate in screening were children who were ineligible per event rules versus adults who chose not to be screened. Future study could offer hypertension and screening to all ages and/or gather data on individuals not participating in screening to better understand how to maximize reach for this type of event.

The mixed methods evaluation was also limited by various feasibility factors. Blood pressure outcomes were collected by clinical staff whose primary objective was to perform the screenings, resulting in some missing quantitative data. For qualitative interviews, purposive sampling was attempted by seeking to interview individuals from different stakeholder groups. However, research staff were also involved in running the event, and qualitative interviews were conducted concurrently with quantitative data collection. Qualitative sampling was ultimately conducted somewhat opportunistically, based upon when various individuals were available to conduct and/or give interviews. Phone interviews were conducted after the event to achieve more representative samples of healthcare workers and community organization employees. However, this introduced variability in in-depth interview format, which may have affected the results.

Future research

Future research should evaluate the long-term impact and sustainability of bringing healthcare staff to community organization sites to screen for hypertension and other cardiometabolic risk factors. In our own institution, we are seeking to identify ways to make community-clinical linkages more efficient and sustainable, acknowledging that this type of event does not align with typical healthcare practices or revenue streams. Similarly, we are exploring two-way community-clinical referral pathways to not only diagnose hypertension among community organization members, but to leverage community organizations to assist in management of known hypertension among health system patients. We suspect that, just as this screening event leveraged the respective skills of the community organization and healthcare teams to identify hypertension, each organization likely has unique capabilities to address the complex milieu of factors which impact development and management of hypertension. In an ongoing project, we will attempt to develop effective, adaptable, and scalable procedures for community organizations and healthcare teams to collaborate in diagnosis and management of hypertension.
