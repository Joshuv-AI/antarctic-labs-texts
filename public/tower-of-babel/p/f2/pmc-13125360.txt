Management of preterm pre‐labor rupture of membranes between 24 and 34 weeks: A before‐and‐after study of the implementation and modifications of an outpatient management protocol
Authors: Antonin Seyral, Mathilde Barrois, Aude Girault, Bruna Perrella, François Goffinet, Camille Le Ray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn preterm pre‐labor rupture of membranes (PPROM) before 34 weeks, expectant management is preferred in the absence of infection to reduce neonatal morbidity. Outpatient management (OM) has emerged as a potential alternative to prolonged hospitalization, but selection criteria remain ill‐defined. Our objective was to evaluate latency between PPROM and delivery, and obstetric and neonatal outcomes before and after the implementation of an OM protocol and its subsequent extensions.Material and MethodsWe included all women with PPROM before 34 weeks admitted between January 1, 2011, and December 31, 2021. Two periods were compared: Period A (January 2011–April 2013), when all patients were hospitalized until delivery, and Period B (May 2013–December 2021), when eligible patients were offered OM. Period B was subdivided into three phases (B1–B3) reflecting progressive expansion of eligibility criteria—from stable singleton pregnancies with cephalic presentation and normal amniotic fluid (B1), to inclusion of twins and shorter stabilization periods (B2), and finally cases with oligohydramnios or non‐cephalic presentations (B3).The primary outcome was latency period (days between PPROM and delivery). Secondary outcomes included obstetric and neonatal complications. Comparisons were made between Periods A and B and across OM subperiods.ResultsA total of 539 patients were included: 145 in Period A and 394 in Period B, of whom 126 (32%) received OM. Mean gestational age at PPROM was similar between periods (28.9 ± 3.1 vs. 28.9 ± 3.3 weeks; p = 0.94), as were latency (median 7 [3–17] days vs. 8 [2–21]; p = 0.66) and gestational age at delivery (30.8 ± 3.3 vs. 30.9 ± 3.8 weeks; p = 0.62). Early neonatal bacterial infection was significantly lower in Period B (24.2% vs. 34.5%; p = 0.01). OM use increased steadily from B1 to B3 without prolonging latency or worsening outcomes.ConclusionFollowing OM protocol implementation, one‐third of eligible women with PPROM before 34 weeks were managed at home. Outpatient care, even with broadened eligibility, appeared safe, did not increase maternal or neonatal morbidity, and may reduce early neonatal infections without extending latency.Keywords: home monitoring, latency period, neonatal outcomes, obstetric complications, outpatient management (OM), preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM)

Key message

Outpatient management of PPROM before 34 weeks is a safe alternative to hospitalization, showing no increase in maternal or neonatal morbidity and a lower rate of early neonatal infection, supporting broader eligibility in clinical practice.

INTRODUCTION

Preterm Pre‐labor rupture of the membranes (PPROM) before 34 weeks occurs in around 1% of pregnancies. 1 It is associated with increased risks of serious morbidity for the mother, fetus, and newborn, due to the risks of preterm birth and infectious complications such as intrauterine infection, maternal sepsis, and early neonatal bacterial infection. 2

To reduce neonatal morbidity and mortality associated with preterm birth due to PPROM before 34 weeks, expectant management is recommended when signs of infection are absent. This includes clinical monitoring of both the mother and fetus, with regular fetal heart rate assessments and laboratory tests for any signs of inflammation. In these cases, labor is generally induced at a gestational age of 37 weeks 3 unless infectious signs or complications appear before then.

Even today, monitoring took place during standard inpatient hospitalization in numerous maternity units. However, despite the absence of clear guidelines, increasingly more maternity wards are now offering outpatient management (OM) to selected patients. The underlying hypotheses are that OM makes it possible to limit maternal‐fetal infections by avoiding exposure to hospital germs, thus increasing the latency period and improving obstetric and neonatal outcomes. Several retrospective cohort studies have compared pregnancy outcomes between inpatients and outpatients. 4 , 5 , 6 A Cochrane meta‐analysis of randomized and quasi‐randomized studies, including a relatively small sample size (116 women in total), found no difference in latency duration or gestational age at birth among women with OM. 7

More recent observational studies have shown that in the absence of risk factors for complications, OM improves women's well‐being and reduces health‐care costs without modifying maternal or neonatal outcomes. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Nonetheless these studies concern small numbers of individuals, and the comparison of women managed at home and those hospitalized is biased as they do not share the same risk factors for maternal or neonatal complications.

Given the risks of infection, cord prolapse, and short latency period, and in the absence of data with an adequate level of evidence, ACOG's 2020 guidelines do not recommend outpatient management for patients with PPROM. 13 In the United Kingdom, OM is permitted on a case‐by‐case basis depending on maternal history, distance between home and hospital, available support at home, and the clinical context (amniotic fluid quantity, laboratory results, gestational age at PPROM), although specific selection criteria are not suggested. 14

The 2018 French guidelines allowed OM but did not specify the selection criteria for patients eligible for OM. 2 In our center, outpatient management of patients with PPROM before 34 weeks has been allowed since 2013. Gradually, in the face of reassuring results, the eligibility criteria for such outpatient follow‐up have evolved over the past decade, with a progressive expansion of the criteria for OM. The large number of patients managed in our tertiary center over the study period enabled us to evaluate this protocol and its successive modifications in a substantial cohort.

The objective of our study was to compare obstetric and neonatal outcomes of patients with PPROM between 24 and 34 weeks before and after the implementation of a protocol for outpatient management and its subsequent modifications in our center.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

We conducted a retrospective before‐and‐after study of the implementation and subsequent modifications of an OM protocol for PROM patients at the Port Royal Maternity Hospital, a level III maternity hospital. All women admitted for PROM between 24 and 34 weeks of gestation between January 1, 2011, and December 31, 2021, who gave birth in our maternity ward were included. The inclusion period was divided into two distinct periods (Figure 1).

Changes in the outpatient management protocol in the Port Royal ward maternity ward during the study period.

During period A (from January 1, 2011, to April 2013), before the implementation of the protocol, all patients were admitted to the hospital as inpatients until delivery. In period B (from April 2013 through December 31, 2021), after the protocol was implemented, some patients — those who met specific discharge criteria — received outpatient management by home hospitalization.

As part of the standardized management protocol, all patients hospitalized for PPROM received a 7‐day course of amoxicillin. Antibiotic therapy was discontinued earlier if vaginal swab cultures identified an organism resistant to amoxicillin.

Period B was then divided into subperiods corresponding to changes in the eligibility criteria for OM

In period B1 (from April 2013 through December 31, 2015), patients could be discharged to OM if they met the following criteria: a singleton pregnancy, clinically stable for 5 days, with no clinical or laboratory signs of infection, fetus in cephalic presentation with a quantity of amniotic fluid excluding severe oligohydramnios (amniotic fluid index ≥2 cm) and a not‐at‐all or only slightly shortened cervix (≥20 mm on ultrasound).

In period B1 (from April 2013 through December 31, 2015), patients could be discharged to OM if they met the following criteria: a singleton pregnancy, clinically stable for 5 days, with no clinical or laboratory signs of infection, fetus in cephalic presentation with a quantity of amniotic fluid excluding severe oligohydramnios (amniotic fluid index ≥2 cm) and a not‐at‐all or only slightly shortened cervix (≥20 mm on ultrasound).

In period B2 (from January 1, 2015, through December 31, 2018), the OM protocol was enlarged: patients could return home after at least 3 days of clinical stability, without a threshold cervical measurement, and even if they were carrying twins.

Outpatient monitoring remained the same throughout period B: clinical monitoring by a midwife three times a week, including FHR recordings and laboratory monitoring (complete blood count, C‐reactive protein, twice a week, along with weekly bacteriological samples [vaginal swab with culture and urine culture]). In‐hospital monitoring took place in the day hospital every 2 weeks and included an ultrasound examination in addition to the standard work‐up. For inpatient management, FHR monitoring and laboratory testing were identical to outpatient management, with daily clinical examinations including temperature and blood pressure assessment.

Positive results of vaginal swab cultures obtained during surveillance were not treated during pregnancy but were used to guide intrapartum antibiotic therapy in cases of resistant organisms.

We collected maternal and obstetric characteristics at PPROM from the obstetric file. Characteristics of mothers and neonates during monitoring and at delivery were collected from both the obstetric and neonatology files.

Our primary outcome was the latency period, defined by the number of days between the date of the PPROM and the date of delivery. The timing of delivery was defined as spontaneous labor before 37 weeks, induction of labor before 37 weeks in cases of suspected intrauterine infection, or induction at 37 weeks in the absence of complications.

Our secondary outcomes included the number of patients with OM, gestational age at birth, mode of delivery, and obstetric morbidity criteria, such as births before 34 weeks, intrauterine infections (defined as maternal fever, uterine tenderness, maternal or fetal tachycardia, and elevated inflammatory markers), placental abruption, cord prolapse, and in utero fetal deaths.

According to the standard management protocol, readmission was indicated in the presence of the following clinical findings: meconium‐stained amniotic fluid, maternal fever, isolated elevation of C‐reactive protein, uterine contractions without labor, vaginal bleeding, or abnormal FHR.

We also studied neonatal outcomes excluding in utero fetal death, such as birth weight (in grams), 5 minutes Apgar score ≤7 and umbilical arterial pH <7.20. Finally neonatal morbidity criteria were studied; these included the NICU admission rate, neonatal mortality, and intraventricular hemorrhage (defined as grade 3 or 4 bleeding into the brain's ventricular system diagnosed by cranial ultrasound according to the Papile classification), necrotizing enterocolitis (defined as intestinal inflammation and necrosis diagnosed clinically and radiographically using modified Bell's criteria at stage II or higher), and early neonatal bacterial infection (defined as a proven infection occurring within the first 72 hours of life requiring antibiotic treatment).

Because the expansion of the criteria allowed us to assess the obstetric and neonatal outcome of patients who had OM over the three successive periods (B1, B2, and B3), we then conducted a subgroup analysis. In this analysis, we studied the length of the initial stay before OM, gestational age at discharge to OM, readmission rate, total duration of hospitalization (total number of hospitalization days until delivery), latency period, gestational age at delivery (in weeks), and obstetrical complications such as intrauterine infection, cord prolapse, placenta abruption, and in utero fetal death.

Overall, missing data were limited. Missingness was approximately 2% for primary outcomes (including latency duration, maternal infection, and key neonatal morbidity indicators) and around 10% for less critical secondary variables, mainly ultrasound parameters (amniotic fluid index and cervical length) and some follow‐up items that were not uniformly documented in the early years of the cohort. Because the rate of missingness for primary outcomes was very low and missingness predominantly affected secondary variables, no imputation was performed. All analyses were conducted on available cases (complete‐case analysis).

To compare qualitative variables, we used Pearson's Chi2 test and Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Quantitative results are expressed as means with their standard deviation or medians and interquartile ranges, and group comparisons were performed using Student's t‐test or non‐parametric tests adequately. For the three outpatient care periods, variables were compared across the three study periods using a p for trend, which assesses the presence of a linear trend across ordered groups. A p‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The statistical analyses were performed using Stata 17 software®.

RESULTS

Throughout the 11‐year study period (2011–2021), 539 women after PPROM occurring between 24 and 34 weeks were admitted in our maternity unit, 145 women during period A and 394 women during period B. We analyzed outcomes for 168 infants in period A (25 multiple pregnancies and excluding 2 in utero fetal deaths) and 471 infants in period B (81 multiple pregnancies and excluding 4 in utero fetal deaths).

Women's general characteristics (Table 1) were comparable between the periods, except for maternal age, which was lower in period A (27.3 years ±5.7 in period A vs. 31.3 years ±5.8 in period B, p = 0.04). Similarly, the obstetric characteristics at admission (Table 1) did not differ significantly between the two periods for gestational age at PPROM, oligohydramnios at ultrasound, or fetal presentation.

DISCUSSION

Since the implementation of our OM protocol, one‐third of patients had home monitoring, with usage increasing over time.

The latency period did not significantly differ between the two periods, which means that OM does not appear to impair obstetric prognosis. OM appears to be a safe strategy for mothers and neonates, as the latency period remains unaffected following its implementation, provided outpatient monitoring conditions are met.

Our results confirm that OM of patients with PPROM before 34 weeks may be a viable and safe option with selection criteria that are adequate but not overly restrictive and does not impair maternal‐fetal prognosis in the short term.

Our study shows a significant reduction in the duration of the initial hospitalization of women with OM, without any notable increase in obstetric complications. Furthermore, our study showed that OM did not significantly affect immediate neonatal outcomes, such as birth weight or Apgar score.

The originality of our study lies in its comparison of the three versions of the OM protocol with their progressive easing of the eligibility criteria for OM. In our subgroup analysis, we observed a trend toward a reduced initial length of stay. However, the rate of women requiring at least one readmission varied significantly across these periods. As the discharge criteria became less stringent, the reasons for readmission also eased over time, yet without negatively impacting the outcomes. Outcomes such as latency or gestational age at birth did not differ between these periods B1 to B3.

These results are consistent with previous studies, which have reported no significant difference in the duration of the latency period between conventional inpatient and outpatient management, as reported by Guckert and in the most recent meta‐analysis. 15 , 16 Gestational age at delivery is earlier in our study than in Bouchghoul et al. 8 population, maybe linked with our specific population from a tertiary regional referent center.

It supports the notion that OM is a reliable alternative that does not increase risk for women and their neonates if rigorous eligibility criteria are applied. The reduction in the length of inpatient stay is an important benefit for women's quality of life, reducing the stress associated with prolonged hospitalization, 17 , 18 while reducing costs to the health care system. 19

The reduction in early neonatal bacterial infections observed in period B compared with period A may be associated with change of antibiotic prophylaxis in the most recent period. Indeed, throughout the study period, an antibiotic stewardship policy was implemented to limit the emergence of bacterial resistance. Home follow‐up likely limits exposure to nosocomial pathogens, which are more frequently encountered during prolonged inpatient stays. In addition, outpatient care may reduce the number of unnecessary vaginal examinations or other invasive assessments, thereby lowering the risk of ascending infection. These elements suggest that, beyond patient comfort and resource optimization, outpatient management may offer a protective effect on infectious morbidity in selected PPROM cases.

However, this decrease may also be explained by modifications of the diagnostic criteria for early neonatal bacterial infections since new guidelines 2017. 20 It is therefore challenging to attribute this outcome solely to the use of OM, as other factors—such as evolving medical practices during the study period‐ likely contributed to the results. In our clinical setting, the analysis of cord blood procalcitonin has played a key role in guiding antibiotic use for the prevention of early‐onset neonatal bacterial infection. 11

We observed, even if not statistically significant, a reduction in intraventricular hemorrhages during period B. This finding is of clinical interest and may be attributed to improved patient monitoring and perinatal management, particularly in the prevention of very preterm birth. It may also be related to a better implementation of magnesium sulfate administration during labor for fetal neuroprotection, although this parameter was not documented in our study. Notably, French national guidelines regarding preterm birth, published in 2011, recommend the use of magnesium sulfate before 32 weeks of gestation. 21 The implementation of these recommendations has likely been progressive over time.

The retrospective nature of our study prevents us from exploring factors such as women's anxiety, experiences, and satisfaction across the different periods. The benefits of OM for women's pregnancy experience have been demonstrated in other conditions, such as preeclampsia. 22 It would be interesting to assess the contribution of OM to women's experience, anxiety, and satisfaction with a standardized questionnaire or qualitative interviews of women undergoing PPROM, regardless of the organization of their monitoring after PPROM. Although the incidence of serious obstetric and neonatal outcomes such as neonatal mortality, cord prolapse, or placental abruption is low, our sample size does not allow definitive conclusions.

From a medico‐economic perspective, outpatient management of PPROM also offers significant advantages. By reducing the length of hospital stay without compromising maternal or neonatal outcomes, OM contributes to lowering overall healthcare costs, including those associated with prolonged inpatient care, staff mobilization, and hospital resources. This approach may help optimize resource allocation within maternity units, particularly in contexts of increasing demand and constrained capacity. Moreover, it supports a more sustainable healthcare model, aligning clinical safety with economic efficiency.

Our study has several strengths besides its originality. It covered more than 10 years and thus included a substantial sample for this situation. As a single‐center study, it ensured uniform patient management. Moreover, the comparison by period with a before‐after study design, as opposed to the comparison between the outpatients and inpatients, allowed us to limit selection bias, contrary to previously published studies. 9 , 19 Our analysis was conducted on all women with PPROM in each study period, as our objective was to assess the global impact of implementing outpatient management at the institutional level. Period B also included both eligible and non‐eligible patients, reflecting real‐life practice.

One strength of our study lies in its contribution to the investigation of rare but serious obstetric and neonatal complications, such as neonatal mortality, cord prolapse, or placenta abruption even if the study was not powered to evaluate very rare adverse outcomes. Therefore, the absence of differences observed for these outcomes should be interpreted with caution, as the study was not designed to detect variations in events of such low incidence.

Our study has also limitations. The retrospective design explains some missing data, mainly affecting secondary variables (≈10%), while primary outcomes were almost complete (≈2%). Analyses were performed on available cases, and the low rate of missing data for main outcomes supports the robustness of our findings. Over the long study period, treatment protocols in obstetrics and neonatology have evolved, which may partly explain some of the observed results. Similarly, the characteristics of parturient have changed over this period, thus limiting the comparability of the groups. In particular, there was a significant difference in maternal age between the different periods, as the age of first pregnancy in France (and elsewhere) has been increasing for several years. 23 The higher BMI observed in period B likely reflects a temporal demographic trend rather than a change in recruitment or management practices.

Another limitation of our study is the absence of a washout period following the introduction of outpatient management. The transition between the pre‐implementation and post‐implementation periods was therefore immediate, without a phase allowing clinicians to fully adapt to the new protocol. This may have introduced a brief learning‐curve effect and a degree of heterogeneity in early management during Period B. However, given the gradual expansion of eligibility criteria and the stability of other clinical practices over time, we believe this potential impact is limited, though it should be acknowledged when interpreting the results. Finally, the retrospective nature of the study restricted assessment of psychologic factors, which would be very interesting to assess in future prospective studies about home management of PPROM.

CONCLUSION

Since the implementation of our OM protocol, one‐third of patients had home monitoring, with usage increasing over time. Our study confirms the potential benefits of this approach by showing that a well‐structured home monitoring protocol, with selection criteria that are adequate but not overly restrictive, does not impair maternal‐fetal prognosis in the short term.

Nonetheless OM for PPROM before 34 weeks, requires clear and complete patient information about its benefits and risks. It is essential that women thoroughly understand the process and adhere to guidelines regarding signs that necessitate medical reassessment.
