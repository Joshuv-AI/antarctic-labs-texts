Laparoscopic mesh sacr ocolpopexy versus lateral vaginal vault suspension in prevention of post hysterectomy vault prolapse: a randomized trial
Authors: Mostafa Hussein Haider, Ahmed Salah Abo Elagha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare laparoscopic mesh sacrocolpopexy (LSC) and laparoscopic lateral suspension (LLS) for preventing post hysterectomy vault prolapse.MethodsThis randomized trial included 40 women who were scheduled for a hysterectomy. The patients were randomly assigned to two groups in a 1:1 ratio: group I underwent LSC immediately after hysterectomy and group II (n=20) underwent LLS immediately after hysterectomy.ResultsPostoperative pelvic organ prolapse quantification measurements (anterior vaginal points anterior vaginal wall points A and posterior vaginal wall points B, vaginal apex point C, and posterior vaginal points anterior prolapse and posterior prolapse) were significantly better in the LSC group, with a lower rate of postoperative vault prolapse beyond the introitus (P=0.047). Both groups showed similar estimated blood loss, urinary tract infection, wound infection, and urinary retention rates. However, LSC was associated with significantly higher postoperative pain scores at 24 hours and 48 hours and a longer hospital stay (P<0.05).ConclusionAlthough LSC demonstrated more favorable anatomical outcomes and a lower incidence of postoperative vault prolapse beyond the introitus than LLS, these findings should be interpreted with caution because of the limited sample size. Both procedures showed comparable safety profiles, although the LSC was associated with greater postoperative pain and longer hospitalization times. Given the study’s exploratory design, small cohort, and short follow-up period, further large-scale multicenter studies are needed to confirm these preliminary observations and to better inform clinical practice regarding the optimal surgical approach for preventing post hysterectomy vault prolapse.Keywords: Laparoscopy, Hysterectomy, Prolapse, Surgery

Introduction

Pelvic organ prolapse (POP) is an increasingly common condition in the aging population, posing a significant challenge to women’s health and overall quality of life [1]. The supportive structures of the pelvic floor, including muscles, ligaments, and connective tissues, may weaken with age, leading to the descent of pelvic organs such as the bladder, uterus, or rectum. When these organs shift downward and protrude into or beyond the vaginal walls, the condition is classified as a vaginal prolapse [2]. POP is one of the leading indications for hysterectomy in women over the age of 50 [3,4].

Various surgical techniques have been developed to manage vaginal vault prolapse, which are classified into vaginal and abdominal approaches. The abdominal approach can be performed using either open surgery or minimally invasive laparoscopic techniques, with the choice of procedure often influenced by patient-specific factors, surgeon expertise, and institutional preferences [5,6]. According to a Cochrane review, laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy (LSC) results in a lower recurrence rate of vaginal vault prolapse than vaginal sacrospinous fixation, making it the preferred option for long-term anatomical correction [7].

The first documented LSC was reported in 1994, highlighting the potential benefits of laparoscopy, including reduced morbidity, shorter hospital stays, and faster recovery times. Additionally, laparoscopic access may facilitate the placement of a mesh along the posterior vaginal wall more effectively than open surgery [8].

However, LSC has certain disadvantages, including potential complications such as mesh erosion, pain, infection, and sexual dysfunction. This requires advanced laparoscopic skills, resulting in a steep learning curve for the surgeons. LSC is often associated with longer operative times, particularly during the initial phases of surgical training. The need for specialized instruments, prolonged anesthesia, and costly mesh materials contributes to a higher overall cost than vaginal approaches. These limitations can reduce the accessibility of LSC in low-resource settings, despite favorable anatomical outcomes [9].

Sacrocolpopexy is the gold standard treatment for apical prolapse. However, LSC is technically demanding and associated with significant risk. Promontory dissection required for LSC carries potential complications, including severe vascular injury, spondylodiscitis, hypogastric nerve damage (which can result in bladder or bowel dysfunction), and sacral nerve root injuries. This aspect of the procedure is particularly challenging for patients with obesity [10].

Therefore, alternative pelvic floor repair techniques are being explored to achieve comparable anatomical and functional outcomes while reducing the perioperative risks. One such approach is laparoscopic lateral suspension (LLS) with a mesh, which eliminates the need for promontory dissection, thereby minimizing the associated complications. Recent studies have suggested that LLS results in similar surgical outcomes. Furthermore, LLS is particularly suitable for hysteropexy, which is the preferred option for many women undergoing POP surgery [11].

Based on these considerations, we hypothesized that LSC is superior to LLS in terms of effectiveness and has a lower complication rate. This study aimed to compare the outcomes of laparoscopic mesh LSC and LLS in preventing post hysterectomy vault prolapse.

Materials and methods

This randomized comparative study included 40 women who were scheduled to undergo hysterectomy. The study was conducted between August 2023 and January 2024, following approval from the ethics committee (approval code: 36264PR740/6/24). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: prior surgical correction for vault prolapse, pelvic malignancy, connective tissue or neurological disorders, chronic constipation, significant urinary tract dysfunction, active pelvic infections, immunosuppressive therapy, and uncontrolled diabetes mellitus.

Before surgery, all patients underwent a thorough review of their medical history, clinical examination, and laboratory tests. Furthermore, they were familiarized with the visual analog scale (VAS) for pain assessment to ensure accurate reporting of pain levels.

Randomization

To maintain the integrity of the study, a random allocation process was used with computer-generated numbers (https://www.randomizer.org/). Each participant’s code was placed in an opaque, sealed envelope. The patients were randomly assigned to two equal groups (1:1 ratio) to undergo LSC in group I or LLS in group II immediately after the hysterectomy. Considering the inherent differences in the surgical techniques, this study was conducted as an open-label trial.

Before surgical intervention, POP severity in each patient was systematically assessed using the Baden-Walker grading system. Additionally, sonographic imaging was used to determine the residual urine volume to ensure an objective evaluation of urinary retention. The patients were subjected to a structured clinical survey that explored various prolapse-related symptoms, including a persistent sensation of heaviness in the lower abdominal region and subjective perception of a vaginal lump.

Further comprehensive assessments included an evaluation of urinary tract symptoms, the presence of constipation, and a detailed medical history review focusing on previous prolapse surgeries and the specific type of hysterectomy performed. Other relevant parameters, such as body mass index and parity, were meticulously documented to provide a holistic understanding of the factors that contribute to the condition.

Throughout the perioperative period, multiple critical parameters were collected, including the total duration of the surgical procedure, planned concomitant surgeries, intraoperative and postoperative complications, estimated blood loss, materials used, and number of sutures placed. This comprehensive documentation facilitated a thorough evaluation of surgical outcomes and patient safety.

To optimize preoperative conditions, all patients underwent bowel preparation on the day before surgery. Additionally, prophylactic antibiotics, such as metronidazole or cefazolin, were administered to minimize the risk of postoperative infection. To mitigate thromboembolic complications, subcutaneous dalteparin was administered both preoperatively and postoperatively in adherence to standard anticoagulation protocols.

POP was classified according to the POP quantification (POP-Q) system, which categorizes the condition into four progressive stages based on anatomical landmarks and the degree of prolapse.

Two experienced urogynecologists performed the POP-Q assessments using a standardized protocol and interobserver reliability was maintained through consensus in cases of discrepancies. In patients with stress urinary incontinence, a tension-free vaginal tape procedure was performed when clinically indicated. However, Burch colposuspension was deliberately excluded from the treatment protocols. Additional prolapse-related surgeries were considered on a case-by-case basis, with final surgical decisions made by the operating surgeon after completing sacrocolpopexy.

LSC technique

LSC was performed under general anesthesia using the four-trocar technique to optimize access and visualization of the surgical field. To facilitate anatomical alignment and proper exposure of the vaginal vault, a vaginal probe was inserted to aid elevation and stabilization of the surgical site. The procedure commenced with a precise laparoscopic incision of the peritoneum, extending from the sacral promontory to the vaginal vault. This meticulous dissection allowed adequate exposure of both the rectovaginal and vesicovaginal fascia, ensuring optimal mesh placement. A type 1 polypropylene mesh was prepared by cutting it into two distinct sections specifically tailored to reinforce the anterior and posterior vaginal walls. These mesh segments were securely affixed to their respective vaginal walls using nonabsorbable sutures to ensure durable structural support. Once positioned, the mesh was anchored to the sacral promontory using surgical staples, further enhancing its stability and long-term efficacy. To prevent direct contact between the mesh and surrounding pelvic structures, the peritoneum was carefully sutured over the mesh (peritonealization). This step aims to minimize the risk of adhesions and postoperative complications, thereby improving patient outcomes (Fig. 1A).

LLS technique

In contrast, LLS involves precise dissection of both the vesicovaginal and rectovaginal spaces, with adhesiolysis performed when necessary to optimize the surgical access and visualization. To provide structural reinforcement, a polypropylene mesh was carefully positioned over the anterior and posterior vaginal walls and secured with interrupted suturing. In cases where uterine preservation was planned, absorbable sutures were used to ensure appropriate fixation while allowing physiological support.

Bilateral skin incisions were made to facilitate mesh placement, allowing the introduction of laparoscopic forceps. A designated pathway was created following the natural trajectory of the round ligament to ensure proper positioning and tension-free suspension of the vaginal vault. This technique aims to minimize the risk of postoperative complications associated with excessive tension or mesh misalignment. Following successful mesh placement, the peritoneum was meticulously sutured over the mesh to prevent direct contact with adjacent pelvic structures, thereby reducing the likelihood of adhesions and associated complications. The side arms of the mesh were then trimmed to the skin level to ensure proper integration, minimize patient discomfort, and enhance long-term outcomes (Fig. 1B).

Perioperative data collection included key surgical parameters, such as the total duration of the procedure, any planned concomitant surgeries, intraoperative and postoperative complications, estimated blood loss, materials used, and number of sutures placed.

Surgical complications were systematically evaluated using the Clavien-Dindo classification [12], which provides a standardized method for assessing postoperative morbidity. Pain levels were carefully monitored using a VAS at 24-hour and 48-hour post-surgery to ensure adequate pain management and patient comfort.

Following the procedure, a urethral catheter was routinely inserted and removed on the first postoperative day unless specific indications required prolonged catheterization. In cases where urinary retention occurred after catheter removal, reinsertion was necessary for an additional 24 hours before reassessment. Catheter reinsertion was performed as a temporary measure to avoid further complications, such as bladder overdistension, detrusor muscle damage, urinary tract infection, and patient discomfort, which are well-documented risks associated with unrelieved acute urinary retention.

Patients were clinically assessed for potential contributing factors, such as the residual effects of anesthesia, pain, fluid status, and potential nerve irritation, particularly after LLS, where dissection near the hypogastric plexus may transiently affect bladder function.

Postoperative follow-up evaluations were conducted at 6 weeks and 6 months to monitor surgical outcomes and assess the recurrence or persistence of prolapse. These assessments included a detailed site-specific evaluation of urethrocele, cystocele, vault prolapse, enterocele, rectocele, and perineal prolapse to ensure comprehensive postoperative surveillance and long-term treatment efficacy.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this study was determined using G*Power 3.1.9.2 (Universitat Kiel, Kiel, Germany). The calculation was based on the reported recurrence rates of POP at the final follow-up, which were documented as 71.4% in the LLS group and 15.6% in the LSC group, according to a previous study [13]. To ensure that the study maintained adequate statistical power, a significance level (α error) of 0.05 and a statistical power of 95% were applied, with an equal allocation ratio (1:1) between groups. Five additional cases were incorporated into the final sample to mitigate potential participant dropouts and maintain the integrity of the study. Consequently, 20 patients were allocated to each group, ensuring robust comparative analysis and reliable outcome assessment.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software version 26 (IBM Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). For quantitative parametric data results are expressed as means with standard deviations, and between-group comparisons were performed using unpaired t-tests. Qualitative variables are reported as frequencies and percentages, with comparisons made using either the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on expected cell counts.

For non-parametric quantitative data, results are reported as medians with interquartile ranges, and comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U-test. Statistical significance was considered at P<0.05, indicating significant differences between groups.

Discussion

The existing literature clearly demonstrates that sacrocolpopexy (SC) is a highly effective surgical intervention that yields exceptionally long-term outcomes [14]. However, variations in SC techniques exist, including differences in the extent of pelvic dissection, use of either pre-shaped or surgeon-shaped meshes, mesh placement on the anterior, posterior, or both vaginal walls, and choice of suture material for fixation [15]. Another important modification involves suspending the vaginal vault in the posterior direction and securing it to the anterior longitudinal ligament at the sacral level, which enhances surgical success. Despite its advantages, SC has certain limitations, primarily related to potential intraoperative complications. These include the risk of significant bleeding due to sacral promontory dissection and the possibility of ureteral injury during the parietal peritoneal incision phase. In some cases, anatomical or patient-specific factors may render SC technically challenging or even infeasible. To address these challenges, Ganatra et al. [16] introduced the laparoscopic mesh sacrocolpopexy (LCS) as an alternative technique. The LCS enables symmetrical, tension-free lateral suspension of the vaginal vault, effectively mimicking the natural support of the broad ligament. This approach maintains the vaginal axis closer to the physiological norm, contributing to favorable outcomes [16]. Furthermore, LCS eliminates the need for retroperitoneal dissection, simplifies the procedure, and reduces the risk of certain complications [17].

The key findings of this study indicate that LSC resulted in significantly better postoperative POP-Q measurements (Aa, Ba, C, AP, and BP) and a lower rate of postoperative vault prolapse beyond the introitus compared to LLS. While the two groups had similar estimated blood loss, urinary tract infections, wound infections, and urinary retention, LSC was associated with significantly higher postoperative pain scores at 24 hours and 48 hours and longer hospital stay. Preoperative and postoperative GH, Pb, TVL, urethrocele, cystocele, enterocele, and rectocele levels were not significantly different between the two groups.

In agreement with our observations, Özel et al. [18] reported that LSC provided superior anatomical outcomes compared to LLS, particularly in terms of POP-Q measurements, with point C being significantly more proximal in the LSC group (−6.6±1.1 cm vs. −5.2±1.5 cm, P=0.037). Additionally, they reported comparable results between the groups for other POP-Q parameters, including Aa, Ba, AP, BP, GH, Pb, and TVL. However, they reported no need for repeat urogynaecological surgeries in either group. This difference may be related to variations in sample size and follow-up duration.

In addition, Malanowska-Jarema et al. [19] noticed that, compared with LSC, LLS has a more manageable learning curve, which means that it is technically less challenging. The annoyance caused by POP symptoms was significantly reduced by both treatments, with comparable objective success rates at 12 months (90.7% for LSC and 89.1% for LLS).

Similarly, Mancini et al. [17] stated that, as shown by the Kaplan-Meier curves (P<0.001), SC resulted in superior anatomical results in the apical compartment (P=0.003) and had a longer-lasting consequence over time compared to LCS. Additionally, they reported that SC required a longer operative time (155 minutes vs. 104 minutes for LCS) and was associated with more postoperative constipation symptoms (P=0.001). In contrast, they did not find any statistically significant changes in either de novo or persistent overactive bladder/stress urinary incontinence (P=0.5 and P=0.9, respectively) and observed a higher anterior defect recurrence in the LCS group (P<0.0001). The differences in the findings may stem from their prospective study, which was conducted in two hospitals using structured assessment and standardized criteria.

Similarly, Isenlik et al. [20] reported that both LLS and LSC resulted in significant improvements in POP-Q measurements and patient-reported outcomes, with high objective and subjective cure rates in both groups. They observed no statistically significant differences between LLS and LSC in terms of the objective cure rate, subjective cure rate, or patient-reported outcomes, reinforcing the effectiveness of these procedures. However, the authors reported a 100% objective cure rate for apical prolapse in the LSC group, while the LLS group had a slightly lower cure rate (92.5%). Variations in study design, follow-up duration, and surgical modifications may account for this disparity.

LSC and LLS differ considerably in terms of their cost, complexity, and technical demand. LSC is more complex and requires advanced laparoscopic skills and a steeper learning curve due to dissection near the sacral promontory and precise mesh fixation. In contrast, LLS are technically less demanding and are often considered easier to learn. LSC is typically more expensive, with an estimated average cost of approximately 30,000 Egyptian Pounds, whereas LLS procedures range between 20,000 and 25,000 Egyptian pounds. These differences may influence procedure selection based on the surgeon’s expertise, institutional resources, and patient preferences.

This study had certain limitations. The small sample size, potential variability in surgical complexity, patient characteristics (e.g., body mass index and parity), and intraoperative factors may have influenced the outcomes and limited the generalizability of our findings. Although randomization was performed, the limited sample size may have restricted the ability to fully balance the groups, and unmeasured confounding factors, such as variability in surgical technique or tissue quality, may have influenced the results. Additionally, the open-label study design may have introduced bias into subjective outcome measures, such as pain assessment. The lack of blinding introduces potential measurement bias, particularly in subjective outcomes, such as pain assessment and evaluation of anatomical support. A follow-up period of 6 months was considered insufficient for evaluating long-term outcomes, such as prolapse recurrence and mesh-related complications, which are known to develop over extended periods. Furthermore, as this was a single-center study, factors such as patient selection, surgical expertise, and institutional perioperative protocols may limit the external validity of the findings, and caution should be exercised when generalizing these results to other clinical settings.

Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution and further multicenter trials with longer follow-up durations are recommended to confirm the durability and safety of both surgical techniques. Future studies should incorporate blinded outcome assessments to improve the objectivity and reliability of their findings.
