Closing the Gate Before the Horse is out of the Barn: A Model to Effectively Predict the First Fall in Patients with Parkinson's Disease
Authors: Saar Anis, Eric Zimmerman, Maxime Munyeshyaka, Hubert H Fernandez, Anson B Rosenfeldt, Jay L Alberts
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMost fall‐prediction models in Parkinson's disease (PD) rely on prior falls, limiting prevention. The Waiting Room of the Future (WROTF) platform collects standardized motor and cognitive measures during routine visits, integrated into electronic health records (EHR).ObjectiveTo develop a first‐fall prediction model combining WROTF‐EHR data and compare its performance with established cognitive‐ and motor‐based models.MethodsPreviously published motor (Paul) and cognitive (CYCLE‐II) models were refit using logistic regression. A new regularized (LASSO) model was developed. All models were evaluated using all participants and only those without prior falls.ResultsAmong 997 PD patients (mean age 69.6 ± 8.8; 62.8% male; disease duration 3.6 ± 4.3 years), AUCs were 0.77 (Paul), 0.70 (CYCLE‐II), and 0.81 (LASSO). In fall‐naïve patients, AUCs were 0.62, 0.68, and 0.70. Key predictors included processing speed, walking speed, disease duration, and age.ConclusionsThe model enables early fall‐risk detection using routinely collected data, supporting proactive and personalized preventive care.Keywords: accidental falls, Parkinson's disease, real‐world data, risk prediction

Falls are among the most feared complications of Parkinson's disease (PD), marking a turning point in independence. 1 , 2 A first fall often represents a clinically meaningful disease milestone, signaling progression to a more advanced disease stage and increased vulnerability to subsequent complications. 3 Clinicians have long sought efficient ways to identify patients at risk before the first fall.

Existing models largely predict recurrent rather than first‐time falls and are predominantly motor‐centric, 4 , 5 often requiring instrumented or recall‐based assessments 6 , 7 limiting routine use. To address this gap, we previously developed a cognitive‐based fall prediction model 8 using control‐arm data from the Cyclical Lower Extremity Exercise for PD II (CYCLE‐II) trial. Processing speed, visual memory, and disease duration were key predictors, with performance comparable to motor‐based models. 9

At the Cleveland Clinic, the “Waiting Room of the Future” (WROTF) platform collects standardized motor and cognitive outcomes before each appointment using iPad‐based modules, with data flowing directly into the electronic health record (EHR). 10 This project aimed to: (1) externally validate our CYCLE‐II Cognitive Model using WROTF data; (2) develop a new fall prediction model using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression (the WROTF LASSO Model); (3) compare the performance of the WROTF LASSO Model to the pre‐existing CYCLE‐II Cognitive Model and Paul Motor Model, and (4) assess each model in predicting first‐time falls.

Methods

Participants

Individuals with PD who completed a WROTF assessment in outpatient settings between January 2021 and August 2024 were identified using ICD‐10 codes. Only the first assessment per patient was analyzed. Participants were included if their fall status during the subsequent 12 months was documented in the EHR. Informed consent was waived due to the study's retrospective design, approved by the Cleveland Clinic IRB (IRB #241007).

Data Collection and Measures

As detailed previously, 10 the WROTF modules were completed in the clinic waiting area before routine visits. Assessments were self‐administered on an iPad under supervision and included standardized tests of motor and cognitive function: processing speed (PST, total correct, raw), 11 visual memory (VMT, total correct symbols plus location, raw), 12 manual dexterity (MDT, dominant and non‐dominant hand times), 13 and 25‐foot comfortable walking speed (WST), with scoring and normative interpretation summarized in the Supplementary Methods. Fall status was derived from structured pre‐visit questionnaires in the EHR and classified as faller, non‐faller, or unknown for the year before and after the WROTF assessment (see Supplementary Methods). For prior non‐fallers who fell within the following year, the most severe first‐fall consequence was identified through chart review.

Predictor Variables

Predictor variables included cognitive (PST, VMT), motor (WST), and clinical measures (Movement Disorder Society–Unified PD Rating Scale [MDS‐UPDRS] part III total score, assessed within 30 days of the WROTF visit, freezing of gait item, disease duration, body‐mass index [BMI], levodopa equivalent daily dose [LEDD], recorded within 6 months of the WROTF visit, and fall history), along with demographic factors (age, sex). MDS‐UPDRS III scores, collected during routine care, may reflect either “on” or “off” medication states.

Missing Data

Missing data (<9%) were imputed using predictive mean matching for continuous variables and logistic regression for binary variables.

Statistical Analysis

The CYCLE‐II Cognitive Model was externally validated on the WROTF dataset. Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).

The new predictive model was developed using LASSO logistic regression and internally validated using 10‐fold cross‐validation, wherein the entire modeling process was trained on nine folds, then tested on the remaining fold, rotating across partitions. The optimal penalty parameter (lambda) was selected using 20‐fold cross‐validation, nested within the outer training folds during model evaluation, and applying the “one‐standard‐error” rule to select a parsimonious model. Model discrimination was summarized as the average of the AUCs from the out‐of‐sample test folds. No independent external validation dataset was available for the WROTF‐LASSO Model; therefore, its performance reflects internal validation only.

The WROTF LASSO Model's performance was compared to the CYCLE‐II Cognitive and Paul Motor Models, retaining their original set of predictors but allowing their coefficients to be refit, so the WROTF LASSO Model is not at an advantage, with performance likewise summarized with the AUC from 10‐fold cross‐validation. Each model was separately trained on both the entire cohort's data and only the subset of participants that did not fall in the prior year.

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.0) in RStudio (version 2025.05.0). This study followed the TRIPOD reporting guidelines for model development and validation (see Supplementary Table S1).

Clinical Characteristics by Prior and Future Fall Status

Demographics and clinical characteristics stratified by prior and future fall status are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. Among 997 participants, 570 reported no falls in the prior year, 367 had falls, and 60 had unknown status.

Participants with prior falls had longer disease duration and higher MDS‐UPDRS III scores compared to those without. Similar patterns were observed among future fallers, who also required higher LEDD and demonstrated slower walking speeds compared with future non‐fallers. Prior fallers performed worse on cognitive tests (PST and VMT) than non‐fallers, with these differences more pronounced among individuals who subsequently fell within the following year.

CYCLE‐II Cognitive Model Performance

Using the published coefficients for external validation, the CYCLE‐II Cognitive Model achieved an AUC (95% CI) of 0.69 (0.65, 0.72) among all participants in the WROTF data. After refitting the model using the same predictor set in the full cohort, the CYCLE‐II Cognitive Model coefficients (back‐transformed to odds ratios) showed that poorer PST performance (total correct; OR = 0.96), lower VMT scores (correct symbols plus location; OR = 0.99), and longer disease duration (years of PD; OR = 1.11) were associated with greater odds of falling. The model demonstrated good discrimination, with a cross‐validated AUC of 0.70 (0.66, 0.73). Receiver operating characteristic curves for all models are presented in Figure 1.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for the CYCLE‐II Cognitive Models, Paul Motor Models, and WROTF LASSO Models, developed and tested on both the full cohort and only prior non‐fallers using 10‐fold cross‐validation (CV). The ROC curve for each test CV‐fold is depicted in gray, the vertically averaged aggregate is depicted in red. The performance of the CYCLE‐II Cognitive Models were consistent regardless of sample. The performance of the Paul Motor Model was reduced when applied to participants without prior falls, reflecting the model's reliance on prior fall history as a key predictor. The WROTF LASSO Models performed the best, regardless of the sample.

Paul Motor Model Performance

In the full cohort, the Paul Motor Model showed that the presence of freezing of gait (OR = 1.15) and a prior fall history (OR = 8.58) were associated with higher odds of future falls, while walking speed above 1.1 m/s on the WST (OR = 0.39) was associated with lower odds. The model demonstrated good discrimination, with a cross‐validated AUC of 0.77 (0.73, 0.80). Much of its predictive power appeared driven by prior fall history; 83% of individuals who had fallen in the prior year experienced another fall during the follow‐up year. Accordingly, reduced performance in fall‐naïve participants reflects the design of the simplified Paul model, which prioritizes clinical feasibility and includes prior falls as a central predictor.

WROTF LASSO Model Performance

In the full cohort, the WROTF LASSO Model retained the following predictors, in decreasing order of importance based on penalization: previous fall history (OR = 4.84), PST total correct (OR = 0.98), WST (m/s, OR = 0.64), years of PD (OR = 1.05), MDS‐UPDRS Part III total score (OR = 1.002), and LEDD (OR = 1.002 per 100 mg). The regularization path is shown in Supplementary Figure S1. Prior fall history dwarfed all other predictors. This model achieved the highest discrimination, with a cross‐validated AUC of 0.81 (0.78, 0.83). Because coefficients were estimated using penalized (LASSO) logistic regression, reported odds ratios are shrunken toward 1 by design and may underrepresent the magnitude of the true associations.

Model Performance in Fall‐Naïve Patients

Among participants without prior falls, external validation of the CYCLE‐II Cognitive Model using the published coefficients yielded an AUC of 0.68 (0.63, 0.72). When the model was refit using the same predictor set within the fall‐naïve cohort, discrimination remained stable (cross‐validated AUC 0.68 [0.64, 0.73]), with odds ratios of 0.96 for PST total correct, 0.99 for VMT correct symbols plus location, and 1.11 for years of PD. The Paul Motor Model showed reduced performance (AUC = 0.62 (0.55, 0.68)), with odds ratios of 0.51 for freezing of gait and 0.36 for walking speed above 1.1 m/s. The WROTF LASSO Model retained a more balanced set of predictors, PST total correct (OR = 0.98), WST (m/s, OR = 0.76), years of PD (OR = 1.03), and age (OR = 1.01), and achieved an AUC of 0.70 (0.65, 0.74). These penalized odds ratios are provided to convey direction and relative contribution within the predictive model.

Sensitivity Analysis

Model performance was reassessed using a complete case approach, excluding participants with missing data (n = 495; 306 prior non‐fallers). Discrimination was comparable to the primary imputed analyses across all models and cohorts (Supplementary Table S3).

Health Care Utilization and Functional Impact Following a First Fall

Among the 203 participants who had not fallen in the year before the WROTF visit but experienced a fall during the subsequent year, 48.8% required an emergency visit, 52.7% underwent imaging, 12.3% were hospitalized, 3.0% were discharged to skilled nursing, and 7.4% required new assistive devices (Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

Leveraging data from nearly 1000 patients with up to 2 years of follow‐up, we validated an existing cognitive‐based model and developed a new integrative model to predict first‐time falls independent of prior fall history. The CYCLE‐II Cognitive Model preserved its performance in fall‐naïve participants, whereas the Paul Motor Model did not. The WROTF‐LASSO Model, combining cognitive, motor, demographic, and disease measures, demonstrated comparable performance to the CYCLE‐II Cognitive Model in fall‐naïve participants, with disease duration, gait speed, age, and processing speed emerging as key predictors, underscoring the multifactorial nature of fall risk in PD.

Interpretation of these results should recognize the inherent difficulty of predicting first falls in PD, particularly among individuals without a prior fall history. First‐fall risk reflects complex interactions among motor, cognitive, disease‐related, and environmental factors that are not fully captured by routinely available clinical variables. As shown in Supplementary Table S5, decision thresholds involve predictable sensitivity–specificity trade‐offs, supporting flexible, context‐dependent application of model outputs as a clinical decision‐support tool rather than a standalone determinant of care.

A key strength of this work is its clinical integration. Tablet‐based WROTF assessments feed directly into the EHR, enabling real‐time risk scores that can inform clinician review and prompt timely referrals or treatment adjustments, an approach previously validated in non‐PD inpatient settings. 14 This model reflects the broader direction of movement disorders care, in which technology‐enabled assessments are increasingly integrated into routine PD clinical practice and research.

Incorporating cognitive assessments represents an important evolution in fall prediction. Deficits in executive function and processing speed impair gait control and adaptive responses to environmental challenges. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Our findings reinforce that cognitive dysfunction independently predicts falls, aligning with evidence that falls in PD arise from complex motor‐cognitive‐environmental interactions. 1 , 3 , 20 , 21

Embedding predictive models within the EHR enables scalable, proactive care, allowing timely interventions before a sentinel fall. 22 Given the substantial downstream health care utilization observed after first falls, this approach aligns with precision medicine and value‐based care initiatives. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26

Limitations include data collection in a clinical setting; although completion rates were high, missing data may affect model generalizability. In addition, PD diagnoses were identified using ICD‐10 codes, which may introduce diagnostic misclassification in EHR‐based research and could affect cohort characterization, including potential inclusion of atypical or secondary parkinsonism. 27 Systematic assessment of dysautonomic features, including orthostatic hypotension, was not consistently available within the WROTF dataset and therefore could not be incorporated, despite their known association with falls in PD. The predominantly white cohort underscores the need for validation in more diverse populations. The retrospective design introduces potential recall and selection biases, while multiple analyses raise overfitting risk, mitigated by penalized regression, cross‐validation, and adequate sample size. 28

In conclusion, the WROTF‐LASSO Model, integrating disease duration, age, gait speed, and processing speed, demonstrated performance comparable to established cognitive‐ and motor‐based models for predicting first falls in PD, including among individuals without prior falls. While no clear superiority over existing approaches was observed, combining cognitive and motor metrics using routinely collected clinical data provides a practical framework for multifactorial fall‐risk assessment. Embedding predictive models within the EHR may provide scalable, proactive identification of patients at increased risk and support timely preventive interventions. Prospective, multi‐center studies are needed to determine whether model‐informed risk assessment translates into meaningful reductions in falls and fall‐related morbidity, and to identify additional predictors that may improve first‐fall prediction.

Curves show predictor coefficients (expressed as odds ratios) across lambda (shrinkage) values. As shrinkage increases, coefficients are forced to grow smaller and eventually leave the model (represented by an odds ratio of one), with the least important predictors removed first.
