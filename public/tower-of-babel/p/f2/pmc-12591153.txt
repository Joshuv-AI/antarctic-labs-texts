Emerging roles of basophils in the resolution of acute respiratory distress syndrome
Authors: Seiko Takasawa, Tomoya Tateishi, Jun Sugihara, Sho Shibata, Junya Ito, Masaru Ito, Koki Hata, Sachiko Miyake, Hajime Karasuyama, Kensuke Miyake, Yasunari Miyazaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract During the resolution phase of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), lung-infiltrating basophils produce interleukin (IL)-4, which acts on neutrophils to suppress anti-apoptotic gene expression and the production of pro-inflammatory mediators, thereby alleviating lung inflammation.

Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a clinical syndrome characterised by acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure caused by lung inflammation [1]. It develops from various clinical conditions, such as pneumonia, nonpulmonary infections, trauma, transfusion, burns, aspiration and shock. Despite the advances in critical care and organ support techniques, ARDS mortality rate remains high at 30–40% [1–4]. Currently, no effective pharmacological cure exists, with only supportive care with mechanical ventilation remaining as the cornerstone of ARDS treatment [1–5].

ARDS pathophysiology involves dysregulated lung inflammation marked by extensive neutrophil recruitment to the alveolar space, alveolar epithelial cell death and activation of macrophages and platelets [2–4, 6]. Studies using animal models have revealed that type 1 cytokines, including interferon-γ, play key roles in initiating acute lung inflammation in ARDS [7–9]. Although specific mechanisms driving the acute inflammatory phase in ARDS have been extensively investigated [10], those involved in resolving lung inflammation remain poorly understood.

Recent human studies have highlighted the potential link between blood basophil levels and ARDS outcomes. For instance, a recent cohort study by Chen et al. [11] reported that the lack of basophils in peripheral blood is associated with the poor prognosis of patients with sepsis in the intensive care unit. Similarly, studies on patients with coronavirus disease 2019 at high risk of developing ARDS have shown that individuals with severe disease often exhibit lower peripheral blood basophil counts than those with mild or moderate disease [12, 13]. These clinical findings indicate the association between low basophil counts and severe ARDS, suggesting the role of basophils in lung inflammation resolution.

Basophils are the least abundant granulocytes, accounting for <1% of the peripheral blood leukocytes. Recent studies have revealed their nonredundant roles in various type 2 and non-type 2 immune responses [14–17]. They exhibit high interleukin (IL)-4 production in response to various stimuli in mice and humans. We and others have previously reported that basophil-derived IL-4 contributes to inflammation resolution in various murine models, including allergic skin inflammation, bacterial infection, and myocardial infarction models [18–21]. Moreover, exogenous IL-4 administration improves survival and promotes recovery of bodyweight, whereas treatment with a neutralising anti-IL-4 antibody impairs ARDS resolution [22]. These reports suggest the beneficial roles of IL-4 in resolving ARDS.

Based on these observations, we hypothesised that basophils alleviate ARDS-associated lung inflammation by producing IL-4. To verify this hypothesis, we used ARDS model mice. Through functional experiments with genetically engineered mice combined with single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) and flow cytometry, we aimed to determine whether basophils alleviate lung inflammation in ARDS.

Methods

All methods are described in detail in the supplementary material.

Mice

C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Sankyo Labo Service Corporation or CLEA Japan. Mcpt8DTR/+, IL-4 reporter G4 (Il4GFP/+), IL-4-deficient, Mcpt8iCre/+ Il4fl and Il4rafl mice have been described previously [23–27]. MRP8Cre and Cx3cr1Cre/+ mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory [28, 29]. All animal procedures were approved by the institutional animal care and use committee of the Institute of Science Tokyo (approval numbers A2024-057C3 and A2024-168C).

Establishment of lipopolysaccharide-induced ARDS model mice and basophil depletion

Mice (aged 7–11 weeks) received an intratracheal injection of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (20 mg·kg−1; Millipore Sigma) under anaesthesia. For basophil depletion, Mcpt8DTR/+ mice were intravenously administered 750 ng of diphtheria toxin (DT) or an inactive DT mutant (mDT) 1 day before and 4 days after LPS administration. Peripheral oxygen saturation was measured using the MouseOx Plus Pulse Oximeter under isoflurane anaesthesia. Lung injury scores were evaluated by two or three independent pulmonologists based on a previous report [30]. For flow cytometric analysis, lungs were perfused with EDTA–PBS and dissociated using a lung dissociation kit (Miltenyi Biotec). Single-cell suspensions were stained with a panel of antibodies and analysed using the FACSLyric flow cytometer with FlowJo software.

scRNA-seq analysis

Single-cell suspensions were enriched via density gradient separation and labelled for scRNA-seq using the BD Rhapsody system. Sequencing was performed on NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina), and data were analysed using the Seurat v5.0.3 [31] in R 4.4.0. Gene set enrichment analysis was performed using clusterProfiler v4.12.0 [32].

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using the GraphPad Prism (v10.2.3) software. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Results

Optimal experimental conditions to study the roles of basophils in ARDS

LPS-induced acute lung injury models are widely used as animal models of ARDS [10, 33]. However, administration of excessive doses of LPS results in fatal lung damage, thereby limiting the analysis to the acute phase and preventing the investigation of the resolution phase of ARDS. To assess the roles of basophils in LPS-induced ARDS, we first optimised the LPS dosage to induce an acute inflammatory response, followed by a resolution phase in C57BL/6 mice. In wild-type (WT) mice intratracheally administered 20 mg·kg−1 LPS, we observed progressive bodyweight loss, hypoxaemia and increased lung wet-to-dry weight ratio up to 4–5 days post-LPS challenge, after which these parameters gradually returned to homeostatic levels by day 10 (figure 1a–c). Consistent with these findings, lung histopathology and injury scores progressively worsened until day 4, characterised by massive cellular infiltration in the lung interstitium and alveolar spaces, followed by recovery to homeostatic conditions by day 10 (figure 1d–e; supplementary figure E1). Flow cytometric analysis of inflamed lungs revealed that neutrophils as the predominant cell types, accounting for 40–60% of CD45+ haematopoietic cells on days 2, 4 and 6 after LPS inhalation (supplementary figure E2). Moreover, numbers of total haematopoietic cells and neutrophils increased until day 4 and then declined to near-baseline levels by day 10 (figure 1f). Additionally, mRNA expression levels of the inflammatory cytokines (Tnf and Il1b) peaked on days 2–4 and gradually returned to baseline levels thereafter (figure 1g). Based on these results, we set days 0–4 and 4–10 as the acute inflammatory and resolution phases of ARDS-associated lung inflammation, respectively, in subsequent experiments.

Basophil-depleted mice fail to resolve lung inflammation

To investigate the roles of basophils in LPS-induced ARDS, we used two distinct methods of basophil depletion: DT administration to Mcpt8DTR mice [23] and anti-CD200R3 antibody treatment of WT mice [34]. For DT-mediated basophil depletion, either DT or its inactive mutant mDT was intravenously administered to Mcpt8DTR mice 1 day before and 4 days after LPS challenge (figure 2a). DT-treated Mcpt8DTR mice, but not mDT-treated controls, failed to recover from the bodyweight loss and hypoxaemia during the resolution phase (days 5–6), although DT treatment had minimal effect during the acute inflammatory phase (days 1–4; figure 2b–c). Consistent with these findings, DT-treated mice exhibited markedly increased cellular infiltration in the lungs and significantly higher lung injury scores than the mDT-treated controls on day 6, with little difference observed during the early inflammatory phase on day 2 (figure 2d–e; supplementary figure E4). Furthermore, wet-to-dry weight ratio, an indicator of gas–blood barrier integrity, was significantly elevated in DT-treated mice during the resolution phase (figure 2f). Flow cytometric analysis confirmed that DT treatment significantly increased the numbers of total haematopoietic cells and neutrophils in the lungs on day 6, but not on day 2, compared to mDT treatment (figure 2g). Indeed, DT treatment almost completely depleted the basophils in the lungs, spleen and bone marrow on days 2 and 6 post-LPS administration (figure 2h; supplementary figure E5a and b). Importantly, DT treatment of Mcpt8DTR mice selectively depleted the basophils in the peripheral blood, with minimal effects on other granulocyte lineages, confirming the specificity of basophil depletion in this model (supplementary figure E5c and d). Notably, in mDT-treated control mice, basophil numbers in the lungs on day 6 were approximately eight-fold higher than those on day 2, although basophils accounted for only 0.15% of the total haematopoietic cells (figure 2h). These findings suggest that, despite their low abundance, basophils are essential for the resolution of ARDS-associated lung inflammation.

Consistent with the DT-mediated basophil depletion findings, mice treated with basophil-depleting antibodies exhibited worse histopathological features and significantly higher lung inflammation scores than the control antibody-treated mice on day 6, but not on day 2, post-inhalation (supplementary figure E6a–c). Flow cytometric analysis revealed increased numbers of neutrophils and total haematopoietic cells in the lungs on day 6 (supplementary figure E6d). These results further support the critical roles of basophils in ARDS resolution.

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed that IL-4 was predominantly expressed by basophils

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the basophil-mediated resolution of lung inflammation, we conducted scRNA-seq of lung cells 6 days post-LPS inhalation. Clustering of scRNA-seq data identified various cell types, including basophils, neutrophils, monocyte–macrophages (MoMacs), alveolar macrophages, dendritic cells, T-cells, B-cells, natural killer cells, endothelial cells and fibroblasts (figure 3a; supplementary figure E7a). Consistent with the flow cytometry results, neutrophils and MoMacs were the major cell types, whereas basophils were a minor population (supplementary figure E7b). Comparison of gene expression profiles across cell types revealed the genes predominantly expressed by basophils, such as Il4 (figure 3b). Consistently, analysis of IL-4 reporter G4 mice revealed that the lung-infiltrating basophils predominantly expressed IL-4 in the inflamed lungs on days 2 and 6 post-inhalation (supplementary figure E8), suggesting the key roles of IL-4 in this model. Notably, Il4 levels in the lungs were relatively low in the acute phase and elevated in the resolution phase (figure 3c), mirroring the time course of the lung basophil count (figure 2h).

Basophil-derived IL-4 is essential for ARDS resolution

Next, we analysed IL-4-deficient mice to determine the specific roles of IL-4 in ARDS resolution. These mice failed to resolve lung inflammation, as evidenced by the impaired recovery of bodyweight loss (figure 4a), prolonged hypoxaemia (figure 4b), worsened lung histopathology (figure 4c, supplementary figure E9a), and increased lung injury scores (figure 4d) during the resolution phase (day 6), but not during the initial inflammatory phase (day 2). IL-4 deficiency also increased the wet-to-dry weight ratio on day 6 post-challenge (figure 4e). Consistently, IL-4-deficient mice exhibited significantly higher numbers of total haematopoietic cells and neutrophils than the WT mice on day 6 post-LPS challenge, despite similar numbers of lung basophils in the two groups (figure 4f). We used basophil-specific IL-4-deficient (Mcpt8iCreIl4fl) mice to further examine the specific contribution of basophil-derived IL-4. These mice exhibited impaired bodyweight recovery (figure 4g), severe histopathological changes (figure 4h, supplementary figure E9b), and increased lung injury scores (figure 4i) during the resolution phase (day 6), but not during the initial inflammatory phase (day 2). Basophil-specific IL-4 deficiency increased the wet-to-dry weight ratio (figure 4j) and neutrophil accumulation in the lungs (figure 4k) during the resolution phase compared to those in the control Mcpt8iCre mice. Collectively, these findings suggest that basophils promote ARDS resolution via IL-4 production.

IL-4 acts on neutrophils to alleviate lung inflammation in the ARDS resolution phase

To clarify the mechanisms by which basophil-derived IL-4 facilitates ARDS resolution, we sought to identify IL-4-responding cell populations. IL-4 reprogrammes macrophages to induce pro-resolving M2-like macrophages, contributing to ARDS resolution [22, 35]. Furthermore, IL-4 suppresses neutrophil survival under hypoxic conditions, thereby alleviating lung inflammation [36]. Therefore, we investigated the roles of MoMacs and neutrophils as potential basophil-derived IL-4 targets under our experimental conditions.

To examine IL-4 signalling in MoMac lineage cells, we used Cx3cr1CreIl4rafl mice, in which IL-4 receptors were selectively depleted in MoMacs. Contrary to expectations, these mice exhibited similar bodyweight changes, histopathology and cellular infiltration as the control Il4rafl mice; however, a slight increase in lung injury scores was observed on day 6 (figure 5a–d; supplementary figure E10a and b). In contrast, neutrophil-specific IL-4 receptor-deficient (MRP8CreIl4rafl) mice showed severe lung histopathology and elevated lung injury scores as early as day 2, with impaired bodyweight recovery, increased neutrophil infiltration and worsened lung inflammation by day 6. Pathological changes in these mice became more pronounced by day 6 (figure 5e–h; supplementary figure E10c and d), indicating the progressive exacerbation of lung inflammation in the absence of IL-4 signalling in neutrophils. Taken together, these results strongly suggest that basophil-derived IL-4 primarily acts on neutrophils, not macrophages, to promote ARDS resolution.

Basophil-derived IL-4 suppresses inflammatory and anti-apoptotic neutrophil generation

Next, we explored the mechanisms by which the basophil–IL-4–neutrophil axis promotes the resolution of lung inflammation in LPS-induced ARDS. We performed scRNA-seq of lung cells from WT and IL-4-deficient mice on day 6 (supplementary figure E11a). IL-4-deficient mouse neutrophils showed higher expression levels of inflammatory cytokine and chemokine genes (Il1a, Il1b and Cxcl2), receptors involved in migration and adhesion (Fpr1, Fpr2 and Itgam), S100A proteins (S100a8 and S100a9), and anti-apoptotic genes (Bcl2a1a, Bcl2a1b and Bcl2a1d) than the WT mouse neutrophils (figure 6a). In contrast, WT mouse neutrophils expressed the apoptosis-promoting gene (Ddit3), and anti-inflammatory transcription factor (Atf5) (figure 6a). These results suggest that the lung neutrophils in IL-4-deficient mice exhibit highly inflammatory properties with higher anti-apoptotic gene levels than those in WT mice.

Re-clustering of neutrophils (S100a8+Mmp9+) revealed five distinct subsets (Neu1–5; figure 6b). IL-4-deficient mice exhibited a lower frequency of Neu1 and higher frequency of Neu5 than WT mice (figure 6c). Gene set enrichment analysis revealed that Neu5 exhibited upregulation of pathways related to “myeloid leukocyte activation”, “regulation of innate immune responses”, “IL-1β production”, “pattern recognition receptor signalling”, “tumour necrosis factor signalling pathway” and “leukocyte transendothelial migration” (figure 6d; supplementary figure E11b). These results suggest that Neu5 corresponds to a highly inflammatory neutrophil population. Increased prevalence of this subpopulation in IL-4-deficient mice suggests that basophil-derived IL-4 prevents neutrophils from becoming highly inflammatory, thereby facilitating ARDS resolution.

Basophil depletion exacerbates bleomycin-induced lung inflammation

To further corroborate our findings in the LPS-induced ARDS model, we examined the role of basophils in an alternative ARDS model. We examined the widely used bleomycin-induced ARDS mouse model. Intratracheal administration of bleomycin increases the number of neutrophils in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) within 24 h, with neutrophil infiltration returning to homeostatic levels by day 11 [33]. Moreover, lung Il1b and Tnf levels are elevated on days 2–3, whereas Il4 levels are upregulated on day 14 post-inhalation [37, 38]. To investigate the roles of basophils in the resolution phase of bleomycin-induced ARDS, we analysed the lung histopathology of basophil-depleted mice on day 14 post-inhalation (supplementary figure E12a). Basophil depletion significantly worsened the lung histopathology and increased the lung injury scores on day 14 (supplementary figure E12b and c), confirming the pro-resolving roles of basophils in ARDS.

Discussion

Recent human cohort studies have reported the potential association between low blood basophil counts and high ARDS severity [11–13]. However, establishing a causal relationship between basophil counts and ARDS pathogenesis in humans remains challenging. In this study, we addressed this issue by analysing LPS-induced ARDS model mice. We found that basophils, although a minor component of lung-infiltrating cells, played critical roles in the resolution of ARDS lung inflammation via secretion of IL-4, which acted on neutrophils to alleviate lung inflammation.

Previous studies have highlighted the roles of IL-4 in suppressing or resolving lung inflammation in LPS-induced ARDS mouse models [22, 35]. D’Alessio et al. [22] demonstrated that repeated intraperitoneal administration of exogenous IL-4 improves the survival and weight and reduces the albumin levels and neutrophil counts in BALF in mice. Liu et al. [35] revealed that the intratracheal transplantation of engineered IL-4-secreting macrophages alleviates LPS-induced lung inflammation. In addition, D’Alessio et al. [22] demonstrated that treatment with an IL-4-neutralising antibody significantly impairs the resolution of lung inflammation, as indicated by the increased albumin levels and neutrophil counts in the BALF, underscoring the importance of endogenous IL-4 in lung inflammation resolution. However, specific cellular sources of IL-4 remain unclear. The present study clearly identified basophils as the predominant producers of IL-4 among all cells in the inflamed lungs. Notably, basophil-specific IL-4-deficient mice failed to resolve lung inflammation during the ARDS resolution phase. Therefore, basophil-derived IL-4 played crucial roles in resolving the lung inflammation in ARDS model mice.

M2-like macrophages are well-known anti-inflammatory cells involved in various inflammatory processes and conditions, such as tissue repair, tumour immunity and metabolic disorders [39–41]. IL-4 is a classic inducer of M2 macrophages, and exogenous IL-4 or engineered IL-4-secreting macrophages increases the levels of M2 markers, such as Arg1, Fizz1 and Ym1, in lung tissues of LPS-induced ARDS models [22, 35], suggesting that IL-4 promotes the generation of pro-resolving M2 macrophages. Furthermore, macrophage depletion using clodronate liposomes exacerbates lung inflammation during the resolution phase, implying that IL-4-induced M2 macrophage generation is important for ARDS resolution [22]. In contrast to these reports, we found that the absence of IL-4 receptors in macrophages had little impact on the resolution of LPS-induced lung inflammation. Instead, IL-4 receptor deficiency in neutrophils significantly impaired the resolution of lung inflammation. The reason for this discrepancy remains unclear. Recently, Culemann et al. [42] reported that neutrophils also take up clodronate liposomes, resulting in functional impairment without complete depletion in a serum transfer arthritis model. Therefore, clodronate liposome administration affects both macrophages and neutrophils, potentially influencing ARDS resolution. Alternatively, IL-4 indirectly promotes the production of pro-resolving M2-like macrophages. As phagocytosis of apoptotic neutrophils induces a pro-resolving phenotype in macrophages [43], we hypothesised that basophil-derived IL-4 promotes neutrophil apoptosis, thereby facilitating the macrophage-mediated clearance of neutrophils and supporting the generation of pro-resolving M2-like macrophages.

Our transcriptomic analyses revealed that lung neutrophils in IL-4-deficient mice included a highly inflammatory subpopulation with elevated levels of anti-apoptotic genes, such as the Bcl2 family genes, suggesting that basophil-derived IL-4 promotes neutrophil apoptosis. Harris et al. [36] demonstrated that neutrophils in both patients with ARDS and LPS-induced ARDS model mice express IL-4 receptors on their surfaces. When cultured ex vivo with recombinant IL-4 under hypoxic conditions, both human and mouse neutrophils show increased apoptosis [36]. This report aligns with our findings of increased anti-apoptotic gene levels in the neutrophils of IL-4-deficient mice. Additionally, IL-4 suppresses LPS-induced expression of pro-inflammatory genes, such as TNF and IL1B, in human neutrophils [36], which is consistent with our findings that lung neutrophils isolated from IL-4-deficient mice exhibited elevated expression levels of genes encoding inflammatory cytokines. Together, these results suggest that IL-4 acts on neutrophils to promote apoptosis and reduce inflammatory mediator secretion in humans and mice, contributing to the resolution of ARDS lung inflammation. However, the cellular source of IL-4 in human ARDS remains unknown, warranting further investigation.

In conclusion, this study found that the basophil–IL-4–neutrophil axis was critical for resolving lung inflammation in LPS-induced ARDS model mice. Considering the correlation between low blood basophil counts and severe disease in patients with respiratory failure, such as sepsis and coronavirus disease 2019 [11, 12, 44, 45], further studies are necessary to validate the relevance of our findings in human ARDS and explore novel therapeutic targets for this syndrome.
