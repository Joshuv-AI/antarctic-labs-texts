Low excision and high suspension hemorrhoidectomy versus conventional Milligan-Morgan hemorrhoidectomy for mixed hemorrhoids: a 12-month prospective randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yingzi Shu, Heng Deng, Jing Zhu, Cheng Zhang, Peiyang Sun, Feifei Wang, Hui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMixed hemorrhoids represent a highly prevalent benign anorectal disorder. Milligan-Morgan hemorrhoidectomy (MM) is the gold-standard treatment but carries substantial risks of severe postoperative pain, delayed recovery, and anal dysfunction due to extensive tissue resection. Low excision and high suspension hemorrhoidectomy (LEHS) is a refined, function-preserving technique, yet high-quality long-term comparative evidence remains limited. This study aimed to assess the efficacy, safety, and anal functional outcomes of LEHS versus MM for grade III-IV mixed hemorrhoids over 12 months.MethodsThis single-center, prospective, randomized, non-inferiority trial enrolled 120 eligible patients between January 2024 and June 2024, randomized 1:1 to LEHS or MM groups with allocation concealment and blinded outcome assessment. Primary outcomes were 12-week clinical effective rate and 12-month hemorrhoid recurrence rate; secondary outcomes included perioperative indicators, pain scores, recovery metrics, complications, anorectal manometry, and patient satisfaction. Statistical analysis was performed via SPSS 26.0 with a -10% non-inferiority margin (P < 0.05).ResultsBaseline characteristics were well balanced between groups (all P > 0.05). The 12-week effective rate (96.4% vs. 92.9%) and 12-month recurrence rate (1.8% vs. 5.4%) confirmed LEHS non-inferiority (both P > 0.05). LEHS yielded longer operative time, less blood loss, lower VAS pain scores, faster recovery, fewer complications (14.8% vs. 33.3%, P = 0.027), and more favorable anorectal physiology (all P < 0.001), with higher patient satisfaction.ConclusionsLEHS is non-inferior to MM in clinical efficacy and long-term recurrence control for grade III-IV mixed hemorrhoids, with superior safety, faster recovery, and better-preserved anal function without excessive hypertonia. Introduction

Symptomatic mixed hemorrhoids are among the most common anorectal diseases worldwide, with a lifetime incidence exceeding 50% in the general population [1]. Characterized by concurrent enlargement and prolapse of internal and external hemorrhoidal plexuses, severe mixed hemorrhoids (Goligher grade III-IV) often present with persistent bleeding, anal prolapse, pain, and pruritus, which significantly impair patients’ quality of life [2]. Conservative treatments (dietary adjustment, topical medications, sclerotherapy) are often ineffective for advanced disease, making surgical intervention the mainstay of care [3].

Milligan-Morgan hemorrhoidectomy, first described in 1937, has long been the gold-standard open excisional procedure for mixed hemorrhoids due to its radical efficacy and low long-term recurrence rate [4]. However, the conventional technique involves wide resection of perianal skin and hemorrhoidal vascular bundles, resulting in substantial tissue trauma, severe postoperative pain [5], high complication rates (perianal edema, anal stenosis, urinary retention), and increased anal tone and discomfort [6]. These drawbacks have driven the development of minimally invasive, function-preserving surgical techniques in anorectal surgery.

Low excision and high suspension hemorrhoidectomy (LEHS) is a refined, function-preserving procedure that adheres to the principles of tissue preservation and anatomical restoration, which is consistent with the modern concept of minimally invasive and function-preserving anorectal surgery reported in recent studies [7, 8]. Unlike the radical resection of MM [9], LEHS combines minimal external hemorrhoid excision with precise internal hemorrhoid management: internal hemorrhoids are fully mobilized before base ligation and high suspension, avoiding excessive dissection and protecting anal sphincter function. Notably, a similar surgical concept termed high suspension and low incision (HSLI) surgery has been reported for mixed hemorrhoids in a previous multi-center randomized controlled trial, which was based on traditional Chinese ligation technique and demonstrated non-inferior clinical efficacy and better pain control compared with Milligan-Morgan hemorrhoidectomy at 12 weeks postoperatively [10].

This prospective RCT was designed to rigorously compare the short-term efficacy, perioperative safety, postoperative recovery, and 12-month long-term outcomes of LEHS and MM for grade III-IV mixed hemorrhoids, with objective anorectal manometry data to verify anal function preservation. The findings aim to provide high-level clinical evidence for the selection of optimal surgical strategies for mixed hemorrhoids.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethics

This was a prospective, randomized, parallel‑controlled, non‑inferiority trial at the Department of Anorectal Surgery. It was conducted and reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 statement [11]. The study protocol was approved by Institutional Review Board of The South District of Hefei First People’s Hospital (No. HF-2024-KY-036) and registered retrospectively at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT07495046). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to enrollment, and the study was conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [12].

Randomization and blinding

A total of 120 eligible patients were enrolled between January 2024 and June 2024. Patients were randomly assigned 1:1 to the LEHS group or MM group using a computer-generated random number table with block randomization (block size = 4). Allocation concealment was achieved using sealed, opaque envelopes, which were opened only on the day of surgery. Outcome assessors (responsible for pain scoring, complication recording, and manometry testing) and statistical analysts were blinded to group assignments to minimize bias.

Surgical procedures

All operations were performed by two senior anorectal surgeons with ≥ 10 years of clinical experience, under spinal or epidural anesthesia, with patients in the prone jackknife position.

Follow-up

All patients were scheduled for follow-up at 1 week, 2 weeks, 4 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months postoperatively. Clinical examinations, symptom assessments, and complication recordings were performed at each visit; anorectal manometry was repeated at 12 weeks, and recurrence was evaluated at 12 months. A total of 12 patients (10% dropout rate) were lost to follow-up (5 in LEHS group, 7 in MM group) due to relocation or poor compliance; 108 patients completed the full 12-month follow-up (54 per group).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 software. Measurement data conforming to normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD) and compared using independent-samples t-test; non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using Mann-Whitney U test. Enumeration data were expressed as cases (percentage) and compared using χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The non-inferiority margin for the primary clinical efficacy outcome was set at -10%, and non-inferiority was confirmed if the lower limit of the 95% confidence interval (CI) of the effective rate difference was higher than − 10%.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated for a non-inferiority design based on the primary endpoint (12-week clinical effective rate). We assumed a baseline effective rate of 92% in the Milligan-Morgan group, a non-inferiority margin of − 10%, a statistical power of 90% (1 − β = 0.90), a two-sided α = 0.05, and an anticipated 10% dropout rate. The calculated required sample size was 54 patients per group, totaling 120 patients.

Primary outcomes

Intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis included all 120 randomized patients. Per-protocol (PP) analysis included 108 patients who completed the 12-month follow-up (54 per group).

For the primary endpoint of 12-week clinical effective rate: In the ITT population, the effective rate was 95.0% (57/60) in the LEHS group and 91.7% (55/60) in the MM group. In the PP population, the clinical effective rate was 96.4% (52/54) in the LEHS group and 92.9% (50/54) in the MM group; the difference in effective rates was 3.5% (95% CI: -2.1% to 9.1%), which was above the non-inferiority margin of -10%, confirming non-inferiority of LEHS (P > 0.05).

At 12 months, the recurrence rate was 1.8% (1/54) in the LEHS group and 5.4% (3/54) in the MM group, with no significant difference between groups (P > 0.05).

Perioperative and recovery outcomes

Although LEHS required longer operative time, the LEHS group exhibited significantly better perioperative and recovery outcomes compared with the MM group, including less blood loss, lower VAS pain scores at all postoperative time points (6 h, 24 h, 72 h, 7d, and 14d) (Fig. 3), faster wound healing, shorter hospital stay, and higher 12-month patient satisfaction (all P < 0.001). Discussion

This prospective randomized controlled trial is the first to evaluate the 12‑month efficacy, safety, and anal functional outcomes of low excision and high suspension hemorrhoidectomy (LEHS) versus conventional Milligan‑Morgan hemorrhoidectomy (MM) for grade III‑IV mixed hemorrhoids. The 10% follow‑up dropout rate was consistent with real‑world clinical practice. Our core findings confirmed that LEHS was non‑inferior to MM in clinical efficacy and long‑term recurrence control. Importantly, LEHS achieved significantly less postoperative pain (the primary concern of patients), a markedly lower risk of delayed bleeding, and more favorable anorectal physiology with minimal hypertonia and no stenosis (the key concerns of surgeons), making it more reliable and safer in clinical practice.

Over recent decades, numerous minimally invasive techniques have been developed to reduce pain, complications, and recurrence after hemorrhoid surgery, including stapled hemorrhoidopexy (PPH) [13], laser hemorrhoidoplasty [14], tissue‑selecting therapy (TST) [15], sclerotherapy [16], and ligation with advanced energy‑based devices [17–19]. Although these approaches offer improved postoperative recovery compared with conventional MM, most rely on specialized instruments, disposable staplers, or laser equipment, which inevitably increase medical costs and may not be widely available in primary or resource‑limited hospitals [20, 21]. Notably, LEHS represents a refined but low‑cost strategy that achieves superior outcomes purely through optimized surgical dissection and ligation, without requiring expensive staplers, energy devices, or additional consumables. By emphasizing full hemorrhoid mobilization, tension‑free ligation, and mucosal preservation, LEHS avoids the shortcomings of both conventional radical resection and costly minimally invasive procedures. This technique only demands improved surgical precision and effort from the operator, making it highly accessible, cost‑effective, and suitable for widespread clinical application.

The fundamental difference between the two procedures lies in surgical philosophy. MM achieves efficacy via radical tissue resection but disrupts perianal microcirculation [22], mucosal elasticity [23], and anatomical alignment, leading to excessive scarring and increased anal tone. In contrast, LEHS follows the modern principle of function-preserving surgery with minimal external excision and precise internal hemorrhoid handling. In LEHS, full mobilization of internal hemorrhoids before 3‑0 absorbable figure‑of‑eight ligation avoids blind dissection and excessive tissue injury, while high ligation and precise excision achieves reliable devascularization without mucosal traction. This tissue‑sparing strategy reduces damage to perianal sensory nerves and soft tissue, resulting in less pain, lower inflammation, less edema, and faster recovery. A previous literature review confirmed that anal sphincter spasm and mucosal traction are major contributors to severe pain after conventional hemorrhoidectomy [24–26]. By adequately mobilizing the hemorrhoidal mass and avoiding mucosal traction, LEHS effectively reduces sphincter irritation and spasm, which explains its significant advantage in postoperative pain relief. Although LEHS required longer operative time, this can be explained by the more precise dissection and high suspension steps. This difference may also reflect a moderate learning curve compared with the well‑standardized Milligan‑Morgan technique. With increased surgical experience, the operative time of LEHS can be reduced. The concept of function-preserving hemorrhoid surgery has been well established by Miyamoto et al. (2023), and the LEHS technique represents a simplified, cost-effective variant that avoids the need for specialized instruments while achieving reliable efficacy.

LEHS reduced the overall complication rate by more than 50% compared with MM, mainly by lowering perianal edema, urinary retention, and anal stenosis. Extensive dissection in MM impairs lymphatic and venous return, causing severe edema [27], whereas LEHS preserves local microcirculation through minimal excision. Anal stenosis, a serious complication of MM, results from excessive circumferential tissue removal; LEHS completely avoided stenosis by preserving skin bridges and rectal mucosa. Furthermore, LEHS significantly reduced delayed bleeding due to its tension‑free ligation. Mobilization before ligation enables precise, low‑tension suturing without mucosal traction, whereas direct ligation in MM creates excessive tension, increasing the risk of mucosal tear and delayed bleeding during defecation [28]. This mechanical advantage further improves the safety of LEHS.

Anorectal manometry revealed comparable baseline anal function between groups. Postoperatively, both groups exhibited mild increases in anal resting pressure, a common finding related to tissue repair and mild scarring. Notably, the MM group showed a significantly greater increase in anal resting pressure, indicating prominent anal sphincter hypertonia and excessive scarring following radical tissue resection. The higher anal resting pressure in the MM group was closely correlated with an increased risk of anal discomfort, edema, and even anal stenosis, consistent with our clinical outcome data. In contrast, LEHS showed minimal pressure changes, reflecting better preservation of the normal physiological state of the anal canal. These findings confirm that LEHS is more physiologically favorable and less likely to cause postoperative anal discomfort or functional disturbance. The 12‑month recurrence rate of LEHS was comparable to that of MM, which supports its role as a valid alternative to the gold‑standard MM.

This study has several limitations: First, it is a moderate sample size; thus, multi‑center large‑scale trials are needed to improve generalizability; Second, the trial was registered retrospectively; Third, only 12-month follow-up data were reported; longer-term follow-up is required to assess ultra‑long‑term efficacy and durability.

Conclusion

Low excision and high suspension hemorrhoidectomy (LEHS) is non-inferior to Milligan-Morgan hemorrhoidectomy for grade III–IV mixed hemorrhoids, with similar 12-month recurrence rates. LEHS provides significantly less pain, lower risks of delayed bleeding and anal stenosis, better-preserved anal function, and faster recovery. As a cost-effective, technique-optimized procedure that requires no special devices, LEHS is safe, reliable, and suitable for widespread clinical use as a function-preserving alternative to conventional surgery.
