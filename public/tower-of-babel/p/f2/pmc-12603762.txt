A randomized clinical trial of surgical intervention with or without acupuncture combined with traditional Chinese medicine to treat intrauterine adhesions
Authors: Zhao Wenwen, Wang Jiaqi, Sun Jing, Tim RE Harris, Li Haixia, Zhao Xiaoqian, Liu Rui, Gu Xinyu, Ma Shumin, Lou Zhifei, Liu Lifei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) and acupuncture in addition to estrogen/progesterone therapy in the post-operative management of intrauterine adhesions.Patients and methodsA single center, randomized, non-blinded, controlled trial in which participants received either estrogen/progesterone (group 1), or estrogen/progesterone combined with TCM (group 2), or estrogen/progesterone combined with TCM and navel acupuncture (group 3) for 3 months following transcervical resection of adhesions. The primary outcome was the difference in the uterine adhesion (American Fertility Society, AFS) score at 3 months post hysteroscopy assessed on days 3–7 post menses. The secondary outcomes included endometrial thickness and traditional Chinese medicine score at 3 months, and pregnancy rate at 6 months after hysteroscopy.Results120 participants were recruited. There were no differences in AFS at baseline but at 3 months AFS scores were [(4.53 (95 % CI 3.85–5.20) vs. 3.58 (95 % CI 2.94–4.21) vs. 2.70 (95 % CI 2.14–3.26), p < 0.001, pairwise comparison p < 0.05)]. Endometrial thickness increased in all groups [6.20 (95 % CI 5.67–6.72) vs. 6.88 (95 % CI 6.38–7.38) vs. 7.82 (95 % CI 7.26–8.38), pairwise comparison p < 0.05)]. There was a statistically significant difference in pregnancy rates between groups 3 and 1 [42.5 % vs. 10.0 %, p < 0.05)], and group 3 and 2 [ 42.5 % vs. 20.0 %, p < 0.05)], but not between group 1 and group 2 (p > 0.05).ConclusionThis pilot randomized controlled trial provides provisional data that the addition of TCM and acupuncture to estrogen therapy following the surgical treatment of intrauterine adhesions may improve outcomes.Trial registrationInternational Traditional Medicine Clinical Trial Registry (ITMCTR2024000258) (http://itmctr.ccebtcm.org.cn/en-US), a member of the WHO Primary Network of ICTRP.Keywords: Intra uterine adhesions, Hysteroscopic adhesiolysis, Randomized trial, Navel acupuncture, Traditional Chinese medicine

Introduction

Intrauterine adhesions (IUA) (Asherman's Syndrome) are characterized by intrauterine fibrous bands which reduce the uterine cavity size with resulting infertility, abdominal pain, dysmenorrhea and/or amenorrhea, but are frequently asymptomatic [1]. Uterine cavity instrumentation and spontaneous abortion are common risk factors factors [2]. Prevalence ranges from 25 % to 30 % in women undergoing surgical termination pregnancy and 31 %–45 % after hysteroscopic myomectomy [3,4]. The overall conception rate is around 40 % after hysteroscopic adhesiolysis [5]. The recurrence rate after transcervical resection of adhesion (TCRA) is as high as 62.5 % [6].

Hysteroscopy is the gold standard method for diagnosis. Surgical treatment is undertaken to relieve symptoms and/or improve fertility and is commonly supplemented by post procedural intra-uterine barrier devices [7]. Hormonal and anti-inflammatory medications are commonly prescribed to reduce post procedural adhesions and promote endometrial regeneration [8,9].

Previous animal and limited human data suggest that traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) and/or acupuncture may reduce post operative IUA, potentially improving outcomes [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. The authors were unable to find published randomized clinical trial data that combined surgical intervention and post procedural hormone therapy with TCM and acupuncture in the treatment of IUA. The authors hypothesized that TCM and/or acupuncture would augment estrogen/progesterone therapy and improve outcomes in the treatment of IUA. The paucity of previous related research saw this designed as a pilot trial.

Materials and methods

Trial design

This single center, randomized clinical trial was performed at a large teaching Hospital in China, and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Liaoning University of Traditional Chinese Medicine [serial number: Y223034CS(K7)-034-01; July 21, 2023]. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and National Chinese guidelines for good clinical practice. Written, informed consent was obtained from all participants. The trial was registered in the International Traditional Medicine Clinical Trial Registry (ITMCTR2024000258) (http://itmctr.ccebtcm.org.cn/en-US), a member of the WHO Primary Network of ICTRP. This trial was registered post recruitment (August 19, 2024) following an error in the initial registration. The authors confirm that all ongoing and related trials for these interventions are registered. The full protocol is provided as supplementary material.

Participants

Women between 24 and 40 who were trying to conceive and met both Western Medicine (WM) diagnostic criteria for IUA (as defined in the 2015 Chinese Expert Consensus on Clinical Diagnosis and Treatment of Uterine Adhesions) and the equivalent TCM diagnostic criteria (as defined by Gynecology of Traditional Chinese Medicine (10th Edition) and Guiding Principles for Clinical Research of New Chinese Medicines for kidney deficiency and blood stasis syndrome) were offered the opportunity to participate in the trial [6,14,15]. Participants were screened and recruited in outpatient clinics after clinical and hysteroscopic evaluation.

Interventions

All participants underwent identical evaluation and surgical therapy. Hysteroscopy was performed following national guidance and an intra-endometrial balloon placed for 7 days post procedure [8]. All patients were treated with identical post operative hormone therapy for three consecutive menstrual cycles. This consisted of one 2 mg estradiol tablet per day in the morning for 14 days then one 2 mg estradiol - 10 mg dydrogesterone combined tablet each day for the subsequent 14 days (Abbott Healthcare Products B. V, Houtenlaan, Weesp, Netherlands). Patients were randomized into three groups. Group 1 received sham acupuncture and placebo TCM; group 2 treatment with TCM and sham acupuncture, and group 3 treatment with TCM and acupuncture.

TCM therapy is fully described in the supplementary material. Drug preparation for treatment and placebo TCM preparations were provide by the Baicaoyuan Pharmacy Bureau of the Affiliated Hospital of Liaoning University of Traditional Chinese Medicine.

Acupuncture was provided by a trained specialist using Qiqiqijiu (0.25mm × 25 mm) acupuncture needles slowly inserted to a depth of 0.5 inches and remaining in situ for 30 min using acupuncture points Kun, Dui, Kan, Zhen and Li (see supplementary material). Sham acupuncture involved identical needle insertion 2 cm distal to these points.

Randomization

Participants were randomized using blocks of 6 and assigned 1:1:1 to groups 1, 2 or 3, that is 2 participants to each group within each block labeled A, B, C, D, E, F. Each block had 90 possible allocations. The resulting 90 allocations were assigned using an on-line random number generation (http://upload.fjnews.com/) set to offer numbers 1–90, Each referring to the corresponding ABCDEF grouping scheme in the random event table.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the intrauterine adhesions score (AFS score, see Table 1, as described by the American Fertility Society (AFS)) at three months as compared to baseline [16]. This describes the presence, extent, and morphological characteristics of adhesions, and the quality of the endometrium. This score is a widely used outcome measure in similar research to assess overall disease severity, predicting symptom severity and subsequent pregnancy rates [9,17]. AFS scores were categorized as mild (1–4), moderate (5–8), or severe (9–12). The assessment was by two certified Gynaecological surgeons independently using hysteroscopic images who were blinded to the treatment group.

TCRA was performed by the same surgeon on day 3–7 post menses of the third menstrual cycle for menstruating women. This ensured minimal infection risk, and that assessment was performed when the endometrium was thinnest. For women with amenorrhea hysteroscopy timing was determined using hormonal assay. The participants were assessed as in the follicular phase if estradiol was 15–127 pg/ml, luteinizing hormone 2.12–10.89 IU/L, progesterone 0.31–1.52 ng/ml, and follicle-stimulating hormone 3.65–6.78 IU/L (Unicel DXI 800 access immunoassay system, Brea, California, America).

Secondary outcomes included (luteal phase) endometrial thickness, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) syndrome score (assessed during the follicular phase, as AFS score) and pregnancy rates at 3 months after completing treatment (6 months post-hysteroscopy). Endometrial thickness was assessed using transvaginal ultrasound performed on day 13–24 day of the menstrual cycle. For women with amenorrhea the equivalent day in the luteal phase was defined as follows: estradiol 49–291 pg/ml, luteinizing hormone 1.20–12.66 IU/L, progesterone 5.16–18.56 ng/ml, and follicle-stimulating hormone 1.79–5.12 IU/L (Unicel DXI 800 access immunoassay system, Brea, State of California, America). The TCM score was assessed using patient reported outcomes combined with physician assessment using pulse analysis and tongue appearance (Table 2). The TCM Syndrome score assesses the syndrome type and severity of the disease by quantifying patients’ symptoms, physical signs, and tongue-pulse findings [14,15]. and has been used in previous IUA research but not formally validated [18,19]. The two assessing physicians (trained in traditional Chinese medicine, not Gynecologists) were trained on the TCM score, blind to all interventions and independent of the study group. If the result difference was >1 point, a third external senior physician who was unaware of the situation would arbitrate, and finally, the consistent result or the arbitration result would be adopted.

Only two team members had access to the database password and all patients assigned a unique code. The intervention implementers could only obtain the “intervention instructions” through the patient's unique number and could not know the specific intervention content.

After stopping the trial medications the patients did not take any contraceptive measures and were followed up for an additional 3 months and assessed for pregnancy Pregnancy was defined by a serum HCG value > 4.5IU/L (Unicel DXI 800 access immunoassay system) followed by a day 40 transvaginal ultrasound performed to confirm viability. Only patients with a day 40 transvaginal ultrasound confirming a viable fetus were defined as pregnant and included in analysis. Pregnancies were not followed up beyond this.

Statistical analysis

There are insufficient published human trial data to inform a power calculation. Therefore, this was a pilot trial to assess the potential treatment effect and to inform future trials. Participant data were analyzed according to the allocated group (intention to treat analysis). Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS (version 27.0, Chicago, Illinois, America). Data were reported as a number (percentages), parametric continuous variables as mean (standard deviation) and non-parametric data as median (inter-quartile range). One-way ANOVA was used for comparison among multiple groups, and the LSD method or Tamhane's T2 was used for pairwise comparisons. The paired sample t-test was used for intra-group comparison and the Chi-square test [n (%)] was used for the comparison of nominal data among multiple groups. The Bonferroni method was used for pairwise comparison among the groups. The significance level was defined as p < 0.05, and the confidence interval was set at 95 %. For continuous data effect size was assessed using Cohen's d: d = mean difference/pooled standard deviation. This was classified as: small effect (0.2–0.5) indicating a slight difference with limited clinical significance; a medium effect (0.5–0.8) indicating a perceptible clinical value of the difference and a clear intervention effect; a large effect (>0.8) indicating a significant difference and that the intervention effect has substantial clinical significance. If the 95 % confidence interval of Cohen's d included zero the effect size of the mean difference between groups is reported not significant, The OR value was calculated by dividing the ratio of the number of people with an event to the number of people without the event in one group by the ratio of the number of people with the event to the number of people without the event in the other group. An OR with 95 % confidence interval that includes 1 indicates no statistically significant difference.

Adverse event analyses

No adverse medication events were observed. There was one case of post operative infection in group 1, no adverse events in group 2, and one case of syncope in group 3 (detailed in the supplementary material).

Discussion

The authors believe that this is the first randomized clinical trial to assess TCM and acupuncture in combination with surgery and post procedure hormone therapy in the treatment of IUA. The findings of this pilot randomized trial suggest that TCM alone or in combination with acupuncture may improve outcomes from surgery, as assessed by the AFS score, uterine wall thickness, TCM syndrome score and pregnancy rates.

The three treatment regimens reduced the AFS scores at 3 months: by 1.22 for surgery/estrogen-progesterone therapy alone, 2.15 with the addition of TCM, and 2.62 with the addition of TCM and acupuncture. The reduction in AFS scores was significantly greater (p < 0.001) in group 3 than groups 2 and 1. The role of medical therapy in improving outcomes after -surgical treatment of IUA remains uncertain however it is widely used in clinical practice. Systematic reviews suggest that the recurrence of adhesions may be reduced but the live birth rate is not improved. The authors highlight the paucity of evidence at low risk of bias and the need for more high quality trials [20,21]. Published studies have reported diverse outcome measures at a range of time points, making comparisons between studies challenging. Few trials have reported live births, which is the most important outcome to patients.

The baseline and post treatment AFS scores reported here are broadly consistent with those reported in previous studies. A 2017 Cochrane review suggested the average AFS score was 5.3 in women who received no post procedural medical therapy after 3 months as compared to 2 in those offered medical therapy [21]. A 2017 prospective, randomized, controlled study evaluated the post hysteroscopic adhesiolysis use of intrauterine balloons covered with sterilized, freeze-dried amnion grafts vs. a balloon inserted without an amnion graft [22]. All patients received postoperative hormone therapy. AFS scores at 3 months were significantly lower in both groups than baseline (treatment group: 10 vs. 2, p < 0.001; placebo group: 10 vs. 4, p < 0.001), and significantly lower in the treatment group (p = 0.032). The reductions in AFS were slightly larger than those in the trial reported here, with higher baseline scores suggesting more severe disease. The pregnancy rate at 3 months was not significantly different between the two groups (23 % and 18 %, p = 0.58). A randomized trial of 91 women who underwent hysteroscopic adhesiolysis for moderate or severe IUA and received either a 30-day placement of a balloon stent or an intrauterine device (IUD) followed by estrogen/progesterone therapy. The authors reported reductions in AFS scores from baseline to the subsequent cycle second-look hysteroscopy at device removal of 8.0 to 1.8, (a reduction of 6.3) in those treated with the balloon, as compared to 7.7 to 2.0 (a reduction of 5.6) with the IUD [23]. There were no further AFS score assessments and the large fall in score may relate to the assessment immediately at balloon removal. Although previous work suggests AFS scores correlate with symptoms and pregnancy rates a quantitative relationship defining a significant improvement in the score to reflect symptoms has not been described.

Limited previous studies have supported the potential role of TCM therapy in improving outcomes after TCRA. A previous randomized trial reported that TCM (using the herb decoction) improved AFS scores (pre-treatment 8.86 to 3.19 post treatment), compared to estrogen-progesterone therapy alone (pre-treatment 8.42 to 4.40 post treatment) [24]. Postoperatively, patients in the TCM group demonstrated a significant reduction in AFS (p < 0.001). In a second randomized trial participants treated with TCRA and a post procedural IUD were randomized to receive intra-uterine hormonal therapy supplemented by intravenous and oral TCM, or hyaluronic acid sodium. The authors reported significant differences in intrauterine re-adhesions (46 % vs. 77 %, p = 0.044) but not pregnancy (46 % vs. 37 %, p = 0.628) [25].

Lab data provides several mechanisms by which estrogen therapy may reduce adhesions, including mitigating transforming growth factor b1 (TGF-b1), a pro-inflammation and fibrosis inducing cytokine, via the Wnt/catenin signaling pathway [26]. There is biochemical data to support the mechanism by which TCM could affect the natural history of post procedure recovery. The process of fibrosis involves multiple inflammatory mediators, including TGF-β1, PI3K-AKT, NF-κB, Wnt/β-catenin and LIF/STAT3 [27]. Research suggests TCM treatment with Bushenhuoxue herbs suppresses endometrial stromal cell proliferation and fibrosis via the PI3K-AKT pathway, inhibiting IUA progression [12]. Small mammal models also demonstrated Tiaoshen Tongluo to modulate the TGF-β1/Smad pathway and reduce fibrosis [10]. Similarly, animal work described electrical acupuncture (EA) as reversing epithelial-mesenchymal transition related protein and reducing fibrosis via Wnt/β-catenin inhibition [28].

Endometrial thickness at 3-months was significantly greater in the combined surgery/estrogen-progesterone/TCM/acupuncture group than in the surgery/estrogen-progesterone/TCM or surgery/estrogen-progesterone groups, suggesting a potential additive effect of acupuncture on TCM. A 2024 comprehensive and analytical review article describes TCM and acupuncture as reducing pro-inflammatory mediators and lowering uterine arterial resistance, improving blood flow and inhibiting fibrosis, thereby promoting endometrial repair [29]. A small mammal study suggested TCM (Bushen Huoxue decoction) may have improve post-surgical endometrial thickness [30].

The 6-month pregnancy rate (3 months post medical therapy) was significantly higher in the combined surgery/estrogen-progesterone/TCM/acupuncture group (42.5 %) than in the other two groups. However, these pregnancies were not followed until birth therefore the number of viable births is unknown. A retrospective, cross-sectional study involving 357 patients with IUAs who underwent hysteroscopic adhesiolysis; 93 % (332 patients) women were followed for an average 27 ± 9 months with a 48.2 % conception rate, an average time to conception of 9.7 ± 3.7 months, a miscarriage rate of 9.4 %, and a live birth rate 85.6 % [31]. A randomized trial including 60 patients with recurrent implantation failure due to IUA who received in vitro fertilization were divided into an observation group (low-dose estrogen, 30 cases) and an acupuncture group (“Tong Yuan Acupuncture” combined with milli-fire-needling, 37 cases) for 3 cycles. After four cycles pregnancy rates were higher in the acupuncture group (16.7 % vs. 40 %, p = 0.045) however live births were not reported [32].

After three treatment cycles the TCM syndrome scores of all patients significantly decreased. During the development of IUA fibroblasts proliferate in the damaged endometrium hindering the formation of new blood vessels and leading to impaired blood circulation, ischemia, and scarring, which is consistent with the essence of TCM blood stasis syndrome [25].

The TCM syndrome scoring scale used in this study was derived in accordance with the standards related to TCM syndromes of intrauterine adhesions (IUA) in Guiding Principles for Clinical Research of New Chinese Medicines organized and published by the Ministry of Health of the People's Republic of China, and the second edition of the textbook Chinese Medicine Gynecology. This scale has been used to in previous studies to assess the clinical efficacy of treatments for IUA [19,33] but has not undergone formal validation against other disease severity scores. Each syndrome element was quantitatively graded [14,15]. Similarly, derived scales have been used in clinical trials on cardiac and neurological diseases [34,35]. Similar scoring systems to that used in the trial reported here been used in other IUA treatment research but may emphasize different score components. Examples include studies by You Zhiheng evaluating IUA symptoms pre and post treatment with hormone or hormone/Chinese traditional medicine combined [18], and by Lan R evaluating the clinical efficacy of acupuncture in patients undergoing IVF-ET [36]. In the study reported here the authors employed the TCM score in order to evaluate the efficacy of the treatment from a Chinese as well as Western Medical view point. The TCM score consists mainly of unpleasant symptoms and can thus be argued be involve patient centered outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, it is a pilot study with a small number of participants, therefore these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis generating. Recruitment was from a single center with a homogeneous population, therefore the finding cannot be generalized to other populations. The study population was also required to meet both the WM and TCM diagnostic criteria study entry and thus these findings are not applicable outside this population. Specifically, 57 patients met TCM but not WM diagnostic criteria, 32 patients met the WM but not TCM diagnostic criteria and 89 screened patients met neither the WM nor TCM diagnostic criteria. The primary outcome was a commonly used measure that predicts symptoms, however pregnancy rates are the most important outcome for most women. The follow up was short at 3 months, therefore the findings may not apply in the longer term. An acupuncture only arm – without TCM – may also improve understanding of the contribution each therapy makes to outcomes but significantly increases complexity and required participant numbers while increasing the probability of chance findings. Similarly, a placebo group with no post operative medical therapy would provide useful data on efficacy however this was not the standard of care in the institution where the trial was performed. Finally, because most medical therapies was self-delivered by patients at home compliance is not known. The Chinese population has a belief in TCM and acupuncture. While the research team offered placebo TCM and sham acupuncture some participants may have sufficient knowledge of these therapies to recognize this consequently altering behavior and potentially influencing assessments. The TCM score used here has not been externally validated and this is a significant trial limitation. The TCM score is subjective and so particularly at risk of bias. In order to reduce this risk we used blinded doctors trained in TCM and in the TCM scoring with a third evaluator asked to adjudicate if there was disagreement as described in the methods section.

Conclusion

This pilot randomized, controlled trial provides provisional data that TCM and acupuncture may improve the symptom control and pregnancy rates when added to estrogen-progesterone therapy for women with IUA. TCM and acupuncture were well tolerated and safe. The trial size was too small to guide clinical decisions and more work is needed.
