Serological profiling of hantavirus infections in chronic kidney disease of unknown etiology non-endemic regions of Sri Lanka
Authors: Pavani Senarathne, Nipun Rathnayake, Rydhnieya Vijeyakumaran, Devinda Muthusinghe, Amanda Fonseka, Videesha Bandara, Sithumini Lokupathirage, Nilanthi Dissanayake, Chandika Gamage, Shuzo Urata, Kumiko Yoshimatsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh seropositivity (40–60%) to hantaviruses among patients with chronic kidney disease of unknown etiology (CKDu) and relatively lower seropositivity (15–20%) among healthy individuals in CKDu endemic areas of Sri Lanka was reported, suggesting a possible link between hantavirus exposure and CKDu. However, only a few studies have been conducted in CKDu non-endemic areas. A recent study revealed the presence of two Thailand orthohantavirus (THAIV)-related viruses, Lanka virus (LNKV) and Anjozorobe virus (ANJZV) in Sri Lanka. This study was conducted in three districts that are predominantly CKDu non-endemic (Kurunegala, Matale, and Kegalle) to assess hantavirus seropositivity in the community.MethodsA total of 760 human sera were collected from 2021 to 2022. Of the 760 samples, 486 were from community controls and 274 were from renal patients. Serum samples were tested using an indirect immunofluorescent antibody assay (IFA) based on both THAIV authentic antigens and recombinant N antigen. Positive sera were further tested with LNKV recombinant glycoprotein (rGP) and serotyped with LNKV and ANJZV recombinant Gn antigens.ResultsSeropositivity among the community participants of Kegalle, Kurunegala, and Matale districts was 8.6%, 10.5%, and 6.4%, respectively, while among renal patients, the rates were 4.3%, 17.2%, and 10%, respectively. Although no significant difference was observed between the community controls and renal patients, significantly higher seroprevalence in males (12.4%) than in females (5.9%) was observed. Of the 45 seropositive sera to rGP, 21 were serotyped as LNKV infection, and 2 were serotyped as ANJZV infection.ConclusionsA total seropositivity in community controls in this study (8.8%) was lower than previously reported. These observations suggest that LNKV exposure occurs in CKDu non-endemic areas, but at a lower frequency than in CKDu endemic areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41182-026-00900-2.Keywords: Hantavirus, Lanka virus, Anjozorobe virus, CKDu, Sri Lanka, Seroprevalence

Introduction

Hantaviruses belong to the family of hantaviridae of the order Eliovirales, class Bunyaviricetes [1, 2]. Studies conducted between 2016 and 2020 suggested that seropositivity to hantavirus was significantly higher in chronic kidney disease of unknown etiology (CKDu) patients in the dry zone of Sri Lanka [3]. However, the relationship between hantavirus infection and CKDu has not yet been fully elucidated in Sri Lanka.

In Sri Lanka, two orthohantaviruses are reported to be circulating: Lanka virus (LNKV, Orthohantavirus lankaense) carried by Mus booduga and the other is Anjozorobe virus (ANJZV, Orthohantavirus thailandense) carried by Rattus rattus [4]. These viruses were genetically and antigenically closed to Thailand virus (THAIV, Orthohantavirus thailandense). Earliest evidence of hantavirus infection in Sri Lanka was reported by Vitarana et al. [5] Norwegian rats (Rattus norvegicus) captured in Colombo harbor shows higher antibody titers to Seoul virus (SEOV). SEOV may have spread globally through shipping by reservoir rats and arrived in Sri Lanka because so far rodent capturing in CKDu endemic areas (Giradurukotte and Polonnaruwa districts) no Norwegian rats was captured [4, 6]. Patient sera from other parts of the country serotyped by plaque reduction neutralization tests for Hantaan (HTNV) and Seoul virus (SEOV) show same titers indicating circulation of different hantavirus from HTNV and SEOV in Sri Lanka. Vitarana et al. did not use THAIV for neutralization test. On the other hand, Yoshimatsu et al. performed neutralization tests using THAIV on the antibody positives in Sri Lanka, and the results showed that THAIV-related viruses are infecting people in Sri Lanka. In addition, cross-reactivities of these sera to Puumala virus (PUUV) were also reported [7]. Study describing leptospirosis patients with high titers of anti-hantavirus IgM antibodies, particularly against PUUV antigens [8] and sero-epidemiological study revealing widespread exposure to hantaviruses antigenically related to PUUV and HTNV [9]. Clinical surveillance of suspected hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS) cases in Sri Lanka identified hantavirus IgM-positive patients with serological cross-reactivity to SEOV, HTNV, and PUUV antigens [10]. To date, despite numerous serological reports, the virus types identified in Sri Lanka by neutralization tests and genetic analysis are SEOV, LNKV and ANJZ.

To differentiate LNKV infection from ANJZV infection, a serotyping strategy was established by Muthusinghe et al., 2025 and it was reported that the most seropositive humans were exposed to LNKV [11]. This finding suggested the importance of Mus booduga as a source of infection. A retrospective study showed 40–60% of hantavirus seroprevalence among CKDu patients and 17–25% in healthy individuals in CKDu endemic areas, while the healthy individuals in CKDu non-endemic areas only showed 3% seropositivity [12]. The incidence rate of rodent infections like hantavirus is more susceptible to seasonal changes, and hantavirus outbreaks are often associated with the increase in rodent population [13, 14]. Although rainfall and climate change affect the changes in rodent populations directly, the annual population fluctuation of the reported reservoir of LNKV infection, M. booduga in Sri Lanka, has not yet been investigated.

Sri Lanka is a tropical island comprised of the wet zone (2500–5500 mm annual rainfall), intermediate zone (1750–2500 mm annual rainfall), and dry zone (below 1750 mm annual rainfall). In this study, three different agro-ecological regions in Sri Lanka with limited documented evidence of hantavirus infection were selected; Kurunegala, Matale, and Kegalle districts. In these three areas, seroprevalence to hantaviruses in community controls and renal patients was examined. A serotyping assay was carried out to differentiate LNKV and ANJZV infection.

Materials and methods

Ethical clearance

The study protocol was approved by the institutional Ethical Review Committee (ERC), Faculty of Medicine, University of Peradeniya, Sri Lanka (2020/EC/55).

Study design

This is a hospital and community-based cross-sectional study followed by an unmatched case–control study. The study was conducted in the Kegalle, Matale, and Kurunegala districts of Sri Lanka, which are non-endemic for CKDu (Fig. 1). According to the Screening Guidelines Chronic Kidney Disease Sri Lanka in 2017 by Epidemiology Unit Ministry of Health [15] identified high risk geographic areas for CKDu in Sri Lanka and according to the published data o Polpithigama and Giribawa DS in Kurunegala district reported as high-risk geographic areas for CKDu. Sample collection

Detailed sample size calculation data are included in a supplementary data file.

Hospital setting

Sample collection was conducted between August 2021 and August 2022. Clinically diagnosed renal patients aged ≥ 18 years residing in the Kegalle, Matale, and Kurunegala districts and attending renal clinics at the nephrology and transplantation unit, General Hospital Kandy (GHK), renal clinics at the Teaching Hospital Peradeniya (THP), and the regional clinics organised by Teaching Hospital Kurunegala (THK), respectively, were recruited. Renal patients were selected based on their diagnosis of kidney damage, confirmed by a consultant nephrologist using either the Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) level, proteinuria level, or serum creatinine level according to the WHO standard guidelines [16].

Community setting

For the community control group, demographic data and serum sample collection were conducted randomly at selected Divisional Secretariats (DS) of selected study areas. Aranayake and Rambukkana DS were selected as community control sample collection sites for Kegalle District. Yatawatta DS was selected as the community control sample collection sites in Matale district, while Alawwa, Ibbagamuwa, Kobeigane and Polgahawela DS were selected as community control sample collection sites for Kurunegala district (Fig. 1). Individuals aged > 18 years, without clinically diagnosed renal disease and normal serum creatinine levels (0.5–1.2 mg/dL) who consented to participate were included in the study. Community controls were selected through divisional secretariat-level screening and were included based on normal serum creatinine levels at the time of sampling, in conjunction with normal blood pressure and absence of albuminuria on urine testing, following clinical evaluation by a consultant nephrologist.

Data and blood sample collection

Data and blood samples were obtained from both renal patients and the community controls. Basic demographic data (age, gender, address, occupation etc.), past medical history, agricultural involvement, and renal clinical information from renal patients were obtained by an interviewer administrated questionnaire. Three millilitres of blood were collected from each participant into plain sterile tubes, without anti-coagulant, and samples were then transported in insulated coolers to the Department of Microbiology, Faculty of Medicine, University of Peradeniya within 2–4 h. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 800 g for 5–10 min, and aliquoted into two 1.8 mL sterile Cryotube vials (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) labelled with the respective laboratory reference number and was then stored at −20 °C until further analysis.

Detection of anti-hantavirus antibodies by indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA)

IgG antibodies were detected using an indirect immunofluorescence antibody (IFA) assay based on THAIV-infected and THAIV recombinant nucleocapsid protein (rNP)-expressing Vero E6 cell antigens, as described previously [17]. Briefly, sera were tested on antigen-expressing Vero E6 cells and bound antibodies were detected using Alexa Fluor™ 488-conjugated Protein A (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). A serum sample showing a clear, specific cytoplasmic fluorescence pattern was considered positive, while absence of specific fluorescence was considered negative. Serum from a Lanka virus genome positive patient serum from Sri Lanka [5] and a healthy individual were included as positive and negative controls, respectively. All slides were independently evaluated by two examiners, and positive results were reconfirmed by a third examiner to minimize inter-observer variability.

Serotyping based on antigenicity of glycoprotein Gn of Lanka virus and Anjozorobe virus

Serotyping was performed as described previously [11]. Briefly, a secondary screening IFA with recombinant glycoproteins (rGPs) of LNKV and ANJZV expressing BHK/T7-9 cells were performed for all THAIV antigen IFA positive human sera, at a serum dilution of 1:200 in PBS. Serum showing a clear immunofluorescence pattern in the cell cytoplasm was considered positive by the IFA and recruited to the serotyping IFA assay.

Serum samples were diluted in twofold serial dilution in 1 × PBS and 20 µl of each diluted sample was added separately into LNKV and ANJZV–rGn antigen slides prepared and incubate overnight at 4º C followed by previously described IFA procedure and end-point titer (EP) against both rGn antigens were determined for each serum sample.

Using the EP data obtained by Gn–IFA assay, the ratio of EP against Lanka virus rGn antigen (EP LNKV) over EP against ANJZV virus rGn antigen was determined for all the tested sera. Samples showing four times or higher titer to LNKV antigen than to ANJZV antigen were considered as suspected Lanka virus infection. Conversely, samples showing four times or higher titer against the ANJZV antigen than the LNKV virus antigen were considered as suspected ANJZV virus infection. Samples with an antibody titer difference less than 2 times were considered as "inconclusive".

Statistical analysis

Data obtained from the structured questionnaire and results obtained from laboratory tests were inserted to Microsoft Excel®. All statistical analyses were conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 23 (IBM SPSS Statistics, USA) and the online tool Med Calc®. Exposure to hantavirus infection and possible risk factors were evaluated by 2 × 2 contingency tables applying Pearson chi-square (χ2) statistic method. Comparisons of the proportions of the hantavirus exposed and non-exposed cohorts were performed by Med Calc®. The p value lower 0.05 (p ≤ 0.05) was considered as statistically significant. Odds ratios (OR) were calculated in the exposed group when compared with the non-exposed group to hantavirus infection and its accompanying 95% confidence interval (CI).

Results

We collected a total of 760 human serum samples from community controls (486) and renal patients (274) residing in selected sample collection areas to identify the distribution of hantavirus seropositivity among renal patients and community controls (Table 2). The samples were analyzed for seroreactivity to THAIV antigens. Serum specimens positive to both THAIV-infected Vero cell antigen and THAIV rNP expressing Vero cell antigen were categorized as seropositive. As shown in Table 2, both the highest seropositivity rate (10.5%) among community controls and the highest seropositivity rate (17.2%) among renal patients were identified in the Kurunegala district. Even though the Matale community controls had the lowest seropositivity of 6.4%, seropositivity among renal patients was as high as 10.0%. Kegalle district has the lowest seropositivity of 4.3% among renal patients. District-level seropositivity was highest in Kurunegala (10.5% in community controls; 17.2% in renal patients) and lowest among renal patients in Kegalle (4.4%), with no significant difference between districts (χ2 = 2.59, p = 0.27). When comparing overall renal patients (10.6%, 29/274) with the community controls (8.8%, 46/486), the difference in seropositivity was not statistically significant (χ2 = 0.14, p = 0.71).

Serum specimens positive to both THAIV infected and recombinant NP antigen-based IFAs were further examined with rGP antigens followed by serotyping using rGn antigens of LNKV virus and ANJZV. Out of the total study participants, only 72/760 (9.4%) tested positive for both THAIV-infected and rNP THAIV expressing IFA antigens, while out of 72 positives only 45 were reactive to LNKV GP antigen in IFA. Subgroup analysis identified male renal patients in Kurunegala as the highest risk group (21.7%, 13/60), demonstrating a significantly higher positivity compared to all other participants (8.2%, 62/760) (χ2 = 8.81, p = 0.0031).

Discussion

The current study aimed to reveal the presence of anti-hantavirus antibodies in clinically diagnosed renal patients and healthy individuals residing in predominantly CKDu non-endemic areas of Sri Lanka. According to the published literature from Sri Lanka, there is a possible association between exposure to hantavirus and being a CKDu patient [12, 17].

Reports of hantavirus infections in Sri Lanka show diverse clinical manifestations ranging from mild febrile illness to renal and pulmonary involvement [18, 19]. Hantavirus genome was detected in febrile patient sera from Polonnaruwa, but patients showed no specific clinical symptoms [11]. These hantavirus reports suggest the possibility of the occurrence of disease spectrum from asymptomatic or mild febrile illness to more severe renal or pulmonary involvement and the need for further pathogenicity studies.

The hantavirus seropositivity in healthy communities in the selected districts was 8.8%. This is higher than the rates reported in several Asian countries. Studies from Thailand have reported seroprevalence ranging from 0.77% in febrile patients in Surin province [20], and rural communities of Vietnam documented rate of 1.1% [21, 22]. In Indonesia, hantavirus prevalence was detected among rodents in Tidung and Panggang Islands, but no hantavirus antibodies were found in residents of these islands [22]. Hantavirus seropositivity in Sri Lanka exceeds the average Asian country figures, suggesting heightened community-level exposure [9, 17]. The reasons for high hantavirus exposure among humans are still unknown. Higher hantavirus exposure may be associated with agricultural work or crop storage practices that promote close contact with Mus booduga, the reservoir for Lanka virus, and the amount of virus shedding from reservoir rodents may be contributing factors. Further studies on the ecology and infection dynamics of the reservoir species are needed for definite conclusion.

The hantavirus seropositivity in healthy communities (8.8%) is higher than 3% (4/132) which is seropositive rate reported from male participants in CKDu non-endemic area, previously [12]. When comparing only male participants, the current study in three areas, Kegalle, Kurunegala, and Matale were 11.9%, 12.2%, and 9.25%, respectively. Data were compared only among males, because samples collected in 2010 study included male participants only. These results indicate that seroprevalence among healthy individuals varied even in CKDu non-endemic areas. In this study, higher hantavirus seropositivity was exhibited significantly in male (12.4%) than females (5.9%). Sex difference of exposure to hantaviruses were reported from Europe and Asia [23, 24]. Due to the gender difference, seropositive rates might be affected. In this study, all data were listed along with gender to avoid gender bias.

CKDu in Sri Lanka is mainly concentrated in the dry zone, particularly in Polonnaruwa, Anuradhapura, Badulla, few areas of Kurunegala and Ampara districts. The selected divisional secretariats in this study are not predominantly CKDu endemic [25]. A key strength of this study is the assessment hantavirus exposure using serological assays in community participants and renal patients residing in non-CKDu endemic regions, a population under-represented in previous Sri Lankan hantavirus research. Earlier work primarily examined CKDu cases and relationship between hantavirus exposure and CKDu; therefore, the burden and risk factors of hantavirus exposure among CKD individuals and general population remained unclear. This study fills that gap and improves understanding of hantavirus circulation outside classic CKDu picture. These clinically diagnosed renal patient cohorts consisted of CKD, glomerulonephritis (GN), Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) and clinically unclassified renal diseases. However, 95% of studied renal patients were diagnosed with CKD. Gamage et al. reported 40.6–60.0% ranges in male CKDu patients in CKDu endemic areas [12]. Sarathkumara et al. reported 25.0% seropositivity among male renal patients from Kandy [17]. Although Kandy was known as CKDu non-endemic area, severe case of hantavirus infection has been reported there [18]In the current study, seropositivity of male renal patients were 5.5%, 21.6%, and 13.5%, suggesting that exposure to hantaviruses among renal patients in these areas are obviously lower than that of CKDu endemic areas and Kandy. The elevated seropositivity among renal patients, even in predominantly non-endemic regions, supports the hypothesis that prior hantavirus infection may contribute to renal pathology or serve as a cofactor in disease progression [26–28]. Notably, seropositivity among renal patients (10.5%) was higher than in the healthy community (8.3%), although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.71). In this assay, Kurunegala exhibited the highest hantavirus seropositivity in both healthy individuals (10.5%) and renal patients (17.2%), suggesting more intense virus circulation or higher rodent exposure in this area.

We were able to identify the circulation of LNKV in all three districts, while two ANJZV positives were only detected in Kegalle district. However, 22 sera could not be assigned to either LNKV or ANJZV, suggesting the possible circulation of additional hantavirus serotypes in the selected districts, decline in antibody titters over time following infection and the high cross-reactivity between LNKV and ANJZV. These findings highlight the need for future studies integrated with IgM antibody detection with molecular approaches, including PCR and genomic sequencing of acute-phase samples, to enable identification of active infections and definitive characterization of circulating hantavirus genotypes. The obtained results suggest that the distribution of ANJZV in selected agro-ecological regions are comparatively lower than LNKV distribution. This is the first report of the circulation of these hantavirus serotypes in CKDu–non-endemic regions in the country.

The three districts studied here, belonging to different agro-ecological regions, are composed of unique climate conditions, soil structure, and land use for different cultivations. Hantavirus incidences have a significant relationship with climate conditions, because favorable climate conditions will increase the rodent population drastically which will eventually cause increased rodent contact with humans causing higher hantavirus seropositivity (13). These unique conditions of different agro-ecological regions might have an impact on population of wild small mammals. To describe the host ecology and distribution of rodent population in different agro-ecological regions, rodent surveys are needed in future.

This study has several limitations. The study was conducted in only three CKDu non-endemic districts thus not representing landscape of hantavirus infection in Sri Lanka. Proportion of positive sera could not be serotyped as either LNKV or ANJZV, suggesting possible antibody decreasing or the presence of uncharacterized hantaviruses, but this could not be resolved without genomic analysis. The unavailability of concurrent rodent surveillance data prevents direct linkage between human exposure and reservoir host dynamics in selected districts. In future, studies on the use of both Gn and Gc glycoproteins in recombinant antigen to enhance the sensitivity and specificity of hantavirus serodiagnosis assays in Sri Lanka will be carried out.

Conclusion

This study reveals significant hantavirus exposure among both healthy individuals and renal patients in Sri Lanka’s predominantly CKDu non-endemic districts, but lower than previously reported in CKDu endemic areas. The serological findings reveal the first definitive serotyping evidence of the LNKV and ANJZV exposure among study districts. Island-wide serological and rodent surveillance studies are essential to assess the true prevalence of hantavirus exposure among the public and to identify circulating hantavirus species and their reservoir host.
