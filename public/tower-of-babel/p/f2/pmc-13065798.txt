A 0.1° monthly potential evapotranspiration dataset based on the optimal models over global vegetation zones
Authors: Zaoying Bi, Shanlei Sun, Qianrong Ma, Yi Liu, Xiaoyuan Li, Jinjian Li, Yibo Liu, Yang Zhou, Botao Zhou, Haishan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChoosing effective models for accurately estimating potential evapotranspiration (PET) is essential in various fields, including climatology, ecology, hydrology, and agronomy. However, many currently dominant PET products rely on multiple models with default parameters, which can introduce uncertainties into PET-related research. To address this issue, we derived the parameters for five widely used PET models using observations from 124 eddy covariance sites worldwide. By comparing their performances across various biomes, we identified the calibrated Priestley-Taylor and Milly-Dunne models as the optimal choices, capable of being applied effectively beyond their original observation sites. Using these optimal models, along with four widely-used meteorological datasets and annual land use and land cover data, we generated a monthly PET dataset at a 0.1° resolution during 1992–2022 across global vegetation zones. Finally, we compared the new dataset with the Global Land Evaporation Amsterdam Model V4.2a PET, including daily comparisons for each biome and annual trend comparisons during 1992–2022. This new PET dataset serves as an alternative resource for conducting PET-related research.Subject terms: Hydrology, Atmospheric science, Hydrology

Background & Summary

Potential evapotranspiration (PET) represents the maximum possible amount of water that can be transferred to the atmosphere from a specific land cover (e.g., soil and water) when the surface has an adequate water supply, meaning there is no water stress1. Initially proposed by Thornthwaite in 19482, PET and its derivative indices (e.g., aridity and drought indices) have been widely used in a variety of scientific disciplines (e.g., climatology, ecology, hydrology, and agronomy) and practical applications, especially for agricultural and natural resource management, where PET is applied at scales from farm to regional and global1,3–8. In summary, estimating PET is crucial for multiple applications, particularly in measuring and predicting climate change (including the trend direction and magnitude) and its impacts on the terrestrial biosphere.

Numerous models for estimating PET have been developed, serving as essential tools for this purpose9–12. These models can be categorized into four classifications based on their theoretical foundations, assumptions, and requirements for input data: mass-transfer-based (e.g., Dalton-type models)12, temperature-based (e.g., the Thornthwaite model)2, radiation-based (e.g., the Turc and Hargreaves models)13, and combination ones (e.g., Penman-Monteith (PM) models)1,14,15. To date, these models have been extensively utilized in various regions and on a global scale across different research fields and practical applications, including hydrological simulations, irrigation scheduling, estimating crop water requirements, modeling crop stress, and monitoring and predicting drought conditions6–8,11. It is important to note that when comparing the magnitudes and changes of PET estimates to reference data—such as measured pan evaporation (Epan), weighing lysimeter measurements, and eddy covariance (EC) observations—the performance of these models can vary significantly, which is closely linked to their theoretical concepts and assumptions11,16–20. For instance, Li et al. evaluated different PET models across various climate zones in China by comparing them to Epan21. Their findings revealed substantial differences in the PET estimates, ranging from 349.6 mm for the Kuzmin model to 1902.6 mm for the Doorenbos-Pruitt model, with the PM model performing the best. By comparing ten PET models across ten Chinese drainage basins, Xie and Wang reported that the Hamon PET model performed best in the Pearl River, Huaihe River, Yangtze River, and Yellow River basins, while the FAO-56 PM model outperformed others in the remaining basins22. This highlights the uncertainty in PET estimates and its potential impact on PET-related studies6,23–27. Milly and Dunne found that three widely used models—the FAO-56 PM, the open-water PM, and the Shuttleworth-Wallace models—tended to overestimate PET changes25. Consequently, the historical and projected continental drying trends indicated by drought indices were also affected. In summary, these studies demonstrate that the choice of PET models is crucial for accurately estimating PET and for conducting PET-related research at various temporal and spatial scales.

As awareness of PET has grown, various regional and global PET datasets have been developed, employing different models. The predominant PET products currently available include earlier versions (e.g., Version 3.7) of the Global Land Evaporation Amsterdam Model (GLEAM)28, which is based on the Priestley-Taylor (PT) model, as well as the GLEAM Version 4.229, the TerraClimate dataset30, the Global-AI_PET databases (Versions 1–3)31, hPET32, and PISCOeo_pm33, all of which rely on the FAO-56 PM model. While these datasets offer convenience for PET-related studies, their accuracy is largely dependent on the performance of the PT and FAO-56 PM models. Research has indicated that the PT model performs well in simulating PET in humid regions; however, its performance declines in other areas21,34–36. This issue primarily arises because the default parameter of 1.26 used in the PT model may not be universally applicable. The FAO-56 PM model, which several organizations recommend as a standard method for estimating PET, also has inherent limitations6,19. It employs fixed and uniform parameters for canopy height (h) and minimum surface resistance (rcmin) at each location1. These parameters often do not reflect reality, as different land cover types (such as vegetated areas, barren land, or land covered with snow/ice) can have varying values for h and rcmin. For instance, in forests, the values of h can range from 3 m to 40 m37. Using a fixed h can introduce uncertainties into the estimated aerodynamic resistance (raH) and consequently affect PET estimates. Maes et al. recalibrated the rcmin values of the FAO-56 PM model using EC measurements from the FLUXNET2015 database19. They found that the optimal rcmin values for 11 biomes differed significantly from the default value of 70 s m−1. These findings suggest that existing PET products based on the PT and FAO-56 PM models can be problematic when default parameters are used, particularly in regions or biomes with values that deviate significantly from these defaults. As a result, uncertainties may be introduced into PET-related studies when relying on these datasets.

In summary, it is essential to carefully consider the significance of selecting PET models and their parameters, as well as the issues associated with the currently dominant PET products used in PET-related studies. There is an urgent need to develop a new global PET dataset based on the most effective PET models tailored for different regions, maximizing performance among the selected models. Specifically, our objectives are: (1) to recalibrate several of the most commonly used PET models using EC observations to determine key parameters for each biome and identify the optimal models, (2) to create a monthly PET product during 1992–2022 over global vegetation zones (GVZ) using the identified optimal PET models and various input data, and (3) to examine the spatiotemporal changes in PET across the globe from 1992 to 2022. This new PET dataset aims to provide accurate PET estimates for PET-related studies.

Methods

EC datasets for calibration and validation

To calibrate and validate the selected PET models and make full use of the available EC observations, we collected six datasets with half-hourly or hourly measurements, including FLUXNET2015 Tier-2 (http://fluxnet.fluxdata.org/data/fluxnet2015-dataset/)37, LaThuile (https://fluxnet.org/data/la-thuile-dataset/), AsiaFlux (http://asiaflux.net/?page_id = 23), OzFlux (https://data.ozflux.org.au/portal/home.jspx), ICOS (https://meta.icos-cp.eu/collections/gdINRHdRH6xknqoLsIU1FOZ4)38, and AmeriFlux (https://ameriflux.lbl.gov/data/download-data/)39. Before utilizing these datasets, several key procedures are necessary to identify the PET observations (i.e., ET observations without water constraints) and their corresponding input data for the PET models. Therefore, we followed the processing methods outlined by Maes et al.19, and the detailed steps are presented below.

First, we screened the data quality control for each dataset. Basic measurements are essential for calibrating and validating the selected models. These include latent heat (LE), sensible heat (H), soil heat flux (G), net radiation fluxes (Rn), wind speed (U), air temperature, relative humidity (Rh), actual vapor pressure (ea), and atmospheric water concentration, as well as vapor pressure deficit (VPD). Consequently, the EC sites that lacked any of these basic measurements were not excluded from further analyses. To address potential issues with surface energy imbalance that may negatively affect our results, we utilized corrected half-hourly or hourly observations of LE and H37. Based on the quality flags provided by the five datasets, we retained the high-quality, gap-filled records of the major heat fluxes (LE, H, and G). If the flags for Rn observations were missed, we used the flags of downward shortwave radiation (Rds) in their place. Additionally, considering the effects of interception loss and condensation on the accuracy of H and LE measurements40, we masked any records with negative values for these measurements. Similarly, we also masked all negative values for Rn.

Second, the half-hourly or hourly records were first aggregated into daytime values. To ensure quality, only days with more than 70% of the basic data and no rainfall occurring from midnight to sunset were included. Additionally, only sites with 80 or more days of data were considered for the subsequent processing steps. A minimum threshold of 5 W m−2 of incoming shortwave radiation at the top of the atmosphere was applied to identify the relevant daytime records19. Finally, the sub-daily measurements between the identified first and last (half-)hours of the daytime were combined to create daytime composites.

Third, we identified days without soil water limitations. To do this, we used an energy balance-based approach to select unstressed days. Several reasons why we did not used the soil moisture criterion included: (i) the majority of the EC sites did not have soil moisture observations, (ii) the energy balance method effectively eliminates not only days with soil water limitations but also days experiencing stress from other environmental factors, such as insect infestations, phenological leaf-out, fires, heat and atmospheric dryness stress, and nutrient deficiencies, and (iii) soil moisture deficits may not accurately represent water stress due to variations in vegetation rooting depth and potential inaccuracies in soil moisture measurements41–43. We selected the evaporative fraction (EF = LE/(LE + H)) as our energy balance criterion, assuming that in conditions without water limitations, the EF should exceed its 95th percentile44–46. Importantly, if fewer than 9 days met this criterion at a specific site, we considered the 9 days with the highest EF values as unstressed days. Here, we retained 178 sites (Fig. 1).

Spatial distribution of 11 biomes and 178 EC sites across the globe. International Geosphere Biosphere Programme (IGBP) classification system: ENF – evergreen needleleaf forest; EBF – evergreen broadleaf forest; DNF – deciduous needleleaf forest; DBF – deciduous broadleaf forest; MF – mixed forest; SHRB – shrubland; WSA – woody savannah; SAV – savannah; GRA – grassland; WET – permanent wetland; CRO – cropland.

In the last step, for meeting the specific requirements (e.g., heights for vegetation and U measurement) to run the PM model, we re-screened the data from the 178 sites and found that 54 sites did have the records for heights for vegetation and U measurement. Therefore, the remaining 124 sites were used for all the PET models. Although these 54 excluded sites cannot be used for the PM model, they can serve as independent data for validating other models (e.g., the Hargreaves and Samani (HS), Oudin (OU), PT, and Milly-Dunne (MD) models). Therefore, in the following sections, if a non-PM model is identified as the optimal model, we will use the data from these 54 sites as an independent sample to further validate the performance of the optimal model. For convenience, the data from the 124 and 54 sites are referred to as Group A and Group B, respectively. The basic information and references of these 178 sites can be found in Tables S1, S2.

Meteorological data

To generate an optimal models-based PET dataset and minimize uncertainties, this study collected four meteorological datasets, which were either monthly or hourly. These datasets include MultiSource Weather (MSWX)-Past47, TerraClimate30, the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts Reanalysis (ERA5)48, and the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) Modern Era Reanalysis for Research and Applications (MERRA-2)49. Based on the identified optimal PET models (detailed information in section of “Technical Validation”), the meteorological variables directly used by these PET models primarily include Rn, G, maximum air temperature (Tmax), minimum air temperature (Tmin), and mean air temperature (Tmean). Given the different radiation variables provided by these meteorological datasets (Table 1), various methods were employed to estimate Rn. Regarding G, it was calculated using a Tmean-based method1. The formulas used to compute Rn and G are detailed in Text S1 and S2, respectively.

Remote sensing data

In this study, we utilized the European Space Agency’s Climate Change Initiative Land Cover data from 1992 to 202250. This dataset, referred to as ESA CCI-LC, has a spatial resolution of 300 m and was chosen to represent various biomes (Table 1). To ensure consistency with the land use and land cover (LULC) types required for the optimal PET models, the CCI-LC dataset was resampled to a spatial resolution of 0.1°. Furthermore, to align with the International Geosphere-Biosphere Programme (IGBP) classification system, which was used to define the biome types for the 124 EC sites (Group A), the resampled CCI-LC dataset was reclassified according to the user’s manual50 and the classification methodology outlined by Yang et al.51. Ultimately, the updated CCI-LC dataset comprised 11 biome types: Evergreen Needleleaf Forests (ENF), Evergreen Broadleaf Forests (EBF), Deciduous Needleleaf Forests (DNF), Deciduous Broadleaf Forests (DBF), Mixed Forests (MF), Shrublands (SHRB), Woody Savannas (WSA), Savannas (SAV), Grasslands (GRA), Permanent Wetlands (WET), and Croplands (CRO), as illustrated in Fig. 1. Additionally, when computing net shortwave radiation and net longwave radiation using the MSWX-Past and TerraClimate, we also used the land surface albedo (α; https://www.glass.hku.hk/archive/ALBEDO/MIX/0.05D/) and emissivity (ε; https://www.glass.hku.hk/archive/BBE/AVHRR/0.05D/) from the Global Land Surface Satellite (GLASS)52–55.

Analysis methods

The workflow of this study mainly included four parts (Fig. 2): (i) collecting and processing various datasets (yellow colour in Fig. 2), (ii) calibrating the PET models and validating their performance (blue colour in Fig. 2), (iii) determining the optimal model for each biome and generating global monthly PET (pink colour in Fig. 2); and lastly (iv) comparing the new PET dataset against the GLEAM V4.2a PET, including a daily comparison for each biome and a comparison for annual trends during 1992–2022 (purple colour in Fig. 2).

Workflow of this study.

Model calibration

To obtain the optimal parameters (αHS, αOU, αPT, αMD, and rc for the HS, OU, PT, MD, and PM models, respectively) for the selected five PET models, this study utilized the Monte Carlo method to calibrate these parameters based on the identified PET observations at each EC site59,60. The following five steps were taken to determine the optimal parameters for these PET models: (i) establish a rough range for each parameter, defined as the default values ± (0.8 × the default values); (ii) conduct 5,000 Monte Carlo simulations, randomly sampling parameter sets from uniform distributions within the specified ranges; (iii) after each simulation, compare the estimated PET with the observed EC PET using an integrated validation metric known as the Kling-Gupta efficiency (KGE)61. This step results in 5,000 KGE values corresponding to the 5,000 parameter sets; (iv) select the mean of the parameters associated with the 10 highest KGE values as the optimal value for each site; and (v) classify the 124 EC sites (Group A) into 11 biomes according to the IGBP classification system62, and determine the best-fit parameter sets for each biome by averaging the optimal parameter values from the sites within that biome type. Through this process, the final parameter values for the 11 biomes were obtained and are presented in Table S2.

Model validation

To quantitatively evaluate the overall performance of the five selected models in simulating PET, we chose a relatively new and widely-used validation metric known as KGE. This metric effectively captures the models’ ability to simulate temporal dynamics (measured by the correlation coefficient R) as well as the volume and distribution of PET (measured by the parameters α and β, respectively). Additionally, α is defined as S/M, and β is defined as σS/σM, where σS and σM are the standard deviations of the simulated and measured PET values, respectively.

KGE was initially calculated using daily estimates derived from the optimal parameters for each site, which were obtained in step (iv) of the Monte Carlo method, along with observations from the 124 EC sites (Group A). Validation was then performed in calibration mode. To assess the transferability of the calibrated parameters from known observations to new locations, and to evaluate the robustness of the developed optimal PET models, we employed the “leave-one-out” cross-validation method63. For each biome, data from one “ungauged” observation was excluded from the optimization process based on the Monte Carlo method. In contrast, data from all other observations of the same biome type were utilized for model calibration, enabling the simulation of conditions at the ungauged location. This process was applied to all 11 biome types. Subsequently, the daily optimal PET estimates obtained through cross-validation were compared with the daily observations from the 124 EC sites (Group A) to further assess the model’s performance. Finally, the transferability of the calibrated models was further tested through an independent validation using the 54 sites from Group B, thereby evaluating their performance with data that played no role in parameter optimization.

Generation of the 0.1o global monthly PET (1992–2022) and related analyses

The global PET was calculated for the period from 1992 to 2022, primarily due to the short time frame of the ESA CCI-LC products. Before running the optimal PET models, both the meteorological data and the ESA CCI-LC data were resampled to a spatial resolution of 0.1°. To minimize uncertainties in the meteorological datasets, we used the ensemble means of PET derived from the four different meteorological datasets. Additionally, we compared the new PET dataset with the GLEAM V4.2a PET, including daily comparisons for each biome and annual trend comparisons during 1992–2022.

Data Records

Data file path

The initial temporal domain for this PET dataset spans 1992–202264. This 0.1° dataset is accessible from the National Tibetan Plateau Data Center64 under the persistent identifiers: 10.11888/Terre.tpdc.302843 and CSTR: https://cstr.cn/18406.11.Terre.tpdc.302843.

Data file format

The PET dataset is organized by year and covers the entire GVZ. Each netCDF file has the format ****.nc, where the filename begins with the year followed by ‘monthly_pet’. For example, the PET dataset for 2019 is stored in the file named ‘2019_monthly_pet.nc’. Each file holds twelve months of data and is approximately 34 MB in size. The total size of the entire dataset, which spans from 1992 to 2022, is approximately 1 GB.

File structure

Each PET file contains four variables: time, latitude, longitude, and PET. Time is expressed in Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) and is represented numerically by the month of the year, starting from January (1 for January, 2 for February, etc.). Latitude values range from 85°N to 60°S with a horizontal resolution of 0.1°. Longitude values range from 180°W to 180°E.

Usage Notes

This dataset is part of an ongoing research effort. While the version cited in this publication64 (10.11888/Terre.tpdc.302843) is permanently available and citable, newer versions may be released in the future. Users are advised to check the repository page for the most current information.

Technical Validation

Performance of the PET models in site-calibration mode

Based on the EC observations and the Monte Carlo method, we calibrated the pivotal parameters of the selected five PET models at each site. Then, we evaluated the overall performance of these models for each biome using the mean KGE as a metric (Fig. 3a). Overall, the performance of each PET model varied across biomes, particularly for the HS and OU models, which showed significant differences. In most biomes, the mean KGE values for the HS model exceeded 0.50, with the highest performance observed in ENF and WET, where the value reached 0.70. In contrast, the OU model exhibited mean KGE values below 0.60 in most biomes, with the worst and best performances observed in WSA and WET, respectively, corresponding to mean KGE values of 0.13 and 0.67. Both the MD and PT models demonstrated mean KGE values exceeding 0.80 across all biomes. Notably, the MD model in ENF, EBF, MF, WET, and CRO and the PT model in WET and CRO showed exceptional performance, with KGE values exceeding 0.90. In general, the PM model had mean KGE values greater than 0.60 in all biomes, except for WSA. The largest mean KGE values were observed in DNF, WET, and CRO, all surpassing 0.80. Based on the mean KGE, the models that performed the best (i.e., those with the highest KGE values) varied among the biomes in site-calibration mode; specifically, the MD model excelled in ENF, EBF, DNF, MF, and CRO, while the PT model performed the best in the other biomes.

The mean KGE values of the selected PET models for each biome in (a) site-calibration mode, (b) cross-validation, and (c) final-validation modes. Error bar represents one standard deviation.

To further assess the performance of the identified optimal models in site-calibration mode, we compared their KGE values with those of other models at each site for every biome, as illustrated in Fig. 4. In ENF, EBF, DNF, MF, and CRO, the KGE values for the MD model were above 0.80 at most sites, particularly at all CRO sites, where the value exceeded 0.90 (Fig. 4a–f). For the optimal model (the PT model) representing the other biomes, its KGE values were generally above 0.70 at all sites, and exceeded 0.90 at all WET sites (Fig. 4g–l). Overall, at the site scale, the optimal models of MD and PT outperformed the other models, with only few sites exhibiting lower KGE values. In summary, the two optimal models identified in site-calibration mode demonstrated superior capability in simulating daily PET at both biome and site scales.

Performance of the optimal PET models in cross-validation mode

Compared to performance in site-calibration mode, the optimal models showed a decline in cross-validation mode for each biome, specifically the MD model for ENF, EBF, DNF, MF, and CRO, and the PT model for the other biomes (Fig. 3a,b). However, it is important to note that for each biome, the reductions in mean KGE did not exceed 0.10. Additionally, the mean KGE of the optimal models remained around or above 0.80, particularly for DNF, WET, and CRO, where values exceeded 0.90. At the site scale, KGE values in cross-validation mode were lower compared to those in site-calibration mode. Most sites experienced decreases of less than 0.20, with KGE values still above 0.70 (Fig. 5). In summary, these analyses suggest that the calibrated parameters of the two optimal models can effectively simulate daily PET and can be utilized in ungauged locations. Recognizing that the optimal models identified during site-calibration mode may differ in cross-validation mode (Figs. 3b, 5), we compared the performance of the MD and PT models with that of other models. At both biome and site scales, the KGE values for either the MD or PT models were still the highest, with the MD model demonstrating the best performance in ENF, EBF, DNF, MF, and CRO, while the PT model excelled in the remaining biomes.

Performance of the optimal PET models in final-validation mode

To determine the final parameters for each biome, we used the method of averaging calibrated parameter values across all sites (Table S3). This required us to reassess the optimal PET models for simulating daily PET with these final parameters. Figure 3c shows the mean KGE values for the selected PET models using the final parameters in each biome. For ENF, EBF, DNF, MF, and CRO, the optimal model is the MD model, while the PT model is optimal for the other biomes. Both the two optimal models achieved KGE values around or above 0.80, indicating good performance in simulating daily PET with the final parameters, despite a decline in performance compared to the site-calibration mode. At the majority of sites for each biome, the KGE values of the optimal models in final-validation mode decreased. However, both models still demonstrated a strong ability to simulate daily PET, with KGE values greater than 0.70 (Fig. 6). In summary, the final parameters can be effectively used for simulating daily PET at both the site and biome scales. Additionally, based on the KGE values in final-validation mode, the MD and PT models continued to outperform the other models at both the biome and site scales (Figs. 3c, 6). Specifically, the MD model was optimal for ENF, EBF, DNF, MF, and CRO, while the PT model was optimal for the remaining biomes when using the final parameters.

Comparison with GLEAM PET

After creating the global PET datasets based on the optimal models, we compared our new dataset with the GLEAM 4.2a PET dataset (https://www.gleam.eu/)29, which is one of the most widely used in hydrology and ecology65,66. Notably, since the MSWX-Past is the primary meteorological input for generating the GLEAM PET, we utilized our dataset based on the MSWX-Past data (referred as PET-MSWX) for the comparisons. First, we conducted the comparison on a daily scale using the identified PET data from all 178 EC sites. As shown in Fig. 8, the mean KGE values for PET-MSWX were above 0.50 in most biomes, with the highest values exceeding 0.65 in WET and CRO. In contrast, the GLEAM data generally corresponded to mean KGE values around or below 0.50. In ENF and DNF, GLEAM performed better, but PET-MSWX consistently outperformed GLEAM in the remaining biomes, especially for MF, SHRB, SAV, GRA, and CRO with mean KGE differences (i.e., PET-MSWX minus GLEAM) exceeding 0.12. Furthermore, we compared the KGE values between PET-MSWX and GLEAM at each site for each biome, as illustrated in Fig. 9. Overall, PET-MSWX exhibited KGE values above 0.50 at more than half of the sites across each biome. Particularly for CRO and WET, over 61% of sites had KGE values above 0.50. With the exception of ENF and DNF, PET-MSWX demonstrated higher KGE values than GLEAM at over half of the sites in the remaining biomes. In summary, our dataset proved to be highly effective in simulating daily PET at both biome and site scales, generally outperforming GLEAM.

Scatterplots for KGE values of PET-MSWX against GLEAM PET. (a–e) Comparisons in ENF, EBF, DNF, MF, and CRO, where PET-MSWX was generated based on the optimal model of MD. (f–k) Comparisons in DBF, SHRB, WSA, SAV, GRA, and WET, where PET-MSWX was generated based on the optimal model of PT.

Second, the annual trends of PET-MSWX and GLEAM PET from 1992 to 2022 were compared (Fig. 10). 20% of GVZ experienced decreases in annual PET-MSWX, primarily in the Huai River Basin of China, the southern Indian Subcontinent, Southeast Asia, northeastern South America, and parts of West Asia and Africa, while 80% showed increases. Larger (<−1.50 mm yr−1) and significant (p < 0.05) decreases were mainly observed in northeastern South America, southern India, and southeastern Southeast Asia, which accounted for 7% of GVZ. Conversely, regions showing increased PET included 56% of GVZ with significant trends (p < 0.05), concentrated in areas north of 40°N, Equatorial Africa, northern and southeastern South America (excluding northeastern regions), and parts of southern China. These areas generally demonstrated upward trends exceeding 2.00 mm yr−1. Overall, GLEAM showed a similar spatial distribution, with 86% of GVZ indicating increases (51% with p < 0.05) and 24% showing decreases (4% with p < 0.05). It is noteworthy that certain regions, such as the northern Indian Subcontinent and parts of Africa, displayed opposing trends in annual PET between PET-MSWX and GLEAM. This discrepancy arose mainly from differences in the estimations of Rn – G and the insensitivity of the PT and MD models to other meteorological variables, including temperature, wind speed, and vapor pressure deficit. An examination of the annual trends of Rn – G used by PET-MSWX and GLEAM (Fig. S1) revealed clear differences between the two estimations. These differences in Rn – G trends can be attributed to various datasets used (e.g., land surface albedo) and the formulas adopted to estimate different energy components (e.g., net longwave radiation and G). The insensitivity of PT and MD models to other meteorological variables might obscure the dominant influences of these variables on changes in PET. For instance, in regions where PET-MSWX trends were positive and GLEAM PET trends were negative, a decline in wind speed should be responsible for decreases in GLEAM PET (Fig. S2). Conversely, in regions where PET-MSWX trends were negative while GLEAM PET trends were positive, increases in wind speed and vapor pressure deficit determined the increase in GLEAM PET. This analysis illustrates that our data can replicate an annual PET trend that is generally aligned with GLEAM. However, caution should be exercised when analyzing PET trends in regions where non-radiation meteorological variables primarily influence changes in PET.

Considering the importance of selecting appropriate PET models and parameters for accurately estimating PET, we calibrated five widely used models and identified the optimal choices for each biome based on EC observations. These optimal models were then used to develop a new global PET dataset incorporating various inputs. Our dataset offers two significant advantages over existing global PET datasets, such as GLEAM v3.728, TerraClimate dataset30, the Global-AI_PET databases (Versions 1–3)31, hPET32, and PISCOeo_pm33. First, our dataset provides more accurate PET estimates for each biome (as shown in Figs. 3–6), which could help reduce uncertainties in PET-related studies. Second, it incorporates realistic land surface information, accounting for spatial variations in biomes and time-varying LULC datasets, resulting in more realistic PET estimates. For instance, numerous studies have demonstrated that land use and land cover change (LULCC) can significantly affect both ET and PET processes by altering the physiological characteristics of vegetation7,67–70. This highlights the importance of considering the effects of LULCC on PET.

In view of these advantages, our global PET dataset can be applied across various disciplines. First, PET is a crucial variable that significantly impacts the performance of hydrological models and the accuracy of estimated agricultural irrigation water71–74. Improved accuracy in PET estimation enhances the simulation accuracy of hydrological models and helps in conserving agricultural irrigation water. Second, PET serves as a key indicator of “atmospheric evaporative demand” and is frequently utilized to calculate various drought and aridity indices75–77. More precise PET estimates will aid in accurately computing these indices and understanding the spatiotemporal changes in drought. Previous studies have mainly concentrated on the impacts of climate change on drought; however, LULCC also influences PET7,67. Incorporating a time-varying LULCC dataset as a necessary input, our PET dataset provides a valuable opportunity to investigate the mechanisms of drought from the perspective of LULCC. For example, this dataset can be used to compute drought indices, allowing for the separation of the impacts of climate change and LULCC on drought evolution. Lastly, the new PET dataset is essential for understanding biodiversity responses to local environmental changes, such as LULCC. PET is generally a reliable measure of how climatic energy limits organisms and influences primary productivity, thereby affecting food availability78–80.

However, uncertainties remain within this dataset. First, the optimal PET models identified for each biome did not take into account the physiological effects of elevated CO2 on vegetation. Elevated CO2 can influence ET and PET by reducing plant stomatal conductance, which suggests that neglecting this factor may lead to overestimations in our PET estimates. Second, uncertainties may arise from the selection of unstressed days used to derive observed PET values, the non-closure of the energy balance at the EC system level, and the gap-filling methods employed (e.g., the marginal distribution sampling (MSD) method)80. Third, the calibrated parameters for these models could pose a significant challenge for their global application81,82. In this study, we made extensive efforts to gather data from various EC observation networks, covering all biomes. Overall, a series of evaluations indicate that the optimal model with the calibrated parameters performs well in simulating PET. However, it is important to note that the spatial distribution of these EC sites is highly uneven, with the majority located in Europe, North America, and Australia. This uneven distribution may bias the selection of the optimal model (e.g., MD for ENF, EBF, DNF, MF, but PT for other biomes) and limit its generalizability to underrepresented regions, such as the Amazon Basin and the Tibetan Plateau. Additionally, due to temporal changes in environmental (e.g., droughts and floods) and vegetation conditions, these parameters may exhibit interannual and seasonal fluctuations83,84. Therefore, neglecting the interannual and seasonal variations in calibrated parameters could compromise the accuracy of our data59,85. To determine the final parameters of the optimal PET models for each biome, we averaged the calibrated parameter values across all sites, neglecting the intra-biome variability that is closely linked to different environmental conditions, such as elevation and climate86,87. This approach may introduce uncertainty into our data, especially in regions where there are substantial differences between the actual and final parameters. In future research, if more data becomes available, we plan to reselect the optimal model and recalibrate the parameters to further enhance the accuracy of our dataset. Fourth, generating global PET data with the optimal models requires LULC and meteorological datasets, such as MSWX-Past, TerraClimate, ERA-5, and MERRA-2. Evaluations of the LULC and meteorological datasets worldwide have revealed discrepancies among them88–91. Consequently, some uncertainties in these datasets may propagate into the PET estimates.
