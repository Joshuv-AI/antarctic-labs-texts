TGFβ1-loaded extracellular matrix hydrogels promote skeletal muscle stem cell regeneration via m6A-mediated integrin–ERK signaling

Introduction

Severe soft tissue loss resulting from trauma, traffic accidents, or prolonged denervation remains a major clinical challenge [1, 2]. Despite advances in surgical interventions, these methods often fall short in fully restoring muscle function and can lead to complications at donor sites. Skeletal muscle stem cells (SkMSCs), located between the myofiber and basal lamina, are crucial for muscle repair due to their ability to proliferate and differentiate in response to injury [3, 4]. This regenerative potential positions them as key therapeutic targets for conditions such as volumetric muscle loss and age-related muscle degeneration [5, 6]. However, successful therapeutic outcomes using SkMSCs depend not only on their activation or manipulation but also on the precise modulation of the microenvironment in which they reside. This involves strategies like gene editing to restore muscle-specific proteins or the use of biomaterials to replace damaged tissues. Understanding the complex signals within the microenvironment that govern SkMSC fate is thus essential for advancing effective regenerative therapies for muscle diseases.

The extracellular matrix (ECM) plays a crucial role in regulating cell behavior, but its properties can be significantly affected by the extraction method, which may involve physical, chemical, or biological reagents [7, 8]. While various ECM-derived biomaterials, such as small intestine submucosa and urinary bladder tissue, have been explored, their use is limited by significant heterogeneity [9]. ECM-based hydrogels, however, offer a promising solution as three-dimensional (3D) scaffolds that closely mimic the native ECM microstructure [10]. Studies have shown that ECM hydrogels, when implanted into skeletal muscle, can support tissue regeneration by promoting tissue development, providing structural support, and facilitating force transmission [11, 12]. These advantages make ECM-based hydrogels a valuable material for improving muscle repair following severe trauma or injury.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that SkMSCs can be transplanted into damaged tissues, where they undergo specific biochemical signaling to regulate proliferation and differentiation during muscle regeneration [13–15]. Cytokines such as prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), TGFβ1, and oncostatin M are known to play pivotal roles in this process. Among these, TGFβ1 has been shown to be a key regulator of muscle regeneration, though the detailed mechanisms underlying its influence on muscle cell proliferation remain unclear. To fully harness the regenerative potential of SkMSCs, we investigated their behavior in synthetic skeletal muscle ECM hydrogels supplemented with TGFβ1. This approach combines the structural advantages of ECM hydrogels with the regenerative effects of TGFβ1, providing a promising strategy to optimize SkMSC function in muscle repair.

To uncover the regulatory mechanisms of SkMSCs in ECM hydrogels with TGF-β1, we reviewed existing literature and our previous studies, identifying that N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methylation may play a key role. m6A modification has been shown to regulate various cellular processes such as mRNA stability, splicing, and translation [16, 17]. Notably, recent research has highlighted that METTL3 enhances cell adhesion by stabilizing integrin in cells. Additionally, m6A modification of integrin 6 mRNA has been linked to the development and progression of cell [18, 19]. However, the role of integrin-mediated m6A modification in the proliferation and differentiation of SkMSCs remains unexplored. This gap in knowledge presents a unique opportunity to investigate the potential mechanisms driving SkMSC behavior and their involvement in muscle regeneration.

In this study, we employed an efficient decellularization method to fabricate an ECM hydrogel with a three-dimensional microstructure and excellent biocompatibility. This hydrogel provides a uniform mechanical signal to cells, mimicking the native muscle environment. By engineering microenvironments with biochemical cues from the matrix, we demonstrated that SkMSC proliferation and differentiation could be regulated effectively. Most notably, we uncovered a novel mechanism by which TGFβ1 influences SkMSC fate in the microenvironment. Our findings suggest that TGFβ1 regulates SkMSC behavior through the METTL3/integrin/ERK signaling axis, offering a promising therapeutic target for tissue regeneration. This research provides a deeper understanding of SkMSC regulation and opens new avenues for regenerative medicine.

Materials and methods

Animals and cell culture

Fresh skeletal muscle tissue was obtained from eight-week-old male Sprague‒Dawley rats (The Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center, Guangzhou). The animals were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a controlled 12-hour light/dark cycle, an ambient temperature of 22 ± 1 °C, and relative humidity of 50 ± 10%, with ad libitum access to standard rodent chow and water. Rat tibialis muscles were dissociated by collagenase type I (0.2% collagenase, Sigma) and then filtered through a 40 μm filter (Biosharp). After isolation, myofiber-associated cells were subjected to flow cytometry analysis (see below). Markers for CD45 (BD Pharmingen), CD11b (BD Pharmingen), anti-integrinβ1 (ab95622, Abcam) and CD34 (ab81289, Abcam) were used. After sorting, the cells were cultured in DMEM with 20% FBS and 1% chick embryo extract. Myogenic differentiation was induced using DMEM with 2% heat-inactivated horse serum.

Decellularization protocols

Fresh skeletal muscle (20 g) was obtained from 8-week-old adult Sprague‒Dawley rats. Samples were decellularized at room temperature according to the following procedures: shaking in 1% SDS for 48 h (method A), shaking in 0.2% sodium deoxycholate for 48 h followed by shaking in 1% SDS for 16 h (method B), shaking in 1% Triton X-100 for 36 h followed by shaking in 1% SDS for 36 h (method C), shaking in 0.1% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) for 6 h followed by shaking in 0.2% sodium deoxycholate for 8 h (method D), shaking in 0.2% trypsin/0.1% EDTA for 36 h followed by shaking in 0.5% Triton X-100 at for 48 h (method E), or shaking in 0.1% EDTA for 6 h followed by shaking in 1% SDS for 12 h (method F). Then, we removed the remaining nuclear materials by shaking in 9 × 106 U/l DNase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and 6 × 107 U/l RNase (Yami Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing) for 24 h. Finally, residual reagents were removed from the samples by repeated washes with deionized water and centrifugation.

Histological analysis

Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) at 4 °C for 24 h. Following fixation, tissues were rinsed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections were cut at a thickness of 5 μm. For histological staining, sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a descending ethanol series to distilled water. Staining was performed according to standard protocols and the manufacturer’s instructions. For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), sections were stained with Harris’s hematoxylin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 5 min, differentiated in 1% acid alcohol, blued in Scott’s tap water substitute, and counterstained with eosin Y (0.5% aqueous solution) for 1 min. Masson’s trichrome staining was carried out using a commercial kit (Sigma-Aldrich, HT15) to visualize collagen fibers (blue), nuclei (dark brown/black), and cytoplasm/muscle fibers (red). For nuclear visualization, sections were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA, H-1200) at a concentration of 1.5 µg/mL for 10 min at room temperature, followed by three washes in PBS. Images of the samples were obtained using an Axio Observer Z1 inverted microscope (Carl Zeiss, Inc., Germany) with an AxioVision 4.8 camera.

Preparation of decellularized rat skeletal muscle matrix hydrogel and assessment of its properties

For preparation of the decellularized skeletal muscle matrix hydrogel, 10 mg of ECM powder was placed in 900 µl of pepsin (Sigma‒Aldrich) solution (0.01 M HCl, pepsin: ECM = 1:10) and then incubated under shaking for 48 h until dissolved. Then, NaOH was added to stop the reaction. Then, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added and evenly mixed, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, 2 h, and 24 h for in vitro gelation. The matrix solution (500 µl) was incubated at 37 °C for gelation and assessed every 20 min. The ECM hydrogel was disinfected under ultraviolet light, and its thickness was very thin.

Quantitative analysis of the DNA content, protein content and growth factors

The DNA content of fresh skeletal muscle, ECM and hydrogel was analyzed by a dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Inc.). For evaluation of the protein content in the three kinds of samples, a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein detection kit (Sigma-Aldrich) was adopted. A Blyscan GAG Assay Kit (Biocolor) and rat enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) (Minneapolis, MN) were used to quantitatively identify the levels of sulfated glycosaminoglycans (s-GAG) and fibroblast growth factor (bFGF).

Scanning electron microscopy

Imaging was conducted at a standard working distance of 10.0 mm using the Everhart-Thornley secondary electron detector (ETD). An accelerating voltage of 30 kV and a spot size of 3.0 were employed to achieve a balance between surface detail penetration and minimal sample damage. Magnifications ranged from 500x for overall structural assessment to 20,000x for detailed surface morphology examination. All images were acquired with a resolution of 2048 × 1768 pixels using the microscope’s integrated xT Microscope Control software (FEI Company). To ensure representative analysis, images were systematically captured from at least five distinct regions per sample. Scale bars were automatically generated and embedded in each micrograph by the acquisition software.

Flow cytometry

Cells were resuspended in ice-cold fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer (PBS supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum and 1 mM EDTA). Cells were incubated with purified anti-mouse antibody (BioLegend, USA) for 10 min at 4 °C to block non-specific Fc receptor binding. Surface staining was performed using optimized concentrations of fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. The antibody panel included the following: CD45 (BD Pharmingen), CD11b (BD Pharmingen), anti-integrinβ1 (ab95622, Abcam) and CD34 (ab81289, Abcam), and a fixable viability dye eFluor™ 780 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Following staining, cells were washed twice with FACS buffer. For intracellular staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.Stained cells were sorted on a high-speed cell sorter (BD FACSAria™ Fusion, BD Biosciences, USA) equipped with a 100 μm nozzle. The target population was sorted in “Purity” mode into collection tubes containing complete growth medium. Sort efficiency and post-sort purity, routinely exceeding 98%, were confirmed by re-analyzing a subset of sorted cells on the analyzer. All procedures were conducted under aseptic conditions within a certified biosafety cabinet, and samples were maintained at 4 °C throughout the process to preserve cell viability and staining integrity.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT‒PCR)

Total RNA extraction and amplification were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The following primers were used: Pax7: forward 5′-GTGCCCTCAGTGAGTTCGAT-3′ and reverse 5′-CCACATCTGAGCCCTCATCC-3′; MyoD: forward 5′-ACCCGAAGACTGCTGTGTC-3′ and reverse 5′-GTCTGACGATTCACAACAGGC-3′; MyHC: forward 5′-TGCCAAGACCGTGAGGAATG-3′ and reverse 5′-AATGCATCACAGCTCCCGTG-3′; and GAPDH: forward 5′-TCGGTGTGAACGGATTTG-3′ and reverse 5′-GGTCTCGCTCCTGGAAGA-3′.

Cell viability analysis

The MTT assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. SkMSCs were seeded in 96-well plates (1 × 104 cells/ml) coated with or without decellularized skeletal muscle ECM hydrogels. The concentration of solubilized MTT formazan was measured by a microplate reader (Tecan, Austria) at an absorbance wavelength of 490 nm.

Cell proliferation assay

5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was adopted to quantitatively evaluate the proliferation of skeletal muscle-derived mesenchymal stem cells (SkMSCs). Briefly, SkMSCs at passage 4–6 were harvested, counted, and seeded in 6-well plates (Corning, USA) at a density of 8 × 10⁵ cells per well. Plates were pre-coated overnight at 4 °C with 1 mL of decellularized skeletal muscle extracellular matrix (ECM) hydrogels (2 mg/mL) to mimic a native muscle niche. Cells were cultured in complete growth medium (α-MEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 2 mM L-glutamine) and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h to allow for complete adhesion. Following attachment, cells were synchronized by serum starvation (0.5% FBS) for 12 h. The medium was then replaced with fresh growth medium, and cells were sorted into the following four treatment groups for a 48-hour intervention: (1) NC group (Negative Control): Cells cultured in standard growth medium only; (2) TGFβ1 group: Cells treated with 2.5 ng/mL recombinant human TGF-β1 (PeproTech, 100 − 21) in growth medium; (3) TGFβ1 + PD98059 group:​ Cells pre-treated for 1 h with 10 µM PD98059 (Selleckchem, S1177), a specific MEK/ERK pathway inhibitor, followed by co-treatment with 2.5 ng/mL TGF-β1; (4) PMA group: Cells treated with 25 nmol/L phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; Sigma-Aldrich, P8139), a potent PKC/ERK pathway activator, in growth medium. All pharmacological agents were prepared as concentrated stock solutions in DMSO, with final DMSO concentrations normalized to ≤ 0.1% (v/v) across all groups, including the vehicle control for the NC group. For the EdU assay, 20 µM EdU (5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine) was added directly to the culture medium of each well 4 h prior to the termination of the 48-hour treatment period. Cells were then processed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Images from five random, non-overlapping fields per well were captured using a Zeiss Axio Observer 7 inverted fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany). The percentage of EdU-positive (proliferating) cells was quantified using ImageJ software with the Click-iT EdU ImageJ Plug-in. Experiments were performed in triplicate wells and repeated independently three times.

Immunofluorescence staining and microscopy

Cultures were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and processed for immunofluorescence and stained with DAPI to identify the nuclei. Primary antibodies against Pax7 (1:100 dilution, ab199010, Abcam, USA), MyoD (1:250 dilution, ab203383, Abcam), myosin heavy chain (1:200 dilution, ab11083, Abcam, USA), laminin (ab11575), collagen IV (ab6586), and desmin cytoskeletal markers (ab32362) (1:100 dilution, ab129002, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) were used. The secondary antibodies goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (1:1000 dilution, Alexa Fluor® 594) (ab150116) and goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488) (1:1000 dilution, ab150077, Abcam) were used. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (ab228549, Abcam, USA). Cell images were obtained by an AxioVision 4.8 camera and an Axio Observer Z1 inverted microscope (Carl Zeiss, Inc., Germany).

Confocal electron microscopy

Cells were seeded on confocal dishes and cultured for 24 h. The primary antibodies included Pax7 (1:200 dilution) and MyoD (1:200 dilution), which were then incubated with goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (1:1000 dilution, Alexa Fluor® 594) (ab150116) and goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488) (1:1000 dilution, ab150077, Abcam) for 1 h. Cell images were obtained using a confocal microscope (Nikon) .

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The luciferase reporter assay was performed to evaluate the regulatory effect of specific sequences on gene expression. A Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions to ensure sequential measurement of firefly and Renilla luciferase activities.Cells were gently washed once with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lysed directly in the wells with 100 µL of 1X Passive Lysis Buffer (Promega) for 15 min on a rotary shaker at room temperature. Cell lysates were then transferred to 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 2 min at 4 °C to pellet debris. A 20 µL aliquot of the supernatant was analyzed for luciferase activity using a GloMax 20/20 Luminometer (Promega). First, 100 µL of Luciferase Assay Reagent II was injected to measure firefly luciferase activity, followed by injection of 100 µL of Stop & Glo Reagent to quench the firefly signal and simultaneously activate the Renillaluciferase. The relative firefly luciferase activity for each sample was calculated by normalizing the firefly luminescence value to the corresponding Renillaluminescence value.

RNA stability assay

RNA stability assay was performed as previous reported. In brief, actinomycin D (Sigma-Aldrich) was added into SkMSCs at 5 mg/ml. Then, these cells were incubated for the indicated time points. After cells were collected, RNA samples were extracted and subjected to reverse transcription and qPCR. mRNA transcription was inhibited with actinomycin D, and the degradation rate of RNA (K decay) was estimated.

Quantitative PCR (MeRIP-qPCR)

To assess site-specific m6A methylation, a methylated RNA immunoprecipitation followed by quantitative PCR (MeRIP-qPCR) assay was performed. Briefly, total RNA was isolated and chemically fragmented. Subsequently, the fragmented RNA was subjected to immunoprecipitation using an anti-m6A antibody conjugated to magnetic beads. Following stringent washes, the m6A-enriched RNA was eluted and purified, alongside a portion of the pre-immunoprecipitation fragmented RNA serving as the input control. Both the immunoprecipitated and input RNA samples were then reverse transcribed into cDNA. Finally, the relative abundance of target RNA fragments in each sample was quantified by qPCR using gene-specific primers, and the m6A enrichment level was calculated by normalizing the immunoprecipitated signal to its corresponding input.

Animals and treatment

SkMSCs purified by FACS were transfected with green fluorescent protein (GFP) lentivirus and transfected into GFP-coated 48-well plates. After incubation for 24 h, SkMSCs were harvested by resuspension and washed several times with FACS buffer. For each engraftment, 5 × 105 SkMSCs with or without ECM hydrogels were immediately transplanted into tibial muscles of adult Sprague‒Dawley rats immunosuppressed with FK506 (5 mg/kg body weight, Sigma) (12–16 weeks, 100–120 g, purchased from The Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center) that had been injured with CTX 2 d before. After the animals were euthanized, we obtained SkMSCs under aseptic conditions. Furthermore, engrafted muscles were harvested 3 weeks after transplantation and fixed in 4% PFA for 30 min at room temperature (RT) followed by incubation in 30% sucrose/PBS overnight at 4 °C.

Overall treadmill activity and in situ muscle function

To evaluate exercise capacity and muscle function, Sprague-Dawley rats underwent an incremental treadmill test to assess exercise capacity, with speed increasing to 20 cm/s in 2 min and then by 5 cm/s every 5 min until exhaustion, defined by failure to resume running after five electric stimuli (0.4 mA) within 10 s. Maximal running speed (Vmax) and distance were recorded. Muscle function was evaluated by in situ measurement of tibialis anterior (TA) contractility in response to nerve stimulation.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Immunohistochemistry was performed as previously described. Briefly, muscle samples were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin and subsequently embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded tissues were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by 3,3’-diaminobenzidine peroxidase (DAB) immunohistochemical staining. After proteolytic digestion and peroxidase blocking, slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with Ki67 antibodies (1:100, ab15580, Abcam, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed to determine the significance of observed differences. For comparisons involving two independent groups, a Student’s t-test​ was applied to assess whether the means of the two groups were significantly different. For comparisons involving three or more independent groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted initially. If the ANOVA indicated a statistically significant overall difference. Statistical significance was set at *P < 0.05, and denoted as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise specified.

Results

Evaluation of decellularized skeletal muscle ECM hydrogels

The decellularization of rat skeletal muscle ECM was performed using six distinct methods (A-F). H&E and DAPI staining confirmed the complete removal of intact nuclei from the decellularized ECM, in contrast to fresh muscle tissue (Fig. 1A, B and Sup Fig. 1A). Despite the absence of cells, the decellularized ECM (Fig. 1C) preserved key components of the extracellular matrix, particularly laminin and type IV collagen, fibronectin, elastinin and Collagen I/III(Sup Fig. 1B-D), which were localized to the basement membrane (Fig. 1D), showing consistency across the various decellularization protocols. This preservation of ECM integrity is crucial for maintaining the structural and biochemical environment of muscle tissue. Subsequently, ECM-based hydrogels were fabricated (Sup Fig. 2). Masson’s trichrome staining revealed a stable collagen presence in the stromal compartment of both decellularized ECM and hydrogel scaffolds, with no significant differences observed when compared to fresh muscle tissue (Sup Fig. 2A). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) demonstrated a porous structure in the hydrogels (Sup Fig. 1B), which is essential for supporting cellular infiltration and tissue regeneration. However, a marked reduction in glycosaminoglycan (GAG) content was noted in the hydrogel compared to fresh muscle ECM (Sup Fig. 2C), potentially impacting its ability to support hydration and signaling pathways. Protein assays (BCA) showed a decreased protein content in the hydrogel compared to fresh muscle, indicating a loss of certain ECM proteins during hydrogel processing (Sup Fig. 2D). Despite this, the hydrogel retained basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), albeit at lower levels than in fresh muscle ECM (Sup Fig. 3A, 3B), suggesting some retention of bioactive molecules critical for cellular behavior. MTT assays demonstrated that stem cell viability on hydrogel scaffolds was comparable to cells cultured in standard culture wells, indicating that the hydrogel provides an environment conducive to cell survival (Sup Fig. 3C). Collectively, these data underscore the importance of ECM components, such as collagen and laminin, in preserving the structural and biochemical cues that promote cellular interactions, while highlighting the challenges associated with maintaining the full protein and GAG content during scaffold fabrication. These findings emphasize the potential of decellularized ECM-derived scaffolds in regenerative medicine, offering a biologically relevant framework for tissue engineering applications.

ECM hydrogels with TGFβ1 affected the proliferation and differentiation of SkMSCs in vitro

Flow cytometric analysis confirmed that SkMSCs expressed CD34 and integrin β1, but lacked CD11b and CD45 (Sup Fig. 4A). Morphologically, the cells displayed a fibroblast-like appearance and strong expression of Pax7 and MyoD (Sup Fig. 5A). Under culture in DMEM supplemented with 2% heat-inactivated horse serum, MyoD expression progressively increased, whereas Pax7 expression gradually declined (Sup Fig. 5B, C). Upon induction of differentiation, the cells exhibited elevated levels of MyoD, desmin, and MyHC, accompanied by a reduction in Pax7-positive cells (Sup Fig. 5D). Functional assays demonstrated that ECM hydrogels enriched with TGFβ1 markedly enhanced SkMSC viability, with a significant increase compared to control groups, whereas hydrogels alone showed no substantial difference from the negative control (Fig. 2A). Immunofluorescence staining further revealed that the hydrogel + TGFβ1 combination robustly promoted the expression of MyoD and MyHC (Fig. 2B, C), a finding corroborated by western blot analysis (Fig. 2D). Collectively, these results indicate that ECM hydrogels, when supplemented with TGFβ1, exert a synergistic effect on SkMSCs by not only supporting cell proliferation but also accelerating myogenic differentiation. This highlights their potential as a bioactive platform for muscle regeneration and repair.

m6A modification of integrin regulated the proliferation and differentiation of SkMSCs

To elucidate how TGFβ1 promotes SkMSC proliferation and differentiation through ERK signaling, we identified a previously unrecognized TGFβ1–METTL3–integrin axis. qPCR analysis revealed that TGFβ1 stimulation markedly upregulated METTL3 and integrin expression, whereas TGFβ1 inhibition produced the opposite effect (Fig. 3A), a pattern confirmed by western blotting (Fig. 3B, C). Functional assays demonstrated that METTL3 is a key upstream regulator of integrin synthesis: METTL3 knockout (KO) suppressed integrin expression, while METTL3 overexpression (OE) enhanced it. Luciferase reporter experiments provided direct evidence that integrin is a downstream target of METTL3, with KO cells transfected with the wild-type integrin 3′UTR/CDS showing reduced luciferase activity, which was restored in constructs carrying mutated m6A sites (Fig. 3D). Conversely, co-expression of METTL3 and wild-type integrin elevated both integrin mRNA levels and reporter activity (Fig. 3F, G). Actinomycin D assays further revealed that METTL3 enhances integrin mRNA stability, reinforcing its post-transcriptional regulatory role (Fig. 3H, I). Collectively, these findings uncover a novel epitranscriptomic mechanism in which TGFβ1 activates METTL3 to drive m6A-dependent stabilization of integrin transcripts, thereby coupling extracellular growth factor signaling to integrin-mediated SkMSC proliferation and myogenic differentiation. This regulatory cascade represents a previously unappreciated therapeutic target for skeletal muscle regeneration.

Identification of extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) as a regulatory target of integrin

To delineate the downstream signaling by which integrin promotes SkMSC proliferation and differentiation, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) identified a strong association between integrin expression and activation of the MAPK/ERK pathway (Fig. 4A). Functional modulation of integrin revealed a direct impact on ERK phosphorylation: integrin knockdown markedly suppressed p-ERK levels (Fig. 4B), whereas integrin overexpression enhanced ERK phosphorylation (Fig. 4C), a pattern confirmed by western blot analysis (Fig. 4D, E). Pharmacological inhibition using the selective ERK inhibitor PMD98089 attenuated integrin-induced ERK phosphorylation (Fig. 4F) and reversed its proliferative effects, as shown by reduced SkMSC growth (Fig. 4G). At the transcriptional level, integrin overexpression upregulated Pax7, MyoD, and MyHC, consistent with enhanced myogenic proliferation and differentiation, whereas ERK inhibition abolished these transcriptional gains (Fig. 4H). Western blot validation further confirmed that ERK blockade neutralized integrin-driven increases in myogenic marker expression (Fig. 4I). These findings establish that integrin promotes SkMSC proliferation and myogenic differentiation through ERK pathway activation, functioning downstream of m6A-dependent METTL3 regulation. This METTL3–integrin–ERK signaling axis integrates extracellular cues and epitranscriptomic control with kinase-driven signaling, providing a mechanistic framework for targeted enhancement of skeletal muscle regeneration.

TGFβ1-loaded ECM hydrogels regulate SkMSC proliferation and myogenic differentiation via ERK signaling

To explore the mechanistic role of ECM hydrogels with TGFβ1 in regulating SkMSC proliferation and differentiation, we performed EdU incorporation assays to assess proliferation (Fig. 5A, B). These assays demonstrated that TGFβ1 significantly enhanced SkMSC proliferation, a response that was abolished by inhibition of ERK signaling using the specific inhibitor PD98059. Conversely, activation of ERK signaling with PMA (phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate) further boosted SkMSC proliferation, indicating that ERK pathway activation is critical for mediating the proliferative effect of TGFβ1-loaded ECM hydrogels. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that TGFβ1 treatment improved the expression of MyoD and MyHC, key markers of myogenic differentiation (Fig. 5C, D). Inhibition of ERK signaling with PD98059 led to downregulation of MyoD and MyHC expression, while PMA treatment resulted in upregulation of these markers, reflecting the modulatory role of ERK signaling in controlling myogenesis. qRT-PCR and western blot analyses provided consistent results, confirming that TGFβ1-loaded ECM hydrogels promoted ERK signaling, as evidenced by increased levels of p-ERK and MyoD/MyHC (Fig. 5E, F, G). The inhibition of ERK signaling with PD98059 reversed TGFβ1’s promoted effects on ERK activation and myogenic marker expression, further supporting the notion that ERK pathway modulation plays a key role in regulating both proliferation and differentiation of SkMSCs. These results suggest that ECM hydrogels with TGFβ1 regulate SkMSC proliferation and differentiation via the ERK signaling pathway, where ERK activation promotes both proliferation and myogenic differentiation, while ERK inhibition suppresses these effects. This integrated signaling mechanism provides valuable insights into how ECM-based biomaterials can modulate stem cell behavior for tissue regeneration.

ECM hydrogels and TGFβ1 synergistically enhance SkMSC growth and muscle regeneration in vivo

To investigate how ECM hydrogels, in conjunction with the regenerating microenvironment, regulate SkMSC proliferation and differentiation, we divided Sprague-Dawley rats into five treatment groups: NC group (non-transplant, baseline), SkMSCs group (SkMSCs + PBS), TGFβ1 group (SkMSCs + TGFβ1), hydrogel group (SkMSCs + hydrogel), and hydrogels + TGFβ1 group (SkMSCs + hydrogel + TGFβ1). We assessed the in vivo effects of ECM hydrogels and TGFβ1 on muscle function and quality. Both ECM and TGFβ1 treatments resulted in moderate weight gain, with the combined ECM + TGFβ1 group showing a significant increase compared to the others (Fig. 6A). By the end of the study, the ECM + TGFβ1 group demonstrated significant increases in gastrocnemius and TA muscle mass, outperforming either treatment alone, while no significant change was observed in the quadriceps (Fig. 6B). Additionally, the combination of ECM and TGFβ1 produced a significant improvement in maximal running distance, surpassing the effects of the individual treatments (Sup Fig. 6A). In situ contractility tests on the TA muscle revealed that ECM and TGFβ1 treatments significantly enhanced both maximal absolute and specific strength compared to the NC group (Sup Fig. 6B, C). The combination of ECM and TGFβ1 further amplified these improvements. Treatment with ECM and TGFβ1 also led to enhanced muscle function, including altered twitch duration, half-relaxation time, and fatigue resistance (Sup Fig. 7A-C).

To examine the cellular effects, primary cultures were derived from different tissue groups and evaluated for Ki67 expression by immunohistochemistry (Fig. 6C). Notably, ECM and TGFβ1 treatment resulted in a marked increase in Ki67 expression (Fig. 6D), indicating enhanced SkMSC proliferation. The combination treatment exhibited a synergistic effect on SkMSC proliferation. Immunofluorescence staining of Pax7 and MyoD in skeletal muscle tissues showed that Pax7 expression was reduced, while MyoD was significantly upregulated in the ECM and TGFβ1-treated groups. The combination of ECM and TGFβ1 further enhanced these effects (Fig. 6E, F). Additionally, gastrocnemius muscles from the ECM and TGFβ1-treated group exhibited elevated levels of ERK phosphorylation, alongside moderate increases in METTL3 and integrin expression (Fig. 6G, H). Furthermore, We administered the METTL3 inhibitor STM2457 to rats via intramuscular injection and found that STM2457 inhibited the promoting effect of TGFβ1 on skeletal muscle cells, resulting in reductions in PAX7, MyoD, and MyHC and observed that the ERK signaling pathway was also suppressed, and this effect was reversed by PMA(Sup Fig. 8A-D). These results demonstrate that ECM hydrogels, in combination with TGFβ1, promote SkMSC proliferation and myogenic differentiation, leading to enhanced muscle function in vivo. The observed effects are mediated through the m6A-modulated integrin-ERK signaling pathway, underscoring the powerful synergistic role of ECM and TGFβ1 in skeletal muscle regeneration.

Discussion

Soft tissue loss resulting from traumatic limb injuries and long-term denervation remains a prevalent and challenging clinical issue. While stem cell-based therapies—particularly those involving skeletal muscle stem cells (SkMSCs)—have shown promise in tissue regeneration, their clinical efficacy is often limited by poor cell viability and functionality post-transplantation. Although SkMSCs can proliferate and differentiate into muscle fibers in vitro, they frequently lose their regenerative capacity in vivo due to the hostile microenvironment of injured tissues. To overcome this limitation, engineered culture platforms that preserve the stemness and viability of SkMSCs are urgently needed. Given their essential role in muscle regeneration, maintaining functional SkMSCs is critical for the success of regenerative therapies. Recent studies have sought to optimize SkMSC expansion and survival by replicating key aspects of the native microenvironment. In this study, we developed a rapid and efficient method for decellularizing skeletal muscle tissue to produce an extracellular matrix (ECM) hydrogel with preserved biochemical composition and a native-like three-dimensional architecture. Importantly, we demonstrated that TGFβ1 enhances m6A methylation levels, thereby promoting SkMSC proliferation and differentiation through activation of the ERK signaling pathway. These findings offer valuable insight into the interplay between biochemical cues and stem cell fate, and provide a potential strategy to improve the therapeutic utility of SkMSCs in muscle regeneration.

Further elucidation of SkMSC behavior in microenvironments with ECM compositions is also critically essential for developing effective therapeutic targeting SkMSCs in muscle diseases [20]. Previous studies have demonstrated that decellularized ECM provides a structure to repair and reconstruct damaged tissues [21]. Recent studies have reported various methods for the decellularization of muscle tissue [22]. However, insufficient treatment may cause inflammation and immune rejection because of the remaining antigenic cellular proteins. Furthermore, excessive decellularization, which can ensure biological safety, may damage the composition and structure of the ECM [23, 24]. Our study demonstrates that ECM hydrogels supplemented with TGFβ1 synergistically enhance SkMSC proliferation and promote robust myogenic differentiation. This is evidenced by the increased expression of MyoD, desmin, and MyHC, alongside a reduction in Pax7-positive cells. While ECM hydrogels alone provide a supportive niche, a permissive microenvironment that supports stem cell function but requires specific growth factor signals to drive proliferation, highlighting a powerful interplay between extracellular matrix signals and growth factor activity. This combined approach not only supports stem cell viability but also accelerates their commitment to the myogenic lineage, underscoring its potential as a bioactive platform for muscle regeneration. This strategy may have significant translational value in skeletal muscle repair, where the concurrent enhancement of both proliferation and differentiation is essential for functional recovery.

Previous studies are required to identify the prevalence of dysfunctional biochemical signals in SkMSCs and molecular mechanisms that may regulate SkMSC fates [25, 26]. Moreover, TGFβ1 was reported to regulate the ERK signaling pathway involved in inflammation, regeneration and differentiation [27, 28]. We further investigated the mechanism underlying the function of the ECM hydrogels with TGFβ1 in terms of the proliferation and differentiation of SkMSCs, and our study indicated that METTL3 regulates integrin and attenuates its m6A-mediated degradation. Integrin activates the ERK signaling pathway and affects the expression of the proliferative marker Ki67 in tissue. Recent studies have shown that transmembrane integrin and activates ERK and ALP activities [29–32]. Here, we proved that METTL3 enhanced integrin. which therefore promoted ERK phosphorylation in SkMSCs. To elucidate the molecular mechanism underpinning these effects, we uncovered a novel TGFβ1–METTL3–integrin signaling pathway in SkMSCs. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed a strong association between integrin expression and the MAPK/ERK pathway. Functional modulation of integrin confirmed its critical role in activating ERK signaling: integrin knockdown reduced ERK phosphorylation, whereas integrin overexpression enhanced p-ERK levels, as validated by western blot analysis. METTL3 regulates integrin expression by stabilizing its mRNA through m6A modification, enhancing integrin synthesis and activating the ERK pathway. TGFβ1-loaded ECM hydrogels support SkMSC proliferation and myogenic differentiation, activating the METTL3–integrin–ERK axis for enhanced regeneration.The ECM + TGFβ1 treatment led to increased body weight and improved muscle mass, especially in the gastrocnemius and TA muscles, but not in the quadriceps. This selective enhancement suggests that ECM hydrogels and TGFβ1 promote regeneration in specific muscles. The combination treatment showed the greatest benefits in muscle performance, as seen in improved running distance and contractility. At the cellular level, ECM and TGFβ1 treatments significantly boosted SkMSC proliferation, with the combination treatment having the most pronounced effect, highlighting a synergistic interaction. This supports previous studies on ECM’s role in tissue repair and TGFβ1’s impact on muscle regeneration.

This study has several limitations. First, the majority of our findings were based on in vitro experiments, and the therapeutic relevance of the ECM–TGFβ1–SkMSC axis requires in vivo validation, particularly regarding cell survival, immune interactions, and functional muscle repair. Our study did not directly test the potential crosstalk between this axis and other pathways known to regulate muscle atrophy, such as the TGF-β/SMAD signaling pathway. Future studies employing specific SMAD pathway inhibitors or genetic knockdowns in conjunction with our model will be essential to determine whether the observed effects are entirely independent or involve interactive signaling networks. Second, although the ECM hydrogel retained a native-like 3D structure, its mechanical properties—such as stiffness and viscoelasticity—were not systematically characterized, which may affect stem cell behavior. Third, while m6A methylation and ERK signaling were implicated downstream of TGFβ1, key regulators such as METTL3 and specific methylation sites on integrin transcripts were not functionally validated. Additionally, the pleiotropic nature of TGFβ1 raises concerns about potential pro-fibrotic or immunomodulatory effects in vivo, highlighting the need for dose and delivery optimization. Finally, the absence of a fully competent immune system represents a limitation of the current in vivo model. Future studies in immunocompetent models will be essential to validate these findings and to investigate the intricate interplay between the implanted construct and the host immune system, which will be a crucial step toward clinical translation.

Conclusion

In summary, we explored how the regenerating microenvironment regulates SkMSC proliferation and differentiation. We found that decellularized ECM scaffolds with TGFβ1 promote these processes through ERK signaling, mediated by m6A modification of integrin. Upregulation of integrin enhances SkMSC proliferation and differentiation, suggesting it as a potential therapeutic target for tissue regeneration.
