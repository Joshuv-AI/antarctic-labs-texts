In-school resistance training improves physical fitness in pubertal girls: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Jiaqian Wan, Zhe Li, Ruiqing Sun, Jiamin Gu, Yuzhang Li, Qingwen Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhysical fitness is a powerful marker of current and future health in children and adolescents. However, global reports have long documented a persistent decline, with an even greater decline among girls. This study aimed to examine the impact of school-based resistance training (RT) on physical fitness and body composition in pubertal girls.MethodsA randomized controlled trial was conducted over eight weeks, with sessions held three times a week. RT program was conducted during physical education classes. A total of 79 girls from four schools were randomly assigned to one of four groups: control group (CON, n = 21), traditional strength training group (TST, n = 20), plyometric training group (PT, n = 19), and combined training group (CT, n = 19). Pre- and post-intervention assessments were performed by evaluating body composition, musculoskeletal fitness, cardiorespiratory fitness, speed and agility.ResultsSignificant improvements were observed in TST for push-ups, 1 min sit-ups, standing long jump (SLJ) and 50 m sprint (p < 0.05). PT and CT groups displayed valuable improvement in push-ups, SLJ, 50 m sprint and Illinois changes of direction test (p < 0.05). Notably, CT yielded a significant increase in the 20 m shuttle run test (p < 0.001). However, non-significant changes in body composition were observed across intervention groups.ConclusionRT program implemented during physical education classes was effective in improving physical fitness. Each RT modalities offering unique advantages.Clinical trial numberChiCTR2500103789; Retrospectively registered on 5/6/2025.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01351-8.

Introduction

A persistent global decline in physical fitness among children and adolescents has been reported over recent decades. characterized by an increment in overweight and obesity, as well as declining speed, agility, cardiorespiratory and musculoskeletal fitness [1–3]. This trend has yet to exhibit a significant rebound in many countries [4–6]. Girls exhibit a larger decrease than boys [7] and engage in less physical activity during puberty [8]. Furthermore, girls exhibit a significant decline in physical fitness during puberty [9, 10], highlighting the potential need for counteract this decline. Physical fitness is a powerful marker of current and future health in children and adolescents [11]. The World Health Organization and several countries have established physical activity guidelines that highlight the need for resistance training (RT) for children and adolescents. RT is an effective method to improve physical fitness, including maximal muscular strength, power, speed and agility [12–14]. A recent study has demonstrated that various RT modalities are beneficial for children and adolescents, including weightlifting, traditional strength training (TST), plyometric training (PT), and a combination of TST and PT, known as combined training (CT) [15]. Previous studies demonstrated that TST and PT significantly enhance muscle strength [14]; the combination of TST and PT is more effective than utilizing TST or PT alone [16]. However, although studies have investigated the efficacy of these RT modalities, there is limited evidence comparing the efficacy of these RT modalities and their potential association with maturation in female youth populations.

Furthermore, during puberty, the growth spurt leads to an opportunity for RT [17]. During puberty, there is an increase in androgen concentrations, fiber-type differentiation, creatine phosphate levels, and additional anatomical development of the musculotendon unit [18]. Maturity-dependent responses may be indicative of synergistic adaptation, which refers to the symbiotic relationship between specific adaptations of an imposed training demand and concomitant growth and maturity-related adaptations [19]. A previous study has demonstrated that CT programs have significantly enhanced muscular power in the upper and lower limbs of mid-adolescent boys [20]. The improvements in reactive strength index and vertical jump exhibited a more significant impact in pre-adolescent boys after PT [21]. Numerous studies have focused on boys, discovering the effects of maturity and modality differences in RT. However, interventions for girls are limited, despite similar effects being observed in girls. A previous study reported that mid-pubertal girls exhibit a larger effect on countermovement jump than post-pubertal girls [22].

School environments are equipped with well-designed playgrounds, sports facilities, qualified teachers and positive peer interactions, providing an optimal environment for physical development [23]. School-based programs ensure universal access to physical activity opportunities irrespective of students’ background or socioeconomic status [24]. Additionally, children generally exhibit a greater physical activity level during school periods than evenings and weekends [25]; thus, this may provide a valuable opportunity for enhancing physical fitness. A previous review study revealed that combined RT interventions in school-based exercise were significantly improved muscular strength, power and endurance [26]. However, few studies have considered school-based RT interventions. Consequently, the primary aim of this study was to examine the effects of school-based RT interventions on physical fitness in pubertal girls. The secondary aim of this study was to compare the training outcomes across three RT modalities (TST, PT, and CT) and provide diverse, well-prescribed and developmentally appropriate exercise programs for pubertal girls.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

A randomized controlled trial was conducted to evaluate the effect of RT on physical fitness in pubertal girls. A priori power analysis was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1) to estimate the required sample size. The analysis employed analysis of variance (ANOVA: Fixed effect, omnibus, one-way), utilizing a type I error rate of 0.05, a statistical power of 0.8 across four groups, and an effect size of 0.4, as determined from a previous study on the effect of jumping performance in pubertal girls [22]. The analysis indicated that a total of 76 participants, with 19 participants in each group, would represent a sufficient sample size. As depicted in Fig. 1, the participants from one school were regarded as one group and randomly (anonymized allocation) assigned to one of the four groups: Control group (CON) (n = 21), TST (n = 20), PT (n = 19), and CT (n = 19) using an online randomizer (https://www.randomizer.org/). This study adhered to Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines for randomized controlled trials.

The study involved an 8-week intervention, which was carried out between September 10 and November 25 in the autumn semester of 2024. The intervention was conducted during physical education (PE) classes. In the current Chinese curriculum, PE classes are scheduled three times per week for 40 min in secondary school. The structured protocol examined in the previous study consisted of 20 min of sports skill-based exercises, 10 min of physical fitness exercises, and 10 min allocated for warm-up, cool-down and instruction [27]. This protocol ensured adherence to prescribed exercise durations and elicited positive effects on physical fitness. The skill-based exercise including skills practices, group practices and competitions (Table S1). RT interventions were conducted during the 10 min physical fitness exercise segment, whereas the CON group continued with their regular comprehensive physical fitness exercise without RT interventions, including speed, agility and balance. The PE lessons were supervised and administered by a trained PE teacher, with assistance from a researcher who monitored exercise intensity using a polar heart rate monitor (Polar Verity Sense, Polar, Finland). Exercise intensity was maintained within the moderate-to-vigorous range (57%-76% maximal heart rate) [28]. Subjects who failed to complete 85% of the scheduled sessions were excluded from the study.

Participants

A total of 79 pubertal girls aged 10–12 years, from grades six of four schools (two classes from each school) in Shanghai, volunteered to participate in this study. The four groups presented similar anthropometric characteristics at baseline (Table 1). The inclusion criteria included (1) participants with biological maturity, assessed using the self-reported Pubertal Development Scale (PDS) [29], and only pubertal girls were included in this study, and (2) participants who were previously engaged in physical activity based on the PE curriculum but had not participated in any systematic strength and conditioning programs. The exclusion criteria included (1) girls with injuries or potential medical problems, (2) girls who participated in sports training after school, and (3) girls with overweight/obesity, due to their significantly lower physical fitness level, earlier pubertal onset [30] and different responses during PE lesson [31]. Participants outside the healthy BMI range were excluded to minimize confounders and individual differences. Participants were informed of study progress through newsletters and received overviews of personal results after tests at each time point.

Measures

All participants were assessed before and after the 8-week intervention for the following independent variables: maturity state, anthropometric measurements and physical fitness tests. A standardized dynamic warm-up was conducted during a familiarization session. After the warm-up and practice trials, participants completed a series of tests in a fixed order over two separate sessions. Session one comprised hand grip strength, push-ups, standing long jump (SLJ), 1 min sit-ups, and the Illinois changes of direction test (ICODT). Session two included 50 m speed and 20 m shuttle run tests (SRT). There was a 24 h interval between the two sessions. All tests were performed at least 24 h after the last PE lesson, in the morning between 8:00 and 10:30 a.m. in the indoor sports hall. Four trained personnel, blinded to the group assignments of the participants, conducted all measurements.

Maturation

PDS [29] is a non-invasive method that is closely related to Tanner’s sexual maturation scale, especially in girls [32–34]. Before completing the scale, female researchers explained the concept of puberty and each item of the scale one by one in a private classroom setting, only for girls. The scale assessed growth characteristics specific to girls, including public hair, height, skin changes (pimples), breast development and menarche. According to PDS scores, the participants were divided into pre-puberty (3 points), puberty (4–11 points) and post-puberty (12 points).

Anthropometrics

Anthropometric measurements were conducted concurrently with the maturation assessment. Standing height was measured to the nearest centimeter using a stadimeter. Body mass and body composition, including fat mass, muscle mass and body fat percentage (BF%), were assessed to the nearest 0.1 kg using bioelectrical impedance analysis (MC-780MA, Tanita, Japan).

Physical fitness

A hand grip dynamometer (K-force, Kinvent, France) was employed to measure the hand grip strength with the dominant hand in the standing position. During the test, the elbow remained extended, while the wrist was maintained in an extended position. Participants applied pressure gradually and continuously for at least 2s [35]. In the test of push-ups, participants flexed their elbows to approximately 90º and subsequently lifted their bodies. During the movement, participants maintained full-body alignment in a straight line from the head to the heel. The sit-up test was performed with subjects in a supine position, with hips and feet on the ground, knees flexed, and hands positioned behind the neck. Subjects were required to lift their trunk to a sitting position. A rigid steel rule was employed for SLJ measurements. The distance was measured between the starting line and the first contact by the heel of the foot. The ICODT was conducted in accordance with Amiri-Khorasani’s protocol [36], utilizing four cones to delineate an area measuring 10 m in length and 5 m in width. Upon the instruction “Go,” participants ran 10 m and returned to the starting line, and subsequently maneuvered in and out of four markers. The participants completed a 10 m sprint and proceeded to the finish line. The 20 m SRT is a field-based fitness test that provides a meaningful measure of cardiorespiratory fitness [37]. Participants were required to run between two lines 20 m apart in pace with the audio signals [38]. The initial running speed was 8.5 km/h, and each stage (1 min) was increased by 0.5 km/h [35]. A fully automated running timing system (Freelap, Swiss technology, Switzerland) was utilized to measure a 50 m sprint. The sprint time was recorded to the nearest 0.01s. Two trials (over 30s rest between two tests) were conducted in hand grip strength and SLJ, and the best result was recorded.

Training programs

As recommended in a previous study, the general strength with moderate training intensity should be emphasized, with progressive intensity increases during maturation [39]. Bodyweight-based movement facilitates safety and induces effective adaptation [14, 40]. The 8–10 repetitions and 1–3 sets of each exercise induce positive adaptation in muscular strength and enable suitable adjustments [21, 41]. Each RT program implemented lower limb, upper limb and core exercises. Each exercise performed three exercises comprising 10 repetitions and 3 sets, with an inter-set rest of 30 s. As evidenced in Table S2, participants in the TST group initially performed half squats, knee-bended push-ups and planked with body weight. After two weeks, resistance was raised by 5%–10% of body weight [42]. An elastic resistance band was introduced to increase training intensity (10lb–30lb) in half squats every two weeks. During weeks 3–4, push-ups without knee flexion were performed for the upper limbs, followed by the addition of resistance bands (10lb − 20lb) in weeks 5–8. The core training movements were based on the Panagoulis [43] program, and the training intensity was raised by increasing difficulty.

PT program was adapted from Lloyd et al[16]. The detailed training program was presented in Table S3. Initially, all participants performed box jumps, landing and jumping up from 20 cm height of plyo boxes. After four weeks, jumping over a 20 cm hurdle was conducted and the height was subsequently increased to 30 cm in the last two weeks. Standing push-ups for upper limbs and declined body inclination were used to increase the training intensity. Overhead ball throws for the core, increased ball weight, and changing position form standing to lying were used to increase the training intensity. The CT program integrated elements of both TST and PT. Each set contained the same intensity and movement of TST and PT with half the volume (five repetitions) of each exercise. During each session, the CON group completed a training protocol comprising one speed (20 m, 30 m and 10 m shuttle sprint), agility (zigzag running and reactive catching games) and balance (30s of single leg balance with or without visual input) exercises, respectively. All exercises were performed in three sets.

Statistical analyses

Data normality was evaluated using a one-sample Shapiro-Wilk test. The mean and standard deviation was used to present normally distributed data. The independent t-test was used on baseline data to ensure equality among the four groups for all experimental variables. The proportion of muscle mass and fat mass were calculated as the percentage of body weight. A one-way analysis of variance was applied to examine the main effects of post-intervention on the three intervention groups. Given the observed heterogeneity in pubertal stage distribution across groups, between-group differences at post-test were analyzed using analysis of covariance, with baseline measurements of physical fitness and body composition included as covariates to control for pre-existing differences [44]. A significance value was set at p < 0.05. Standardized effect size (Cohen’s d) was used and classified as trivial (d < 0.20), small (d < 0.50), medium (0.50 ≤ d < 0.80), and large (d ≥ 0.8) [45]. All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS 29.0 program (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Effect of RT on body composition

During the 8-week intervention, all participants adhered to their allocated protocol as attended over 85% of the scheduled sessions. No test and RT-related injuries occurred over the protocol. Figure 2 illustrated the body composition of each group at baseline and after training. The three RT groups demonstrated trends toward increased muscle mass and decreased fat mass, though these changes did not reach statistical significance.

Effect of RT on musculoskeletal fitness

Figure 3 compares the pre and post-tests values for hand grip strength, push-ups, 1 min sit-ups and SLJ. In the control group, the hand grip strength, 1 min sit-ups and SLJ present no significant change, while a significant decline was observed in push-ups. Following the 8-week intervention, significant improvement in the hand grip strength were observed in the PT (p < 0.01, d = 0.36, 95% CI [0.19, 0.72]) and CT (p < 0.001, d = 0.51, 95% CI [0.02, 0.98]) groups. TST (p < 0.01, d = 1.11, 95% CI [0.54, 1.66]), PT (p < 0.01, d = 0.43, 95% CI [0.05, 0.89]) and CT (p < 0.001, d = 0.32, 95% CI [0.20, 0.72]) demonstrated substantial improvement in push-ups, and all three groups presented remarkable differences compared to CON. TST (p < 0.001, d = 1.65, 95% CI [0.96, 2.32]), PT( p < 0.01, d = 0.60, 95% CI [0.11, 1.09]) and CT (p < 0.001, d = 0.89, 95% CI [0.35, 1.42]) exhibited the largest effect on 1 min sit-ups. The SLJ results revealed similar improvements across TST (p < 0.05, d = 0.66, 95% CI [0.17, 1.14]), PT (p < 0.001, d = 0.67, 95% CI [0.18, 1.16]) and CT (p < 0.001, d = 0.69, 95% CI [0.17, 1.17]) groups.

Discussion

This study compared the effect of 8-week RT program (TST, PT and CT) during physical education classes on physical fitness in pubertal girls. Following short-term and school-based RT intervention, a positive trend in physical fitness was observed in all three RT groups, and each RT modality exerted distinct effects.In order to better enhance physical activity levels and physical fitness in children and adolescents, many school programs have been implemented, including additional physical activity lesson, longer PE lesson [46], physical activity in class [47] and school break times [48]. These strategies were mainly based on the duration of physical activity. Previous studies have demonstrated that the integration of short-duration (7–20 min) RT into PE curricula can elicit meaningful improvements in various measurements of physical fitness. One previous study demonstrated that a 7 min program significantly enhanced push-ups and sit-ups [49]. Studies employing 10–15 min RT protocols documented positive adaptations in handgrip strength, push-ups, 1 min sit-ups, SLJ and vertical jump [50, 51]. Similarly, a 10–20 min RT intervention produced significant improvement in SLJ and 20mSRT [52].

TST showed small effect on 50 m sprint, a moderate effect on SLJ and substantial effect on push-ups and 1 min sit-ups. Body weight exercises, free weights, and resistance machines were predominantly used in previous school-based interventions, demonstrating a small to moderate impact on both upper limb and lower limb strength [14]. TST is especially effective for tasks requiring concentric strength and correlated with improvements in maximum strength outcomes [44]. A study revealed that significant strength gains of approximately 30% after 8 to 20 weeks of TST [42]. Moreover, by significantly increasing testosterone levels in children and adolescents [53], RT augmented muscle protein synthesis, resulting in increasedmuscle mass and muscular strength.

PT exhibited a trivial effect on agility, a small effect on hand grip, and a moderate effect on SLJ, push-ups, and 50 m sprint. PT incorporated rapid, powerful movements utilizing the stretch-shortening cycle, thereby enhancing elastic energy and subsequent force generation during muscle contraction [54]. This mechanism critically influences strength [55], sprint [56], and agility [12]. Numerous studies have investigated the effect of PT on lower limb strength in youth, revealing a significant and homogenous effect on jumping performance (e.g., countermovement jump, squat jump, SLJ and drop jump) [57, 58]. Furthermore, PT induced a moderate to large effect in 10–30 m sprints and agility [59].

CT, which combines TST and PT, enhances muscular strength more effectively than TST or PT alone. Herein, CT produced a small effect on agility, a moderate effect on hand grip strength, SLJ, 50 m sprint, and 20 m SRT, and a significant effect on push-ups and 1 min sit-ups. These findings align with those of previous studies demonstrating significant improvements in strength [20], sprint and agility [60] following a CT program. Remarkably, the 20 m SRT also showed significant improvement after 8-week of CT. Even though RT cannot enhance the VO2max, multi-joint exercises provide a superior neuromuscular stimulus, hence boosting running economy [61], which can improve cardiorespiratory fitness. The running economy is influenced by coordination level, multiplanar control and synergistic muscle activation [62], which can be improved with RT. Numerous studies have demonstrated that running economy improved after various modalities of RT [63, 64], providing a better specificity level and transfer of training effect to 20 m SRT. Moreover, muscular fitness, especially SLJ is an indicator of cardiorespiratory fitness [9].

The three modalities of RT increased physical fitness from different aspects, each demonstrating unique advantages. In three training groups, TST exhibited the most significant effect on abdominal muscle endurance and upper limb maximal muscle strength. PT produced a slightly higher effect of SLJ than TST, supporting findings in boys [44]. CT induced the most substantial improvement in hand grip strength, SLJ, sprint, agility and endurance, with larger effect sizes than TST and PT. Notably, PT and CT significantly improved the ICODT, and a superior improvement was obtained in CT. The observed sprint enhancements further confirm agility improvements, which involve rapid directional shifts dependent on strength and power development [65]. These results corroborate previous studies indicating that the combination of TST and PT surpasses single approaches when matched for intensity and volume [16, 66]. No significant change in physical fitness were observed in CON group. These results were consistent with previous study that 6 weeks regular PE lessons without RT produced no significant improvements in musculoskeletal fitness , speed, agility and cardiorespiratory fitness among pubertal girls [67]. After eight months, female participants in the CON group showed modest gains in handgrip strength (3.1 kg) and vertical jump (1.8 cm) [68]. Exercise science principles suggest progressive overload constitutes a necessary condition for eliciting physical fitness improvements [69], however regular PE curricula often struggle to implement structured progressive overload protocols. This study demonstrated that incorporating structured and progressive progressive overload RT into PE lessons effectively enhances physical fitness.

The body composition exhibited negligible changes after an 8-week RT intervention across all modalities. Previous study indicated minimal change (up to 0.5%) in BMI among following 8 weeks RT [67]. Previous review studies have also reported no significant changes in body fat following PT and CT, whereas RT demonstrated significant improvements in muscle thickness and fiber cross-sectional area [70]. During puberty, fat mass progressively increases steadily during puberty (5.7%) in girls, with a significant spurt of approximately 2.9 kg following one year of menarche [71]. In the participant with healthy weight, modifications in body composition may require a longer intervention period or integration with aerobic exercise. Studies have reported significant BMI and fat mass changes after the intervention for over three months [72]. In addition, our findings demonstrated no statistically significant increase in muscle mass within either experimental group. Girls experienced less muscle mass gain and fat mass reduction through exercise than boys. After an 8-month PT intervention, consisting of twice-weekly 10 min sessions, boys gained 8.9% muscle mass, whereas girls gained only 3.8% [68], which is attributed to the lower testosterone in girls. During puberty, the testosterone level increases fourfold; however, it remains significantly lower than that of boys [73]. In addition, a previous experimental study observed a small effect (0.4) of PT on countermovement jump in pubertal girls [22], which is less than that reported in boys (0.47–0.55) [44]. Overall, evidence highlights a significant difference in physiological change and training adaptation during puberty between sexes, underscoring the urgent physical need for appropriate interventions to address this problem [74]. The youth physical development model recommends that girls should participate in strength and conditioning programs (including balance, perturbation and various RT modalities) and continue into adulthood [19].

Usefulness and practical implications of school-based RT

A 10 min RT program was easily to integrated into PE lessons and provided significant benefit to physical fitness with a time-efficient impact. Additionally, the movement of all training modalities was based on body weight activities and did not strictly rely on venue and facility requirements, making it appropriate for most students and schools. When implementing RT programs, teachers should evaluate factors, including participant characteristics and specific weaknesses in physical fitness, to ensure the selection of the most appropriate modality.

Limitations

The findings should be interpreted in light of the following limitations. First, the school-based setting inherently introduced practical constraints and environmental variability. This study failed to account for potential variations in participants’ lifestyle factors, including nutritional intake, growth, and sleep patterns. Nutritional intake has a crucial role in growth and development [75], potentially affecting the body composition change and muscular strength gain after RT in girls [76]. Second, this study included only healthy weight girls with relatively small sample size per group, restricting statistical power and generalizability to boys, overweight/obese populations. The sample size in each group was not large, which may have limited the statistical power for some outcomes. Third, outcome assessments focused primarily on physical fitness measures, omitting underlying physiological mechanisms (e.g., neuromuscular or endocrine adaptations) and psychological dimensions (e.g., motivation, self-esteem), thereby providing a narrow perspective on on physical and mental health in pubertal girls. Finally, the intervention was limited to 10 min sessions integrated within PE classes over an 8-week period. This brief duration per session, short intervention period and lack of long-term follow-up, limited the interpretation of the results. The relatively short duration of the intervention may also account for the absence of significant change in body composition.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that 10 min RT program integrated into PE curricula was an economical approach that improved physical fitness. The impact of three RT modalities on physical fitness in pubertal girls, indicating the distinct benefits of each approach. TST primarily focuses on enhancing maximal muscle strength and power, whereas PT predominantly seeks to enhance power, speed, and agility. A synergistic program integrating TST and PT may result in a more comprehensive enhancement of physical fitness.
