Acute temperature effects on cilia beating increase coral deoxygenation
Authors: Cesar O. Pacherres, Max S. Dhillon, Mads Bilbo, Mikkel Hansen, Douglas R. Brumley, Manuel Aranda, Soeren Ahmerkamp, Michael Kühl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cilia-induced vortical flows are critical for regulating oxygen (O2) and metabolite exchange across coral-water interfaces. While this active ventilation affects the coral tissue microenvironment, its role in thermal stress remains poorly understood. Using high-speed imaging of cilia beating, particle image velocimetry with O2-sensitive nanoparticles, and a mechanistic transport model, we quantified how ciliary dynamics in the reef-building coral Porites lutea respond to acute warming in darkness. Moderate warming (~35°C) enhanced ciliary activity and advective transport yet paradoxically thickened the concentration boundary layer with O2-depleted water, exposing tissues to transient hypoxia. At higher temperatures, ventilation failed to meet rising metabolic demands and anoxic regions expanded rapidly. Above ~37°C, ciliary coordination collapsed and vortical flows dissipated, shifting transport to a diffusion-limited regime accelerating coral mortality. These results identify ciliary beating as a key regulator of thermal tolerance and early indicator of critical physiological tipping points for reef-building corals in a warming, deoxygenating ocean.

INTRODUCTION

Tropical coral reefs are among the most important and diverse marine ecosystems (1). The complex three-dimensional structures of these reefs are built by scleractinian corals and harbor an intricate ecosystem comprising a plethora of species (2, 3). Through active coral calcification, these tiny colonial Cnidarians, which are composed of a thin veneer of tissue on and within a complex, calcium carbonate skeleton, construct the largest biogenic structures on Earth (4). Coral growth, and by extension the health of the entire coral reef ecosystem, relies on the well-functioning symbiotic interactions between the coral animal host, a suite of microorganims and viruses, and dinoflagellate microalgae of the family Symbiodiniaceae (5, 6). The photosymbionts are harbored in the coral’s gastrodermis, where they export byproducts of photosynthesis, i.e., carbohydrates and O2, to the host covering most of its energy demand to maintain metabolic functions, including respiration and calcification (7, 8).

However, global ocean warming (along with other more localized stressors, such as ocean deoxygenation and eutrophication), as a result of anthropogenic climate change, is increasingly harming coral reefs worldwide (9–11). With marine heatwaves growing in intensity and frequency, critical temperature thresholds are breached at a rate and scale hitherto unseen, leading to mass coral bleaching and mortality (12, 13). Temperature stress affects many vital processes in the coral holobiont but has mainly been studied in the context of coral bleaching, i.e., the breakdown of the symbiosis between the coral host and its photosynthetic endosymbionts, due to enhanced levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) or nutrient imbalance leading to a cascade of biochemical events disrupting the symbiosis (14). While strong microenvironmental changes in light and O2 have been demonstrated in bleaching corals (15, 16), much less is known about the extent to which corals are able to actively influence their microenvironment to mitigate environmental stress (17, 18).

Here, cilia present a potentially important stress alleviating mechanism. These 10- to 15-μm-long, flexible organelles are found extending from epidermal and endodermal cells in most scleractinian corals (19) and beat in a coordinated, wave-like pattern (20). This induces local advective flows and transport of mucus, which are essential in coral tissue cleaning and feeding, as they are capable of moving particles to and from the coral mouth opening (movie S1) (21–23). Primary cilia may also be found on the calcifying calicoblastic ectoderm, where they are thought to serve a sensory function (24). However, cilia can also play an important role for solute exchange at the coral tissue-water interface (20, 25, 26), where their coordinated beating can lead to formation of vortical, advective flows that locally enhance mass transfer above the passive diffusive exchange across the coral tissue-seawater interface. This combined action of diffusion and advection creates a thin concentration boundary layer (CBL) (27), which regulates solute exchange of corals with the surrounding seawater (28–31). However, it is still unclear how ciliary activity, and the cilia-induced mass transfer, respond to environmental changes, and whether this response can be coupled to the stress response (and ultimately survival) of corals experiencing, for example, deoxygenation events or marine heatwaves.

In this study, we used high-speed imaging with a microscope chamber to determine cilia beating frequencies (CBFs) and particle image velocimetry (PIV) in combination with chemical imaging (SensPIV) for mapping flow and O2 concentration fields (32) of corals exposed to increasing seawater temperature. Our data provide important insights into the ability of corals to modulate their immediate microenvironment under acute environmental stress and point to a hitherto overlooked yet important role of changing ciliary activity as part of coral stress responses to elevated seawater temperatures.

RESULTS

Temperature effects on cilia beating

Using a novel in vivo cilia observation chamber (Fig. 1A and fig. S1), we quantified the frequency of ciliary movement of corals exposed to a thermal ramping treatment and control conditions (see Materials and Methods for details). CBFs of the massive coral Porites lutea under control conditions (27°C) were 21.60 ± 0.82 Hz and remained relatively stable throughout the entire experimental period (900 min; Fig. 1B, blue line). During the thermal ramping treatment, CBFs increased with temperature, rising steadily from 27° to 37°C (0 to 660 min) with a similar pattern observed across all biological replicates (n = 3). At 27°C, CBFs were measured at 20.78 ± 1.35 Hz, after which they linearly increased by ~1 Hz/°C [R2 (coefficient of determination) = 0.87; Fig. 1, B and C] before reaching a maximum of 30.54 ± 3.14 Hz at 37°C, i.e., a Q10 (temperature coefficient) of 1.51 over the 27° to 37°C range.

Above the apparent temperature threshold of 37°C, the CBFs of all corals declined markedly between 38° and 40°C, with a simultaneous increase in the corresponding SDs (Fig. 1B). At 41°C, corals showed little to no sign of cilia movement, and the mortality rate reached 100%.

To better understand the anomalies in CBFs observed at elevated temperatures (Fig. 1B), we performed a detailed analysis of beating frequencies at distinct positions along the cilia layer—specifically at the base, middle, and tip of individual cilia (fig. S2). Between 27° and 35°C, the SDs from the mean frequency remained low, indicating a stable and coordinated beating pattern along the entire length of the cilia. However, at 37°C, this rhythmicity began to break down, as evidenced by increased frequency variability, particularly at the tip and middle, highlighting a loss of the regular whipping motion characteristic of cilia (Fig. 1D). At higher temperatures, the SD decreased again, but this reduction reflects the progressive collapse of ciliary activity rather than restored synchrony. In these conditions, many cilia exhibit strongly reduced or arrested motion, resulting in minimal displacement and therefore lower variability across the ciliary layer.

Vortical flow and O2 dynamics at increasing temperatures

To evaluate the influence of the thermally induced increase in CBFs on the hydrodynamics and O2 dynamics at the immediate surface of the coral, we used a PIV setup seeded with a combination of fluorescent PIV and SensPIV particles (see details in Materials and Methods and fig. S3). Particle trajectories revealed laminar conditions along the entire water column following the externally imposed flow, with cilia-produced vortices present at the surface of the coral at almost all temperatures but disappearing at around 39°C (fig. S4 and movie S2). The vortex length (measured in the direction of the averaged flow) was ~1000 to 1200 μm and remained consistent across most temperatures, while vortex height varied with temperature, ranging between 400 and 600 μm at 27°C, 800 and 900 μm at 32° and 35°C, and 600 to 700 μm at 37°C but absent altogether at 39°C.

Velocity vectors extracted along a curve parallel to, and ~400 μm above, the coral surface showed similar magnitudes along all evaluated temperatures, with maxima varying between 400 and 600 μm s−1. However, the vertical component of the velocity showed increasing values along the first stages of the thermal regime, i.e., up to 440 μm s−1 at 27°C, 499 μm s−1 at 32°C, and 601 μm s−1 at 35°C (Q10 of 1.48). At 37°C, the point at which we began to observe a breakdown in the rhythmicity of the cilia (Fig. 1B), the vertical component of velocity also began to decrease (with a maximum value of 400 μm s−1). At 39°C, the severely inhibited cilia stopped producing vortices, leading to a vertical velocity close to 0 (Fig. 2).

The O2 distribution over the coral-water interface revealed the presence of a concentration boundary layer (CBL), with low O2 (values below 50% air saturation) concentrations observed at the coral surface (Fig. 2). The extent to which these low O2 zones reach into the water was influenced by the strength of the cilia-generated currents and reached 1000 μm into the bulk water column at 27°C and ~1200 μm between 32° and 35°C. However, at 37°C, the approximate thermal threshold for the onset of ciliary malfunction, the CBL is reduced to ~1000-μm thickness. Similarly, at 39°C, as ciliary movement continued to decrease, the CBL continued to shrink (600 μm) (Figs. 2 and 3A). Extracted O2 concentration profiles across the CBL above the coral surface (dashed line in Fig. 3C) revealed strong temperature-dependent patterns (Fig. 3A). At 27°C, O2 concentrations within the CBL reached ~25% air saturation near the coral surface and increased steadily with height above the tissue. With rising temperatures (32° to 37°C) and following the increase in CBF, O2 concentrations were influenced by ciliary currents with deoxygenated waters reaching the upper layers of the CBL, resulting in the accumulation of low-O2 water within the CBL. At 39°C, O2 concentrations increased again throughout the CBL, coinciding with the cessation of ciliary beating and a reduction in CBL thickness, which likely enhanced diffusive exchange with the surrounding water. We further explored the accumulation of low-O2 water inside the CBL by extracting the mean O2 concentration within the entire CBL above the coral surface (Fig. 3D).

Extracting values for vertical velocity and O2 concentration at the coral surface (see dashed line in Fig. 3C), we calculated the mass transport by advection and diffusion (Fig. 3B). At 27°C, the net advective flux normal to the coral surface averaged was 0.8 nmol O2 cm−2 s−1, a number that increased to 1.4 nmol O2 cm−2 s−1 at 32°C and decreased in the following temperature increments, being 1.1 nmol O2 cm−2 s−1 at 35°C, 0.7 nmol O2 cm−2 s−1 at 37°C, and −0.04 nmol O2 cm−2 s−1 at 39°C. In contrast to the net advective flux, the net diffusive flux (averaged along the coral surface) fluctuated on an increasing trend, with an initial value of 0.006 nmol O2 cm−2 s−1 at 27°C, 0.004 nmol O2 cm−2 s−1 at 32°C, 0.007 nmol O2 cm−2 s−1 at 35°C, 0.006 nmol O2 cm−2 s−1 at 37°C, and increasing to 0.015 nmol O2 cm−2 s−1 at 39°C (Fig. 3E).

The ratio of advection over diffusion can also be obtained using the nondimensional Sherwood number Sh = Jadv/Jdiff, where Jadv and Jdiff are the average net advective and diffusive fluxes previously calculated. Across the range of temperatures studied, Sh was calculated to be ~137, 366, 152, 128, and 3 at 27°, 32°, 35°, 37°, and 39°C, respectively, with advection contributing more than 99% of the overall mass transport at all temperatures except 39°C, where its contribution decreased to 75%.

Transport between coral tissue, CBL, and surrounding water

To mechanistically investigate the reciprocal coupling between ciliary transport and metabolic activity of coral tissue, we used a simplified two-dimensional multiphysics model. Here, O2 consumption within the tissue was prescribed as a volumetric rate based on previously measured values (28) and scaled with temperature using a Q10 value of 3 (fig. S5) for the increase in respiration rate from 27° to 37°C (from 3.3 μmol cm−3 hour−1 to 9.9 μmol cm−3 hour−1). O2 consumption was implemented using Michaelis-Menten kinetics with a half-saturation constant (Km). The half-saturation coefficient for oxygen (O2) uptake by coral tissue is not routinely constrained experimentally and is therefore poorly defined. Reported or plausible values may span from the intrinsic mitochondrial Km on the order of ~0.1 μM to apparent values of several micromolar (up to ~5 μM), which implicitly integrate microscale transport limitations, cellular aggregation, and physiological heterogeneity within the coral tissue (33, 34). To account for this uncertainty, we explored a broader range of plausible half-saturation constants in our model. The inflow velocity was set to 1 mm s−1 to match experimental conditions. All physical parameters—including the molecular diffusion coefficient of O2, the O2 concentration in air-saturated seawater, and the kinematic viscosity of seawater—were taken from temperature-dependent empirical data (see table S1). Ciliary flow velocities were linked to temperature by assuming a linear relationship between beating frequency and flow speed, as derived from the data shown in Fig. 1C. Together, these assumptions allow the model to be parameterized solely by temperature, enabling simulations of temperature-induced variability.

To validate the model, simulations were performed under the same conditions as in Fig. 2 [A (27°C) and C (35°C)], serving as reference cases between which ciliary flow velocities increased substantially, while ciliary coordination remained unaffected (Fig. 1Eb). Model predictions and experimental measurements show good qualitative and quantitative agreement for both test cases. The simulated ciliary flows reproduced the observed flow geometry, with a lateral extent and vertical height of ~1 mm. Modeled ciliary flow magnitude increased from ~400 μm s−1 to ~600 μm s−1. The simulated O2 distribution follows the structure of the ciliary flows and is relatively homogeneous within the vortices with sharp gradients toward the ambient water column (Fig. 4 and fig. S6).

The model further enables a direct comparison of tissue O2 concentrations between cases with active and deactivated ciliary flows. In the absence of ciliary flow, tissue O2 concentrations were reduced by ~26% relative to flow-active conditions; however, in both cases, concentrations remained well above 50 μM (Fig. 4, A and B). When temperature increased to 35°C, O2 levels in both the tissue and the CBL declined sharply. Under these conditions, active ciliary flows increased tissue O2 concentrations by ~65% compared to the diffusion-only case, demonstrating their ventilatory effect (fig. S6) (20, 25, 30). In the purely diffusive system, average O2 concentrations were already close to anoxia, i.e., below 5 μM (35, 36). Variations in the assumed Km values introduce only a modest sensitivity in the model output, with resulting differences not exceeding ~25%.

To assess the metabolic consequences of O2 limitation for coral tissue and symbionts, we quantified the anoxic area within the tissue using the model framework. In the diffusion-only scenario, ~15% of the tissue layer became anoxic at 35°C, whereas with active ciliary flows, O2 was maintained (substantially above 5 μM) throughout almost the entire tissue layer. We subsequently increased temperature incrementally to examine changes in tissue O2 concentrations. Ciliary flows enhanced O2 levels within the tissue up to temperatures of ~36° to 37°C. Beyond this threshold, however, anoxic regions began to form irrespective of whether ciliary flows were active (Fig. 4C). The temperature threshold at which anoxic microenvironments form varies in dependence of the Km values by ~±3°C, but the trends are similar for cases with ciliary flows and diffusion. To account for uncertainty in the temperature sensitivity of respiration, we explored a range of plausible metabolic temperature sensitivities (Q10 = 2 to 3) in the model. Across this range, temperature-driven increases in respiration consistently outpaced the capacity of ciliary flows to supply O2, leading to the emergence of anoxic microenvironments once a critical temperature was exceeded. The predicted threshold for the onset of anoxia varied from ~34°C for Q10 = 3 to ~38°C for Q10 = 2 (fig. S5). These results indicate that while the qualitative outcome of the model is robust, the precise temperature at which tissue anoxia develops depends strongly on the temperature sensitivity of respiration.

DISCUSSION

We demonstrate that coral cilia dynamics are profoundly sensitive to thermal stress, with cascading consequences for the exchange of solutes and gases across the coral’s CBL and, consequently, its internal tissue microenvironment. By combining measurements of ciliary beating frequencies, flow fields, and O2 distributions with a mechanistic transport model, we show that moderate warming transiently enhances ciliary-driven ventilation and mass transfer. However, experimental and model results jointly demonstrate that this ventilation benefit is short-lived with an increasing exposure to deoxygenated seawater in the CBL until a critical thermal threshold, above which ciliary rhythmicity collapses, vortical flow structures dissipate, and transport transitions from an advection-dominated regime to a more diffusion-controlled exchange. This rapid sequence of changes exposes corals to an increasingly unstable microenvironment that exacerbates physiological stress and seemingly represents an irreversible pathway to mortality.

We found that the dynamics of ciliary transport are closely linked to the spatial scale of the vortices generated by the coral. As the CBF increased with temperature, the resulting vortices became larger and more vigorous, amplifying both the rate and spatial extent of local flow. This temperature-dependent scaling highlights ciliary hydrodynamics as a finely tuned mechanism capable of modulating mass transport across the coral tissue-water interface. However, the overall pattern of mass transport revealed a more complex and unexpected response to warming. In our experiments conducted under dark conditions with coral respiration consuming O2, moderate warming initially stimulated ciliary activity and enhanced advective O2 transport across the CBL. This transient increase in exchange efficiency likely extended the functional reach of coral microenvironments (30), suggesting that ciliary motion can temporarily buffer corals against mild environmental stress. This benefit, however, appears short-lived.

As temperatures continued to climb and metabolic rates accelerated (37), advective fluxes began to decline, indicating that the same mechanism that initially promoted O2 delivery became progressively less effective at renewing O2 near the tissue surface. Under these warmer, dark conditions, ciliary flows redistribute deoxygenated water within the CBL, thickening the layer with a hypoxic microenvironment and weakening the coral’s capacity to sustain a stable O2 supply. The model reveals that this decline is not only solely driven by transport failure but also by feedback, in which enhanced ventilation sustains elevated respiration, accelerating local O2 depletion within the CBL and tissue. As a result, the ciliary mechanism that initially enhanced O2 exchange began to work against the coral, trapping low-O2 water close to the tissue surface and intensifying hypoxic stress. Beyond this threshold, the model identifies a critical shift: Ciliary flows, while continuing to enhance transport, sustain elevated metabolic rates that rapidly deplete local O2, driving an accelerated expansion of anoxic tissue. Consistent with experimental evidence that hypoxia lowers thermal tolerance and activates cellular hypoxia responses before bleaching (38), these results reveal a microenvironmental feedback linking enhanced transport, metabolic escalation, and O2 collapse. This reversal illustrates the dual nature of ciliary transport, a process that supports physiological stability under moderate warming but becomes detrimental as O2 demand outpaces supply from the upper water column under more extreme conditions.

While our study focused on coral metabolism in darkness, we speculate that similar mechanisms linked to cilia-driven mass transfer and O2 dynamics in the CBL may also be important for light-exposed corals. During daytime, when photosynthetic O2 production by symbionts exceeds respiratory demand, the coral microenvironment can shift from hypoxic to hyperoxic conditions (28). Under high irradiance and elevated temperatures, O2 concentrations at the tissue surface may reach several times air saturation, particularly within the thickened CBL, where ciliary mixing redistributes but does not efficiently remove excess O2, leading to localized hyperoxic microzones and steep O2 gradients (25). While increased O2 availability can transiently support aerobic metabolism by alleviating O2 limitation in the tissue (28, 39), its physiological effects are strongly context dependent. Access to external O2 has been shown to enhance thermal tolerance when symbiont O2 production declines, especially under low-flow conditions where diffusion from the bulk water becomes critical (40). Consistent with this, our model demonstrates that ciliary transport not only enhances tissue oxygenation under moderate stress but also shows that this benefit depends on the balance between metabolic demand and transport capacity. Nevertheless, when O2 levels remain elevated near the tissue surface, particularly at high temperatures, ROS production can increase (41, 42), especially as antioxidant capacity becomes compromised and metabolic rates rise (43, 44). The combined effects of O2 supersaturation and thermal stress may therefore exacerbate oxidative damage in both host and symbiont cells, destabilizing photosynthesis and accelerating symbiosis breakdown. These strong diel fluctuations between nighttime hypoxia and daytime hyperoxia reveal that ciliary transport does not simply buffer the coral microenvironment but can also amplify metabolic stress depending on how O2 supply and demand are balanced.

As these microenvironmental O2 fluctuations intensify under warming, they impose strong constraints on coral physiology. Low O2 within the CBL has profound implications for coral physiology (45). Host respiration requires continuous O2 delivery to sustain energy production, and even moderate reductions can constrain energy-demanding processes such as ion regulation and protein synthesis (46). Moreover, low O2 availability can lead to the production and accumulation of reactive O2 species (47), compounding oxidative damage that can induce bleaching (48). Prolonged CBL hypoxia may also impede proton removal from the calcifying space, disrupting pH homeostasis and impairing skeleton deposition (49). Rather than acting in isolation, these O2-driven constraints interact with thermal stress to push corals toward critical physiological limits, setting the stage for the abrupt failure of ciliary regulation observed at higher temperatures.

At a temperature of ~37°C and above, ciliary coordination started to break down, eventually leading to cessation of vortical mixing. This led to a sharp decline in advective O2 transport and thinning of the CBL, whereafter O2 transport became primarily driven by diffusion across a thin water layer similar to the diffusive boundary layer observed around nonciliated tissue, impermeable sediments, and biofilms (50). The reduced CBL thickness led to increased diffusive exchange, but this compensation came after the coral had lost most of its ciliary buffering capacity and was approaching lethal stress. This abrupt loss of ciliary function coincides with thermal thresholds that commonly trigger bleaching and mortality in the field (51, 52). While coral bleaching and mortality have classically been attributed to the breakdown of the coral-algal symbiosis under oxidative stress and/or nutrient imbalance (14), our findings suggest an additional pathway of vulnerability: a physiological cascade, where impaired ciliary movement leads to a collapse in vortices and vortical mixing, resulting in reduced transport of O2, destabilization of tissue homeostasis, and, ultimately, mortality.

These dynamics carry particular weight in the context of ocean deoxygenation and the buildup of hypoxic zones (defined as ≤40% air saturation, ≤2 mg O2 liter−1 or ≤61 μmol O2 liter−1) (35, 36); a stressor that is intensifying globally due to climate change and coastal eutrophication (11). Deoxygenation events are already occurring with greater frequency and severity in reef systems globally (36), and our results highlight how warming and hypoxia may interact to amplify coral stress. Under normoxic conditions, ciliary activity sustains advective transport creating dynamic flow patterns that draw fluid parcels from beyond the CBL into close proximity of the coral tissue surface (20, 25, 30). However, our data show that warming-induced enhancement of CBF and vortical flows paradoxically thickens the CBL with low-O2 water during dark periods, such that tissues experience hypoxic stress even when bulk seawater remains well-oxygenated. When this warming-driven hypoxic stress is superimposed on declining ambient O2 levels in the bulk seawater, tissue-level O2 limitation may occur long before bulk concentrations reach critical thresholds.

At higher temperatures, when ciliary coordination fails and mass transport shifts to a diffusion-dominated state, corals in deoxygenated waters would experience a dramatic sudden O2 deficit, potentially forcing tissues into anaerobic metabolism and triggering cellular-level hypoxia responses (53, 54). This projected cooccurrence of marine heatwaves and microenvironmental deoxygenation events therefore represents a particularly dangerous synergistic interaction, where corals lose their ability to buffer against hypoxic stress precisely when O2 availability is lowest. These results underscore the importance of considering microenvironmental O2 dynamics when assessing coral vulnerability to climate-driven deoxygenation and suggest that species or morphologies with more robust ciliary function may be more resilient under combined warming and hypoxic stress. Further research into species-specific variations in CBF and their response to environmental stress will be needed to assess this hypothesis.

The consistent collapse of ciliary transport across replicates reinforces the robustness of the observed response and supports the mechanistic conclusions drawn from these experiments. We note that our experimental setup, based on a one-way temperature ramp, may not capture acclimatory or recovery responses that could occur under natural, fluctuating thermal regimes. This approach may therefore accentuate the abruptness of observed physiological thresholds. However, these rapid temperature increases have been observed on reefs over similar timescales during rapid and extreme fluctuations in sea surface temperature during severe marine heatwaves (51, 55, 56) and aligns with the thermal regime used in other experimental studies (40, 57). Flow velocities were selected to represent low-flow conditions commonly experienced by corals in sheltered reef environments (58, 59), where laminar flow can dominate over millimeter-to-centimeter scales. Under these conditions, cilia-generated flows play a primary role in regulating transport at the immediate tissue-water interface and are not overwhelmed by ambient turbulence. While higher-flow regimes can thin the CBL and enhance bulk exchange (60, 61), they do not eliminate the near-surface microenvironment shaped by ciliary activity (25, 62). Our experimental conditions thus provide a realistic and conservative framework for isolating cilia-driven transport processes that are likely most relevant during calm and warm periods, conditions under which corals are particularly vulnerable to thermal and O2 stress (63, 64).

An important source of uncertainty in this framework relates to the temperature sensitivity of metabolic processes within the coral holobiont. While ciliary transport increased with temperature with a relatively modest Q10 (~1.5) in our measurements, the temperature dependence of metabolic O2 consumption is less well constrained. In our modeling approach, the metabolic Q10 represents the temperature dependence of the cellular dark respiration of the coral tissue. These temperature sensitivities arise from the activation energies of enzymatic reactions governing cellular respiration and following Arrhenius-type relationships, whereby increasing temperature accelerates reaction rates as a larger fraction of enzyme-substrate interactions surpass the activation energy barrier. These activation energies typically fall in the range of ~50 to 75 kJ mol−1, corresponding to Q10 values of ~2 to 3. In agreement, while coral respiration mostly falls within the same range (Q10 = 2 to 3) (65), reported values span an extremely wide range, reflecting both biological variability and methodological limitations. Most available estimates are derived from bulk O2 flux measurements at the colony scale (66), which integrate the metabolic activity of the coral host, algal symbionts, and associated microbial communities that influenced by transport limitations and microscale gradients within tissues and CBLs, which can obscure the intrinsic temperature responses of the underlying biochemical processes. As a result, current estimates likely represent emergent holobiont-scale responses rather than the temperature sensitivities of individual metabolic pathways. The temperature dependence of photosynthesis within Symbiodiniaceae is similarly complex, as different symbiont lineages exhibit markedly different thermal sensitivities associated with variations in photophysiology and photosystem stability (67), light-enhanced respiration (28), and different levels of energetic coupling between photosynthesis and respiration (68). Resolving temperature responses across multiple biological scales spanning from enzymatic reactions and cellular metabolism to tissue- and colony-scale fluxes thus remains an important challenge for understanding how O2 production and consumption scale with temperature in coral microenvironments.

Although our study focused on microenvironment-level processes, we argue that the implications can extend to larger spatial scales. At the colony level, impaired ciliary function may reduce O2 exchange across large surface areas, creating localized deoxygenated zones within dense colonies or sheltered lagoons, especially under warm, calm-flow conditions (58, 59). As deoxygenation propagates across colonies due to water currents, community-scale respiration, calcification, and primary productivity may decline (45). At the reef scale, cumulative reductions in solute and gaseous exchange could exacerbate nighttime or event-driven O2 minima, tipping coral reef ecosystems toward more frequent hypoxia-induced mortality events (69). Because ciliary activity and CBL structure/dynamics may vary among species and morphologies (31), climate stressors may also reshape community composition, favoring corals with microenvironments less prone to deoxygenation and exposing tissues to hypoxic conditions. We speculate that this variation in cilia-driven transport and CBL characteristics may be an underlying cause for the observed variability in oxyregulatory capacity among corals (70, 71) and the observed heterogeneity in bleaching patterns on coral reefs (72). Thus, processes unfolding at the micrometer scale may have ecosystem-level consequences for reef resistance, resilience, and biogeochemical cycling.

In summary, our findings identify ciliary function as both a critical regulator of solute exchange and a determinant of coral resistance and resilience under ocean warming. By showing how ciliary transport first enhances and then undermines O2 availability during warming, we reveal a hitherto unknown physiological tipping point in coral-environment interactions. The loss of ciliary rhythmicity emerges as a potential early, definitive indicator of acute stress, preceding or amplifying the onset of bleaching and/or mortality. More broadly, this work emphasizes the need to integrate tissue-level mechanisms such as cilia beating and CBL processes into coral stress ecology, thereby linking microenvironmental regulation to holobiont physiology and reef-scale resistance and resilience in a rapidly changing ocean.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Coral fragments

Colonies of the massive coral P. lutea were obtained from Dejong Marine Life (Netherlands). For each experiment, small colonies (ca. 2 cm by 1 cm by 1 cm) were created by fragmentation of larger colonies. All fragments used for experiments were allowed to heal for a minimum of 2 weeks in the same tanks, and under conditions, they were previously growing in (12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle; photon irradiance, 400 to 700 nm, of 150-μmol photons m−2 s−1; 26°C; fed with Artemia nauplii once a week). For all experiments, water from the culturing tanks was filtered with a pore size filter of 0.2 μm (Nalgene Rapid-Flow 75-mm bottle top filter, 500 ml; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and autoclaved (SX-500 Lab Autoclave, Tomy, USA) before use in the experimental setups. All experiments were conducted under dark conditions.

In vivo cilia observation chamber

An in vivo cilia observation chamber (52-mm diameter by 17.4-mm high) (Fig. 1A and fig. S7) was engineered and three dimensionally (3D) printed in tough polylactic acid (T-PLA) using fused filament fabrication (UltiMaker S7 Pro, UltiMaker, Netherlands). A small flow-diffusing T-PLA plate was fitted at the water inlet to achieve a dispersed flow around the chamber and avoid direct exposure of the coral to incoming water. The chamber bottom was sealed with a Ø 30-mm round cover glass slide (VWR, Denmark), enabling illumination from below.

The chamber elements were designed using CAD software (SOLIDWORKS, USA). 3D models were exported as STereoLithography (.stl) files and used to generate G-code in Cura 5.11 (Ultimaker) for fused deposition modeling (FDM) printing (see data availability for details on access to STL files). All models were printed with Tough PLA [and natural polyvinyl alcohol ( PVA) as removable support material] at 100% grid infill with a layer height of 0.06 mm using a 3D printer (ULTIMAKER S5 with materials station, Ultimaker, The Netherlands). T-PLA and natural PVA filaments were sourced from 3D Eksperten (https://3deksperten.dk). For all chamber elements, printing was completed with a 6-mm brim on the bottom to ensure the print remained flat. To minimize warping, the print was not removed from the heated print bed until both had cooled to room temperature. After printing, all prints were washed for 2 hours in fresh deionized water to remove the support material. For assembly, a Ø 30-mm glass coverslip (VWR, Denmark) was glued to the inner bottom ring of the chamber using silicone glue (DuPont, Germany). The same silicone glue was used to fit the diffuser element to the inlet of the chamber. The glue was allowed to be fully cured for 24 hours at ambient temperature.

The chamber was mounted on an upright compound microscope (Olympus BX50WI, Olympus, Japan) with a 40× water dipping objective (LUMPlanFI; Olympus, Japan). Cilia movement was captured via a high-speed camera (Chronos 1.4, Kron Technologies, Canada) that was attached to the microscope and recorded high-speed videos (1000 fps). A stable flow of seawater (10 ml min−1) through the chamber was facilitated by a pump (MasterFlex Ismatec, Germany), which was connected to the chamber and a reservoir of aerated and thermostated seawater via tubing (AGN00012 Versilon, Saint Gobain, France; inner tube Ø 5 mm) (fig. S1). An air pump (Air100, EHeim GmbH & Co. KG, Germany) was used to maintain the seawater at air-saturated O2 levels, while seawater temperature was regulated by placing the reservoir on a magnetic stirrer/heating plate (RCT Basic, IKA-Werke GmbH & Co.KG, Germany), which was equipped with contact thermometer and temperature controller (ETS-D4 fuzzy, IKA-Werke GmbH & Co.KG, Germany). Both O2 and temperature in the circulating seawater were constantly monitored with optical flow through cells (PyroScience GmbH, Germany), which were placed in line with the tubing before the chamber inlet and connected to a four-channel fiber-optic multianalyte meter (FireSting Pro, FSPRO-4, PyroScience Gmbh, Germany) that was interfaced to a PC running the PyroWorkbench software (fig. S1).

In vivo flow-through observation chamber

We optimized the 3D printed laminar flow chamber (T-PLA thermoplastic) described by Pacherres et al. (62), by carving it in aluminum via Computer Numerical Control (CNC) machining (Haas UMC 750, Haas Automation Inc. USA) (see the Supplementary Materials and fig. S8) before fitting it with a microscope glass slide (76 by 26 mm) to enable the observation of the coral and particles in the flowing seawater from the side (fig. S3). The toolpaths were generated using EdgeCam (Hexagon AB, Sweden), ensuring optimal efficiency and accuracy in the machining process. Once built, the surface of the raw aluminum chamber was anodized with a black matte finish (see data availability for details on access to STL files).

For all experiments using the flow-through chamber, a constant seawater flow (10 ml min−1) was provided by connecting it to a nonpulsating pump (MasterFlex Ismatec, Germany). Similarly to the cilia observation chamber, the O2 and temperature of the circulating seawater within the setup were regulated via an external reservoir (fig. S3).

To resolve the flow and O2 distribution over the coral colony at increasing seawater temperatures, the chamber was filled with seawater and seeded with a combination of small fluorescent particles (5 μm; Applied Microspheres GmbH, Germany; excitation: 468 nm, emission: 530 nm) and O2-sensitive luminescent nanoparticles (SensPIV) (PtTFTPP with Macrolex Yellow, on 500-nm polydimethylsiloxane nanoparticles) [see details in (30, 32, 62)]. To generate a clear and visible map of the flow field and O2 dynamics created by the coral’s cilia movement, the fluorescent particle density was ~1 × 106 particles liter−1 (25, 62), while SensPIV particles were added at a dilution of 1:55 from a stock solution of 3.5 mg ml−1.

The coral was illuminated vertically from above with a light sheet from a laser diode (460 nm; LD-PS, OPTOLUTION, Germany) while observing the coral sample from the side via the chamber glass wall using a camera (Grasshopper3, FLIR, USA) attached to a long-distance microscope (K2 DistaMAX, INFINITY, USA). Image acquisition was controlled in Python following the frame-straddling technique for lifetime-proportional luminescence imaging of O2 (73).

Experimental design

Temperature effects on cilia beating

To observe the influence of increasing temperature on CBFs of the massive coral P. lutea, water temperature in the microscope chamber was stepwise increased using the heating plate of the magnetic stirrer and by adjusting the set temperature of the connected contact thermostat. Before each experiment, the coral fragment was placed in the cilia observation chamber for 30 min to allow time for acclimation to the new conditions in darkness. After the acclimation period, the temperature inside the chamber was increased by 1°C every 30 min and kept at that temperature for another 30 min before recording a sequence of ~1500 images at 1000 fps with the high-speed camera. The initial temperature was set at 27°C, and experiments concluded at 41°C. The illumination for the image acquisition was provided by the microscope light (photon irradiance, 400 to 700 nm, ~1000 to 2000 μmol photons m−2 s−1), which was briefly turned on (~5 s) for image capture. A total of three coral colonies were subjected to the experimental treatment; we performed three to four technical replicates of CBF determination for each specimen. Each temperature ramp-up experiment lasted for about 14 to 15 hours. In addition, control experiments were performed, in which the coral was kept at 27°C for 15 hours with images being taken at intervals of 60 min, mimicking the duration of the temperature ramp-up experiment. This was done to rule out any influence the time spent inside the chamber might have had on the CBF.

Temperature effects on vortex formation and O2 dynamics

To resolve the flow and O2 distribution over the coral colony at increasing seawater temperatures, the temperature ramp-up was conducted in the same manner as in the CBF experiment, i.e., 30-min increments of 1°C followed by 30 min under each of the specific temperature steps starting at 27°C and ending at 41°C. By the end of each temperature step, a series of 200 images were acquired at 27 fps. All experiments were conducted under dark conditions.

Mathematical model

Numerical simulations were performed in COMSOL (COMSOL Multiphysics, v6.2). The flow field satisfies the steady Navier-Stokes equationsρ(T)(u·∇)u=−∇p+μ(T)∇2u(1)where u is the fluid velocity vector, p the pressure, μ(T) the temperature-dependent dynamic viscosity of seawater with T the temperature in °C, ∇ is the gradient operator, and ρ(T) the temperature-dependent density of the fluid. The continuity equation for incompressible fluids reads∇·u=0(2)

The O2 distribution was calculated by solving the steady advection-diffusion-reaction equation0=D(T)∇2C−u·∇C−R(T)(3)where R(T) is the volumetric O2 consumption rate and D(T) is the temperature-dependent diffusion coefficient for O2 in seawater. The computational domain was divided into two subdomains. The lower subdomain, with a thickness of 100 μm, represents the coral tissue layer including the endosymbiotic Symbiodiniaceae, while the overlying subdomain has a thickness of 2 mm. The width of the domain was set to 120 mm. The left boundary was defined as an inflow boundary, and the right boundary was defined as an outflow boundary, i.e., zero diffusive flux but nonzero advective flux. The background flow of ~1 mm s−1 was induced by a pressure gradient of ∇p = 0.001 Pa/m. In the coral tissue, the velocity is set to zero, i.e., exchange only occurs through diffusion. According to the model proposed by Shapiro et al. (20), the cilia-induced currents were simulated assuming spatially varying horizontal velocity components at the tissue surface (slip boundary condition), u(x,z) = (ux,uz), where boundary conditions at z = 0ux(x,0)=cvel(T)·sin[(2π/δ)x](4)where x is the horizontal distance, cvel denotes the temperature dependent cilia beating velocity, and δ is the characteristic length scale of the vortices (see table S1). In the model, temperature was varied to modify the parameterization; however, it was assumed to be spatially uniform throughout the computational domain.

Although dynamic viscosity, density, O2 solubility, and diffusion coefficients are intrinsic thermodynamic properties of seawater and independent of coral metabolism, we adopted the temperature dependence as a Q10 formulation [RT = R(Tref) · Q10[(T − Tref)/10]; see fig. S9]. This parameterization does not imply biological regulation of these properties but instead provides a convenient and transparent scaling framework that allows direct comparison with the temperature sensitivities typically used to describe coral metabolic and physiological processes.

Data analysis

Determination of CBF

The captured videos were converted to image sequences. To further process the image sequence, kymographs of cilia motility were generated from each movie in ImageJ by using the line tool to draw a line through the base of the cilia layer (Fig. 1Ea). The kymograph showed the variations over time of the gray scale values of each pixel along a defined line in an image sequence. This kymograph was then analyzed using a custom-made algorithm (see Data, code, and materials availability section for details on access to code) in MATLAB (MathWorks, R2024b). The kymograph was subdivided into 10-μm subsections (fig. S2 bracketed line), from which the CBF signal was calculated. To extract the CBF, we calculated the correlation function for each pixel location, as a function of the time lag, Δt. The ciliary beating period was determined by identifying the first nonzero value of Δt corresponding to a local maximum in the correlation function (fig. S10). To analyze variations in beating frequency along the length of the cilium with increasing temperature, three kymographs were extracted from each video sequence at distinct positions across the cilia layer, i.e., the tip, middle, and base (see fig. S2 orange, blue, and black lines). Mean values and SDs were then calculated for each kymograph. All graphs were plotted in RStudio (R Core Team 2024) against their respective temperature treatments.

SensPIV-velocity data

Image sequences were processed using PIVlab (v3.01) (74) in MATLAB (MathWorks, R2024b). To obtain the velocity field, three interrogation areas were used (200, 100, and 50 pixel) with a 50% overlap each. The PIV output was then postprocessed to obtain the velocity magnitude and vertical velocity component.

SensPIV-O2 data

Each image sequence was analyzed using MATLAB to obtain a color-coded 2D map of O2 concentration at each temperature step following the protocol described in (73). The calibration of the O2-dependent particle luminescence intensity and lifetime was performed in advance by capturing images of the suspended particles in the flow chamber under light sheet illumination at defined O2 concentrations and temperature, as measured with the same optical flow-through sensors for temperature and O2 (TPFlow and OXFlow, Pyroscience GmbH, Germany) and reader (Firesting-Pro, Pyroscience GmbH, Germany). The sensor was calibrated linearly from measurements in O2-free (sodium sulfide) and air-saturated seawater at known salinity and temperature.

On the basis of the measured velocity fields and O2 concentration maps, the advective (Jadv) and diffusive (Jdiff) O2 fluxes were calculated along a line ~400 μm above the coral tissue and 20 μm in thickness (see example in fig. S11).

The advective flux is a vector, defined as the product of the local velocity vector (u) and the O2 concentration (C)Jadv=u·C

Here, we considered only the component normal to the coral surface, i.e., the vertical (z) component of the advective fluxJadv,z=uz·Cwhere uz is the velocity perpendicular to the coral surface.

The diffusive flux was similarly treated as a vector, parallel to the local concentration gradientJdiff=−D∇Cand we evaluated only its normal component,Jadv,z=−D∂C/∂z

The vertical concentration gradient ∂C/∂z was approximated by a finite difference, ΔC/Δz, using concentration values spaced by Δz = 20 μm. The molecular diffusion coefficient D was adjusted according to the experimental temperature (75).
