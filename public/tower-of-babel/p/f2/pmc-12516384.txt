Open management of massive venous bleeding in anterior lumbar spine surgery—a narrative review
Authors: Charlie R Faulks, Gregory M Malham, William P Bradley, Matthew H Claydon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveAnterior lumbar spine surgery (ALSS) provides multiple benefits for the patient with spinal pathology. Vascular complications are uncommon and usually managed with simple surgical techniques and ancillary products. Rarely, confronting massive haemorrhage can occur after a venous injury which endangers the patient. We aimed to review the anatomical basis and risk factors for venous injury, propose a staged, systematic approach for management and discuss open surgical techniques, instruments, products, and strategies in detail for each of the stages.MethodsThis was a narrative review. A systematic approach was used. A review of national databases (PubMed, Ovid Medline, and Google Scholar) was performed using literature from 2000 to 2024 in English. Keywords included terms “anterior”, “lumbar”, “spine”, “haemorrhage”, “venous injury”, “vascular”, “repair”, “ligation”, “damage control”, and “venous thromboembolism”. Studies that aimed to describe the anatomy, risk factors, incidence, surgical techniques, complications, clinical and radiological outcomes of ALSS were included. Other generalised searches, not included in the initial systematic search, were conducted and included in our narrative review.Key Content and FindingsThis review considers the relevant anatomy, risk factors, patient work-up, lists possibly useful instruments and consumables, the broad stages of open management, specific operative strategies and techniques, and the post-operative management of the patient.ConclusionsMassive venous injury (MVI) in ALSS is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication. Multiple open surgical techniques can be employed to control and manage the injury(s). The surgical and anaesthetic teams should have formulated a unified contingency plan against the eventuality of massive venous haemorrhage. The best outcome is only achieved with a team approach to the situation, recruitment of the necessary personnel and the deployment of the necessary equipment to enable the open management.Keywords: Venous injury, lumbar spine surgery, haemorrhage, vascular repair, anterior

Introduction

Anterior lumbar spine surgery (ALSS) is increasingly used in contemporary spinal practice (1,2).

Complications are rare after anterior exposure of the lumbar spine and vascular injuries (venous and arterial) are the most acute (3,4). Of the vascular injuries, 70% are venous injuries (3-6).

Large volume haemorrhage can create a potentially lethal spiral. The hypovolaemia leads to a triad of acidosis, hypothermia, and coagulopathy (7). Hypoperfusion and hypoxic injury to the microcirculation causes activation of the endothelium and a systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) (8). The coagulopathy results from tissue injury, consumption of clotting factors, and a dilutional component (7). SIRS can result in multiple-organ failure (8). Acidosis and hypothermia can contribute to the coagulopathy (7,9).

We aimed to review the anatomical basis and risk factors for venous injury, propose a staged, systematic approach for the management of massive venous injury (MVI). We review in detail the open surgical techniques/strategies for control and management of venous haemorrhage including instruments, products and strategies.

We propose categories of venous injury, including MVI and present a possible algorithm for its management. This article is the first of two in this issue discussing management of venous injury causing massive haemorrhage. This article focusses open surgical management, and we discuss endovascular management in its companion article—endovascular management of massive venous bleeding in ALSS: a systematic narrative review. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-25-7/rc).

Methods

This was a narrative review. A systematic approach was used (Table 1). A review of national databases (PubMed, Ovid Medline, and Google Scholar) was performed using literature from 2000 to 2024 in English. Keywords included terms “anterior”, “lumbar”, “spine”, “haemorrhage”, “venous injury”, “vascular”, “repair”, “ligation”, “damage control”, and “venous thromboembolism”. Two independent reviewers conducted title and abstract screening using an inclusion criterion (as below). The full text of all selected articles was screened for eligibility.

Venous injury

We define three categories of venous injury, informed by the literature (10-13) (see Table 2). A minor venous injury as defined by Brau et al. is a venous injury ≤4 mm in length which may not require a formal repair rather controlled with adjunctive measures. If a surgical repair is required, it is in the form of a simple lateral suture (10). A major venous injury is one which is >4 mm in length requiring a formal repair (14). We define an MVI as one in which the blood loss mandates the activation of the institution’s massive transfusion protocol (usually >1,000 mL) (11). An MVI requires anaesthetic strategies to avoid and correct patient haemodynamic instability and physiological change, and an ordered approach for surgical management.

Incidence

The reported incidence of vascular injury in ALSS ranges widely (1.9–26%) (15), in part due to the non-standardised definition of vascular injury. A major vein laceration requiring repair is reported with a range of 1.4–4.3% (10,16). The highest incidence of venous injury in ALSS occurs at the L4/5 level, with up to 85% of injuries occurring at this level (17).

The left common iliac vein (CIV) is the most frequently injured (53%) (1,4,18). The Left CIV is immediately adjacent to the vertebral column, courses obliquely across vertebral column and has tethering tributaries. Retraction of the left CIV without ligation of its tethering tributaries, particularly the ascending lumbar vein (ALV), endangers the vein (19-21). Injuries to the right CIV and distal inferior vena cava (IVC) are reported. Fantini et al. report that in their series all of the right CIV injuries occurred during exposure of the L4/5 level (1).

Multiple vessels may be injured in ALSS. Up to 8% of vascular injuries involved multiple vessels (4). This increases the complexity and risks significantly greater haemorrhage.

Anatomy

IVC variants have a prevalence of up to 3% (22). These variants include a left IVC, dual IVC, and absent infrarenal IVC. There are variations of the major pelvic veins. The ALV is single in approximately 2/3 patients (20,21). The aortic bifurcation is located at the level of the L4 vertebral body in 60–80% of patients, and the confluence of the IVC is above the L5/S1 disc in the majority of patients (23,24).

Work up

Pre-operatively, the imaging should be reviewed in all cases for anatomical variants, evidence of inflammation, the presence or absence of fat planes between the vessels and adjacent structures, vascular pathology (including calcification, atherosclerosis, stenosis/occlusion, aneurysms, venous occlusion or hypoplasia, multiple venous collaterals), and other pelvic anomalies including pelvic or horseshoe kidney (24,25).

Re-do retroperitoneal approaches increase the risk of both vascular and visceral injury (26). If a re-do exposure of the same level is required, a transperitoneal approach should be considered. For adjacent segment disease at L5/S1, if the left-sided retroperitoneal approach was previously used, a right retroperitoneal approach could be employed. Edgard-Rosa et al. have described a right retroperitoneal approach for L4/5 and above with satisfactory results for use when the left-sided approach is hostile (27). The authors routinely use the right retroperitoneal plane for L5/S1 to preserve the left for anterior surgery for adjacent segment disease.

Transitional anatomy may place the target level behind the confluence of the IVC making exposure more challenging.

Zahradnik et al. reported from their series that the risk of vascular injury was increased by any previous spinal exposure (anterior or posterior) to 19% from 7% in patients with no previous spine exposure. They report that re-do anterior surgery increases the risk fivefold (52% vs. 11%), and a tumour diagnosis triples in the incidence (36% vs. 12.5%) (4).

The spinal prosthesis used changes the risk of a venous injury—total disc replacement requires greater retraction in comparison to fusion (4).

Radiotherapy and significant previous infection in the vicinity of the vessels (e.g., diverticular abscess) causes scarring and may obliterate the natural planes completely. Radiation causes loss of elasticity and increased fragility in the vessel wall rendering it prone to tearing with minimal manipulation. Attempts to dissect and develop an obliterated plane, or clamp the vessel are very likely to cause further tears/injury and are contraindicated in this setting, especially in the presence of established bleeding (1,17,32). Unfortunately, patients do not always recall their history of previous radiotherapy.

Planning and risk factor identification is important in avoiding complications (33). It allows contingency planning, especially in identified high-risk patients. Planning allows the surgeon to ensure the availability of the requisite equipment and staff to manage a vascular injury. It also allows a team briefing (34,35).

Multidisciplinary team planning between the surgical and anaesthetic teams is important for improving outcomes and minimising risks in high-risk ALSS cases. Planning allows for a coordinated tailored approach using pre-considered operative management strategies. It also allows for appropriate anaesthetic vascular access devices, such as a multi-lumen access catheter (MAC; Teleflex®, Wayne, PA, USA), rapid infusion catheter (RIC; Arrow® International, Reading, PA, USA), or large bore peripheral cannulas in the event of a massive transfusion event. Norris et al. demonstrated that the multidisciplinary approach to complex spinal surgery was effective in reducing the incidence of massive haemorrhage from 13.2% to 4.2% (36).

Access surgeons are often employed for exposure in ALSS, and most agree that an ‘access surgeon’ should be recruited for difficult cases (37). In the rare incidence of an MVI, ideally the surgeon(s) would possess both open and endovascular skills (30). A vascular surgeon must be recruited if a massive injury occurs.

Mechanisms

Retraction is the commonest mechanism of injury resulting in branch avulsion or laceration of the vein (19). Injury commonly occurs at the junction with a tributary (e.g., the ALV) (3). A tear at the crux of the IVC can result from excessive distraction of the right and left CIVs (10). An injury to the adjacent vein can result from inadvertent sharp injury while instrumenting the disc space (32).

Intra-operative management of venous injury

Open repair of vascular injury is widely described (1,3,4,10,14,17,19,32,33,38-42). Open management of an MVI should broadly progress through the same stages: initial manoeuvres, identification and exposure, control, and definitive management (see Figure 1). In some cases, definitive management will be repair of the injured vein, while in others this may be impossible, and ligation rather than repair may be employed. If the patient is too unwell, damage control rather than definitive management can be employed in some cases which allows resuscitation, recruitment and preparation for the management of the injury (12,29,32,38,43-49). Endovascular techniques for repair are also addressed in a separate article in this edition. They may occasionally be used together (e.g., endovascular balloon control and open repair). This paper concerns itself with open operative management of a venous injury.

Veins have a high capacitance and have thin, relatively fragile walls. The venous system is low-pressure, and this allows haemostasis to be achieved with adjunctive haemostatic products and pressure and is more forgiving of a repair which is not quite perfect (12,41).

Most venous injuries are minor and can be controlled with compression and adjunct products alone (3,14,33). Larger injuries require exposure, control, and formal repair (3,14,19) (Figure 2). The repair may still be a simple lateral suture, combined with adjunct haemostatic products (e.g., Floseal©, Surgicel© Baxter, Deerfield, IL, USA). The blood loss after these injuries is usually modest and does not physiologically challenge the patient.

An MVI is the most confronting injury which challenges the patient physiologically (11). The management of these injuries is aided by contingency planning and pre-considered strategies.

Initial manoeuvres

When a venous injury with massive haemorrhage first occurs, the initial manoeuvres are the same, whether the ultimate management is open or endovascular. Pressure is applied to control the bleeding vessel (e.g., with a rolled pack), the anaesthetic team is notified, and extra personnel and equipment are recruited if available.

During the initial stage, the focus should be on the resuscitation of the patient and recruitment of the necessary equipment and personnel. Premature and rushed attempts to expose, control and repair the injury are detrimental. Clear communication with the anaesthetic team allows them to resuscitate the patient, recruit equipment (including large-bore intravenous access and massive transfusion devices), and help and prepare for further blood loss. In the case of massive haemorrhage, the anaesthetist should consider early consultation with a haematologist, especially in the setting of on-going bleeding.

Increased real-time anaesthetic monitoring may be required including heart rate (HR), blood pressure (BP), central venous pressure (CVP), and cardiac output. The massive transfusion protocol should be activated. Resuscitation with crystalloids, colloids, and blood products (packed red blood cells, fresh frozen plasma, platelets, and fibrinogen concentrate) can then occur, restoring the circulatory volume without exacerbating the coagulopathy. Vasopressors and inotropes can be administered.

Blood samples should be regularly repeated after a massive haemorrhage event to monitor the patient’s acid-base status, ionized calcium, coagulation profile, and fibrinogen levels. Point of care testing may be available [e.g., rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM) or thromboelastography (TEG)] which allows a more specific and targets response to a patient’s coagulopathy. The anaesthetist(s) should collaborate with the haematologist to administer targeted therapy to correct coagulopathy including fibrinogen concentrate, cryoprecipitate, and anti-fibrinolytic agents (e.g., transexamic acid). Calcium supplementation may be required.

Measures to help preserve the patients core temperature should be used including forced-air warming devices (e.g., Bair Hugger™, 3M™, Maplewood, MN, USA) and warmed intravenous fluids (14,39).

Major bleeding can emanate even from modest venous injuries (12). Lowering the venous pressure can significantly reduce the volume of bleeding, allowing the injury to be identified, controlled, and repaired. To lower the venous pressure, the patient should be placed in the Trendelenburg position (50) and any isolated proximal venous obstruction relieved (e.g. from retractor blades). Ensuring full neuromuscular blockage helps the surgeon to achieve the required exposure with less retractive force (1,51).

Appropriate surgical and theatre staff should be recruited (radiographer, vascular surgeon, and extra assistant). If a vascular surgeon is not present, one should be called. Any required equipment is requested. Ideally, a pre-arranged list of equipment for this contingency will be close at hand and can be opened. A suggested list is presented in Table 4.

Identification and exposure

After the necessary staff and equipment have been recruited, and the patient has been resuscitated, an assessment of the injury can be made.

The pressure over the bleeding site is briefly released to allow inspection of the injury site. With adequate suction, the injured vessel may be able to be seen, and the control options assessed before pressure is replaced.

The adequacy of the incision should be assessed. Adequate exposure is paramount in the event of massive venous haemorrhage and cosmesis needs to be relegated to a secondary consideration. Access may be improved by extending the existing incision (either transverse or longitudinal). Alternatively (or in addition), a further incision may be added to improve exposure and reduce the required retractive force (e.g., a perpendicular extension to the original incision). The distal aorta, IVC, and iliac vessels should be exposed with sufficient access to be able to perform the repair.

Most ALSS is performed through a retroperitoneal approach. In some circumstances, conversion to a transperitoneal approach is warranted—for example, if prohibitive retroperitoneal scarring has been encountered (53,54). A transperitoneal approach can provide an unscarred corridor to the major vessels and provide greater scope for control and definitive management of the injury. Furthermore, a transperitoneal approach allows the evisceration of the intra-peritoneal contents which can improve access and reduce the required retractive force, especially in patients with an elevated Body Mass Index.

A table-based retractor should be employed if not already, and additional retractor blades may be required. This can be supplemented with hand-held retraction, and vascular retraction pins (55,56). The retraction may need repositioning to allow identification of the injury (52). The minimum of required force should be used. Retractor blades can both increase and decrease bleeding. A retractor blade causing proximal obstruction of the IVC can increase venous bleeding substantially. Conversely, if the retractor blades compress the veins on both sides of the injury, it can reduce the haemorrhage.

Radiotherapy or previous infection can cause scarring and adhesions, and also makes the vessel wall fragile. In this scenario, attempts at dissection/mobilization of the vein from the surrounding structures carries the risk of overwhelming dual vessel injury (artery and vein). A case of an 80 litre blood loss from such an injury has been reported (4). If a hostile surgical environment is encountered, further attempts at dissection should be abandoned and alternative strategies such as ligation employed.

It may be possible to proceed immediately with the definitive control. However, often the injured vein may be completely or partially obscured, and further dissection is required. Where possible, maintain pressure on the injury to control bleeding while further exposure is achieved (e.g., with a swab-on-a-stick). However, if the volume of bleeding precludes this, further dissection should be deferred until alternative more distant venous control can be achieved permitting dissection and exposure.

The iliac veins and IVC are partially obscured by the overlying iliac arteries. The iliac artery usually needs to be retracted medically or laterally. Active haemorrhage makes this difficult and great care is required not to extend the venous injury. The artery should initially be separated from the vein somewhat distant to the site of venous injury as the plane between the artery and vein will be more evident here. The plane and dissection can then be developed toward the venous injury. Great caution is needed to ensure that only the artery is retracted, and not the vein wall.

The presence of robust adhesions between the artery and the vein carries the likelihood that the venous injury will be extended during dissection. The CIV is often more adherent to its arterial counterpart than the internal or external iliac veins: it is usually advisable to initially separate the external iliac artery and vein, and then work toward the common iliac vessels.

Diathermy should be used with caution or avoided, as should ’spreading’ techniques with scissors or other instruments. Precise sharp dissection with scissors can be employed when the plane is clear and loose areolar/adhesive tissue is present. Multiple small incisions are favoured over larger, ‘decisive’ incisions. A peanut swab on a curved artery forceps used gently can be useful in separating the vein from the surrounding structures. Avoid using excessive force when developing the planes due to the substantial risk of further venous injury. If excessive force is required, then the dissection should be abandoned.

Division of the overlying right common iliac artery to expose the injured left CIV has been described (57). However, it is rarely performed and is difficult in the setting of a major injury carrying the risk of extending the venous injury. It should only be performed by an experienced vascular surgeon. Significant arterial pathology (e.g., atherosclerosis, heavy calcification) are relative contra-indications to division of the iliac artery for access.

If the injury cannot be adequately visualised due to hostile pathology (e.g., scarring, past infection, radiotherapy change), endovascular techniques, damage control, or ligation should be employed as an alternative.

Control

Once the injury is identified, the bleeding needs to be controlled sufficiently to allow repair. The nature, size, and location of the injury, the condition of the vessel wall dictate which techniques are suitable for control. A combination of techniques is often required, and different control techniques may be used proximally and distally as anatomy permits (e.g., direct pressure for proximal control and Potts loop control distally) (Table 5).

Manoeuvres for control of haemorrhage

If there is sufficient exposure, the direct pressure over the venous injury can be replaced with pressure proximal and distal to the injury (e.g., with a swab on a stick), controlling the bleeding while allowing repair.

Alternatively, a rolled pack initially directly compressing the venous injury can be rolled off slowly. As the injury is exposed, the two edges of the venous injury can be grasped with an atraumatic instrument (e.g., Babcock or Allise forceps) maintaining control. The pack continues to be rolled off exposing more of the injury which is again grasped with additional grasping forceps until the entire injury is exposed. The grasping forceps are then underrun with a polypropylene suture (3/0 or 4/0) and the forceps sequentially removed. It is paramount to ensure that both edges of the injury are grasped and sutured.

If there is sufficient freedom and laxity in the vein wall at the site of the injury, it may be possible to pick up both sides of the injured vein with an atraumatic forceps (e.g., DeBakey) and with gentle traction place a side-biting clamp (e.g., Satinsky) to allow repair.

If the vein proximal and/or distal to the injury can be safely circumnavigated with a right-angle forceps, a rubber vessel loop may be passed in a ‘Potts double loop’ configuration. Traction on the vessel loop will occlude the vein.

In some cases, a No. 10 urinary catheter may be passed into the vein lumen through the injury and gently inflated to achieve haemostasis.

In many cases, the distal abdominal aorta can be accessed. If it is relatively normal and disease free, it can be clamped when control is difficult to achieve. This will reduce the arterial flow into the tissues and act as an indirect form of inflow control (58). It may affect a substantial reduction in the venous bleeding and be a useful adjunct when combined with other manoeuvres. It should not be forgotten in cases of MVI.

A combination of the above techniques may allow repair. Haemostasis does not need to be perfect before performing the repair, rather the bleeding should be reduced to a level which will permit repair without prohibitive blood loss. As the injury is closed, the bleeding will be reduced to a level where topical haemostatic agents and pressure will allow final haemostasis.

The placement of clamps on veins, particularly by non-vascular surgeons with limited experience, may extend the injury or cause a separate injury (e.g., especially if the vein is adherent to surrounding structures, or very fragile). Most authors caution against the use of vascular cross clamps (1).

Aborting the procedure in favour of ‘damage control’ should be considered when control and safe definitive management cannot be achieved.

Management

Repair

There are multiple techniques for repairing the venous injury after adequate control has been achieved (see Table 6). The nature of the venous injury dictates which techniques are required.

Techniques for repair of venous injury

Small injuries of <5–10 mm may simply be grasped with atraumatic Debakey forceps and a primary suture repair performed. However, it is unlikely that these injuries would be responsible for massive venous haemorrhage. Longer injuries require a running polypropylene suture (47).

Pledgets may need to be used if the vein wall is fragile (Table 4). There are commercially available Teflon pledgets, but pledgets can be fashioned from other prosthetic material (e.g., Dacron arterial patch), bovine pericardium, or the patient’s native tissue (a small 0.5 mm square piece of fascia from the anterior abdominal wall).

If a section of the vein wall has been lost, it will leave a deficit in the vein wall. A primary repair will narrow the vein lumen excessively. In this scenario, a patch repair is required. This can be performed with autogenous vein, prosthetic material, or bovine pericardial tissue (59).

If the vein has been partially or completely transected, a primary end-to-end anastomosis after vein edge debridement is necessary provided it can be performed without excessive tension. Veins usually have some laxity, and this can often be achieved unless extensive debridement has been necessary, or the vein is encased in scar. The anastomosis is performed with a running polypropylene suture (e.g., 3/0 or 4/0).

In rare cases, an interposition graft may be necessary to restore patency and continuity of the vein without excessive tension. In MVI, this is likely to be a rare circumstance due to the patient’s haemodynamic instability, availability of suitable conduit, surgeon fatigue, and time requirements. In these settings, ligation should be strongly considered. The control of the venous haemorrhage needs to be assured if an interposition graft is undertaken.

Venous ligation

Ligation proximal and distal to the venous injury is an option in severe cases (1). It is preferable to maintain venous patency but ligation is justified in several circumstances: (I) if the magnitude of the injury is too great and requires a complex repair; (II) if there are multiple vascular injuries; (III) the surgical field is hostile due to adhesions/scarring; (IV) the vein wall is abnormally fragile (e.g., post radiotherapy or infection); and (V) the bleeding is inadequately controlled despite multiple techniques.

In these settings, distal then proximal ligation of the vein separate to the injury is an alternative. It is best to ligate the vein as close as safely possible to the injury. However, a segment of the vein is required which allows safe circumferential dissection to allow a heavy tie (e.g., 0 Silk) to be passed and the vein ligated.

Formal ligation requires proximal and distal dissection and mobilisation of the injured vein circumferentially to pass the required ties. If the scarring/adhesions are more widespread, it may not be possible to safely circumnavigate the vein without a high risk of further injury. In these cases, a polypropylene suture (e.g., 3/0 or 4/0) may be used to underrun the vein in-situ without circumnavigating it but still effectively ligating the vein.

The literature regarding vein ligation is heterogenous, lacks detail, and usually reports ligation after multi-system trauma which is not equivalent to a single system injury. With this caveat, ligation of the iliac vein or the infra-renal IVC is usually well tolerated, with relatively mild, short-duration oedema (7–10 days) (10,60-63). Resultant long-term oedema is uncommon (60). The oedema had settled by discharge in 86% of cases in one series (62). Oedema also occurs after venous repair (59,62,64).

There are reports after venous ligation in multi-trauma patients of severe swelling requiring fasciotomy or even amputation (61,65).

Published literature on IVC ligation is limited, but ligation of the IVC below the renal veins can be undertaken safely without an increase in mortality (10,22,23,47,64-69).

Damage control

Damage control staged laparotomy was described as a useful technique in retroperitoneal, pelvic, and abdominal vascular trauma (38,70). The presence of a major vascular injury in a multi-trauma patient drops survival by 30% (70,71).

The principles of damage control are haemorrhage control, limitation of peritoneal contamination, and temporary abdominal closure. Damage control was conceived for the multiply traumatised patient, but in severe cases of MVI during ALSS, the principles still apply. It allows stabilisation of the patient in the intensive care unit (ICU) including correction of hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy. It also allows planning for definitive management.

In ALSS, with an MVI, damage control takes the form of packing for haemorrhage control and temporary abdominal closure.

Damage control should be employed in patients with MVI if the patients physiological parameters remain poor (HR >120 beats per minute, BP <90 mmHg) or if hypothermia (<34 degrees), coagulopathy or acidosis (pH <7.2) is present, especially if the bleeding remains uncontrolled except with packing (70). If the surgical expertise required to address the problem are unavailable, damage control should be employed earlier (70).

Damage control surgery does carry complications, including wound complications (up to 25%), intra-abdominal sepsis, vascular thrombosis, distal ischaemia, and hernia (70).

Abandonment of the spinal procedure

The MVI may not become apparent until after the discectomy has been performed, before prosthesis implantation. Discectomy does not render the spine unstable—the middle and posterior column of the spine are still intact (72). The placement of a spinal prosthesis can be aborted in the setting of massive haemorrhage if required. It is preferable to abandon rather than allow further bleeding and its potentially severe sequelae. There are multiple reported cases in which the spinal procedure was aborted, and damage control employed, and a posterior procedure performed later (10,29,39).

Post-operative management

After an MVI, the patient should be managed in ICU. If a damage control procedure was employed with abdominal packing and temporary abdominal closure, a return to theatre within 72 hours is planned.

The incidence of deep venous thrombosis (DVT) after a major venous injury is high with the reported rates ranging from 10% to 100%. Frank et al. [2017] reported a DVT rate of 24% with 3% pulmonary embolism rate (73). Venous thromboembolism (VTE) prophylaxis is strongly recommended as each day withheld increased the risk of VTE by 28% (73). The incidence of VTE after ligation is similar to that after repair, and the incidence of pulmonary embolism may even be lower (60,67-69,74).

Compression hosiery and leg elevation will help control the resultant leg swelling after venous repair or ligation.

After an unexpected MVI, we routinely employ a period of intravenous antibiotics (minimum of 48 hours) as major bleeding does increase the risk of infection (75).

Conclusions

MVI in ALSS is a rare but potentially catastrophic complication.

The vascular and spinal surgeons must be cognizant of the anatomy and its variants, and the factors that increase the surgical risk. They must review the patient and their imaging and have a contingency plan in the event of an MVI.

The best outcome after an MVI is only achieved with a team approach, recruitment of the necessary personnel and the deployment of the necessary equipment to enable the open management following a pre-considered contingency plan.
