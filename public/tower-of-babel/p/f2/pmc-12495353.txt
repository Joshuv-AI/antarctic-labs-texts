Pilot study on the effects of dapagliflozin on echocardiographic parameters in dogs with symptomatic myxomatous mitral valve disease
Authors: Nakkawee Saengklub, Anusak Kijtawornrat, Robert L Hamlin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Dapagliflozin improved cardiac geometry and function in dogs with stage C MMVD.•Short-term dosing reduced LA and LV size without affecting heart rate or blood pressure.•No hypoglycemia occurred despite significant glucosuria induced by SGLT-2 inhibition.•Study supports the potential cardioprotective role of SGLT-2 inhibitors in canine MMVD.Keywords: Dapagliflozin, Dog, Echocardiography, Mitral regurgitation, Pharmacodynamics

Introduction

Mitral regurgitation (MR) is the most common valvular heart disease in both humans (Asgar et al., 2015) and dogs (Keene et al., 2019), and its prevalence increases with age (Fox, 2012). In humans, MR may be classified as primary or secondary (i.e., functional); however, myxomatous mitral valve disease (MMVD) is the primary cause of MR in geriatric small- and medium-sized dogs (Connell et al., 2012). During the asymptomatic phase of MMVD, compensatory mechanisms develop to maintain cardiac output, although these mechanisms are ultimately maladaptive. As the disease progresses, affected dogs enter the symptomatic phase and develop, often characterized by congestive heart failure (CHF). Pimobendan has been shown to delay the onset of CHF when administered at an appropriate stage (i.e., stage B2), while a combination of pimobendan, diuretics, and an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEi) (Keene et al., 2019), or dual therapy with pimobendan and diuretics (Wess et al., 2020), is recommended during the symptomatic phase. Nevertheless, despite advancements in medical therapy, dogs with MMVD eventually develop end-stage CHF and die.

Sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT-2i) (e.g., canagliflozin, dapagliflozin, empagliflozin) were initially developed to manage diabetes mellitus (DM) but have been recognized for their cardioprotective effects (Filippatos et al., 2019). SGLT-2i reduces glucose and sodium reabsorption in the renal proximal convoluted tubule (Salvatore et al., 2022), causing natriuresis and diuresis. In humans, clinical studies have demonstrated that dapagliflozin reduces the risk of heart failure (HF) in patients with type-two DM (T2DM) (Ibrahim et al., 2020; Singh et al., 2020). In addition, its beneficial effects have also been observed in patients with HF regardless of diabetic status, suggesting a class effect on cardiac function (McMurray et al., 2019; Nassif et al., 2019). Although the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood, SGLT-2i are believed to reduce preload and afterload, thereby alleviating HF symptoms and lowering hospitalization rates (Xie et al., 2023). Additional proposed mechanisms include reduced intracellular sodium via inhibition of the sodium-hydrogen exchanger (NHE), improved calcium handling, and enhanced mitochondrial calcium concentrations, all of which may contribute to improved cardiac function.

Given these potential benefits, dapagliflozin represents a promising candidate for adjunctive therapy in dogs with CHF secondary to MMVD. To date, no studies have investigated its use in this population. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the short-term effects of adding dapagliflozin to conventional therapy (pimobendan, furosemide, and ramipril) in symptomatic dogs with MMVD. We hypothesized that oral dapagliflozin administration would be well tolerated and would improve cardiac function and geometry. These preliminary findings may support the potential role of SGLT-2i in the management of CHF in dogs with MMVD.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The study was a prospective, randomized, single-blind study. All the animal experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Chulalongkorn University Laboratory Animal Center (protocol number 2273018) and the IACUC of the Faculty of Pharmacy, Mahidol University (protocol number PYR007/2022). This study was carried out in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NRC, 2011) and the Animals for Scientific Purposes Act (2015 BE), and the informed consent of the dogs’ owners was secured. Additionally, this study was executed in compliance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for dogs in the main pharmacodynamics study

All enrolled dogs were existing patients of our hospital and had been monitored longitudinally since being diagnosed with MMVD stage B2. Owners were instructed to measure resting respiratory rate (RRR) weekly at home. If the respiratory rate increased by >80 % from baseline values or exceeded 40 breaths per minute, the dog was brought in for thoracic radiography. Dogs were classified as having progressed to stage C MMVD if radiographs revealed pulmonary edema (Fig. 1), specifically an interstitial pattern in the perihilar region, in accordance with the American College of Veterinary Internal Medicine (ACVIM) guidelines. Upon diagnosis of acute CHF, the dogs received standard therapy (typically for 1–2 days) until their condition stabilized. Only then were they considered for enrollment into either the control or dapagliflozin group. To be enrolled in the study, the dogs had to be diagnosed with stage-C MMVD, as defined by ACVIM Consensus Statements (Keene et al., 2019). The criteria included historical and clinical signs consistent with CHF (e.g., increased resting respiratory rate, cough, exercise intolerance), thoracic radiographs showing evidence of mild pulmonary edema, typically with an interstitial or alveolar pattern in the perihilar or caudodorsal lung fields, echocardiographic confirmation of MMVD with left atrial and/or left ventricular enlargement, and characteristic mitral valve thickening or prolapse. All dogs were client-owned, diagnosed with stage C MMVD for the first time, and had been receiving pimobendan since stage B2 as recommended by current guidelines. Dogs had not been previously treated with ACE inhibitors, spironolactone, or diuretics prior to enrollment. The exclusion criteria included the presence of systemic diseases of non-cardiac origin, cardiac arrhythmias, pulmonary arterial hypertension, and/or congenital heart disease.

Animal experimental procedures

Prior to enrollment in the study, all the animals underwent comprehensive evaluations, including physical examination, echocardiography, electrocardiography, thoracic radiography, arterial blood pressure measurement, routine hematology, and serum chemistry profiling comprising measurements of the creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and alkaline phosphatase (ALKP). These assessments were repeated on 28-, 84-, and 140-day post-treatment. Blood and urine samples were collected before (baseline) and after treatment (28, 84, and 140 days) for the analysis of the blood glucose concentration and urine glucose excretion (UGE). Blood samples were also used for determination of N-terminal prohormone of brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) concentration at baseline and at the end of the study (140 days post-treatment). Baseline evaluation also included right lateral thoracic radiography to assess vertebral heart score (VHS), which was used to estimate cardiac silhouette size. A single experienced sonographer performed all echocardiographic examinations to minimize inter-operator variability. Intra- and inter-observer variability assessments were conducted prior to the study, as reported in a previous publication (Saengklub et al., 2021). All the dogs in both treatment groups received pimobendan (0.25–0.30 mg/kg, every 12 h), (ramipril (0.125-0.200 mg/kg, every 24 h), and furosemide (1.5–2.0 mg/kg, every 12 h). In the dapagliflozin group, the dogs were additionally administered dapagliflozin orally at a dose of 0.31 mg/kg every 24 h. In the placebo group, dogs received an identical capsule containing lactose powder. The dogs’ owners were instructed to maintain consistent feeding regimens in terms of the type and timing throughout the five-month study period. The dose of dapagliflozin (0.31 mg/kg, PO, q24h) was chosen based on our unpublished in-house pharmacokinetic and tolerability study in healthy dogs, which evaluated three dose levels: 0.31, 0.62, and 1.25 mg/kg. While the lowest dose was well tolerated, higher doses resulted in penile edema (1 out of 6 dogs). Therefore, 0.31 mg/kg was selected as the safest effective dose for this pilot study. The pharmacokinetic parameters at this dose also supported its suitability for clinical evaluation. Ramipril was administered at a dose of 0.125–0.2 mg/kg q24h, consistent with dosing protocols used in the VALVE trial (Wess et al., 2020). The dosage was determined based on body weight and adjusted if clinical indicators such as systemic hypertension, proteinuria, or renal dysfunction were observed. No dogs required up-titration during the study period.

Echocardiographic examinations were performed on all the dogs four times, once at baseline and again at 28, 84, and 140 days post-treatment, using an ultrasound system (DC-70 X-Insight, Mindray, Shenzhen, China) equipped with P10-4E phased array transducers, without sedation. Measurements of the left atrium to the aortic root ratio (LA/Ao), left ventricular internal diameter in diastole normalized by body weight (LVIDDN), and shortening fraction (SF) were obtained from the right parasternal short-axis view with M-mode using previously described methods in the EPIC study (Boswood et al., 2016) and in accordance with the ACVIM consensus statements (Keene et al., 2019). The LA/Ao was determined following the recommended approach, measuring the aortic root at the junction of the non-coronary and left coronary valvar sinuses and the left atrium (LA) along the same line, ensuring accurate assessment without extension into the pulmonary veins. The end-diastolic volume (EDV), end-systolic volume (ESV), and ejection fraction (EF) were calculated using a modified version of Simpson’s Method of Discs from the right parasternal long-axis four-chamber view. Both the EDV and ESV were indexed to the body surface area, referred to as the end-diastolic volume index (EDVI) and end-systolic volume index (ESVI), respectively. The blood glucose and urinary glucose excretion measurements were performed using a glucometer (VetMate®, i-SENS Inc., CA, USA). The arterial BP was recorded using an oscillometric device (petMAP, CardioCommand, Inc., Tampa, FL, USA) placed on the right forelimb upon the median artery between the elbow and the carpal pad. Five consecutive measurements were obtained at each timepoint, and the mean of three consistent readings was used as the final BP value. This method is consistent with a previous study (Saengklub et al., 2021) and is further detailed in Haginoya and Thomovsky (Haginoya & Thomovsky, 2024). The NT-proBNP was measured according to the guidelines established by the manufacturer and analyzed using a commercially available assay for the measurement of canine NT-proBNP (MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, USA).

Standard lead II electrocardiography (ECG) was performed with the dogs positioned in right lateral recumbency, with all limbs perpendicular to the long axis of the body. Surface electrodes were attached to the skin of all four limbs and connected to a computerized ECG system (Cardimax FX-7302, Fukuda Denshi, Tokyo, Japan). ECG recordings were acquired over a 1-minute period. Although a six-lead system was available, lead II was used for all measurements. Heart rate and rhythm were assessed. All ECG tracings were reviewed and interpreted by a single veterinarian experienced in cardiology (N.S.), who was blinded to group allocation.

Statistical analyses

The statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM, USA). The percentage changes from baseline (D0) were calculated, and the differences between groups at the same timepoint were compared. Due to the small sample size, data were assumed to be non-normally distributed. As such, the Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare differences between groups at each timepoint. Data are presented as median (interquartile range) unless otherwise specified. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Safety and tolerability

Dapagliflozin, administered at a dose of 0.25–0.45 mg/kg q24h, was safe and well tolerated in dogs with stage C MMVD. No adverse events (e.g., urinary tract infections or penile edema) were reported throughout the study. Routine health monitoring, including urinalysis, did not reveal any signs of urinary tract infection, such as hematuria, pyuria, or bacteriuria. However, as urine cultures were not performed, the presence of subclinical infections cannot be entirely excluded. During the 140-day follow-up, no new onset of CHF occurred in any dog in either the dapagliflozin or control group. Physical examination findings, including appetite, hydration status, and activity, remained stable. No episodes of new-onset pulmonary edema or CHF occurred.

Population of dogs in the study

At enrollment, 14 dogs were randomized to the dapagliflozin (n= 7) or control group (n= 7). Two dogs in the dapagliflozin group were lost to follow-up after Day 28—one due to relocation and the other due to owner withdrawal from the study; neither case was related to adverse events. The remaining 12 dogs consisted of eight males and four females (age range: 7–15.2 years; weight range: 1.9–10.2 kg). The enrolled breeds included Poodle (n= 1), Pomeranian (n= 1), Shih Tzu (n= 3), Beagle (n= 1), American Cocker Spaniel (n= 1), Jack Russell Terrier (n= 2), and small mixed breeds (n= 3).

The final groups included five dogs in the dapagliflozin group and seven in the control group (Table 1). At baseline (Day 0), no statistically significant differences were observed between groups in age (dapagliflozin: 12.6 ± 0.8 years vs. control: 11.1 ± 2.8 years, P= 0.210), sex distribution (dapagliflozin: 5 males, 0 females vs. control: 3 males, 4 females), or body weight (dapagliflozin: 7.3 ± 0.9 kg vs. control: 4.8 ± 1.1 kg, P= 0.108). All dogs met the criteria for stage C MMVD based on the ACVIM guidelines. Despite individual variations, the inclusion criteria ensured comparable disease severity across groups.

The mean VHS was 11.08 ± 0.20 (range: 10.6–11.8) in the dapagliflozin group and 11.33 ± 0.18 (range: 10.8–11.8) in the control group. All dogs had a murmur intensity grade of IV/VI to V/VI.

In the dapagliflozin group, dogs received dapagliflozin (0.25–0.45 mg/kg, q24h) in addition to pimobendan (0.26 ± 0.01 mg/kg, q12h), ramipril (0.137 ± 0.02 mg/kg, q 24h), and furosemide (1.75 ± 0.04 mg/kg, q12h). In the control group, dogs were treated with pimobendan (0.28 ± 0.02 mg/kg, q12h), ramipril (0.126 ± 0.02 mg/kg, q 24h), and furosemide (1.74 ± 0.07 mg/kg, q12h).

Over the 140-day study period, three of the five dogs in the dapagliflozin group lost 0.2 kg (weight loss:1.98 % to –3.64 %), one lost 0.4 kg (–4.35 %), and one maintained stable weight. In the control group (n= 7), three dogs gained 0.1 kg (weight gain: 1.10 % to 3.33 %), two lost 0.1 kg (–1.25 % to –2.50 %), and two had stable weights. These changes were not statistically significant. Moreover, during the 140-day follow-up period, none of the enrolled dogs developed new-onset pulmonary edema or signs of acute CHF. All dogs remained clinically stable on their assigned treatment protocols, and there were no modifications to drug types or dosages throughout the study.

Other parameters

Other monitored parameters included blood pressure (systolic, mean, and diastolic), blood glucose concentration, NT-proBNP, hematology, serum biochemistry (creatinine, BUN, ALT, and ALKP), and ECG data. There were no significant differences in blood pressure between groups at any timepoint. However, percentage changes in blood glucose concentration at days 28 (P= 0.073) and 140 (P= 0.073) tended to reduce in the dapagliflozin group compared to the control group. The NT-proBNP level also tended to reduce at day 140 (P= 0.061).

In the dapagliflozin group, urinary glucose concentrations were 5.29 ± 0.38 %, 4.94 ± 0.44 %, and 4.89 ± 0.46 % at days 28, 84, and 140, respectively. No glucose was detected at baseline or at any timepoint in the control group.

No significant arrhythmias were observed on ECG in either group during the study period. Hematology and serum biochemistry parameters remained within normal limits and showed no clinically relevant changes over 140 days.

Discussion

This study determines that the short-term administration of dapagliflozin, an SGLT2i, is safe and well tolerated in dogs with stage C MMVD. Importantly, dapagliflozin treatment led to significant improvements in echocardiographic parameters indicative of LA and LV remodeling and function, without adverse effects on heart rate, blood pressure, or blood glucose concentrations. Our safety monitoring approach was guided by the known adverse effects of dapagliflozin in human patients, including genitourinary infections, volume depletion, and hypotension (Dahal et al., 2025; Kodur et al., 2025). Although euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis is a rare but serious complication in diabetic patients, it was considered less relevant in our non-diabetic cohort. Nevertheless, we monitored blood glucose, blood pressure, and urinary parameters to assess potential adverse effects. No clinically significant adverse events were observed in any of the treated dogs. These findings support the potential use of dapagliflozin as an adjunct treatment in the management of MMVD in dogs.

Although dapagliflozin did not influence electrocardiographic or blood pressure indices during the 140-day study period, it tended to reduce blood glucose concentrations through increased urinary glucose excretion. This effect results from the inhibition of SGLT-2 receptors located in the proximal renal tubules, which are responsible for approximately 90 % of glucose reabsorption. Notably, despite the glucose-lowering effect, no episodes of hypoglycemia were observed, as all dogs maintained blood glucose concentrations above the lower limit of normal (60 mg/dL or 3.3 mmol/L) (Idowu & Heading, 2018).

To evaluate the disease progression and drug effects, dogs with stage C MMVD were allocated into either the control group or the dapagliflozin treatment group. Most dogs enrolled were male, of small to medium-sized breeds, and aged between 7 and 15.2 years—consistent with the known predisposition of these demographic groups to MMVD (Atkins & Häggström, 2012; Kijtawornrat et al., 2017).

In the dapagliflozin group, a modest reduction in body weight was observed over the 140-day period. This likely reflects the drug’s osmotic diuretic and glucosuric effects, which can reduce extracellular fluid and modestly affect body composition (Bolinder et al., 2012). Given the small magnitude of change and the absence of muscle loss, appetite reduction, or recurrent CHF, cardiogenic cachexia was considered unlikely in these cases. Nonetheless, in patients with more advanced MMVD, weight loss should prompt careful evaluation to distinguish between drug-related effects and pathologic cachexia.

Dapagliflozin treatment resulted in favorable changes in cardiac geometry and function. Specifically, reductions in LA/Ao, LVIDDN, EDV, EDVI, ESV, and ESVI were observed. Additionally, indices of systolic function (i.e., EF) significantly improved in the treatment group. These changes occurred without any significant effect on heart rate or systemic blood pressure, suggesting that the cardiac improvements were not simply due to hemodynamic alterations but may involve direct myocardial effects or reductions in preload.

The effects of dapagliflozin observed in this study align with previous research in experimental models. In rodent models of myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury and chronic MR, dapagliflozin was shown to improve mitochondrial function, reduce myocardial fibrosis and apoptosis, and modulate endoplasmic reticulum stress—all contributing to improved LV function and attenuation of adverse cardiac remodeling (Lahnwong et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2021). Additional studies in chronic MR models demonstrated positive inotropic effects of dapagliflozin, as indicated by enhanced end-systolic pressure–volume relationship (ESPVR) and maximum rate of LV pressure rise (dP/dtmax) (Lin et al., 2021). Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that dapagliflozin may improve cardiac function by reducing expression of the sodium-hydrogen exchanger-1 (NHE-1) and its interaction with heat shock protein 70 (Hsp70) (Wichaiyo & Saengklub, 2022; Ye et al., 2018).

Although all dogs were classified as Stage C MMVD, the baseline HR measurements were obtained after stabilization from acute CHF episodes, which may account for the relatively lower HR observed. Over the follow-up period, gradual increases in HR were noted in both treatment groups. This is consistent with previously reported findings in dogs with MMVD, where chronic progression of disease correlates with increased HR and reduced heart rate variability—reflecting autonomic imbalance and sympathetic predominance over time (Keene et al., 2019; Dos Santos et al., 2021; Pirintr et al., 2023). Regarding a progressive condition of MMVD, our observations of improved echocardiographic parameters in both groups may reflect an initial reverse remodeling effect following stabilization and medical therapy rather than true disease regression. Similar findings have been reported in dogs with stage B2 MMVD treated with pimobendan, where both left ventricular dimensions and VHS decreased, consistent with early reverse remodeling (Saengklub et al., 2021; Pirintr et al., 2023; Crosland et al., 2024; van Staveren et al., 2024). This phenomenon likely explains the echocardiographic improvements observed during the early follow-up period in our study.

In our study, NT-proBNP levels, a biomarker released in response to myocardial stretch, tended to reduce in the dapagliflozin group compared with controls. This trend likely reflects a decrease in cardiac volume load, as indicated by the reduced EDV, EDVI, ESV, and ESVI. Similar findings have been reported in human patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), where dapagliflozin significantly decreased NT-proBNP levels after 8 months of treatment (Butt et al., 2021). However, inconsistent responses have been observed in other populations; for example, NT-proBNP levels remained unchanged in diabetic patients with coronary artery disease treated with dapagliflozin over 6 months (Biter et al., 2024). These disparities may be attributed to differences in disease pathophysiology, patient demographics, or study duration.

Potential limitations

This pilot study has several important limitations that warrant cautious interpretation of the findings. The most critical limitation is the small sample size, which reduces statistical power, increases the likelihood of baseline imbalances despite randomization, and limits the generalizability of the results. Consequently, some comparisons may not have reached statistical significance despite potentially meaningful differences, such as baseline body weight or volumetric indices. Readers should interpret non-significant findings with caution, as absence of statistical significance does not equate to absence of effect.

Additional limitations include that only short-term outcomes were evaluated; long-term effects of dapagliflozin on survival, hospitalization rate, or progression of MMVD remain unknown. The exact timing of CHF onset could not be determined, as dogs naturally developed the disease, and the effects of dapagliflozin at different stages (e.g., stage B2 or stage D) were not investigated.

Methodological constraints also deserve consideration. The severity of MR was not quantified using advanced indices such as regurgitant fraction or proximal isovelocity surface area. NT-proBNP concentrations were measured with an ELISA kit reporting values in pg/mL, whereas veterinary clinical practice typically uses pmol/L; this limits direct comparison with other studies. Left ventricular volumes were indexed to body surface area rather than body weight, which may affect comparability with published reference intervals, though it does not affect internal validity of within-study percentage change comparisons. Finally, echocardiographic measures directly reflecting left atrial pressure (e.g., E-wave velocity, tissue Doppler imaging) were not included, and future studies should incorporate these parameters to better characterize hemodynamic changes.

Despite these limitations, the study provides preliminary but valuable evidence supporting further investigation of SGLT-2 inhibitors in dogs with MMVD.

Conclusions

In this pilot study, dapagliflozin, when added to standard therapy, was associated with favorable trends in several echocardiographic parameters in dogs with stage C MMVD. These results suggest a potential role for dapagliflozin in modulating cardiac remodeling; however, no conclusions can be drawn regarding quality of life, clinical outcomes, or survival. Larger, prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings and to determine the clinical relevance of dapagliflozin in the management of MMVD.
