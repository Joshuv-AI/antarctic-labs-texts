Synergistic effects of dexamethasone and vitamins D and K on mesenchymal stem cell differentiation
Authors: Jana Dvorakova, Jana Kolaja Dobra, Lucie Hauer, Petra Kocanek Chocholata, Miroslava Cedikova, Anna Nekleionova, Vaclav Babuska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe ability to guide stem cells toward the bone-forming lineage is a fundamental aspect of regenerative medicine. Conventional protocols for osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells typically rely on continuous exposure to the synthetic steroid dexamethasone, in combination with other supplements such as ascorbate and glycerophosphate. Although dexamethasone effectively induces bone-related gene expression and matrix mineralization, prolonged or excessive exposure can lead to adverse catabolic and cytotoxic effects. Therefore, there is a need to develop improved culture conditions that retain efficacy while enhancing physiological relevance, safety, and reproducibility. This study investigated whether 1-week dexamethasone exposure and supplementing the medium with vitamin D3 and the synthetic vitamin K analogue K3 could enhance osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells.MethodsStem cells were cultured for 28 days in four types of osteogenic media differing in the duration of dexamethasone exposure and the presence of vitamins D3 and K3. Osteogenic differentiation was evaluated using histochemical staining for mineralized matrix, quantification of calcium deposition, analysis of alkaline phosphatase activity, fluorescence microscopy of cellular morphology and mitochondrial organization, and gene expression of osteogenic markers including RUNX2, alkaline phosphatase, osteocalcin, and bone morphogenetic protein 2.ResultsA short exposure to dexamethasone alone was insufficient to support robust osteogenic progression, confirming the importance of sustained stimulation. In contrast, the combination of vitamins D3 and K3 produced a clear synergistic effect, characterized by stronger mineralization, increased alkaline phosphatase activity, and enhanced expression of key osteogenic genes.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that the combined use of vitamins D3 and K3, together with optimized dexamethasone exposure, provides a simple and biologically compatible strategy to improve the osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells. This approach offers a physiologically relevant, safe, and cost-effective modification of standard osteogenic protocols and may contribute to the development of more efficient cell-based systems for bone tissue engineering and regenerative medicine.

Introduction

Tissue engineering is a promising strategy for the repair and regeneration of damaged human tissues. Among the various types of stem cells, bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BM-MSCs) are particularly attractive for bone tissue engineering due to their intrinsic capacity to differentiate into osteogenic lineage [1]. Furthermore, BM-MSCs can be harvested autologously, minimizing the risk of immune rejection and supporting personalized regenerative approaches. Beyond osteogenesis, BM-MSCs exhibit the potential to differentiate into mesodermal derivatives, including cartilage, tendon, muscle, cardiomyocytes, adipose tissue, and even neural-like cells, highlighting their broad therapeutic versatility [2].

Despite their potential, a major limitation is the low abundance of BM-MSCs in the bone marrow, which is further complicated by donor-related variables such as age, health status, and environmental exposure [3, 4]. Strategies to address these limitations include the optimization of cell isolation and expansion protocols, exploration of alternative MSCs sources, and enhancement of post-transplant cell survival and integration.

The development of biomaterials for bone tissue replacement requires rigorous evaluation of osteoinductivity and the ability to induce osteogenic differentiation, which is a critical parameter [5]. In vitro testing of osteodifferentiation typically relies on standardized culture protocols designed to ensure reproducible differentiation of MSCs into osteoblast-like cells, while avoiding undesired cell lineages [6]. Conventional osteogenic induction protocols employ pharmacological agents and recombinant proteins, but some have drawbacks such as limited efficacy and adverse effects [7]. Consequently, ongoing efforts aim to refine these protocols by incorporating innovative osteoinductive agents that complement the standard combination of dexamethasone (DEX), ascorbic acid, and β-glycerophosphate βGLY [8]. The use of such supplements was first introduced in 1986 [9], and current research has explored additional molecules including vitamin D3, bone morphogenic proteins, and physical stimuli [10].

DEX, a synthetic glucocorticoid with anti-inflammatory properties [11], is widely employed in vitro to enhance MSCs proliferation, prevent apoptosis, and directly differentiate into osteogenic, adipogenic, or chondrogenic lineages [12]. During osteogenic commitment, DEX induces RUNX2 expression via FHL2/β-catenin-mediated transcriptional activation and upregulates downstream targets such as TAZ (transcriptional coactivator with PDZ binding motif) and MKP1 (mitogen-activated protein kinase phosphatase 1), thereby reinforcing the osteogenic phenotype [8]. However, despite its proven efficacy in vitro, prolonged high-dose DEX administration in vivo is associated with deleterious skeletal effects, including osteopenia and increased fracture risk [13], rendering its application in osteogenic protocols a subject of ongoing debate. Although DEX is a cornerstone of traditional osteogenic media, it has also been shown to inhibit osteoblast proliferation and induce apoptosis under certain conditions [14]. This finding highlights the need to reassess its role in osteogenic induction. According to the literature, the most appropriate concentration range of DEX for promoting osteogenic differentiation and calcium deposition is 10–100 nM. Concentrations below this range failed to induce adequate mineralization, whereas higher concentrations were associated with a shift toward adipogenic differentiation [7, 8, 15].

Ascorbic acid (vitamin C) plays a fundamental role in collagen synthesis and supports the normal physiological functions of connective tissues, vasculature, and bones [16]. It also promotes osteoblastogenesis, inhibits osteoclast differentiation, and contributes to bone homeostasis [17]. Ascorbic acid enhanced the expression of osteoblast markers and matrix mineralization in vitro [18]. Owing to its instability and oxidative lability, a more stable derivative, ascorbate-2-phosphate, is often used in culture systems. Ascorbate-2-phosphate (AsAP) supplementation increases mineralized nodule formation in BM-MSCs [19], with optimal osteoinductive effects observed at 30–60 µg/mL [18, 20–22]. Concentrations exceeding 1 mM may be cytotoxic [23].

β-Glycerophosphate (βGLY) serves as a phosphate donor rather than a direct osteoinductive agent. In vitro, it supports the mineralization process by supplying phosphate ions necessary for hydroxyapatite formation [19]. Clinically, βGLY is also used to treat phosphate metabolism disorders [24].

Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol), a fat-soluble secosteroid, plays a central role in calcium and phosphate metabolism and directly influences bone mineralization [25]. In vitro studies have confirmed that vitamin D3 promotes extracellular matrix (ECM) mineralization and enhances osteogenic differentiation [6].

Vitamin K regulates proteins involved in coagulation and promotes bone mineralization and osteoblast maturation by enhancing alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and the expression of growth factors such as IGF-1 [26, 27]. At the molecular level, vitamin K serves as an essential cofactor for γ-carboxylation of vitamin K–dependent proteins such as osteocalcin and matrix Gla protein. Therefore, coordinated supplementation with vitamins D and K may enhance osteogenesis through complementary mechanisms, where vitamin D increases the availability of osteogenic matrix proteins and vitamin K supports their post-translational activation. In addition, both vitamins have been reported to influence osteogenic signaling pathways, including RUNX2-associated transcriptional programs and Wnt/β-catenin signaling, suggesting potential molecular synergy during mesenchymal stem cell differentiation.

Beyond in vitro differentiation protocols, there is growing clinical evidence indicating that adequate intake of vitamins D and K is essential for maintaining bone health. Deficiency of vitamin D has been widely associated with impaired mineral metabolism and increased fracture risk, while insufficient vitamin K intake has been linked to reduced activation of bone-related proteins such as osteocalcin and compromised bone quality. Importantly, several clinical studies suggest that combined insufficiency of both vitamins may have particularly detrimental effects on bone fragility. These observations provide a clinically relevant framework for investigating how vitamin-based supplementation may modulate osteogenic differentiation and bone-forming capacity at the cellular level [28, 29].

The aim of this study was to compare the degree of osteogenic differentiation of BM-MSCs cultured with DEX for only 1 week versus the conventional 28-day exposure, and to assess the osteogenic potential of vitamin D3 and vitamin K3 as alternative or complementary inducers.

Results and discussion

Morphology

BM-MSCs exhibited morphological changes indicative of early osteogenic differentiation as early as day 7 of culture. In all tested media, the cells transitioned from their characteristic spindle-shaped, fibroblast-like morphology to a cuboidal or polygonal shape (Fig. 1). In the DM2 group, DEX was removed from the medium after seven days. By day 14, these cells reverted to a spindle-shaped morphology resembling their pre-differentiation phenotype, in contrast to the cells cultured in DM1, DM3, and DM4. Jaiswal et al. [23] reported that morphological changes in differentiating MSCs can be observed as early as day 2 and become prominent by day 4. In their 16-day culture study, various basal media supplemented with FBS, DEX (1–1000 nM), AsAP (0.01–4 mM) or ascorbic acid (0.25 mM), and βGLY (1–10 mM) were tested. Optimal osteogenic outcomes were achieved with a combination of 100 nM DEX, 0.05 mM AsAP, and 10 mM βGLY in DMEM-based medium. This formulation has served as a standard osteogenic induction protocol for nearly three decades.

However, recent studies have questioned the use of DEX because of its potential to inhibit terminal osteoblast maturation and promote adipogenic differentiation. Della Bella et al. [30] demonstrated that 1-week DEX exposure to the first week of differentiation did not impair the mineralization capacity of MSCs.

Furthermore, Umrath et al. showed that MSCs derived from the jaw periosteum could differentiate into osteoblast-like cells in the absence of DEX when cultured in media supplemented with human platelet lysate (hPL) instead of FBS. In addition to modulating gene expression and ECM composition, DEX alters cytokine secretion profiles and induces osteoclastogenesis in peripheral blood mononuclear cells [12]. Similarly, He et al. [31] confirmed that DEX-free osteogenic differentiation was feasible when hPL was used instead of FBS.

In contrast, our findings suggest that the presence of DEX remains critical in media supplemented by FBS. Media containing βGLY and AsAP but lacking DEX failed to induce the typical morphological transformation from spindle-shaped to cuboidal cells and did not result in substantial mineralization. According to Walsh et al. [32], the physiologically relevant concentration of DEX is 10 nM. Although effective for osteogenic induction, higher concentrations (>100 nM) have been shown to impair osteoblast differentiation and are associated with glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis in vivo.

Von Kossa staining of calcium phosphate

Small, black-colored mineralized nodules of comparable density appeared in each of the four types of differentiation media after 7 days of culture. By day 14, clear differences had emerged between the DEX-free medium (DM2) and the other three media (DM1, DM3, and DM4). Mineralization in DM2 was markedly reduced compared with that in the DEX-containing groups. Interestingly, the medium lacking vitamin D3 (DM1) exhibited greater mineral deposition than DM3, which contained vitamin D3. The most extensive mineralized matrix formation was observed in cells cultured in DM4 supplemented with vitamins D3 and K3. Metzger et al. [35] reported that vitamin D3 alone does not significantly affect calcium deposition, and that additional osteoinductive factors are required to induce a fully differentiated osteoblast phenotype. Similarly, Koshihara et al. [36] investigated the role of vitamins K1, K2, and K3 in the osteogenic differentiation of human periosteal osteoblasts, both in the presence and absence of vitamin D3. They found that vitamin K alone had no significant effect on mineralization. Moreover, only vitamins K1 and K2, containing an isoprene side chain, enhanced mineralization at a concentration of 2.5 µM, whereas synthetic vitamin K3 (which lacks this structure) had no effect.

In contrast to their findings, our results demonstrated that vitamin K3 in combination with vitamin D3 accelerates mineralization and increases ALP activity. Notably, the concentration of vitamin K3 used in this study was much lower (10 nM). Previous studies have shown that vitamin K concentrations exceeding 10⁻5 M can negatively affect cell viability [37, 38].

The synergistic effect of vitamins D3 and K3 observed in this study is consistent with accumulating clinical evidence highlighting the importance of both vitamins in skeletal health. Vitamin D is essential for calcium homeostasis and osteogenic signaling, whereas vitamin K is required for the post-translational activation of osteocalcin, a key regulator of bone mineralization. Clinical reports increasingly suggest that combined deficiency of vitamins D and K may exacerbate bone fragility and contribute to osteoporosis-related fracture risk. In this context, our findings provide mechanistic cellular support for the concept that coordinated vitamin signaling contributes to effective osteogenesis and matrix maturation. Although our experiments were conducted in vitro, the observed enhancement of mineral deposition and osteogenic marker expression suggests potential relevance for translational approaches aiming to optimize bone regeneration strategies and improve physiologically relevant culture systems [39–41].

Measuring of ALP activity

In the DM1 medium, ALP activity peaked on day 14 of osteogenic differentiation, consistent with previous studies using the same medium composition [42]. A similar peak in ALP activity was observed in the other tested media (DM2, DM3, and DM4) on day 14, with no statistically significant differences compared with DM1. ALP activity measured on days 7 and 14 was comparable across all media regardless of supplementation and did not reach statistical significance.

Statistically significant differences were only observed on day 28 of differentiation. Compared with day 14, ALP activity declined in all groups, which is consistent with the expected temporal profile of osteoblast maturation. However, cells cultured in DM3 and DM4 media supplemented with vitamin D3 and both vitamins, respectively, exhibited higher residual ALP activity on day 28 than cells cultured in DM1 and DM2. The presence of vitamin D3 in DM3 appeared to help maintain ALP activity at a higher level, whereas the addition of vitamin K3 in DM4 did not further enhance enzymatic activity compared to DM3 (Fig. 4B).

ALP is a well-established early marker of osteoblast differentiation that plays a key functional role in initiating matrix mineralization by hydrolyzing phosphate-containing substrates and calcification. Previous studies have shown that BM-MSCs reach peak ALP activity between days 7 and 14, followed by a gradual decline as the cultures transition toward the mineralization phase [43]. In the present study, the observed modulation of ALP activity across different culture conditions is consistent with the differences detected in mineral deposition and osteogenic gene expression, supporting the interpretation that vitamin-based supplementation influences early osteogenic commitment rather than solely late-stage mineralization.

A limitation of this study is that osteogenic differentiation was assessed using cells at a single passage number. It is well established that mesenchymal stem cells may undergo passage-dependent changes affecting proliferation and differentiation potential. Nevertheless, cells at passage 10 in our study retained robust osteogenic capacity, as demonstrated by increased alkaline phosphatase activity, osteogenic gene expression, and mineralized matrix formation, indicating that the proposed culture strategy remains effective even after substantial in vitro expansion.

Visualization of actin filaments in maturing osteoblasts

The organization of the actin cytoskeleton was assessed on day 28 of differentiation by immunofluorescence staining with Phalloidin–Tetramethylrhodamine B isothiocyanate and compared to day 0, when the cells were still in an undifferentiated state. Prominent F-actin filaments were observed in cells cultured in DM3 and DM4, where the cytoskeleton transformed from thin microfilament bundles dispersed throughout the cytoplasm to thick actin bundles localized predominantly at the cell periphery. Actin plays a crucial role not only in cell adhesion and spreading but also in cell differentiation. As cells commit to a specific lineage, their mechanical properties adapt. Actin filaments influence both cell and nuclear shapes [44]. Unlike adipogenic differentiation, which is associated with disassembly of the actin cytoskeleton, osteogenic differentiation is characterized by increased matrix stiffness, enhanced actin polymerization, and higher density of stress fibers [45]. In addition to structural support, the cytoskeleton acts as a central component of mechanotransduction, regulating intracellular signaling pathways and gene expression in response to biomechanical stimuli [46].

Cytoskeletal architecture varies significantly among cells of mesodermal origin—such as myocytes, osteoblasts, endothelial cells, and renal epithelial cells—reflecting lineage-specific adaptations. These differences support the strong correlation between F-actin organization and osteogenic differentiation [46, 47]. Accordingly, substantial reorganization of the actin cytoskeleton occurs in MSCs undergoing osteogenic differentiation [48].

Monitoring mitochondria during osteodifferentiation

Mitochondria were visualized on day 28 using MitoTracker Red CMXRos and compared with undifferentiated control cells. As shown in Fig. 5, the cells cultured in DM2 exhibited a mitochondrial network comparable to that of undifferentiated MSCs. In contrast, cells in DM1 and DM3 displayed denser mitochondrial networks, with the highest mitochondrial concentration observed in the perinuclear region. The most extensive and dense mitochondrial network was present in the cells cultured in DM4.

Alterations in mitochondrial number, morphology, function, and intracellular distribution are closely associated with cellular energy demands and differentiation status [49]. During osteogenic differentiation of human MSCs, mitochondrial DNA content, the expression of respiratory chain enzyme subunits, intracellular ATP levels, and the efficiency of oxidative phosphorylation increase [50, 51].

Our findings suggest that standard osteogenic medium containing DEX, AsAP, and βGLY, but lacking vitamins D3 and K3, exerts a limited effect on both the actin cytoskeleton and mitochondrial remodeling, thereby reducing the extent of osteoblast maturation and functional competence. Notably, the removal of DEX after the first week of differentiation (DM2) led to morphological changes at both the subcellular and cellular levels, affecting organelles such as mitochondria and the organization of microfilaments.

Real-time PCR

To evaluate the impact of the tested supplements on the osteogenic differentiation of BM-MSCs, the transcript levels of selected osteogenic markers were analyzed using qRT-PCR.

RUNX2, a master transcription factor essential for early osteogenic commitment [52], was used as a marker of the preosteoblastic stage. In undifferentiated MSCs, RUNX2 is expressed at low levels; however, during osteodifferentiation, it is transiently upregulated in preosteoblasts and immature osteoblasts, where it promotes the expression of key bone matrix genes such as ALP, COL1A1, OCN, BMP2, and BMP4 [53–56].

In our experiment, RUNX2 expression increased by day 7 in cells cultured in DM4 (medium containing both vitamins D3 and K3) compared to media containing either one or none of the vitamins. This suggests that a combination of vitamins may accelerate the early osteogenic differentiation. The elevated expression was maintained on day 28 (Fig. 6A). The transient decrease in RUNX2 expression observed at day 14 likely reflects a transition phase from early commitment toward a more mature osteoblastic phenotype. The subsequent increase at day 28 may indicate continued differentiation and the presence of heterogeneous cell populations at different stages of osteogenesis, as well as sustained osteogenic signaling within the culture. Similar trends have been reported in other studies, such as by Chouaib et al. Kim et al. [58] reported that short-term exposure (24 h) to DEX reduces RUNX2 expression in human gingiva-derived MSCs. However, this effect was not observed in the present study. In contrast, when DEX was removed from the medium after 7 days (DM2), RUNX2 expression decreased slightly on days 14 and 28 compared with that in cells continuously exposed to DEX (DM1).

Vitamin D3 has been shown to enhance RUNX2 expression and promote its interaction with the vitamin D receptor, regulating downstream osteoblast-specific genes such as OCN. Vitamin K is crucial for the carboxylation of OCN, suggesting a synergistic role of vitamins D3 and K3 in promoting efficient osteoblast differentiation and mineralization [59].

ALP was analyzed as a marker of immature osteoblasts. As an early osteogenic marker, ALP facilitates the hydrolysis of phosphate groups and contributes to calcium phosphate deposition, supporting ECM mineralization [60]. ALP expression typically peaks around day 14 of differentiation and decreases thereafter in mature osteoblasts [61, 62].

Our results confirm this trend. ALP expression increased from day 7 in all the media, with the highest expression observed on day 28. However, the lowest expression was recorded for DM3 (vitamin D3 only) (Fig. 6B). DM4, containing both vitamins, supported earlier and stronger ALP expression, consistent with accelerated osteodifferentiation. In contrast, DEX withdrawal after 1 week (DM2) impaired ECM mineralization (Fig. 3), despite reports suggesting that the presence of AsAP and βGLY may compensate for the absence of DEX [30]. Other studies have shown that reducing DEX below 10 nM disrupts calcium deposition and reduces the expression of osteogenic markers, such as COL1A1 [63]. Although DEX can enhance osteogenic differentiation, it may also promote adipogenesis under certain conditions, particularly at concentrations of approximately 100 nM [7].

Ghali et al. [15] found that 100 nM DEX increased the mRNA levels of RUNX2, OCN, and osterix, but not ALP. Similarly, Pereira et al. [64] demonstrated that vitamin D3 concentrations above 10 nM significantly increased RUNX2 and ALP expression, which is consistent with our experimental results. Moreover, vitamin D3 supplementation in scaffolds has been shown to enhance ALP activity and hydroxyapatite formation [65]. Other studies have confirmed the positive effects of vitamin D on osteoblast differentiation and its therapeutic potential in bone regeneration [66]. Vitamin K at ≥10 nM has also been reported to enhance calcium mineralization and ALP activity [6, 38], whereas concentrations above 10⁻5 M may reduce cell viability [37]. In our study, vitamin D alone had little effect on ALP expression without vitamin K. However, ALP activity measurements (Fig. 4B) did not reveal significant differences between the groups.

OCN, also known as bone γ-carboxyglutamate protein (BGLAP), is a late-stage osteoblast marker involved in bone remodeling and energy metabolism. It is a vitamin K-dependent protein that binds calcium and hydroxyapatite, thereby supporting matrix mineralization [60, 67].

In our study (Fig. 6C), OCN expression increased rapidly in DM4 as early as day 7 and remained stable thereafter. In contrast, vitamin D3 alone (DM3) did not significantly enhance OCN expression until day 28. This delay corresponds to the findings of Metzger et al. [35], who observed vitamin D3-induced OCN upregulation by day 19. The simultaneous presence of both vitamins appears to accelerate osteoblast maturation [68].

Interestingly, OCN expression increased in the DEX-free group (DM2) on day 28 compared with that in DM1. While DEX promotes early osteogenic markers, such as RUNX2, it has been reported to suppress OCN expression at later stages by downregulating the vitamin D receptor [69, 70]. This dual effect of DEX might explain the observed differences in gene expression over time.

Vitamin K is essential for OCN carboxylation, promoting osteoblast maturation and inhibiting apoptosis and osteoclast differentiation [71]. Koshihara et al. [36] showed that vitamin D3-induced OCN mRNA expression was further enhanced by vitamin K2, although vitamin K2 alone had no effect.

BMP2, a member of the transforming growth factor beta superfamily, is a potent osteoinductive factor that initiates osteoblast differentiation through SMAD signaling pathways [72].

Our results for BMP2 expression mirrored those for OCN. Transcript levels increased during differentiation in all media except for DM1, where expression remained largely unchanged. The most rapid upregulation occurred on day 7 in the DM4 group (Fig. 6D), again supporting the synergistic effect of combined vitamin supplementation. Among the genes analyzed, only RUNX2 expression appeared to be directly affected by the presence or absence of DEX, whereas ALP, OCN, and BMP2 were primarily influenced by vitamin supplementation. DEX withdrawal also alters cell morphology, mitochondrial content, cytoskeletal organization, and ECM calcification.

Notably, mRNA levels do not always directly correlate with protein abundance or enzymatic activity because of post-transcriptional regulation, translation efficiency, protein stability, and biological variability [73]. Some studies reported strong mRNA–protein correlations, while others did not [74], which may explain the discrepancies between gene expression and ALP activity in our study.

Conclusion

The development of cost-effective and physiologically relevant in vitro osteodifferentiation protocols is essential for advancing the field of bone tissue engineering. Currently, the most widely validated osteogenic induction medium for MSCs is DMEM supplemented with 10–15% FBS, 100 nM DEX, 50 µg/mL AsAP, and 10 mM βGLY. Our results support the use of dexamethasone at 100 nM under the applied culture conditions to promote osteogenic differentiation. Although some studies have suggested that DEX may be dispensable in hPL-based systems, our findings demonstrate that removing DEX from FBS-containing media after the first week of differentiation adversely affects osteoblast maturation, as evidenced by impaired cell morphology and reduced mineralization in cells cultured in DM2 medium on days 14 and 28.

Although vitamin D3 is known to stimulate the expression of bone-related proteins such as osteocalcin and osteopontin that support calcium deposition, its effects were insufficient to drive robust mineralization when used as a single supplement. Instead, vitamin D3 appears to act as a modulatory factor that supports extracellular matrix maturation rather than a strong osteoinductive stimulus. In line with our findings, vitamin D3 acted synergistically with dexamethasone, as reflected by increased alkaline phosphatase activity and early osteogenic commitment. In this context, dexamethasone initiates differentiation, whereas vitamin D3 contributes to the progression toward a mature, mineralizing osteoblastic phenotype.

A major finding of this study is the significant osteoinductive effect of combining vitamin D3 and K3 with conventional osteogenic medium. This supplementation leads to enhanced mineral deposition, increased ALP activity, and more advanced cytoskeletal and mitochondrial development. Importantly, the observed effects were demonstrated in expanded mesenchymal stem cells, supporting the potential applicability of this approach under conditions relevant to large-scale cell manufacturing. Gene expression analysis further confirmed the accelerated upregulation of key osteogenic markers including RUNX2, ALP, OCN, and BMP2 in response to the combined vitamin treatment. The synergistic interaction between vitamins D and K appears to support both the early and late stages of osteoblast differentiation more effectively than either vitamin alone.

In contrast to commonly investigated growth factors, such as fibroblast growth factor, transforming growth factor beta, and bone morphogenetic proteins, which require supraphysiological concentrations and pose challenges related to cost, stability, and side effects, vitamin-based supplementation represents a simple, biocompatible, and economically viable strategy for promoting osteogenic commitment under near-physiological conditions. Future studies should investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying this synergy, including the interplay between vitamin D receptor signaling and vitamin K-dependent carboxylation processes. Additionally, evaluating the effects of this supplementation in three-dimensional culture systems, bioprinted scaffolds, and microfluidic organ-on-chip platforms could provide further insights into its translational potential. The influence of donor characteristics such as age, sex, and MSCs’ origin on responsiveness to vitamin supplementation also warrants further attention. Finally, the development of serum-free or xeno-free media incorporating vitamins D and K may further enhance the clinical applicability of MSC-based approaches in bone regenerative medicine.

Cell culture

The experiment was performed using a total of 48 wells seeded per experiment. For each culture condition, 12 wells were used and analyzed at four time points (days 0, 7, 14, and 28). At each timepoint, cells were harvested from independent wells to ensure biological replication. This design allowed systematic evaluation of osteogenic differentiation across all experimental conditions and time points. Normal human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BM-MSCs) (ATCC PCS-500-012™) (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were used for experiments. All experiments were performed using cells from the same commercial cell source to ensure consistency across experimental conditions. The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM high glucose), supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2.5 mM L-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 mg/mL streptomycin (Biosera Europe, Nuaille, France). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells at passage 10 were used in all experiments. This passage number was selected to ensure sufficient cell yield for the complete experimental design, including multiple culture conditions and time points, while maintaining consistency across all experiments. Importantly, the use of a higher passage number is also relevant from a translational perspective, as clinical applications typically require extensive in vitro expansion to obtain adequate cell numbers for therapeutic use.

Osteogenic differentiation

Osteogenic differentiation was evaluated at defined cultivation time points (days 0, 7, 14, and 28), as illustrated in Fig. 7. Cells were seeded at a density of 2,000 cells per cm2 in 12-well plates. After 24 h of cultivation in basal medium, cells reached the recommended 80% confluence for the initiation of osteogenic differentiation, in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Control samples (day 0) were collected, and the culture medium was replaced with one of the four tested differentiation media. Osteogenic medium was prepared in four different ways by supplementing the basal culture medium with dexamethasone, 2-phospho-L-ascorbic acid trisodium salt, β-glycerophosphate disodium salt hydrate, vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol), and vitamin K3 (menadione) (all from Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), as specified in Table 1. Vitamin K3 is a synthetic vitamin K analogue and is not the predominant dietary form of vitamin K in humans. Nevertheless, it is frequently used in in vitro studies due to its defined chemical structure, solubility, and reproducibility, and it can serve as a practical model compound for investigating vitamin K-dependent processes related to extracellular matrix maturation. Composition of differentiation media

DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 mg/mL streptomycin, 2.5 mM L-glutamine stable and various concentration of osteogenic supplements: dexamethasone (DEX), 2-phospho-L-ascorbic acid trisodium salt (AsAP), β-glycerophosphate disodium salt hydrate (βGLY), vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) and vitamin K3 (menadione)

To minimize degradation of light-sensitive supplements, stock solutions of DEX, vitamin D3, and vitamin K3 were prepared under reduced ambient light conditions and stored protected from light. All vitamin-containing stock solutions were aliquoted in amber microtubes and stored at −80 °C until use. During osteogenic medium preparation, vitamin supplements were added immediately before use, and media were kept protected from light by covering tubes and culture vessels with aluminum foil. Special care was taken for vitamin K3 due to its higher photosensitivity; all handling steps were performed rapidly and under light-protected conditions.

The experimental design omitted a group treated with vitamin K alone, as previous literature suggests that vitamin K is not a primary inducer of osteogenic differentiation in isolation but rather modulates the activity of vitamin D-dependent pathways and enhances the post-translational activation of bone matrix proteins [68]. Cells were cultured in the respective differentiation media for 4 weeks. The medium was refreshed twice weekly. Samples for histological staining, gene expression analysis, and ALP activity measurements were performed in triplicate. All triplicates represent biological replicates unless stated otherwise. For gene expression analysis, each biological replicate was analyzed in technical duplicate, while alkaline phosphatase activity was measured in technical triplicate. Histological staining was performed using biological replicates only.

Vitamin D3 and vitamin K3 were both used at a final concentration of 10 nM. These concentrations were selected to represent a low-dose supplementation strategy within the nanomolar range, aiming to maintain physiological relevance during long-term culture and to minimize potential cytotoxicity or oxidative stress, which has been reported particularly for vitamin K3 at higher concentrations. The goal of this study was not to maximize osteogenic output by supraphysiological dosing, but rather to evaluate whether modest vitamin supplementation can enhance osteogenic differentiation when combined with standard osteogenic inducers.

Morphology of living differentiating cells

Cells cultured for 24 h in basal medium (control), as well as cells incubated for 7, 14, and 28 days in the four differentiation media, were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and imaged in Live Cell Imaging Solution (1×) (LCIS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using an Olympus CKX41 inverted microscope (Olympus Europa SE, Hamburg, Germany) at the respective time points.

Von Kossa staining of calcium phosphate

Von Kossa staining was used to assess biological mineralization in differentiated osteoblasts. This method visualizes calcium phosphate deposition, a hallmark of osteoblast maturation, using silver ions that react with phosphate to form brown to black precipitates [33].

To detect mineralization, the cells were washed twice with PBS and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in PBS for 30 min. After fixation, the cells were washed again with PBS and incubated with 2.5% silver nitrate (AgNO3) for 20 min. Subsequently, the cells were rinsed twice with deionized water and incubated with 0.5% hydroquinone for 2 min at room temperature in the dark. After removing the hydroquinone solution, the cells were treated with 5% sodium thiosulfate (Na2S2O3) for 2 min, followed by a final rinse with deionized water. The stained samples were then air-dried and imaged using an Olympus CKX41 inverted microscope (Olympus, Europa SE, Hamburg, Germany). The images were acquired from the central region of each culture dish, which consistently represented the primary site of mineral deposition. This standardized approach was applied across all experimental groups to ensure representative and unbiased image acquisition.

Alizarin Red staining and quantification of deposited calcium

Alizarin Red staining detects calcium ions and serves as a useful complement to von Kossa staining, which identifies phosphate ions, in the assessment of osteoblast mineralization [75]. After 0, 7, 14, and 28 days of incubation and differentiation, the cells were washed twice with PBS and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in PBS for 30 min. Following fixation, the cells were rinsed with PBS and stained with 1% Alizarin Red S solution (pH 4.18; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 20 min. After removing the staining solution, the cells were washed with deionized water and imaged using an Olympus CKX41 inverted microscope (Olympus Europa SE, Hamburg, Germany).

To quantify calcium deposition, bound Alizarin Red was extracted using 10% cetylpyridinium chloride (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) dissolved in 10 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) for 60 min at room temperature. The absorbance was measured at 562 nm using a Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). This procedure allowed relative comparison of mineral deposition between experimental groups. All samples were processed under identical conditions to ensure valid comparative assessment across groups.

Measuring of ALP activity

ALP activity was quantified in cell lysates using the BIOLATEST ® Erba Alkaline Phosphatase MEG kit (Erba Diagnostics Mannheim GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) following the constant-time enzymatic method. Frozen cells were lysed in 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), sonicated using an ultrasonic cleaner USC 100TH (VWR International, Darmstadt, Germany) in an ice bath for 10 min, and centrifuged at 4 °C, 16,000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatants were collected for analysis. The absorbance was measured at 420 nm in technical triplicate using a Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). The results were normalized to total protein concentration.

Total protein content measurement

Total protein concentrations in lysates prepared as described above were determined using a modified linearized Bradford protein assay [76]. Briefly, 10 μL of the collected supernatant was mixed with 200 μL Bradford reagent and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. Absorbance was measured at 590 and 450 nm using a Synergy H1 microplate reader (Biotek, Winooski, VT, USA). Protein concentrations were calculated based on the A590/A450 ratio using a calibration curve generated by serial dilution of a 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) standard (BioConcept Ltd., Allschwil, Switzerland).

Visualization of actin filaments in maturing osteoblasts

Cells cultured in differentiation medium at various time points were washed twice with PBS and fixed overnight at 4 °C in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in PBS. This was followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min at room temperature and blocking with 1% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Between each step, the cells were rinsed three times with PBS.

Immunofluorescence staining for filamentous actin (F-actin) was performed using Phalloidin–Tetramethylrhodamine B isothiocyanate (TRITC; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Samples were incubated with 100 μg/mL fluorescent phalloidin conjugate in PBS containing 1% BSA for 1 h in the dark at room temperature to reduce non-specific background staining. Nuclei were counterstained with one drop of NucBlue™ Live ReadyProbes™ reagent (Hoechst 33342; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Eugene, OR, USA) per 1 mL of PBS for 30 min.

The stained cells were visualized using an Olympus CKX41 inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus Europa SE, Hamburg, Germany). Image processing was performed using the ImageJ software (W.S. Rasband, U.S. National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Monitoring of mitochondria during osteodifferentiation

Cultured cells were stained with MitoTracker™ Red CMXRos (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Eugene, Oregon, USA) to label and visualize the mitochondria, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the cells were thoroughly washed with PBS and incubated with 100 nM MitoTracker™ Red CMXRos working solution in serum-free DMEM for 30 min at 37 °C. The staining solution was then replaced with fresh, pre-warmed complete medium containing one drop of NucBlue™ Live ReadyProbes™ Reagent per mL for staining, and cells were incubated for an additional 30 min at 37 °C. The cells were then rinsed with PBS at each step.

For imaging, the culture medium was replaced with LCIS. The stained cells were analyzed using an Olympus CKX41 inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Europa SE, Hamburg, Germany). Image processing was performed using the ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All measurements were performed using a minimum of three biological replicates per group. Statistical analysis was performed using a two-tailed unpaired t-test to compare predefined pairs of experimental conditions. This approach was chosen as the study focused on specific pairwise comparisons between culture media rather than on exploratory comparison across all groups. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed using the SigmaPlot 12.5 software (Systat Software Inc., San Jose, CA, USA).
