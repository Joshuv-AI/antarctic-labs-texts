Improving nutritional quality and aflatoxin detoxification of peanut meal by co-fermentation with Weizmannia coagulans, Bacillus subtilis, and supplemented enzymes
Authors: Youwei Chen, Hao Huang, Dan Li, Limei Zou, Wendi Yu, Weiwei Dong, Xiang Yu, Yanli Feng, Jun Liu, Shumiao Zhao, Yunxiang Liang, Yuanliang Hu, Nan Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeanut meal, a high-protein agricultural by-product, faces challenges as animal feed due to anti-nutritional factors, poor protein digestibility, aflatoxin contamination, and imbalanced amino acids. Microbial fermentation is one of the most effective methods to reduce anti-nutritional factors and enhance the nutritional value of peanut meal. Compared to single-strain fermentation, microbial-enzyme co-fermentation exhibits enhanced degradation efficiency, accelerates nutrient release, improves product safety by reducing mycotoxins and anti-nutritional factors, enhances sensory properties, and increases fermentation consistency and stability. However, research on microbial-enzyme co-fermentation of peanut meal remains limited, particularly regarding the co-fermentation of Weizmannia coagulans and Bacillus subtilis with enzyme preparations, which has yet to be systematically investigated. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate and optimize the use of Weizmannia coagulans for microbial-enzyme co-fermentation to enhance the nutritional quality and reduce anti-nutritional factors in peanut meal.ResultsPeanut meal fermentation with Weizmannia coagulans BC01, Bacillus subtilis BS27, and the synergistic enzyme system (acid protease and hemicellulose) was optimized through single factor experiments and response surface methodology. This process led to significant reductions in crude fiber (from 7.05 to 2.88%) and anti-nutritional factors, with trypsin inhibitors decreasing from 0.30 to 0.03% and phytic acid from 1.43 to 0.35%. Aflatoxin B1 was reduced from 43.87 µg/kg to 6.20 µg/kg. Nutritional quality improved markedly, with flavonoid content increasing from 1.2 to 3.01%, reducing sugars increasing from 0.7 to 7.02%, and total acids increasing from 1.65 to 5.92%. Protein composition and digestibility were markedly improved, with crude protein, acid-soluble protein, and small peptide contents increasing by 18.2%, 546%, and 447%, respectively. Additionally, the degree of protein hydrolysis and in vitro digestibility rose to 40.77% and 68.91%, respectively. Total amino acid content increased by 14.3%, contributing to a more balanced amino acid profile.ConclusionThe study indicates that microbial-enzyme co-fermentation involving W. coagulans effectively reduces anti-nutritional factors in peanut meal while enhancing its nutritional value. These findings provide a sustainable approach to transforming peanut meal into high-value animal feed, offering a practical solution to address the protein feed supply gap. Further validation via animal trials is necessary to evaluate its efficacy as a replacement for conventional protein feed sources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12934-025-02816-8.Keywords: Peanut meal, Weizmannia coagulans, Microbe-enzyme synergistic fermentation, High-protein feed

Introduction

Antibiotics have been widely applied in animal husbandry for diseases prevention and growth promotion. However, this practice has led to the emergence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria, antibiotic residues in food product, and environmental contamination, posing serious risks to public health and the sustainability of animal agriculture [1]. The global trend toward antibiotic-free livestock production has further spurred the development of new, efficient, and eco-friendly feed alternatives [2, 3]. Peanut meal is a by-product obtained during oil extraction from Arachis hypogaea (peanut) seeds, typically through mechanical pressing or solvent extraction [4]. Peanut meal is a rich protein source that also contains polysaccharides, minerals, and vitamins. However, its application in animal feed is limited by the presence of anti-nutritional factors, limited digestibility of macromolecular proteins, and contamination by aflatoxin [5]. At present, high-quality protein feeds are largely derived from fish meal and soybean meal, contributing to persistently high production costs [6]. Therefore, converting underutilized peanut meal into high-quality protein feed through microbial fermentation not only promotes resources efficiency but also reduces costs in the livestock industry, thereby enabling value-added utilization of this low-cost by-product.

Weizmannia coagulans (formerly Bacillus coagulans) is a lactic acid-producing probiotic widely distributed in nature [7]. This bacterium exhibits strong tolerance, allowing it to survive, colonize and function in the gastrointestinal tract. Its probiotic mechanisms include oxygen scavenging, inhibition of pathogenic bacteria, secretion of antimicrobial peptides and digestive enzymes, modulation of the host immune response, and the production of organic acids and extracellular polysaccharides [8, 9]. Owing to its spore-forming ability, B. coagulans can survive harsh gastrointestinal conditions, supporting its application in modulating immune responses, preventing and treating intestinal disorders, and enhancing the level of crude protein, acid-soluble protein, and small peptide content in feed [10]. For instance, studies have shown that administration of B. coagulans to broilers infected with Clostridium perfringens significantly upregulates intestinal immune-related cytokines, thereby enhancing mucosal immune response [11]. Cottonseed meal, an important plant-derived protein source for animal feed, contains the anti-nutritional compound gossypol, which can be effectively detoxified through solid-state fermentation by B. coagulans [12]. However, research on microbial-enzyme co-fermentation of peanut meal remains limited, and the use of W. coagulans combination with enzyme preparations has not yet been reported.

This study aimed to improve the nutritional quality and safety of peanut meal while reducing anti-nutritional factors and aflatoxin through synergistic fermentation involving W. coagulans, Bacillus subtilis, and enzyme preparations. By optimizing solid-state fermentation conditions and evaluating the fermentation effects, this research provides a novel technique for the value-added and sustainable use of peanut meal, this work lays the foundation for alleviating the shortage of high-quality protein feed and enhancing animal production efficiency.

Materials and methods

Strain selection and identification

Fecal samples were collected from a poultry farm in Huangshi, Hubei Province. To eliminate non-thermotolerant bacteria, the samples were treated in a water bath at 80 °C for 10 min. The treated samples were then subjected to 10-fold serial dilutions in sterile water up to a dilution factor of 10⁶. Subsequently, 100 µL of each dilution was plated on modified MRS agar medium [13] supplemented with 0.4% bromocresol purple and incubated at 45 °C for 24 h. A total of 24 single colonies were obtained. Colonies exhibiting phenotypic characteristics consistent with W. coagulans [14] were further purified by streaking twice to obtain pure cultures. The purified isolates were identified by PCR amplification of the 16 S rRNA gene using universal primers followed by sequencing.

Tolerance and antibacterial properties of the strain

The strain was activated on MRS solid medium at 45 °C for 24 h, followed by inoculation into liquid MRS and cultured at 45 °C with shaking at 160 rpm for 12 h. A 2 mL aliquot of the culture was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 10 min, and the resulting cell pellet was resuspended in an equal volume of PBS buffer to obtain a suspension. Equal volumes of this suspension were added to 10 mL of PBS containing bile salts at concentrations of 0.1%, 0.3%, and 0.5% (pH 7.0), which served as the experimental groups. PBS without bile salt was used as the control group in this step. After incubation for 1 h, the viable cell counts were determined to assess bile salt tolerance. The suspension was diluted fivefold and exposed to temperatures of 60 °C, 70 °C, 80 °C, and 90 °C for 30 min to assess thermotolerance, with samples incubated at room temperature serving as controls. Additionally, the cell suspension was resuspended in PBS buffer with pH values of 2, 3, and 4, while pH 7 PBS was used as the control. After stirring for 1-hour, viable cell counts were determined to evaluate acid tolerance. Antibiotic susceptibility testing was conducted using the disc diffusion method [15]. Inhibition zone diameters were measured after 24-hour incubation at 45 °C to determine antibiotic sensitivity.

Screening of compound strain and inoculation ratio

Four safe bacterial strains—Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Lactobacillus plantarum, Bacillus subtilis BS27, and Bacillus licheniformis—all of which are listed in the feed additive catalog, were selected for use in both single and co-fermentation of peanut meal due to their distinct functional characteristics. S. cerevisiae, isolated from the Baijiu fermentation process, is known for enhancing flavor, palatability, and nutrient enrichment through its metabolic activities, and also possesses the capacity to degrade certain non-cellulosic polysaccharides [16]. Lactobacillus plantarum, Bacillus subtilis BS27, and Bacillus licheniformis were isolated from the Baijiu stacking process. L. plantarum, a lactic acid bacterium, exhibits strong acidification capacity and probiotic potential [17], which may act synergistically with W. coagulans to create an acidic environment conducive to the degradation of anti-nutritional factors. B. subtilis is well-known for secreting a wide range of extracellular enzymes, including proteases and amylases, that facilitate the breakdown of proteins and starches, thereby enhancing digestibility [18]. B. licheniformis is particularly recognized for its ability to produce thermostable enzymes such as alkaline proteases and phytases, which are effective in degrading heat-stable anti-nutritional compounds like phytic acid [19, 20]. This combination of strains was selected to evaluate potential synergistic effects in improving the nutritional quality, digestibility, and detoxification capacity of peanut meal.

During fermentation, acid production, viable cell count, spore count, crude protein content, acid-soluble protein content, and small peptide content were measured to determine the most suitable co-fermenting strain for W. coagulans BC01. The optimal co-fermenting strains were then tested at different inoculation ratios of 1:1, 1:2, 1:3, 2:1, and 3:1, with the above parameters being measured to determine the optimal ratio.

Single-factor experiment

Fermentation optimization experiments were conducted using the following baseline conditions: temperature 40 °C, fermentation time 48 h, solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:1 (w/v), inoculum volume 10% (v/w), and the previously identified optimal strain combination and inoculation ratio. To further refine the fermentation parameters, the effects of different temperatures (30, 35, 40, 45, and 50 °C), fermentation times (24, 36, 48, 60, and 72 h), solid-to-liquid ratios (1:0.5, 1:1, 1:1.5, 1:2, and 1:2.5), inoculum volumes (5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% v/m), and inorganic salt types (5 g/L of NaCl, MnSO₄, MgSO₄, CaCl₂, and KH₂PO₄) were systematically evaluated. In addition, compound enzyme preparations consisting of acid protease (200,000 U/mg, Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd.) and hemicellulase (50,000 U/mg, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.) were tested at different mixing ratios (3:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3) and total enzyme dosages (100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 U/g).

The main fermentation outcomes analyzed included total acid production, viable cell count, spore count, crude protein content, acid-soluble protein content, and small peptide content in the fermented peanut meal. Based on the optimal inorganic salt type identified, further experiments were conducted to determine the optimal concentration (1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 g/L).

Plackett-Burman experimental design

Based on the results from the single-factor experiments, an inoculation ratio of 2:1 for W. coagulans and B. subtilis, and an addition ratio of 2:1 for acid protease to hemicellulase were selected, while the inorganic salt additive was set as a constant. Six key factors influencing fermentation efficiency—temperature (A), fermentation time (B), inoculation amount (C), solid-to-liquid ratio (D), CaCl₂ addition (E), and enzyme complex addition (F)—were chosen for a Plackett-Burman experiment using a two-level (−1, 1) factorial design, as shown in Table S1.

Steepest ascent experimental design

Based on the results of the single-factor experiments and Plackett-Burman test, the factors significantly affecting small peptide content in peanut meal were selected. The direction of factor adjustment and the step size for each level were determined using the regression coefficient equations from the Plackett-Burman experiment, and the steepest ascent experiment was conducted accordingly (Table S2).

Box-Behnken response surface design

Using temperature (40 °C), inoculation amount (15%), CaCl₂ (3 g/L), and a 2:1 ratio of acid protease to hemicellulase (U/U) as constants, a Box-Behnken experiment was designed based on the results of the steepest ascent test. The experiment included three factors—fermentation time, enzyme complex addition, and solid-to-liquid ratio—each at three levels to identify the optimal and sub-optimal combinations (Table S3).

Physicochemical properties determination

After fermentation, 3.00 g of peanut meal was suspended in 27 mL of sterile water and shaken at 180 rpm for 30 min, and the pH of the supernatant was measured. Spore counts were determined using the plate count method following heat treatment of the culture at 80 °C for 15 min and cooling in an ice bath for 3 min. To qualitatively detect antigen proteins, 1.00 g of sample was extracted with 10.00 mL of 0.1 mol/L Tris-HCL buffer (pH 8.0) by shaking for 2 h. The suspension was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Sample volumes were determined based on equivalent protein content, and SDS-PAGE electrophoresis was performed.

The content of aflatoxin B1 was determined using an ELISA quantification kit (Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), following the instructions provided by the manufacturer. Crude protein content was measured by the Kjeldahl method according to GB/T 6432 − 2018: a 0.5 g sample was accurately weighed, mixed with 10 mL of sulfuric acid and catalyst, digested using a digestion instrument, followed by distillation and titration using an automated Kjeldahl analyzer. Acid-soluble protein content was determined by weighing 0.2 g of sample, adding 10 mL of 15% trichloroacetic acid solution and 10 mL of distilled water, mixing thoroughly, shaking at 180 rpm for 30 min, and centrifuging at 9000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant (10 mL) was used to determine protein content as described above. Small peptide content was calculated as the ratio of acid-soluble protein to crude protein. Degree of hydrolysis (DH) of protein was measured using the formaldehyde titration method. The in vitro digestibility of peanut meal was determined using Betchem’s two-step hydrolysis method. The contents of trypsin inhibitor (TI) and phytic acid (PA) were determined according to the Chinese National Standards GB 5009.224–2016 (for trypsin inhibitor) and GB 5009.153–2016 (for phytic acid), respectively.

For amino acid analysis, 0.01 g of sample was mixed with 10 mL of 6 mol/L hydrochloric acid and hydrolyzed under nitrogen at 110 °C for 24 h. The acid hydrolysate was then filtered. A 20 µL aliquot of the acid hydrolysate was freeze-dried, followed by addition of 20 µL of buffer. After mixing, 20 µL of derivatizing reagent was added, the mixture was heated at 60 °C for 30 min, cooled, and diluted five-fold for HPLC analysis to determine the amino acid content [21].

Data processing and analysis

All experimental results were performed in triplicate and expressed as “mean ± standard deviation.” Data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel and IBM SPSS Statistics 25 software. Single-factor ANOVA was conducted using SPSS, and the results were plotted using Origin 2018 software.

Results and discussion

Screening and identification of the strain

Based on the distinct morphological characteristics of W. coagulans (Fig. S1), we successfully isolated a target bacterial strain. Genetic sequencing subsequently identified the strain as W. coagulans, which was designated BC01 (Fig. 1). During fermentation, the accumulation of organic acids, particularly L-lactic acid, increased progressively over time (Fig. S2). This accumulation led to a significant decrease in culture pH, which is consistent with the known fermentation profile of W. coagulans.

Phylogenetic tree of strain BC01 based 16 S rDNA sequence

Tolerance and antimicrobial properties of the strain

The tolerance and antibiotic sensitivity of the W. coagulans BC01 strain were evaluated. The strain BC01 exhibited high resistance to acidic pH, elevated temperatures, and bile salts. BC01 exhibited a survival rate of 92.8% at pH 4, and 54.2% viability at pH 2, indicating strong acid tolerance. (Table S4). BC01 exhibited survival rates of 94.37%, 84.52%, and 73.87% at 60 °C, 70 °C, and 80 °C, respectively, and maintained 51.02% viability at 90 °C, indicating strong thermotolerance (Table S5). Moreover, BC01 maintained 76.37% viability at a bile salt concentration of 0.5% (Table S6), further supporting its adaptability to the animal gastrointestinal environment. Antibiotic sensitivity testing (Table S7) indicated that BC01 was susceptible to 13 antibiotics, including vancomycin, tetracycline, and chloramphenicol, and exhibited intermediate resistance only to ciprofloxacin and ceftriaxone, suggesting a low potential for antibiotic resistance. These results indicate that BC01 exhibits excellent probiotic tolerance and poses a low risk of antibiotic-resistant development, supporting its potential application in animal gut health regulation and pathogen suppression.

Screening of mixed strains and optimal inoculation ratio

This study evaluated the fermentation of peanut meal using both monocultures and mixed cultures. L. plantarum, whether used alone or in co-fermentation with BC01, exhibited the highest acid production under all fermentation conditions, with BC01 monoculture producing slightly less (Fig. 2a). In terms of sporulation, BC01 exhibited the highest spore count when fermentation alone, followed by its co-culture with B. subtilis (Fig. 2b). Fermentation of peanut meal with B. subtilis, either alone or in combination with W. coagulans, significantly increased the levels of acid-soluble proteins and small peptides content compared to other treatments. These protein fractions are more readily absorbed by animals, enhancing the nutritional value of the final product. This improvement is primarily attributed to the strong proteolytic capacity of B. subtilis. As shown in Fig. 2c, W. coagulans alone exhibited limited proteolytic activity and contributed minimally to protein degradation. Considering these factors, the co-fermentation of W. coagulans and B. subtilis was identified as the optimal strategy.

Optimization of strain ratios revealed that a 2:1 ratio of W. coagulans 01 to B. subtilis 27 achieved the highest acid production (Fig. 2d). Concurrently, this ratio also yielded relatively high viable cell counts, spore counts, and increased levels of acid-soluble protein and small peptide (Fig. 2e and f). So, the 2:1 inoculation ratio was selected for subsequent fermentation experiments to further enhance the nutritional value of peanut meal.

Single-factor experiment

The fermentation process is critically dependent on microbial activity, particularly the role of viable probiotic strains in modulating the intestinal microbiota and enhancing nutrient digestibility in animals. Accordingly, monitoring microbial counts in fermented peanut meal serves as an important indicator for evaluating feed microbiological quality. The production of organic acids by W. coagulans during fermentation lowers the pH of the medium; A moderate decrease in pH can enhance feed palatability and promote protein hydrolysis, whereas excessive acidification may irritate the gastrointestinal tract. Therefore, monitoring pH changes is essential for maintaining an optimal acidity level in fermented feed. Additionally, crude protein, acid-soluble protein, and small peptides serve as key indicators for evaluating the nutritional quality of protein-rich feed.

Temperature has a profound effect on microbial growth, metabolism, and the production of bioactive substances, which subsequently affect enzyme activity [22, 23]. The acid peaked at 50 °C (Fig. 3a), whereas the maximum spore count was recorded at 40 °C (Fig. 3b). The levels of crude protein, acid-soluble protein, and small peptides peaked at both 40 °C and 45 °C (Fig. 3c). Based on microbial viability, protein degradation efficiency, and energy requirements, 40 °C was selected as the optimal fermentation temperature.

As fermentation progresses, available nutrients in the fermentation system gradually decreases, requiring a balance between microbial proliferation and the preservation of feed nutritional value [24]. As depicted in Fig. 3d, the pH stabilized around 8 after 86 h of fermentation, whereas viable cell counts progressively declined. In contrast, spore counts remained high at 48, 60, and 72 h (Fig. 3e). Meanwhile, the contents of small peptides and acid-soluble protein increased over time, stabilized after 48 h (Fig. 3f), suggesting that enzyme activity and protein hydrolysis reached an optimal level at this stage. Considering the balance between microbial viability and nutritional retention, 48 h was chosen as the optimal fermentation time for subsequent experiments.

The solid-to-liquid ratio strongly impacts oxygen availability, heat generation, and enzyme diffusion efficiency during fermentation [25]. Acid production increased initially and then decreased with an increasing solid-to-liquid ratio, reaching the highest pH at a ratio of 1:1.5(Fig. 3g). At a ratio of 1:1.2, the viable cell and spore counts reached their highest level, significantly exceeding those of other groups (Fig. 3h). Nutritional analysis showed that acid-soluble protein and small peptides contents peaked at a ratio of 1:1.5 (Fig. 3i). Therefore, a ratio at 1:1.5 was determined to be the optimal solid-to-liquid ratio for fermentation.

Excessive inoculum levels may trigger nutrient competition and quorum-sensing effects, that suppress microbial growth; while insufficient inoculation can result in incomplete fermentation [26]. Experimental results showed that inoculum level had no significant effect on acid production (Fig. 3j). The highest viable cell and spore counts were observed at an inoculum level of 5%, although spore formation slightly declined at higher levels, with viable cell counts remaining relatively stable (Fig. 3k). Crude protein content peaked at inoculum levels of 15% and 20% (Fig. 3l), suggesting enhanced protein accumulation at higher inoculation densities. Based on a comprehensive evaluation of microbial activity and nutrient enrichment, 15% was selected as the optimal inoculum level for subsequent fermentation.

Inorganic salts play a crucial role in spore formation, with divalent cations such as Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ also being essential for microbial enzyme production [27]. The addition of inorganic salts had no significant effect on acid production (Fig. 3m). Although both Ca²⁺ and Mn²⁺ promoted viable cell and spore formation (Fig. 3n); CaCl₂, exhibited superior performance compared to MnSO₄ in acid-soluble protein and small peptide accumulation (Fig. 3o). Consequently, CaCl₂ was selected as the most suitable inorganic salt additive. Different concentrations of CaCl₂ significantly elevated spore counts, with the highest viable cell observed at 5 g/L (Fig. 3q). Notably, crude protein, acid-soluble protein, and small peptide contents peaked at 3 g/L (Fig. 3r). Thus, 3 g/L was selected as the optimal concentration of CaCl₂ for enhancing the nutritional quality of fermented peanut meal.

The addition of a composite enzyme synergistically enhances microbial degradation of macromolecules like proteins and polysaccharides in peanut meal [28]. The composite enzyme treatment significantly increased small peptide content, indicating improved protein hydrolysis efficiency (Fig. 3u). Lower pH values and higher viable cell counts were observed when the ratio of acid protease to hemicellulase was 1:2 or 1:3 (Fig. 3s and t). In contrast, the highest spore count and peak acid-soluble protein content were achieved at a 2:1 enzyme ratio (Fig. 3t and u), indicating that different enzyme proportions distinctly affect fermentation performance. Therefore, the optimal enzyme addition ratio was determined to be 2:1. With increasing levels of compound enzyme addition, both the pH value and spore count of fermented peanut meal initially increased and then declined (Fig. 3v and w). In contrast, the number of viable bacteria and total protein content remained relatively stable without distinct trends (Fig. 3w and x). A U-shaped response was observed in small peptide content, with levels decreasing initially and rising again as the enzyme addition increased (Fig. 3x). Based on the combined evaluation of pH and small peptide content, an enzyme addition level of 500 U/g was determined to be optimal for inoculation.

Response surface results

Based on the response surface methodology results (Table 3) and the analysis of variance (ANOVA) results (Table S9), a regression equation was established as follows: y = 40.49 + 3.44 A + 3.54B + 1.31 C + 1.57AB − 0.8858AC + 1.02BC − 5.24 A² − 7.94B² − 5.82 C. The response surface model was statistically significant. The model had a high correlation coefficient (R² = 0.9766, Radj² = 0.9465), a low coefficient of variation (CV% < 10%), and high precision (Ap > 4), demonstrating good model fitting that can be used to predict small peptide content. Fermentation time (A) and composite enzyme addition level (B) significantly influenced small peptide content, while the solid-to-liquid ratio (C) had no significant effect. The quadratic terms A², B², and C² showed particularly significant effects, indicating a nonlinear relationship between factors and the response value. The factors influencing small peptide content, in order of importance, were: composite enzyme addition (B) > fermentation time (A) > solid-to-liquid ratio (C).

Response surface analysis and validation

Response surface interaction contour plots (Fig. 4a-c) revealed significant interactions between AB, AC, and BC. The response surface 3D plots (Fig. 4d-f) indicated that fermentation time, composite enzyme addition, and solid-to-liquid ratio had a significant influence on the outcome, with a maximum response value existing within the range of the center points, demonstrating the reliability of the experimental results. Using ANOVA analysis in Design-Expert 13.0 software, under fixed conditions (temperature of 40 °C, inoculum size of 15%, CaCl₂ addition of 3 g/L, and composite enzyme ratio of 2:1), the predicted optimal fermentation conditions were determined as follows: fermentation time of 64.305 h, composite enzyme addition of 513.272 U/g, and solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:1.922, with the theoretical small peptide content expected to be 41.65%.

Effect of interaction on the small peptide content in fermented peanut meal

The model was validated experimentally by adjusting the conditions to fermentation time of 64 h, composite enzyme addition of 515 U/g, and solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:1.9. The experimentally obtained small peptide content after fermentation was 41.69%, consistent with the model predictions, confirming the reliability of the model.

Evaluation of peanut meal feed quality

The fermentation process involving strain selection and optimization was able to significantly enhance the small peptide content in peanut meal, demonstrating the effectiveness of solid-state fermentation for enhancing plant-based feed protein content. Evaluating the feed quality of peanut meal fermented under optimized conditions provides valuable insights into the feasibility of using mixed fermentation to enhance the nutritional value of peanut meal as a novel protein feed.

Fermentation significantly improved the nutritional quality of peanut meal. After fermentation, the contents of moisture, ash, crude fat, and nitrogen-free extract in peanut meal decreased, while the contents of dry matter, total sugars, flavonoids, reducing sugars, and total acids increased significantly (Fig. S3). Notably, the crude fiber content decreased from 7.05 to 2.88%, making it more digestible for monogastric animals. The increase in reducing sugars suggested that non-starch polysaccharides, such as starch and cellulose, were degraded, providing an additional energy source for animals. The increased total acid content improved feed palatability while reducing energy expenditure during digestion. Furthermore, the increased flavonoid content after fermentation could enhance the anti-inflammatory and disease resistance capabilities of animals. Overall, peanut meal fermentation with W. coagulans proved to be an effective method for enhancing the nutritional value of peanut meal, providing a promising approach for animal feed production.

After fermentation, the crude protein content of peanut meal (Fig. 5a) increased significantly, primarily due to microbial consumption of non-protein substances, resulting in a protein concentration effect and the formation of microbial protein through microbial growth. Additionally, the content of acid-soluble protein (Fig. 5b) as well as small peptides (Fig. 5c) increased significantly, reflecting improved digestibility in the animal gut. The in vitro digestibility of peanut meal increased by 27.19% after fermentation (Fig. 5d), enhancing energy conversion efficiency, lowering the feed conversion ratio, and improving animal welfare.

Aflatoxin B1 (AFB1) is a mycotoxin widely distributed in nature, with significant negative effects on animal growth and survival [29]. AFB1 content was effectively reduced from 43.87 µg/kg to 6.20 µg/kg after fermentation (Fig. 5e), which is below the feed safety standard limit of 20 µg/kg set by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and the World Health Organization (WHO) [30], verifying the effectiveness of bioremediation. This finding aligns with previous reports showing that Bacillus subtilis can reduce AFB1 content in corn gluten meal by up to 66.08% [31], supporting the detoxification potential observed in our study. The ability of B. subtilis to degrade AFB1 has been attributed to the production of specific oxidoreductases involved in bacilysin biosynthesis [32]. In addition, W. coagulans has also demonstrated AFB1 detoxification potential, with the laccase enzyme Lac-W identified as a key contributor to its aflatoxin-degrading activity [33]. These results support the detoxifying potential of the co-fermentation system employed in this study.

Trypsin inhibitors are one of the major anti-nutritional factors in feed, suppressing the activity of trypsin in animals and negatively affecting protein digestion, feed utilization, and growth performance [34]. Our study found that the trypsin inhibitor content in fermented peanut meal decreased significantly from 0.30 to 0.03% (Fig. 5f), which may be attributed to the combined action of externally added acid proteases and the proteases produced by B. subtilis, which degrade trypsin inhibitors into small peptides or amino acids [35]. At the same time, the acidification of B. coagulans creates favorable conditions for exogenously added acidic proteases, and destroys the plant cell wall structure, increasing the accessibility of proteins and anti-nutritional factors, making them more easily hydrolyzed by enzymes. The proteases and amylases secreted by B. subtilis in turn provide carbon and nitrogen sources for the growth of B. coagulans. Phytic acid, a common anti-nutritional factor in plant feed, forms stable complexes with multivalent metal ions, hindering mineral absorption in the intestine [36]. Fermentation significantly reduced phytic acid content from 1.43 to 0.35% (Fig. 5g), potentially improving mineral bioavailability in feed. This effect is likely attributed to the activity of phytase enzymes, which have been reported in both B. coagulans and B. subtilis [37, 38]. These results suggest that co-fermentation could effectively reduce the anti-nutritional factor content of peanut meal, improving its digestibility and utilization efficiency, thus supporting feed quality enhancement and improved animal growth performance.

Fermentation significantly increased the degree of protein hydrolysis, rising from 5.81 to 40.77% (Fig. 5h). This indicates that proteins were more effectively hydrolyzed into small peptides and free amino acids after fermentation, which are more readily absorbed and utilized by the digestive tract of animals. The SDS-PAGE gel analysis (Fig. 5i) showed that prior to fermentation, the major macromolecular antigenic proteins in peanut meal were located within the 20–70 kDa range, whereas after fermentation, these macromolecular antigenic proteins were hydrolyzed into peptides smaller than 15 kDa, indicating that fermentation treatment helped convert macromolecular proteins into smaller peptides that are more easily absorbed in the gastrointestinal tract. The significant degradation of antigenic proteins is closely related to the exogenously added acidic protease and the protease produced by B. subtilis [35]. The enhancement in nutrient availability and reduction of anti-nutritional factors supports previous findings on the benefits of solid-state fermentation, as described by Chong et al. [39], further validating the use of microbial-enzymatic co-fermentation for waste valorization.

Owing to the unbalanced amino acid composition of peanut meal, additional amino acids are typically required to meet the nutritional needs of animals, limiting the proportion of peanut meal used in feed formulations [40, 41]. Prior to fermentation, three essential amino acids—Thr, Met, and His—were undetectable due to their low content. Following fermentation, these amino acids increased significantly, reaching 1.87%, 1.25%, and 1.76%, respectively, whereas the originally abundant Arg and Val decreased (Table 4). These changes suggest that fermentation effectively balanced the amino acid composition of peanut meal, thereby removing the limitations on its inclusion rate in animal feed. Moreover, the total amino acid content increased from 29.75 to 34.01%, further enhancing the nutritional value of peanut meal as a feed component.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated the successful application of W. coagulans BC01 in enhancing the nutritional quality of peanut meal through co-fermentation with Bacillus subtilis BS27. Under the optimized conditions (40 °C, 48 h, 15% inoculation, 1:1.5 solid-to-liquid ratio, 3 g/L calcium chloride, and a 2:1 enzyme mixture), a significant improvement in the nutritional quality of peanut meal was achieved. Fermentation reduced anti-nutritional factors, with trypsin inhibitors decreasing to 0.03%, phytic acid decreasing to 0.35%, and aflatoxin B1 decreasing to safe levels (6.20 µg/kg). Nutritional quality was substantially improved, evidenced by an 18.2% increase in crude protein, a 546% increase in acid-soluble protein, and a 447% increase in small peptide content, and the in vitro digestibility increased to 68.91%.

This study underscores the potential of microbial fermentation to transform agricultural by-products into high-value protein feed, contributing to sustainable livestock nutrition. Future research should validate these findings through in vivo animal trials and explore scalability and economic feasibility of this approach for large-scale feed production.
