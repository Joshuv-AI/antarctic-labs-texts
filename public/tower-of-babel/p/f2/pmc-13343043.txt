Implementation of a nurse-led visit and follow-up for patients with myeloproliferative neoplasms: a quality improvement project
Authors: Daniela Berardinelli, Carmen Fava, Federica Sinisi, Inga Ventimiglia, Ilaria Maglione, Andrea Ricotti, Cristina Mentone, Valentina Bonuomo, Selene Grano, Sara Campagna, Paola Di Giulio, Valerio Dimonte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) are chronic haematological disorders with a significant symptom burden and implications for quality of life. Traditional physician-led outpatient models often lack sufficient time for in-depth symptom monitoring and patient engagement. This quality improvement project aimed to implement (1) a structured nurse-led visit within a haematology outpatient clinic in northern Italy, (2) routine symptom monitoring and (3) evaluation of patient-related experience.MethodsThe nurse-led visit was integrated into routine 1-day hospital activities and focused on a structured comprehensive symptom assessment, health education and self-management support. Patient-reported outcomes to assess physical, psychological, nutritional and functional aspects and patient satisfaction as patient-reported experience measures were collected using validated tools and recorded on a patient symptom diary with patients’ self-care recommendations. The Plan–Do–Study–Act cycle and the person-centred care framework guided the intervention design and iterative improvement.ResultsOver the first 6 months, 102 patients underwent nurse-led visits with a high adherence rate and follow-up compliance. Patients reported high satisfaction with the nurse-led visit, with a mean score of 9.6±0.75 points on a 0–10 points visual scale. The intervention optimised waiting times, promoted patient activation and strengthened nurse-physician collaboration. No adverse events occurred.ConclusionsThe implementation of nurse-led visits was feasible, sustainable and highly accepted by patients. This approach aims to enhance symptom management, patient engagement and care coordination for individuals with MPNs and may prove to be a promising model for broader implementation in chronic disease management.Keywords: Self Care, Quality improvement, Patient satisfaction, Patient Reported Outcome Measures

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study demonstrates that a nurse-led visit model for patients with myeloproliferative neoplasms is feasible and acceptable in an outpatient haematology setting, enabling tailored symptom assessment, patient-centred care, interprofessional collaboration and the routine use of validated patient-reported outcome and experience measures.

Introduction

The problem

Our facility is an academic haematology outpatient clinic of a hospital in northern Italy. It provides diagnosis and therapeutic care to about 200 patients with Philadelphia-negative (Ph–) myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs), with around 20 new diagnoses per year. Patients are referred to our service by their general practitioner or a specialist, as the diagnosis is often incidental.

Patients with new diagnoses, complex cases, suspicion of disease progression, need for hospital-dispensed drugs and resistant or intolerant to therapy are defined as high demand (HD) and require at least three outpatient visits per year. Follow-up visits consist of a 1-day outpatient visit, including blood tests followed by the medical visit. Medical visits are scheduled once weekly, from 8:30 to 14:30 by two alternating haematologists from a team of four, who visit, on average, 8 HD Ph– MPNs patients, besides patients with other diagnoses.

Patients typically arrive at the clinic from 8:00 onwards, with scheduled appointments that include designated time slots for blood testing. However, they may wait several hours before seeing the physician. Each medical visit lasts approximately 20 min and includes history-taking and clinical examination.

Between December 2023 and February 2024, three focus groups were conducted involving 16 HD patients, using an interpretative phenomenological analysis.1 Patients reported insufficient time to discuss their symptoms and disease management, a need for more comprehensive information about their condition and dissatisfaction with the long waiting times for haematology visits.

To address these unmet needs and improve symptom monitoring, communication and overall follow-up quality, a structured nurse-led visit was integrated into the physician-led visits.

Background

MPN-related patient symptom burden is consistent, fatigue prominent, resulting in negative impact on daily activities, functional status and ability to work.2

Misalignment between physician and patient perceptions of communication and disease management was documented.3 Systematic assessment of symptom burden can help identify unmet needs and guide more tailored care.2 4 Structured care models in oncology are recommended to improve medication knowledge, reduce errors and promote best practices.5 6

Nurse-led clinics where nurses with advanced competencies deliver autonomous and multidisciplinary-based care commonly include symptom monitoring, education, counselling, procedures and case management.7 These models have been associated with reductions in healthcare utilisation, including service use, hospital readmissions, shorter length of stay and decreased medication use (central factors for quality economic evaluations).8 Self-management is especially important in these patients for a proactive role in their care and for optimal self-administration of oral anticancer drugs.

In oncology, these care models are widely used to manage treatment side effects and support self-care and self-management skills.9 10 No evidence is available to date to assess the implementation of a nurse-led visit for MPN patients in a hospital’s outpatient clinic. The aim of this quality improvement (QI) project was to implement and evaluate a structured nurse-led visit, integrated into the haematology outpatient service, to improve care for HD MPN patients. Over a 6-month period, the project aimed to achieve the following SMART (Specific S, Measurable M, Achievable A, Realistic R, and Timely T) objectives: (1) ensure that ≥80% of eligible patients received a nurse-led visit; (2) implement routine symptom monitoring using PROMs (patient-reported outcome measures) and (3) evaluation of patient experience related to the nurse-led visit.

Methods

Context

The haematology outpatient clinic is equipped with six beds and eight recliner chairs in five rooms. The multidisciplinary team comprises five nurses, two nursing assistants and four haematologists. The nursing team has a nursing station and a room dedicated to blood sampling, while the haematologists have each a separate office for patient visits. Each doctor visits on average 12–13 patients per day. Nurses work Monday to Friday during daytime hours, managing an average of 50 daily appointments. Their responsibilities include welcoming patients and performing clinical and diagnostic activities, such as blood sampling, treatment administration and assisting physicians during invasive procedures like bone marrow aspirates and biopsies. All nurses hold at least a bachelor’s degree and have at least 10 years of professional experience. Before the intervention, the nursing role was primarily technical, with limited involvement in comprehensive needs assessment or structured follow-up.

Design

A multidisciplinary team (two haematologists, a head nurse, a haematology nurse, a nursing professor and a doctoral student) developed the intervention. Focus group findings informed its content.

The intervention was grounded in the theoretical framework of person-centred care (PCC),11 and structured according to the Donabedian model12 for healthcare improvement, encompassing the ‘structure’, ‘process’ and ‘outcome’ indicators.11 The structure indicators include PCC domains related to the context in which care is provided; the process domains related to interaction and communication between patients and health providers; and the results domains related to care facilities and PROMs. The framework was incorporated into planning, implementing and assessing the Plan–Do–Study–Act (PDSA) cycles (online supplemental table 1). The PDSA model was used to test different phases of the intervention before wide-scale implementation.13

The nurse-led visit was delivered by a doctoral student trained in haematology care, person-centred communication and motivational interviewing from an experienced coach specialised in coaching healthcare providers. Additional support was provided by trained nurses during clinic days.

The visit takes place in a dedicated nursing office and is offered to all HD patients, a cohort of 102. During the visit, the nurse measures blood pressure and weight. The handgrip strength test and 30 s chair stand test are conducted in collaboration with a kinesiologist as measures of upper and lower body strength and indicators of overall physical ability.

Patients are approached during waiting times to obtain informed consent for the nursing visit, conducted during the waiting period between blood tests and medical visits.

During the nurse-led visit, patients are encouraged to share their symptoms and supported in increasing self-esteem, developing strategies to improve symptom control and management, and finally agree on attainable goals. Self-management skills are explored and supported to improve disease management. The nurse begins the session by explaining the project’s scope, which aims to improve care and services for MPN patients by capturing a wide range of health information and knowledge about their disease and management. The patients are explained the importance of sharing their opinions and viewpoints as the visit is an opportunity to identify unexplored problems. Validated questionnaires are provided as a means of facilitating a nurse–patient dialogue with questions concerning changes in perception of health, symptoms, information and support needs, as well as concerns or negative beliefs about diseases or medications. Nutritional state and medication adherence are investigated, and further information is provided on their tolerance or adverse side effects. Finally, the experience with the nurse’s visit and the perception of the service’s usefulness are asked.

When deemed necessary, the nurse can refer the patients to other health professionals such as physiotherapists, nutritionists or psychologists, in accordance with the haematologists. The first nurse-led visit (from now ‘assessment visit’) may require up to 1 hour; therefore, initially, a maximum of 5 visits per day were planned.

A dedicated email service is available to patients to communicate directly with the nurse and receive support in case of difficulty in managing symptoms or therapy.

A symptom diary was designed to monitor the information collected at each visit. The diary includes recommendations to support patient self-management skills. At the end of the visit, the nurse accompanies the patient to the haematologist, reports a summary of the visit, and stresses any critical points that require thorough investigation.

During follow-ups, the nurse collects information on any visits or admissions for any cause, measures weight and blood pressure; in addition to the self-report questionnaires, the nurse assesses overall patient well-being, reinforces self-management skills and re-evaluates the need to refer the patient to another health professional. Follow-up visits last nearly 20 to 30 min, as patients are already familiar with the symptom diary and are scheduled at 3, 6, 9 and 12 months (online supplemental box 1 shows the implementation process).

At the end of each MPN clinic day, dedicated debriefing time between nurse and haematologist was introduced to review patients’ care plans and strengthen interdisciplinary collaboration.

Measurement

During the nursing visit, PROMs to assess physical, psychological, nutritional and functional aspects and patient-reported experience measures are collected (details in online supplemental table 2).

To capture disease-related symptom burden, the MPN Symptom Assessment Form Total Symptom Score (MPN-SAF TSS)14 and FACIT-Fatigue-10 (Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy-Fatigue Scale)15 are administered. Medication adherence is investigated with the Medication Adherence Report Scale-5 item, Italian version16; nutritional risk (due to splenomegaly with loss of appetite) with the Malnutrition Screening Tool17; anxiety and depression with Generalised Anxiety Disorder-718 and Patient Health Questionnaire-9,19 respectively. European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire (EORTC QLQ-F17),20 Work Productivity and Activity Impairment Questionnaire21 measures quality of life and ability to work to better understand the disease’s impact on social life and activities. Patients’ disease knowledge and their ability to manage symptoms are evaluated using the Patient Activation Measure (PAM13-I),22 which identifies four stages of patient activation in disease management: from level 1 (not believing activation is important) to level 4 (actively taking action).

The hand-grip test and the 30 s chair stand allow to collect objective strength data, to set the bases for future development of interventions supporting person-centred physical activity.23,25

To capture patients’ satisfaction, a predesigned questionnaire on a 0–10 visual scale is administered at the end of the visit, asking to rate their overall experience, including the organisation of the visit and the nurse’s listening ability. While PROMs were reassessed longitudinally during follow-up visits to monitor symptom trends, patient satisfaction was collected only at the first nurse-led visit to evaluate the initial acceptability of the intervention and at 1-year follow-up to evaluate changes over time (details in online supplemental table 2).

To evaluate the QI initiative, the team assessed potential unintended consequences by monitoring balance measures, including patient waiting times, organisational breakdowns due to potentially overlapping visits and staff workload. Waiting times before the nursing visit were considered acceptable if less than 1 hour.

Descriptive statistics were used to analyse the data.

Strategy

This QI project had a 1-year design phase and started in September 2024 and is still ongoing. Main problems and solutions implemented are reported below.

PDSA cycles

The improvement initiative was implemented through five sequential PDSA cycles over a 3-month period, designed to iteratively refine the intervention and respond to emerging challenges (table 1).

PDSA cycles for care improvement of HD patients with MPN

HD, high demand; MPN, myeloproliferative neoplasm; PDSA, Plan–Do–Study–Act.

PDSA cycle 1: Testing Feasibility of the Nurse-Led Visit

In the first 2 weeks, the implementation of the nurse-led visit within the existing clinical workflow was assessed. The visit was well accepted by patients, but only 50% of eligible patients could be assessed due to time constraints, highlighting the need for additional personnel to ensure complete coverage.

PDSA cycle 2: Enhancing Sustainability and Identifying Patient needs

During weeks 3 and 4, two alternating nurses were added to the team to deliver the nursing visits to all eligible patients. This adjustment enabled full patient coverage and revealed additional insights: many patients expressed informational needs and requested further support in understanding their condition and symptoms management, highlighting the importance of structured nurse-led education and self-care and management support.

PDSA cycle 3: Development and Introduction of a Symptom Diary

In response to the informational, self-care and management needs, a symptom diary was implemented in weeks 5–6 to facilitate symptom assessment, enhance patient awareness, and provide a basis for discussion during follow-up by enabling a better comparison of any changes occurred. Patients were introduced to the diary during the nurse-led visit, and initial feedback indicated that the diary was useful and appreciated.

PDSA Cycle 4: Evaluation of Symptom Diary Use

In the fourth cycle (weeks 7–12), the focus was to evaluate the use and impact of the symptom diary at the 3-month follow-up visit. Patients were encouraged to complete the diary at home and bring it to their next visit. Most patients expressed the preference to fill it with the nurse during the visit. Nurses reported more structured follow-up discussions, while patients expressed that the diary helped them reflect on their symptoms.

PDSA Cycle 5: Evaluation of Patient-Completed Diary Content

Following the successful integration of the symptom diary, the fifth PDSA cycle was focused on assessing the quality and completeness of the diary entries. During the third month, nurses conducted a shared reading of the diary with the patients. Patients reported feeling more engaged and better prepared for visits. Notably, five patients out of the first 20 assessed at 3 months added narrative information on their physical and psychological health to the diary on a dedicated page. These additions revealed active patient participation in documenting symptoms and well-being, fostering greater engagement and improving focused patient–nurse communication.

Results

Process measures

Compliance with the nurse-led visits and use of the symptom diary was optimal, with all scheduled patients always bringing the diary with them. None refused the visit nor declined the follow-up. In the first 6 months, all patients received the assessment visit (roughly 20 HD patients per month). The assessment visit lasted on average 1 hour, in a few cases even longer. Clinical and PROMs

Symptom burden (MPN-SAF TSS) showed a slight decline over 6 months (figure 1). Similar descriptive improvements were observed for anxiety and depressive symptoms, whereas fatigue, assessed using the FACIT-Fatigue scale, remained stable (table 2).

Four patients were identified as being at risk of malnutrition and were referred for specialist nutritional assessment. The mean (SD) EORTC QLQ-C30 global health status/quality of life score remained broadly stable over time, as did the other EORTC subscales, with only minor fluctuations, with the exception of emotional functioning, which showed improvement and cognitive functioning, which demonstrated a decline.

At 3 months, only one patient—not previously at risk—experienced weight loss and was strictly monitored. Medication adherence remained high and stable. Patient experience with the nurse-led visit was highly positive (mean satisfaction score 9.6±0.75).

Seven patients would have liked to know more about the disease and its management (PAM13-I levels I–II). Five out of 20 patients completed the questionnaires autonomously before the nursing visit, while the majority together with the nurse during the visit, as they valued the opportunity to share and discuss their symptoms in real time. These patients added extra information to the symptom diary about new problems, symptoms or other events they believed important to share. Only one patient used the email service to express concerns regarding the therapy. No clinical adverse events were reported during the 6-month period.

In the first 2 weeks, due to the longer duration of previous visits, five patients were not visited on the planned day but had to be rescheduled. Additionally, during the first 3 months, occasional high physician workload resulted in three patients being seen by the physician before the nurse.

Balance measures

Waiting time

All visits were conducted within 30 min of the patients scheduled arrival time, as appointments were pre-assigned to specific time slots. The nurse-led visits did not cause delays or increase patient waiting times.

Nurse workload

The introduction of two alternating dedicated nurses on MPN clinic days ensured adequate staffing for the assessment visits, without impacting on the organisation and workload. No unscheduled visits or unplanned additional patient access were recorded. Follow-up visits can be managed by a single nurse.

Overall, the intervention did not negatively impact staff workload or service use and was associated with high patient satisfaction, confirming that the improvement was sustainable and well-integrated into routine clinical practice.

The main challenge encountered was allocating a room dedicated to nursing visits, in order to allow a safe space for patient dialogue and promote symptoms or problems expression, which required a reorganisation of existing workspaces. Additionally, managing integrated visit bookings demanded close coordination between physicians and nurses.

Discussion

Lessons

Our findings suggest that the nurse-led visit was feasible, well accepted, and successfully integrated into routine workflow. All patients accepted the visit, and the timing between the assessment visit and the first follow-up was respected. Compliance with the visits and with the filling of questionnaires was high, reflecting the nurses’ ability to engage patients.

The implementation of the intervention was time-consuming and required detailed and careful planning. A well-established collaboration between nurses and physician teams is an essential element: the project would not have been possible without the medical team’s support.26 The haematologists and the nurses had to agree on the content of the nurse-led visit, define the main data to be collected for patient monitoring. Communication and collaboration between nurses and haematologists improved, also by establishing a shared-care communication model focused on patients’ needs. Effective nurse–physician communication is the cornerstone of high-quality and safe care for its positive impact on patient outcomes by enhancing patient satisfaction and reducing adverse events and healthcare costs.27

The appointment schedule had to be adapted: nursing visits are generally planned before medical visits to ensure efficient patient waiting times. However, on some occasions, when physicians had a particularly high caseload, if the medical visit preceded the nurse-led visit, clinically relevant information was transmitted from the haematologist to the nurse.

The use of a symptom diary is also suggested to improve symptom control, knowledge and disease awareness.28 A distinctive and innovative feature of the intervention is the regular PROs collection to facilitate symptom monitoring and tailor care to specific patient needs. However, to limit the burden of completing PROs, that can negatively impact the quality of data and support in completing questionnaires was provided.29

Unlike many nurse-led models focused primarily on reducing healthcare utilisation, this project emphasised empowerment and self-management with potential sustained impact over time.30 Nowadays, reducing the burden of rare conditions remains a major challenge for healthcare systems, and greater patient empowerment is needed to support effective self-management, as this may foster innovative research and contribute to improving healthcare standards tailored to patients’ specific conditions.31 Preliminary trends suggest potential benefits on psychological outcomes, though larger studies are needed. The diary was perceived as a helpful tool, offering practical tips and recommendations to promote self-care and coping.

Key points were the availability of a dedicated nurse trained in haematology and sustained continuity within the outpatient setting, together with the shared development of the project involving other healthcare professionals, including psychologists, nutritionists, physiotherapists and exercise science specialists. This multidisciplinary approach supported the rigorous selection of assessment tools and their systematic monitoring over time. An additional service that could be considered and which has demonstrated effectiveness in previous studies for symptom monitoring is structured telephone follow-up for high-need patients or complex cases with a tailored timing.

Although an economic analysis was not conducted, the project incurred minimal costs of: dedicated nurse-time for 6 hours per week, to ensure the continuity of the intervention.

The external support required in the initial phase to manage the increased workload related to first visits is no longer necessary. The service is now delivered by a trained staff nurse embedded in the outpatient team, ensuring sustainability.

Patients were highly satisfied with the nurse-led care. However, despite the increasing popularity of nursing clinics worldwide, their implementation in Italy is less widespread.32 The primary challenges in disseminating nurse-led clinics arise from the availability of physical space for the clinic, the need for sufficient and flexible staff to ensure sustainability, and the requirement for motivated and qualified staff to deliver a competent service.7

Limitations

The present project has some limitations. First, this single-centre QI project limits generalisability. An issue still to be solved is the documentation of the nurse’s visit in the medical record (information is now stored in a nursing record). In the near future, the nurse assessment will be integrated into the electronic health record system to share patient-focused, accurate and relevant information between nurses and doctors.

Nurses are not yet enabled to independently refer patients to specialists (such as psychologists, nutritionists or physiotherapists), which may delay some interventions. Protocol-based referral authority would improve efficiency.

Conclusions

Integrating a structured nurse-led visit into an academic haematology outpatient clinic proved feasible, sustainable and highly acceptable to HD patients with MPN. The intervention strengthened symptom monitoring, enhanced patient education and engagement, and improved interdisciplinary collaboration without increasing workload. In the future, this project will enable us to identify patients at the greatest risk for their haematologic disease and/or comorbidities and those who require additional care and support that can be provided by nurses. This shared medical–nursing model represents a promising approach to delivering PCC in chronic haematological conditions and may be adapted to other outpatient settings managing complex long-term diseases.
