Transfer of a novel discriminative function across functional stimulus class members in rats
Authors: Madeleine G Mason, Elijah J Richardson, Cassondra Giarrusso, Skylar M Murphy, Katherine E Bruce, Mark Galizio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis experiment explored a rodent model of functional class formation by assessing the transfer of a novel discriminative function across class members. Following simple successive discrimination reversal training and consistently strong probe performance indicative of the formation of two six‐member functional classes (X1–X6 and Y1–Y6), subjects were trained on a novel discrimination task to respond in the right‐side port in the presence of odor X1 and in the left‐side port in the presence of Y1. When rats demonstrated high accuracy on the left–right (LR) task as well as maintenance of the functional classes, LR probe sessions were conducted in which X2 or Y2 were presented on some trials as nonreinforced probes to test for transfer of the novel function (left or right responding). The LR probe sessions were conducted in this fashion for each pair of X and Y stimuli that had never been directly trained in the LR discrimination procedure. Transfer of the novel function was observed in five out of six subjects on the initial probe session, but only two rats showed consistent transfer across subsequent probes. The results offer preliminary evidence for transfer of novel function in rats and support further investigation using a similar approach.Keywords: functional equivalence, odors, rats, stimulus equivalence, transfer of function

The study of concepts, categories, and symbols is one of the most fundamental areas of psychology (Guinther & Dougher, 2015; Zentall et al., 2002). Beginning with a landmark study of stimulus equivalence by Sidman and Tailby (1982), several decades of theoretical and empirical work have demonstrated that behavior analysis can account for symbolic, generative behavior in a way that identifies observable and manipulable environmental conditions, not mental constructs, as causal variables (Galizio, 1996; Sidman, 1994; Wills & Pothos, 2012). Implications have been far‐reaching, from educational interventions (e.g., de Rose et al., 1996; Fields et al., 2009) to mental health treatment (e.g., Guinther & Dougher, 2015; Hayes et al., 2006) to social issues such as stereotyping and prejudice (e.g., Dixon & Lemke, 2007; Hayes et al., 1991).

Symbolic (or associative) concepts are classes of stimuli that are related not by physical similarity but by their shared function in occasioning a common response (Zentall et al., 2002; Urcuioli, 2013). Equivalence classes are a special type of associative concept that are generated from a particular learning history and demonstrate a set of defining properties derived from mathematical set theory: reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity (Sidman & Tailby, 1982; Sidman, 2000). Stimuli in an equivalence class are said to be symbolically related because they are functionally substitutable and interchangeable for one another (Pilgrim, 2020; Sidman, 1994).

In addition to these defining properties, other predictable outcomes of equivalence classes have been well documented (in human subjects), such as transfer of function, a phenomenon in which a novel function is trained to as few as one member of an equivalence class and the function of other class members is likewise altered without further training (Goldiamond, 1966; Pilgrim, 2020; Sidman, 1994). Various types of stimulus function have been demonstrated to transfer across class members including discriminative (e.g., Hayes et al., 1987), consequential (e.g., Hayes et al., 1991), and respondent (e.g., Dougher et al., 1994; Valverde et al., 2009) functions (see Dougher & Markham, 1994, and Dymond & Rehfeldt, 2000, for reviews). It should be noted that classes that demonstrate transfer of novel function but not emergent conditional relations are termed functional equivalence classes (Dougher & Markham, 1994), but they are generally believed to represent the same phenomenon as stimulus equivalence (Pilgrim, 2016; Sidman, 1994; Vaughan, 1988; cf. Hayes, 1989; Sidman et al., 1989).

Transfer of function can be assessed even when it is not possible to test for emergent relations, such as when simple discrimination training is used instead of the traditional arbitrary matching‐to‐sample paradigm. For example, de Souza Canovas et al. (2015) trained adult participants on a successive simple discrimination task to select the “Y” key on a computer keyboard (R1) in the presence of A1, B1, or C1 and the “B” key (R2) in the presence of A2, B2, or C2 (stimuli were pictures of abstract shapes). Once these differential responses had been mastered, participants were trained to select a new key (R3 or R4) in the presence of only one member from each class (A1 and A2). Tests conducted in extinction showed transfer of the novel function: B1 and C1 became discriminative for R3, and B2 and C2 became discriminative for R4, despite no explicit reinforcement history for these discriminations. Finally, equivalence class formation was verified using a successive simple discrimination test with class‐consistent (e.g., A1B1) and class‐inconsistent (e.g., A2B1) compounds.

Outcomes of equivalence beyond reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity may be particularly useful in the study of symbolic processes in nonhumans. Despite its advances, a major roadblock in the study of equivalence has been the lack of a viable animal model. Sidman et al. (1982) used a conditional discrimination training and testing paradigm analogous to that used by Sidman and Tailby (1982) but found no evidence for the emergence of symmetry in the performances of rhesus monkeys and baboons. Similar negative or inconclusive results in tests for equivalence have been demonstrated repeatedly over the past several decades in a range of nonhuman species (see Lionello‐DeNolf, 2009, 2021, for reviews).

Sophisticated procedural modifications have resulted in a handful of demonstrations of equivalence or equivalence‐like processes in nonhumans (for reviews, see Galizio & Bruce, 2018; Lionello‐DeNolf, 2021; Zentall et al., 2014). For example, Urcuioli, Zentall, and colleagues obtained convergent evidence for functional equivalence in pigeons using many‐to‐one match‐to‐sample training and clever alternative testing procedures including tests for transfer of training (Urcuioli et al., 1989), discriminability of class‐consistent versus ‐inconsistent samples, partial versus total discrimination reversals (Zentall et al., 1991), and facilitation of acquisition or retention of class‐consistent versus ‐inconsistent discriminations (Zentall et al., 1993; for reviews, see Zentall, 2006; Zentall & Urcuioli, 1993). Urcuioli (2008) devised a procedure that addressed unintended functional control by the spatial and temporal properties of stimuli and resulted in strong evidence for emergent symmetry in pigeons following identity and arbitrary match‐to‐sample training (see also Frank & Wasserman, 2005; Lionello & Urcuioli, 1998; Urcuioli & Swisher, 2015). Using a different approach, Schusterman and Kastak (1993) trained a California sea lion on a series of arbitrary symmetrical relations (known as multiple‐exemplar training or MET). After experience with several trained exemplars, the sea lion began demonstrating emergent symmetry with novel exemplars before contacting reinforcement. However, neither MET nor the Urcuioli approach were successful in producing consistent emergent symmetry in rats (Bruce et al., 2022; Prichard et al., 2015).

Another alternative procedure, which formed the basis of the present study, was developed by Vaughan (1988), who hypothesized that an equivalence class could be established solely on the basis of shared stimulus function. Pigeons were trained on a successive simple discrimination procedure using 40 distinct slides as visual stimuli. Pecking to a subset of these slides, arbitrarily designated as Set 1, was reinforced, whereas pecking to the other images, arbitrarily designated as Set 2, was not. Following initial acquisition of the discriminations, the contingencies associated with each set were reversed such that now Set 2 was reinforced. Contingencies were re‐reversed every several sessions for the remainder of the experiment. For the first several reversals, pigeons' discrimination performance on the first presentation of the stimuli following a reversal was well below chance, as they continued to respond to each stimulus according to the contingencies that had been in effect the last time that stimulus was encountered. Interestingly, discrimination performance on the first presentation of the stimuli following a reversal gradually increased as a function of reversal number. That is, when a few members of a set became discriminative for responding following a reversal, the rest of the members of that set became discriminative for responding as well, even before the new contingency had been contacted for each stimulus. These results suggest that the stimuli in each set had formed a functional equivalence class of which any member can substitute or act as a conditional stimulus for other members.

Vaughan's (1988) simple discrimination reversal procedure has been replicated in several species/populations including dolphins (von Fersen & Delius, 2000), sea lions (Kastak et al., 2001), human adults (Sidman et al., 1989), and children (Lionello‐DeNolf et al., 2008). To develop a versatile rodent model of functional equivalence, Mason et al. (2021) conducted a systematic replication of Vaughan's repeated simple discrimination reversal procedure with rats using olfactory stimuli. However, even after many reversals, no consistent evidence for class formation was found. In fact, the rats had developed a pattern of responding nondifferentially to all stimuli at the beginning of each session and then typically responded highly discriminatively for the remainder of the session. This apparent win–stay, lose–shift strategy pattern may have obscured any evidence of transfer of function across the first presentation of the stimuli following a reversal. On each rat's final reversal session, the stimulus sets were “shuffled” such that three members from each class were switched into the other class. The authors hypothesized that, without class formation, rats' performance would not be affected by these shuffled sets. However, if rapid adjustment to newly reversed contingencies was facilitated by class formation, then adjustment following the shuffled reversal should be much slower. Indeed, five of six rats showed disrupted recovery of discriminative performance relative to their most recent nonshuffled reversals. This offered some evidence for functional class formation in rats using the repeated reversal procedure.

Subsequent work by Galizio et al. (2023) sought to address some of the limitations of Mason et al. (2021), particularly the issue of indiscriminate responding at the beginning of sessions, using a testing strategy similar to that used by von Fersen & Delius (2000), Jitsumori et al. (2002), and Lionello‐DeNolf et al. (2008). On a reversal session, one stimulus from each set was not presented until the final trial block of the session. Galizio et al. called these delayed stimulus probes (DSPs), a term that was adopted for the present study. This allowed the newly reversed contingency to be repeatedly contacted with the other class members prior to a test of transfer of function. Responding consistent with the newly reversed contingency on the first presentation of the delayed probe stimuli would be possible only with transfer of function. This procedure yielded above‐chance responding on some DSP trials, but the data were quite variable due to the nature of the measure (taken from only the first presentation of the positive and negative probe stimulus). In a follow‐up experiment, Galizio et al. added what they called a differential‐reinforcement‐of‐other‐behavior (DRO) contingency to negative trials. Responding during the latter half of negative stimulus presentations resulted in 2 s being added to the trial, extending the S− presentation and delaying the next opportunity to respond for reinforcement. Any behavior other than responding during negative trials would thus be reinforced by escape from the S− and advancement to the next trial. The DRO procedure, together with the DSP arrangement, yielded strong evidence for functional class formation in most subjects.

Simple discrimination procedures offer promise for animal models of equivalence by circumventing the apparent stimulus control challenges inherent to the traditional arbitrary matching‐to‐sample paradigm when used with nonhumans. Although the prospect of a rodent model of class formation is exciting, many questions about the properties and utility of the model developed by Galizio et al. (2023) remain unanswered. For example, to what extent are the classes formed by rats in this model analogous to those involved in human symbolic behavior? The present experiment sought to advance this line of research by examining the extent to which the classes formed in rats demonstrate transfer of a novel discriminative function, a fundamental emergent outcome of equivalence in humans. This experiment also sought to address critiques of the repeated simple discrimination reversal procedure, which point out the possibility that subjects in this procedure are essentially directly trained on a series of conditional discriminations (e.g., Sidman, 1989). Tests for transfer of a novel function avoid this potential confound.

METHOD

Subjects

Six male Sprague Dawley rats sourced from Envigo served as subjects (female rats were not available in the university's colony at the time of this study). The rats came from two cohorts: Cohort 1 (H rats, n = 3) were approximately 195 days old at the start of this experiment. Cohort 2 (J rats, n = 3) were aged approximately 270 days (J13 and J14) to 440 days (J12) at the start of this experiment and had previously completed another study involving the simple discrimination reversal procedure and met criteria for class formation before beginning this study. All subjects were housed individually within a secure vivarium with a 12 hr reverse day–night light cycle. The animals were maintained at approximately 85%–90% of their free‐feeding weight and fed daily rations of LabDiet 5001 rodent chow pellets no less than 45 min after the training session, with unrestricted access to water in the home cage. They were tested between 8:00 a.m. and 6:00 p.m., typically Monday–Saturday. The schedule was occasionally interrupted by university holiday breaks of 1 to 2 weeks. All animal care and experimental protocols were approved by the university's institutional animal care and use committee and followed all U.S. legal and ethical requirements for vertebrate animal research.

Apparatus and stimuli

The experimental procedures were conducted using Med Associates operant chambers measuring 30.5 cm long by 24 cm wide by 21 cm high. The chambers featured an interior side panel with three response ports, each equipped with an infrared beam to detect responses, a stimulus light, and two small openings through which odors were pumped in and vacuumed out, respectively. A houselight was located above the response ports. A lighted food hopper on the opposite side panel was attached to a pellet dispenser located on the exterior of the chamber. Reinforcers were 45‐mg unflavored sucrose pellets manufactured by BioServ. Each chamber was housed within a light and sound attenuating cabinet in a darkened room equipped with a white noise generator.

The chambers were each equipped with three five‐channel Med Associates olfactometers (ENV‐275‐5), with five jars connected to each olfactometer. Each jar contained 5 mL of odor solution, except for one jar on one olfactometer for each box that was kept empty and used to deliver unscented air between trials. To deliver an odor into the chamber, an input pump (Linear AC0102 pump, 2.84 lb/in.2 [1996.72 kg/m2] with an airflow of 0.177 ft3/min [5.01 L/min]) forced air into the selected jar and through C‐Flex (Cole‐Parmer) tubing to a solenoid that, when opened, delivered the scented air into a small opening in the center response port. At the same time, a vacuum (Linear VP0125 pump, 9.84‐Hg vacuum and air displacement of 0.247 ft3/min [7.0 L/min]) continuously removed air through an opening in the bottom of the port and through plastic tubing to the outside of the cabinet. Fourteen different odor solutions were prepared using scented oils purchased from Great American Spice Company (cinnamon, apricot, bubblegum, apple, coconut, brandy, vanilla butternut, almond, strawberry, and pistachio) and Nature's Garden (root beer, fresh brewed coffee, fresh cut grass, and sandalwood). These were diluted with distilled water (0.67 mL oil per 100 mL distilled water). Odor solutions were stored in mason jars in a refrigerator and shaken by hand prior to solution being added to the olfactometer jars using an odorant‐specific syringe. The olfactometer jars were cleaned with Liquinox solution and tap water twice per week at the end of the day (typically Wednesdays and Saturdays) and left to dry overnight, and new solution was added to the jars the following morning, twice per week (typically Mondays and Thursdays). Procedure

Nose‐poke response shaping

The rats were first habituated to their assigned chamber and trained to respond for sucrose pellets by inserting their nose or paw into the center response port. At the start of shaping, a sucrose pellet was delivered noncontingently every 30 s across a 30‐min session. After a rat consumed all pellets delivered in a session, a fixed‐ratio (FR) 1 center‐port response requirement was introduced. The schedule was thinned to an FR 2 requirement after the rat emitted at least 30 responses in one 45‐min session and was again thinned to FR 4 following one session with an increased rate of responding. If response rates continued to increase, rats then advanced to discrimination training. No odors were presented during shaping, but rats were exposed to stimulus lights within the chamber as described below.

Simple discrimination training

All simple discrimination (SD) sessions consisted of 60 trials, 30 in which responding was reinforced (positive or S+ trials) and 30 in which responding was not reinforced (negative or S− trials). The order of stimulus presentations varied quasirandomly across sessions, with no more than four positive or negative trial types presented consecutively. Stimuli were presented in blocks such that each odor was presented once per trial block. Each trial began with the illumination of the houselight and center response port light. When an observing response was made in the center port, an odor was pumped through a small aperture at the top of the port while the vacuum pump continuously evacuated air through a small opening in the bottom of the port. The observing response also initiated a fixed‐interval (FI) schedule, detailed below. On positive (S+) trials, completion of the FI requirement terminated the odor presentation and resulted in the delivery of a sucrose pellet. Following each trial, the response port and houselights turned off for a 30‐s intertrial interval (ITI); the hopper light was illuminated for the first 5 s of the ITI on positive trials. The pump and vacuum remained on during the ITI to pump unscented air into the chamber and remove any residual odorant between trials. Responses were recorded during the first 5 s (or 10 s for rats trained on an FI 10‐s schedule) of odor presentation of each trial to allow for comparison across trials. Responses to positive and negative trials were used to calculate response rates as well as a discrimination index (DI): the number of responses to positive trials divided by the total number of responses to positive and negative trials.

Initial SD training differed between the two cohorts of rats (H and J; see Figure 1). Rats H3, H4, and H19 were first trained on a simple olfactory discrimination with two odors arbitrarily designated as X1 (S+) and Y1 (S−), respectively. Upon mastery of this discrimination, additional stimuli were mixed into the baseline one pair at a time (starting with X2 and Y2), with a new pair of stimuli added after two consecutive sessions with a DI of at least .75, until all six members of each class were included in the training baseline. Rats J12, J13, and J14 also began SD training with odors X1 and Y1. When this discrimination was acquired, defined as an overall DI of at least .75 for one session, rats then received identical training with another pair of odors, X2 (S+) and Y2 (S‐). When the same performance criterion was reached for this discrimination, rats were trained on a third olfactory discrimination, X3 (S+) and Y3 (S−). Upon mastery of this third discrimination, rats advanced to concurrent simple discrimination training in which all six stimuli (X1, X2, X3, Y1, Y2, Y3) were included and presented in quasirandom order. Then as part of a separate experiment, the two functional classes were gradually expanded from three to six members using the simple discrimination reversal procedure (Richardson et al., 2025).

The schedule parameters also differed across the two cohorts. For rats J12, J13, and J14, reinforcement required two responses within 2 s of each other after the 5‐s FI had elapsed (interresponse time [IRT] < 2 s schedule), intended as differential reinforcement of high rates of responding (DRH), similar to a procedure used by Vaughan (1988). Negative (S−) trials terminated following the 5‐s odor presentation, with the additional requirement that no responses could be emitted during the last 2 s of the interval. A response made in the last 2 s of the interval extended the interval by 2 s, intended as a DRO procedure—that is, reinforcement of nonresponding on negative trials by escape from the S− and access to opportunities to respond for sucrose pellets on subsequent positive trials (Galizio et al., 2023). For rats H3, H4, and H19, responding on positive trials was reinforced on an FI 10‐s schedule, with no IRT < t requirement, and negative trials required no responding for 5 s before the S− was terminated. For rat H4, beginning on Session 293, just before Reversal 42, the schedule parameters were changed from those just described to those used for the J rats. This was done to improve the relatively slow recovery of discriminative performance after reversals, noting that the J rats' performances tended to recover more quickly following reversals. For all subjects in this experiment, each experimenter‐defined stimulus class consisted of six odors (Table 1).

Simple discrimination reversals

When a rat met a set of performance criteria, the contingencies were reversed at the start of the next session such that Y stimuli became positive and X stimuli became negative. The criteria for a reversal differed somewhat across the two cohorts. For the J rats, the first reversal was administered after completion of a minimum of four sessions of training on the full simple discrimination baseline described above and upon reaching a DI of .70 or greater for two consecutive sessions. Across subsequent sessions, the contingencies were repeatedly reversed each time that subjects obtained a session DI of at least .75 and a DI of at least .70 for each of the 12‐trial Blocks 2–5 for one session (Block 1 was excluded given the findings of nondifferential responding during this block by Mason et al., 2021). There was a minimum of two sessions and a maximum of 10 sessions between reversals, including the most recent reversal session. The H rats received their first reversal following a minimum of 10 training sessions with the full six‐stimulus classes and at least two consecutive sessions with an overall DI of .75 and a DI of at least .75 for each trial block (excluding Block 1). Subsequent reversals also required two consecutive sessions with an overall DI of .75 and a DI of at least .75 for each trial block (excluding Block 1). As noted above, Rat H4 began receiving reversals according to the less stringent criteria used for the J rats, beginning on session 293, just before Reversal 42.

Delayed stimulus probes

Delayed stimulus probles like those used by Galizio et al. (2023) were added to reversal sessions starting with the J rats' 15th reversal and the H rats' 11th reversal. Reversal sessions for all rats included five blocks of 10 trials plus a sixth block of 10 trials consisting of two odors that were not presented until this final block of trials. Critical DSP trials were the first positive and first negative stimuli presented in this block. The pair of stimuli withheld to serve as DSPs varied across reversal sessions. Subjects advanced to LR discrimination training when they had completed a minimum of 18 reversals with the complete six‐stimulus sets and when they had achieved a DI of at least .70 on DSP trials across four consecutive reversals, indicative of class formation.

Left–right discrimination training

Left–right shaping

Figure 2 provides an overview of LR training stages. At the start of each LR trial, the houselight flashed on and off at a rate of 0.25 s to create a unique context for this task. No odors were presented during the left–right shaping phase. To shape left and right responding after an extensive training history with only center‐port responding, rats were trained to respond in each port on a FR 5 schedule in a repeating sequence of center port—left port—right port. A stimulus light illuminated within each port when a response was required in that port and turned off after five responses were emitted. Satisfaction of the FR 5 requirement terminated the trial with delivery of a sucrose pellet, illumination of the hopper light for 5 s, and a 10‐s ITI with no stimulus lights illuminated. Then, the next port in the sequence was illuminated. Subjects were required to complete 80 trials per session for two consecutive sessions before advancing to the next phase.

Left–right discrimination training stages and performance criteria for each subject cohort. DI = discrimination index, LR = left–right, and SDR = simple discrimination reversal.

Correction procedure

Rats then began LR discrimination training with a correction procedure in place. At the start of each LR trial, the houselight began to flash at a rate of 0.25 s and the center port light was illuminated. Following an observing response, an odor, either X1 or Y1, was presented through the center port for 5 s. The first response following the 5‐s interval caused the odor presentation to terminate, the center port light to extinguish, and the left and right response port lights to illuminate. Responding in the port designated as correct (always right for X1 and left for Y1) was reinforced on a FR 1 schedule with delivery of a pellet and 5‐s illumination of the hopper light followed by a 25‐s ITI with all lights extinguished. A response in the port designated as incorrect resulted in a 30‐s ITI followed by a re‐presentation of the trial as a correction procedure. Correction trials were repeated in this fashion until a correct response was emitted. A correct response on a correction trial produced reinforcement and advanced the session to the next trial. The session terminated after 48 trials were completed, not including correction trials, or until 45 min elapsed, whichever came first. The order of trial types varied quasirandomly across sessions with no more than three consecutive presentations of the same odor (not including correction trials). Rats advanced to the next phase (no corrections) after three consecutive sessions with no less than 70% correct responding for both X1 or Y1 trials and an average of at least 80% correct for both trial types across these three sessions (again, not including correction trials).

No corrections

When subjects satisfied the above criteria, correction trials were removed from sessions. An incorrect response now terminated the trial in a 30‐s ITI followed by the next trial. Otherwise, the procedures were identical to those in the corrections phase described above. Rats advanced to the next phase, concurrent training, after three consecutive sessions with no less than 70% correct responding for both X1 or Y1 trials and an average of at least 80% correct for both trial types across these three sessions.

Concurrent training and reinforcement probability thinning

Subjects continued maintenance training on the LR task as well as the SD reversal procedure to that the ensure classes remained intact. In the concurrent SD/LR training phase, SD sessions occurred 4 days per week (typically Monday–Thursday) and LR sessions occurred the remaining 2 days per week (typically Friday and Saturday). For the remainder of the study, subjects received a reversal following one SD session with a DI of at least .75 (subjects H3 and H19) or .70 (all other subjects) in Blocks 2–5. Note that LR discriminations remained consistent (X1 ➔ right, Y1 ➔ left) regardless of which stimulus set was positive or negative in the most recent SD session.

Thinning of the reinforcement schedule for baseline LR trials was necessary to reduce the discriminability and disruptiveness of nonreinforced probe trials (detailed in the subsection below). Reinforcement probability for correct responding on both X1 and Y1 trial types was thinned from 1.0 to .90 following three sessions with an average of at least 80% correct responding. After three additional sessions with an average of at least 80% correct responding, reinforcement probability was thinned to .80 where it remained for the rest of the experiment. Once subjects transitioned to the concurrent SD/LR training phase and reinforcement probability for correct LR responding had been thinned to .80 for at least three sessions, they became eligible to meet criteria for repeated transfer of function (TOF) probes.

Transfer of function probes

Figure 3 provides an overview of the TOF probe phases. For all rats, the criteria to receive a TOF probe were (a) two consecutive SD reversals (one of each type [X‐ and Y‐positive reversals]) within a 2‐week period, each with a DSP DI of at least .70 and delayed‐stimulus block DI of at least .70, and (b) at least 80% correct responding on both X1 and Y1 trial types on the LR session preceding the TOF probe session. All subjects were required to complete a SD reversal and meet all criteria within the same week to be eligible for a TOF probe session. A maximum of one TOF probe session was administered per week.

Transfer of function probe phases and performance criteria. DI = discrimination index, DSP = delayed stimulus probe, LR = left–right, TOF = transfer of function, and MET = multiple exemplar training.

TOF probe sessions included interspersed trials of X1/Y1 with one X and one Y class member that had never been directly trained in the LR task. All probe trials were nonreinforced, and baseline X1/Y1 trials were partially reinforced to maintain an overall .80 reinforcement probability for correct responding across the session. The TOF probe sessions consisted of 48 trials divided into four segments of 12 trials. The first segment contained five reinforced trials each of X1 and Y1, one nonreinforced trial each of X1 and Y1, and no probe trials. Each of the three segments thereafter contained one X and one Y probe trial (nonreinforced) and one nonreinforced X1 or Y1 trial (one segment contained no nonreinforced X1 or Y1 trials for a total of two of each of these trial types per probe session; this was determined randomly). The remaining trials were reinforced X1 or Y1 trials for a total of 19 of each trial type distributed across the four segments (because 19 is an odd number, segments contained four or five of each trial type). Trials were presented in quasirandom order, with the constraints that there could be no more than three X or Y trial types in a row and that no more than one nonreinforced trial could be presented consecutively. Different combinations of opposing class members (e.g., X2/Y2, X3/Y5) were probed in quasirandom order across probe sessions. Rats H4, J12, J13, and J14 also received one or more control probes. In these probe sessions, two unfamiliar odors, vanilla‐butternut and sandalwood, were presented on nonreinforced probe trials. The purpose of control probes was to compare LR responding to non‐class members versus untrained class members. Rats advanced to MET after completing the entire TOF probe series two or three times (for a total of 10 or 15 probe sessions).

Multiple‐exemplar training

Three stimuli from each class (X1, X2, X3, Y1, Y2, and Y3) were included in all subsequent LR training sessions, with right‐port responses now reinforced for X2 and X3 (along with X1) and left‐port responses reinforced for Y2 and Y3 (along with Y1). The same performance criteria described above were used for schedule thinning and to determine when a subject received a MET TOF probe session. The TOF probes in the MET phase were similar to those in the sessions described above, with nonreinforced probe trials interspersed with partially reinforced training trials. Each LR probe session included the three training stimuli from each class as well as one untrained X class member (X4, X5, or X6) and the corresponding untrained Y class member (Y4, Y5, and Y6) as probe stimuli. An error in the software used for H3 resulted in this rat being trained on three exemplars from the X class (X1, X2, X3) but only two exemplars from the Y class (Y1, Y2). This error also caused H3 to receive MET probes for only two X‐class members (X4 and X5) and three Y‐class members (Y3, Y4, Y5). It was intended that each subject complete a total of nine LR MET probe sessions (three of each type, or 54 probe trials total). Unfortunately, several subjects (H4, H19, J13, J14), aged about 2.5 years by this point in the study, became ill or stopped responding during baseline sessions and were discontinued from the experiment before completing all programmed probes.

Data analysis

Above‐chance discrimination on DSP trials (i.e., significantly more responding to positive than to negative probe trials) was considered evidence for functional class formation. Responses to positive and negative probe trials were compared using a Wilcoxon signed‐rank test (a nonparametric t test). Above‐chance accuracy on LR probe trials was considered evidence for transfer of the novel response across class members. Binomial tests were conducted to test whether the observed distribution of left versus right responses differed from the expected distribution of 50% on each side.

RESULTS

Simple discrimination acquisition

The number of sessions to criterion at each stage of initial SD training and the number of sessions required to meet criteria for the first reversal are shown in Table 2 for each rat. All subjects completed response shaping within 4–6 sessions. The H rats quickly progressed through stepwise simple discrimination training, reaching the full six‐member classes within 9–15 sessions and meeting criteria for the first reversal within 10–14 sessions. Likewise, the J rats quickly progressed through simple discrimination training, each reaching the full three‐member baseline in five sessions, and showed little to no disruption when the discriminations were combined into the full three‐member baseline. Acquisition of left–right discrimination

Acquisition of left and right responding can be seen in Figure 5, which shows the percentage of correct responding for X1 (right; filled circles) and Y1 (left; open squares) across the stages of LR training. The number of sessions required to meet criteria to advance through each training step varied across subjects and is summarized in Table 4. Subjects began LR training after 18–70 SD reversals. Shaping of left and right responding was relatively quick, with subjects requiring only 2–4 sessions to meet criterion. Initial LR discrimination training with corrections required between nine (H19) and 33 sessions (J12) to mastery. As seen in Figure 5, Rats H19, H3, J12, and to some extent H4, had a clear side bias at the start of LR discrimination training (left for H19 and H4, right for J12 and H3), but responding become more equally allocated across the two side ports across sessions. When the correction procedure was removed, most subjects required only 4–5 sessions to meet criterion and showed little disruption to accuracy, except for J13 whose performance was more variable. Thinning the reinforcement density from 1.0 to .80 progressed quite quickly, requiring 6–9 sessions when conducted prior to reintroducing SD training (H19 and H3, respectively) and 7–17 sessions when introduced with a return to SD training (H4, J12, J13, and J14). Schedule thinning during the concurrent training stage appeared to be more efficient than thinning reinforcement probability prior to beginning concurrent training; Rats H3 and H19 required more LR sessions across these two stages (18 and 21 sessions, respectively) than the other subjects, who required between eight and 17 sessions. Likewise, H3 and H19 required more SD sessions (22 and 38, respectively) to recover SD performance and meet criteria for the first TOF probe, whereas the remaining rats required 7–21 SD sessions (Table 4). In general, LR accuracy remained high or recovered quickly when concurrent SD/LR training was initiated.

Transfer of function probe phase

The main findings regarding transfer of novel function, performance on the critical TOF probe trials, are shown in Figure 6. The left panel shows the results from the one‐exemplar phase, and the right panel shows the results for the three‐exemplar phase. DISCUSSION

All six subjects demonstrated robust evidence of class formation following extensive SD reversal training that was maintained throughout the experiment, replicating the results of Galizio et al. (2023) in rats and numerous studies in other species (e.g., Kastak et al., 2001; Lionello‐DeNolf, 2008; von Fersen & Delius, 2000). As in Galizio et al., evidence of class formation required the use of DSPs, but in the present study, classes were observed both under FI 5‐s and FI 10‐s schedules and with and without the addition of an IRT < t schedule. These schedule variations did not seem to affect the rate of discrimination reversal or class acquisition.

The major purpose of this study was to test transfer of a novel discriminative function for the first time in rats. Accurate performances on the initial TOF probe suggested transfer of the novel discriminative function to class members with no direct training history in five of the six rats. Accuracy declined to chance levels with repeated probes in most cases, but two subjects (H3 and J12) demonstrated reliable transfer across all probes in the one‐exemplar phase. Multiple‐exemplar training slightly improved the probe performances of two of the four rats that completed this phase. Statistically significant transfer of function was replicated in Subject J12 but not for the remaining three subjects.

Although results were mixed, it is noteworthy that two rats (J12, H3) showed consistent evidence of transfer of function throughout the one‐exemplar phase. The only previous comparable findings in animals were those of Kastak et al. (2001) in two sea lions. After training two classes (letter and number symbols) through SDR training, they found that class‐consistent responding transferred to an arbitrary matching‐to‐sample arrangement. When a member of one class was presented as a sample (e.g., a number), the sea lions selected a class member training through SDRs (e.g., another number) without direct training. The present results extend Kastak et al. to a case where a novel response (left/right pokes) trained to one or more class members transfers without direct training to other members of that class.

Although consistent transfer across multiple probes was observed in only two rats, it is interesting that five of the six rats performed highly accurately on the first TOF probe but less accurately on subsequent probes. This decline in transfer across probes suggests the possibility that repeated exposure to nonreinforced probe trials disrupted performance, as context‐novel odors were always associated with extinction. The use of nonreinforced probe trials is common in equivalence literature, as this strategy allows for repeated testing of emergent behavior without directly training such behavior (e.g., Sidman & Tailby, 1982). However, this method risks extinction of emergent responding. Because of this risk of learning across probe trials, the first test of emergent behavior is often considered to be the most critical. By this metric, data from this experiment's first TOF probe is reasonably strong evidence of transfer. Further replications are certainly needed to establish the robustness of this finding and to understand the effects of repeated exposures to extinction on probe trials.

For all subjects but H19, a side bias became evident on later probe trials, with most rats more likely to respond in the right‐side port (the “X” side); only Rat H3 was more likely to respond in the left‐side port by the end of the probe phase. Although the cause of this tendency to respond in the right‐side port is not clear, one plausible explanation is that the right port was located closer to the rear of the chamber and rats may have preferred to be away from the chamber door in an instance of species‐specific thigmotactic (wall hugging) behavior. However, this pattern was not evident on the first TOF probe or on baseline LR trials; it emerged across repeated probe exposures, lending support to the hypothesis that position biases could have been induced by discrimination of the extinction contingency on probe trials.

A caveat that should be noted, however, is that every subject's first transfer probe used X2 and Y2 as probe stimuli. All subjects who performed well on the first transfer probe also demonstrated high accuracy when X2 and Y2 were included in a subsequent probe session, limiting the interpretability of these observations. Rat H3's Probe 8, J12's Probe 7, J13's Probe 6, H19's Probe 9, and H4's Probe 11 each tested X2/Y2 as probe stimuli. All five of these subjects showed high levels of accuracy (67%–100%) on both X and Y probe trials during these sessions, whereas accuracy on other probes ranged 0%–100%. Why rats would perform more accurately with these stimuli (apricot and strawberry) is unclear. Follow‐up investigations should carefully counterbalance probe odors, particularly for the first probe session.

All rats included in this experiment demonstrated evidence of class formation in the SDR task, but response rates to positive and negative DSP stimuli were quite variable across rats. The variables responsible for differing response rates between subjects are unknown, but it does seem clear that the IRT < t requirement did not systematically increase positive response rates as was intended, as the rat with the highest positive rates (H19) was not trained on this requirement, whereas the two rats with the lowest rates (J12 and J14) were. In the same vein, it cannot be concluded that the FR 10‐s schedule with a higher DRO requirement of 5 s on negative trials systematically decreased negative response rates relative to the FR 5‐s schedule, as H19 also had the highest negative rates despite this requirement. Future research should continue to systematically explore schedule parameters to optimize the efficiency and yield of the SD reversal approach.

Procedural variability across subjects, reflective of pragmatic adjustments to the novel arrangements employed, was a limitation of this study. These adjustments were often attempts to accelerate the experiment. The time required to complete the experiment varied widely between subjects but was generally quite extensive, another clear limitation of this procedure. Rats required dozens of reversals and upwards of 350 SD sessions to meet criteria for class formation. Training on the LR task progressed relatively quickly and caused minimal disruption to performance in the SD task. However, the TOF probe phases of this experiment required a substantial amount of time, as rats were required to meet strict performance criteria for both class maintenance and baseline LR accuracy prior to each TOF probe. Several rats had to be discontinued from the study before completing all intended three‐exemplar probes due to their age and health status by the end of this lengthy experiment. Indeed, subjects were aged about 2–2.5 years by the conclusion of the experiment, which is the typical lifespan of a Sprague Dawley rat. Another limitation of this study was that only male rats were included; future research should include female rats.

Despite these limitations, further investigation is certainly warranted. Future research should focus on improving training efficiency and maximizing opportunities for repeated TOF tests, as the most significant limitations of this study were procedural variability between subjects and the amount of time required to complete the procedures as programmed. Parametric analyses of variables in the SDR procedure that improve rate of acquisition and class formation would be of value. Such analyses should attempt to determine the optimal arrangement of variables such as schedule parameters, punishment for responding on negative trials, and number of training trials/exemplars presented prior to DSP trials as well as the most effective remediation strategies. A between‐groups design may be better suited for investigating the effects of MET in this procedure, as such a design would eliminate the confound of training time and ensure that rats could complete all programmed probes before the onset of any age‐related declines in health. Another potentially useful approach for assessing transfer of function would be to compare the rate of acquisition of class‐consistent versus class‐inconsistent discriminations in the LR task. Such an arrangement would avoid the use of nonreinforced probes, which may have disrupted transfer in the present study.

Nonhuman models remain some of the most useful tools for analyzing controlling variables and eliminating confounds inherent to human behavioral research. Such models are needed to provide insight into the necessary and sufficient conditions for the development of conceptual behavior, which would have translational implications for a variety of domains (Dougher, 1998). The present work contributes to the literature on nonhuman concept learning by investigating an alternative procedure to the arbitrary matching‐to‐sample arrangement required to test for the defining properties of reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity (Sidman & Tailby, 1982). These traditional approaches to the study of equivalence, although very successful with verbally able humans, have consistently yielded negative or inconclusive results when attempted with nonhumans. Given these limitations, perhaps it is time to divert scientific focus away from the “search for symmetry” (Lionello‐DeNolf, 2009, 2021; Sidman et al., 1982) and instead endeavor to develop procedures that test other properties and outcomes of equivalence. Indeed, as advocated by Pilgrim (2016), it seems prudent to expand our definition of equivalence to be less limited to the matching‐to‐sample procedure and more generic to the fundamental processes involved. Emergent relations among conditional and discriminative stimuli are, after all, not the only predictable emergent outcomes of equivalence and may not totally encompass the fundamental processes that govern symbolic behavior. Pilgrim's suggested definition is consistent with Sidman's (2000) theoretical account of equivalence relations as “ordered pairs of all positive elements that participate in the contingency” (p. 128). Such a definition is sufficiently generic to encompass not only reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity but also other emergent outcomes observed in the literature such as the inclusion of reinforcers as class members; the emergent interchangeability of stimuli presented only in simple discrimination arrangements; and transfer of function, as the response is included as a positive element of the contingency. This expanded definition remains parsimonious while also allowing a variety of predictions to be tested outside the confines of any specific laboratory procedure (Dougher & Markham, 1994; Galizio, 1996; MacKay et al., 2011), generating novel experiments such as the present study.
