Association between hearing loss but not age and mistuning perception in music
Authors: Sara MK Madsen, Heather A Kreft, Elaea Purmalietis, Torsten Dau, Andrew J Oxenham
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe enjoyment of music can be severely disrupted when instruments or voices are out of tune with each other. Both hearing loss and aging may degrade pitch perception, potentially affecting the ability to detect mistuning. For younger listeners with normal hearing, mistuning perception in music relies on both beats (amplitude fluctuations between closely spaced frequency components) and inharmonicity (non-integer frequency relationships between components from different sources). It is unknown whether the accuracy or nature of mistuning perception changes with age or hearing loss. To address this question, we tested groups of younger and older listeners with normal hearing and older listeners with hearing loss, as determined by their audiometric thresholds (N = 91 total). Results showed that older listeners with normal hearing performed similarly to younger listeners. In contrast, hearing loss was associated with poorer mistuning detection but was not associated with poorer performance in our measures of beat or inharmonicity detection. Reduction of either beating or inharmonicity cues in music had comparable effects across all groups. Overall, the results suggest that hearing loss, but not age, is associated with poorer mistuning detection in music, but that similar cues are used by all groups to detect mistuning.Keywords: Mistuning, Music perception, Beating, Amplitude modulation, Inharmonicity, Aging, Hearing loss

Introduction

A large body of auditory research has focused on the effects of age and hearing loss on speech perception in quiet and noise and has tried to disentangle what mechanisms underlie such effects (Bernstein et al., 2013; Füllgrabe et al., 2015; Gallun et al., 2022; Souza et al., 2015). In contrast, the effects of age and hearing loss on music perception have received much less attention. Most people enjoy listening to music, but that enjoyment can sometimes be disrupted by an out-of-tune singer or instrument. For music creation, being able to detect mistuning is a crucial ingredient of sensory feedback when playing an instrument with continuous tuning, such as a violin or a flute, or when singing. A severe deficit in mistuning perception, such as that associated with amusia (Hyde and Peretz, 2004; Peretz and Hyde, 2003; Vuvan et al., 2015), can reduce music appreciation, to the point where music is no longer enjoyable (McDonald et al., 2008; Omigie et al., 2012). Conversely, a milder deficit may actually increase the enjoyment of some music when it is out of tune, such as when listening to a beginner’s recital. Mistuning detection abilities vary considerably even among young individuals with normal hearing and, not surprisingly, are generally more accurate for musicians than for non-musicians (Madsen and Oxenham, 2024). Some hearing-impaired listeners report that music is less enjoyable than it was before their hearing loss (Looi et al., 2019), and difficulties with pitch perception were found to be a commonly reported problem among hearing-aid users in a survey study (Greasley et al., 2020). However, it remains unclear whether hearing loss or aging is associated with reduced sensitivity to mistuning in music.

Two acoustic cues are thought to contribute to mistuning detection between simultaneously presented notes in music: beats (amplitude fluctuations resulting from the interactions between nearby frequency components) and inharmonicity (the deviation of components of multiple notes from a single simple harmonic series) (Madsen and Oxenham, 2024). Detecting inharmonicity in the absence of beating cues relies on accurate encoding of the frequencies of individual harmonics; thus, it is likely to be related to frequency and pitch discrimination abilities more generally. Both age and hearing loss have been linked to deficits in pitch and frequency discrimination (Abel et al., 1990; Arehart, 1994; Bernstein and Oxenham, 2006; Hoekstra and Ritsma, 1977; Moore and Peters, 1992; Russo et al., 2012). Moreover, both behavioral and neural studies have linked age and hearing loss to decreases in listeners’ ability to detect the mistuning of a single harmonic in an isolated tone (Alain et al., 2001; Grube et al., 2003; Lentz et al., 2022; Mamo et al., 2019). Consequently, it would be reasonable to predict that the aspects of mistuning detection relying on inharmonicity would be negatively affected by both age and hearing loss.

The detection and discrimination of beats are likely related to the perception of amplitude modulation (AM). The detection of AM does not seem to be as strongly associated with age (Füllgrabe et al., 2015; Paraouty et al., 2016; Paraouty and Lorenzi, 2017; Schoof and Rosen, 2014; Whiteford et al., 2017), although the discrimination of more complex patterns of AM may be (Ardoint et al., 2008; Ives et al., 2013, 2014). Hearing loss does not generally appear to degrade AM detection; indeed some studies suggest that hearing loss is associated with enhanced AM detection with pure-tone carriers when the stimuli are amplified for the hearing-impaired participants and/or presented at the same sensation level (SL) as for the normal-hearing participants (Regev et al., 2024; Schlittenlacher and Moore, 2016; Wiinberg et al., 2019). The enhancement of AM perception and detection with pure-tone carriers may be attributed to the loss of peripheral compression associated with hearing loss (Moore et al., 1996; Moore and Oxenham, 1998; Oxenham and Plack, 1997). However, the impact of hearing loss on AM detection or discrimination with complex-tone carriers remains unclear. For instance, the inherent beating between the harmonics of a single complex tone at its fundamental frequency (F0) may be stronger in listeners with hearing loss, due to their poorer frequency selectivity (Glasberg and Moore, 1986; Nelson and Schroder, 1995). Such inherent beating might then result in more modulation masking of any additional modulations caused by the beats associated with mistuning (Bacon and Grantham, 1989; Ewert and Dau, 2000; Houtgast, 1989).

In this study, we investigated the association of age and hearing loss with mistuning detection in music by testing young normal-hearing (YNH), older normal-hearing (ONH), and older hearing-impaired (OHI) participants with mild-to-moderate sensorineural hearing loss. In the first experiment we tested mistuning detection in music for different mistuning sizes. In the second experiment we investigated the relative contributions of beating and inharmonicity cues to detecting mistuning in music by assessing the effects of reducing the availability of each cue separately. In the third and fourth experiments we measured our participants’ sensitivity to inharmonicity and beats, respectively, in isolated tones to assess the extent to which performance in each was associated with their sensitivity to mistuning in music. In a fifth experiment, we used an abbreviated version of the notched-noise test to assess frequency selectivity. Finally, we compared the results from all the experiments and examined their relationship to individual participants’ musical experience. An understanding of how mistuning is detected, and whether the relative reliance on different acoustic cues changes with age or hearing status, will deepen our understanding of how everyday music is perceived and may point towards new approaches for rehabilitation and treatment in cases where difficulties with music perception are reported.

Method

Participants

Ninety-one participants took part in the experiments. Thirty-one were YNH (17 female, 14 male; age: mean = 20.5 years, SD =3.38 years), 30 were ONH (21 female, 9 male; age: mean = 65.9 years, SD = 8.05 years) and 30 were older OHI (21 female, 9 male; age: mean = 75.2 years, SD = 5.68 years). All but one YNH participant completed all experiments; that one participant did not complete the beat detection experiment but their data for all other experiments were included in the analysis. The YNH participants were younger than 30 years of age and the ONH and OHI participants were aged 50 or older. A summary of the age distribution of the participants in each group is shown in Fig. 1A. Audiometric thresholds were measured at octave frequencies between 250 and 8000 Hz for the YNH participants and included extra frequencies of 125, 3000, and 6000 Hz for the older participants. The YNH participants had audiometric thresholds no greater than 20 dB HL in the test ear, and the ONH participants were required to have audiometric pure-tone thresholds no greater than 30 dB HL between 125–2000 Hz and a pure-tone average (PTA) across 0.5, 1, 2, and 3 kHz of less than 25 dB HL in the test ear. The OHI participants were required to have a sensorineural hearing loss as confirmed by air-bone gaps less than or equal to 10 dB or reported in audiological evaluations as long-standing sensorineural hearing loss (air conduction thresholds were tested, just not bone) in the test ear. They had PTAs across 0.5, 1, 2, and 3 kHz of greater than 25 dB HL in the test ear. Mean audiometric thresholds for each group are shown in Fig. 1B. Analysis for only the ONH and the OHI groups showed a significant correlation between PTA and age (Fig. S1). Nineteen of the 30 OHI participants reported being in possession of a hearing aid, but no hearing aids were used in this study.

Musical experience was quantified with the musician rank item (“Which title best describes you”) from Ollen’s Musical Sophistication index questionnaire (Ollen, 2006), as this was found to be the best single-item predictor of overall musical sophistication (Zhang and Schubert, 2019). The participants could choose between six response options ranging from “non-musician” to “professional musician.” This metric is useful because it can otherwise be difficult to compare years of musical experience across groups differing in age. Responses from the three groups (Fig. 1C) were similar, and a cumulative link model ANOVA found no evidence of a statistical difference between groups in terms of musical sophistication (χ22=0.68, p = 0.713 RMF2<0.01, where RMF2 refers to McFadden’s pseudo R2). This outcome suggests the groups were well balanced in terms of musical experience.

General stimuli and procedure

The study was conducted in a sound-attenuating booth using Matlab (Mathworks, Natick, MA). The stimuli were converted to analog at a sampling rate of 44.1 kHz using a Lynx E22 sound card with 24-bit resolution and presented monaurally via HD650 Sennheiser headphones (Old Lyme, CT). The test ear was pseudorandomized. This was considered reasonable because the average pure-tone thresholds (PTA) across 0.5, 1,2, and 3 kHz differed by 15 dB HL or less for all but one OHI participant, whose PTA difference was 16.3 dB HL. For octave frequencies in the frequency range tested in this study (between 125 and 3000 Hz), the difference in pure-tone thresholds between ears were larger than 15 dB at two adjacent frequencies for three OHI participants and larger than 15 dB at three adjacent frequencies for one OHI participant. The notched-noise, beat-detection, and inharmonicity-detection experiments were conducted using the AFC software package (Ewert, 2013). The stimuli in the beat-detection, inharmonicity--detection, and both mistuning experiments, were presented at 60 dB SPL before any addition amplification was applied (see below). All experiments were approved by the University of Minnesota’s Institutional Review Board.

For participants in the OHI group, the stimuli in all but the notched-noise experiment were amplified using the same modified half-gain rule that was applied by David et al. (2018). This process involved bandpass filtering the stimuli using octave-wide filters centered on 125, 250, 500, 1000, and 2000 Hz. Any band in which the audiometric threshold, T, exceeded 20 dB HL was then amplified by (T-20)/2 dB, up to a maximum gain of 22.5 dB (for someone with an audiometric threshold of 65 dB HL or worse). In all experiments, a noise, filtered to have the same long-term spectral envelope as the experimental stimuli was presented to the contralateral ear at a level 20 dB lower than that of the experimental stimuli, to prevent the detection of acoustic or electrical crosstalk. In each trial, the noise started at the same time as the stimuli in the first interval and continued until the end of the stimuli in the last interval. It was gated on and off with 50-ms onset and offset ramps.

Complex tones in the inharmonicity and beat detection experiments were spectrally shaped to have the same long-term spectral envelope as the piano stimuli in the main mistuning detection experiments. All participants completed the first three experiments in the following order: Notched noise (experiment 5), mistuning experiment 1, and mistuning experiment 2. Half the participants (selected at random) then completed the beat-detection (experiment 3) followed by inharmonicity-detection experiments (experiment 4), and the other half then completed the inharmonicity-detection followed by beat-detection experiments.

Both mistuning detection experiments used the same music examples and paradigm as in our earlier study (Madsen and Oxenham, 2024): participants were presented with short passages extracted from J. S. Bach’s Two-part Inventions (BWV 772–786). These short keyboard pieces are written in two parts, with two notes usually present at any one time. Excerpts of 4.25 - 5 s were selected with the constraint that both voices were present throughout the excerpt. The parts were played with a piano (experiment 1 and 2) or marimba sound (experiment 2) from GarageBand (Apple, Cupertino, CA). The stimuli were low-pass filtered with a 16th-order Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 3000 Hz to reduce any potential effects of greater differences in audibility above 3 kHz between the YNH and ONH groups. Participants were asked to judge whether the passage was in tune or not, using a four-point rating scale: “sure in-tune”, “maybe in-tune”, “maybe out-of-tune”, “sure out-of-tune”. Sensitivity was then quantified in terms of d’, calculated as: d’=2⋅qnorm(AUC), where qnorm is the inverse of the normal cumulative distribution function, and AUC is the area under the receiver operating curve (ROC). The AUC was estimated with the Matlab trapz function on false alarm and hit rates for the most liberal (all responses count as being mistuned) to the most conservative criteria (all responses count as being in-tune). The false alarm and hit rates were corrected using the loglinear approach (Hautus, 1995) before they were converted to d’ to avoid infinite d’ values. Correlations were calculated in Matlab and other statistical analysis was conducted with R (R Core Team, 2020).

Training consisted of 20 trials (half in tune and half mistuned) with feedback. As in our earlier study (Madsen and Oxenham, 2024), the five excerpts used for training were different from the sequences used for testing. Each of the five training excerpts occurred twice in tune, once with the higher voice mistuned by −0.9 semitones (ST) and once with the higher voice mistuned by −0.3 ST for training exerpt 1, by −0.5 ST for training excerpts 2 and 3, and by −0.7 ST for training exerpts 4 and 5. An overview of the experimental conditions included in this study is provided in Table 1.

Overview of the experiments.

Experiment 1: effect of mistuning size on detection

This experiment tested mistuning detection for mistuning sizes of −0.3 to −0.9 ST in steps of 0.2 ST and included an equal number of intune and out-of-tune trials, selected at random with equal (50 %) probability. If the passage was out of tune, the upper part was lowered in pitch by 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, or 0.9 ST Each stimulus was presented twice, resulting in a total of 160 trials (4 mistuning sizes x 2 repetitions x 10 excerpts x 2 in-tune or mistuned). The order of the conditions was randomized across all conditions. The participants received no feedback. The d’ values were calculated separately for each mistuning size. The results from this experiment determined the mistuning size used for each participant in Experiment 2, as well as the beat frequency used in the beat detection experiment. If at least one d’ value was above 1.5 in Experiment 1, the smallest mistuning size for which this was the case was chosen for Experiment 2. If all d’ values were 1.5 or below but one or more were above chance, the largest mistuning size for which this was the case was chosen.

Experiment 2: effect of reducing beats or inharmonicity cues

This experiment tested both positive (i.e., stretched musical intervals) and negative (i.e., compressed musical intervals, as in Experiment 1) mistuning values. One third of the trials were in-tune, one third were mistuned with compressed intervals, and one third were mistuned with stretched intervals.

Five conditions were tested at the individual mistuning levels determined in Experiment 1. The first was our baseline piano condition with all notes generated using a GarageBand piano sound (as in Experiment 1). The second and third were conditions with reduced access to beating cues: The marimba condition, with all notes generated using a GarageBand marimba sound, which includes very little energy in the upper harmonics (see Madsen and Oxenham, 2024) and the filtered condition with the lower part low-pass filtered and the upper part high-pass filtered, both with a cutoff frequency of 800 Hz as illustrated in Fig. 2A. Finally, the fourth and fifth conditions reduced inharmonicity cues: The chimera condition manipulated the mistuned stimuli to maintain beating cues but reduce access to inharmonicity cues (Madsen and Oxenham, 2024; Smith et al., 2002), using processing that assumes peripheral filtering bandwidths in line with normal hearing; the chimera-wide condition also reduced access to inharmonicity cues, but used filtering with approximately twice the bandwidths associated with normal hearing, more similar to that expected with moderate hearing loss of about 40 dB HL (Glasberg and Moore, 1986), in line with the mean threshold values of our OHI participants around 1 kHz. All notes in the filtered and in the two chimera conditions were piano sounds generated in GarageBand and processed in Matlab. The stimuli for the two chimera conditions were processed with a chimerizer (Smith et al., 2002) that in each frequency channel combined the temporal envelope from one signal with the temporal fine structure from another signal. As in our earlier study, both the mistuned and in-tune stimuli were processed this way (Madsen and Oxenham, 2024). For the mistuned chimera conditions, this processing resulted in an output waveform that included the acoustic beats associated with mistuning in the temporal envelope, while maintaining harmonic relationships between spectral components of the in-tune condition in the temporal fine structure (see Fig 2B). For the in-tune stimuli, in each sub-band, the original in-tune temporal fine structure was combined with the temporal envelope of a transposed version of the in-tune stimuli, maintaining both the in-tune fine structure and temporal envelope. Processing both the in-tune and the mistuned stimuli limits the use of potential artifacts from the chimera processing as a cue for mistuning detection.

Illustration of processing used in experiment 2. A) Illustration of the lowpass filtering used for the low-part tones and the bandpass filtering used for the high-part tones in the filtered condition. B) Diagram showing the steps used by the chimerizer to produce the mistuned stimuli for the two chimera conditions. This includes an illustration of the concepts of envelope and fine structure.

We used a 24-channel chimerizer for the chimera condition and a 12-channel chimerizer for the chimera-wide condition. The channels were spaced equally on a log scale with a lower cutoff frequency of 80 Hz to an upper cutoff frequency of 17,640 Hz, but only frequencies up to 3 kHz were passed through the final lowpass filter.

Experiment 3: beat detection

This experiment used a 3-interval 3-alternative forced-choice (AFC) procedure, with a 2-down 1-up adaptive rule that tracks the 70.7 % correct point of the psychometric function (Levitt, 1971) to measure beat detection thresholds for pure tones and complex tones. The fundamental frequency of the complex tones was centered at 261.6 Hz (C4) and the frequency of the pure tones was centered at 1046.4 Hz corresponding to the 4th harmonic of the complex tone. The frequency or F0 was roved by ±1.5 ST from the center for each tone with uniform distribution in ST. Both the complex tones and the contralateral noise (white noise) were filtered to have the same long-term average spectral envelope as the piano stimuli in the mistuning experiments (experiment 1 and 2), and all tones and noise were gated with 50-ms raised-cosine onset and offset ramps.

The target tone (tone with beats) was produced by adding a higher tone of the same type to the primary tone to generate beating. The frequency or F0 difference between the two tones corresponded to the mistuning size used for each participant in Experiment 2. All complex tones consisted of all harmonics from the second to the highest harmonic with a frequency less than 3000 Hz. The complex tones were presented at a level of 60 dB SPL and the pure tones at level of 63 dB SPL, but both amplified by the modified half-gain rule in the OHI listeners. For instance, for a participant with an audiometric threshold of 40 dB HL at 1 kHz, the complex-tone presentation level was 70 dB SPL [60 + (40–20)/2], thus ensuring good audibility. The contralateral noise was for both tone types presented at a level of 40 dB SPL and was also amplified by the modified half-gain rule based on the audiometric thresholds of the test ear.

The level of the higher added tone (which created the beats) was varied adaptively starting at −3 dB, relative to the primary tone, and was initially changed with a step size of 6 dB. The step size was reduced to 2 dB after the second reversal in the direction of the tracking procedure and to 1 dB after the fourth reversal. The run then continued for another six reversals at the smallest step size. The threshold for a run was calculated as the mean depth at those last six reversal points. The experiment started with a training run of each condition (pure and complex tones) followed by three blocks each containing one pure-tone run and one complex-tone run in random order. The reported thresholds are the mean across the three test runs. If the standard deviation was larger than 3 dB, an extra run of that condition was added, and the threshold was calculated across all four runs.

Experiment 4: inharmonicity detection

In a 3AFC paradigm, the stimuli consisted of two harmonic tones and one inharmonic tone (target), similar to the stimuli employed by Graves et al. (2023). The participants were asked to indicate which of the tones was different but to ignore the differences in pitch. All the tones consisted of the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 5th, and 9th component in a harmonic series, added in sine phase. In the inharmonic tones, all frequency components were shifted up by the same amount in Hertz. The adaptive parameter was the size of the frequency shift. As in the beat detection experiment (experiment 3), a 2-down 1-up procedure was used to track the 70.7 % correct point of the psychometric function. The F0 was roved between intervals by ±1.5 ST around 261.6 Hz (C4) with uniform distribution in ST. The tones were filtered to have the same spectral slope as the piano stimuli in the main mistuning experiments. The tones were presented at a level of 60 dB SPL but amplified by the modified half-gain rule in the OHI listeners. All tones had a duration of 500 ms and were gated on and off with 50-ms raised-cosine onset and offset ramps.

An adaptive run started with a frequency shift of 50 Hz. The shift initially changed by a factor of 2.51 until the second reversal in the adaptive procedure, at which point the shift changed by a factor of 1.58 for the next two reversals, and then by a factor of 1.26 for the final six reversals. Thresholds were calculated as the geometric mean frequency shift at the last six reversal points. Participants received one training run before embarking on three test runs. The final threshold was defined as the geometric mean across the three test runs.

Experiment 5: notched noise

Frequency selectivity was estimated by comparing thresholds for a 1-kHz tone in a bandpass noise, filtered between 20 and 2800 Hz, with those in the same noise but with a spectral notch, extending from 600 to 1400 Hz (0.4fs on each side of the 1-kHz tone, where fs refers to signal frequency). The difference in thresholds between the bandpass and notched noise provides a rough estimate of frequency selectivity (Moore et al., 1985). The spectrum level of the Gaussian white noise was 50 dB SPL. Thresholds for the tone in quiet were also measured to test whether audibility was limiting performance in the presence of the noise masker. When present, the tone was 300 ms in duration and was temporally centered in a 500-ms noise. The noise and tones were gated on and off with 20- and 10-ms raised cosine ramps, respectively. The contralateral noise was a threshold equalizing noise (TEN; Moore et al., 2000), filtered between 20 and 2800 Hz. The presentation level of the background noise was defined in terms of the level within an estimated human equivalent rectangular bandwidth (ERB) around 1 kHz (Glasberg and Moore, 1990) varied with the level of the signal. It was always 20 dB lower than the level of the signal to rule out contralateral detection of the signal, without producing any direct masking. The contralateral noise was gated on 20 ms before the first interval and gated off 20 ms after the last interval of each trial. The experiment used a 3AFC procedure with a 2-down 1-up adaptive rule. The initial level of the tone was 85 dB SPL in the bandpass noise, 70 dB SPL in the notched noise, and 60 dB SPL in the quiet condition. The initial step size of the adaptive procedure was 8 dB, which was reduced to 4 dB after two reversals. After two more reversals, the step size was reduced to 2 dB for the final six reversals. The threshold for each run was defined as the mean of the signal level at those last six reversal points. Participants were trained with one run of each condition and were tested in three following runs. If the standard deviation across the three test runs exceeded 3 dB, a fourth run was added, and the threshold was calculated as the mean across all four runs. Thresholds in quiet were measured first, followed by thresholds in the bandpass and notched noise, presented in random order on each of the three repetitions.

Statistical analysis

Mistuning- and beat-detection data were analyzed with linear mixed-effects models using the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015). Model selection was conducted with the lmerTest package (Kuznetsova et al., 2017) following the backward selection approach based on stepwise deletion of model terms with high p-values. The inharmonicity-detection data were analyzed with a one-way ANOVA using the car package (Fox and Weisberg, 2019). The emmeans package (Lenth et al., 2019) was used for multiple comparisons and effect sizes were estimated with the effectsize package (Ben-Shachar et al., 2020). Due to large deviations from normality and homogeneity of variance, the notched-noise data were analyzed with a Kruskall-Wallis test for the main analysis and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test for the pairwise comparisons. The latter and calculation of effect sizes were performed using the rstatix package (Kassambara, 2023).

Both musical experience (from Ollen’s Musical Sophistication index) and sex were included in the analysis for experiment 1–4 as main fixed factors. Interactions with these factors were not included in the analysis, since the numbers of female and male participants were unbalanced and not all levels of musical sophistication were represented in each group. To compare musical experience between groups, a cumulative link model using the ordinal package (Christensen, 2023) was used because of the ordinal nature of the item from Ollen’s Musical Sophistication index. All tests of paired comparisons and correlations were two-tailed.

Results

Experiment 1: mistuning detection in music

Fig. 3A shows that mistuning detection performance for all three groups improved with increasing mistuning size between −0.3 and −0.5 ST and then reached a plateau at around −0.7 ST. Performance was generally poorer for the OHI group compared to both NH groups, especially at the smallest mistuning size. At the larger mistuning sizes, the YNH group seemed to reach a plateau at a slightly lower level of performance than the ONH group. A linear mixed-effects model analysis, with mistuning size, listener group, sex, and musical sophistication as fixed factors and participants as a random factor with a random intercept, confirmed significant effects of mistuning size (F3,264 = 148.99, p < 0.001, ωp2=0.62) and listener group (F2,82 = 9.08, p < 0.001, ωp2=0.16), along with a significant interaction between mistuning size and group (F6,264 = 6.87, p < 0.001, ωp2=0.11). Bonferroni-corrected multiple comparisons of listener groups (αBonferroni=0.05/12=0.004) for each mistuning level revealed significant differences between the ONH and the OHI group at all but the largest mistuning level (−0.3 ST: t127 = 4.45, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.40; −0.5 ST: t127 = 4.34, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.39; −0.7 ST: t127 = 3.52, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.31; −0.9 ST: t127 = 2.87, p = 0.005, Cohen’s d = 0.25), confirming a significant effect of hearing loss on mistuning detection accuracy. The difference between the YNH and OHI groups was significant at the smallest mistuning level (t129 = 4.56, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.40), but was not significant at the larger mistuning levels, where performance of the YNH seemed to saturate (−0.5 ST: t129 = 2.58, p = 0.011, Cohen’s d = 0.23; −0.7 ST: t129 = 1.20, p = 0.233, Cohen’s d = 0.11; −0.9 ST: t129 = 0.15, p = 0.884, Cohen’s d = 0.01). Pairwise comparisons between the YNH and ONH groups showed no significant difference (−0.3 ST: t127 < 0.01, p = 0.998, Cohen’s d = 0.00; −0.5 ST: t127 = −1.85, p = 0.067, Cohen’s d = −0.16; −0.7 ST: t127 = −2.39, p = 0.019, Cohen’s d = −0.21; −0.9 ST: t127 = −2.76, p = 0.007, Cohen’s d = −0.24).

B) Relationship between the self-rated level of musicianship from Ollen’s Musical Sophistication Index and mistuning detection performance for −0.3 ST mistuning. The positive Spearman’s ranked correlation coefficient (ρ) indicates that greater musical sophistication was associated with improved performance in the mistuning task.

Overall, the comparison between the ONH and OHI groups suggests that hearing loss is associated with poorer mistuning detection in music. In contrast, the comparison between the YNH and ONH groups does not reveal any detrimental effect of age on performance. Indeed, while the performance of the two groups was very similar at the smallest mistuning levels, the ONH group outperformed the YNH group at larger mistuning levels, where a plateau was reached. This pattern may be due to more diligence or motivation on the part of the older listeners, relative to the younger listeners, and a potentially lower lapse rate in responding (Dai and Micheyl, 2011).

The analysis found no effect of sex (F1,82 = 0.08, p = 0.776, ωp2=0.00) but a significant effect of musical experience, in terms of the selected item from Ollen’s musical sophistication index (F5,82 = 3.85, p = 0.004, ωp2=0.14). This is consistent with the correlation found between Ollen’s index and −0.3 ST mistuning detection (Fig. 3B; Spearman’s ρ = 0.37, p < 0.001).This mistuning size was selected as it was also the size used for correlations in our earlier study (Madsen and Oxenham, 2024).

Although we attempted to match the ages of the two older groups, the mean age of the ONH group was significantly lower than that of the OHI group. For this reason, an additional analysis was carried out with just the ONH and OHI groups (see Table S1), treating age and hearing loss (PTA) as continuous variables. This analysis was consistent with the above except that it showed a significant interaction between mistuning size and age (F3,174 = 7.91, p < 0.001, ωp2=0.10). This seems to mainly be explained by the smaller effect of mistuning size (flatter curve), for the youngest of the older participants (Fig S2B). However, the four of the five youngest ONH also had greater than median musical experience (Ollen’s index > 2) and low PTAs. It is therefore not clear what drove this effect.

Experiment 2: relative importance of beats and inharmonicity cues

The amount of mistuning in Experiment 2 was set for each participant individually, based on their performance in Experiment 1. The data were analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model with processing condition, mistuning direction (compressed or stretched), listener group, sex, and musical sophistication as fixed effects and participants as a random effect. Random intercepts were included for each participant to account for differences in performance. We also included random slopes for processing condition and mistuning direction, allowing these effects to vary across participants.

The results are shown in Fig. 4. Because the level of mistuning was individualized to produce roughly equal performance across all participants, it is the relative, rather than the absolute, level of performance across conditions that is of interest here. Performance in the original (piano) condition, which included both beating and inharmonicity cues, was high, as expected. Performance in all other conditions was poorer, as confirmed by a significant main effect of condition (F4,88 = 135.64, p < 0.001, ωp2=0.85), with performance in the piano condition being significantly higher after correction for multiple comparisons (αBonferroni=0.05/10=0.005) than in any of the other conditions (marimba: t88 = 19.03, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 2.03; filtered: t88 = 17.21, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.83; Chimera: t88 = 18.81, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 2.00; and Chimera-wide: t88 = 19.02, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 2.03). Moreover, the differences between the marimba and the filtered condition (t88 = −6.73, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = −0.72) as well as between the marimba and the chimera condition (t88 = −3.02, p = 0.003, Cohen’s d = −0.32) was significant while performance in the other conditions did not differ significantly from each other (p > 0.005 in all comparisons).

Results from mistuning Experiment 2, showing the effect of reduced beats (marimba and filtered conditions) and reduced inharmonicity cues (chimera and chimera-wide conditions), relative to baseline performance (piano condition).

Performance was similar between listener groups, with no significant main effect of group (F2,86 = 0.15, p = 0.859, ωp2=0.00), indicating successful equalization of difficulty using individualized mistuning sizes. More importantly, there was no significant interaction between processing condition and listener group (F8,88 = 1.87, p = 0.075, ωp2=0.07), suggesting no significant differences in weight assigned to beats and inharmonicity cues for mistuning detection by age or hearing status. There was a significant main effect of mistuning direction (F1,88 = 76.39, p < 0.001, ωp2=0.46), with compressed mistuning (as tested in Experiment 1) producing generally better performance than stretched mistuning, consistent with previous findings in only young NH listeners (Madsen and Oxenham, 2024). Interactions between processing condition and mistuning direction (F4,352 = 31.94, p < 0.001, ωp2=0.26) and mistuning direction and group (F2,88 = 5.06, p = 0.008, ωp2=0.08), as well as the three-way interaction between processing condition, mistuning direction, and group (F8,352 = 6.31, p < 0.001, ωp2=0.11) were significant. These interactions seem to be driven by the larger difference between compressed and stretched mistuning in the filtered condition for the ONH than for the other groups. This interpretation is confirmed by pairwise comparisons of the interactions between mistuning direction and group (see Table 2) showing that the difference between the compressed and stretched condition is only different between the YNH and ONH group and between the ONH and OHI groups, and then only for the filtered condition. As in experiment 1, there was no effect of sex (F1,82 = 0.07, p = 0.799, ωp2=0.00) but a significant effect of musical experience, in terms of the selected item from Ollen’s index (F5,82 = 6.71, p < 0.001, ωp2=0.25).

Experiment 3: beat detection

This experiment tested basic beat detection using both pure tones and harmonic complex tones as carriers. We hypothesized that hearing loss should be associated with poorer beat detection for complex tones but not for pure tones, due to broadened auditory filters and subsequently more modulation masking produced by interactions between the harmonic components. The beat detection thresholds, shown in Fig. 5A, were analyzed with a linear mixed-effects model, with tone type (pure or complex), group (YNH, ONH, and OHI), sex and musical sophistication as fixed factors and participants as a random factor with random intercept. There were significant effects of tone type (F1,87 = 49.97, p < 0.001, ωp2=0.35) and listener group (F2,81 = 5.68, p = 0.005, ωp2=0.10), as well as a significant interaction (F2,87 = 4.21, p = 0.018, ωp2=0.07). The main effect of tone type appears to reflect the somewhat lower (better) thresholds for the pure-tone than the complex-tone carrier, and the main effect of group may reflect the somewhat lower (better) mean thresholds of the OHI group, relative to the NH groups (Fig. 5A). The interaction may reflect the smaller differences between groups for the complex-tone than for the pure-tone carrier. Indeed, complex-tone thresholds were similar for all groups (αBonferroni=0.05/6=0.008; YNH vs ONH: t126 = 1.08, p = 0.283, Cohen’s d = 0.10; YNH vs OHI: t127 = 2.16, p = 0.033, Cohen’s d = 0.19; ONH vs OHI: t125 = 1.04, p = 0.299, Cohen’s d = 0.09), whereas mean pure-tone thresholds were lower (better) for the OHI group than for either of the NH groups (αBonferroni=0.05/6=0.008; YNH vs OHI: t127 = 3.21, p = 0.002, Cohen’s d = 0.28; ONH and OHI: t125 = 3.77, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.34). As before, the results obtained from analyzing the data from just the two older groups and treating age and PTA as continuous variables resulted in the same conclusions: there was a significant effect of both tone type and PTA, and a significant interaction between them; however, there were no significant effects of or interactions with age (Table S3).

The pure-tone results are consistent with earlier findings showing better pure-tone AM detection in OHI listeners than NH listeners, when levels are similar in terms of sensational level (Schlittenlacher and Moore, 2016; Wiinberg et al., 2019). However, the results do not support our initial hypothesis that complex-tone AM detection thresholds would be poorer for OHI listeners, due to modulation masking produced by greater inherent beating between the components.

The analysis found no effect of musical experience (F5,81 = 1.01, p = 0.416, ωp2<0.01) which is consistent with the lack of correlation between beat detection and musical experience (Fig. 5E; r = 0.15, p = 0.172). However, we did find a small but significant effect of sex (F1,81 = 6.24, p = 0.014, ωp2=0.06) reflecting the slightly better performance of the male than the female participants (Fig. S5).

Experiment 4: inharmonicity detection

Inharmonicity detection thresholds were measured to determine whether any differences between groups and/or individuals observed in Experiments 1 could be explained in terms of sensitivity to basic inharmonicity. Results were very similar between groups (Fig. 5B). A three-way ANOVA performed on the log-transformed threshold values with listener group, sex, and musical sophistication as fixed factors found a small but significant main effect of group (F2,82 = 3.75, p = 0.028, ωp2=0.05). However, pairwise comparisons showed that only the difference between the YNH and the ONH group was significant (αBonferroni=0.05/3=0.017; YNH vs ONH: t82 = −2.72, p = 0.008, Cohen’s d = −0.30; YNH vs OHI: t82 = −1.00, p = 0.320, Cohen’s d = −0.11; ONH vs OHI: t82 = 1.70, p = 0.092, Cohen’s d = 0.19). The lack of a difference between the ONH and the OHI group suggests that inharmonicity detection performance is not affected by hearing loss. In fact, performance was slightly better for the OHI than for the ONH group. Moreover, even though the performance was slightly better for the YNH than the two older groups, the difference was not significant between the YNH and the OHI groups. This finding, together with the lack of an age effect when only considering the data from the ONH and OHI groups (Table S4), suggests that age did not affect our listeners’ ability to detect inharmonicity with these stimuli. Despite the small differences in mean performance between the groups, the spread of individual thresholds appears to be less in the YNH group, with some particularly poor performers among the ONH and OHI groups. Interestingly, as shown in the Supplementary Information (Fig. S3), this tendency is counteracted by some particularly good older performers, perhaps due to extensive musical training, resulting in no clear effect of either age or hearing loss. Consistent with this explanation, we did find a significant effect of musical experience in terms of the item from Ollen’s index here (F5,82 = 4.58, p < 0.001, ωp2=0.16) and when only including data from the ONH and OHI groups (Supplementary Information; Table S4). Moreover, a correlation showed a clear association between inharmonicity detection thresholds and the selected item from Ollen’s index (Fig. 5D; ρ = −0.30, p = 0.003). There was no effect of sex (F1,82 < 0.01, p = 0.956, ωp2=0.00).

Experiment 5: notched noise

Fig. 1D shows that thresholds in the bandpass noise were similar for all groups, whereas thresholds in the presence of the notched noise were higher (poorer) for the OHI group than for the other two groups. The difference in thresholds obtained in the bandpass and notched noises provides a summary measure of frequency selectivity (Moore et al., 1985), with smaller differences indicating broader auditory filters. Fig. 1E shows a tendency for this difference to be smaller in the OHI group than in the YNH and ONH groups. Due to large deviations from normality and homogeneity of variance, nonparametric tests were used for the analysis of this difference measure. A Kruskal-Wallis test showed a significant effect of group (H2 = 21.8, p < 0.001, η2=0.23) and paired Wilcoxon rank test comparisons confirmed significant differences between the OHI and the other two groups (YNH and OHI: W = 738, p < 0.001, r = 0.55; ONH and OHI: W = 687, p < 0.001, r = 0.50; αBonferroni=0.05/3=0.017) but not between the YNH and the ONH groups (W = 472, p = 0.925, r = 0.01). A Spearman rank correlation conducted on only the data from the ONH and OHI groups showed a significant association between our measure of auditory filter width and PTA (ρ = −0.55, p < 0.05; Fig. S4). Separate analyses found no evidence of an association with musical experience (Ollen’s index; ρ = 0.10, p = 0.326) or sex (W= 1071.5, p = 0.228, r = 0.13).

Relationships between measures

We compared the three measures with each other, revealing a relationship between mistuning sensitivity and inharmonicity detection thresholds (Fig. 5G; r = 0.33, p = 0.001) but not between beat and inharmonicity detection thresholds (Fig. 5E; r = 0.15, p = 0.172) or between beat detection thresholds and mistuning sensitivity (Fig. 5F; r = 0.13, p = 0.230).

Discussion

The results from Experiment 1 (Fig. 3A) showed that mistuning detection is better for ONH than for OHI participants, consistent with our hypothesis that hearing loss has a deleterious effect on mistuning detection in music. However, our findings do not support the hypothesis that mistuning detection worsens with increasing age, as mean performance in the ONH group matched or exceeded that of the YNH group. The poorer mistuning detection observed in the OHI listeners is consistent with results from earlier studies demonstrating an association between hearing loss and degraded pitch processing in terms of poorer pitch discrimination (Arehart, 1994; Bernstein and Oxenham, 2006; Hoekstra and Ritsma, 1977; Moore and Peters, 1992), poorer frequency modulation detection (Buss et al., 2004; Moore and Skrodzka, 2002; Paraouty et al., 2016; Strelcyk and Dau, 2009; Whiteford et al., 2020), and poorer inharmonicity discrimination (Bonnard et al., 2017). In contrast, the effect of age on pitch processing remains less clear. A few studies have reported degraded pitch discrimination with age (Abel et al., 1990; Moore and Peters, 1992; Russo et al., 2012) but the within-group variability was generally high relative to the sample sizes. Results from studies measuring frequency-modulation detection are also somewhat inconsistent, with most (Moore et al., 2019a; Paraouty et al., 2016; Whiteford et al., 2017) but not all (Schoof and Rosen, 2014) studies showing a detrimental effect of age. The lack of effect of age in our study adds to our understanding of effect of aging on pitch perception when using more realistic music sequences. In fact, our use of actual music sequences, rather than isolated tones, means that our results are much more likely to reflect mistuning detection when listening to music in real life.

The pattern of results across conditions in Experiment 2 (Fig. 4) suggests that neither age nor hearing loss had a strong influence on listeners’ weighting of beating or inharmonicity cues for mistuning detection. As observed previously among NH listeners (Madsen and Oxenham, 2024), decreasing the access to either cue had a similar effect on overall performance in all three groups.

The similar performance across all listener groups in the beat detection experiment for complex tones (Fig. 5A) and the inharmonicity detection experiment (Fig. 5B) is consistent with results from the second mistuning experiment. Moreover, the better performance of some OHI participants than NH participants for pure tones in the beat detection experiment is consistent with findings from other studies showing improved amplitude modulation detection thresholds in OHI listeners when tested at the same SL or with amplified stimuli, as in our study (Bianchi et al., 2016; Schlittenlacher and Moore, 2016; Wiinberg et al., 2019). The results provide some support for our hypothesis that wider auditory filters increase interaction between harmonics, leading to inherent amplitude fluctuations that might mask beats and therefore worsen beat detection for complex but not pure tones. This is reflected by the larger difference between thresholds for the two types of tones for the OHI than for the other groups. However, the fact that performance was no worse for the OHI group than for the other groups for complex tones fails to explain their poorer performance in the mistuning detection task of Experiment 1. Envelope fluctuations also contribute to timbre and thereby music perception more generally and has been shown to be important for speech intelligibility (Drullman et al., 1994a, 1994b; Elliott and Theunissen, 2009; Relano-Iborra and Dau, 2022; Shannon et al., 1995). However, the similar complex-tone beat detection thresholds across groups indicates that any group differences found for speech perception and music perception more generally are not associated with degraded envelope fluctuation detection abilities, although they may be related with the ability to discriminate more complex temporal-envelope patterns (Ives et al., 2013).

Inharmonicity thresholds for the YNH group (Fig. 5B) were comparable to results for the control group in an earlier study using similar stimuli (Graves et al., 2023). The absence of an effect of hearing loss appears inconsistent with the small deleterious effect of hearing loss reported by Bonnard et al. (2017). However, only eight OHI participants were tested in that study. In the present study, within-group variability seemed greater for the two older groups, and there was a significant effect of listener group. However, additional analysis found neither an effect of hearing loss nor an effect of age. Perhaps the lack of an effect can be explained by the average F0 used in this experiment (around 261.6 Hz; C4), with the second harmonic around 523.2 Hz. The average amount of hearing loss of our OHI participants was mild at these low frequencies (Fig. 1B) meaning that any effects of such mild hearing loss may be difficult to detect. It is possible that inharmonicity detection would be more severely degraded for people with greater low-frequency hearing loss, or if the measurements had been carried out with only higher-frequency stimuli, in frequency regions where the hearing loss was greater. More generally, harmonicity is critical for pitch perception (McPherson and McDermott, 2018) and inharmonicity degrades the ability to segregate instruments or voices, which impairs music scene analysis and intelligibility of speech masked by speech or noise (Bogaard et al., 2025; McPherson et al., 2022; Popham et al., 2018).

The results from Experiment 2, 3, and 4, suggest that the poorer mistuning detection of the OHI listeners in Experiment 1 cannot be explained by degraded beat and/or inharmonicity detection abilities. Perhaps, the difficulties detection mistuning for a small mistuning size is instead related to poorer pitch discrimination of OHI listeners (Bernstein and Oxenham, 2006; Bianchi et al., 2016).

The lack of relationship between our measures of musical experience and auditory filter width is consistent with results from earlier studies (Fine and Moore, 1993; Moore et al., 2019b; Oxenham et al., 2003). Correlations between musical experience and inharmonicity and mistuning detection in music, as well as between mistuning and inharmonicity detection, suggest that mistuning detection in music is related to inharmonicity and not beat detection. This conclusion is consistent with findings from studies on the perception of consonance and dissonance of isolated tones, showing that subjective ratings of strength of preference for consonant over dissonant stimuli are associated with strength of preference for harmonic over inharmonic stimuli but not with strength of preference for stimuli without beats (Cousineau et al., 2012; McDermott et al., 2010, 2016). It is interesting that their subjective ratings and our discrimination thresholds and d’ values lead to similar conclusions. Such similarity suggests a close relationship between the perception of dissonance for isolated tone combinations and mistuning detection in music sequences. However, the lack of relationship between beat detection and mistuning detection is inconsistent with our finding that removal of beating cues considerably worsens mistuning detection, demonstrating that beats are indeed used as a cue. This apparent discrepancy might be explained by a dissociation between the ability to detect beats (i.e., the presence of any amplitude fluctuations) and the strength of association between beats or roughness and mistuning and thereby the ability to use beats as cues for mistuning detection. Such an association may be learned and could explain why mistuning detection in a condition with reduced inharmonicity cues in our previous study was correlated with musical experience (Madsen and Oxenham, 2024). Therefore, although musical training does not improve the ability to detect beats, it may enhance the ability to use beats as a cue for mistuning detection.

As in our previous study (Madsen and Oxenham, 2024), one limitation is our use of chimeric signal processing for reducing inharmonicity cues (Smith et al., 2002). Analyses presented shown in the Supplemental Materials of that study confirmed that inharmonicity cues were indeed limited. However, we cannot entirely rule out the possibility of unintended cues that may have been introduced as artifacts by the signal processing involved in producing auditory chimeras (Smith et al., 2002). Another limitation is the range of ages tested. Although the ONH group was intended to serve as an age-matched comparison for the OHI group, the ONH participants were, on average, younger (mean age ONH = 65.9, mean age OHI = 75.2 years; t52= −5.15, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = −1.43). Additionally, the inclusion of listeners aged 50–65 years in the ONH group may have led to an underestimate of the effects of age, which may be greater at ages higher than 65. This difference might in part have explained the poorer performance of the OHI group compared to the ONH group in the first mistuning experiment. Indeed, the partial correlation between age and mistuning detection scores for −0.3 ST mistuning just failed to reach significance when controlling for musical experience and pure-tone average across 0.5, 1, and 2 kHz (r57 = −0.25, p = 0.054).

In summary, the results suggest that hearing loss, but not age, is associated with a somewhat poorer ability to detect mistuning in ongoing music. However, the balance between beats and inharmonicity cues used for mistuning detection does not appear to be affected, with evidence indicating that both cues are utilized effectively. This outcome is consistent with the lack of an association between age or hearing loss and sensitivity to inharmonicity and beats (for complex tones) found in the detection tasks. Overall, the results provide new insights into the effects of age and hearing loss on music perception, using more natural stimuli than have been typically employed in the past.
