Mapping content and dynamics in the stream of consciousness through latent brain state analysis
Authors: Yuhua Yu, Quentin Raffaelli, Delaney Freveletti, Jack J Brossart, Eric Andrews, Ramsey R Wilcox, Matthew D Grilli, Jessica R Andrews-Hanna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryHuman thoughts often arise unprompted, forming a stream of consciousness. Despite their ubiquity, it remains unknown how the brain supports unprompted thoughts as an integrated experience with rich content and dynamics. Applying latent state modeling to fMRI data collected while individuals spoke aloud their thoughts, we identified recurring brain states linked to moment-to-moment thought content. A brain state with activation in core default mode network (DMN) regions tracked early emergence of internally oriented thought, while a state involving executive control and dorsal attention networks (DANs) tracked externally oriented thought. Another state involving the medial temporal lobe and DAN engaged later during internal thought verbalization, suggesting roles in elaboration. The latter state was also associated with a large-language-model derived surprisal metric, perplexity, signaling novelty and internal event boundaries. Our findings reveal a multistage architecture of unprompted thoughts, where DMN subsystems interact with attention and control networks to scaffold their fluid progression.Subject areas: Neuroscience, Cognitive neuroscience

Introduction

Most of our thoughts arise unprompted throughout our waking life, manifesting as a “stream of consciousness.”1 Whether emerging spontaneously (as when mind-wandering), guided by automatic constraints (when ruminating), or developed deliberately (as when focused on a goal), unprompted thoughts encompass a rich structure of content and dynamics.2,3 While substantial progress has been made in characterizing the neural substrates underlying unprompted thoughts,4,5,6 their content and dynamics are often studied in isolation. Bridging this divide—by linking moment-to-moment content and dynamics with ongoing brain activity—is essential for building a comprehensive understanding of human cognition.

Previous research has primarily relied on two paradigms to study unprompted thoughts. One involves retrospective surveys in which participants are asked to retrospectively reflect on their thoughts during a task or period of rest.7,8,9 The other is online experience sampling,10,11,12,13,14 in which thoughts are probed during experimental tasks, rest, or in everyday life at discrete intervals ranging from seconds15 to days.16 While experience sampling helps to overcome mnemonic failures or biases associated with remembering thoughts that previously occurred, thought probes cannot provide the full temporal profile of unprompted thought.

Another approach that has gained less attention until recent years is the “think-aloud” paradigm (TAP), where individuals verbalize their stream of consciousness in real-time.17,18,19,20 Compared to experience sampling, the TAP captures not only the thought content but also its moment-to-moment dynamics.21,22 Growing evidence supports its validity, showing that oral reporting does not significantly alter the frequency or nature of self-generated thoughts.15,18,23,24,25 Features extracted from an individual’s think-aloud data have been linked to mental health,23,26 creativity,27 and aging.17

Although the richness of the TAP has been explored since the 80s, its applications in neuroimaging studies have been limited compared to experience-sampling methods (cf.28,29 This under-exploration is at least partially due to the methodological challenges associated with mapping real-time neural signals with unstructured verbal content. To align blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signals in fMRI studies with verbal content, prior studies typically assume a fixed delay of 4–6 s30,31 or apply a canonical hemodynamic response function32,33 that peaks around 5 s. However, the delay between the emergence of thought and its verbalization (thought-to-verbalization delay) has not been directly investigated. This delay encompasses complex processes of forming thought content and a further lag related to language production.34,35 A systematic evaluation across a range of temporal offsets would shed light on the temporal landscape of the brain-behavior association. Another major concern with the think-aloud approach in fMRI studies pertains to motion artifacts introduced by speaking in the scanner. However, recent advances in both acquisition and analysis have made it increasingly feasible to collect spoken responses during fMRI.28,31,33,36

Challenges notwithstanding, combining the TAP with fMRI offers an opportunity to extend existing knowledge of the neural basis of unprompted thoughts. Prior work has highlighted the central role of the default mode network (DMN) in internally oriented thoughts.37,38,39 Within the DMN, distinct subsystems appear to support specialized cognitive operations while working together to enable internal thoughts.40,41 Beyond the DMN, unprompted thoughts also recruit broader, large-scale networks involved in executive control,10,42,43 attention,15 salience detection, and sensory processing.8 These findings suggest that the generation and elaboration of unprompted thoughts involve dynamic interactions across distributed brain networks, each with distinct functional roles depending on the phase or content of thought.28,44,45 To capture these complex and temporally fluid processes, a holistic approach that moves beyond static ROI analyses and intermittent sampling is essential. By leveraging the continuous and content-rich nature of the TAP, we aim to disentangle the brain processes, both temporally and spatially, associated with the unfolding of unprompted thoughts.

In the present study, we used a hidden Markov model (HMM),46 a data-driven approach, to identify recurring patterns in BOLD signals as participants verbalized their stream of consciousness. Latent-state analyses have gained traction in neuroscience across imaging modalities.47,48,49 In fMRI studies, consistent patterns of brain states—characterized by activation topographies or functional connectivity profiles—have emerged across diverse research contexts and model assumptions.50,51 Notably, multiple studies have identified brain states characterized by DMN activity and associated these states with behavioral outcomes such as attentional engagement.52,53,54 Based on the above findings, we hypothesized that one or more HMM-derived brain states during think-aloud would exhibit DMN engagement. Furthermore, because the think-aloud task is mostly internally driven and generates diverse content, we expected that distinct DMN subsystems would emerge in different brain states, offering a temporal dissociation of their contribution to unprompted thoughts.

To meaningfully link the temporal dynamics of the brain states with ongoing thought, we paired the HMM-derived brain states with equally rich, time-resolved behavioral data from the think-aloud task. We used a manual rating approach to estimate moment-to-moment changes in thought orientation -- the degree to which thought content was internally oriented (pertaining to mental processes such as memories or intentions) or externally oriented (pertaining to external, environmental stimuli). We also replicated findings with an LLM-derived metric of attentional orientation.

In addition, we used LLMs to quantify the novelty of think-aloud content—an alternative dimension of thought dynamics. Specifically, we adopted a measure of perplexity, a core concept in LLM, which quantifies how surprising a segment of text is given the preceding text.55,56 Novel content may emerge when people reconstruct narratives, experience involuntary thoughts,57 or transition across internally generated event boundaries.31,58,59 Thus, we expected higher perplexity to be associated with topic transitions.21 As both the generation and encoding of novel information have been associated with the hippocampus and medial temporal regions,58,60 we hypothesized that higher perplexity would be associated with brain states involving activation in these medial temporal regions.

An overview of the current study is presented in Figure 1. Participants underwent one resting-state run and two think-aloud runs, each lasting 7 min in the scanner. We used an HMM to extract latent brain states reflecting recurring patterns of neural activity. By associating moment-to-moment brain states with behavioral metrics extracted from think-aloud content across a range of brain-behavioral lag times, we aimed to uncover how and when large-scale brain networks correspond to the unfolding of unprompted thoughts.

Overview of the experiment and analysis paradigm

Participants underwent one resting-state run and two think-aloud runs in the scanner, each lasting 7 min in the scanner. They completed a short survey in between runs. BOLD signals were fit to a hidden Markov model and subsequently decoded into a sequence of latent brain states by the model. The audio recording of the think-aloud was scored by trained raters on a continuous scale reflecting the degree to which each thought was internally or externally oriented. In parallel, an LLM was used to assess sentence-by-sentence novelty via the perplexity measure. The current study focused on the dynamic relationship between content rating, perplexity, and the latent brain states extracted from BOLD signals.

Results

Identifying latent brain states

Following quality control and preprocessing (see STAR Methods), we extracted fMRI BOLD time series from 17 cortical networks61 during rest and the think-aloud task. We fit a hidden Markov model to the concatenated time series across runs and identified eight latent brain states. Figure 2A depicts the whole-brain activity of each state, and Figure S1 displays masked z-scores of large-scale brain networks during each brain state.

Five of the states could be characterized by the activation or deactivation of distinct large-scale brain networks. When the brain was in the S1 state, it showed mostly deactivated BOLD activity across the cortex, except for some weakly positive loadings from DMN-A and DMN-C, from the Yeo et al. parcellation,61 as well as visual areas. For convenience, we labeled this state as the Passive State. When the brain was in the S2 state, it showed widespread activation, especially in all of the frontal-parietal control regions, as well as the dorsal attention network. Accordingly, we labeled it as an Act-Control State. S3 featured activation in the salience and ventral attentional networks, along with deactivation in DMN regions. It was thus labeled as SAL-VAN State. S5 featured activation in the core region of the default mode network, including DMN-A and DMN-B; therefore, we labeled this state as the Default Mode State. S7 featured activation in the DMN-C (including the parahippocampal gyrus and retrosplenial cortex), as well as the dorsal attention network, labeled as MTL-DAN State. The remaining three states, S0, S4, and S6, did not exhibit distinct activation patterns (i.e., z-scores in most voxels were around 0). Upon further examination, we observed the probability time course of these states was positively correlated with framewise displacement (Figure 2B). In other words, high motion TRs tend to occur during S0, S4, and S6, leaving the low motion TRs in the remaining states. Due to this possible motion-contamination, together with their lack of distinct activation patterns (making the state difficult to interpret), we excluded S0, S4, and S6 in the following analyses.

The state occupancy (percentage of time spent in a brain state) and dwell time (average length of time consecutively spent in a state) are depicted in Figure 2C. Across the five states we were interested in, state occupancies were similarly distributed. The distributions were all above zero, indicating that all participants visited all the states. Therefore, the results were not driven by a subset of participants, although state occupancy varied among individuals. Dwell time distribution indicated that brain states tended to be transient, with each visit lasting 6–8 s (∼6 TRs) on average across participants.

To test whether the extracted brain states critically depended on the number of states—we compared the current model (HMM8) with models of different state numbers: HMM6 (a 6-state model) and HMM10 (a 10-state model). As shown in Figure S3, each brain state from HMM8 was distinctly correlated with a brain state in another model (HMM6 and HMM8), suggesting they can be extracted with different model setups. A state in a lower-order model may correlate with more than one state in a higher-order model (e.g., S1 in HMM6 was correlated with both S7 and S3 in HMM8), suggesting that some brain states can split when the model allows more granularity. Importantly, all five brain states of interest in the current main analysis had their correspondence in HMM6 and HMM10, supporting the robustness of these recurring states.

To test how global signal regression in the processing pipeline affected the HMM results, we ran the same analysis on BOLD data that went through the same processing pipeline but without the global signal regressor (Figure S6). Stability across two think-aloud runs remained reliable for most of the states (Table S3), but the overall correlations were lower than those in the main model (see Table 1 for comparison). The correlations between the rest and the TA runs were also lower. The reduced correlation suggested that global signal regression is effective at dampening the impact of non-neural signal on the data.

Characteristics of latent brain states

In addition to the cortical BOLD activities, the brain states were further associated with subcortical activities, functional connectivity, and exhibited a hierarchical temporal structure during between-state transitions. Although these characteristics were not the focus for exploring behavioral correlates, they provided additional information for interpreting the brain states.

Brain states and subcortical activity

While subcortical data were not included in the model fit (only cortical data), we investigated subcortical activities grouped by brain states. Subcortical regions were differentially activated or deactivated within different brain states (Figure S1), suggesting their coordinated activity with the cortical regions. We were particularly interested in which brain states were coactivated with the hippocampus, given its critical role in explicit long-term memory. Three states were accompanied by hippocampal activation, with the strongest activity within the S7 (S7-MTL-DAN: mean = 0.100, p < 0.001; S1-Passive: mean = 0.064, p = 0.004; S5-Default-Mode: mean = 0.079, p = 0.004). S3 (SAL-VAN) was accompanied by hippocampal deactivation (mean = −0.173, p < 0.001). Given recent evidence implicating differential functions and cortical connectivity along the hippocampal long axis,62,63 we included hippocampal activity along the y axis (posterior to anterior) grouped by each brain state in Figure S2. Although the current fMRI dataset had limited spatial granularity within the hippocampus, we observed a pattern of more anterior, rather than posterior, hippocampal activation during S7 (MTL-DAN).

Brain states and functional connectivity

As global metrics of functional connectivity have been widely linked to human cognition and may provide insight into the functional roles of brain states,64,65 we reported two global metrics of functional connectivity, efficiency, and modularity for each brain state in Figures 2D and 2E, respectively. Modularity reflects the degree to which a network can be divided into clearly separated subnetworks, while global efficiency reflects how efficiently information is exchanged across the broader network (see STAR Methods). Among the five brain states, S5 (DMN) and S7 (MTL-DAN) had the highest modularity index (0.118 and 0.114, respectively), while S1 (Passive) and S2 (Act-Cont) had the lowest (0.090 and 0.095). S3 (SAL-VAN) and S5 (Default Mode) had the lowest efficiency score (0.232 and 0.230) while S1 (Passive) and S2 (Act-Cont) had the highest (0.239, 0.236). Mixed-effect models indicated that the global organization of the functional networks was significantly modulated by the brain states (Tables S1 and S2).

Dynamic transitions between brain states

The dynamics extracted from the HMM also allowed us to characterize the temporal relationship between brain states. The transition from one state to another was hierarchically structured. A community detection algorithm found that the five brain states can be segregated into two communities, indicating groups of brain states that are more likely to transition between each other. S1 (Passive) and S2 (Act-Cont) formed one community, while S3 (SAL-VAN), S5 (Default Mode), and S7 (MTL-DAN) formed another (Figure 2F). The temporal proximity reflected in the transition pattern was consistent with the global functional connectivity organizations characterized in Figures 2D and 2E, in that brain states within the same community also had similar modularity and efficiency scores.

Linking brain states to think aloud content

Descriptive statistics of the think aloud paradigm

Over a 7-min TA run, participants spoke a mean of 51.5 ± 21.8 sentences, with each sentence containing 13.1 ± 9.2 words. In response to the retrospective question “How similar were your thoughts to those you experience in day-to-day life?,” participants reported an average score of 0.64 ± 0.25 on a [0,1] scale, supporting the ecological validity of the think-aloud paradigm.26,27 In response to “To what extent did you censor yourself?,” participants reported an average of 0.16 ± 0.19 on a scale of 0–1, consistent with the previously reported low level of self-censorship.17

Trained raters evaluated the moment-by-moment content orientation of participants’ thoughts, yielding dynamic behavioral time courses that we then compared with brain states (see STAR Methods). Raters rated the extent to which the content was internally oriented or externally oriented on a continuous scale between 0 (external) and 1(internal). Raters reached a high agreement in the average ratings across runs with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC3) of 0.775. As we were interested in the moment-to-moment change in thought content, we also evaluated the correlation between the time courses of the ratings (from two raters) within each run. The Pearson correlation averaged 0.539 ± 0.242 across runs. The manual ratings were further validated by correlating with ChatGPT-generated ratings. The correlation increased when shifting the manual rating forward, from 0.45 at a 0-s offset to 0.55 at a 4-s offset, likely reflecting a processing delay in human raters (Figure S5). Thus, all human-generated dynamic ratings were shifted by 4 s in the following analyses to account for the raters’ processing delay in making ratings.

Brain states and thought content

To investigate the central question of whether any brain states were associated with internally or externally oriented thought, we grouped dynamic ratings by brain states. A positive (negative) rating within a brain state would indicate that participants tend to produce internally (externally) oriented thought while visiting that state. Because generating thoughts is a complex process and extends over time, we presented multiple sets of rating-state associations with brain states shifted by various offsets to provide a temporal landscape of the brain to thought content association (Figure 3A).

Visiting S5 (Default-Mode) was significantly associated with internally oriented content. The peak effect occurred at the 0-s offset, although the effect was significant in the −2 to 2-s offsets, where the p-value was estimated using a permutation test (see STAR Methods). People were more likely to visit the S7 (MTL-DAN) during a later time window relative to verbalizing internal thought, with the significant effect observed from an 8-s offset. Furthermore, visiting S2 (Act-Cont) was associated with externally oriented thought, exhibiting the same temporal pattern as S5 but in the opposite direction.

We further validated the above results using the ChatGPT-generated content ratings (see supplemental information). Although ChatGPT was not able to generate truly continuous ratings (the output took discrete values despite the instruction), the same patterns of time-resolved association were observed (Figure S6): S5 and S2 were significantly associated with internally and externally oriented content, respectively. S7 was significantly associated with internal content at a delayed time window.

To test whether the observed effects were confounded by factors such as age, individual differences, or collinearity between brain state probabilities, we fit mixed-effect models to investigate the same relationship between brain states and dynamic ratings of Internal-External orientation. We used the content rating of each sentence as the dependent variable and the average occupancies of each of the brain states during the verbalization of the sentence as independent variables. The age group and the length of the sentence (verbalization time in seconds) were included as covariates, and a random intercept was fitted to each think-aloud run. Because the continuous analysis found significant effects in two temporal windows – an early one (−2 to 2 s) and a later one (>8 s) – we fitted mixed-effect models using the offsets that had the weakest effect in each time window: 2 s for the early one and 8 s for the later one (Table 2). In the case of the 2-s offset, S5 was significantly associated with higher internal ratings. For the 8-s offset, both S5 and S7 were associated with the internal ratings.

Mixed-effect model associating state occupancy with content rating

Again, to test how global signal regression in the preprocessing pipeline affected the HMM results, we conducted a supplemental analysis on BOLD data processed without the global signal regressor. We identified state mapping between the supplemental analysis and the main model based on their similarity in activation topography (Figure S7A). The states corresponding to S5 and S7 in the main model showed a similar relationship with the internally oriented thought, although the p-values did not reach significance (Figure S7B). The states corresponding to S2 and S3 were both significantly associated with externally oriented thoughts in a time window preceding verbalization. Altogether, the supplemental model yielded a similar pattern in thought content × brain state relationship as in the main analyses, but the effect is likely weakened by noisier signals.

Estimating the delay associated with verbalization

Verbalizing a thought is a lagged behavior preceded by the neural activity that generates the thought. Therefore, the offset that maps BOLD signals to the think-aloud behavior should be shorter than the typically assumed 5 s of hemodynamic response delay due to the behavioral delay (Figure 3B inset). To assist the interpretation of the above temporal patterns, we conducted a control analysis to understand the BOLD signal offset corresponding to verbalizing a simple thought -- the sounds in the experimental room. We chose auditory words such as “loud” and “hammering” because they arise from processing sound in the MRI scanner. We aligned the BOLD activity in the primary auditory cortex, in which the neural activity giving rise to the auditory words occurred, with the verbalization of auditory words (see STAR Methods). The result indicated that auditory cortex activity peaked at 2 s offset following the verbalization of auditory words (Figure 3B).

Thus, the BOLD signal offset for a simple auditory thought was in the middle of the two temporal windows during which we observed significant associations between brain states and verbalizing internal thoughts (Figure 3A). This implied that the early effect of S5 (Default Mode) was potentially associated with the early generation phase of the internally oriented thought (−4 to 0 s) while the later effect of S7 (MTL-DAN) was a delayed response associated with verbalizing the thought (>6 s).

Brain states are associated with novel content in the think-aloud

Besides the thought orientation (internal-external), we used an LLM-generated measure, perplexity, to index the content novelty of each sentence given the previous context. There was a moderate correlation between perplexity and content orientation ratings (r = 0.32, p =0 .01), indicating that participants’ internally oriented thoughts had richer content. Importantly, perplexity indexed thought dynamics as it was directly linked to topic transitions: a sentence’s perplexity was higher if the sentence was about a different topic from the previous sentence, compared to the same topic (paired t(71) = 9.420, p <0 .001).

To determine which brain states were associated with producing novel thought content, we fit a mixed-effect model with perplexity as the dependent variable and brain state occupancies as the independent variables (Table 3). The result indicated that more time spent in S7 (MTL-DAN) was associated with more novel content in the current sentence relative to the previous context. An offset of 2 s was applied in this model, given the peak BOLD-verbalization offset in the control analysis. However, the result was robust to different choices for the offsets.

Discussion

Unprompted thoughts are shaped by two fundamentally intertwined components that give rise to the stream of consciousness: what people think and when their thoughts occur. To investigate the brain mechanisms underlying this holistic experience, the current study adopted a latent brain state approach to analyze fMRI data collected during a think-aloud task. Recurring brain states were characterized by coordinated activation and deactivation across cortical and subcortical networks. These states were associated with the temporal dynamics of thought content orientation (internal vs. external) and content novelty.

The early emergence of internal thought was associated with the Default Mode State (S5), whose probability of occurrence began to rise 2 s prior to the verbalization. S5 was marked by activation in core DMN regions (medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, inferior parietal lobule, and lateral temporal regions), concurrent with mixed activity in the frontoparietal control regions (detailed later in discussion). The coordinated activity suggests that the early conceptualization of an internal thought may rely on DMN engagement,66,67,68,69,70,71 coupled with the suppression of externally directed attention43,69,72,73 and selective involvement of executive control capacity.3,43,74 To our knowledge, only one prior study has reported a time-resolved, pre-verbal neural correlate of unprompted thought,67 in which the authors reported early peaks in fMRI signals prior to the spontaneous arising of a thought, indicated by experienced mindfulness practitioners, in brain regions that partially overlapped with those identified in the current study. Conversely, visiting S2 (Act-Cont), marked by strong activation in the dorsal attention network (intraparietal sulcus, parieto-occipital sulcus, and MT/V5) and control networks, was associated with externally oriented thoughts. This provides converging support for the involvement of DAN in externally oriented cognition.75,76

Another brain state, S7 (MTL-DAN), showed a delayed association with internal thought. This state was characterized by activation in the medial temporal region, including the hippocampus and parahippocampal cortex, as well as retrosplenial cortex and regions of the dorsal attention network (frontal eye fields, superior parietal lobule, intraparietal sulcus). The timing of the S7 effect was relatively late (peak at 8-s offset), compared to the 2-s thought-to-verbalization delay estimated in the control analysis with sound-related thoughts. This delayed pattern may reflect memory processes induced by elaborating internal thoughts. Elaboration not only requires the retrieval of past memory but may also prompt the encoding of new information generated through consolidation or imaginative simulation,77 which in turn can trigger further memory retrieval. The iterative processes of retrieval and encoding can span multiple sentences, unfolding over a longer timescale than a transient latent brain state (∼6 s). Thus, the gradual increase in S7 probability may capture the cumulative dynamics of the memory-related operations.78

Together, S5 and S7, the two DMN-related brain states, were both positively associated with internally oriented thought. Yet they exhibited distinct temporal profiles and engaged different DMN subsystems. While reinforcing the DMN’s established role in self-generated cognition, our findings highlight the complex and multistage nature of internal thought. As such thoughts evolve from pre-conscious motifs and non-verbal conceptualization, to elaborated and articulated content, different memory and attention circuits were recruited iteratively for encoding and retrieval.78 Furthermore, brain regions were differentially associated with retrieving general versus specific episodic information79,80 when people recall autobiographical memory81 or engage in episodic simulation.20 The temporally connected S5 and S7 (as reflected in the transition probability) may play a role in interleaving diverse content to form a coherent stream of consciousness. Moreover, the brain-wise activation patterns during S5 and S7 states suggest that brain circuits beyond the DMN contributed to internal thoughts.8,28,30,44 For example, subsystems of the frontoparietal control network exhibited differential activity across the two states: during S5, the so-called Cont-A network (including posterior middle temporal gyrus, inferior frontal sulcus, intraparietal sulcus) in the Yeo-17 parcellation was deactivated, while Cont-B (middle temporal gyrus, middle frontal gyrus, rostral lateral PFC) was activated. This pattern reversed during the S7 state. Thus, the temporal dissociation of S5 and S7 aligns with prior studies suggesting functional differentiation between FPCN subsystems.82,83

Additionally, the association between S7 and novelty of semantic content (perplexity) supports the idea that the hippocampus and the surrounding cortical regions facilitate the recombination of stored information into novel thought sequences.84 Perplexity measures the amount of “new information” in the current sentence given the preceding context: lower perplexity reflects more predictable, routine expressions. In the context of unprompted thoughts, higher perplexity was directly associated with topic transitions and therefore related to an “event boundary.”85,86 The link between high perplexity content and S7 is consistent with recent work that associated spatiotemporal and behavioral boundaries, whether perceived58,87,88 or internally generated,31 with transient neural activities prominently represented in the medial posterior cortex and hippocampus. In addition to discrete boundaries, hippocampal sharp wave ripples have been associated with generating more vivid, visually imaginable mental content,89 further corroborating the previous interpretation of this state (S7) on its role in developing rich, internally oriented narratives. The rich content may also take a longer time to unfold, consistent with the delayed association of S7 and internal thought. Our preliminary evidence supports the utility of perplexity for extending event boundary research to unstructured verbal outputs. Despite perplexity’s central role in next-word prediction models underlying the exponential growth of large language models, its potential in analyzing human language production remains largely untapped.

The current data-driven analysis also opens questions for future inquiries. In particular, the coactivation of the DAN and MTL within S7 during the later stage of verbalizing internal thoughts does not readily reconcile with the decoupling theory during self-generated experience.90,91 Although the DAN and DMN have traditionally been viewed as anticorrelated,92 growing evidence suggests that interactions between the DAN and MTL can vary significantly across contexts,93 and may even become positively correlated during tasks requiring the integration of social and visual processing.94,95 Densely scanned individual fMRI data have further revealed distinct coupling between regions within the MTL and the DAN.63 One possible explanation for the observed DAN-MTL coactivation is that DAN was recruited as part of the widespread cortical regions to support visually rich representational forms of thought, as participants imagined scenes.96 Recalling specific details (e.g., regarding place or object) has been shown to involve widespread activity in category-selected regions, including parts of the DAN.33,44 Finally, we cannot rule out the possibility of spatial blurring related to group averaging. The DAN is distributed across the cortex and arranged in a parallel and juxtaposed manner adjacent to the subsystems of the DMN.40,97 Recent work using precision fMRI98 found that imagining scenes activated DMN regions located near the borders of the DAN and other visual networks.

Beyond elucidating dynamic neural substrates of unprompted thoughts, the current study also contributes to the growing literature advancing latent brain state modeling as a powerful analytic approach in neuroimaging.51,52,54 Despite different task context and participant demographics, brain states identified during the TAP bear resemblance to those reported in prior studies using resting-state and task-based data, supporting the idea of transient brain states as building blocks for cognition.45,52,53 The current study shows that individuals exhibited stable tendencies in occupying certain brain states across rest and think-aloud runs, suggesting trait-like consistency. Notably, this stability cannot be attributed to region-specific BOLD activity amplitudes, as signals were z-scored within each run before the HMM analysis. Rather, it appears to arise from the whole-brain patterns of coordinated activation, deactivation, and functional connectivity. We also provided evidence that brain states dynamically modulate the global pattern of functional connectivity, indexed by efficiency and modularity score. Interestingly, brain networks showed higher modularity during S5 and S7 compared to other states. Although we did not explore their implication in the think-aloud behavior due to the absence of specific hypotheses, the reported findings can inform future research aiming to more fully characterize brain states and their functional roles.

Another methodological contribution comes from the estimation of the delay associated with verbalizing thoughts, an important consideration when combining the think-aloud paradigm with neuroimaging. Previous studies have suggested a time lag between the emergence of a thought and its entry into conscious awareness, even among experienced meditators closely monitoring their mental states.67 A decision can be decoded from brain activities seconds before it enters awareness.99,100 Although it remains challenging to disentangle the various components contributing to the observed delay – including subconscious conceptualization, elaboration, linguistic processes, as well as the hemodynamic response delay – we found peak activity in the auditory cortex lagging the verbalization of auditory words by 2 s. Incorporating the typically assumed hemodynamic response delay of 5 s, this suggested a verbalization delay of approximately 3 s between the neural activity generating a thought and the verbalization of the thought. In the TAP, the thought-to-verbalization delay may be even longer than 3 s because internally generated thoughts tend to be more abstract or elaborate than simple auditory concepts. The estimated thought-to-verbalization delay is consistent with a previous study, in which verbal recall was preceded by the selective hippocampal reactivation, based on single-neuron firing, by roughly 3 s in a free-recall task.34 The estimate also aligns with linguistic processing delay, such as a 1.5-s lag associated with morpheme-selection,35 a subcomponent of the verbalization delay.

Finally, the current study contributes to the growing body of evidence supporting the feasibility of fMRI studies that incorporate contemporaneous, overt reports, despite concerns about motion artifacts exacerbated by speaking. Studies have shown that the effect of head motion during acquisition can be mitigated during postprocessing, even without excluding high-motion scans.101 Similar to other fMRI studies involving overt reports,28,33,36 we leveraged a range of advanced denoising tools to minimize the impact of motion. These tools -- including ICA-based nuisance removal,102 3dDespike, the regression of white matter and cerebrospinal fluid signals,103,104 and global signal regression105 -- have been shown to significantly improve signal-to-noise ratios across metrics and contexts.106,107 In particular, the supplemental analysis suggested that global signal regression was effective in increasing the stability of estimates across runs (a form of test-retest reliability) in the HMM analyses. It is also worth noting that the hidden Markov model identified multiple motion-related brain states in the post-processed data. Therefore, latent brain state analyses may also serve as an additional temporal filter to isolate motion-contaminated time slices, preserving less-impacted brain states for further analyses.

An important future direction is to leverage the rich think-aloud data and explore content metrics beyond internal orientation and content novelty. Prior work has examined spontaneous thought along additional dimensions such as valence,108 self-other orientation,30 and content categories.109,110 Brain states during TAP may provide a direct link to some well-studied constructs. For example, the activated regions in S5 overlapped with neural circuits implicated in self-processing.111 Due to the complexity already involved in developing the current analytic framework – combining HMM with TAP- we limited the scope of the current study to one content dimension (internal-external) and one dynamic metric (perplexity), leaving the comprehensive characterization of thought to future research. We are only at the beginning of linking thought content and dynamics112 with individual differences in cognition,23 aging, and mental health.17,113

In sum, our findings highlight subsystems of the DMN as key substrates of internally generated thoughts, while broader cortical and subcortical networks contribute through structured, dynamic interactions. By linking temporally evolving brain dynamics with contemporaneous verbal reports, this work extends methodologies for proving the neural basis of unprompted thought, offering new avenues for studying the architecture of self-generated experience.

Limitations of the study

There are several limitations in the current study. To control in-scan motion, we asked participants to keep their hands by their side during the think-aloud so as not to gesture. This may possibly disrupt the flow of thought for some people. Although participants reported reasonable similarity between their thoughts in the scanner and their day-to-day experiences in the post-scan survey, it will be useful to quantify the effect of such restrictions on generating unprompted thoughts. Second, future studies can further improve noise reduction and potentially lower the exclusion rate, which remained relatively high despite a lenient movement criterion adopted here. Movement-restraining methods114,115,116 represent a promising avenue for reducing motion-related data loss, although minimizing the discomfort remains a challenge. Third, as part of a larger project investigating age-related change in unprompted thoughts, the current dataset contains participants with a wide age range. Because the current study focused on the main effect of brain states, the main analysis adopted mixed-effect models that included age group as a covariate, rather than explicitly investigating the age effect. Our results suggested an association between brain states and thought dynamics regardless of age group, and a supplemental analysis indicated that similar patterns can be observed from the subgroup only including young adults (Figure S8). Nevertheless, previous research has documented age-related shifts in unprompted thoughts.17,57 Future studies, particularly with a larger sample size, can illuminate how latent state and thought dynamics evolve with aging. Fourth, while recent work has found few differences in thought content, meta-awareness, and dynamics between think-aloud and silent conditions,24,117 there has been mixed evidence in prior literature regarding to what extent, and in what context, verbalization alters thoughts.21,118,119 For example, by encouraging participants to speak continuously, the TAP might reduce participants’ propensities to engage in mind blanking,120 or to have multiple thoughts simultaneously.121 Neural data acquired under TAP is also affected by language processes that may differ from those under silent conditions. Therefore, caution should be taken when generalizing the current results to other thinking conditions. Fifth, our samples were mostly White, Non-Hispanic, which may not generalize across other races/ethnicities. Sixth, despite measures to minimize self-censorship (such as clarifying that an experimenter other than the one present in the study will transcribe the recordings) and a low level of self-reported censoring, people may still avoid verbalizing certain thoughts. However, this is a similar challenge facing many other self-reported approaches for studying self-generated thoughts. Nevertheless, TAP can still provide insight into the neural substrates of spontaneously arisen thoughts, censored or not. In other words, while the possibility of self-censorship may limit the generalizability of the current findings, it is not expected to bias the observed associations.

Experimental model and study participant details

A total of 58 participants (40 female; mean age = 54.3±20.6 yrs) screened for current drug abuse and major psychiatric disease, completed the current fMRI study. Inclusion criteria were detailed in a previous behavioral publication.17 fMRI data from 12 individuals were excluded due to high movement (mean framewise displacement >.6mm). Thus, the brain state analyses were derived from 46 participants (mean age = 54.2±20.3 yrs, 31 female).

Due to inaudible think-aloud recordings from seven participants, the analyses relating to brain states and think-aloud were conducted on the final sample of 39 participants (mean age 53.8±19.9 yrs, 27 female), with 18 young adults (20-35 yrs) and 21 older adults (60-85 yrs). Altogether, participants contributed 71 think-aloud runs (i.e., 7 participants had one run missing due to data loss).

Details of the participants’ age, gender, race, ethnicity, and years of education is available in the online data repository https://osf.io/7vx2g/.

All procedures were approved by the University of Arizona’s Institutional Review Board under the protocol number 1706573229. Participants gave their informed consent at the beginning of the experiment.

Method details

Experiment procedure

Participants underwent one rest and two think-aloud runs in an fMRI scanner. Inside the scanner, they were asked to keep their fixation on a crosshair at the center of the screen and remain still, whether talking or not. Each run lasted 7 minutes. Following each run, participants completed a self-paced survey with questions like “How do you feel now?”, “How similar were your thoughts to those you experience in day-to-day life?”.

The procedure and instructions for the think-aloud task have been detailed elsewhere.17 Briefly, participants practiced speaking their thoughts aloud before the task and were then audio-recorded as they spoke aloud the contents of their conscious experience in an MRI scanner. The instruction emphasized that participants should be “as still as possible, particularly when speaking aloud,” and participants secured their arms at their side to avoid movement associated with gesturing. For the think-aloud runs, participants were instructed to “voice out loud whatever comes to your mind……. At times, your attention might be oriented outwards towards the sights and sounds of your external environment. At other times, you may be thinking about a particular topic, or your attention might be oriented towards your feelings, bodily sensations, and emotions……”. To discourage self-censorship, participants were informed that the experimenters could not hear their audio and that a different experimenter would transcribe their recording at a later time.

Processing the think-aloud audio recording and dynamic rating

Audio data from the think-aloud paradigm was manually transcribed and marked for non-fluencies and filler words.17 Because some analyses were performed at the sentence level, long sentences (>35 words) in the transcripts were broken into shorter sentences if there were natural semantic breaks. The beginning and end of each sentence were manually timestamped.

To analyze how thought content changed from moment to moment, we used a dynamic rating procedure to continuously rate the content. Raters first listened to the recording without rating. They then used PsychoPy to replay the recordings and used a sliding cursor to rate the thought content on the internal-external dimension. This dimension assessed the source of thought, expressed on a range between being generated by internally guided processes and by extrinsic, environmental stimuli. Description of the current physical environment or physical sensations is rated as entirely external (rating = 0), while mental or psychological processes are rated as entirely internal (rating = 1). Raters changed the scale continuously depending on the degree to which a thought leaned on external or internal.

The dynamic rating procedure resulted in a continuous score in the range of [0, 1] throughout the 7-minute duration of a think-aloud run. Raters were trained in multiple sessions and practiced on sample files until they reached a satisfactory understanding of the rating protocol.

Content analyses with LLM

We used LLM to extract several metrics from the transcripts. First, we used ChatGPT (gpt-3.5-turbo) to rate thought content on the internal-external dimension. This provided validation for the dynamic rating provided by human raters. We generated the prompt for ChatGPT using a similar definition as in the human rating manual: “Each sentence needs to be rated on a scale from 0 to 1 in terms of whether it was about an internal thought (rating = 1) or the current external environment (rating = 0). Examples of internal thought include episodic or semantic memory, …”. The complete prompt was provided in the online data repository.

Because it took time for raters to comprehend the auditory content and respond with a mouse movement, the potential rating delay can bias the analyses that required alignment between content ratings and brain signals. LLM-generated ratings provided accurate timing that was used to calibrate the human rating lag. To estimate the lag, we correlated the sentence-by-sentence ratings from humans and ChatGPT. The average correlation increased as we shifted the human rating forward, accounting for processing and motor response delay in human raters. The average correlation increased from .45±.25 at the 0-second lag to .55±.23 at a 4-second lag, where it reached its maximum (see supplemental information). Therefore, we shifted the dynamic ratings by human raters 4 seconds forward to synchronize the dynamic ratings to the think-aloud in all the analyses.

Despite the instruction to rate the content on a continuous scale, ChatGPT ratings mostly fell on 0, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, and 1. Therefore, we used ChatGPT only for validation rather than as the main target for content analysis.

Next, we used LLM to generate the perplexity score which quantifies the amount of novel information in a text segment. Perplexity is inversely related to the likelihood of generating a particular sequence of text X = (x0, x1, …, xt) following the previous text. It is defined as the exponentiated average negative log-likelihood of a tokenized sequence exp⁡{−1t∑i=1tlogp(xi|x<i), where p(xi|x<i) is the likelihood of the ith token (word) conditioned on the previous tokens. The length of the context was set to 90 tokens to avoid exceeding the context limit of the model. We used a pre-trained LLM (gpt2-medium) to compute the perplexity.

Finally, we identified topic transitions between sentences. Following a previous publication,21 we used SentenceTransformer (all-mpnet-base-v2) to compute the sentence-by-sentence embedding vectors. Then we applied Agglomerative Clustering to find clusters among sentences (topics). A topic transition was marked if the topic (cluster) of the current sentence was different from the topic of the previous sentence.

fMRI data acquisition and preprocessing

Participants were scanned with a 3 T Siemens Skyra scanner with a 32-channel head coil. The anatomical image protocol utilized a 3D MPRAGE sequence with the following key parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2.3 s, echo time (TE) = 2.95 ms, inversion time = 0.9 s, flip angle = 9°, and slice thickness = 1 mm.

Functional images were acquired using a T2∗-weighted echo-planar imaging sequence with TR = 1.3 s, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 68°, and multiband acceleration factor = 2. The acquisition matrix was 84 × 84 with resulting isotropic voxels of 3 mm. Each run lasted 7 minutes or 323 volumes.

Imaging data was preprocessed using fMRIPrep 24.1.1.122 The T1w image was corrected for intensity non-uniformity with N4BiasFieldCorrection and used as T1w-reference throughout the workflow. fsl_fast was used for brain tissue segmentation of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), white-matter (WM), and gray matter (GM). antsRegistration (ANTs 2.5.3) was used for volume-based spatial normalization to the standard space, MNI152NLin6Asym.

For the functional data, head-motion parameters with respect to the BOLD reference were estimated first using mcflirt (FSL). The estimated fieldmap was then aligned with rigid registration to the target EPI reference run. The BOLD reference was then co-registered to the T1w reference and was configured with six degrees of freedom. Framewise displacement (absolute sum of relative motions) was computed to quantify the amount of motion within a run. Additionally, anatomical (aCompCor) PCA components were calculated separately within the WM and CSF masks. Further, ICA-AROMA classification125 was performed on the preprocessed BOLD series on MNI152NLin6Asym space using the fMRIPost-AROMA pipeline (non-aggressive).

Finally, the eXtensible Connectivity Pipeline-DCAN (XCP-D)123 was used to post-process the outputs of fMRIPrep. For each run, the first ten volumes of the BOLD data were discarded as non-steady-state volumes. The BOLD data were despiked with AFNI’s 3dDespike. Nuisance regressors included the top 5 aCompCor principal components from the white matter and cerebrospinal fluid compartments, the AROMA-labeled “noise” components signal103,107 and the global signal. The BOLD data and confounds were band-pass filtered to retain signals between 0.01-0.08 Hz. The resulting time series were then denoised using linear regression. The denoised BOLD was smoothed using Nilearn124 with a Gaussian kernel (FWHM=6 mm). While the global signal regression has been shown to be effective for denoising, there has been concern that it may distort functional connectivity and remove relevant signals. To test its impact in the current study, we performed the same HMM analyses (as detailed below) with the above preprocessing pipeline, but excluding the global signal regressor.

Hidden Markov model

Although prior work has fit HMM to BOLD signals either with or without subcortical data, we only included cortical data in model fitting to ensure that results were not affected by often smaller and potentially noisier subcortical signals. Denoised functional data were parcellated into 17 functional networks using the Yeo 17 atlas.61 For each network, BOLD series were extracted using Nilearn with standardization (z-scoring). The BOLD signals were concatenated across subjects and runs. A Hidden Markov Model (HMM) with 8 states was fitted to the concatenated time series using the hmmlearn library. The HMM was initialized using k-means clustering (scikit-learn) with cluster centers serving as initial state means53 and a global covariance matrix computed from the data. The HMM was trained using the Baum-Welch algorithm with full covariance matrices and 2000 maximum iterations. The above procedure was repeated 20 times with different random seeds, and the fit with the highest score (log-likelihood) was selected. We then inferred the state sequence and probabilities from the best-fit model. Each time slice (TR) was represented by the posterior probabilities of the estimated states, of which the one with the highest probability was assigned as the brain state at the current time slice.

We chose a model with eight brain states as it was in the middle of the range (4 – 12 brain states) reported in previous relevant studies that adopted HMM.45,51,52,53,54 Given the lack of a benchmark metric that guides the optimal choice of the meta-parameter (number of states) in HMM, choosing 8 states provided a tradeoff between granularity and interpretability. To test the robustness and potential dependence on the meta-parameter, we fitted models with 6 (HMM6) and 10 (HMM10) states, respectively. If a brain state from one model corresponds to a brain state in another model, then the time series of posterior probabilities of these two brain states in the same run should be highly correlated.54 Therefore, we computed the correlation between each state probability time series from one model (HMM6 and HMM10) to each state probability from another model (HMM8).

To analyze the extracted brain states, we first reported the global activation topography for each brain state. The BOLD signal of each voxel was averaged across all TRs assigned to the same brain state. To assess how large-scale functional networks (Yeo17) were represented in each brain state, we averaged the signals within each network and compared the mean with the null distribution obtained by random shuffling of the run labels 2000 times so that the temporal structure was preserved. Similarly, we investigated the subcortical activity accompanying each brain state and constructed a null distribution with 2000 permutations of run labels. We used the Harvard-Oxford parcellation (nilearn, sub-maxprob-thr50-1mm) and extracted 10 subcortical regions, combining the left and right hemispheres.

We further characterized the brain states by their dwell time and temporal connectivity. Dwell time measures how transient a brain state is. It is computed by the number of seconds the brain stays in a state before transitioning into a different one. Temporal connectivity is the probability of transitioning into state j in the next time step from the current state i. It is represented as a transition probability matrix. To investigate whether a community structure exists among the brain states, we applied the Louvain community detection algorithm126 to the transition probability matrix. If multiple communities are detected, it indicates that a more preferential transition between states within the same community over states across communities.

To characterize how the global organization of the functional connectivity differed across brain states, we computed efficiency and modularity scores for each brain state. The modularity quantifies the degree to which the network may be subdivided into such clearly delineated groups, while the global efficiency is the average inverse shortest path length in the network. We extracted BOLD signals with the Schaefer400 parcellation, grouped the TR by assigned brain states, and computed the correlation matrix for each state. We then computed the modularity (modularity.modularity_und) in the Brain Connectivity Toolbox and efficiency (efficiency_wei) on the binarized correlation matrix and the Yeo-17 network assignment.

Control analysis for estimating verbalization delay

To estimate the possible delay between forming a thought or concept in the brain and verbalizing it, we conducted a control analysis using the well-defined auditory cortex, as it is directly related to thoughts about auditory words arising from the experiment environment – the scanner. These words were noise, sound, jackhammer, hammering, and loud. We extracted the timestamps when these words were verbalized using an extended Whisper package (stable-ts 2.0.0).

To extract BOLD signals from the primary auditory cortex (PAC), we used the Schaefer400 atlas and average voxels labeled as LH_SomMotB_Aud_2 (coordinates = (-56, -21,8)) and RH_SomMotB_Aud_2 (coordinates = (54, -14,6)). We then time-locked the PAC signal to each instance of the auditory word. To avoid overlapping time windows, we excluded any two auditory words that were verbalized within 12 seconds. We then averaged PAC signals in all TRs around the auditory words, from 8 seconds prior to 10 seconds after. We computed the mean and standard error of the signals in 2-second buckets.

Quantification and statistical analysis

To associate the dynamic content rating with brain states, we conducted two analyses—one continuous and one discrete—with different statistical formulations to ensure the robustness of the findings. Data from 39 participants (n=39) were included in the analyses. The first 60 seconds were discarded in both analyses to allow participants to get used to verbalizing their thoughts in the scanner (see Figure S9 for the steep drift in thought content at the beginning of a run). Despite the practice, participants had more difficulty thinking aloud at the start. For the continuous analysis, we first normalized the dynamic ratings within a run, downsampled them to match the BOLD signal TRs, and concatenated them across all runs. Then, we computed the average ratings grouped by brain states. The results were plotted in Figure 3A. To assess the significance of the effect, we permuted the run labels 2,000 times and recomputed the conditional means, thereby preserving the temporal structure of both the brain states and dynamic ratings. To investigate the temporal relationship between the brain states and content, the above procedure was repeated for each temporal offset, where the brain state time courses were shifted from -4 seconds (lead) to +8 seconds (lag). The significance threshold was set to p < 0.05.

For the discrete analyses, we estimated linear mixed-effect models at the sentence level. This allowed us to better account for potential confounding variables such as individual differences and collinearity within brain states. We used the internal rating of each sentence (dynamic rating sampled at the end of the sentence) as the dependent variable. Given that long sentences with multiple topics were broken down during transcribing (see above), the rating at the end of each (relatively short) sentence represented the current content comprehensively. We used the average occupancies of each of the five brain states during the sentence utterance as independent variables. The age group and the duration of the sentence (in seconds) were included as covariates. A random intercept was fitted to each think-aloud run. We shifted the brain state time course early by 2 seconds and 8 seconds in two separate model fits, corresponding to the temporal windows identified in the continuous analysis.
