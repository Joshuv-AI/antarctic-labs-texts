Leonardo da Vinci’s depiction of the liver
Authors: Thomas M van Gulik
Published: 2025

The iconic anatomical atlas of Andreas Vesalius, published in 1543, was celebrated as the first systematic study of human anatomy based on dissections of human corpses. What was unknown at the time was that Leonardo da Vinci [1452–1519] had already made strikingly accurate drawings of the human body decades earlier (1).

One of the most remarkable stories comes from an autopsy that Da Vinci carried out in Florence, Italy, on the body of a 100-year-old man, hoping to uncover in his organs the secret of “long life” (2). Although the man reportedly had no health complaints, Da Vinci discovered during the examination, a badly damaged liver with the features that we now recognize as cirrhosis and signs of portal hypertension. Da Vinci sketched the liver and its blood vessels with extraordinary precision (Figure 1). Even though he made these drawings as early as 1507, long before Vesalius’s famous atlas, they remained hidden for more than three centuries after his death. In fact, his notes contain the very first description of cirrhosis of the liver and its vascular consequences.

Leonardo da Vinci’s drawings of the liver and surrounding blood vessels, made in 1507 during the autopsy of a recently deceased 100-year-old man. Left: the bilobed liver with gallbladder and bile duct leading to the (sectioned) duodenum. The portal vein is shown alongside the hepatic artery as a continuation of the splenic vein (incorrectly without connection to the superior mesenteric vein). Right: the hepatic veins draining into the vena cava. Leonardo wrote left-handed and in mirror script (3).

On the left side of the drawing (Figure 1) we see the liver with its blood vessels, gallbladder, and bile duct leading to the (sectioned) duodenum. The portal vein is depicted alongside the hepatic artery, extending from the splenic vein (though he mistakenly left out its connection with the superior mesenteric vein). On the right he drew the hepatic veins (including accessory veins) flowing into the vena cava below the right atrium—all rendered with a level of precision unprecedented for the time. In his notes, Da Vinci described the thickened vessel walls and convolutions of the portal vein. The liver itself appeared “dried out” and brittle, unlike what he had observed in previous dissections. Without knowing the disease, Leonardo gave the very first description of cirrhosis and portal hypertension.

Leonardo da Vinci, the universal genius of the Renaissance, is remembered above all as a painter and inventor of diverse machines. Less well known is his fascination with anatomy. By dissecting both humans and animals, he hoped to better understand the body—so as to capture it more truthfully in his art. As early as 1489, he sawed open a human skull and drew the inside with astonishing detail. Da Vinci, who wrote left-handed and in mirror script, left behind many notebooks filled with anatomical sketches. These reveal that his interest was not limited to the morphology of structures; he also sought to understand their function (2).

Da Vinci had planned to publish an anatomical atlas based on his drawings and notes, but for many reasons this never happened. After his death in 1519, the sheets ended up in different collections and were only rediscovered centuries later. In 1773, they surfaced in the library of Windsor Castle in England, and in 1898 they were finally published in a facsimile edition (4). Had his contributions not remained unnoticed for centuries, Leonardo da Vinci would be remembered today not only as a great artist and inventor, but also as the true anatomical reformer of the Renaissance.
