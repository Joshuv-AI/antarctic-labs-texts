Methotrexate-Induced Accelerated Nodulosis in a Patient with Systemic Lupus Erythematosus
Authors: Hyeong Mok Kwon, Eun Hye Jeong, Jung Eun Yim, Hye Ri Kim, Dong Hoon Shin, Jong Soo Choi, Young Kyung Bae
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractMethotrexate (MTX)-induced accelerated nodulosis (MIAN) reportedly occurs in patients with rheumatic arthritis receiving MTX therapy. However, it has also been reported in patients with other autoinflammatory conditions, such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). A 38-year-old woman diagnosed with SLE presented with multiple movable, firm, flesh-colored nodules on both hands that had developed 3 years ago. She was taking oral medications, specifically hydroxychloroquine, azathioprine, and MTX. Histopathological examination revealed palisaded granulomatous inflammation, surrounded by histiocytes and lymphocytes, along the dermis to the subcutaneous fat layer. Fibrinoid degeneration was observed at the center of the granulomatous inflammation, and dermal mucin deposition was not observed. The patient was diagnosed with MIAN, and therefore discontinuation of MTX was recommended. Subsequently, the lesions almost completely disappeared with no signs of recurrence. MIAN exhibits clinicopathological features similar to those of rheumatoid nodules; therefore, it can be easily misdiagnosed. Herein, we report a case of MIAN in a patient with SLE to contribute to the accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.Keywords: Methotrexate, Rheumatoid nodule

INTRODUCTION

Methotrexate (MTX) has been commonly used to treat rheumatic diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA). Among its various systemic side effects is a rare but specific cutaneous manifestation known as MTX-induced accelerated nodulosis (MIAN)1. It has reportedly occurred among patients with RA patients MTX therapy. However, it has rarely been reported in patients with other autoinflammatory conditions, such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)2. Additionally, it is often confused with rheumatoid nodules, which occur in 30% to 40% of patients with RA. It indicates higher disease severity and activity of RA3.

CASE REPORT

This case involved a 38-year-old woman, presenting with multiple movable, firm, flesh-colored nodules on both hands, that had developed 3 years ago (Fig. 1). The patient denied any pruritus, pain, or tenderness around the lesion. Her rheumatoid factor level was within the normal range. The patient was diagnosed with SLE, and she received oral medications, specifically hydroxychloroquine, azathioprine, and MTX, at the rheumatology clinic. Two months after taking oral medications, nodules developed on her hands, particularly on the palmar aspect of her fingers. The total cumulative MTX dose was 80 mg just before nodules developed. She was diagnosed with granuloma annulare based on a skin biopsy performed at her previous dermatology clinic. Since after the diagnosis, she periodically received triamcinolone acetonide for her lesions which immediately decreased in size. After a few days, the lesions returned to their original size, so she was referred to our hospital. A skin biopsy was performed on one of her skin lesions.

Multiple flesh-colored, firm, movable nodules on both hands.

Histopathological examination revealed an essentially normal epidermis, and palisaded granulomatous inflammation, surrounded by histiocytes and lymphocytes, along the dermis to the subcutaneous fat layer. Extensive fibrinoid degeneration was also observed at the center of the granulomatous inflammation. Dermal interstitial mucin was not observed in the Alcian blue stain (Fig. 2). Based on these findings, the patient was suspected of having MIAN. MTX discontinuation was recommended, and intralesional injections of triamcinolone acetonide were administered concurrently. The total cumulative MTX dose was 1,272.5 mg just before she stopped MTX. Within a month after the medication had been discontinued, the lesions almost completely disappeared, further confirming the original diagnosis. The patient is currently under follow-up for 4 months with no signs of recurrence.

DISCUSSION

The estimated incidence of MIAN among patients with RA receiving MTX is 8%3. Berkun et al.4 reported that the overall rate of MIAN among patients with MTX-treated RA was 11.6%, but its prevalence varied between 8% and 22% in different series. However, MIAN has rarely been reported in other autoinflammatory conditions, such as SLE. Thus, its estimated incidence remains unknown2.

Among patients with MIAN, 64% present with nodules in the fingers. Other involved sites include the elbows, knees, and feet. The duration from initial MTX administration to the onset of nodules reportedly varies between 3 and 144 months, and the cumulative dose of MTX was 60 to 7,200 mg5.

Since the histopathologic features of MIAN and rheumatoid nodules are similar, clinical information helps achieve a correct diagnosis. Compared to rheumatoid nodules, MIAN presents as smaller nodules, which reportedly grow and develop more rapidly. In addition, rheumatoid nodules have a predilection for the articular area of the digits, while MIAN typically affects the hands, feet, and ears3. Rheumatoid nodules usually develop on the extensor surface and at sites of pressure or repetitive trauma, especially the olecranon and dorsal aspect of the hand. However, it has been reported that the finger is the most common site among various areas where MIAN can occur, but there is no obvious report on whether the dorsal or palmar side of the hand occurs more frequently and additional study will be needed6. Clinically, chronic gouty tophi, nodular scleroderma, or renal osteodystrophy in chronic kidney disease may show similar nodular lesions in the joint areas, but based on the patient’s medical history and histopathologic examination, it can be excluded.

MIAN is reportedly caused by the stimulation of the adenosine A1 receptor on macrophages. Its binding to the adenosine A2 receptors increases extracellular adenosine concentrations, resulting in anti-inflammatory effects. However, the exact pathogenic mechanism for the development of MIAN is unknown3,4. In genetic studies, the HLA-DRB1*0401 gene has been reported to be associated with MIAN, and this HLA allele has been reported in another series to be associated with a risk of developing more aggressive RA, particularly the risk for extraarticular disease manifestations. Further studies are needed to determine how this particular HLA allele might further promote such a process, but this may partly explain why patients with psoriasis or atopic dermatitis who were treated with MTX rarely exhibit MIAN7,8,9.

The management of MIAN is the withdrawal of the causative drug. In patients who cannot discontinue MTX therapy or whose nodulosis fails to regress even with MTX discontinuation, additional antirheumatic drugs, such as hydroxychloroquine, colchicine, sulfasalazine, azathioprine, or D-penicillamine, may be given5.

Although it has been reported to occur in patients with RA, MIAN has rarely been reported in other autoinflammatory conditions, such as SLE2. In addition, MIAN exhibits clinicopathological features similar to those of rheumatoid nodules, so it can be easily misdiagnosed. Hence, physicians should be informed regarding this entity to accurately diagnose and appropriately manage it.
