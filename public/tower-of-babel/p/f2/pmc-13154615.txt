National Trends and In-Hospital Outcomes of Valve-in-Valve Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement With Cerebral Embolic Protection
Authors: Ammar A Hasnie, Avneet Hans, Emad A Chishti, Joseph Leith, Hannah Murphy, Alyssa H Harris, Kory Heier, Kristen McQuerry, Steve W Leung, Islam Y Elgendy
Published: 2026

Periprocedural stroke remains a significant complication during transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR), primarily due to embolization of dislodged debris from the native valve or vasculature, and is linked to considerable morbidity and mortality. Cerebral embolic protection devices (CEPDs) were introduced to mitigate the risk of periprocedural stroke, but pivotal randomized controlled trials (RCTs) did not demonstrate a reduction in stroke or mortality with routine use.1,2 Patients undergoing valve-in-valve (ViV) TAVR may face greater risk of embolism because of degenerated bioprosthetic valves.3 However, data on CEPD use in this higher-risk population remains limited because they were excluded from the pivotal RCTs. Therefore, we sought to evaluate the contemporary trends and outcomes of ViV-TAVR with and without CEPD using a large national database.

This observational study used the Vizient Clinical Data Base, which contains nationwide patient-level discharge data that include demographic, clinical, cost, and readmission data from >1350 US academic medical centers and affiliated hospitals. We identified patients who underwent ViV-TAVR between January 2020 and March 2025.4 Patients were categorized to CEPD (ICD-10-PCS procedure code X2A5312) vs none.5 We evaluated the trends of CEPD use during the study period using the χ2 testing. The in-hospital outcomes included stroke, mortality, cardiac arrest, acute kidney injury, length of stay (LOS), and 30-day readmission for any hospitalization within Vizient-participating institutions. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to assess associations between CEPD use and clinical outcomes, adjusting for patient- and hospital-related characteristics. Covariates selected for the multivariable logistic regression included age; sex; procedure year; comorbidities including prior stroke, atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, peripheral arterial disease, and coronary artery disease; and hospital characteristics (bed size, teaching status, and region). For LOS, the natural log was modeled using multivariate linear regression to assess the association with CEPD use, adjusting for the same characteristics as the logistic models. The University of Kentucky Institutional Review Board deemed this study exempt as not human subject research. Data from the Vizient Clinical Data Base was used with the permission of Vizient (all rights reserved).

Among 3728 patients undergoing ViV-TAVR during the study period (median age, 78 years; IQR, 73-84 years; 44% women), 522 (14%) received a CEPD. The baseline demographics were largely similar, although patients with CEPDs had higher rates of heart failure (84.3% vs 77.8%) but lower rates of chronic kidney disease (32.2% vs 37.2%). There was no difference in the prevalence of prior stroke (16.7% vs 15.9%). CEPD was more likely used at large (55% vs 26%; P < .001) and teaching hospitals (71.6% vs 57.1%; P < .001). CEPD utilization peaked until 2021 but declined thereafter (P < .0001) (Figure 1A).

There was no difference in the incidence of stroke (1.0% vs 1.7%; adjusted odds ratio [OR], 0.57; 95% CI, 0.20-1.39), in-hospital mortality (1.5% vs 1.5%; adjusted OR, 1.33; 95% CI, 0.56-2.77), cardiac arrest (0.8% vs 0.9%; adjusted OR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.25-2.29), acute kidney injury (10.5% vs 14.7%; adjusted OR, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.53-1.00), and 30-day readmission rates (5.7% vs 8.9%; adjusted OR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.46-1.02) (Figure 1B). CEPD use was associated with shorter LOS (2.43 vs 2.80 days; P = .003).

In this large contemporary observational analysis of patients who underwent ViV-TAVR, CEPD use among selected patients was not associated with a lower incidence of in-hospital stroke or mortality. This aligns with findings from RCTs for native-valve TAVR. Our study extended the knowledge by showing a lack of stroke or mortality reduction with CEPD in this higher-risk ViV population, where embolic burden might be expected to be greater. However, given the low event rates in our analysis, small reduction in stroke risk with CEPD could not be fully excluded.

We also found that CEPD use declined in ViV-TAVR after 2021, corresponding to the reduction in CEPD use nationally for native-valve TAVR since the publication of the PROTECTED TAVR trial.5 This temporal association could also be explained by a reduction in reimbursement, institutional preference, and operator behavior.

Our findings should be interpreted in the context of certain limitations. The observational nature of the study introduces potential for unmeasured confounding despite adjustment. The administrative database is subject to coding errors. The event rates for stroke and mortality were low with wide CIs, limiting statistical power to detect small differences. The device type including manufacturer and whether it was a commercially available vs investigational could not be identified in the Vizient database. Additionally, the dataset does not include procedural data, such as valve size, implantations depth, or fluoroscopic time, and does not capture long-term outcomes or subclinical neurologic end points.

As seen in past studies that failed to show benefit from routine CEPD, this study also failed to show any benefits in CEPD use in the ViV patient population.1,2 However, given the small number of events and wide CI, larger studies are necessary to confirm these findings.
