Knotless Suture‐Bridge Technique for All‐Arthroscopic Repair of Proximal Knee Medial Collateral Ligament Injuries
Authors: Yang Chen, Kehan Li, Haijun Zhang, Bo Zhang, Pengzhen Gu, Qiaojie Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article describes an all‐arthroscopic knotless suture‐bridge technique for medial collateral ligament repair. Its key steps involve placing a medial row anchor and a lateral row anchor, connected via sutures passed through the torn ligament, thereby applying the suture‐bridge technique to achieve secure knotless fixation. Compared with conventional open knotted repair, this method offers a fully arthroscopic approach that avoids extensive dissection and eliminates knot‐related complications. The technique effectively restores knee joint stability, providing a minimally invasive surgical option for medial collateral ligament injuries.

The medial collateral ligament (MCL) is crucial for knee stability. 1 , 2 Although conservative treatment is common for MCL tears, some patients, particularly active individuals with grade III injuries, may experience residual instability, necessitating surgical intervention. 3 , 4 The surgical treatment of MCL injuries remains a significant challenge. 5 Traditional open knotted repair requires large incisions, causes significant soft tissue trauma, and prolongs recovery. Even arthroscopic techniques often rely on knotted sutures, which can cause soft tissue irritation or knot‐related complications such as prominence, impingement, or loosening. Improper anchor placement may also lead to inadequate tension and poor long‐term stability. 6 , 7 Therefore, a need exists for a more minimally invasive and stable fixation method.

The suture‐bridge technique, widely used in arthroscopic rotator cuff repair, offers a potential solution. 8 , 9 We have adapted this technique for MCL repair. The key innovation involves using a “medial row anchor proximally and lateral row anchor distally” configuation within the correct subfascial plane to achieve knotless, anatomical fixation under full arthroscopic visualization, eliminating the need for open exposure. This approach offers substantial benefits for patients with MCL injuries—particularly athletes and active individuals seeking a rapid return to physical activity—and provides a therapeutic perspective for sports medicine surgeons (Figures 1 and 2).

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Indications

Patients with complete MCL tears at the femoral attachment require surgery. 10

Preoperative Evaluation

Standard preoperative workup includes laboratory tests, electrocardiography, lower extremity ultrasonography, and knee magnetic resonance imaging. A positive valgus stress test on physical examination confirms the diagnosis. Surgical contraindications must be excluded. 11 , 12 , 13

Surgical Procedure

1. The patient is positioned supine under anesthesia. A thigh tourniquet is applied, and the tourniquet time during surgery does not exceed 90 minutes to avoid limb ischemic injury. Standard arthroscopic portals are established along the anteromedial and anterolateral aspects of the knee joint, and a 30° arthroscope is inserted to sequentially inspect the patellofemoral joint, medial compartment, lateral compartment, and intercondylar fossa (Video 1). A comprehensive evaluation of the menisci, cruciate ligaments, articular cartilage, and other structures is performed. If any concomitant intra‐articular pathologies such as meniscal tears, cartilage lesions, or loose bodies are identified, they are managed concurrently with meniscal suture/partial meniscectomy, chondroplasty, loose body removal, or other appropriate techniques.

2. The medial joint space and joint line are confirmed. Under arthroscopic guidance, a trocar is used to mark the corresponding skin level of the knee joint line. A small incision is made at an appropriate site as the viewing portal; a distinct “pop” is felt when the trocar penetrates the fascia, and attention is paid to avoiding injury to subcutaneous blood vessels and saphenous nerve branches at this time. The arthroscope is then placed within the subfascial plane, the angle of the scope is adjusted to initially explore the subfascial anatomical layers, and the scope is fixed after confirming no interference from important blood vessels or nerves.

3. A working portal is created next. Systematic debridement of the subfascial plane is performed along the subfascial space, removing locally proliferated fibrous tissue, scar tissue, and a small amount of adipose tissue; the shaving head is kept facing the tissue side to avoid damaging surrounding normal ligaments and soft tissues. For small vascular bleeding points encountered during surgery, precise hemostasis is achieved using a radiofrequency ablation device. Arthroscopic exploration reveals the torn MCL within the deep subfascial plane, characterized by disrupted continuity of ligament fibers, rough stumps, and irregular alignment; avulsion of the ligament insertion site bone is observed in some cases. The bone bed surrounding the femoral attachment of the torn MCL is freshened, creating a freshened bone bed to promote healing between the ligament and bone tissue (Figure 3).

4. Under arthroscopic visualization, 1 medial row anchor (STAR Sports Medicine, Beijing, China) is implanted at the proximal end of the MCL tear (typically 1‐2 cm above the native femoral insertion site of the MCL on the adductor tubercle). A self‐designed “banana suture passer” (Figure 4)—a curved injector with polydioxanone sutures—is percutaneously passed through the MCL tissue near the joint line, avoiding the saphenous nerve and great saphenous vein, and precisely inserted into the substance of the MCL near the joint line at the site of the tear to ensure the puncture needle completely penetrates the ligament tissue and prevents suture slippage. The polydioxanone sutures are grasped under arthroscopic guidance to direct the anchor sutures through the torn MCL tissue to ensure the sutures run smoothly within the ligament without torsion or jamming (Figure 5).

5. Four suture strands are retrieved proximally using a suture grasper, and attention is paid to maintaining uniform tension on the sutures to avoid secondary injury to the ligament caused by excessive traction on a single suture. The intended insertion site for the lateral row anchor (STAR Sports Medicine, Beijing, China) is preoperatively planned. Under direct arthroscopic visualization, the suture tension is adjusted to closely approximate the torn ends of the MCL, while maintaining the knee joint in a varus and flexed position to avoid excessive tension or laxity of the ligament. The lateral row anchor is then slowly compressed to ensure tight adherence to the bone surface, completing the suture‐bridge procedure. Through tension transmission of the sutures, suture plication and compression fixation of the torn tissue at the MCL insertion site are achieved, promoting healing of the ligament insertion (Figure 6).

Postoperative Management

Postoperative protocol includes intravenous antibiotics, symptomatic management, and bracing. Passive rehabilitation begins immediately under guidance, transitioning to active exercises at 4 weeks. Patients are monitored for pain, incision healing, and neurological status, with regular follow‐up to assess recovery and guide gradual return to activity.

Clinical Outcomes

Physical examination reveals a negative valgus stress test, indicating restored medial stability. Postoperative magnetic resonance imaging confirms the continuity and uniform signal of the repaired MCL, consistent with healing.

DISCUSSION

MCL injury is a common clinical condition in sports medicine and orthopaedics, primarily caused by high‐impact sports, traumatic collisions, or chronic overuse. 11 , 14 Postinjury, inadequate healing often leads to loss of joint stability, chronic pain, and abnormal motion, 15 increasing the risk of secondary injuries and osteoarthritis. 16 , 17 The shift toward minimally invasive surgery addresses the drawbacks of open techniques. Our application of the knotless suture‐bridge technique achieves stable fixation through precise subfascial plane surgery. 18 It minimizes soft tissue dissection, reduces postoperative pain, and may shorten rehabilitation. Knotless fixation avoids knot‐related problems, 19 , 20 while the bridge configuration distributes tension evenly, potentially improving pull‐out strength compared with point fixation. Current indications are limited to acute, transverse femoral‐side MCL tears. It is not suited for tibial‐sided tears, longitudinal tears, chronic cases, or infections. Further instrument development and long‐term outcome studies are needed. In conclusion, this all‐arthroscopic knotless suture‐bridge technique provides a minimally invasive, stable fixation option for select MCL injuries, with significant clinical potential.
