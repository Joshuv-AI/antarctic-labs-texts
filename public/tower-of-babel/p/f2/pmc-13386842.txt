Effect of preoperative oral montelukast prescription on postoperative pain levels following zygomaticomaxillary complex fracture surgery: a randomized control trial
Authors: Farrokh Farhadi, Saeed Nezafati, Mohsen Hashemi, Marjan Dehdilani, Mojtaba Sheykhian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimThe negative impact of postoperative pain on various aspects of patients’ lives is evident, leading to the inability to perform daily activities and the inability to quickly return to normal function. The use of medications that can reduce pain and accelerate the healing process for patients. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to determine the effect of preoperative administration of oral montelukast on pain following surgery for zygomaticomaxillary complex fractures.MethodsThe present study was a triple-blind, randomized, controlled trial (Trial registration number: IR.TBZMED.DENTISTRY.REC.1403.043) that investigated 80 patients undergoing surgery for complex zygomaticomaxillary fractures who were randomly divided into two intervention and control groups. For each subgroup, 10 volunteers were used in each test (in each test, the drug montelukast was administered at different times: 0, 6, 12, and 24 h before the zygomaticomaxillary complex fracture surgery). The duration of surgery was also examined as a variable in this study and its effect on pain intensity was also examined. The collected data were analyzed using SPSS software. Different time intervals of drug administration before surgery, 6, 12 and 24 h and the effect of time of administration on pain intensity, the effect of surgery duration on pain intensity in the study groups were measured. Pain intensity was scored from 0 to 10 based on visual analog scale and verbal confession of the patient.ResultsBased on the results of an independent t-test, the mean pain score in patients receiving montelukast 6 h before surgery was significantly reduced compared to the control group at one, three, six, twelve, eighteen, and 24 h after surgery (p < 0.05). In the intervention group that received montelukast 24 h before surgery, the lowest mean pain scores were observed one hour after surgery and the highest mean pain scores were observed eighteen hours after surgery in the intervention group (p < 0.05). Based on the results of an independent t-test, the mean pain score in patients receiving montelukast before surgery was significantly reduced compared to the control group at three, six, twelve, eighteen, and 24 h (p < 0.05).ConclusionPreoperative administration of montelukast significantly reduces postoperative pain following zygomaticomaxillary complex fracture surgery, with maximal efficacy when given six hours before surgery.

Introduction

The zygomaticomaxillary complex (ZMC), a fundamental component of facial structure, protects the contents of the eye socket and contributes significantly to the midface shape [1]. ZMC fractures are among the most common maxillofacial injuries [2]. Most ZMC fractures result from violent assaults, road traffic accidents, falls, and sports-related injuries [3]. ZMC fracture can lead to various problems and pain. Treatment for a ZMC fracture usually involves surgery and specialized care. The main goal of surgery is to reconstruct and stabilize the zygomatic bones and return the face to its normal position. The surgeon uses screws, plates, and pins to stabilize the broken bones [4, 5]. Fear of uncontrollable pain after surgery is one of the main concerns of patients. Effective control of postoperative pain is an important factor in reducing mortality and promoting early mobilization and discharge from the hospital [6]. Therefore, there is a need to introduce new agents for postoperative pain management that also have fewer side effects.

Montelukast is a CysLT receptor antagonist used as an anti-inflammatory and anti-asthma drug. Given the anti-inflammatory properties of montelukast and its effect on reducing postoperative pain, it may be important to consider preoperative administration of oral montelukast in patients undergoing ZMC fracture surgery. After surgery, patients may experience severe pain and post-surgical complications, which can affect their quality of life and recovery process [7]. Studies have shown that administering oral montelukast before ZMC fracture surgery may help reduce postoperative pain and improve these complications [8]. Ansari et al. [9] found that patients receiving oral montelukast experienced less pain compared to patients receiving placebo, and the number of patients requiring postoperative painkillers was significantly higher in the placebo group than in the montelukast group.

Studies have shown that the drug montelukast may be effective in reducing inflammation and pain after surgery. The need for this plan may be due to the difficulties associated with pain control after ZMC fracture surgery. Postoperative pain can negatively impact a patient’s quality of life, resulting in an inability to perform daily activities and return to normal function quickly. Therefore, the use of non-opioid medications that can reduce pain and accelerate the healing process can be beneficial for patients, although very few studies have examined this issue, according to the search results. Considering the results of previous studies on the effectiveness of oral montelukast on pain levels before surgical interventions, the purpose of this study was to determine the effect of preoperative administration of oral montelukast on pain levels following ZMC fracture surgery.

Method

The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences (IR.TBZMED.DENTISTRY.REC.1403.043). The present study was a triple-blind, randomized, controlled trial study conducted on patients undergoing ZMC fracture surgery admitted to the hospital’s maxillofacial surgery department. The study sample included 80 patients undergoing ZMC fracture surgery.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The population selected for the present study was between the ages of 18 and 60 years, of both sexes, with a BMI of less than 30 kg/m2 (non-obese). Participating patients were selected from among those with ZMC fractures who had been referred to the hospital’s maxillofacial surgery department for treatment.

Exclusion criteria included preoperative drug use or dependence, peptic ulcer disease, hepatic or renal dysfunction, mental illness, allergy to amide local anesthetics and narcotics, and seizures. Also, in cases where the participant required hospitalization in the postoperative intensive care unit, that patient was excluded from the study.

Sampling method and sample size

Sampling in the present study was conducted using convenience sampling method. Triple-blind randomized allocation was used. This was done using a random number table. To select the sample from the random number table, randomly starting from a point in the row or column direction. In the present study (a triple-blind randomized clinical trial), the patient, the treating surgeon, and the data analyst were unaware of the randomization codes.

The number of samples in the present study was 80 patients, who were divided into two experimental groups (40 patients) and a control group (40 patients), and each group was divided into four subgroups of ten patients (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Number of studied samples in each group and subgroup

Group division

Group 2: the effect of montelukast on reducing surgical pain in ZMC fracture when taken 6 h before surgery: Control group (n = 10), Montelukast solution 10 mg group (n = 10).

Group 4: the effect of montelukast on reducing surgical pain in ZMC fracture when taken 24 h before surgery: Control group (n = 10), Montelukast solution 10 mg group (n = 10).

In all of the above steps and subgroup analyses, the duration of ZMC fracture surgery was recorded and reported in the specifications section.

Randomization and allocation concealment

A computer-generated random sequence was used to randomly assign participants to the intervention and control groups. Allocations were made in opaque, sealed, sequentially numbered envelopes that were opened only by the study coordinator, who had no role in patient care or assessment.

Method and intervention

The Research and Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences approved the protocol of this clinical trial. The CONSORT statement and the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki were followed in this study, and patients participated in the study only after providing written and fully informed consent.

Montelukast is a safe drug, and the safety profile of montelukast, even at doses higher than the recommended daily dose, is similar to placebo during short-term and long-term administration. Minor adverse events reported include headache, ear infection, nausea, abdominal pain, and pharyngitis [10]. Recently, the US Food and Drug Administration reviewed clinical trial data on reports of aggression, hallucinations, depression, and insomnia with montelukast. However, all of these side effects occur with continued use, given that in the present study, each participant received only one dose of the drug, the likelihood of side effects was very low. No acute side effects of this drug that required special precautions were reported. Therefore, no complications or anticipated risks threaten individuals. On the other hand, all participants were under medical supervision and control while receiving the drug. On the other hand, the results of this study are promising and the benefits of the study far outweigh the potential risks of the study. However, if participants had any concerns or needed guidance and advice, either during or after the study, they were supported and guided by the research team.

Patients who finally met the inclusion criteria were selected and randomly divided into control and montelukast groups (control and intervention groups). Demographic characteristics of all participants in the study, such as age, gender, weight, and degree of jaw dysfunction, were carefully recorded. Also, a form was filled out by the patients regarding the use of any analgesic or psychiatric medications and the individual’s medical history.

The montelukast group received a glass of juice containing 10 mg of montelukast dissolved in it at different time intervals of 0, 6, 12, and 24 h before transfer to the operating room, while the control group received only a glass of plain juice. Questionnaires, interviews, and observations were used to collect the necessary data for this study.

To ensure patient safety and compliance with preoperative fasting guidelines, the administration of montelukast at “0 h” was performed under the close supervision of the research and anesthesia teams. A 10 mg dose of montelukast was dissolved in a small volume of clear fruit juice and given to the patient. This approach is consistent with current guidelines for the ingestion of clear liquids prior to surgery, which allow small volumes up to 2 h before anesthesia. The anesthesiology team approved this protocol and assessed the risk of aspiration as minimal. Patients were closely monitored during this period, and no adverse events related to oral montelukast administration were reported.

All patients, regardless of the group they were assigned to, received the same postoperative care to reduce their risk of infection, pain, and swelling. Dexamethasone 8 mg was administered intravenously one hour before surgery, every four hours during surgery, and every eight hours after surgery to reduce postoperative pain and swelling. Postoperative pain was controlled with intravenous acetaminophen 1 g every 6 h and diclofenac 100 mg suppository every 12 h. To reduce the risk of infection, patients received intravenous cefazolin (1 g) every 6 h after surgery. There are no known drug interactions between any of the drugs taken with montelukast. The surgical team was the same and all surgeries were performed under the same general anesthesia protocol. The duration of surgery was recorded in the relevant registry. Electrocardiography and heart rate analysis device were used to monitor each patient. During surgery, heart rate was continuously monitored to detect any possible arrhythmia or severe hypotension [9].

Pain assessment (baseline and postoperative)

Preoperative baseline pain was assessed for all participants prior to any intervention using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS, 0–10). This baseline measurement allowed for control of individual variability in preoperative pain and served as a reference for postoperative pain evaluation. Postoperative pain was subsequently assessed at 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 h after surgery using the same VAS instrument.

Pain and swelling assessment

Postoperative pain intensity was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), a validated tool ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst possible pain). Pain assessments were conducted at 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 h postoperatively. Assessments were performed by a trained research nurse who was blinded to group allocation to ensure objectivity. Patients were also instructed to self-report their pain scores at each time point.

Welling was measured using a standardized linear measurement method between defined anatomical landmarks. Measurements were taken preoperatively (baseline) and at 24 h postoperatively. The percentage increase from baseline was calculated. All swelling measurements were performed by a single blinded investigator.

Statistical data analysis

The collected data were statistically analyzed using SPSS software version 23. The data were analyzed at two levels of descriptive and inferential statistics; In the descriptive statistics section, mean, standard deviation, and frequency percentage were used. In the inferential statistics section, to compare the variables under study (different times of drug use before surgery: 0, 6, 12, and 24 h and the effect of time of use on pain intensity, the effect of surgery duration on pain intensity) in the study groups, after examining the normal distribution of the data using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, one-way analysis of variance and relevant post hoc tests were used. The tests were evaluated at a 95% confidence level.

Result

The mean age of patients undergoing ZMC fracture surgery in the intervention group was 30.55 ± 5.44 years and in control group was 29.6 ± 57.01 years (p = 0.540). 62.5% and 65% of participants in the intervention and control groups were male, respectively. The mean body mass index of patients undergoing ZMC fracture surgery in the intervention group was 25.40 ± 1.84 and in the control group was 25.40 ± 1.85 (p = 0.907). The mean duration of surgery in the intervention group was 192.27 ± 14.92 min and in control group was 192.77 ± 16.21 min (p = 0.886).

Discussion

The present study was a randomized, triple-blind clinical trial investigating the effect of preoperative montelukast administration at 0, 6, 12, and 24 h before ZMC surgery on postoperative pain at 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 h. Postoperative pain following ZMC surgery is often moderate to severe [11, 12], and pharmacological interventions such as NSAIDs and opioids are commonly employed [13, 14]. These agents, however, may cause complications including postoperative nausea and vomiting [15, 16].

In the present study, average postoperative pain generally increased from 1 to 24 h. At all-time intervals, pain intensity in the montelukast group was significantly lower than in the control group. The lowest pain intensity was observed in the subgroup receiving montelukast 6 h preoperatively, whereas the 24-h preoperative subgroup showed the least effect, with pain levels approaching those of the control group. These findings are consistent with Ansari et al. [9], which demonstrated the analgesic efficacy of preoperative montelukast. Also, in other similar studies, the prescribed dose of oral montelukast before surgery was considered to be 10 mg [17, 18].

A significant positive correlation between surgery duration and postoperative pain was observed, aligning with prior studies by Ansari et al. [9], Samii-Rad et al. [19], and Mobini et al. [20]. Patients in the control group consistently reported higher pain levels and required more analgesics than those receiving montelukast, corroborating findings by Kumar et al., 2023 [21].

As observed in the present study, at all-time intervals, the mean pain scores were significantly higher in the control group compared to the montelukast group. Kumar et al. [21] showed that patients in the control group had higher pain levels after orthognathic surgery at all time intervals than the experimental group, who were prescribed montelukast before surgery. Also, the control group required more painkillers than the experimental group.

Although montelukast is not a conventional analgesic, it exhibits an indirect anti-inflammatory effect through antagonism of cysteinyl leukotriene receptors. Leukotrienes play a pivotal role in the inflammatory cascade and contribute to nociceptor sensitization, thereby amplifying pain perception [22]. By inhibiting leukotriene-mediated pathways, montelukast may attenuate local concentrations of inflammatory mediators, including SICAM-1, eosinophil cationic protein (ECP), and neuropeptides such as substance P, which are critically involved in postoperative nociception [23]. This pharmacological profile provides a scientific rationale for investigating montelukast as an adjunct to standard analgesic regimens in patients undergoing ZMC fracture surgery, a procedure typically associated with moderate to severe postoperative pain.

Montelukast’s analgesic effect may relate to its inhibition of leukotriene-mediated inflammation, reducing inflammatory mediators such as SICAM-1, ECP, and substance P [17, 24]. This mechanism is supported by studies in dysmenorrhea, tonsillectomy, sinus surgery, and COVID-19 hospitalized patients [20, 24–26]. Montelukast can serve as an adjunctive agent, enhancing analgesia alongside NSAIDs without the need for opioids [24].

Research results indicate that long-term use of leukotriene receptor antagonists will cause side effects (rash, dry or sore throat, fever, and headache, as well as anxiety, delirium, hallucinations, and sleep disorders) [27]. A single dose of montelukast is unlikely to cause significant side effects. Evidence suggests that a single oral dose of 10 mg montelukast does not significantly interfere with the effectiveness of steroids [28, 29]. Since it takes three to four hours for montelukast to reach its maximum plasma concentration [21], In the present study, the drug was administered at 24, 12, 6, and immediately before ZMC surgery. The time between preoperative administration of montelukast and full recovery from anesthesia is sufficient for the drug to reach its maximum plasma concentration [30]. Also, the analgesic effects of this drug persist for a long time after surgery because it is administered as a single dose and its half-life is estimated to be between 2.7 and 5.5 h [9, 30, 31].

The present study had some limitations; the duration of the study and other limiting factors led to the number of samples being considered for this study being 80 patients undergoing surgery; Even with the inclusion criteria in place, confounding variables such as the length of surgery and the weight of the participants could affect the study results. However, one solution to minimize the impact of confounding variables was to randomize the sampling. Randomization ensures that all potential confounding variables, even those that cannot be directly observed in the study itself, have the same average value between different groups. Random sampling was used, the effects of the variables of weight and length of surgery were minimized in this study, and if their effects remained, they were analyzed using statistical methods. Since these variables did not differ by group assignment, they could not be associated with the independent variable and therefore could not confound the study. One of the principal limitations of the present study was the widespread and uniform application of an aggressive multimodal analgesic protocol in both the intervention and control groups. This protocol involved repeated administration of intravenous dexamethasone (8 mg administered before, during, and after surgery), intravenous paracetamol (acetaminophen), and diclofenac suppositories, all of which independently exert well-established anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects. Although such a regimen is ethically and clinically justified to ensure adequate postoperative pain management, it may function as a confounding factor and substantially limit the study’s capacity to isolate the independent analgesic effect of montelukast. Under these conditions, the observed reduction in postoperative pain in the montelukast-treated group cannot be attributed solely to an independent analgesic effect; rather, the findings are more consistent with an adjunctive effect of montelukast when administered alongside a robust and standardized multimodal analgesic regimen. Although the analgesic protocol was identical across both study groups, thereby minimizing systematic bias, the absence of a precise evaluation of rescue analgesic requirements may further constrain the ability to fully elucidate the analgesic contribution of montelukast. Future investigations may clarify the role of montelukast as an adjunctive agent in postoperative pain control by systematically recording rescue analgesic consumption, reducing the intensity of concurrent analgesic regimens, or employing stratified or comparative study designs.

Based on the results of the present study, it is suggested that future studies examine multicenter populations from across the country to obtain a larger sample size and to investigate the effect of montelukast on pain reduction over a longer period of time (up to 72 h) after ZMC surgery. Also, the results of pain intensity after administration of different doses of montelukast before ZMC surgery should be examined and the effect of montelukast on pain reduction in maxillofacial surgeries should be investigated.

Conclusion

Preoperative administration of montelukast effectively reduces postoperative pain in patients undergoing zygomaticomaxillary complex fracture surgery, with the greatest analgesic effect observed when the drug is administered six hours prior to surgery. Postoperative pain was assessed over the first 24 h, and the findings indicate that longer surgical duration is associated with higher pain intensity. These results underscore the importance of considering both the timing of montelukast administration and the length of surgery when optimizing postoperative pain management strategies.
