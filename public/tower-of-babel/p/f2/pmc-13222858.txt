CLARIS: Control-based language-guided realistic imperfection synthesis
Authors: Eunho Kim, Jongpil Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Automated vision inspection is vital in modern manufacturing, but advanced processes with high yield rates cause a severe data imbalance: abundant normal data and scarce defective data. To overcome this, we propose CLARIS (Control-based Language-guided Realistic Imperfection Synthesis), a novel framework combining natural language semantic flexibility with 3D structural constraints to generate physically consistent, high-quality defect images. CLARIS utilizes a Vision-Language Model (VLM) to interpret user instructions and input images, dynamically generating tailored text prompts and defect masks. Subsequently, ControlNet ensures the synthesized defects adhere to the object’s physical shape and surface curvature by explicitly applying normal maps as constraints. Furthermore, Textual Inversion (TI) and Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) are employed to efficiently learn and reflect the unique visual characteristics of specific defects using minimal parameters. Evaluated on the 15 categories of the MVTec Anomaly Detection (MVTec AD) dataset, the framework achieved an average Kernel Inception Distance (KID) of 11.07, Inception Score (IS) of 1.63, and intra-cluster pairwise LPIPS distance (IC-LPIPS) of 0.27.

Introduction

In modern manufacturing, automated vision inspection is pivotal for ensuring quality in precision products like semiconductor wafers and pharmaceuticals. However, the high yields of stabilized production lines lead to a severe data imbalance where normal data is abundant but defect samples are scarce, hindering supervised learning. To mitigate this, initial research focused on unsupervised anomaly detection, utilizing reconstruction-based autoencoders1 or feature embedding-based deep networks2,3 to identify deviations from normal distributions. While effective at detection, these methods lack the ability to classify specific defect types.

To address this proactively, generative approaches have emerged. Early model-free methods like CutPaste4, Crop-Paste5, and DRAEM6 synthesized defects by overlaying patches or textures, yet they often lacked realism by ignoring physical contexts like surface curvature. Subsequently, Generative adversarial network (GAN)-based frameworks such as DefectGAN7 and SDGAN8 improved visual quality9, though they faced instability, mode collapse, and high data requirements. Even data-efficient fine-tuning of StyleGAN2 in DFMGAN10,11 struggled with precise mask alignment and complex textures.

The paradigm has recently shifted toward diffusion models12. While early denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs)13 were computationally expensive, latent diffusion models (LDMs)14 improved efficiency by operating in compressed latent spaces, leading to advanced defect synthesis tools like AnomalyDiffusion15 and DefectFill16. Despite their success, these models often fail to explicitly reflect 3D structural constraints or material-specific optical characteristics. To enhance control, ControlNet17 introduced spatial conditioning via zero convolutions, while personalization techniques like Textual Inversion (TI)18 allowed learning new defect concepts with minimal memory.

Further challenges involve the computational cost of domain-specific adaptation. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT), particularly Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)19, enables adapting large models by updating only low-rank matrices. Additionally, while text-to-image paradigms using cross-attention have expanded via InstructPix2Pix20, Prompt-to-Prompt21, and GLIGEN22, they often require unintuitive prompt engineering or structured spatial inputs.

This study overcomes these constraints with CLARIS (Control-based Language-guided Realistic Imperfection Synthesis), a framework that integrates semantic and structural control. To maximize practical applicability, CLARIS features a dual inference workflow: skilled users can utilize manual masks for pixel-level precision, while field practitioners can seamlessly generate defects through simple natural language instructions, eliminating the need for complex prompt engineering. Specifically, CLARIS employs a vision-language model (VLM)23 to dynamically interpret natural instructions into semantic prompts and procedural masks, while ControlNet-based normal maps enforce explicit 3D geometric constraints. By applying Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) and Textual Inversion to the U-Net architecture, CLARIS enables highly efficient learning of unique defect characteristics. Evaluations on the MVTec AD dataset demonstrate the framework’s effectiveness in generating high-quality, physically consistent defects on the MVTec AD benchmark, achieving an average Kernel Inception Distance (KID) of 11.07, an Inception Score (IS) of 1.63, and an Intra-Cluster LPIPS (IC-LPIPS) of 0.27 across 15 categories.

When considering the average values of the three metrics along with their characteristics, the defect images generated by CLARIS demonstrate high statistical similarity to the distribution of the original defect images, as well as high diversity, consistency, and quality, validating their effectiveness. In particular, the low KID values indicate that the feature distributions of the defect images generated by CLARIS are generally close to those of the original defect images, meaning that defects with distinct structural and textural characteristics are effectively integrated into normal images. The average IS value is 1.63, indicating that CLARIS has the ability to generate highly diverse and high-quality defect images. Finally, the IC-LPIPS value of 0.27 indicates that there are sufficient perceptual differences between the defect images generated within each class, validating the model’s data augmentation and generalization capabilities.

Category-based analysis shows that objects with distinct 3D structures and relatively uniform surfaces, such as the screw, bottle, and grid, recorded very low KID values and achieved high realism. This demonstrates that the ControlNet-based normal map control, one of the core design elements of this study, was particularly effective for these uniform objects. Normal map control extends beyond simply synthesizing defect patterns on 2D planes; it enhances physical naturalness by ensuring that generated defects reflect the actual surface curvature, depth variations, and lighting directions. As a result, for objects with distinct 3D structures, the location, size, and shape of defects naturally blend with the original geometric structure, achieving a high level of realism.

However, for categories where complex and irregular textures such as leather, tile, and carpet are the primary visual features, relatively high KID values were recorded. This occurs because normal maps are unable to adequately capture detailed material information such as fine wrinkles of leather, fiber strands of a carpet, and irregular texture patterns on tile surfaces, despite effectively capturing the macroscopic surface geometric structure. As a result, subtle discrepancies between the generated defects and the original surface texture emerge, revealing differences not only in human visual perception but also in statistical metrics like KID. These results suggest that, while CLARIS demonstrates strengths in structural control, it may require additional texture-specific signals or high-resolution post-processing modules to enhance its high-frequency texture representation capabilities.

Alternatively, conditioning approaches beyond ControlNet-based normal maps are worth exploring in future work. Additionally, category-specific IC-LPIPS analysis shows that relatively high diversity was maintained even in categories with simple surface structures. This means that CLARIS can generate multiple scenarios tailored to the location, size, and shape of defects, even for the same object and defect type. For example, in the case of grid or screw defects, instead of repeatedly generating defects of the same size at the same locations, CLARIS ensures dataset diversity by introducing subtle changes in shape and texture. Ablation study

To evaluate the individual contributions of LoRA, Textual Inversion, and ControlNet to the CLARIS framework, we conducted an ablation study on the MVTec AD dataset. The quantitative results across four incremental settings are presented in Table 4.

The baseline model with LoRA, Textual Inversion, and ControlNet disabled recorded a KID of 6.33, an IS of 1.16, and an IC-LPIPS of 0.23. A low KID value indicates that the statistical distance from the actual image distribution is relatively small, but an IS of 1.16 is very low, suggesting that the visual diversity and quality of the generated defects are suboptimal. When only LoRA was activated, the KID increased to 11.77, but the IS significantly improved to 1.64, and the IC-LPIPS increased to 0.26. When Textual Inversion was subsequently added, the KID decreased slightly to 11.33, the IS remained the same at 1.64, and the IC-LPIPS increased slightly to 0.27.

Finally, when combining LoRA, Textual Inversion, and ControlNet, the model achieved a KID of 11.07, an IS of 1.63, and an IC-LPIPS of 0.27. Compared to the previous setting, the addition of ControlNet-based normal map control further decreased the KID while maintaining a comparable level of IS and IC-LPIPS.

Discussion

The CLARIS framework proposed in this study has been experimentally verified to enable the realistic synthesis of various defect types by combining LoRA, Textual Inversion, and ControlNet-based normal map control. While LoRA activation led to a numerical increase in KID from 6.33 to 11.77, this phenomenon should be interpreted in conjunction with the significant improvement in IS (from 1.16 to 1.64). The baseline’s lower KID, while appearing favorable at first glance, is accompanied by an extremely low IS, suggesting a state of mode collapse where the model merely reproduces overly simplified and unrealistic patterns. In contrast, the simultaneous rise in KID, IS, and IC-LPIPS (from 0.23 to 0.26) confirms that LoRA successfully captures and reproduces the highly complex and subtle textural details of actual defects. Although these patterns are statistically further from the sparse training distribution, they demonstrate improved quality and realism more consistent with defects observed in actual industrial settings, thereby enhancing the utility of the synthesized data. Textual Inversion definitively maintains quality and diversity while coherently expanding the data by reflecting object characteristics encoded in words/tokens when generating defects within a class.

From an industrial perspective, CLARIS significantly enhances the productivity and accessibility of defect data augmentation. For instance, in domains such as automotive painting-where defect type diversity and scarcity hinder inspection model training-or in semiconductor and display manufacturing-where micro-defect detection is critical-the controllable synthesis pipeline of CLARIS offers a promising avenue for data augmentation. However, direct validation on such production-line data remains a subject of future work, and the present results should be interpreted within the scope of the MVTec AD benchmark. Nevertheless, the efficacy of these generative capabilities involves nuanced trade-offs; specifically, the observation that IC-LPIPS values remained high while IS decreased slightly in certain categories reflects the stabilizing effect of ControlNet-based structural constraints. While these constraints may suppress extreme deformations and thus reduce pure diversity, they ensure the stable generation of visually convincing defects, suggesting that a composite evaluation is more informative than relying on any single metric. Furthermore, the reported mean AUROC of 0.75 highlights a remaining domain gap that varies significantly by category. The high performance in rigid categories, such as Bottle (0.99) and Tile (0.90), validates the efficacy of our 3D structural constraints in maintaining geometric consistency, whereas the lower scores in texture-sensitive categories like Leather (0.55) suggest that the current normal map pipeline may lose critical high-frequency textural details during the estimation process. Similarly, Transistor exhibited notably low scores across all localization metrics (AUROC 0.62, AP 0.09), attributable to its fine-pitch component patterns and metallic reflectance that are difficult to encode via surface normals alone. To bridge this gap, future work will incorporate multi-modal constraints-such as Canny edges or texture-aware encoders-to simultaneously preserve both the micro-texture and macro-structure of synthesized defects.

The choice of the MVTec AD dataset as our primary evaluation benchmark is justified by its status as a de facto standard in industrial anomaly detection, offering a diverse array of 15 categories that encompass both rigid object geometries and non-rigid textures. This variety provides a rigorous environment for evaluating structural and textural consistency under diverse manufacturing conditions. While MVTec AD serves as a comprehensive baseline for initial validation, we acknowledge that single-dataset evaluation has inherent limitations in proving universal applicability across all possible production lines. Beyond this evaluation scope, the proposed framework itself has a few technical limitations. First, CLARIS relies on high-quality normal maps, so its application may be limited to flat surfaces where 3D information is difficult to obtain, such as the wood category in MVTec AD. In future research, the use of multiple cameras for photogrammetry or transformer-based 3D reconstruction can be used to enhance this model. Second, VLM-based mask generation is currently limited to defects with simple shapes, and the spatial understanding capabilities of VLM must be improved to extend this to defects with complex shapes. In future research, we aim to improve performance by utilizing various structural conditions, such as depth maps and sketches in addition to normal maps, and by adding spatial reasoning and detailed mask generation modules to the VLM. Furthermore, we seek to establish a verification framework to evaluate the statistical reliability of the generated synthetic data and prevent confusion with real data, thereby developing a commercially viable defect generation solution that can be safely and efficiently utilized in industrial settings.

Methods

This section describes the overall structure of the proposed defect synthesis framework and details the components. The entire pipeline considers as input a normal image without defects and a user’s natural language instructions and outputs a defect image that naturally synthesizes specific defects over the normal image according to the instructions.

This process consists of five interdependent steps. The first stage analyzes the user’s natural language instructions to determine the semantic attributes (defect type) and the spatial attributes, such as location, size, and shape. Then this information is formalized to generate a binary mask that defines the defect region. The second stage extracts a normal vector map containing surface structure and shape information of objects from the input defect-free image and uses it as a condition for the generation model, preventing potential issues of shape and structure change during synthesis. The third stage utilizes a pre-trained latent diffusion model as a backbone, applying the text prompt, mask image, and normal map extracted from the normal image as conditions to synthesize defects as realistically as possible. The fourth stage trains the model to understand specific types of defects that occur in industrial settings using only a small number of examples. This involves learning defect concepts as virtual words and fine-tuning the U-Net structure of the diffusion model in a parameter-efficient manner to enable the model to learn the unique visual features of each defect. Finally, this framework optimizes the entire learning process by constructing a loss function that guides the generated defects to reproduce the features of normal images and blend naturally with the background.

Overall framework

Figure 1 shows the overall structure of the proposed control-based language-guided realistic imperfection synthesis (CLARIS) framework, depicting how semantic control and structural control are processed independently before being combined in the final inpainting stage. The ultimate goal is to complement the limitations of existing methodologies and facilitate the generation of defect images. The core concept of the framework is to divide the problem into two parts: identify the defect to draw and the mechanism with which to draw it. Here, semantic control defines the type of defect to be generated and its external characteristics according to defect type and object, while structural control imposes geometric constraints to ensure that the synthesized defect appears naturally and preserves the physical structure and shape of the original object.

The process occurs as follows. First, the user freely specifies the type, location, and size of the defect using natural language. A pre-trained VLM then interprets this unstructured text. The language model extracts keywords indicating the defect type, location, and size information in a structured data format through context analysis. The extracted defect keywords are converted into controllable text conditions that determine the semantic direction of the generation model. Simultaneously, the extracted location and size information is transmitted to a procedural mask-generation module, which converts it into a binary mask with the same resolution as the original image. This mask explicitly complements the diffusion model by specifying the areas of defect concentration. At the same time, a pre-trained normal estimator processes the defect-free input image for pixel brightness and color changes to estimate the normal direction at each pixel location and outputs it as a normal map in RGB image format. This normal map contains rich information about the subtle curvature of the object surface and its overall geometric structure, providing strong structural constraints to the diffusion model.

Subsequently, the latent diffusion-based generator performs conditional image generation with input of all the multi-modal conditions prepared earlier: the text conditions defining the type of defect, the binary mask specifying the defect location, and the normal map constraining the object shape. The generator operates based on the vast visual and linguistic capabilities of a pre-trained visual text-to-image model. To adapt to new defect types, it simultaneously utilizes LoRA techniques and new token embedding learning techniques to efficiently learn only a small number of parameters. Finally, the low-dimensional latent representations generated through the diffusion process are decoded to images at the original resolution via a high-performance decoder, completing the final synthesis of realistic defects that accurately reflect the user intent.

User instruction analysis and mask generation

One of the key features of this framework is providing an interface that allows non-expert users to intuitively control defect generation using natural language. The core of this functionality is a user instruction analysis module that converts users’ unstructured linguistic requests into structured parameters that can be understood by machines. This module leverages the powerful zero-shot inference capabilities of google/gemma-3n-e4b-it (Gemma3)25, a pre-trained VLM that identifies and separates expressions representing the essence of defects and modifiers that refer to their spatial attributes through grammatical analysis of input sentences. To better illustrate this procedure, consider an unstructured natural language instruction provided by a user: ”Make a medium glue in the center.” Instead of relying on manual mask drawing, the VLM functions as a semantic decomposer. It processes this text and extracts explicit defect attributes: the object category (leather), the type of defect (glue), the location (center), and the size or shape characteristics (medium). These parsed attributes are then spatially grounded to generate a procedural mask and condition the latent diffusion model, ensuring that the generated defect accurately reflects the user’s intent without requiring complex prompt engineering. As illustrated in Figure 1, the VLM parses a natural language prompt into dual-stream outputs; while spatial attributes such as ’center’ and ’medium’ are directly fed into the procedural mask generator, the semantic attributes are mapped to a structured JSON payload. For instance, the instruction ”Make a medium glue in the center” is parsed by the VLM into semantic and spatial parameters such as { ”category”: ”leather”, ”defect_key”: ”glue” }. Concurrently, the procedural mask generator translates these spatial attributes into a specific coordinate set. The extracted defect type keywords are mapped to the most likely standard defect type by comparing them with a predefined list of known defect types based on semantic similarity. For example, when processing user requests, the VLM extracts the defect type, location information, size information, etc. This matching with the defect list is based not only on simple word matching but also on the intersection of synonym sets to ensure flexibility. If no clear matches exist, the core keywords extracted from the sentence are used as temporary defect types to construct the generation prompt. Once the location, size, and shape information is successfully identified, a rule-based procedural mask generator is activated to generate a binary mask based on this information. The mask generator selects various geometric shapes such as rectangles, circles, ellipses, and lines according to the image resolution and determines the position and size parameters of the shapes based on the interpreted user instructions, rendering them on the mask canvas. If the user does not explicitly specify the defect shape, the generator randomly selects a shape from the basic pool to allow the model to learn various defect shapes. While this initial implementation utilizes geometric primitives for stability validation, the framework’s modular architecture is inherently designed to accommodate the complex topological structures of real-world defects without requiring model retraining. By decoupling the mask generation logic from the core diffusion process, the system can be scaled to support intricate morphologies through plug-and-play library updates. For instance, complex branching structures such as erratic cracks can be synthesized by integrating Voronoi diagram algorithms, while organic patterns like stains or corrosion can be modeled via Perlin noise functions. Furthermore, curved scratches can be accurately represented using Bezier curves to capture realistic surface interactions. During training, the ground truth masks of actual defects in the dataset are used; however, during inference, the masks dynamically generated through the language model and rule-based system play a crucial role in controlling the areas of defect insertion. Additionally, considering the practical issue that visual-language model responses may not always be perfectly structured or complete, the pipeline includes preliminary rule-based logic to ensure robustness. If the language model fails to accurately match the defect type or generates an unexpected response format, the system activates an alternative mechanism that directly extracts key nouns or adjectives from the user instruction text to generate a list of defect keyword candidates. These keywords are first compared with actual object category names in the dataset and then expanded in meaning through an embedded dictionary-lookup thesaurus to maximize matching success rates. These multiple safety mechanisms mitigate various uncertainties that may arise in automated natural language processing, improving the overall system stability and user experience. Image generation

The image generation module of this framework is based on an LDM. Instead of directly processing high-resolution images, it compresses the original image into a low-dimensional latent representation using a variational autoencoder and then performs diffusion and reverse diffusion processes within this latent space. This approach efficiently handles high-frequency details during compression and synthesizes core semantic and structural features during the generation stage, optimizing the balance between computational efficiency and generation quality. The entire generation process is designed to integrate multiple condition inputs to precisely reflect user intent.

First, to establish the structural conditions required for generation, ensuring that the synthesized defects naturally conform to the physical geometry of the target object is critical. To achieve this, we explicitly integrate a normal map pipeline into our framework. Specifically, a pre-trained normal estimator processes the defect-free input image, analyzing pixel brightness and color variations to estimate the normal direction at each pixel location. It then outputs this spatial information as a normal map in RGB image format. This map explicitly represents the minute curvatures and overall three-dimensionality of the object surface and acts as a powerful spatial constraint throughout the diffusion process. During generation, the previously generated normal map, the text prompt and binary mask derived from the user instruction analysis stage, and the original normal image are integrated. The mask clearly specifies the specific areas for defect generation in the latent space, and the RGB normal map serves as the direct geometric constraint for the NormalControlNet. By modulating the features of the U-Net encoder, the NormalControlNet enforces structural awareness, preventing the synthesized defects from appearing as unrealistic, flat 2D overlays and ensuring they follow the natural 3D curvature of the object’s surface. In the inference stage, the final defect synthesis is performed using fully trained model parameters. Given the mask and text conditions obtained from user instructions, along with the normal map obtained from the preprocessor, the latent diffusion pipeline generates the target defects in the specified area and finally reconstructs them as high-resolution image features through the decoder.

The core of the learning process is to combine noise-added latent representations with masks, enabling the model to follow the target defect distribution within the defect region and the original image distribution in the background region. The cross-attention mechanism acts as a channel to effectively inject text conditions into the latent representation channels, reflecting the specific defect concepts learned through the Textual Inversion technique as semantic guidelines. Simultaneously, the low-level features of ControlNet directly respond to the mask and normal map, contributing to the maximum preservation of the geometry and lighting conditions of the surface. The integration of these multiple conditions is the key mechanism for generating high-quality images in which the location of visually harmonious defects is clearly controlled.

The main difference between the training and inference processes lies in the generation of input latent representations and origination locations of the masks. During training, both normal images and actual defect images are provided, and each can be processed by an autoencoder and used to learn realistic reconstruction of defect areas. In contrast, during inference, only normal images and procedurally generated masks are provided, and the model must fill in the mask areas based on knowledge learned during training. Therefore, the model recognizes the role of the mask during training and is trained to generate visual features specific to a defect in its corresponding area. The matrices injected via the low-rank adapter focus on learning the fine textures and patterns unique to defects during this process. With this training, the adapter enables stable and fast convergence with fewer steps during inference.

The ControlNet used in this study uses only two spatial conditions: a single normal map and a binary mask. Complex control functions that combine multiple types of condition maps or dynamically adjust the influence of each condition are beyond the scope of this study. Instead, structural information is provided through a single normal map, and positional information is clearly separated and provided through a mask. The U-Net architecture of ControlNet stably accepts these conditions through an additional module initialized to zero. Textual conditions are injected into latent features through cross-attention, enabling semantic integration of natural language instructions in the generation process. The final image generation process can be fine-tuned through key hyperparameters such as the guidance scale, which adjusts the total number of steps in the diffusion scheduler or the relative influence of semantic and structural conditions. This allows users to control the intensity and detail of defect representation and achieve desired results.

Fine-tuning

Adapting large-scale pre-trained models to specific industrial defects requires learning new visual concepts with minimal samples and computational cost. To achieve this, CLARIS employs a Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) strategy that combines Textual Inversion and Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA).

Specifically, Textual Inversion and LoRA are applied simultaneously. Textual Inversion learns the abstract semantic concept of specific defects by optimizing a new virtual word token in the frozen text encoder’s vocabulary, a memory-efficient approach that preserves existing generalization even when learning multiple defect types simultaneously. Concurrently, grounded in the empirical observation that task-specific weight updates are inherently low-rank, LoRA is applied to the U-Net’s attention layers. By updating only parallel low-rank adapter modules while freezing the original weight matrices, the model efficiently captures the subtle forms and unique textures of the defects with significantly reduced parameters.

These two techniques operate synergistically: Textual Inversion provides the semantic guideline (”what” to draw), while the LoRA adapters supply the detailed stylistic and textural information (”how” to draw). Both parameter groups are optimized independently. This modular approach ensures high scalability and memory efficiency, allowing manufacturers to swiftly update defect synthesis models for new defect types without retraining the entire system or requiring massive storage space.

Loss function

The proposed framework aims to optimize the model to reconstruct reference defect images as realistically as possible under multiple given conditions by updating only a small number of parameters. To achieve this, the study combines multiple loss terms that complement each other to form the overall learning objective function. Fundamentally, the learning objective of diffusion models is formulated as a noise prediction problem. Therefore, the most critical loss term is the mean squared error between the actual noise added to the latent representation of the input defect image and the noise predicted by the current model. This error is calculated by applying a mask that explicitly defines the defect region, focusing only on the areas of defect insertion. This forces the model to restore the accurate noise distribution within the target region without unnecessarily altering the information in the defect-free background, enabling the model to learn the structural features of the defect. Performance metrics

To evaluate the visual quality of images generated by the model, we used kernel inception distance (KID)27, Inception Score (IS)28, and intra-cluster pairwise LPIPS distance (IC-LPIPS) as metrics. KID is a metric that measures the distance between the feature distributions of real images and generated images, calculated as the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) between feature vectors extracted using the Inception-v329 network. A lower value indicates greater similarity between the two image distributions. Conclusions

This study proposed CLARIS, a novel defect synthesis framework that effectively addresses data imbalance in smart manufacturing by integrating VLM-based semantic control with ControlNet-driven 3D structural constraints. By leveraging PEFT techniques like LoRA and Textual Inversion, CLARIS generates high-fidelity, physically consistent defect images with minimal computational overhead. Experimental results on the MVTec AD dataset demonstrate high realism (KID 11.07) and diversity (IS 1.63). Ultimately, CLARIS lowers the barrier for non-experts to generate high-quality industrial data, providing a promising foundation for defect data augmentation in industrial vision inspection within the scope of the MVTec AD benchmark, with broader applicability to be established through future cross-domain validation.
