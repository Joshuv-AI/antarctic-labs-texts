Letter to the Editor: Commentary on Overview of Cervical Spine Injuries Caused by Diving Into Shallow Water on Jeju Island: A 9-Year Retrospective Study in a Regional Trauma Center (Korean J Neurotrauma 2025;21:79-92)
Authors: Byung-Jou Lee
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

As economic prosperity has increased in South Korea, there has been a growing emphasis on well-being and quality of life, leading to a rise in various leisure activities.1) In particular, interest in marine sports has risen, resulting in more people traveling domestically and internationally to enjoy water-related activities such as diving.3) Consequently, the incidence of cervical spine injuries related to diving has also increased in Korea.3) In particular, reporting cervical spine injuries caused by diving on Jeju Island—the nation’s most popular tourist destination—is especially valuable for managing sports-related cervical cord injuries. I read with great interest the recently published article about cervical cord injury from diving on Jeju Island.2) The authors present a valuable analysis of cervical spine injuries resulting from diving into shallow water, a topic with both local relevance and global significance. I would like to commend the authors on addressing a critical public health issue that is particularly relevant in tourist areas such as Jeju Island. In addition, this study provides valuable epidemiological insights into cervical spinal cord injuries associated with diving, a problem that is often underreported and underrecognized, particularly in areas with a high influx of tourists. The retrospective data collected by the authors over a nine-year period offers a long-term perspective on injury patterns, mechanisms, and outcomes, which is extremely valuable for both prevention and management strategies. By incorporating detailed imaging characteristics such as maximal canal compromise, maximal spinal cord compromise, and lesion length, the study adds considerable depth to our understanding of prognostic factors. This may help to facilitate more effective patient stratification and guide surgical decision-making. Nevertheless, I would like to highlight some limitations that merit further discussion. As the authors acknowledge, the sample size is relatively small (n=34), limiting the statistical power of their regression analyses. Although their observations regarding mean canal compromise and injury severity are valuable, these findings need to be interpreted cautiously and validated in larger, multicenter studies. Additionally, the study could have provided more detailed information on post-discharge rehabilitation protocols, particularly their potential influence on long-term neurological recovery. Given that functional outcomes are heavily influenced by the accessibility and intensity of rehabilitation, future studies might consider incorporating these parameters into their analyses. Overall, this study makes an important contribution by raising awareness of the mechanisms, risk factors, and clinical features of shallow-water diving injuries in a tourist-heavy region. I appreciate the authors’ efforts and encourage further multicenter, prospective studies to validate and expand upon these findings, ultimately leading to better prevention and treatment strategies for this devastating type of injury.
