WNT7A mRNA Lipid Nanoparticles Promote Muscle Hypertrophy and Reduce Fatty Infiltration
Authors: Larion Martin Santiago, Kasoorelope Oguntuyo, Britney Chin-Young, Damien Laudier, Zhixin Yu, Pedro Henrique Alves da Silva, Fei Fang, Angelo Amabile, Woojin M Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeMyosteatosis and muscle atrophy are key pathological features of skeletal muscle degeneration in chronic injuries, degenerative myopathies, and aging. While recombinant WNT7A has shown promise in stimulating muscle hypertrophy and reducing fatty infiltration, its clinical translation is limited by challenges in delivery, scalability, and cost. The objective of this study was to evaluate the feasibility of lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-mediated mRNA delivery of WNT7A (W7a-LNP) as an alternative strategy for mitigating muscle degeneration.MethodsW7a-LNP efficacy was assessed in vitro and in vivo using primary murine fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs), C2C12 myoblasts, and mouse models of muscle injury. FAP adipogenesis and myofiber size were quantified following W7a-LNP treatment. In vivo, W7a-LNP was administered via intramuscular injection in uninjured and glycerol-injured muscles, and its effects on myofiber size and intramuscular adipose tissue (IMAT) formation were analyzed.ResultsW7a-LNP inhibited adipogenesis and increased myofiber size in vitro. Introduction

Myosteatosis, characterized by intramuscular adipose tissue (IMAT), and muscle atrophy are pathologic features observed in chronic skeletal muscle injuries, degenerative myopathies, and aging [1–5]. Both conditions contribute to reduced muscle function, impaired regeneration, and poor surgical repair outcomes [1–5]. For instance, in Duchenne muscular dystrophy, progressive fatty infiltration is correlated with disease severity and declining ambulatory performance, while concomitant muscle atrophy exacerbates functional decline [1, 6, 7]. Similarly, in rotator cuff tears, tear size is associated with IMAT severity and muscle atrophy, which persist post-repair and contribute to higher repair failure rates and diminished muscle strength [4, 5]. Beyond these conditions, increased IMAT and atrophy are also observed in prolonged bed rest, knee osteoarthritis, low back pain, sarcopenia, and spaceflight, highlighting its widespread impact and the need for engineered therapies that target both IMAT and muscle atrophy to preserve muscle function [8–12].

Fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs), a mesenchymal stromal cell population residing in the interstitial space between myofibers, are key regulators of IMAT development and muscle atrophy [13–16]. In healthy muscle, FAPs support muscle homeostasis and regeneration by sensing injury signals and coordinating muscle stem cell (MuSC) activation [13]. Conditional ablation of FAPs in uninjured muscles leads to muscle atrophy and loss of MuSCs [13]. Upon muscle injury or exercise, infiltrating immune cells clear debris and release IL-4, which activates FAPs [17–19]. Activated FAPs secrete pro-myogenic factors that stimulate MuSC activation and expansion, enabling their differentiation into myofibers [14]. As the inflammation resolves, macrophage-derived tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), induces apoptotic clearance of FAPs to prevent excessive remodeling [20]. However, in chronic muscle pathology, dysregulated FAP expansion leads to differentiation into adipocytes, contributing to IMAT, or myofibroblasts, leading to fibrosis [15, 16, 21]. These pathologic changes not only disrupt muscle architecture, but also promote myofiber loss, reducing contractile force [2, 22]. Importantly, fatty infiltration and muscle atrophy often progress irreversibly, and no effective treatments exist to restore lost muscle mass or prevent IMAT accumulation. Given the pivotal role of FAPs in IMAT formation and homeostasis, targeting FAPs while simultaneously supporting myofibers represents a critical therapeutic strategy to mitigate IMAT, preserve muscle structure, and improve functional recovery.

We previously demonstrated that recombinant wingless-type MMTV integration site family member 7A (WNT7A) counteracts muscle degeneration by inhibiting FAP adipogenesis and reducing skeletal muscle fatty infiltration without inducing fibrosis [23]. Beyond suppressing FAP adipogenesis, WNT7A also promotes muscle regeneration by stimulating MuSC expansion, enhancing myofiber hypertrophy, and increasing muscle strength [24–29]. These combined effects make recombinant WNT7A a promising therapeutic candidate for muscle repair. However, recombinant protein therapies face significant limitations, including production scalability, therapeutic efficacy, and immunogenicity [30–32]. These challenges highlight the need for alternative, cost-effective strategies to deliver WNT7A efficiently and effectively.

Lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-mediated mRNA delivery offers a scalable and efficient alternative for therapeutic applications [33]. Compared to plasmid DNA, viral vectors, or recombinant proteins, in vitro-transcribed (IVT) mRNA offers several advantages: It eliminates the risk of insertional mutagenesis, has transient activity, and is naturally degraded through physiological metabolic pathways [31]. Additionally, IVT mRNA synthesis is a simple, cost-effective, single-container process, making it more scalable than recombinant protein production [30–32]. However, mRNA’s inherent instability, immunogenicity, and susceptibility to nucleases present significant challenges. These can be mitigated by incorporating a 5′ cap, poly(A) tail, 5′- and 3′-untranslated regions (UTRs), and chemically modified nucleosides that reduce the immunostimulatory effect of IVT mRNA [31]. To overcome delivery barriers, LNPs have emerged as a leading technology, efficiently protecting mRNA from nuclease degradation, facilitating cellular uptake, and promoting endosomal escape for cytosolic translation [33].

The objective of this study is to evaluate the feasibility of localized skeletal muscle delivery of LNP-encapsulated WNT7A mRNA (W7a-LNP) and assess its efficacy in mitigating both intramuscular fatty infiltration and muscle atrophy. Using freshly isolated primary murine FAPs and C2C12 myoblasts, we demonstrate that W7a-LNP effectively reduce adipogenic potential of FAPs, while promoting myofiber hypertrophy in vitro. In uninjured muscle, W7a-LNP delivery enhances muscle hypertrophy without inducing fibrosis. In the glycerol injury model, early post-injury delivery of W7a-LNP showed variable effects on IMAT reduction, likely due to the absence of viable myofibers for mRNA uptake and protein production. Delayed delivery at 4 days post-injury significantly reduced fatty infiltration, highlighting the importance of timing and target cell availability for therapeutic efficacy. These findings serve as proof-of-concept that W7a-LNP can transform skeletal muscle cells into “in vivo protein factories”, transiently reprogramming the local environment to limit IMAT accumulation, preserve muscle mass, and enhance regeneration. This approach offers a scalable, cost-effective, and highly translational therapy for targeting FAPs and myofibers to mitigate pathological muscle degeneration.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Animal procedures were conducted under the approval of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai (#PROTO202100002). Mice were housed and maintained in the Center for Comparative Medicine and Surgery Facility at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai. C57Bl/6 J mice (12–14 weeks old, #000664) and Ai14-TdTomato mice (22 weeks old, #007914) were acquired from the Jackson Laboratory. Both male and female mice were included in the study in a randomized manner.

DNA Templates for RNA In Vitro Transcription

Plasmid templates for RNA in vitro transcription (IVT) were synthetized by Twist Bioscience incorporating a T7 promoter sequence, a 5′ UTR, an open reading frame, and a 3′ UTR. Our construct employed NASAR UTRs for enhanced gene expression [34]. IVT templates were generated from plasmids by amplifying DNA with Q5 High-Fidelity Master Mix (NEB). A 120-nucleotide poly-A tail was encoded into the PCR template using a reverse primer containing a 120-nucleotide poly-T. Following PCR, DpnI (NEB) was used to digest plasmid contaminants at 37 °C for 30 min. The PCR products were purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen), and concentrations were measured with the NanoDropTM 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) before IVT.

Synthesis and Purification of mRNAs

Modified mRNAs (modRNA) were synthesized using the HiScribe T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (NEB). N1-methylpseudouridine−5′-triphosphate (N1m), sourced from TriLink Biotechnologies, was fully substituted for UTP in the reaction to produce modRNAs. A 1:4 premix of CleanCap reagent AG (TriLink) was included for co-transcriptional capping. Reaction mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 5 h, followed by a 30-minute incubation with TURBO DNase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to remove residual DNA template. RNA products were then purified using the Monarch RNA Cleanup Kit (NEB). RNA concentration was determined with the NanoDropTM 2000, and species purity and size were verified using a denaturing RNA gel.

Lipid Nanoparticle Formation

mRNA encapsulation in LNP was performed using the NanoAssemblerTM IgniteTM microfluidic mixing device (Cytiva). SM-102 particles were formulated with helper lipids DSPC (1,2-disteraroyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine), cholesterol, and DMG-PEG2000 (1,2-dimyristoyl-sn-glycerol methoxypolyethylene glycol) at a molar ratio of 50:10:38.5:1.5, with modRNA dissolved in a citrate buffer [35]. The freshly formed RNA-LNPs were dialyzed overnight against PBS buffer using Slide-A-Lyzer dialysis cassettes (3.5 K MWCO, Life Technologies) and concentrated to the desired concentration using Amicon® Ultra Centrifugal Filters (10 kDa MWCO, Sigma). Particle size and zeta potential were measured using a Zetasizer Advance (Malvern Panalytical) at 25 °C and a scattering angle of 173°. Encapsulation efficiency was determined using the Quant-itTM RiboGreen RNA Assay Kit following the manufacturer’s protocol, with quantification performed on a Cytation 3 Cell Imaging Reader (BioTek).

Isolation of Primary FAPs

FAPs were isolated from the hindlimb muscles of mice (5–10 weeks old) using magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) [23]. Muscles were dissected, minced, and digested for 1.5 h at 37 °C in DMEM containing 0.2% (w/v) collagenase type II and 2.5 U/mL Dispase II. Digestion was stopped by adding Ham’s F-10 supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer, centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min at 4 °C, and resuspended in staining buffer (0.5% BSA and 2 mM EDTA in PBS). The suspension was then filtered through a 35 μm strainer. Cells were incubated at 4 °C for 45 min with biotin-conjugated antibodies targeting CD31 (BioLegend, cat. no. 102503; 1:150), CD45 (BioLegend, cat. no. 103103; 1:150), and integrin α7 (Miltenyi, cat. no. 130-101-979; 1:10). After centrifugation, cells were incubated with streptavidin beads (1:30) for 15 min at 4 °C and passed through an LD column (Miltenyi, cat. no. 130-042-901) for negative selection. The remaining cells were labeled with a biotin-conjugated Ly-6A/E (SCA-1) antibody (BioLegend, cat. no. 122504; 1:75) for 20 min at 4 °C, followed by streptavidin beads (1:30) for 10 min. Positively labeled SCA-1 + cells were enriched using an LS column (Miltenyi, cat. no. 130-042-401). Finally, the purified cells were filtered through a 35 μm strainer and prepared for downstream cell culture assays.

FAPs and C2C12 Culture and Differentiation

Cultures were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2. FAPs were expanded in growth medium (DMEM with 10% FBS and 1X penicillin-streptomycin) on laminin (10 µg/mL) and collagen I (5 µg/mL)-coated plates. Adipogenic differentiation was induced by switching to differentiation medium (DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1X penicillin-streptomycin, 0.5 mM 3-isobutyl-1-methylxanthine, 0.25 µM dexamethasone, and 1 µg/mL insulin). C2C12 myoblasts were expanded in growth medium (DMEM with 10% FBS and 1X penicillin-streptomycin) on laminin (10 µg/mL) and collagen I (5 µg/mL)-coated plates. Differentiation was induced by switching ~ 80% confluent cells to differentiation medium (DMEM with 2% horse serum and 1X penicillin-streptomycin).

mRNA LNP Administration and Dose Selection

Intramuscular mRNA LNP injections were performed in multiple experimental conditions using either cre-LNP for transgene expression validation or W7a-LNP for therapeutic evaluation. A dose of 10–15 µg per injection was selected based on previous SARS-CoV-2 mRNA LNP vaccine and CRISPR-Cas9 mRNA LNP muscular dystrophy studies [36, 37], which demonstrated efficient mRNA delivery in skeletal muscle.

Intramuscular cre-LNP Administration

Ai14-TdTomato mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and a single subcutaneous dose of Ethiqa XR (extended-release buprenorphine) was administered on the day of the procedure for pain management. The TA muscle was injected intramuscularly with 15 µg/30 µL cre-LNP, while 30 µL of PBS vehicle was injected into the contralateral TA muscle. Mice were euthanized at week 2 for histological analysis.

W7a-LNP Administration in Uninjured Supraspinatus Muscles

C57Bl/6 J mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and a single subcutaneous dose of Ethiqa XR was administered on the day of the procedure for pain management. The left forelimb was positioned on a 2 mm elevated surface, extended, and externally rotated to have the palm facing upward. Mouse fur overlying the supraspinatus muscle was removed with Nair. A 1 cm linear incision was made to the skin and the supraspinatus muscle was injected intramuscularly with 10 µg/30 µL W7a-LNP, while 30 µL of PBS vehicle was injected into the contralateral supraspinatus muscle, and incision was sutured. For the single-injection cohort, mice received injections at week 0 and were euthanized at week 4 for histological analysis. For the multiple-injection cohort, mice received injections at week 0, 2, and 4, then were euthanized at week 8 for histological analysis.

Glycerol Injuries and W7a-LNP Administration

C57Bl/6 J mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and a single subcutaneous dose of Ethiqa XR was administered on the day of the procedure. 50 µL of 50% (v/v) glycerol in saline was injected intramuscularly into the tibialis anterior (TA) muscle to induce injury [23]. After 24 h, the TA muscles were injected with 2.5 µg/30 µL recombinant human WNT7A (PeproTech; cat. no. 120-31-15UG), 10 µg/30 µL W7a-LNP, or 30 µL PBS. Mice were euthanized on day 14 for histological analysis.

Immunocytochemistry Staining

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 20 min at room temperature. Samples were washed three times with 1X PBS and incubated in blocking/permeabilization buffer (5.0% goat serum, 2.0% bovine serum albumin, 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS) overnight at 4 °C. The following primary and secondary antibodies were used for immunocytochemistry in this study: Anti-perilipin-1 (Cell Signaling Technology; cat. no. 9349S; 1:200); MF20 (DSHB; cat. no. AB 2147781; 1:100); human Wnt-7a/b antibody (R&D Systems; cat. no. AF3008; 10 µg/mL); goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; cat. no. A11008; 1:500); goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; cat. no. A32723; 1:500); rabbit anti-goat Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; cat. no A11078; 1:500). Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; cat. no. 62249, 1:1,000) was used to stain nuclei.

Immunohistochemistry Staining

TA and supraspinatus muscles were dissected and frozen in liquid nitrogen-chilled methylbutane. 10 µm sections were obtained from the frozen muscle using a cryostat. Tissue sections were fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min at room temperature, then washed three times with 1X PBS and incubated using blocking/permeabilization buffer (5.0% goat serum, 2.0% bovine serum albumin, 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 1 hour at room temperature. The following primary and secondary antibodies were used for tissue immunohistochemistry in this study: Anti-perilipin-1 (Cell Signaling Technology; cat. no. 9349S; 1:200), anti-laminin (Sigma Aldrich; cat. no. L9393; 1:100), and goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; cat. no. A11008; 1:500). Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; cat. no. 62249, 1:1000) and Alexa Fluor 647 Phalloidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific; cat. no. A22287; 1:1000) were used to stain nuclei and F-actin, respectively. Tissue sections were also processed for routine Oil Red O and Picrosirius red staining.

Imaging and Image Analysis

Images were captured using a Leica Microsystems THUNDER DMi8 microscope and processed with LAS-X software. Quantitative image analysis was performed using ImageJ/FIJI.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. Two-tailed t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post-hoc analysis was performed depending on the number of comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

W7a-LNPs Demonstrate Excellent Size Uniformity and Complete mRNA Encapsulation

WNT7A promotes myofiber hypertrophy [25, 26], MuSC expansion [24, 27], and suppresses FAP-induced fatty infiltration [23] (Fig. 1A), highlighting its therapeutic potential for muscle regeneration and disease treatment. However, recombinant WNT7A (rWNT7A) protein therapy faces significant translational challenges, including stability, delivery efficiency, and bioavailability [30–32]. To overcome these limitations, we designed W7a-LNP to enable endogenous muscle cells to transiently produce native WNT7A, acting as “in vivo protein factories” (Fig. 1A).

W7a-LNPs demonstrate excellent size uniformity and complete mRNA encapsulation. (A) Schematic illustration of W7a-LNP delivery and function. Upon uptake by endogenous muscle cells, W7a-LNP enables transient production and secretion of native WNT7A protein, affecting local MuSCs, myofibers, and FAPs. (B) Characterization of mRNA LNPs used in this study, including W7a-LNP (three batches) and cre-LNPs (two batches)

To achieve this, we formulated W7a-LNPs using a lipid composition of SM-102, DOPE, cholesterol, and DMG-PEG2000 at an optimized molar ratio (50:10:38.5:1.5) and complexed them with in vitro-transcribed WNT7A mod-RNA using a microfluidic device for precise and reproducible assembly. The resulting W7a-LNPs had a mean diameter of 75.83 nm, a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.0940, and an encapsulation efficiency of 97.53% at 570.45 µg/mL total mRNA (Fig. 1B). Notably, W7a-LNPs remained stable for over 6 weeks at − 80 °C in 10% sucrose (Supplementary Fig 1), following a storage protocol similar to Moderna’s SARS-CoV-2 vaccine [38]. The z-average diameter remained below 100 nm, and the PDI stayed well below 0.3, indicating minimal aggregation and a uniform particle population (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Efficient mRNA LNP Delivery Drives Transgene Expression in Muscle Cells in vitro and in vivo

To assess the feasibility of our LNP platform for use in skeletal muscle applications, we delivered cre mRNA LNPs (characteristics summarized in Fig. 1B) to primary FAPs isolated from Ai14-TdTomato (TdT) reporter mice, a model in which Cre-mediated recombination activates TdT expression (Fig. 2A). After 24 h in culture, both 1 and 5 µg/mL cre mRNA LNP achieved nearly 100% recombination efficiency, as indicated by strong TdT expression in FAPs (Fig. 2B, C). These results confirm that LNP-mediated mRNA delivery is highly efficient for driving functional gene recombination in primary muscle-resident cells.

To further evaluate our LNP platform in vivo, we administered a single intramuscular injection of cre mRNA LNPs (15 µg/30 µL) into the TA muscle of Ai14-TdTomato mice. 2 weeks post-injection, the treated muscle exhibited a significant increase in TdT + myofibers, with over 50% of myofibers expressing TdT, while the PBS vehicle-treated contralateral muscle showed no TdT expression (Fig. 2D, E). These findings demonstrate the feasibility of localized mRNA delivery in skeletal muscle and confirm that LNP-encapsulated mRNA can be effectively delivered, translated, and drive gene recombination in vivo.

To further confirm that LNP delivery results in functional protein expression, we treated C2C12 cells with 2 µg/mL W7a-LNP for 24 h and observed a marked increase in WNT7A staining intensity compared to vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 2F, G). While vehicle-treated cells exhibited low baseline signal, likely due to endogenous expression, the enhanced signal in the W7a-LNP group supports successful mRNA uptake and translation. Together with the observed functional effects in vitro, these results validate that W7a-LNP induces increased WNT7A production and activity in muscle cells.

W7a-LNP Suppresses FAP Adipogenesis in vitro

Building on our demonstration of efficient mRNA delivery and translation in muscle cells, we next investigated whether W7a-LNP could replicate the anti-adipogenic effects of recombinant WNT7A in vitro [23]. To test this, we isolated primary FAPs from the skeletal muscles of C57Bl/6 J mice, expanded them in growth media for 4 days, and then switched to adipogenic differentiation media with either vehicle (PBS), rWNT7A (200 ng/mL; positive control), or W7a-LNP at 1, 10, or 100 ng/mL (Fig. 3A). Our prior work established that 200 ng/mL of recombinant WNT7A effectively suppresses FAP adipogenesis [23]. Perilipin immunostaining on day 11 revealed that W7a-LNP at both 10 and 100 ng/mL significantly reduced adipogenesis, matching or exceeding the inhibition achieved by rWNT7A (Fig. 3B, C).

(B) Representative images of perilipin-labeled FAPs. Scale bar: 100 µm. (C) Perilipin area normalized by cell number. One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc. n = 8/group. * p < 0.05; ** p < 0.01. To ensure that the effect is due to WNT7A mRNA and not LNP formulation or nonspecific mRNA translation, we repeated the experiment and compared W7a-LNPs’ ability to suppress FAP adipogenesis against cre mRNA LNP. Here, we used cre mRNA LNP as a control because it encodes a nuclear recombinase not expected to influence adipogenesis and serves as a reference mRNA-loaded LNP with no known activity on this pathway. cre mRNA-LNP-treated FAPs showed no reduction in perilipin + adipocytes compared to vehicle, whereas W7a-LNP significantly reduced adipogenesis relative to both vehicle and cre-LNP groups (Fig. 3D, E). This supports that the observed anti-adipogenic effect is specific to WNT7A mRNA and not due to nonspecific effects of LNP delivery or mRNA translation. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that W7a-LNP effectively recapitulates rWNT7A’s anti-adipogenic function in vitro, supporting its potential as a therapeutic strategy to modulate FAP behavior.

W7a-LNP Increase Fusion and Myotube Size in vitro

Having established that W7a-LNP suppresses FAP adipogenesis, we next examined its effects on myotube formation and growth. WNT7A promotes myofiber growth by activating the non-canonical AKT/mTOR pathway [25]. To determine whether W7a-LNP replicates this effect, we expanded C2C12 myoblasts for 3 days, and then induced differentiation in the presence of either vehicle (PBS), rWNT7A (50 ng/mL; positive control) [25, 27, 29], or W7a-LNP at 1, 10, or 100 ng/mL for 3 additional days (Fig. 4A). As expected, rWNT7A significantly increased myotube size compared to the vehicle control (Fig. 4B, C). Notably, W7a-LNP at both 10 and 100 ng/mL produced comparable increases in myotube size, demonstrating that W7a-LNP effectively enhances myotube growth in vitro. Treatment with cre and luciferase mRNA LNPs had no effect on myotube size, supporting that the observed increase is specific to WNT7A mRNA (Supplementary Fig. 2).

To further determine whether W7a-LNP impacts proliferation and fusion, we quantified total nuclear counts and the fusion index (Fig. 4D, E). W7a-LNP significantly increased fusion index at all doses compared to vehicle, while rWNT7A did not have a significant effect on fusion (Fig. 4D). Total nuclear counts were significantly elevated in the rWNT7A-, 10 ng/mL-, and 100 ng/mL-W7a-LNP conditions (Fig. 4E). These results suggest that W7a-LNP may promote both myoblast expansion and enhanced myoblast fusion, which together contribute to increased myotube size under these conditions.

W7a-LNP Promotes Myofiber Hypertrophy Without Inducing Fibrosis in vivo

Given that W7a-LNP promotes myofiber hypertrophy in vitro, we next investigated whether it elicits similar effects in vivo without negatively impacting muscle quality. To test this, we injected W7a-LNP (10 µg/30 µL) into the supraspinatus muscle of mice, with the contralateral muscle receiving vehicle as a control. Mice received either a single injection, with analysis at 4 weeks, or three injections over 4 weeks, with analysis at 8 weeks (Fig. 5A). A single injection had no detectable effect on myofiber size at 4 weeks. However, repeated W7a-LNP administration significantly increased myofiber size at 4 weeks post-final injection, as quantified by minimum Feret’s diameter, suggesting that sustained W7a-LNP delivery is required for inducing hypertrophy (Fig. 5B, C). Picrosirius red and hematoxylin/eosin (H&E) staining revealed no signs of fibrosis, abnormal muscle morphology, and inflammation, indicating that repeated W7a-LNP administration does not promote fibrotic remodeling or tissue damage (Fig. 5D, Supplementary Fig. 3). These findings confirm that repeated W7a-LNP delivery promotes muscle hypertrophy without compromising tissue integrity, supporting its therapeutic potential in muscle degeneration.

W7a-LNP Efficacy in Glycerol-Injured Muscle Depends on Delivery Timing and Myofiber Viability

To assess the efficacy of W7a-LNP in an acute injury setting, we used a glycerol injury model, which induces myofiber necrosis through osmotic stress and primes endogenous FAPs for adipogenic differentiation, leading to transient fatty infiltration [39]. One-day post-injury, we administered 2.5 µg/30 µL rWNT7A, 10 µg/30 µL W7a-LNP, or 30 µL PBS vehicle to the injured TA muscles. As expected, the vehicle-treated control group exhibited robust perilipin and Oil Red O (ORO) staining, confirming extensive fatty infiltration (Fig. 6A, B). rWNT7A treatment reduced fatty infiltration compared to controls (Fig. 6C, D); however, W7a-LNP did not significantly reduce fatty infiltration (Fig. 6C, D). Furthermore, we observed high variability in response among W7a-LNP–treated muscles, with some showing reduced IMAT while others were indistinguishable from controls. This discrepancy may be due to differences in protein availability, as recombinant WNT7A is immediately active upon injection, whereas W7a-LNP relies on cellular uptake, mRNA translation, and subsequent protein secretion, which may be influenced by the injury environment. The variability suggests that factors such as injury severity, dosage, or the timing of administration may influence therapeutic outcomes.

To test whether residual glycerol impacts LNP stability, we performed DLS analysis after incubating W7a-LNPs in PBS, 25% glycerol, or 50% glycerol for 10 or 30 min. We observed an increase in z-average particle diameter with increasing glycerol concentration (Supplementary Fig. 4), likely due to altered hydration or solvent viscosity effects. However, the PDI remained low across all conditions, indicating that LNPs remained monodisperse and did not aggregate. These data suggest that while glycerol may influence apparent particle size, it does not drastically compromise LNP integrity.

An alternative explanation is that glycerol injury acutely ablates myofibers, eliminating the “biofactory” required for WNT7A production following LNP delivery. To test this, we performed an in vivo experiment in which W7a-LNPs were administered 4 days post-injury (Fig. 6E), a time point when regenerating myofibers begin to reappear [40, 41]. In this setting, W7a-LNP significantly reduced intramuscular fatty infiltration (Fig. 6F, G) and increased muscle wet weight (Fig. 6H), supporting the notion that effective delivery and function require the presence of viable, transfectable myofibers.

Discussions

The objective of this study was to evaluate the feasibility and therapeutic potential of W7a-LNP, a lipid nanoparticle-based mRNA delivery platform designed to enable in vivo production of native WNT7A. While recombinant WNT7A protein has shown promise in promoting muscle hypertrophy and inhibiting fatty infiltration, its clinical translation is limited by challenges in delivery efficiency, production cost, and scalability. mRNA-LNP platform offers a viable alternative by enabling transient, localized production of therapeutic proteins directly within target tissue. Given WNT7A’s combined role in mitigating intramuscular fatty infiltration and muscle atrophy, we hypothesized that W7a-LNP could serve as a scalable, cost-effective strategy to overcome the limitations of recombinant protein therapy. Our findings demonstrate that W7a-LNP effectively recapitulates key functions of recombinant WNT7A in vitro, reducing FAP adipogenesis while promoting myofiber hypertrophy. In vivo, repeated W7a-LNP delivery induced muscle hypertrophy without fibrosis, supporting its safety and efficacy in uninjured skeletal muscle. However, in an acute glycerol injury model, the effects of early W7a-LNP administration on fatty infiltration were variable, with some animals responding favorably while others showed no significant improvement. Delayed delivery at 4 days post-injury significantly reduced fatty infiltration, supporting the importance of timing and target cell availability for therapeutic efficacy. Together, these results provide proof-of-concept that W7a-LNP can be leveraged to transiently reprogram the muscle environment in a context-dependent manner.

In this study, we demonstrated that W7a-LNP effectively recapitulated the key functions of recombinant WNT7A in vitro, suppressing FAP adipogenesis (Fig. 3) and increasing myofiber size (Fig. 4). These results indicate that muscle cells can take up W7a-LNP, translate WNT7A mRNA, and secrete functional protein capable of modulating both FAP and myofiber function. Using a cre-LNP system, we showed that mRNA-LNP delivery enables efficient transgene expression in both primary FAPs in vitro and myofibers in vivo, with robust TdT reporter activation in both settings (Fig. 2). This highlights the feasibility of localized mRNA delivery for skeletal muscle applications.

We observed a dose-dependent effect on FAP adipogenesis, with 100 ng/mL W7a-LNP exerting a stronger anti-adipogenic response than 1 ng/mL (Fig. 3). Notably, 10–100 ng/mL W7a-LNP suppressed adipogenesis more consistently than recombinant WNT7A, as reflected by reduced variability in adipogenic inhibition (Fig. 3). Whether WNT7A promotes reversion of mature adipocytes to FAP-like states remains an open question. While intriguing, this mechanism is outside the scope of the current study, which focuses on W7a-LNP delivery.

Similarly, we observed a robust fusion and hypertrophic response in myofibers starting at 10 ng/mL W7a-LNP, further supporting the dose-dependent efficacy of W7a-LNP in enhancing myofiber differentiation and growth (Fig. 4). This suggests that WNT7A levels produced from transfected cells can be modulated by mRNA dose, providing a tunable strategy for regulating both FAP and myofiber differentiation and hypertrophy. However, the actual amount of WNT7A protein secreted remains to be quantified. Future studies will determine these levels to assess whether W7a-LNP achieves therapeutically relevant WNT7A concentrations comparable to recombinant protein treatment.

To evaluate the in vivo efficacy of W7a-LNP, we tested both single and multiple intramuscular injections (Fig. 5). A single injection had no significant effect on myofiber hypertrophy, whereas multiple injections were required to induce hypertrophy, likely due to the transient nature of mRNA expression necessitating repeated dosing to sustain WNT7A levels. We administered three injections over 4 weeks (every 2 weeks) to allow sufficient time for muscle recovery between transfections. Following muscle injury, such as needle puncture, regeneration typically completes within 10–14 days, and the tissue returns to a homeostatic condition by week 4 [40, 41]. Evaluating tissues 4 weeks after the final injection ensured we captured stable hypertrophic outcomes, not transient regenerative or inflammatory effects. This may explain why multiple injections resulted in a mild but significant hypertrophic effect, whereas a single injection had no detectable effect. These findings suggest that dose and delivery frequency are critical parameters to optimize, particularly for chronic degenerative conditions. Despite the need for dose refinement, repeated W7a-LNP administration did not induce fibrosis or other observable pathological changes, supporting its safety and tolerability in skeletal muscle. Altogether, these results support the safety and feasibility of mRNA-LNP technology for muscle regeneration.

A limitation of this experiment is that we cannot directly compare the hypertrophic effects of W7a-LNP to rWNT7A, as a positive control was not included (Fig. 5). Such benchmarking would require a dedicated study with optimized protein dose, timing, and delivery frequency, which was beyond the scope of this initial screen. Our goal was to test whether transient mRNA delivery alone could induce myofiber hypertrophy using a limited, clinically relevant regimen. In this context, vehicle served as an appropriate reference control to evaluate biological activity. Prior studies, including von Maltzahn et al., have demonstrated hypertrophy with rWNT7A [25], but under different experimental conditions. Importantly, our in vitro studies showed that W7a-LNP, but not cre/luciferase-LNP controls, significantly increased myotube size, supporting that the observed effects are specific to WNT7A mRNA and not due to LNP delivery or nonspecific mRNA translation (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. 2). Future head-to-head comparisons will be important for dose optimization and contextualizing W7a-LNP efficacy.

Our cre-LNP study demonstrated that W7a-LNP delivery remained localized within the injected muscle without detectable leakage into the contralateral muscle (Fig. 2D, E). However, the broader biodistribution profile to other organs remains to be determined. Previous LNP studies have shown that nanoparticles often accumulate in the liver and kidneys, which serve as primary sites of clearance [42, 43]. Within the injected muscle, our findings of broad TdT expression are consistent with recent reports showing that mRNA LNPs can disseminate locally following intramuscular injection. For example, Shi et al. [44] demonstrated robust mRNA expression in muscle fibers after intramuscular SARS-CoV-2 mRNA LNP vaccination [44], and Chen et al. [45] reported widespread Cre recombination in muscle following intramuscular cre-LNP injection [45]. Similarly, Kenjo et al. [37] observed extensive dystrophin restoration using Cas9 mRNA LNPs delivered intramuscularly [37]. This broad distribution may reflect passive diffusion of LNPs through the extracellular matrix, as well as proximal-to-distal trafficking of mRNA within multinucleated syncytium of myofibers. Given these dynamics, future studies should assess W7a-LNP distribution in these tissues and other non-target organs to determine potential systemic and off-target effects. Further optimizing the dose and delivery parameters may help minimize unintended accumulation in other organs while maintaining effective WNT7A expression in skeletal muscle.

Intramuscular delivery of recombinant WNT7A significantly reduced IMAT following acute glycerol injury (Fig. 6), as we reported previously [23]. However, we observed variability in response to injury following W7a-LNP administration, with some animals exhibiting reduced fatty infiltration while others showed no significant change (Fig. 6). This suggests that the therapeutic effects of W7a-LNP may be influenced by injury severity, host-specific factors, or delivery timing. To investigate whether residual glycerol interferes with LNP stability, we performed DLS analysis and found that although particle diameter increased with higher glycerol concentrations, polydispersity remained low, suggesting the LNPs remained stable and monodisperse (Supplementary Fig. 3). Since glycerol ablates all multi-nucleated myofibers, which are presumed to perform the bulk of protein synthesis, administering W7a-LNP one day post-injury may not have allowed for sufficient uptake, translation, and secretion of WNT7A. To test this, we administered W7a-LNP at 4 days post-injury, when regenerating myofibers begin to re-emerge [40, 41]. In this setting, W7a-LNP significantly reduced fatty infiltration (Fig. 6F, G), supporting the idea that viable myofibers are required for effective mRNA LNP–mediated protein production and function. In clinically relevant degenerative conditions like rotator cuff injuries, where myofibers remain viable despite undergoing atrophy [19, 46], LNP-based delivery may be more effective due to sustained cellular uptake and protein production. Further optimization of the LNP formulation and mRNA structural elements may be needed to improve LNP uptake and sustained expression of the therapeutic protein in injured muscle. Additionally, the effects of W7a-LNP on immune cells remain unknown, and future studies should investigate how immune modulation may influence its therapeutic potential in skeletal muscle regeneration.

In conclusion, we demonstrated W7a-LNPs successfully promote myofiber hypertrophy without inducing fibrosis while reducing the adipogenic potential of FAPs. By transiently reprogramming the muscle microenvironment, this approach holds potential for mitigating muscle atrophy and pathological fatty infiltration in degenerative conditions. These findings support LNP-mediated delivery of WNT7A mRNA as a viable, scalable, and cost-effective alternative to recombinant protein therapy for localized muscle treatment. Further research is needed to optimize dosing, delivery timing, biodistribution, LNP formulation, RNA structural elements, and long-term therapeutic outcomes for clinical translation.
