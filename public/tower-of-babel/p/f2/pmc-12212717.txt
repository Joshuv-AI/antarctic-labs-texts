A retrospective cohort study to examine the association between the persistence of abdominal pain after cholecystectomy and ejection fraction on HIDA scan in patients with biliary dyskinesia
Authors: Baldeep Kaur Mann, Janpreet Singh Bhandohal, Ishaan Kalha, Brian Jean
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsThe persistence of pain in patients who underwent cholecystectomy primarily for biliary dyskinesia has been associated with gall bladder ejection fraction (GBEF) found on hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid (HIDA) scan. Cholecystectomy is a definitive treatment for cholelithiasis but is questionable in biliary dyskinesia. During the postoperative follow-up, gastroenterologists and surgeons often found that some patients with biliary dyskinesia continue to experience biliary pain even after surgery. This study aims to investigate whether the value of GBEF found on the HIDA scan predicts the persistence of pain after cholecystectomy in patients with biliary dyskinesia (EF <35% on HIDA scan).MethodsIt is a retrospective cohort study conducted at Kern Medical by recruiting patients from November 2019 to October 2022 in consecutive manner. It is a single sample study consisting of post-cholecystectomy biliary dyskinesia patients with dichotomous outcomes, i.e., persistent typical recurrent epigastric or right upper quadrant pain with/without meals versus no pain. The primary outcome was the presence of pain 30 days after the cholecystectomy. Univariate analysis was performed with some of the bivariate indicators and continuous predictors, which contained fewer missing elements and had more variation.ResultsUnivariate logistic regression suggested that HIDA GBEF as a continuous variable does not suggest a relationship with pain after surgery (p = 0.3951). A ROC analysis suggested a cutoff of HIDA GBEF = 16 (AUC = 0.60, Specificity = 0.5455, Sensitivity = 0.7333).ConclusionOur study does not support the relationship between GBEF found on HIDA scan to improved outcomes after the surgery. But larger controlled trials comparing outcomes of the patients with biliary dyskinesia who did and didn't undergo cholecystectomy are needed to look for other factors.Keywords: Post-cholecystectomy syndrome, Biliary dyskinesia, Gall bladder ejection fraction, PCS, HIDA, Cholecystectomy

Introduction

Womack et al. described post-cholecystectomy syndrome (PCS) in 1947 for the first time, which can occur in 5%–47% of patients undergoing cholecystectomy [1]. PCS and surgical complications after cholecystectomy cause a substantial financial burden in health care [2]. HIDA scan is a valuable tool often used by clinicians to evaluate biliary pain without cholelithiasis or common bile duct (CBD) dilatation. Biliary dyskinesia is a central differential in these cases if GBEF is less than 35% [3]. Cholecystectomy is the definitive treatment for cholelithiasis but is questionable in biliary dyskinesia [4]. During the postoperative follow-up, gastroenterologists and surgeons often find a subset of patients with biliary dyskinesia, who continue to experience biliary pain even after surgery. Usually, in these cases, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDS), hyoscine, or opioids are used to alleviate symptoms of these challenging patients, as no major studies are available for guiding their management. This is the first retrospective study to investigate whether the value of EF found on the HIDA scan predicts the persistence of pain after cholecystectomy in patients with biliary dyskinesia and whether the lower EF values are associated with increased chances of persistence of pain after cholecystectomy. This study is an attempt to figure out whether EF found on HIDA scan has any clinical relevance in the outcome of biliary dyskinesia or some other unknown factors are responsible for pain in this entity and whether it is a wise approach to decide about cholecystectomy solely on the basis of GBEF.

Methods

It is a retrospective cohort study conducted at Kern Medical, the area's only Level II Trauma Center and teaching hospital. Patients from both inpatient and outpatient settings were recruited from January 2020 to October 2022 in a consecutive manner with International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes involving acute cholecystitis (K81, K81.0, K81.1, K81.2, K81.9), PCS K91.5 and biliary dyskinesia K82.8. We found 3288 patients and 1531 patients were removed as they were duplicates (Fig. 1). All those patients who got cholecystectomy for primarily biliary dyskinesia were enrolled even though they may have discovered gallstones or polyps on histopathology. The manuscript was written based on the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) checklist. It is a single sample study consisting of post-cholecystectomy biliary dyskinesia patients with dichotomous outcome, i.e., persistent typical recurrent epigastric or right upper quadrant pain with/without meals versus no pain.

Variables

The primary outcome was the presence of pain 30 days after the cholecystectomy in patients with biliary dyskinesia. The secondary outcomes were pain improvement with dietary modification or medications (psychotropic drugs, ursodeoxycholic acid/UDCA, opioids, PPI, hyoscine, acetaminophen, and Ibuprofen). The exposure of the study was to undergo cholecystectomy. The GBEF found on the Tc-99 HIDA scan was the predictor variable.

Potential confounders or covariables collected were age, gender, body mass index (BMI), urgency of cholecystectomy (urgent or routine), type of indication of surgery (only biliary dyskinesia or concomitant gall stones or other etiology like polyps), presence of diabetes mellitus, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), history of cirrhosis, previous history of non-biliary surgery, type of cholecystectomy (open vs. laparoscopic), immediate post-surgery complications, duration of pain before surgery, history of psychiatric illness, elevated bilirubin, liver enzymes alkaline phosphatase, lipase, presence of helicobacter pylori (H.pylori), histopathology of the gallbladder, liver ultrasound, computed tomography (CT), esophagoduodenoscopy (EGD) and colonoscopy findings, dilated CBD on imaging, visualization time of liver, CBD, gallbladder, duodenum on HIDA, and duodenogastric reflux on HIDA. The qualitative variables were grouped into proportions in patients with the presence or absence of pain after cholecystectomy (Table 1). Unfortunately, H. pylori was not included in the analysis because there were 12 missing values and only one positive observation. Only one patient had undergone colonoscopy, and a tubular adenoma was found on histopathology. None of the patients had dilated CBD on imaging.

At our facility, as a part of standard protocol for HIDA scan was to record right upper quadrant images, following intravenous administration of 6.2–8.8 mCi of technetium 99 m Choletec/mebrofenin, for about 1 h under the gamma camera and note the uptake in the liver, CBD, gallbladder and small bowel. Kinevac (cholecystokinin analog), usually used for GBEF calculation, was not available to our Center, and thus patients were given fatty meals to eat instead. Shortly after that (about 10 min), dynamic images were obtained of the right upper quadrant for another 30–60 min, GBEF recorded, and activity noted in the small bowel on post fatty meal images if there was no activity on initial images. Diagnostic criteria used at our facility for biliary dyskinesia was GBEF of less than 35%, and almost all these patients were evaluated for possible cholecystectomy by general surgery instead of conservative measures. The only patients who did not undergo surgery were poor surgical candidates or those waiting for cholecystectomy.

Bias

The study can have selection bias as we enrolled only those patients with biliary dyskinesia who underwent cholecystectomy and did not compare with those with biliary dyskinesia who did not undergo surgery. Only one patient with biliary dyskinesia was deceased and could not be followed. Thus, there was minimal information bias due to the loss of follow-up. The confusion bias due to confounding was minimized using the multivariate technique, i.e., logistic regression.

Study size

The sample size needed was 26 for the dichotomous endpoint, one sample study for the anticipated incidence of PCS in biliary dyskinesia patients to be 20% and is already known to be 5% in various populations. (Type I alpha error 0.05, Type II beta error 0.2, power 80%) (Sample Size Calculator (clincalc.com)).

Statistical methods

The collected data will be compiled on a Microsoft Excel sheet. The variables identified as continuous variables were studied using descriptive statistics and were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and quartiles. The p-value of ≤0.05 will be considered significant for the study. The missing data underwent deletion via dropping variables if the data was missing more than 60% of the observations and those variables were insignificant. Logistic regression was used to control numerous confounders, and it gave the adjusted odds ratio, which is controlled and adjusted for multiple confounders or covariates. All the covariates collected were not included in univariate logistic regression either because of a high percentage of missing values or lack of variation. Univariate analysis was performed with some of the bivariate indicators and continuous predictors, which contained fewer missing elements and had more variation (Table 3).

Results

Outcome data

Fifteen patients reported residual pain after surgery (57.69%) after an average time period of 15.13 months after cholecystectomy. The patients described the pain as stabbing or dull, or burning; located in the epigastric area or right upper or left upper quadrants; some had radiation towards the back; aggravating factors like eating; relieving factors like putting pressure on the belly, holding fist against the belly, milk, walking, antacid, postural change, the passage of bowel. The average NRS of pain was 6.13 reported by the patients during their clinic visits. The pain was the predominant symptom associated with heartburn, nausea, and burping. Eight patients described that there were no relieving factors, and each pain episode lasted for 2–40 min. Patients tried acetaminophen, ibuprofen, hyoscine, omeprazole, and gabapentin with some or little relief. None of the patients were using choleretic drugs or opioids. Dietary modifications like avoiding lemon and eating less fried foods improved the pain in 1–2 patients. One patient mentioned that losing weight made the pain better.

Univariate logistic regression suggested that HIDA GBEF as a continuous variable does not suggest a relationship with pain after surgery (p = 0.3951) (Fig. 2). A ROC analysis suggested a cutoff of HIDA GBEF = 16 (AUC = 0.60, Specificity = 0.5455, Sensitivity = 0.7333). As an indicator variable, HIDA GBEF remained statistically insignificant (p = 0.1558). Univariate logistic regression also suggested a lack of evidence of an association with diabetes mellitus (p = 0.3582). Univariate logistic regression indicated a lack of statistical significance for all other predictor variables except for HbA1c as a continuous variable (p = 0.07). A ROC analysis resulted in a suggested cutoff threshold of 6.7 (AUC = 0.696, Specificity = 0.50, Sensitivity = 0.9286). This may be an anomaly because only one patient had A1c ≥ 6.7 with persistent pain after cholecystectomy. The odds ratio was approximately 12:1, indicating that those with A1c < 6.7 were 12 times more likely to experience pain. The model estimate and odds ratio indicated that the odds of pain decrease as HbA1c increases. The small sample size makes anything regarding HbA1c questionable.

Discussion

In the past, authors have emphasized clarifying the indications for cholecystectomy in patients with Functional Gallbladder Disorder (FGBD) and the need for prospective studies comparing patients who underwent cholecystectomy versus conservative measures [5]. Various cut-offs ranging from 35% to 50% have been found and suggested by various authors to define biliary dyskinesia [4], and therefore a cut-off of GBEF of less than 35% was taken in our study to diagnose biliary dyskinesia. Following Rome IV criteria diagnostic criteria for biliary dyskinesia have been revised to minimize the number of patients undergoing invasive procedures. However, the GBEF is no longer required to diagnose biliary dyskinesia but remains an essential supportive criterion [6]. In a systematic review by DiBaise and Oleynikov, GBEF was reported as a valuable diagnostic tool in predicting positive postoperative outcomes in treating functional gallbladder disorder [7]. However, the authors of a recent review concluded that currently, available studies about GBEF as a predictor of functional gallbladder disorder had poor study design preventing any standard recommendations [4]. A meta-analysis conducted in 2003 by Delgado-Aros et al. concluded that the data do not support the use of GBEF to select patients with suspected functional biliary pain for cholecystectomy [8].

We conducted a retrospective cohort study to evaluate the effect of GBEF and various other variables that may affect outcomes of patients having biliary dyskinesia. In our study, we found that the GBEF found on the pre-operative HIDA scan as a continuous variable does not suggest a relationship with the persistence of pain after cholecystectomy in patients with biliary dyskinesia. However, it has been found that at our facility, surgeons went ahead with cholecystectomy if the GBEF was found to be <35% and if the patient was a good surgical candidate. The ROC analysis suggested a cut-off of 16% for EF on the HIDA scan with low sensitivity and specificity. Therefore, it is hard to relate this value of 16 with the outcome. Univariate logistic regression also suggested a lack of evidence of an association of diabetes mellitus or other predictor variables with the persistence of pain, with the exception of HbA1c as a continuous variable (p = 0.07). The patients who were having residual pain were having lesser HbA1c, which again may indicate that pain was not related to diabetes mellitus.

The normal hepatobiliary scan demonstrates concentration of the tracer in the liver within a few minutes, then begins to concentrate in the biliary ducts and gallbladder, with the gallbladder usually visualized by 45–60 min and visualization of the gallbladder later than 30–60 min after administration of the radiopharmaceutical is considered delayed [9]. In our study, the mean visualization time for liver, CBD, gallbladder, and duodenum were 5, 11.17 ± 5.27, 17.26 ± 9.87 and 21.41 ± 14.53 min, respectively. In the past, authors have found that jejunal visualization time was more delayed (>1 h) in patients with PCS than the duodenal visualization time [10]. In our study, the small bowel was not visualized in the initial 60 min before the consumption of fatty meals in two patients.

Some authors may relate the PCS with gastroduodenal reflux, peptic ulcer disease, irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), pancreatitis, bile salt-induced diarrhea, retained calculi, bile leak, biliary strictures, cystic duct remnant, stenosis, dyskinesia of the sphincter of Oddi, psychiatric and neurologic disorders [11]. Duodenal-specific visceral hyperalgesia has been cited as the cause of PCS [12]. Bisgaard et al. found that the incidence of PCS was higher in patients who had experienced intense acute postoperative pain [13]. Duodenogastric reflux of bile acid may be responsible for the persistence of symptoms after cholecystectomy [14]. Therefore, we included all these as covariables (the post-surgical complications, history of psychiatric illness, and gastroduodenal reflux on HIDA) in our study as they may affect the outcome. As of note, none of our patients had CBD dilated on prior imaging. Some authors hypothesize that the spasm of the sphincter of Oddi in the absence of a gall bladder reservoir leads to pressure increase, which may partly explain the development of PCS [15]. That is why manometry and sphincterotomy are considered for the evaluation of PCS. At the same time, other authors do not support ERCP and sphincterotomy for these patients as they did not help relieve pain [16].

Several factors, like diabetes mellitus, cirrhosis, myotonic dystrophy, IBS, celiac disease (deficiency of CCK), and obesity, are associated with impaired gallbladder emptying [17]. Thus, we included HbA1c, history of cirrhosis, and BMI as covariates as well. Studies have shown in the past that gallbladder contractility is reduced in diabetic patients more than in nondiabetic patients [18]. However, our results showed no association of GBEF to diabetes mellitus. In fact, PCS patients were found to have lower HbA1c. Filip et al. reported that elevated serum bilirubin and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) in combination with abdominal ultrasound accurately predicted PCS in 90% of cases [19]. Elevated pre-operative alkaline phosphatase and prior abdominal surgery were significantly associated with PCS [20]. However, in our study, univariate logistic regression, the conjugated bilirubin, and previous non-biliary surgery did not correlate with PCS. The mean values found in our study for pre-operative alkaline phosphatase, post-operative alkaline phosphatase, AST, ALT, and lipase were 94.4 units/L, 94 units/L, 34.12 units/L, 44 units/L, 118.15 units/L respectively, but due to lack of variation were not included in the analysis. Opioids have been found to be precipitating pain in post-cholecystectomy patients [21], but none of our patients with PCS were using opioids. Some authors related that Helicobacter pylori positivity following cholecystectomy might contribute to PCS [22]. On the other hand, some authors suggest the low importance of H. pylori infection in determining this syndrome [23]. Nevertheless, only one of our patients with PCS had positive H. pylori in our study, but we were missing H. pylori status for 12 patients. Choleretic drugs, including UDCA and/or traditional Chinese herbs, have been reported to relieve postoperative pain [24] significantly. However, none of our patients used these drugs. A low-fat diet does not influence the improvement of symptoms after cholecystectomy [25]. One of our patients found that reducing weight by ingesting a low-fat diet decreased her symptoms.

The internal validity of our study was maximized by controlling the confounding variables, and there was only one patient who was not available for follow-up. To improve the external validity/generalizability, we included clear eligibility criteria, collected multiple variables about the population group, and were able to collect an appropriate sample size. The study reliability depended on intraobserver and interobserver variability of radiologist reading for calculating EF on the HIDA scan and if patients did not answer the questions correctly or differently each clinical visit. The patient answers were found to be consistent during their sequential clinic visits about their pain, which is indicative of the reliability of the study.

The limitation of our study was that we did a single sample study because most of the patients at our facility were exposed to cholecystectomy. It would be ideal to conduct a prospective randomized controlled trial where some of the patients with biliary dyskinesia are managed with conservative measures instead of cholecystectomy and then compare both groups for the persistence of pain. Our study is a retrospective cohort, which tends to have selection bias.

Conclusion

Our study found that GBEF found on a pre-operative HIDA scan does not suggest a relationship with the persistence of pain after cholecystectomy in patients with biliary dyskinesia. There was also a lack of evidence of an association between other variables (age; gender; BMI; duration of pain prior to surgery; previous non-biliary surgery; post-surgery complications; history of psychiatric illness; the presence of duodenogastric reflux; conjugated bilirubin levels; liver, gallbladder, CBD, duodenal visualization time on HIDA scan; and diabetes mellitus) with the persistence of pain. Most practitioners expose their patients to surgery when they find GBEF less than 35%. We need more randomized controlled trials where one arm is exposed to conservative measures to see if surgery made a difference significantly in these patients. One of the explanations is that there may be other unknown factors playing a role in the persistence of pain after cholecystectomy.
