Integrated single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analysis reveals mCAF-SPP1⁺ macrophage-T cell crosstalk shaping immunosuppressive niches in colorectal cancer liver metastasis
Authors: Mengyao Chang, Mingyang Feng, Yanna Lei, Yingyuan Wang, Liangliang Bai, Yongsheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer liver metastasis (CRC-LM) carries poor prognosis and responds poorly to current immunotherapies. Liver metastases often display T-cell exclusion, but how stromal and myeloid circuits jointly shape T-cell states across primary and metastatic sites, and how to capture this in robust prognostic markers, remains unclear.MethodsWe integrated multi-cohort single-cell RNA sequencing datasets of primary colorectal cancer and CRC-LM to define tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs), cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and T-cell subsets, and used CellChat and NicheNet to map ligand–receptor networks and downstream programs. Visium spatial transcriptomics with cell2location deconvolution delineated stromal–immune architectures in CRC-LM. A stromal–myeloid gene signature derived from key populations was evaluated in TCGA-COAD/READ and GSE39582, and a GCC-targeted second-generation CAR-T model was used to test the impact of recombinant SPP1 and MIF on tumour killing.ResultsIn this study, we found that CRC-LM were enriched for stress-response (TSTR) and exhausted (Tex) T cells and featured a recurrent mCAF–SPP1⁺ TAM–Tex/TSTR niche. Spatial analysis revealed a stereotyped three-layer organization at the tumour–stroma interface, with outer mCAF belts, inner SPP1⁺ TAM–rich zones and intervening TSTR/Tex bands. Communication analysis identified SPP1–CD44, FN1–CD44 and MIF/CXCL12 as dominant stromal–myeloid axes promoting T-cell retention, stress and exhaustion. In GCC-directed CAR-T assays, recombinant SPP1 and MIF impaired tumour clearance, and a three-gene stromal–myeloid signature (KLF2, ZBTB20, ARL4C) robustly stratified recurrence and disease-free survival independent of TNM stage.ConclusionsIntegrating single-cell, spatial and functional data, we define a stromal–myeloid niche that underlies immune confinement in CRC-LM and highlight actionable ligand–receptor pathways. Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks third in global incidence and second in mortality [1, 2]. The liver is the most common site of distant metastasis, and epidemiological studies show that ~ 50% of patients develop liver metastasis during the disease course. Among them, 15–25% present with synchronous liver metastasis at diagnosis, and an additional 18–25% develop metachronous lesions within 3–5 years [3, 4].

Current management of colorectal cancer liver metastasis (CRC-LM) centers on multidisciplinary evaluation followed by curative-intent hepatectomy whenever feasible. Patients with unresectable disease receive chemotherapy combined with targeted therapy based on molecular profiling, whereas immunotherapy remains largely restricted to the MSI-H/dMMR subgroup and overall efficacy is still limited [5, 6]. Increasing evidence suggests that liver metastases are characterised by immune exclusion and spatial confinement, accompanied by a shift of T cells from memory/effector states toward stress- and exhaustion-related phenotypes [4, 7]. The liver is intrinsically tolerogenic, with hepatic endothelial and resident myeloid circuits capable of dampening T-cell activation through immunoregulatory programs (e.g., IL-10/TGF-β) and related suppressive pathways [5, 8]. Persistent antigen exposure and microenvironmental constraints in metastatic lesions can further bias infiltrating T cells toward stress and exhaustion states, a process increasingly linked to redox imbalance in tolerogenic tissues [9, 10]. Meanwhile, stromal remodelling and myeloid enrichment—particularly CAF-driven ECM deposition and TAM-associated cues—may jointly restrict T-cell access and reinforce dysfunctional reprogramming at the tumour–liver interface [11–14].

Indeed, prior single-cell and spatial studies in CRC and metastatic settings have repeatedly implicated matrix-active/myofibroblastic CAF states, myeloid-skewed macrophage programs (including SPP1⁺ TAMs), and stress/exhausted T-cell phenotypes as recurrent features of immunosuppressive microenvironments [7, 8, 13, 15, 16]. However, these observations are frequently presented as parallel layers rather than a connected evidence chain, leaving unresolved how CAF–myeloid programs are organised into reproducible interface-localised neighbourhoods and which stromal–myeloid signalling axes are spatially plausible drivers of the T-cell stress-to-exhaustion transition in CRC-LM [11]. Given the limited benefit of immune checkpoint blockade in most CRC-LM cases, defining the stromal–myeloid determinants of immune exclusion and T-cell dysfunction is clinically relevant for identifying rational combination strategies and actionable targets to re-sensitise metastatic lesions to immunotherapy [7, 12, 16, 17].

Methodologically, single-cell RNA sequencing combined with spatial transcriptomics enables paired primary–liver metastasis comparisons of cellular states and interactions. Yet, studies explicitly linking CAF- and TAM-driven communication pathways with T-cell stress–exhaustion programs across anatomical sites remain limited, highlighting the need for a cross-scale stromal–immune circuit map to identify key steps in T-cell reprogramming and potential intervention points [4, 8, 18].

Here, we addressed these gaps by integrating multiple single-cell RNA sequencing datasets of primary CRC and CRC-LM with Visium-based spatial transcriptomics and CellChat/NicheNet analyses [19, 20]. Focusing on myofibroblastic CAFs, SPP1⁺ tumour-associated macrophages, and stress/exhausted T-cell states, we aimed to map how their spatial organization contributes to immune confinement. Based on the key stromal–myeloid components in this niche, we further sought to derive a concise gene signature and to evaluate its prognostic value in independent CRC cohorts.

Materials and methods

Single-cell data sources and preprocessing

We integrated three publicly available single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets.

[1] GSE166555 from GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), containing 13 primary CRC samples (GSM5075660, GSM5075662, GSM5075665, GSM5075666, GSM5075668, GSM5075670, GSM5075672, GSM5075674, GSM5075676, GSM5075678, GSM5075680, GSM5075682, GSM5075683).

[2] GSE178318, which includes paired primary and liver metastatic samples from six individuals (COL07, COL12, COL15, COL16, COL17, COL18).

[3] Stanford_P6198 from the Stanford DNA Discovery dataset (dbGaP: phs001818; https://dna-discovery.stanford.edu/research/datasets/), comprising seven CRC-LM samples (5784, 5915, 6198, 6335, 6593, 6648, 8640).

In total, these datasets provide 19 primary and 13 liver metastatic samples. All data were de-identified and obtained under the ethical approvals and usage terms of the original studies. No new human subjects were enrolled in this work (Fig. 1A).

Quality control and normalization

We processed each dataset using Seurat v4.3.0. Cell-level quality control was performed based on detected gene counts (nFeature_RNA) and mitochondrial transcript proportion (percent.mt). We excluded cells expressing < 300 or > 8,500 genes, or with > 20% mitochondrial content. Doublets were removed using DoubletsFinder v2.0.3.

Batch correction and single-cell integration

After merging the three datasets, we obtained 211,594 cells, and 198,973 cells remained after QC. We retained expressed genes, performed log-normalization and scaling, and used highly variable genes for PCA. We then applied Harmony v1.2.3 to correct batch effects at the dataset/sample level. A shared nearest-neighbor graph was constructed on the Harmony embeddings, followed by clustering (FindNeighbors/FindClusters, resolution = 0.4) and UMAP visualization.

Differential expression and cell-type annotation

We used FindAllMarkers (Wilcoxon test, default parameters) to identify cluster-specific DEGs. Cell-type annotation was guided by canonical markers and published references. When naming inconsistencies arose between datasets, we relied on marker profiles and biological coherence for manual harmonization.

Monocle3 trajectory analysis and GO enrichment

We performed lineage reconstruction for T cells and TAMs using monocle3 within the UMAP space aligned to the Seurat–Harmony embedding. We constructed a cell_data_set from Seurat counts and metadata, ran preprocess_cds (≈ 50 PCs) and reduce_dimension, and applied Louvain clustering on the fixed Harmony–UMAP coordinates. Trajectory graphs were learned using learn_graph, and root states were assigned to Tcm (for T cells) and FOLR2⁺ Reg-TAMs (for TAMs) using order_cells. To suppress spurious branches, the minimum branch length was set to 20 in learn_graph_control. Visualization was performed with plot_cells, showing cell-type and pseudotime maps, and exported as PDFs.

CellChat analysis

To compare T–TAM and T–CAF interactions between primary CRC and CRC-LM, we performed CellChat v1.6.1. Each dataset was preprocessed using Seurat (normalization, variable-gene selection, scaling, PCA/UMAP). We generated combined T–CAF and T–TAM objects, grouping by cell type and separating Primary vs. LM when needed.

CellChat objects were built using CellChatDB.human, followed by filtering and overexpression testing to identify candidate ligand–receptor (L–R) pairs. Communication probabilities and pathway-level activities were computed (computeCommunProb, computeCommunProbPathway), and weighted networks were constructed for centrality analysis (aggregateNet, netAnalysis_computeCentrality).

We visualized results using dot plots for pathway strength, circle and role scatter plots for network structure, and bubble plots for directional signalling. Both T–CAF and T–TAM pipelines used identical parameters to ensure comparability across datasets and conditions.

NicheNet analysis

We used NicheNet (nichenetr v2.2.1) with Seurat to model ligand–receptor and ligand–target relationships in LM vs. Primary.

T-cell, TAM, and CAF annotations were merged into a unified Seurat object. Stress-T and Tex cells were combined as receiver cells. DEGs were identified by comparing these cells to other T cells (p_val_adj < 0.05, avg_log2FC > 0), and intersected with NicheNet’s ligand–target matrix to define the geneset of interest.

Sender populations included SPP1⁺ inflam-TAMs, SPP1⁺ LA-TAMs, and mCAFs. We loaded NicheNet v2 resources (ligand–target matrix, LR network, weighted networks) and used nichenet_seuratobj_aggregate with LM as condition of interest and Primary as reference (geneset = “up”, lfc_cutoff = 0.4).

Prior knowledge–based L–R pairs (e.g., SPP1–CD44, MIF–CD74/CXCR4, CXCL12–CXCR4, FN1–CD44, NECTIN2–TIGIT, CD99–CD99, APP–CD74) were incorporated to focus the analysis.

Gene enrichment analysis

We performed over-representation analysis (ORA) on DEG lists. Gene symbols were converted to Entrez IDs using org.Hs.eg.db, and duplicates were removed. Background genes were defined as all detectable genes per comparison.

Gene sets were compiled from MSigDB Hallmark, C2 (KEGG, Reactome), immune-related signatures, and ReactomePA.

Statistics used a hypergeometric test with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction (p.adjust < 0.05). Gene set size filters were set to minGSSize = 10 and maxGSSize = 500.

Visualization used enrichplot::dotplot, where dot size reflects gene count, color indicates FDR, and the x-axis shows GeneRatio.

Spatial transcriptomics and Cell2location

We collected four CRC-LM Visium slices from GSE217414 (GSM6716963, GSM6716964, GSM6716965, GSM6716967).

Spatial data were processed using cell2location v0.1.4. Visium matrices were loaded with scanpy.read_visium, mitochondrial genes were flagged, and counts were retained for QC.

Using LM scRNA-seq as reference, ENSEMBL IDs were unified and filtered under the “very-permissive” rule. Cell-type signatures were estimated via RegressionModel (negative binomial), controlling for batch/sample effects.

After QC and convergence, reference and spatial data were aligned by common genes. Squidpy was used to estimate per-spot cell numbers to set N_cells_per_location (with adult tissue–based detection_alpha). Cell2location was then run with fixed seeds, and posterior q05/q95 abundance estimates were used for conservative visualization.

Construction of a metastasis-associated prognostic gene signature

We focused on four tumour microenvironment cell states enriched in CRC-LM: mCAFs, SPP1⁺ TAMs, Tstr and Tex. Candidate genes were taken from the single-cell marker lists of these four populations and intersected with differentially expressed genes between liver metastases and primary tumours. Bulk expression data and clinical information were obtained from TCGA-COAD/READ (training cohort) ( https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga) and GSE39582, GSE14333(validation cohort). Expression values were normalized and log2-transformed.

Using the TCGA cohort, we fitted a LASSO–Cox proportional hazards model on the metastasis-associated gene set with RFS/DFS as the endpoint (R package “glmnet”). Ten-fold cross-validation was used to select the optimal lambda. Three genes with non-zero coefficients (KLF2, ZBTB20, ARL4C) were retained and defined as the SC_signature. For each patient, an SC_signature score was calculated as the sum of the expression of these genes weighted by their Cox coefficients. Patients were divided into high- and low-risk groups by the median score in each cohort. RFS/DFS was compared between groups using Kaplan–Meier curves and the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox models were used to assess the association between SC_signature (high vs. low) and recurrence risk, adjusting for age (> 60 vs. ≤ 60 years), sex (male vs. female) and TNM stage (III–IV vs. I–II).

Cell lines and reagents

HCT116 and HEK293T cells were obtained from the State Key Laboratory of Biotherapy, Sichuan University, and cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and penicillin–streptomycin. Recombinant human SPP1 (Nearshore Protein, C544) and MIF (Solarbio, P02738) were prepared according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Lentiviral production and CAR-T cell generation

Lentiviral particles were produced by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with the GCC CAR transfer plasmid, psPAX2, and pMD2.G. Viral supernatants were collected at 48–72 h, filtered, and concentrated.Human PBMCs were activated with CD3/CD28 magnetic beads for 48 h and then transduced with the concentrated lentivirus. Cells were expanded for 7 days in medium supplemented with IL-2, IL-7, and IL-15. CAR expression was confirmed by flow cytometry before use.

In-vitro cytotoxicity assay

HCT116 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at 1 × 105 cells/well in 500 µL medium and incubated overnight. CAR-T cells were added with or without SPP1 (1 µg/mL) or MIF (100 ng/mL). The selected doses were guided by prior in-vitro CRC studies using comparable concentrations of recombinant OPN/SPP1 and MIF to assess functional effects [21–25]. Three groups were tested: SPP1, MIF, and UTD, each in triplicate.Co-cultures were run for 48 h (E: T = 1:2) or 72 h (E: T = 1:4). Live tumour cells were quantified by flow cytometry. Residual tumour burden was calculated relative to the UTD group.

Statistical analysis

All analyses except spatial deconvolution were performed in R 4.4.3; Cell2location was run in Python 3.9. Two-sided tests were used, and FDR was controlled by the Benjamini–Hochberg method (significance defined as FDR < 0.05 unless otherwise stated). Cox proportional hazards regression was applied, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Kaplan–Meier curves compared by log-rank test, and time-dependent ROC curves (timeROC) to assess discrimination, with 3-year AUC as the primary metric. Group comparisons in cytotoxicity assays were evaluated using one-way ANOVA.

Result

Single-cell atlas of primary CRC and liver metastasis

To construct a comprehensive immune–stromal atlas of colorectal cancer across anatomical sites, we integrated three single-cell RNA-sequencing datasets (GSE166555, GSE178318, and Stanford_P6198). After stringent quality control (Fig. 1A), a total of 198,973 high-quality cells were retained for downstream analyses. Batch correction using Harmony achieved effective mixing across cohorts and platforms, with primary and liver metastasis (LM) samples well interspersed in the low-dimensional space (Fig. 1B). The absence of dataset-driven “islands” indicates successful cross-study integration and suggests that major transcriptional variation arises from biological states rather than technical biases. A small number of clusters displayed relative enrichment for either primary or LM samples, indicating site-associated programs that were further examined in later analyses (Supplementary Fig. 1A).

Coloring cells by patient identity revealed that each cluster comprised contributions from multiple individuals, with no cluster dominated by a single subject (Supplementary Fig. 1B). This pattern further supports robust integration across patients, sampling platforms and sequencing batches.

Using canonical marker genes, we annotated eight major lineages, including B cells (CD79A, CD79B, CD19), endothelial cells (CLDN5, vWF), epithelial cells (EPCAM, KRT19, KRT8), fibroblasts (COL1A1, FAP, PDGFRA), mast cells (TPSAB1, TPSB2, KIT), myeloid cells spanning TAM/DC/neutrophil-like states (S100A8, S100A9, CD68, MACRO), NK/T cells (CD3D, TRAC, NKG7, IL7R), and plasma cells (IGKC, IGHK1, JCHAIN) (Figs. 1 C–D).

Despite notable inter-individual variation in cell-type composition, all major lineages were reproducibly detected across patients (Figs. 1E-F). These findings indicate substantial heterogeneity in the tumour microenvironment (TME) across CRC cases, while preserving a shared cellular framework on which site-specific immune and stromal programs are superimposed.

T cells shift from memory/effector states toward stress–exhaustion in CRC liver metastasis

We reclustered the NK/T compartment in the UMAP space and removed non-T lineages, proliferative clusters, and low-quality or doublet cells, yielding a refined T-cell atlas. The final annotation included Tcm (central memory T), Teff (GZMK⁺ effector-like T, non-terminal T cells distinct from cytotoxic effector T cells), NK cells, Tstr (stress-response T), Tex (exhausted T), CXCL13 + Tfh-like (CXCL13 + follicular helper like T cells), and Tregs (regulatory T cells) (Figs. 2A–B). These subsets displayed distinct molecular signatures: Teff cells expressed high levels of GZMK and were enriched for translation and ribosome biogenesis; Tstr cells upregulated HSPA1A/HSPA1B/HSPA6, reflecting enhanced unfolded-protein response and proteostasis pathways; Tex cells showed coordinated induction of PDCD1, LAG3, TIGIT, HAVCR2, and the transcription factor TOX, together with apoptosis and protein-folding stress programs. CXCL13 + Tfh-like cells were marked by CXCL13 and ICOS, while Tregs expressed FOXP3, IL2RA (CD25), CTLA4, and CCR8 (Supplementary Fig. 1–2).

Comparison of T-cell composition revealed a pronounced increase in Tstr and Tex populations in CRC-LM, whereas Tcm and Teff dominated in primary tumours (Fig. 2E). Monocle3 trajectory reconstruction identified two terminal branches emerging from a Tcm/early-effector backbone: one progressed through Tstr to Tex, and the other led to Tregs. Although Tregs formed a distinct terminal state, they were not enriched in LM; instead, Tstr and Tex constituted the dominant LM-associated endpoint populations and occupied the highest pseudotime regions (Figs. 2C–D).

GO and Reactome enrichment of LM-derived T cells highlighted selective upregulation of UPR, proteostasis, and apoptosis pathways, whereas primary-site T cells were enriched for OXPHOS and translation-related processes, indicating a metabolic shift from mitochondrial activity toward stress-adaptive protein-quality control (Fig. 2F). This stress-to-exhaustion bias mirrors observations from pan-cancer single-cell studies, where Tstr accumulation in metastatic sites correlates with immune suppression and reduced responsiveness to checkpoint blockade.

Together, compositional changes, transcriptional state positioning along the continuum, and pathway enrichment converge on a consistent conclusion: the LM microenvironment drives a redistribution of T cells from memory/early effector states toward stress-response and exhaustion phenotypes.

Macrophage reprogramming across primary CRC and liver metastasis

The classical M1/M2 dichotomy provides limited resolution for understanding the continuous spectrum of macrophage phenotypes within the tumour microenvironment. Guided by canonical markers and functional enrichment profiles, we identified eight transcriptional states of tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs), including antigen-presenting, regulatory/tissue-resident–like, interferon-responsive, SPP1⁺ inflammatory and lipid-associated states, invasion- and hypoxia-responsive states, and stress-responsive states (Figs. 3B–C; Supplementary Fig. 3). These states were well integrated across datasets, with uniform mixing in the UMAP embedding and no evidence of batch-driven clustering. RNA count distributions were also comparable across samples (Fig. 3A).

The composition of TAM subsets differed markedly between anatomical sites. Liver metastases were enriched for antigen-presenting TAMs and SPP1⁺ populations, whereas primary tumours contained relatively more FOLR2⁺ regulatory/tissue-resident–like macrophages and lipid/lysosomal-associated subsets (Fig. 3D). Functional programs further reflected these differences: antigen-presenting TAMs displayed a tolerogenic phenotype characterised by high HLA-II expression and induction of PD-L1 and IL-10, while SPP1⁺ TAMs upregulated extracellular matrix and adhesion modules—including SPP1, VCAN, THBS2 and CD44-associated pathways—suggesting a remodelling, immunosuppressive state. These patterns are consistent with previous reports that SPP1⁺ TAMs accumulate in hypoxic or necrotic regions and associate with poor prognosis, whereas FOLR2⁺ tissue-resident macrophages localise to stromal niches and often correlate with improved survival [26, 27].

Trajectory reconstruction using Monocle3 placed FOLR2⁺ regulatory and antigen-presenting TAMs, together with interferon-responsive cells, near the proximal axis, while SPP1⁺ inflammatory, SPP1⁺ lipid-associated and invasion/hypoxia-responsive TAMs localised toward the distal end of the continuum, forming a transition from steady-state and presenting phenotypes toward suppressive and remodelling programs; this proximal–distal organisation reflects relative transcriptional similarity rather than inferred ontogeny or lineage direction. (Fig. 3F). Terminal lipid/lysosomal-associated macrophages exhibited foam-cell–like characteristics, including elevated lipid accumulation and enhanced lysosomal activity, consistent with a dysfunctional-like phenotype. Genes associated with pseudotime progression were enriched for pathways related to adhesion and cytoskeletal remodelling, oxidative and redox metabolism, and regulators of cell–environment interactions and growth (Fig. 3G). These signatures collectively delineate a dynamic transcriptional continuum in which macrophages progressively acquire remodelling, suppressive and stress-adaptive features within the metastatic microenvironment.

Comparison of LM and primary TAM transcriptomes revealed widespread induction of translation and protein-synthesis pathways in metastasis, consistent with increased production of secreted mediators. Nutrient- and amino-acid–stress programs also increased, suggesting adaptation to the metabolic constraints of the metastatic niche. In addition, inflammatory modules centered on LPS and TNFα–NF-κB signalling were strongly activated (Fig. 3E). Although several T cell–labeled gene sets appeared in enrichment analysis, their presence likely reflects shared inflammation-induced transcriptional circuits rather than contamination.

Together, these findings indicate that TAMs in CRC liver metastases undergo coordinated metabolic, inflammatory and matrix-remodelling reprogramming. This evolution may contribute to the shift of T cells toward stress and exhaustion phenotypes observed in the metastatic immune microenvironment.

Heterogeneity of CAFs and their regulatory impact on T cells

Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) constitute a major stromal component that can both form dense extracellular matrix barriers and secrete cytokines that modulate immune infiltration. Reclustering of fibroblasts yielded two dominant subsets, matrix CAFs (mCAFs) and immune-regulatory CAFs (iCAFs) (Figs. 4 A–B). Compositionally, primary tumours exhibited a more balanced distribution of mCAFs and iCAFs, whereas liver metastases were characterised by a strong predominance of mCAFs and a marked reduction of iCAFs (Fig. 4C). This pattern aligns with prior observations that mCAFs arise largely from hepatic stellate cells or portal fibroblasts, which rapidly convert into α-SMA–high myofibroblasts upon exposure to tumour-derived signals such as TGF-β or CXCR4 ligands, thereby promoting CRC liver metastasis [28, 29]. Correspondingly, LM-associated mCAFs showed increased expression of ECM genes including COL1/3/11, FN1, POSTN, and THBS2. These findings suggest that therapeutic strategies targeting mCAFs or fibrosis may help “soften” the metastatic niche and facilitate immune-cell infiltration.

CAF-mediated modulation of T-cell states

To compare CAF-T-cell communication between primary CRC and liver metastases, we performed CellChat analysis under each condition. Primary tumours showed dominant signalling from iCAFs to Tcm and Teff cells, whereas liver metastases displayed globally amplified communication redirected toward mCAF-Tstr/Tex interactions (Fig. 4D). Several ligand–receptor axes were markedly elevated in LM, including MIF-(CD74 + CXCR4/CD44), CXCL12-CXCR4, ECM (FN1, COL1/4/6)-CD44, NECTIN2-TIGIT, and THBS1/2-CD47. Notably, MIF, CXCL12 and ECM pathways supported Tcm/Teff cells in primary tumours but shifted toward Tstr/Tex in metastases. HLA-II–associated antigen-presenting pathways were also more broadly engaged in LM (Figs. 4G–H), and ligand–receptor expression patterns confirmed matched upregulation on CAFs and corresponding T-cell subsets (Figs. 4E–F).

These rewired circuits reflect a coordinated remodelling of CAF-driven communication. MIF and CXCL12 shift from promoting the migration and retention of memory/effector T cells to instead attracting and anchoring stress- and exhaustion-prone T cells [30]. The ECM–CD44 axis transitions from a permissive matrix scaffold to a densified and restrictive network that traps dysfunctional T cells. In parallel, NECTIN2–TIGIT and THBS1/2–CD47 introduce additional inhibitory layers that dampen TCR signalling and cytotoxic activity [31]. Collectively, CAF-T cell signalling in LM is characterised by a redirection from iCAF-Tcm/Teff to mCAF-Tstr/Tex and a pronounced amplification of suppressive pathways, forming a matrix–immune suppressive module centered on mCAFs. This architecture aligns with established roles of MIF–CD74/CD44–CXCR4, CXCL12–CXCR4, ECM–CD44, and TIGIT/CD47 signalling in immune exclusion and metastatic progression.

TAM-mediated modulation of T-cell states

We next compared ligand–receptor communication between tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs) and T-cell subsets. Overall signalling strength was substantially higher in liver metastases (LM) than in primary tumours, with interactions shifting from Tcm/Teff toward effector, Tstr and Tex populations (Fig. 5A–C and A–C). This pattern mirrors the CAF→T rewiring observed earlier and reflects a coordinated transition toward an “antigen presentation preserved, retention increased, inhibition elevated → deeper exhaustion” state.

Three major ligand–receptor axes dominated TAM–T communication in LM: SPP1–CD44, FN1 (ECM)–CD44, and MIF– (CD74 ± CXCR4/CD44). The ECM–CD44 pathways reflected strengthened spatial anchoring, whereas the MIF axis added both chemotactic and suppressive cues. Correspondingly, Tex and Tstr cells in LM upregulated CD44, CXCR4, and CD74, forming a matched “receptor poised, pathway elevated” configuration (Fig. 5C). SPP1⁺ TAMs served as communication hubs in both settings, but their retention- and suppression-associated signatures intensified in LM (Fig. 5E).

Beyond these core axes, LM exhibited broad enhancement of suppressive pathways. LGALS9–HAVCR2 (TIM-3) added an additional inhibitory layer. The targets of HLA-II–CD4 signalling shifted from Tregs in primary tumours to CXCL13 + Tfh like cells in LM, indicating a reoriented helper-cell landscape. Although HLA-I–CD8 signalling increased in LM, its signals were largely received by Tex/Tstr, suggesting that antigen presentation was intact but the downstream response remained “functionally trapped.” By contrast, primary tumours showed a more diffusely distributed TNF–TNFRSF1B network, whereas LM communication became dominated by a smaller set of potent inhibitory axes (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Pathway-level analysis further supported these shifts. Primary tumours were enriched for COLLAGEN, ANXA1, TGFβ, and VCAM/ICAM/JAM modules, forming a structural barrier with baseline suppressive tone. In LM, MHC-I, IFN-II, LCK, and CD70 signalling rose in parallel with suppressive pathways involving FN1/SPP1 (ECM–CD44 retention), MIF, IL10, CXCL, and COMPLEMENT (Fig. 5D). These findings indicate that LM immunosuppression does not result from impaired antigen presentation; instead, enhanced presentation coexists with ECM–CD44–mediated physical confinement and MIF/IL10-driven inhibitory signalling, together fostering the accumulation of low-reactive Tstr and Tex cells.

Collectively, TAM–T communication in LM is characterised by a matrix-anchored and inhibition-amplified network centered on SPP1, FN1, and MIF, whereas primary tumours maintain a more structural and adhesion-based steady-state program. The marked upregulation of CD44, CXCR4, and CD74 on LM T cells matches the ligand redistribution from TAMs, supporting the concept that LM establishes a microenvironment where immune responses initiate but fail to progress, consistent with a suppressed, confinement-driven immune niche.

To test whether SPP1 and MIF affect T-cell cytotoxicity, we generated GCC CAR-T cells and performed in-vitro killing assays. HCT116 tumour cells were co-cultured with CAR-T cells with or without recombinant SPP1 or MIF. At an effector-to-target ratio (E: T) of 1:2 for 48 h, SPP1 showed a clear trend of increasing residual tumour cells. MIF caused a smaller increase. These changes were not statistically significant, which may reflect the short exposure time. We then extended the assay to 72 h and used an E: T ratio of 1:4. Under these conditions, both SPP1 and MIF significantly increased residual tumour survival (Fig. 5G). This indicates that prolonged exposure to these ligands weakens CAR-T killing activity. These results support the CellChat-predicted SPP1–CD44 and MIF–CD74/CXCR4 pathways. They suggest that mCAF and TAM-derived ligands can promote T-cell retention and stress responses, leading to progressive functional decline in CRC-LM.

NicheNet reveals a hierarchical stromal signalling network targeting stress–exhausted T cells in liver metastasis

Building on the broadly enhanced intercellular communication observed by CellChat in CRC liver metastasis (CRC-LM), we next applied NicheNet to dissect ligand–target relationships linking SPP1⁺ TAMs and mCAFs to Tstr/Tex cells. By modeling the probabilistic mapping between ligands expressed by sender populations and gene programs induced in receiver T cells, we quantified the regulatory potential of candidate ligands and identified the top 20 drivers predicted to influence T-cell state transitions (Fig. 6A).

Hierarchical stromal–myeloid signalling network identified by NicheNet. (A) Top 20 ligands ranked by predicted regulatory potential for Tstr/Tex states. (B) Predicted ligand–target modules showing functional clustering into inflammation (IL32), lipid metabolism (APOE), and fibrosis (UTS2). (C) Hierarchical stromal signalling tiers: top-tier drivers (MIF, SERPINE1), mid-tier remodelling factors (VCAN, MMP14, TFPI), and spatially restricted ligands (TNFSF14, CD320). (D) Spatial neighborhood association at spot scale (edge-avg z·z) showing non-random adjacency of mCAF, SPP1⁺ TAM, Tex and Tstr at the tumour–liver interface. (E) Spatial distribution maps showing preferential enrichment of all four cell types along the invasive margin. (F) Representative three-layer niche structure: outer mCAF band, inner Tex/Tstr belt, and SPP1⁺ TAM aggregates at the invasive/necrotic edge

Integrating predicted regulatory potential, expression upregulation in LM, and sender-cell coverage, we delineated a hierarchical stromal signalling network. At the highest tier, MIF and SERPINE1 (PAI-1) were strongly induced in LM, exhibited the highest regulatory scores, and were broadly expressed across SPP1⁺ TAMs and mCAFs, suggesting that they represent key drivers of T-cell stress and functional suppression. A second tier consisted of VCAN, MMP14, and TFPI, which also showed consistent upregulation and intermediate regulatory potential. These ligands may reinforce ECM organization, protease activity, and coagulation–immune coupling to establish and maintain a permissive suppressive niche. A third, more spatially restricted tier included TNFSF14 and CD320, which demonstrated substantial predicted impact but limited sender coverage, consistent with activity confined to specific microdomains (Fig. 6C).

Predicted downstream targets further highlighted functional divergence among ligand modules. MIF, VCAN, and TFPI converged on stress- and inflammation-associated genes such as IL32; CD320 preferentially regulated lipid-metabolic genes including APOE; and SERPINE1 connected to fibrosis-related regulators such as UTS2 (Fig. 6B). Together, these associations suggest that a small set of high-leverage ligands from SPP1⁺ TAMs and mCAFs may cooperate through inflammatory, metabolic, and fibrotic pathways to steer CD4⁺ T cells toward stress and exhaustion phenotypes.

Overall, NicheNet analysis provides coherent computational evidence for a layered stromal signalling architecture, in which top-tier drivers (MIF, SERPINE1) and mid-tier remodelling/conditioning factors (VCAN, MMP14, TFPI) converge on CD44/ CXCR4/CD74 receptor platforms on T cells. This hierarchy aligns with the biased mCAF/SPP1⁺ TAM→Tstr/Tex communication revealed by CellChat (Figs. 4G–H and 5E), reinforcing a model in which stromal–myeloid cues collectively reshape the T-cell landscape in metastatic CRC.

mCAF–SPP1⁺ TAM cooperation establishes a spatial Tex/Tstr niche at the tumour–liver interface

Guided by our earlier findings that mCAFs and SPP1⁺ TAMs jointly shape a suppressive Tex/Tstr “band-like niche,” we next validated this architecture using spatial transcriptomics. Applying cell2location to multiple CRC-LM Visium sections, we mapped mCAFs, SPP1⁺ TAMs, Tex and Tstr cells back to tissue space (Figs. 4G–H; Supplementary Fig. 5A-D). Notably, this analysis operates at the spot/neighbourhood (mesoscale) level and does not infer single-cell physical contacts. Across all samples, these four populations were consistently enriched along the tumour–liver parenchyma interface, whereas normal lobular regions distant from the tumour exhibited weak signals, indicating that immune remodeling is concentrated at the invasive front of metastatic lesions (Figs. 6D–E).

A representative section revealed a reproducible and morphologically distinct three-layer structure. First, mCAFs accumulated on the parenchymal side of the tumour border, forming continuous or semi-continuous fibrotic bands that constitute an “outer stromal barrier.” Second, hotspots of Tex and Tstr tightly abutted the inner edge of this band, forming a continuous or near-continuous belt of CD8⁺ T cells with impaired effector function. This belt extended a few spots into the tumour side, resembling a “rim of functionally compromised T cells” wrapped around the tumour margin. Third, SPP1⁺ TAMs were preferentially positioned at the inner boundary of the Tex/Tstr belt and around focal necrotic areas, forming patchy aggregates that outlined a macrophage-enriched zone adjacent to the invasive front and necrotic edges (Fig. 6F).

Across sections, we observed a consistent boundary-aligned layered pattern at the tumour–liver interface, with modest section-to-section variability in effect size.: Tex and Tstr consistently co-enriched and formed a boundary-aligned band; mCAFs occupied the fibrotic region immediately outside this band; and SPP1⁺ TAMs resided along its inner margin and around the invasive/necrotic interface. Edge-averaged z·z co-enrichment analysis further confirmed that these populations exhibit non-random, mutually adjacent spatial organization near the tumour–liver interface (Fig. 6D). Statistical significance was further assessed by within-section permutations (n = 1000; Supplementary Fig. 5E), indicating non-random neighbourhood organization.

Together, these data provide tissue-scale support for a cooperative model in which mCAFs and SPP1⁺ TAMs establish and maintain a spatial niche that concentrates Tex/Tstr cells and reinforces T-cell stress and exhaustion in CRC liver metastasis.

Construction of a metastasis-associated gene signature derived from key TME cell states

To evaluate the prognostic significance of metastasis-enriched TME populations, we focused on four cell states implicated in CRC-LM: mCAFs, SPP1⁺ TAMs, Tstr, and Tex. Candidate marker genes were obtained from their single-cell signatures and intersected with “LM vs Primary” differential expression. Using the TCGA cohort as a training set, we performed LASSO-Cox modelling and derived a three-gene signature, SC_signature (KLF2, ZBTB20, ARL4C). Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups by the median signature score. Kaplan–Meier curves showed significantly worse RFS/DFS in the high-risk group, with early and sustained separation of the survival curves (Figs. 7Cite E–F; Supplementary Fig. 6A).

Construction and validation of a metastasis-associated prognostic signature. (A) Univariate Cox analysis of SC_signature (KLF2, ZBTB20, ARL4C). (B) Multivariate Cox analysis adjusting for TNM stage, age and sex showing independent prognostic value. (C) Distribution of SC_signature scores in the TCGA cohort. (D) Separation of high- vs low-risk groups by signature score. (E) Kaplan–Meier curves showing worse DFS/RFS in high-risk patients. (F) External validation in GSE39582 showing consistent prognostic stratification

In univariate Cox models, SC_signature (high vs. low) was associated with increased recurrence risk. Importantly, after adjusting for TNM stage (III–IV vs. I–II), age (> 60 vs. ≤ 60), and sex, SC_signature remained significant, indicating that it provides prognostic value independent of standard clinicopathological variables (Figs. 7A–D; Supplementary Fig. 6B). These results demonstrate that the signature captures key metastasis-related biology and enables more precise risk stratification than traditional models.

Discussion

The liver provides a uniquely tolerogenic immune environment, which facilitates the establishment of an immunosuppressive niche during CRC liver metastasis (CRC-LM). Baseline hepatic tolerance—mediated by Kupffer-cell– and sinusoidal endothelium–driven antigen clearance together with tonic IL-10/TGF-β signalling—creates a state in which antigen presentation is “present but inefficient,” driving T cells toward stress and exhaustion phenotypes [8, 32–34].

Our findings refine immune exclusion in CRC-LM from a generic “barrier” phenotype to a reproducible, layered interface microanatomy at the tumour–liver boundary. Rather than simply preventing T-cell entry, the metastatic interface concentrates T cells into a narrow rim enriched for stress/exhaustion programs and positions mCAFs and SPP1⁺ TAMs on either side, creating a confinement zone where antigen presentation may persist but productive effector conversion fails. Prior CRC/CRC-LM studies have largely described SPP1⁺ TAMs, matrix CAF programs and Tex as cell-type enrichments; here we extend these observations by resolving how these components assemble into an ordered interface-level organisation principle. Integrating CellChat/NicheNet with spatial co-enrichment enabled prioritisation of transcriptomically predicted interactions that are spatially feasible at the metastatic boundary. While established here in CRC-LM, whether this architecture reflects a broader liver-metastatic principle remains to be determined.

In this setting, we show that tumour-infiltrating T cells in CRC-LM undergo a marked shift from memory/effector states (Tcm, GZMK⁺Teff) toward stress-response and exhausted states (Tstr/Tex), characterised by induction of PD-1, TIM-3, LAG-3 and TOX, together with activation of UPR, proteostasis and apoptotic programs. This is consistent with recent studies identifying stress-response T cells as heat-shock/proteostasis–driven dysfunctional states overlapping with Tex, with GZMK⁺ T cells representing pre-dysfunctional intermediates [16, 32]. Notably, chemotactic and restrictive signals from MIF and CXCL12 have been shown to confine immune cells and promote immune escape [35]; our findings indicate that in CRC-LM, MIF/CXCL12 signalling preferentially retains stress/exhausted T cells over memory/effector states. Their association with ICB non-response further suggests potential biomarker and therapeutic relevance [36–40]. Conceptually, these observations indicate that T cells in CRC-LM are not simply under-recruited; rather, they are preferentially retained at the metastatic boundary and concurrently biased toward stress/exhaustion programs, consistent with “spatial confinement and functional reprogramming.”

Our macrophage analysis identified a continuum of TAM states—including AP-TAMs, FOLR2⁺Reg-TAMs, IFN-TAMs, SPP1-Inflam/LA-TAMs, and Ly/LA-TAMs—spanning from antigen-presenting/steady-state phenotypes to highly suppressive/remodelling programs. SPP1⁺ TAMs showed strong ECM-remodelling and immunosuppressive programs and were enriched in CRC-LM, consistent with their reported association with T-cell stress/exhaustion and ICI resistance [41, 42]. Conversely, FOLR2⁺ macrophages were more abundant in primary CRC, matching studies showing that FOLR2⁺ tissue-resident macrophages localise to vascular/stromal niches, correlate with CD8⁺ infiltration and better outcomes, and represent a more “steady-state/presenting” phenotype [43]. At the invasive edge of CRC-LM, suppressive SPP1⁺ TAMs diminish presenting and recruitment signals from AP-, FOLR2⁺Reg- and IFN-TAMs, resulting in antigen presentation without effective effector conversion [8].

On the stromal axis, CRC-LM showed increased mCAF abundance and reduced iCAF proportion, coupled with upregulation of COL1/3/11, FN1, POSTN and THBS2. This aligns with reports that metastatic niches—particularly the liver—favor contracted, matrix-producing CAF phenotypes rather than inflammatory iCAFs [44]. CellChat revealed that CAF-T signalling shifted from Tcm/Teff-supportive pathways in primary tumours toward anchoring and consolidating Tex/Tstr in metastases.

Across CAF/TAM-T interactions, FN1–CD44 and SPP1–CD44 (matrix anchoring/retention), together with MIF–CD74/CXCR4 and CXCL12–CXCR4 (directive inhibition/chemotaxis), emerged as dominant pathways in CRC-LM, with NECTIN–TIGIT and THBS2–CD47 adding checkpoint and anti-phagocytic suppression. NicheNet prioritized MIF and SERPINE1 as high-leverage upstream drivers, while VCAN, MMP14 and TFPI may provide matrix/permissive cues. Spatial transcriptomics further revealed stable co-enrichment of mCAFs, Tex/Tstr and SPP1⁺ TAMs, supporting a stromal–immune “spatial confinement + functional reprogramming” model. Consistent CAF–TAM co-enrichment has been reported in CRC, where FAP⁺ CAF–SPP1⁺ TAM co-infiltration associates with immune-excluded stroma and poor PFS [15]. Mechanistically, coupling CellChat/NicheNet with spatial co-enrichment yields insight beyond either modality alone. Spatial adjacency constrains candidate ligand–receptor pairs to interactions that are physically plausible at the metastatic boundary, while downstream target modelling prioritises ligands that best explain the stress/exhaustion programmes observed in T cells. Together, this cross-scale integration supports a mechanistically plausible sequence—matrix anchoring and chemotactic retention followed by inhibitory reprogramming—rather than a descriptive catalogue of signals.

Using single-cell–derived markers, we constructed the SC_signature (KLF2, ZBTB20, ARL4C), with all three genes independently linked to CRC prognosis [45–47]. Clinically, CRC-LM is characterised by limited immunotherapy benefit and high postoperative recurrence. Our findings converge on an actionable theme: matrix anchoring + inhibitory signalling. Dense ECM from mCAFs restricts T-cell infiltration, while SPP1⁺ TAMs apply suppressive/stress pressure, creating a niche where antigen presentation occurs but effector conversion is impaired. Because stress-response T cells are associated with ICI resistance, combination strategies that first reduce stromal anchoring and then restore T-cell effector function may improve tumour infiltration and therapeutic responses [16]. Recent studies of antioxidant- and phenolic-rich plant-derived compounds offer hypothesis-generating examples of redox modulation that may influence macrophage and T-cell stress states in tolerogenic niches [48–50]. SC_signature may be used for high-risk stratification, follow-up planning and clinical trial enrichment, complemented by simplified mIHC scoring of mCAF/SPP1⁺TAM density and T-cell proximity.

Importantly, we provide functional validation of these interactions. Using a GCC-directed CAR-T cytotoxicity model, we demonstrate that recombinant SPP1 and MIF significantly impair CAR-T–mediated tumour killing. This finding establishes that the suppressive signals identified by single-cell and spatial analysis are not merely correlative but exert direct inhibitory effects on engineered T-cell function. These results underscore that both matrix-anchoring (SPP1–CD44) and inhibitory chemokine axes (MIF–CD74/CXCR) can diminish the cytotoxic capacity of adoptively transferred T cells, highlighting translational opportunities to combine stromal or myeloid-targeted therapies with cellular immunotherapy in CRC-LM.

Whether this architecture is CRC-LM-specific or reflects a broader liver-metastatic niche remains an open but testable question. Other liver metastases may share a similar layered geometry at the tumour–parenchyma interface, while differing in the identity or strength of dominant axes (e.g., SPP1/FN1–CD44, MIF–CD74/CXCR4), which can be evaluated in cross-cancer spatial atlases.

This study has limitations. First, as a multi-cohort retrospective analysis, residual confounding remains possible despite batch correction and sensitivity analyses. Second, ligand–receptor and regulatory interactions inferred from CellChat/NicheNet remain largely correlative. Although we functionally validated representative axes (SPP1 and MIF) in vitro, additional functional validation of the ligand–receptor axes, ideally incorporating receptor-level perturbation, is warranted in future work. Third, 10x Visium lacks single-cell spatial resolution; accordingly, our spatial analyses capture spot- and neighbourhood-scale co-enrichment rather than direct cell–cell contact. An inherent limitation of scRNA-seq is that it reflects dynamic transcriptional states rather than definitive cellular origin, precluding firm conclusions regarding lineage versus plasticity. Finally, because our data are derived from CRC-LM, the generalisability of this interface architecture to other liver metastases remains to be validated in cross-cancer spatial datasets.

In summary, we propose that immune exclusion in CRC-LM is best understood as a layered stromal–myeloid confinement unit at the tumour–liver interface, in which mCAFs and SPP1⁺ TAMs cooperate to retain and reprogram T cells toward TSTR/Tex states through matrix-anchoring and inhibitory signalling. This framework offers mechanistic insight for risk assessment and personalised therapies, and provides a resource for investigating stromal–immune axes in metastatic CRC.
