Conversion Total Hip Arthroplasty From a Long Cephalomedullary Nail Through the Direct Anterior Approach
Authors: Wyatt H. Buchalter, Geordie C. Lonza, William K. Crockatt, Carl L. Herndon, Roshan P. Shah, H. John Cooper, Jeffrey A. Geller, Alexander L. Neuwirth, Nana O. Sarpong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Conversion total hip arthroplasty after prior open hip surgery can present with challenges. The direct anterior approach on a standard operating room table allows for the ease of use of fluoroscopy to facilitate hardware removal and implant positioning, accurate determination of leg lengths, and improved operative efficiency. We present a surgical technique to demonstrate a safe and effective method for performing conversion total hip arthroplasty with removal of a long cephalomedullary nail and subsequent conversion through a direct anterior approach on a standard operating room table.

Introduction

Conversion total hip arthroplasty (cTHA) following failed internal fixation with a long cephalomedullary nail (CMN) for an intertrochanteric hip fracture poses a unique technical challenge and increased morbidity compared to primary THA [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Compared to a sliding hip screw, cTHA following failed CMN fixation portends worse outcomes with higher complication rates [8,9]. As the incidence of intertrochanteric hip fractures continues to increase and CMNs have become the more popular fixation option over sliding hip screws, cTHA following failure of CMN fixation will undoubtedly become an increasingly common procedure [10].

As cTHA is often performed through the posterior approach to provide extensile exposure, there is a paucity of literature on the procedure in the context of the direct anterior approach (DAA). The DAA for THA has become more popular in recent years due to its advantages, including shorter hospital stay, earlier functional recovery, and lower dislocation risk [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. The DAA also allows for the ease of use of fluoroscopy to aid in hardware removal and to accurately determine leg lengths [1]. Further, the DAA on a standard operating room table (colloquially referred to as the “off-table” method) allows for the ability to check leg lengths clinically by draping in both extremities [15,16].

Recent studies suggest that anterior-based approaches may be safe and effective for cTHA and may confer many of the same advantages as in the primary setting. However, specific methods and technical pearls for removing longer implants through the anterior approach are not well described. Therefore, we aim to describe the steps for performing cTHA from a long CMN through the DAA using a standard operating room table and multiple incision technique. Patient informed consent was obtained.

Surgical technique

Surgical indications, preoperative evaluation, and imaging

cTHA from prior hip fracture fixation is indicated in the case of failed internal fixation, post-traumatic arthrosis, malrotation of the extremity, or excessive leg length discrepancy. The patient may report pain in the back, groin, or hip, and physical exam may reveal a limp, abnormal limb lengths, limitations in hip motion, and a positive Stinchfield test. The patient’s gait and leg lengths should be closely evaluated. Plain radiographs may reveal hip degenerative joint disease, a significant leg length discrepancy, and a fracture nonunion or malunion as demonstrated by the preoperative images in Figure 1. Standard preoperative labs along with inflammatory markers should be obtained, as well as a fluid aspiration if inflammatory markers are elevated. The patient should be made aware of the increased risk associated with cTHA compared to primary THA, including increased blood loss, longer case duration, and increased risk of complications. If not already completed, plain radiographs of the full-length femur should be obtained to assess for union status, bone stock, nail length, and distal interlocking screw location. An anteroposterior pelvis should be obtained for templating and determination of leg length discrepancy. The nail manufacturer and type are identified to ensure the appropriate removal tools available at the time of surgery, and extraction is planned as previously described by Puri et al. [17]. Anesthetic considerations and patient positioning

The surgery may be performed under either general or neuraxial anesthesia, though complete neuromuscular relaxation is critical, especially in the case of a significant leg length discrepancy. Once anesthesia is induced, the patient is placed supine on a standard operating room table with the prominence of the greater trochanters positioned at the “break” of the table to allow the potential for the legs to drop into hip extension during femoral exposure as previously described [18]. Bumps or other positioning equipment are not required. If not already done in the preoperative area, the patient’s prior surgical scars are marked with a skin marker, so that they may be easily identified once draping is complete. Both legs are prepped and draped into the surgical field to allow for intraoperative assessment of leg length as demonstrated in Figure 2. For patients with a significant leg length discrepancy, the use of a standard table is advantageous for facilitating direct tactile assessment and correction of leg lengths. We prefer to utilize a single-step draping system for this procedure. A larger window is cut into the drape and covered with Ioban (3M, St. Paul, MN) to provide access to the entire femur for removal of the distal interlock screws or in the event that an extensile approach becomes necessary (eg, iatrogenic fracture requiring cable placement). A C-arm is used for intraoperative fluoroscopy and is positioned perpendicular to the operating room table on the contralateral side of the operative extremity.Figure 2Patient set up. Distal interlocking screw removal, DAA, and lag screw removal

First, the 2 distal interlocking screws are identified through the patient’s prior surgical scars, and small percutaneous incisions are made through skin and the iliotibial band. A tonsil clamp is used to remove any impeding soft tissue, and the appropriate screw driver is used to localize each screw sequentially. Fluoroscopy is utilized as needed. A mallet may be used to hit the back of the screwdriver and ensure it is properly seated in the screws prior to backing them out to avoid potential screw stripping, as shown in Figure 3. Broken screw removal sets with reverse threaded extractors and trephine reamers should be available in case the screws are stripped or broken [17].Figure 3Distal interlock screw removal. Due to the posterior and proximal nature of the prior surgical incisions for the nail and the lag screw, a separate standard incision for the DAA to the hip is utilized. We prefer to perform the DAA to the hip through a standard longitudinal incision centered over the mid-portion of the tensor fascia lata muscle as previously described by Rodriguez et al. [19]. You may choose to perform the DAA approach at this time, or after lag screw removal, as described in detail below.

Next, we proceed with accessing the nail for loosening of the set screw and removal of the lag screw. The prior surgical incision proximal to the tip of the greater trochanter is utilized to access the set screw and to later attach the threaded extraction tool. The incision is made through skin and fascia until the tip of the nail can be palpated. Any impeding soft tissue is removed by pushing an entry guide pin into the top of the nail and localizing the nail under fluoroscopy. The nail extractor and cannulated set screw driver are guided along the wire as demonstrated in Figure 4 and seated into the top of the set screw. If there is overgrown bone at the top of the nail, the opening reamer for the nail may be used to better clear a path for extraction, as described by Puri et al. [17]. The set screw is loosened to allow for removal of the lag screw. The location of the lag screw is confirmed under fluoroscopy, and the lag screw is then approached through its separate lateral incision. The iliotibial band is incised and a Bovie is used to remove any impeding soft tissue from the head of the lag screw. Once it is appropriately visualized, and the driver is attached and the lag screw is removed under fluoroscopy, as shown in Figure 5. Alternatively, if initial loosening of the set screw is not possible, some nail systems may allow for breakage of the set screw with removal of the lag screw. In the authors’ experience, lag screws in most CMN systems can be removed without first loosening the proximal set screw as long as secure fixation of the removal instrument can be obtained in the lateral portion of the lag screw. With sufficient counterclockwise torque applied to the lag screw, the interference fit from the set screw can be overcome and the lag screw backed out without difficulty. This approach may save the patient an accessory proximal incision if so desired.Figure 4Intraoperative AP radiographs of the right hip (bottom) with corresponding surgical images (top). Acetabular cup insertion

Once any hardware in the femoral neck is removed, we return to our DAA incision. We recommend leaving the nail in place until femoral exposure is completed for ease of removal and to prevent iatrogenic fracture of the proximal femur during femoral elevation. The neck cut is made with a narrow oscillating saw at the pretemplated level, and a napkin ring cut is made proximal to this to aid in head removal. The napkin ring and the femoral head can be removed with either a corkscrew or a threaded Steinmann pin, whichever is preferred, and anterior and posterior retractors are placed to expose the acetabulum as demonstrated in Figure 6.Figure 6Acetabular exposure.

Acetabular exposure.

The labrum and pulvinar tissues are removed for ease of reamer insertion and for visualization of the medial wall. The acetabulum is reamed under fluoroscopic guidance with a single-reamer technique, 1 mm below the templated cup size. The appropriately sized acetabular shell is impacted into place in the appropriate abduction and version, and positioning is confirmed fluoroscopically. A supplemental screw may be inserted to provide additional fixation. We prefer to place the final liner at this time, and the liner is impacted to engage in the locking mechanism. Femoral exposure, elevation, and nail removal

The femur is approached in standard fashion and the posterior capsule and the conjoint tendon are released to allow for appropriate elevation of the femur as previously described by Rodriguez et al. [19]. The piriformis tendon, if undamaged by the prior nail insertion, may be released as needed for additional femoral elevation. The lateral capsular limb is released from the inside of the greater trochanter to expose the tip of the nail, and the extraction tool is attached. If the tip of the nail is buried within the greater trochanter, it may be exposed by using a burr to remove any overlying bone or the opening reamer corresponding to this nail type and manufacturer [17]. The nail is then removed using a slap hammer as demonstrated in Figure 8; however, if the nail does not have a tight interference fit with the endosteal bone, it can often be removed just by pulling it out with a rongeur. Nail removal can be performed at this stage either through the DAA incision or through the incision proximal to the tip of the greater trochanter, though we feel this is easier through the DAA incision.Figure 8Nail removal using the conical extraction tool, back slap, and slotted mallet (left) and the nail after removal (right).

Lag screw defect occlusion, cementation, and femoral stem insertion

Following nail extraction, the remaining steps for femoral preparation and placement of the femoral component are performed as usual. We prefer to open the canal and lateralize with a boxed osteotome, broach by hand, and calcar plane as necessary, as shown in Figure 9. Trial components are placed, and leg length and stability are assessed clinically by palpating the medial malleoli and externally rotating and extending the hip. Fluoroscopy is used to confirm appropriate component positioning and sizing as shown in Figure 10. The trial components are removed, and final implants are prepared. The decision between cementless vs cemented fixation is based on surgeon preference and considerations should include patient age, bone quality, and medical comorbidities. We prefer in this case to utilize a cemented stem due to the patient’s bone quality. To minimize cement extravasation through the lag screw hole in the lateral wall of the femur and to optimize cement pressurization, the lag screw hole is occluded with a settable, resorbable bone putty (MONTAGE, Abyrx, Stamford, CT) as shown in Figure 11.Figure 9Broaching (left) and calcar planing (right).Figure 10AP radiograph of the right hip (left) and external rotation hip view (right) demonstrating appropriate trial component positioning and sizing. Cementing of the femoral stem is performed in standard fashion using fourth generation cementation technique as previously described by El-Othmani et al [20]. The final stem is impacted into place, cement is allowed to dry, a final trial reduction is performed, and stability and leg lengths are checked again before choosing the final head size. The trial head is removed, the final head is impacted onto the trunnion, and the hip is reduced. Leg lengths and stability are assessed clinically 1 additional time, and final fluoroscopic images are obtained to confirm appropriate leg lengths and no evidence of iatrogenic fracture. The wounds are copiously irrigated and closed in a standard layered fashion. Negative pressure wound therapy dressings are typically not required, although they may be considered in high-risk cases. Postoperative protocol

We prefer to admit the patient postoperatively for overnight monitoring given the complex nature of the cTHA. Typically, patients may be weight-bearing as tolerated without hip precautions.

Discussion

This surgical technique details the steps to perform a cTHA with removal of a long CMN through the DAA on a standard operating room table. cTHA is typically indicated in failed previous hip surgery such as internal fixation, hemiarthroplasty, core decompression, osteotomy, or incision and drainage that results in osteonecrosis, malunion, nonunion, post-traumatic arthritis, leg length discrepancy, or symptomatic or failed hardware, and complication rates of this procedure are higher than those of primary THA [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Complication rates are particularly high in cTHA after CMN, potentially due to diminished proximal femur bone stock and disruption of the abductor mechanism during initial insertion or removal of the nail at the time of conversion [4,8,9,21]. Performing cTHA via the DAA on a standard operating room table allows for a more tactile intraoperative assessment of leg lengths, stability, and soft tissue tension and may improve both operative efficiency and cost-effectiveness by avoiding the expense of a specialized traction table and eliminating the need for personnel to assist with limb positioning [15,16]. Furthermore, use of a standard table may reduce the risk of intraoperative fracture during femoral broaching by allowing the free limb to dissipate applied forces, whereas rigid fixation of the limb on the traction table may increase impulse transmission to the femur [22]. The DAA more seamlessly integrates the use of intraoperative fluoroscopy than the posterior approach, which can reduce operative time by assisting with implant removal, implant positioning, and recreation of appropriate leg length and offset [1].

Though the benefits of the DAA in primary THA are well-established, there is limited literature on its use for the specific challenge of converting a long CMN to THA. Existing studies on anterior-based approaches for cTHA often group various implants or do not isolate the DAA, limiting their applicability to this specific scenario. A retrospective study by Call et al. [23] compared cTHA via an anterior-based muscle-sparing (ABMS) approach vs a posterior approach, finding both to be safe and effective with high functional scores. However, their conclusions regarding CMN conversions were limited, as these implants constituted only a small subset of their patients (8 [9%] ABMS; 4 [24%] posterior), and statistical comparisons were restricted by small sample sizes, suggesting the need for further focused investigation. Similarly, a retrospective review by Crockatt et al. found a lower rate of complications, revisions, and a shorter hospital length of stay for cTHA performed through an anterior-based approach (DAA or ABMS) than when performed through a posterior approach [1]. However, these data were not specific to the DAA only, and long CMN cTHAs comprised only a small portion of the cases (12.8% anterior-based; 14.3% posterior). A retrospective review by Randelli et al. on 20 DAA cTHA patients reported a low complication rate (2 periprosthetic fractures unrelated to cTHA) and significant improvement in Harris Hip scores, providing direct evidence for the safety and efficacy of DAA for cTHA [24]. Yun et al. conducted a retrospective review of 42 patients who underwent DAA cTHA for failed in situ screw fixation of femoral neck fractures, excluding extracapsular or intertrochanteric hip fractures. While they reported no postoperative dislocations, periprosthetic fractures, or infections, they did report 1 case of immediate foot drop and 2 readmissions for medical issues, as well as 1 mortality, indicating the inherently higher risk of cTHA compared to primary THA [25].

The cTHA may be performed with a cemented or cementless femoral stem. A recent study by Crockatt et al. demonstrated that cementless fixation in cTHA was associated with shorter operative times with no significant difference in length of stay, discharge disposition, revisions, or complication rates as compared to cemented cTHA following failed osteosynthesis of proximal femur fractures [26]. The choice of cemented vs cementless stem in cTHA should ultimately be based on the patient’s bone quality as well as location of stress risers secondary to the patient’s prior surgery. In the case of a short CMN, the surgeon may be required to use a longer femoral stem, or even a diaphyseal engaging stem, to bypass the distal interlock screw site. If cemented fixation is chosen, surgeons should consider using the French Paradox technique [27,28] as they may be unable to achieve the required cement mantle needed in taper-slip and composite beam techniques.

The described technique incorporates specific technical pearls designed to address the unique challenges of long CMN removal. First, the intramedullary nail component of the CMN is intentionally left in situ during neck cut, acetabular component preparation, and femoral elevation. This is done to maintain stability to the proximal femur during exposure and elevation and reduce the risk of iatrogenic fracture during these steps, a significant concern in these cases since the femur is already compromised. Second, settable, resorbable bone putty is utilized to prevent cement extravasation and to fill any bony defects left by the CMN. This may mitigate the risk of periprosthetic fracture through addressing potential stress risers created by the prior hardware. In this case, and particularly because our cemented stem bypassed the lag screw hole, we did not feel it was necessary to use a prophylactic cable prior to femoral broaching, although this could certainly be considered, especially if the surgeon chooses to use a cementless femoral stem.

Summary

We describe a surgical technique for performing cTHA with the removal of a long CMN through the DAA on a standard operating room table. Supine patient positioning and utilization of a regular operating room table facilitates seamless fluoroscopy use for hardware removal and implant positioning. Draping in both lower extremities with the use of an “off-table” technique facilitates ease of clinical leg length determination and optimization. While existing literature supports anterior-based approaches for cTHA, a specific gap exists regarding long CMN removal; future studies are certainly warranted to assess long-term results of cTHA from long CMNs through the DAA.
