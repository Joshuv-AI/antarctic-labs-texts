Coronary Endothelial Dysfunction Is Associated With Major Depressive Disorder in Men With Chest Pain and Nonobstructive Coronary Artery Disease
Authors: Jaskanwal D S Sara, Scott Breitinger, Nazanin Rajai, Lilach O Lerman, Amir Lerman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStudies have demonstrated an association between endothelial dysfunction and major depressive disorder (MDD). However, the link between coronary endothelial dysfunction (CED) and an incident diagnosis of MDD across sexes is unclear.MethodsPatients with nonobstructive coronary artery disease (stenosis <40%) underwent an invasive assessment of CED. Macrovascular CED was defined as a percentage change in coronary diameter in response to acetylcholine of ≤−10% compared with baseline. Microvascular CED was defined as a percentage change in coronary blood flow in response to acetylcholine of ≤−50% compared with baseline. In the period after the index procedure, patients were followed up for developing a diagnosis of MDD, and logistic regression was performed to assess the association between baseline CED and incident MDD.ResultsIn total, 2280 patients underwent invasive testing for CED. A total of 516 patients (23%) underwent an evaluation for MDD at a date after the index procedure (mean±SD age, 53±12 years; 72% women). A total of 160 (31%) were diagnosed with MDD. The percentage change in coronary diameter in response to acetylcholine was lower in those diagnosed with MDD in all patients (−10.8% versus −5.3%; P=0.008), and in men (−19.0% versus −4.8%; P=0.001). In men, a higher proportion of those with macrovascular CED were diagnosed with MDD compared with those not diagnosed with MDD (59% versus 38%; P=0.035). In multivariable analyses, macrovascular CED was associated with MDD in men (odds ratio: 2.99 [95% CI, 1.26–7.45]; P=0.015).ConclusionsMacrovascular CED is significantly associated with incident MDD in men with nonobstructive coronary artery disease. Endothelial dysfunction in the coronary arteries could thus be a helpful biomarker to identify patients at risk of MDD.Keywords: chest pain, coronary endothelial dysfunction, depression, ischemiaSubject Categories: Biomarkers, Coronary Circulation, Endothelium/Vascular Type/Nitric Oxide, Vascular Biology, Risk Factors

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a mood disorder characterized by low mood or an inability to experience usual feelings of pleasure together with variable psychomotor, cognitive, and neurovegetative dysfunction. 1 The prevalence of MDD is between 5% and 10% and is estimated to affect >300 million people worldwide. 2 Individuals with MDD are at an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and premature mortality. 3 In fact, MDD is associated with an increased risk of coronary artery disease (CAD) as well as a >4‐fold increased risk of myocardial infarction after adjusting for psychiatric diagnoses and traditional risk factors. 4 On the other hand, the prevalence of MDD in patients with known CVD is 3 times greater than that in the general population, 5 and more than half of individuals with stable CAD and acute coronary syndromes have significant symptoms of depression. 6 Furthermore, depression is an independent risk factor for worse clinical outcomes following a cardiovascular event. 7 Thus, MDD and CVD appear to have a bidirectional and reciprocal relationship. 8

Various pathophysiological mechanisms underpin the bidirectional association between MDD and CVD, including endothelial dysfunction, which is characterized by impaired endothelium‐dependent vasodilatation. 9 Endothelial dysfunction is independently associated with an increased risk of CVD, 10 and it is associated with MDD. 11 , 12 , 13 Although most studies demonstrating an association between endothelial dysfunction have had a cross‐sectional design, there are reports demonstrating an association between endothelial dysfunction and incident depression, 14 , 15 including a recent report from our group in which we reported the association between peripheral endothelial dysfunction and incident MDD. 16 However, the relationship between coronary endothelial dysfunction (CED) and incident MDD has not previously been reported, nor has the impact of biologic sex on this association been delineated, despite its likely importance given higher rates of CVD in men 17 and higher rates of MDD in women. 18

Thus, in the current study, we evaluate the association between macrovascular and microvascular CED and subsequently diagnosed MDD across men and women separately in patients who present with chest pain and nonobstructive CAD.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. This prospective cohort study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board, and all study participants provided their informed consent. Patients were referred for clinically indicated coronary angiography in the setting of symptoms of angina or an abnormal stress test result. The following patients were excluded from the current study: those with nonobstructive CAD (<40% luminal stenosis by visual estimation), acute coronary syndrome, hypertensive urgency or emergency, acute renal failure, and heart failure with or without a reduced ejection fraction. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26

CED Protocol

Consecutive patients presented to the catheterization laboratory in the fasting state, and all vasoactive cardiac medications had been discontinued for at least 24 hours. Diagnostic coronary angiography was performed in keeping with usual clinical protocols, followed by an invasive evaluation of coronary endothelial function, as previously described. 27 , 28 First, an appropriate weight‐based dose of intravenous heparin was administered. Next, a 0.014‐inch diameter Doppler guidewire (Flowire, Volcano) placed inside a 3F Slip‐Cath Infusion Catheter (Cook Medical) was advanced into the left anterior descending coronary artery, such that the guidewire tip was ≈2 to 3 mm distal to the end of the infusion catheter.

Throughout the duration of each procedure, the patient's blood pressure and heart rate were continuously monitored. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26

Using the Doppler wire, mean peak velocity was measured and recorded at baseline and after infusions of acetylcholine at increasing concentrations (ie, 10−6, 10−5, and 10−4 M) 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 using a pump. Coronary angiography was repeated after each successive recording. In a similar vein, coronary artery diameter was measured at baseline, and after each infusion dose of acetylcholine. Endothelial‐dependent coronary blood flow (CBF) was estimated as follows: CBF=(mean peak velocity/2) (coronary artery diameter/2)2. 27 , 29 Then, the maximum percentage increase in CBF from baseline to the CBF following provocation with acetylcholine was calculated (%ΔCBFAch). The maximum percentage change in coronary artery diameter following provocation with acetylcholine (%ΔCADAch) was also calculated using the following formula: (coronary artery diameter after acetylcholine infusion–coronary artery diameter at baseline)/coronary artery diameter at baseline. 30 , 31 Each diameter was measured in the same vessel segment for each patient, usually 5 mm distal to the Doppler guidewire, and the measuring investigator was blinded to Doppler data. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26

Definition of Terms

Macrovascular CED was characterized as a coronary artery diameter following provocation with acetylcholine (%ΔCADAch) of ≤−10% (ie, a vasoconstrictive response with at least a 10% decrease in coronary artery diameter in response to acetylcholine relative to baseline). Microvascular CED was characterized as a maximum percentage augmentation in CBF following provocation with acetylcholine (%ΔCBFAch) of ≤50% compared with baseline. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23

CED was categorized as the presence of either microvascular CED and/or macrovascular CED.

Patient Information

Information was abstracted on important comorbidities, such as hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, history of myocardial infarction, medication use, and laboratory parameters, such as total cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein, and high‐density lipoprotein. Blood test results were based on samples obtained on the morning of the index procedure. A detailed definition of relevant variables has been described elsewhere. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 Each patient medical record was evaluated to identify those who underwent a clinically indicated evaluation for MDD at a time after the date of the index invasive coronary angiogram. MDD was diagnosed (or excluded) using a structured clinical interview by a trained clinician. 32 In cases where patients underwent multiple assessments for MDD, only the first was considered for the current study.

Statistical Analysis

At baseline, patients were categorized as having normal coronary endothelial function versus CED. In addition, they were separately categorized as normal macrovascular coronary endothelial function versus macrovascular CED as well as normal microvascular coronary endothelial function versus microvascular CED. Patients in each group were then grouped into those who subsequently developed MDD, and the percentage of patients with any, macrovascular, or microvascular CED who developed MDD was compared with those who did not develop MDD in all patients, and in men and women separately. In addition, %ΔCADAch, as a continuous measure of macrovascular coronary endothelial function, and %ΔCBFAch, as a continuous measure of microvascular coronary endothelial function, was compared between patients who developed MDD versus those who did not develop MDD, in all patients and separately in men and women.

Logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the association between baseline CED and incident MDD (dependent variable) in all patients, and in men and women separately. The independent variables evaluated were macrovascular, microvascular, and any CED as categorical variables, in both univariable and multivariable analyses, as well as %ΔCADAch and %ΔCBFAch as continuous variables in multivariable analyses. We planned, a priori, to perform stratified analyses across both sexes. In our multivariable analyses, we planned to adjust for age and other cardiovascular risk factors associated with both CED and MDD, such as hypertension and hyperlipidemia. We were, however, limited in how many variables we could adjust for in light of limitations in sample size and the risk of overfitting. No sensitivity analyses were performed.

All patients included in this study had complete data with respect to coronary endothelial function testing and clinical variables and had ≥90% complete data with respect to laboratory values. No adjustment was made to address missing data. Continuous variables were presented as a mean (SD) for normally distributed data and as a median (quartile 1–quartile 3) for skewed data. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages). Differences between groups for continuous variables were analyzed using Student t test and Wilcoxon rank‐sum test; and differences for categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson χ2 test. P<0.05 was considered significant, and statistical analyses were performed using JMP 9 software (SAS Institute, Inc, Cary, NC).

RESULTS

Sample Overview

Figure 1 demonstrates patient flow through the study. Between January 1992 and December 2021, 2280 patients (mean±SD age, 52±13 years; 67% women) underwent coronary angiography and invasive testing for CED. A total of 516 patients (23%) underwent a formal evaluation for MDD at a date after the index procedure and had measurements available for both %ΔCADAch and %ΔCBFAch (mean±SD age, 53±12 years; 72% women; 95% White; 30.7±6.7 kg/m2). Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of all patients with and without any CED (ie, microvascular CED and/or microvascular CED). Individuals with any abnormal CED were on average older than those with normal coronary endothelial function (mean±SD age, 54±12 versus 52±12 years; P=0.049). Otherwise, there were no other significant differences between groups with respect to demographic, clinical, or laboratory variables or in medication use.

Coronary Endothelial Function According to Diagnosis of MDD

Figures 2 and 3 demonstrate the %ΔCADAch, as an index of macrovascular coronary endothelial function, and %ΔCBFAch, as an index of microvascular coronary endothelial function, respectively, in those diagnosed with versus without MDD. In all patients, those diagnosed with MDD had a significantly lower (more negative) %ΔCADAch compared with those without MDD (−10.8% versus −5.3%; P=0.008), implying that individuals with MDD experienced greater macrovascular vasoconstriction in response to acetylcholine, consistent with worse macrovascular endothelial function. Similarly, among men, those with a diagnosis of MDD had a significantly lower (more negative) %ΔCADAch compared with those without MDD (−19.0% versus −4.8%; P=0.001). There was no significant difference in the %ΔCADAch between those with versus without MDD among women. When evaluating %ΔCBFAch as an index of coronary microvascular endothelial function, there were no significant difference in %ΔCBFAch between those with versus those without MDD among all patients or after stratifying by sex.

Frequency of MDD According to Type of CED

Table 2 demonstrates the proportion of individuals who underwent a formal evaluation for MDD who were diagnosed with MDD according to the presence of CED in all patients and by sex. A total of 160 (31%) of all individuals were diagnosed with MDD. In men and women separately, 32 (24%) and 133 (34%) were diagnosed with MDD, respectively. In all patients and in women only, the proportion diagnosed with MDD was not significantly different to the proportion not diagnosed with MDD among those with macrovascular CED, microvascular CED, or any type of CED. In men, a significantly higher proportion of those with macrovascular CED were diagnosed with MDD compared with those who were not diagnosed with MDD (59% versus 38%; P=0.035). There were no significant differences in the proportion diagnosed with MDD among those with microvascular CED or any type of CED in men. Thus, in univariable analyses, CED was not associated with MDD among all patients and women only. On the other hand, macrovascular CED was significantly associated with MDD among men (odds ratio [OR], 1.87 [95% CI, 1.03–3.40]; P=0.035).

CED and MDD: Multivariable Analysis

Table 3 demonstrates the associations between CED types (as categorical variables) and MDD in multivariable analyses adjusted for age, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. In all patients and in women only, CED was not associated with MDD. In men, macrovascular CED was significantly associated with MDD after adjusting for covariables (OR, 2.99 [95% CI, 1.26–7.45]; P=0.015). Microvascular CED and any type of CED was not significantly associated with MDD in men. Table 4 demonstrates the associations between %ΔCADAch and %ΔCBFAch as continuous variables and MDD in multivariable analyses adjusted for age, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. Among all patients, %ΔCADAch was significantly associated with MDD after adjusting for covariables (OR for a 1% ΔCADAch, 0.98 [95% CI, 0.97–0.99]; P=0.006), such that a 10% decrease in coronary artery diameter in response to acetylcholine (vasoconstriction) was associated with a 20% greater odds of being diagnosed with MDD. The %ΔCBFAch was not associated with MDD among all patients. Among men, %ΔCADAch was significantly associated with MDD after adjusting for covariables (OR for a 1% ΔCADAch, 0.96 [95% CI, 0.94–0.98]; P=0.001), such that a 10% decrease in coronary artery diameter in response to acetylcholine (vasoconstriction) was associated with a 40% greater odds of being diagnosed with MDD. The %ΔCBFAch was not associated with MDD among men. The %ΔCADAch and %ΔCBFAch was not associated with MDD in women.

DISCUSSION

In the current study, we demonstrated the following: (1) among patients with nonobstructive CAD on invasive coronary angiography who were referred for an evaluation of MDD, 31% were diagnosed with MDD; (2) CED was prevalent in those with MDD, affecting 69% of all individuals (74% of all men and 68% of all women); and (3) macrovascular CED was significantly associated with incident MDD in men in univariable and multivariable analyses. MDD is highly prevalent in the United States and worldwide. 2 Furthermore, MDD was the third leading cause of years lived with disability between 2007 and 2017 33 and is projected to be the second leading cause of disability‐adjusted life years by 2030. 34 The management of MDD, however, is challenging given its complex and multifaceted cause, variable disease course, and inconsistent response to therapy. 35 , 36 Thus, a better understanding of the underlying biological mechanisms of MDD would be of value to identify markers that predict depression onset and course, which may, in turn, pave the way for the development of new therapeutic targets. The current study supports the notion that endothelial dysfunction may play a mechanistic role in the development of depression, and suggests that CED could be a marker of risk for the development of MDD in male subjects. In this way, endothelial dysfunction, and impaired vascular health more generally, could form a therapeutic target to reduce the risk of incident depression and to improve clinical outcomes.

Individuals with MDD are at an increased risk of CVD and premature mortality, 3 including an increased risk of CAD and myocardial infarction, even after adjusting for psychiatric diagnoses and traditional risk factors. 4 At the same time, MDD has been noted to be more prevalent in patients with stable chronic CVD as well as in acute coronary syndromes than in the general population, 5 , 6 and is independently associated with worse clinical outcomes following a cardiovascular event. 7 Thus, MDD and CVD appear to have a bidirectional and reciprocal relationship. 8 Several pathophysiological mechanisms appear to underpin this bidirectional association. These include unhealthy lifestyle factors, such as smoking, physical inactivity, and poor diet, 37 , 38 overlapping genetic factors and epigenetic modulation of gene expression, 8 stress‐induced hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis dysfunction, 39 inflammation, 40 and increased platelet reactivity. 41 Central to these processes is endothelial dysfunction, which is a marker of preclinical atherosclerosis and is characterized by impaired endothelium‐dependent vasodilatation in conjunction with a procoagulant and proinflammatory state. 9 Although endothelial dysfunction is known to be independently associated with an increased risk of CVD, 10 meta‐analyses and systematic reviews have consistently demonstrated the association between endothelial dysfunction and MDD. 11 , 12 , 13 Studies included in these reports evaluated endothelial function peripherally using techniques such as flow‐mediated dilatation, which noninvasively assess vasodilator response after shear stress induced by the release on nitric oxide. 9 This technique, however, relies on ultrasound imaging of the brachial artery and so its operator dependence may lead to inaccuracies. In current study, we evaluated endothelial function in the coronary arteries, a clinically important vascular bed given its direct relationship to notable cardiovascular events, such as myocardial infarction and acute coronary syndromes. Furthermore, our assessment of endothelial function was comprehensive in that we tested for both macrovascular and microvascular CED using objective measures and techniques that are considered to be the reference standard. 42

A previous study by Kim et al demonstrated a significant association between macrovascular CED, using similar techniques used in the current study by measuring %ΔCADAch, with depressive symptoms measured using clinical surveys. 43 That study included 155 individuals, 52 of whom were categorized as having depressive symptoms, whereas the current study included 516 individuals, of whom 160 had been formally diagnosed with MDD. We elected to focus on the diagnosis of MDD as opposed to depressive symptoms in the current study as symptoms do not always correlate with a diagnosis of MDD and may vary throughout an individual's disease course. 32 Furthermore, the association between depressive symptoms and endothelial dysfunction has been demonstrated less consistently than that with a diagnosis of MDD per se. 44 Studies have also shown that even patients with treated MDD have evidence of peripheral endothelial dysfunction, 45 suggesting that the vascular underpinning of depression may be limited to those whose burden of clinical features crosses the threshold into satisfying the formal diagnostic criteria for MDD rather than those who experience depressive symptoms alone, which are more prevalent in the general population. 2

Previous studies, 11 , 12 , 13 including the one by Kim et al, 43 used cross‐sectional designs hindering the inference of causality. Indeed, previous studies have demonstrated an association between both peripheral 16 and cerebral endothelial dysfunction and incident MDD. 14 To our knowledge, the current study is the first to extend these findings by demonstrating an association between CED and an incident diagnosis of MDD. Thus, our findings support the notion that endothelial dysfunction is a systemic process, and its presence in one vascular bed may be related to disease processes in other vascular beds, affecting distinct biological systems. Moreover, these findings implicate the potential role of endothelial dysfunction as an underlying mechanism in the cause of MDD, although further prospective studies will be required to confirm these findings. Endothelial dysfunction could lead to depression by several different pathways. First, cerebral endothelial dysfunction could lead to cerebral perfusion deficits and chronic ischemia along the lines of the “vascular depression” hypothesis, 46 which, if localized to regions involved in mood regulation, could provide a plausible basis for resulting in depression. Second, endothelial dysfunction is closely related to low‐grade inflammation and oxidative stress, which have been independently associated with depression. 47 , 48 In 1 study, both endothelial dysfunction and low‐grade inflammation were shown to be associated with incident depression, and in fact 60% of the total effect of endothelial dysfunction on incident depression could be attributed to low‐grade inflammation. 15 In another study, oxidative stress–induced reductions in nitric oxide–dependent dilatation directly contributed to peripheral microvascular dysfunction in adults with MDD. 49 The cerebral endothelium may be more vulnerable to oxidative stress because of the high metabolic demand of the brain, leading to greater production of reactive oxygen species 48 coupled with the brain's limited antioxidant defenses. 50 Third, mental stress can result in dysregulation of the autonomic nervous system and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis 51 and may contribute separately to endothelial dysfunction 52 and depression by way of elevated cortisol levels, which, in turn, lead to cerebral atrophy and impaired neurogenesis, plasticity, and signaling. 53 Last, endothelial dysfunction may promote the disruption of the blood–brain barrier 14 and may thus expose the brain to toxins and deprive it of nutrients that may contribute to the pathophysiological milieu of MDD.

The current study demonstrated an association between CED and MDD in men only. The link between CVD in general and endothelial dysfunction as an index of vascular health more specifically and MDD is likely to be modulated by sex. Premenopausal women have, on average, a lower risk of CVD compared with men, 17 yet women have a significantly higher prevalence of depressive disorders compared with men. 18 Moreover, MDD appears to be associated with a greater incidence of CVD in women compared with men. 54 Interestingly, in animal models, females exhibit increased behavioral susceptibility to depression, but demonstrate less severe impairments to endothelial function compared with their male counterparts, 55 , 56 which is thought to be related to greater antioxidant defense mechanisms. 55 , 56 Thus, biological sex may mediate the effects of endothelial function on the risk of MDD. Depression is known to demonstrate variable clinical phenotypes across the sexes, 57 and studies have also demonstrated biological differences in men and women with MDD with respect to the brain on neuroimaging 58 as well as genetic factors. 59 These biological differences may extend to the vasculature, which may be implicated in distinct causal mechanisms of MDD in men and women, with the former potentially being more at risk of so‐called vascular depression. 46 Distinguishing the cause of MDD along the lines of sex may be helpful in guiding therapy. Selective serotonin receptor inhibitors play a central role in the management of moderate to severe MDD. A previous study demonstrated that the selective serotonin receptor inhibitor escitalopram favorably modulated biochemical markers of endothelial damage, such as circulating endothelial cells and endothelial progenitor cells, as well as plasma levels of soluble von Willebrand factor in patients with MDD. 60 Thus, when endothelial dysfunction is present, selective serotonin receptor inhibitors may have an additive role in reducing the risk of CVD. Moreover, in cases where endothelial dysfunction is thought to be implicated in MDD, clinicians could consider treatments that have been shown to ameliorate endothelial dysfunction, such as exercise programs, 61 dietary modification, 62 and even statin therapy, 63 as adjunctive strategies to prevent and treat depression. Further studies will be required to investigate these hypotheses.

The current study has some limitations. First, our sample is made up of patients presenting with chest pain who were referred for coronary angiography at a tertiary referral center and, thus, comprises a select population. Second, the diagnosis of MDD after the index procedure was ascertained by medical record review and was not adjudicated by the investigators of the current study. Indeed, in clinical practice, MDD can classified differently by different providers. Third, we did not evaluate additional disease‐related information that may be relevant, including duration of diagnosis, disease course, and subsequent response to antidepressant or other therapies. Further studies will be required to evaluate the effects of both pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic interventions on endothelial dysfunction to reduce the risk and severity of depression. Fourth, although menopausal status may play a role in the association between endothelial function and depression, this variable was not consistently documented in female participants and, therefore, could not be accounted for in our analyses. In addition, this study consisted of patients who underwent routine clinically informed evaluations, and so although oxidative stress and inflammation are known to play a role in the pathophysiology of both MDD and endothelial dysfunction, we did not collect markers of these pathologic processes in our patients. Thus, we are unable to evaluate the role of oxidative stress or inflammation in the interplay between endothelial dysfunction and MDD in our patients. Furthermore, we were limited in the number of potentially relevant covariables we could adjust for in our analyses to limit overfitting (including variables such as renal function or blood glucose). Last, the sample included in this study was recruited across a long period of time, during which patient characteristics and clinical practice patterns have likely changed. This may limit the homogeneity of our sample and the internal validity of our findings.

CONCLUSIONS

Macrovascular CED is significantly associated with incident MDD in men who present with nonobstructive CAD. Endothelial dysfunction in the epicardial coronary arteries could thus be a helpful biomarker to identify patients at increased risk of MDD, who may, in turn, be candidates for aggressive preventive measures. Moreover, endothelial dysfunction may play an important role in the pathophysiology of MDD in a subset of patients, consistent with the “vascular depression” hypothesis.
