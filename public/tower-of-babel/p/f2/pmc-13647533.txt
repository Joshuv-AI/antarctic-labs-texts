Introduction

Malaria is a life-threatening disease spread to humans by some types of mosquitoes. The female Anopheles mosquito causes it. It is mostly found in tropical countries. Globally in 2023, there were an estimated 263 million malaria cases and 597,000 malaria deaths in 83 countries (WHO). The African region was home to 94% of malaria cases (246 million) and 95% (569,000) of malaria deaths, and children under 5 accounted for about 76% of all malaria deaths in the region [1]. In Rwanda, malaria continues to be a major public health concern and priority because it causes death, and the entire population is at risk of being infected with malaria.

Though research has shown that student engagement has positive impact in advancing malaria eradication in Rwanda by improving community awareness where sometimes they make outreach for explaining the population how they can prevent the spread of malaria vectors and also through making Anti-malaria clubs where they are working together for making people understanding that malaria is preventable and controllable [2] via different interventions like sensitizing local people to change their behavior toward malaria prevention, education and awareness for long term impact and for this plan youth should play their role in empowering organization’s vision of an integrated, prosperous peaceful Africa without malaria [3] because they have power, skills for fighting against malaria.

Consequently, many individuals reside in rural areas where environmental conditions are conducive to mosquito breeding, and adjacent forested areas serve as reservoirs for disease vectors. In these contexts, students are often tasked with promoting awareness of preventive measures. However, they frequently face challenges related to community attitudes and behaviors, as many community members lack understanding of or acceptance of mosquito net use and may undervalue or disregard student-led health education initiatives [4], where they use them in wrong ways, and where there low skills regarding malaria treatment where they fused to reach the hospital, and some who reach it didn’t take medication as prescribed by health workers and take few doses, which make resistance of malaria.

So this narrative review is sought out to highlight the role of university students in advancing malaria eradication through different approaches like improving community efforts, increasing awareness campaigns, and research toward malaria causes and how to prevent it because they gained knowledge through student workshops [5] and made sure that the community, together with village health, understood different steps toward malaria eradication and practiced measures for controlling the spread of its vectors. Those different interventions were made to reach the government projects of eradicating malaria 2030.

Overview of malaria efforts in Rwanda

Rwanda experienced a significant malaria outbreak with over five million cases in 2016. From 2016 to 2023, malaria prevalence dropped by 88%, and mortality rates declined by more than 90%. This progress was neither accidental nor confined to a single effort. It was driven by a succession of national strategies aimed at equitable health access for all. These strategies are targeted for diagnosis and treatment, community ownership, real-time surveillance, and evidence-based policy. This achievement relied on two key national frameworks: the Extended Malaria Strategic Plan (EMSP) 2013–2020 and the Malaria Strategic Plan (MSP) 2020–2024. The EMSP aimed to reduce malaria cases by 75% and deaths by 50% from the 2011 baseline. It focused on universal access to preventive and treatment measures, particularly in high-burden districts (Bugesera, Gatsibo, Gisagara, Kirehe, and Nyagatare), according to the Ministry of Health in 2013. Scaling up, the MSP 2020–2024 aimed at malaria elimination by targeting less than 50 cases per 1000 population, reducing deaths by 60% and achieving pre-elimination status in at least eight districts by 2024. That means reducing malaria morbidity and mortality by at least 50% of the 2019 levels [6].

This progress was underpinned by strategic community- and facility-level interventions in which community health workers played a pivotal role in malaria case management, diagnosing and treating uncomplicated cases using rapid diagnostic tests and artemisinin-based combination therapies, with two workers deployed in each village. By 2023, CHWs managed over 60% of all malaria cases, up from 18% in 2016, showing a big shift toward decentralization and early access to care [7].

Moreover, Rwanda used evidence-based approaches to malaria control, including information dissemination and the distribution of long-lasting insecticidal nets, with a total number of 80,709 LLINs being distributed to pregnant women through ANC services, while 257,677 LLINs were distributed to children under one year of age through EPI services in all 30 districts, complementing the last mass LLIN distribution completed in February 2017. Additionally, indoor residual spraying with a total of 480,438 of 483,595 structures sprayed (average structure coverage of 99.3%) and 1,932,272 of 1,940,692 protected, and early diagnosis and treatment of malaria cases. Extensive IRS campaigns in 12 districts and LLIN distribution in 18 districts have led to a 71% decrease in malaria cases between 2016 and 2021 [8].

In 2020, the RBC introduced larviciding using drones, aimed at killing mosquito larvae in water sources before they mature into adult mosquitoes [9]. Additionally, a new vector management program was implemented, incorporating community-based training to identify mosquito breeding sites and prevent malaria transmission. Furthermore, the integration of real-time surveillance systems has strengthened Rwanda’s capacity to detect and respond to malaria threats. The HMIS enables comprehensive, monthly reporting from both health facilities and community levels. The Rapid SMS platform, a mobile health tool that allows CHWs to report cases, stock-outs, and treatment data in real time, supports data-driven decision-making, detects outbreaks early, and guides IRS campaigns [8].

Rwanda has made momentous changes in controlling malaria. However, challenges remain. The big problem is widespread insecticide resistance, which threatens the effectiveness of both LLINs and IRS. Entomological surveillance shows resistance in multiple districts, demanding the continuous rotation of insecticides and the evaluation of new vector control tools [10]. Additionally, relying on donor funding from partners like the Global Fund and PMI creates risks, especially true with changing priorities in global health financing. Limited domestic resource mobilization and geographic disparities in transmission, particularly in the Southern Province, where environmental and socioeconomic factors drive higher infection rates, impact sustainability efforts.

Nonetheless, Rwanda remains well-positioned to achieve malaria elimination. Plans include piloting the malaria vaccine in kids aged 3 to 5 years, expanding IRS to additional districts, strengthening and improving entomological capacity, and integrating climate data into outbreak prediction models [11].

Types of contributions by university students

University students play a crucial role in advancing malaria eradication efforts in Rwanda through various contributions that leverage their knowledge, innovation, and community engagement. One of the primary ways students contribute through research and academic studies is that they provide valuable insights into malaria prevention and control strategies. Many students, particularly those in health sciences and biomedical fields, conduct studies on malaria transmission patterns, the effectiveness of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), and the impact of environmental factors on malaria prevalence [12]. Their findings help inform national policies and interventions led by the Rwanda Biomedical Center (RBC) and other health organizations.

Additionally, university students actively participate in community outreach programs, where they educate local populations on malaria prevention methods, such as proper use of ITNs, indoor residual spraying (IRS), and early diagnosis and treatment [13]. These initiatives are frequently implemented in collaboration with the Rwanda Biomedical Centre (RBC) and other health institutions, ensuring that scientifically validated information reaches vulnerable communities. Through the organization of awareness campaigns and workshops, students help bridge the gap between scientific knowledge and its practical application in malaria prevention.

Another significant contribution comes from technological innovations developed by students in fields such as engineering and information technology. Some students have designed mobile applications that provide real-time malaria surveillance data, enabling health officials to track outbreaks and respond promptly [14]. Others have worked on improving diagnostic tools, such as rapid malaria test kits, which enhance early detection and treatment, reducing malaria-related mortality rates.

Furthermore, university students engage in policy advocacy and leadership, influencing malaria eradication strategies at both local and national levels. Through student-led organizations and partnerships with RBC, they advocate for increased funding for malaria research, better healthcare infrastructure, and the integration of malaria education into school curricula [15]. Their involvement in policy discussions ensures that youth perspectives are considered in shaping Rwanda’s malaria eradication efforts.

Lastly, students contribute through volunteerism and fieldwork, assisting healthcare professionals in malaria prevention programs. Many students participate in RBC-led initiatives, such as distributing ITNs, conducting malaria screenings, and supporting treatment programs in rural areas [12]. Their hands-on involvement not only aids malaria control but also provides them with practical experience that enhances their future careers in public health and medicine.

Through research, community outreach, technological innovation, policy advocacy, and volunteerism, university students in Rwanda significantly contribute to malaria eradication efforts. Their engagement strengthens national strategies and ensures that malaria prevention remains a priority in public health discourse. By leveraging their skills and knowledge, students continue to play a vital role in reducing malaria prevalence and advancing Rwanda’s goal of complete malaria eradication.

Challenges faced by university students

University students possess immense potential to contribute to the eradication of malaria through the campaigns, research, and outreach programs they can organize. Despite their interest in malaria eradication, they face many hurdles that prevent them from contributing fully.

Firstly, these efforts are constrained by limited resources and insufficient funding. According to the 2022 World Malaria Report, only approximately 50% of the estimated USD 7.3 billion required for global malaria eradication was mobilized [16]. This shows a great gap in the funds allocated for malaria elimination, which in turn also affects the university students and the activities they can organize. They need money to host campaigns, design education materials, and hold impactful outreach programs, and they also need resources like ITNs, which can help prevent malaria infection.

In addition, the students willing to participate often lack a connection to the health authorities or other relevant organizations, which makes it a big challenge for the students to make meaningful contributions towards malaria eradication. According to the research conducted in Ghana on whether community participation in malaria eradication is a myth or a reality, the results showed how no matter the policies the authorities can take and bring them down to the people, it won’t be as effective as it would be when there is a mutual connection between the two sides instead of those top-down approaches. That’s why, mostly, community participation is aired as a myth [17]. But if there could be a platform that connects the students or community at large with the authorities, it would be a perfect strategy for the eradication of malaria because, for instance, the students can get to talk to the leader about their views on the process and also communicate real-time challenges being faced and work together to find solutions. I believe this people-centered health coordination governance can yield good results.

Another pressing challenge is the insufficient education and knowledge of university students on malaria infection and its treatments. Over time, student-led organizations have proven to be effective in global health initiatives [18]. Consequently, if students are not adequately educated about malaria, their capacity to contribute effectively to its eradication is substantially limited. Educational interventions are therefore recognized as a critical component of malaria elimination strategies [19], as insufficient knowledge undermines the potential impact of other supportive factors within student-led initiatives. This shows an urgent need for training to be given to the students to equip them with the necessary knowledge to help them become good agents of change through the community-focused initiatives they embark on against malaria.

Lastly, university students are limited by sociopolitical challenges. The students might be enthusiastic and motivated to organize a campaign but are denied permission or support from the government officials. Or else, they may have great ideas and then submit them to the health authorities for support or partnership, and in the end, it takes months to years for approval, which demoralizes their spirit of contribution. Also, the students are challenged by the community's mistrust. Because of being perceived as too young, people in the community might think they are inexperienced and don’t take them seriously, which may even cause people to refuse to attend malaria sessions just because students lead them.

To conclude, even though university students show great potential in contributing to malaria eradication, they still lack funds and resources. They face barriers from sociopolitical issues, receive little educational training, and lack coordination with health facilities.

Contribution of university students’ roles

Students at the University of Rwanda collaborated with Charis UAS to deploy drones in Huye and Rusizi Districts, mapping and spraying mosquito breeding grounds. This initiative positioned Rwanda as the first African country to use drone technology as part of a national malaria control strategy [20]. Similarly, students from the African Leadership University developed MediX, a virtual reality medical training simulation aimed at enhancing healthcare service delivery, which indirectly aids malaria diagnosis [21]. Another impactful initiative involved University of Rwanda students in Ruhuha, who launched a citizen science project that engaged residents in mosquito monitoring [22]. Complementing these efforts, UNFPA Rwanda trained 90 student Change Ambassadors to lead School Health Clubs, promoting malaria education and reaching an estimated 34,000 schoolmates through peer-to-peer health initiatives [23].

Within just eight months, the drone project achieved a remarkable 90.6% reduction in malaria incidence in Gasabo District [20]. In Ruhuha, 112 citizen science volunteers collected 3,793 mosquito samples, of which 10.8% were identified as Anopheles species carrying malaria. These findings were vital in crafting more targeted malaria interventions [22]. Although there is limited quantitative data on the direct impact of school health clubs, anecdotal evidence suggests that student-led awareness campaigns improved malaria prevention efforts in districts such as Karongi, Nyamasheke, and Rusizi [23].

These student-led initiatives have had a profound influence on national malaria policies. The drone technology used in the University of Rwanda project was later officially adopted by the Ministry of Health [20]. Likewise, the Ruhuha citizen science project demonstrated how community-driven surveillance could strengthen national malaria strategies [22]. Meanwhile, the School Health Clubs program proved that student-led health education can be impactful and scalable, setting a precedent for youth involvement in public health policy [23]. These examples underscore how Rwandan students transform classroom knowledge into innovative, community-based solutions that gain traction at the national level.

Recommendations

Algeria, Cape Verde, and Mauritius have successfully eliminated malaria. Globally, only 44 countries have eradicated malaria [24]. In 2000, there were 108 countries classified as malaria-endemic. Over the subsequent 15 years, this number saw sizable reductions. The decline became more pronounced after 2015, but with a noticeable deceleration around 2019. By 2022, the number had dropped to 85. These results are consistent with the trends in malaria case numbers and deaths reported by the WHO [25]. Improving socioeconomic settings has long proved to be efficient in malaria eradication. Whether in Italy or emerging economies such as China, significant improvements in socioeconomic conditions have been indispensable for successful malaria eradication. Despite notable economic growth since the mid-1990s, poverty remains widespread across Africa, and inadequate access to education and housing still contributes to the increased risk of Plasmodium infection [25]. A lot of effort in malaria eradication is made by the government, especially in African countries, and this does not make an enormous difference, as when youth understand the assignment and take the initiative in malaria eradication contribution. Youth play a significant role in malaria eradication and targeting university students since they are the ones who majorly understand the implications of this disease.

Assistance by university students in manufacturing insecticides and in spraying them in mosquito nets, through collaboration with industries and nonprofit organizations. Long-lasting insecticide-treated nets have been reported to reduce all-cause child mortality by an average of eighteen percent in Sub-Saharan Africa (with a range of 14–29%). This implies that 5.5 lives could be saved per 1000 children under five years of age per year. Use of LLINs in Africa increased mean birth weight by 55 g, reduced low birth weight by 23% and reduced miscarriages/stillbirths by 33% in the first few pregnancies when compared with a control arm in which there were no mosquito nets [26].

University administrators should equip students with the necessary knowledge and skills to participate in malaria surveillance and to volunteer in vulnerable communities for early detection and prevention efforts. Additionally, evidence indicates that during malaria episodes, household expenditures for diagnosis and treatment increase, particularly among families lacking subsidized community-based health insurance (CBHI), a factor that has been associated with delays in seeking timely healthcare [27].

Youth have to advocate for the local community to play a role in malaria prevention. Those young people who have attended schools, especially university students, ought to increase public awareness of the disease of malaria, its different stages, and how it propagates. Lack of knowledge seems to prevent some from seeking medical attention. Sometimes malaria may become very severe and is then associated with convulsions and loss of consciousness; some people may think that they have been bewitched and decide to go for witchcraft. Male, 28 yrs. FGD transportation cooperatives. A variety of herbal medicines were also reported as being used by some community members, including Chenopodiaceae, Tragia brevipes Pax, Basellaceae, Manihot, and Asteraceae [27].

Conclusion

University students showed their contributions toward malaria eradication in Rwanda as they are improving awareness in the population, using technology like mobile apps and drones, and also different outreach programs for educating people. These are valuable changes made by them; their efforts have already been expressed in reducing malaria cases and enhancing the country's strategic plan. However, student engagement is constrained by several factors, including inadequate funding, low levels of trust from community members, and limited support from local authorities. These challenges are further compounded by insufficient training opportunities and instances in which students seek assistance but do not receive adequate responses. These challenges must be clearly identified to enable meaningful and sustained contributions. Given their youth and potential to advocate within communities, students require enhanced training, institutional support, adequate funding, and strong partnerships to effectively contribute to national goals of malaria eradication by 2030 and to improve community understanding of malaria transmission and prevention.
