Microglia heterogeneity in vascular dementia pathology
Authors: Zhanfei Tan, Dongchen Xu, Yu Cao, Wenhan Li, Jinye Ma, Hao Li, Wei Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMicroglia exhibit remarkable phenotypic heterogeneity and functional plasticity across brain regions, time, and disease states. In vascular dementia (VaD)—the second most common dementia—cerebrovascular pathology drives distinct microglial activation states. This review synthesizes current understanding of microglial phenotypes in neurological disease, focusing on their contributions to VaD following vascular insults such as chronic cerebral hypoperfusion and stroke. Key VaD-associated phenotypes are described, including spatially segregated subsets in ischemic territories (ICAM and IPAM microglia), TREM1+-activated microglia in hemorrhagic foci, and cytokine-responsive microglia (CRM) identified in human VaD brain. The molecular drivers of this heterogeneity are discussed, arguing for moving beyond the reductive M1/M2 dichotomy. The broader significance lies in a proposed framework for microglia-targeted therapeutic strategies, encompassing precision immunomodulation, antibody-mediated approaches, and in situ cellular reprogramming as promising avenues for future intervention.

Introduction

Vascular dementia (VaD), the second most common form of dementia, encompasses a range of syndromes caused by cerebrovascular pathology. These pathologies, including chronic hypoperfusion, stroke, and small vessel disease, lead to cognitive impairment through disrupted neural-glial-vascular interactions.1,2 With aging populations and rising cardiovascular disease rates, VaD cases are projected to reach 152.8 million by 2050, posing a severe global health challenge.1,2,3,4

A key trigger is chronic cerebral hypoperfusion, which impairs the neurovascular unit (NVU) and blood-brain barrier.5 This disruption alters the brain’s microenvironment, prompting a neuroinflammatory response and ultimately driving cognitive decline. Understanding early pathological changes—particularly in microglial activation—is therefore critical for diagnosis and therapy.

Microglia, the central nervous system (CNS)’s resident immune cells, are essential first responders. They constantly survey the brain environment and rapidly activate upon injury.6,7,8 However, in VaD, the chronic and multifaceted nature of vascular insults drives a complex microglial response far beyond simple activation.

Distinct microglial subtypes play dynamic and often opposing roles in VaD. For example, after ischemia, pro-inflammatory, glycolytic microglia dominate the core, while neuroprotective, oxidative phosphorylation-dependent microglia populate the penumbra.9 Similarly, specific subtypes are implicated in hemorrhage and chronic white matter damage.10,11 Modern techniques like single-cell RNA sequencing have revealed this remarkable heterogeneity, identifying precise phenotypes such as disease-associated microglia (DAM), lipid-accumulating, and human VaD-specific microglia.11,12,13

This review focuses specifically on how vascular insults shape unique microglial responses in VaD. We summarized recent advances in characterizing this heterogeneity and evaluate the translational potential of targeting these specific microglial states for therapy.

Study selection criteria

Inclusion criteria

Studies were included if they discussed microglial heterogeneity in the context of dementia or related neuropathology. Only English-language publications—including clinical studies, animal models, and review articles—with clearly stated conclusions and well-described methodologies were considered. Furthermore, only studies reporting per-animal replication (as opposed to per-slice or per-cell-culture-well) were included.

Exclusion criteria

Case reports, comments, conference abstracts, and non-English publications were excluded.

Data extraction and analysis

Literature screening and selection were performed independently by two reviewers (J.M. and W.L.). An initial screening was conducted based on titles and abstracts. For records that could not be conclusively categorized based on this information, the full text was examined. Articles that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded. Additional highly relevant preclinical studies and reviews, along with their reference lists, were hand-searched to supplement the electronic database results.

Microglia diversity in development

In the developing brain, microglia exhibit distinct transcriptional profiles across different stages, reflecting their functional diversity and dynamic roles. A comprehensive single-cell study by the Stevens group, which analyzed over 76,000 microglia across ages and states, identified multiple subpopulations—some defined by specialized functions and others representing transitional phenotypes.14 For instance, cluster 6 microglia, highly enriched in embryonic day 14.5 mice, share transcriptional features with limbic macrophages and express markers such as Ccr1, Mrc1, and Dab2, suggesting a precursor identity for perivascular macrophages or mature microglia.14

Further supporting developmental heterogeneity, Francesca et al. reported that peripheral macrophages and microglia exhibit overlapping expression of markers like Gpr84, Socs3, and Tyrobp, indicating potential phenotypic convergence during development.15 Their comparison of microglia at embryonic and early postnatal stages (postnatal day 1) revealed elevated expression of genes including Fxyd6, Stmn2, Fez1, Nsg2, Mtap2, Tubb2, B3gat1, Mapt, and Rtn1—genes also active in neuronal and oligodendrocyte precursors—implying a role for microglia in regulating neurodevelopment. In contrast, adult microglia express transcripts predominantly associated with neural circuit maturation and homeostatic maintenance.15 Another developmentally significant subset, cluster 4, is associated with unmyelinated axon tracts and peaks around postnatal days 4–5. These microglia secrete osteopontin (Spp1), which supports remyelination; yet, this population largely declines before active myelination begins.14 Other clusters, including clusters 2a–c and 3, are enriched in pathways related to metabolism, growth, motility, and proliferation. Meanwhile, inflammatory subpopulations such as cluster 8/OA2/IR2.2 and IR2.3 express neurotoxic mediators including Ccl3, Ccl2, Ccl4, Ccl7, Ccl9, Ccl12, Il1b, and Tnf, suggesting a potential role in developmental neuroinflammation or injury.14

During development, distinct microglia subpopulations play specialized roles in shaping neural circuitry and function. For example, neonatal CD11c+ microglia exhibit significant enrichment of genes associated with cell cycle progression, proliferation, axonogenesis, neuronal projection development, and myelination compared to adult microglia.16 This suggests that the capacity of microglia to support neurogenesis and broader neurodevelopmental processes declines with age.10 Another developmentally critical subset, arginase-1 (ARG1)+ microglia, emerges in the basal forebrain and ventral striatum during early postnatal stages in mice.17 These cells display a unique molecular signature characterized not only by high ARG1 expression, but also upregulated genes such as Apoe, Clec7a, Igf1, Lgals3, and Mgl2.17 Functionally, this subpopulation contributes to the regulation of cholinergic innervation and neuronal projection patterns, underscoring its specific role in the development of cognitive behaviors.17

The link between the development and aging of microglial populations and VaD remains insufficiently supported by direct evidence. Abnormal migration or proliferation of microglia during embryonic stages, or impaired maturation in early postnatal life, may result in neurodevelopmental defects and compromise immune surveillance capabilities.14 Inflammatory microglial subsets rich in chemokines can persist throughout development, reducing the brain’s capacity to manage early inflammatory challenges, thereby increasing susceptibility to VaD in later life.14

In the young brain, microglial heterogeneity is primarily shaped by bioenergetic and immunoregulatory pathways, with regions such as the cerebellum and hippocampus exhibiting heightened immune vigilance. However, aging diminishes the distinct subpopulation profiles between these regions.18 Aged microglia also undergo metabolic shifts, including enhanced mTOR signaling and increased inflammatory responses mediated through translational regulation.18 As a result, senescent microglia may sustain a chronic pro-inflammatory state, occupy pivotal neural niches, and impair the function of anti-inflammatory and regenerative subpopulations. This exacerbates neuroinflammation, damages NVUs, and increases the risk of vascular cognitive impairment.

Regional heterogeneity of microglia

Microglia are not a uniform population but exhibit regionally distinct phenotypic signatures, reflecting specialized roles across the brain. Chotima et al. revealed that human microglia can be categorized into different subpopulations based on their expression of specific markers: subpopulation 1, found predominantly in the subventricular zone and thalamus, express high levels of proliferative markers, including cyclin A, cyclin B1, and Ki67, indicating that microglia in these regions are in a more active state; In contrast, subpopulations 2 and 3 are more prevalent in the temporal and frontal lobes,19 and express lower levels of the M2 polarized macrophage marker, CD206, along with variations in CD64 and EMR1 levels, suggesting regional differences in microglia function and activation state.19

Additional microglia subpopulations and their distributions across different brain regions have also been reported. Rei et al. identified four microglia subpopulations, C0, C1, C2, and C3, each associated with distinct functions, such as electron transport, mRNA metabolic processing, interferon (IFN) response, and chemokine response. C0 microglia are widely distributed across the brain, while C1 microglia are enriched in the hypothalamus.20 C2 microglia are also broadly distributed, whereas the C3 subpopulation are specifically detected in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus.20 Additionally, a high proportion (around 50%) of CD11c microglia was observed in the mouse olfactory bulb at postnatal day 4, and in the cerebellum at postnatal day 7, with decreasing proportions across all regions after postnatal days 4/7; this dynamic distribution is driven by various promoting factors, including Itgax, and CD11c microglia in the forebrain and spinal cord typically express high levels of Igf1, Clec7a, and Trem2.21

Moreover, there are fine interregional differences in the distribution of microglia with varying morphologies. In human transient brain structures, subchondral granular layer microglia adopt a transient morphology, whereas subplate region microglia morphology diversifies according to subplate developmental stage, with differences dependent on tissue development in the cortex and neurogenetic signaling.22 In CNS white matter, microglia contain long primary branches, with few elongated secondary branches (morphotype 3) and short primary branches, with sparse secondary branches (morphotype 4). In contrast, most microglia in the medulla oblongata and spinal cord gray matter branch radially or longitudinally, with relatively sparse secondary branching.23 These differences may be related to the dominant neurological functions of white and gray matter.

Microglia in pathological and aging conditions

Hypoxia-ischemia insults

Hypoxia-ischemia (HI) and ischemic brain injury significantly influence microglia phenotypes and morphology. In the post-ischemic brain, monocyte-derived macrophages infiltrate the affected area, proliferate, and transition into a microglia-like phenotype, characterized by high CD45 expression (CD45high).24 Additionally, oxygen glucose deprivation (OGD) activates resident microglia and converts them into Hsp70 protein associated microglia.25 After a transient ischemic attack, the HSP70+ subgroup aggregates in the infarcted area to enhance phagocytic activity, while the HSP70– subgroup (including rod-shaped microglia) exists in the penumbra, suggesting an activated phenotype in response to infarct migration.25

Intermittent hypoxia triggers a microglia initiation state, marked by upregulation of HLA-DRα, CD86, and CX3CR1,26 which predisposes them to overreact to subsequent inflammatory stimuli, potentially leading to cognitive deficits and impaired brain function.27,28 In neonatal HI models, reduced expression of α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors results in diminished microglia polarization toward a reparative Mox phenotype, further affecting the response of the brain to injury.29

Specific microglia subclusters have been identified in models of ischemic brain injury, such as the mouse model of middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO). These subclusters, including MG1(defined by CCL12, CCL2, and CCL7), MG2 (highly expression of LGALS3, LPL, SPP1, and matrix metalloproteinase 12 [MMP12]), MG3 (marked by ISG15, IFIT3, and IRF7), and MG4 (defined by Cst7, LPL, and ApoE), have distinct gene expression profiles associated with monocyte chemotactic proteins, hypoxia response, viral response, and proliferation, respectively.30 Differential expression analysis for these microglial subclusters was performed with the individual mouse as the independent experimental unit.30 A unique CD11c+ microglia isoform, with upregulation of genes related to phagocytosis and lipid metabolism, was detected in the thalamus following cortical stroke, and may be a form of neurodegenerative DAM (defined by high expression of Cst7, LPL, and ApoE).31

In the early stages of ischemia, at 24 h post-injury, microglia with branching and phagocytic phenotypes are concentrated in the lesion, while Ym1- and CD206-positive microglia cluster in the ischemic core, indicating their protective and injury-preventive roles32; however, by 7 days post-injury, the population of Ym1-positive cells decreases, and that of CD68+ microglia increases, invading the ischemic core and enhancing neuronal phagocytosis and debris removal.32 These findings suggest a dynamic and specific spatial and temporal activation of microglia phenotypes in response to ischemic lesions, reflecting the potential for microglia differentiation influenced by the microenvironment.

Metabolic dysfunction

Metabolic dysfunction significantly influences microglia heterogeneity and function. During the metabolism of acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate, microglia activate the GRP109A receptor, which suppresses their activation after brain injury and promotes a neuroprotective phenotype.33 In mouse models, a high-fat diet upregulates markers of DAM, such as complement component 4B (C4b), potassium inwardly rectifying channel subfamily J member 2 (Kcnj2), and discoidin domain receptor 1 (Ddr1).34 Targeting microglial phenotypic shifts in the context of obesity-related VaD has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy.35

A common clinical feature in VaD is reduced activity of protein phosphatase-2A (PP2A), which acts as an upstream regulator implicated in glucolipid metabolic disorders through multiple signaling pathways including AMPK and AKT.36,37 PP2A is gaining attention as a potential therapeutic target, given its role in modulating the TNF-α/Arg-1 axis to regulate specific subunit phosphorylation and protect microglial polarization.38 Additionally, in chronic cerebral hypoperfusion injury, impaired lipocalin/peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor signaling inhibits neuroprotective microglial responses and exacerbates ischemic neuronal damage.39

Chronic alcohol exposure

Chronic alcohol exposure is widely recognized to accelerate brain aging and contribute to cognitive decline,40 although direct evidence linking it specifically to an increased burden of VaD remains limited. Patients with alcohol-related dementia and those with VaD show comparable deficits in executive control, while individuals with alcohol-related dementia perform slightly worse than those with subcortical VaD in tasks involving delayed verbal recall and recognition.41 This suggests that chronic alcohol abuse may lead to cognition-related neuropathological changes.

Chronic alcohol exposure disrupts microglial function, impairing phagocytic capacity and altering the distribution of microglial subtypes. In individuals with chronic alcohol use, a reduction in perivascular CD68+ microglia has been observed, accompanied by increases in Iba1+ and CD11b+ cells-a pattern indicative of accelerated microglial dystrophy.42 Additionally, a slight upregulation of OX-6/MHCII+ microglia following alcohol intake suggests enhanced susceptibility to ethanol-induced damage.43 In a model of nocturnal alcohol abuse, elevated expression and immunoreactivity of microglial markers such as Iba-1 and Aif-1 further demonstrate sustained microglial reactivity under repeated ethanol exposure.44 Moreover, adolescent rats subjected to alcohol show upregulation of activation markers consistent with a shift toward a reparative or anti-inflammatory phenotype.45

APOE variants and TREM2

APOE, TREM2, and Notch3 represent major genetic risk factors closely associated with VaD. The APOE ε4 allele demonstrates a dose-dependent effect, with the E4/E4 genotype conferring the highest risk of VaD.46,47,48 This risk is mediated through multiple mechanisms, including the promotion of cerebrovascular pathology and neurofibrillary tangle formation, alongside involvement in lipid metabolism, inflammatory responses, and oxidative stress within astrocytes and microglia.49

TREM2, an immune receptor predominantly expressed by microglia, plays a critical role in neuroinflammatory regulation. Functional impairment of TREM2 disrupts downstream signaling cascades, compromising myelin phagocytosis and lipid metabolism, thereby increasing dementia risk.50 Specifically, TREM2-deficient microglia exhibit a reduced capacity to clear myelin-derived cholesterol esters (CEs), leading to pathological lipid accumulation.51 These findings suggest that TREM2-APOE signaling axis may modulate microglial functional phenotypes, thereby regulate lipid transport and metabolism, and contributing to VaD pathogenesis.52

Notably, a recently identified ACSL1-positive microglial subtype, enriched in dementia patients carrying the APOE4/4 genotype, is associated with upregulation of lipid-synthesizing genes.53 These cells promote Tau phosphorylation and exert neurotoxic effects in an APOE-dependent manner.

Heterogeneity of microglia in VaD

Microglia are heterogeneously distributed throughout brain tissue and exhibit a highly dynamic capacity to sense and respond to alterations in the CNS microenvironment. They engage in continuous crosstalk with components of the NVU and the peripheral immune system, integrating multiple signals that collectively influence neural function and integrity.54 Microglial phenotypes are already altered in pre-VaD conditions, such as cerebrovascular disease, and exhibit context-dependent variations across different pathological models and functional states. This functional and spatial heterogeneity plays a critical role in the progression and pathology of VaD. Morphological and signaling changes

The duration of HI injury significantly influences microglial morphology and signaling. Following ischemia, the brain tissue becomes infiltrated by monocyte-derived macrophages, which proliferate, engage in phagocytosis, and eventually transition into a microglia-like CD45high phenotype.24 Although the OGD model can partially simulate certain features of microglial activation—such as morphological changes and the initial suppression of transcription and translation, it fundamentally fails to replicate the hallmark pathogenic responses of the disease.24,55 Most notably, OGD does not induce the specific inflammatory microglial subgroups defined in human tissue by a robust upregulation of MHC class II gene expression.55,56

Temporal and spatial distribution

In experimental models of transient ischemic attack, rod-shaped microglia rapidly appear in the ipsilateral cortical penumbra within 10 min post-injury and persist for 24–48 h after reperfusion.25 This transient morphology is region-specific: after 24 h of OGD, significant neuronal damage in the CA1 region coincided with the presence of rod-shaped microglia, whereas the relatively spared CA3 pyramidal neurons showed no such microglial transformation.57 By 36 h, CA1 neuronal damage intensified, and the rod-shaped morphology was no longer observed; most microglia had transitioned to an amoeboid/phagocytic phenotype.57 This spatiotemporal pattern suggests that rod-shaped microglia represent a transient activated state, potentially intermediary between homeostatic and fully phagocytic phenotypes. Research has demonstrated a lack of significant neuroprotective effect from rod-shaped microglia following ischemic injury.58,59 Interestingly, infrared laser therapy applied during this transitional phase appears to mitigate pro-inflammatory activation, preventing the formation of rod-shaped microglia in CA1 and promoting a shift toward anti-inflammatory phenotypes.57,59 Although their precise function is uncertain, rod-shaped microglia may congregate in areas of severe injury to facilitate phagocytic activity.

The phenotypic response of microglia post-HI is also influenced by developmental stage. Postnatal day 9 mice exhibit reduced microglial branching, while at postnatal day 30, elevated expression of TGF-β receptor, Serpine1, and MerTK suggests a shift toward reparative phenotype characterized by markers such as TGF-β and MerTK.60 In the acute phase of ischemic stroke, distinct spatial partitioning of microglial subpopulations occurs: ischemia core-associated microglia (cluster 3) occupy the central infarct zone, while penumbra-associated microglia (cluster 1) surround the lesion periphery by 12 h post-ischemia.61 The core-associated subpopulation is glycolytically promoted and secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, whereas penumbra-associated microglia are modulated by glucocorticoids to alleviate inflammatory metabolism and demyelination. This spatial dichotomy highlights how heterogeneous microglial responses collectively contribute to maintaining homeostasis after ischemic insult.9

Microglia and immunity in ischemic brain damage

Role of microglia in inflammatory immunity

Microglia have a central role in the inflammatory immune response during ischemic stroke. Patients who are cognitively intact following stroke exhibit activated microglia in the deep white matter, while those with post-stroke dementia have a significantly increased proportion of activated perivascular CD68+ cells, including cells with reactive or amoeboid phenotypes.62 These findings suggest that a subset of CD68+ cells may respond differentially to perivascular inflammation within glial vascular units, contributing to neuropathy in VaD. The analysis using scRNA seq showed that IFN-responsive microglia (IRM) are regulated by C9orf72. Steady state microglia transition to an inflammatory state characterized by enhanced IFN in the absence of C9orf72, enhancing synaptic loss and neuronal defects, while paradoxically improving plaque clearance.63

Microglia subclusters in ischemic injury

In a mouse model of MCAO, five distinct inflammatory ischemia-associated microglial subclusters were identified within the first 24 h post-injury.29 These include the aforementioned: MG1, which expresses genes encoding monocyte chemotactic proteins (CCL12, CCL2, and CCL7) and the hypoxia-associated gene IER3; MG2, the predominant ischemia-associated subcluster, characterized by high expression of MMP12 and ADAM8, implicated in regulating neuroinflammation via transcription factors such as Creb3, Sp3, and Ets2; MG3, which exhibits elevated expression of IFN-stimulated genes (ISG15, IFIT3, and IRF7) and CXCL10, suggesting activation through viral and IFN signaling pathways; MG4 a proliferative subcluster enriched in cell cycle-related genes (BIRC5, UBE2C, TOP2A, and STMN1), potentially amplifying the inflammatory response through expansion.29

Microglia also exhibit divergent differentiation trajectories following ischemic insult. Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed distinct microglial subpopulations following ischemic stroke. Pro-inflammatory subclusters (clusters 4, 9, and 10) were markedly enriched for genes involved in TNFα, IL-6, and IL-2 signaling pathways, aligning with hypoxic and inflammatory responses.64 In contrast, clusters 6 and 8 did not exhibit significant upregulation of canonical anti-inflammatory markers (Arg1, Ym1, and IL-10).64 This disparity suggests a temporal delay in the differentiation of anti-inflammatory microglia compared to the rapid activation of pro-inflammatory populations during the acute phase of ischemia.

A novel stroke-associated microglia (SAM) phenotype has been identified in the acute phase, exhibiting enhanced antioxidant functions through high expression of Prdx1, Txn1, Srx1, and Mt1/2.65 This subpopulation is activated via a Prdx1-dependent pathway in response to ischemia/reperfusion injury, where it helps mitigate oxidative stress and reduce neuronal cell death.65,66 Significantly, spatial and transcriptional analyses took into consideration the non-independence of multiple samples or regions derived from the same animal.9 Ischemic core-associated microglia (ICAM), driven by glycolysis and expressing LGALS3 and PLAU, and ischemic penumbra-associated microglia (IPAM), which are neuroprotective and express PIK3IP1 and GPR65.9 Both subtypes may play essential roles in maintaining tissue homeostasis in distinct regions of the ischemic brain.

Post-stroke thalamic injury

Microglia associated with secondary thalamic injury after cortical infarction exhibit diverse activation states, accompanied by dynamic and differential changes in gene expression and morphology. A distinct CD11c+ microglial subtype, sharing features with DAM, which upregulating phagocytosis and lipid metabolism-related genes Cst7, LPL, and ApoE, has been identified in the post-stroke thalamus.31 These microglia show upregulation of genes involved in phagocytosis and lipid metabolism, suggesting a potentially beneficial role in responding to injury and organizing tissue repair.31 In a rat model of brainstem infarction, SPP1+ microglia interact with oligodendrocytes and promote axonal demyelination of nerve tracts, thereby contributing to protective mechanisms facilitating functional recovery after brainstem stroke.67 However, even during the stroke recovery phase, aged microglia exhibit functional impairments compared to those in younger mice. Transcriptomic analyses reveal a downregulation of genes related to cell motility and intercellular signaling (such as CXCL10), blunted upregulation of pro-angiogenic genes, and delayed accumulation within ischemic brain tissue.66 Such impaired neovascularization can lead to aberrant cerebral perfusion, which in turn contributes to poor neuronal recovery and unfavorable cognitive outcomes.68,69 While these models provide invaluable insights into specific aspects of ischemic injury, it is crucial to interpret their findings within the context of their inherent limitations. These limitations are as follows: (1) bilateral common carotid artery stenosis (BCAS) models chronic white matter disease and cognitive decline, but does not simulate cortical infarcts or amyloid pathology;31,66,67 (2) MCAO model mimics focal ischemia and core-penumbra dynamics, but is primarily acute and not ideal for studying chronic progression;66,67 (3) ICH model directly models hemorrhagic stroke with robust inflammation, but its pathology may not generalize to ischemic VaD subtypes;66,68 and (4) OGD in vitro model offers high mechanistic control and is suitable for screening, but lacks the integrated NVU, systemic inflammation, and chronicity features.68,69

Microglia and intracerebral hemorrhage

ICH represents a severe stroke subtype in which inflammatory processes significantly contribute to secondary brain injury and neurological deterioration. Following ICH, microglia exhibit pronounced phenotypic heterogeneity. Initially, a pro-inflammatory microglial subset emerges, releasing cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and chemokines including CCL2 and CCL3, which amplify neuroinflammation and facilitate the recruitment of peripheral immune cells.70 A key regulator of this inflammatory response is triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 1 (TREM1+), which is upregulated on microglia post-ICH.71 TREM1 activation engages the PKCδ/CARD9 signaling pathway and interacts with its ligand, high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1).71 This interaction suppresses the polarization of microglia toward an M2-like anti-inflammatory phenotype, thereby disrupting the balance between neuroinflammatory and reparative responses. The sustained pro-inflammatory environment exacerbates neural damage and worsens neurobehavioral outcomes, underscoring the role of microglial heterogeneity in the pathogenesis of ICH and its potential implications for VaD.71

Additional mechanisms include iron overload resulting from erythrocyte lysis, which induces ferroptosis and oxidative stress in microglia, further amplifying inflammatory damage.72 Moreover, subsets of microglia contribute to hematoma clearance via phagocytosis, while others release MMPs that disrupt blood-brain barrier integrity,73 highlighting the dual and context-dependent roles of microglia in ICH pathology.

Integration of microglia activity in VaD pathogenesis

Microglia play a central role in the progression of VaD through dynamic interactions with NVU components, peripheral immune cells, and in response to diverse vascular insults including hypoperfusion, ischemic stroke, and ICH.54 Their functional heterogeneity—reflected in distinct phenotypic states, regional specialization, and temporal activation patterns (Table S2)—underpins their contribution to chronic neuroinflammation and cognitive decline in VaD. New evidence further indicates that transcriptomic alterations observed in microglia within VaD models reflect dysregulation in molecular pathways typically associated with AD,11 pointing to a possible convergence between vascular and AD pathology. In addition, microglial metabolic reprogramming, such as a shift toward glycolysis in pro-inflammatory states or oxidative phosphorylation in restorative states, further modulates their function in VaD progression.74

Microglia as markers and therapeutic targets in VaD

Diagnosis

The direct in vivo detection of specific microglial phenotypes in humans remains challenging. Clinically, VaD is primarily diagnosed using neuroimaging techniques that identify characteristic structural lesions. These include cerebral atrophy, white matter hyperintensities (reflecting axonal loss, demyelination, and gliosis), lacunes, and cerebral microbleeds, which are cardinal features of cerebrovascular pathology.75,76

Beyond these conventional structural assessments, advanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques offer insights into processes linked to microglial activity. For instance, detecting iron accumulation in microglia and astrocytes via specific MRI sequences serves as a proxy for axonal injury and neuroinflammation.77 More recently, a novel non-invasive diffusion-weighted MRI method has been developed to visualize morphological changes in glial cells directly. This technique quantifies parameters like rod fraction and dispersion, which exhibit high sensitivity specifically to microglial activation.78 Prior to this, diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance spectroscopy (DW-MRS) had been employed to observe diffusion changes in brain metabolites, providing indirect insight into glial cell morphology.79

Positron emission tomography (PET) represents a valuable non-invasive molecular imaging approach for detecting neuroinflammation. Hypotheses for microglia-targeted therapies in VaD

Current clinical management of VaD remains limited. Here, we frame four emerging strategies as testable working hypotheses, each outlining its rationale, supporting evidence, and specific validation requirements tailored for VaD.

Hypothesis 1: Chemogenetic precision modulation via DREADD/AAV

This hypothesis aims to achieve spatiotemporally precise control of specific microglial signaling pathways using designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADDs) technology.86 Targeted transgene delivery is facilitated by adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors incorporating microglia-specific promoters, such as Hexb.85,87 While this platform is well-established in basic neuroscience, its application to VaD requires rigorous validation: confirming cell-type specificity within the NVU using tools like Hexb-Cre85; demonstrating efficacy in improving white matter integrity, cognitive function, and cerebral blood flow in chronic hypoperfusion models (BCAS); and systematically evaluating long-term safety and potential impacts on neural networks, utilizing DREADD variants with low basal activity and inert ligands.86,88

Hypothesis 2: Nanoparticle-mediated phenotypic reprogramming of microglia

This strategy centers on engineering functionalized nanoparticles to cross the compromised blood-brain barrier in VaD and deliver immunomodulatory payloads (FTY720) specifically to microglia within lesions, thereby polarizing them toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype.89 Proof-of-concept studies in a mouse model of ischemic stroke demonstrated that neutrophil-membrane-camouflaged nanoparticles successfully delivered FTY720 to the ischemic brain, reprogramming microglia via the CEBPB-NLRP3/CCCL2 pathway and improving neurological outcomes.89 For successful translation to VaD, subsequent work must confirm targeting efficiency to microglia in VaD-relevant white matter regions, define the optimal therapeutic dosage within the context of chronic hypoperfusion, and establish attenuation of white matter damage and improvement in vascular cognitive impairment as key efficacy endpoints.

Hypothesis 3: Immunotherapy for pathological protein clearance

This hypothesis proposes to clear VaD-associated pathological proteins (aberrant Tau) or modulate microglial states by inducing sustained, specific immune responses through either active vaccination or passive antibody administration. The rationale is preliminarily supported in neurodegenerative models: for instance, the FlaB-TauRD vaccine reduced pathological Tau and extended survival in P301S Tau transgenic mice (an AD-related model),90 while Bacillus Calmette-Guérin enhanced pro-regenerative microglial functions and reduced myelin debris in aged mouse models of demyelinating disease.91,92 Applying this to VaD presents core challenges: identifying unique VaD-relevant antigenic targets, ensuring sufficient CNS exposure of therapeutic antibodies/immunomodulators, and rigorous safety monitoring, with efficacy ultimately measured by pathological clearance and cognitive recovery.

Hypothesis 4: In situ conversion of microglia to neuroprotective or neuronal phenotypes

This hypothesis proposes leveraging the strategic localization, proliferative capacity, and plasticity of microglia to directly reprogram them into neuroprotective cells or functional neurons via key transcription factors, thereby repairing damaged neural circuits in VaD. Studies in tMCAO mouse models have demonstrated successful conversion of microglia into induced neurons, contributing to functional recovery; notably, this conversion is feasible even from a pro-inflammatory state.93,94 However, major challenges remain for its application in the chronic VaD milieu, including enhancing reprogramming efficiency within the inhibitory microenvironment of persistent inflammation and deficient neurotrophic support, ensuring functional integration of newborn neurons, and evaluating long-term safety risks, such as tumorigenesis. Future research must utilize techniques like live imaging and single-cell sequencing to precisely track the fate and function of converted cells.

Conclusion

This review synthesizes newly identified microglial phenotypes in VaD that extend beyond the traditional binary classification, emphasizing their dynamic roles throughout disease progression. Specific risk factors trigger distinct microglial subtypes that differentially affect recovery and prognosis, while interventions such as a low-fat diet, exercise, and adequate sleep promote beneficial phenotypes and reduce neuroinflammatory damage.

Future research must dissect microglial heterogeneity, particularly distinguishing subtypes linked to early cognitive impairment from those involved in later pathology. Longitudinal clinical studies tracking brain changes are essential to define VaD trajectories and identify high-risk individuals.95

Translating these findings into clinical applications remains challenging. Current evidence is fragmented and reliant on animal models, despite significant differences in human microglial complexity. Humanized chimeric models incorporating human microglia into VaD systems are needed to better mimic human disease and accelerate therapeutic development. Additional challenges include preserving blood-brain barrier integrity, determining optimal intervention timing, and understanding glial interactions.

In summary, clarifying the spatiotemporal specificity of microglial phenotypes in VaD offers promising avenues for preventing and mitigating neurological decline.
