Is Bariatric Surgery at Risk Due to Semaglutide?
Authors: Jane Tian, Shubham Bhatia, Christina Sneed, Patrick Kiarie, Mikayla Fuchs, Andrew Miele, Darshak Shah, Noman Khan, Martine A. Louis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeObesity remains a significant public health issue in the Western world, affecting millions of Americans and contributing to chronic conditions such as diabetes and hypertension. In June 2021, the Food and Drug Administration approved semaglutide for long-term weight management. While its effectiveness in promoting weight loss has been well-documented, its impact on the field of bariatric surgery remains uncertain.Materials and MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 515 bariatric patients from January 2022 to November 2023. Patients on semaglutide in the preoperative period were compared to those not using the medication, with outcomes including time to surgery, cancellation rates, weight loss at different time points, and secondary outcomes such as side effects.ResultsThirteen percent of patients were on semaglutide. Semaglutide users had higher rates of diabetes (P<0.001). Starting and preoperative weights were comparable between both groups. Patients on semaglutide were less likely to proceed with surgery (P=0.001). For patients who had surgery, those on semaglutide experienced longer times from evaluation to surgery, compared to patients not taking this medication (8.8 months vs. 7.4 months, P=0.152). Weight loss outcomes at 2- and 6-month post-surgery were similar between groups. Notably, 22% of semaglutide patients no longer qualified for surgery due to successful weight loss.ConclusionThe use of semaglutide has the potential to reduce the number of bariatric surgeries and delay surgical intervention for some patients. However, the long-term effects of semaglutide on weight maintenance and its broader implications for clinical practice require further investigation.

INTRODUCTION

Obesity is a global epidemic with serious health consequences, including type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), cardiovascular disease, and reduced quality of life. Bariatric surgery, particularly sleeve gastrectomy (SG) and Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB), has demonstrated remarkable efficacy in achieving sustained weight loss and improving metabolic outcomes [1]. Effective weight management is important to reduce associated morbidity and mortality and mitigate costs of obesity and associated chronic diseases, projected to reach $1.71 trillion in the United States. Since the onset of the first bariatric procedure for weight loss in the 1950s, the last 60 years have shown improved techniques with better outcomes and lower complication rates [2]. Landmark studies like the STAMPEDE (Surgical Treatment and Medications Potentially Eradicate Diabetes Efficiently) trial have highlighted the superiority of bariatric surgery over intensive medical therapy for long-term glycemic control and cardiovascular risk reduction in patients with obesity and T2DM [3].

Parallel advancements in pharmacological therapies, such as semaglutide, are reshaping treatment paradigms. The STEP (Semaglutide Treatment Effect in People with Obesity) trials confirmed semaglutide’s efficacy in inducing significant weight loss and improving cardiometabolic health in non-surgical settings. Semaglutide’s potential to enhance preoperative weight loss or, in some cases, delay or eliminate the need for surgery warrants investigation. This study evaluates the impact of semaglutide as a preoperative treatment strategy for patients in a bariatric surgery practice.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This retrospective cohort study reviewed the medical records of bariatric surgery patients evaluated at a single institution from January 2022 to November 2023. Patients were categorized into 2 groups based on whether or not they were on semaglutide. Data was collected on demographic variables, comorbid conditions, weight-related metrics, and surgical outcomes to assess differences between these groups.

Criteria for semaglutide use were multifactorial and included patient comorbidities (such as the presence of diabetes), patient preference for a trial of pharmacologic therapy, and the recommendations of the primary care physician. Dosing ranged from 0.25 mg to 2 mg weekly, and the duration of therapy varied from 8.5 to 38 months. Adherence to therapy was confirmed during follow-up preoperative visits with the bariatric surgery team. Patients were not restarted on semaglutide during the 6-month postoperative follow-up period. Patients were considered to ‘no longer qualify’ for bariatric surgery if, during follow-up, their body mass index (BMI) dropped below 35 kg/m2 and they no longer met National Institutes of Health or institutional criteria for surgical candidacy.

The primary outcomes included the time from initial surgical evaluation to surgery, cancellation rates, and weight loss at specific time points (initial evaluation, preoperative assessment, and post- surgery weight loss at 2 and 6 months). Post-surgery weight loss data was only available for 6 months at the time of data collection. Secondary outcomes addressed the frequency of side effects or adverse events linked to semaglutide use and whether patients remained eligible for bariatric surgery following weight loss during the preoperative period.

The study was approved by Flushing Hospital Medical Center Institutional Review Board (IRB) prior to data collection and analysis (IRB ID #2136313). The board determined that the study met exempt criteria 4iii as it included only secondary use of information collected for healthcare operations per HIPAA Privacy Rule 45 CFR 164.501. The board determined that the study was eligible for a waiver of informed consent, as it satisfied the criteria in HIPAA Privacy Rule 45 CFR 46.117c.

The analysis utilized statistical tests tailored to the nature of the data. Continuous variables such as weight and BMI were evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test or t-test, while categorical variables such as surgery completion and the presence of comorbidities were assessed using the χ2 test. Nonparametric tests were used where sample sizes were small and did not demonstrate a normal distribution. Statistical significance was set at a P value of less than 0.05. All analyses were performed via R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The study was conducted, and the results reported, according to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

RESULTS

A total of 515 patients were included in the study cohort, with 69 patients (13%) identified as semaglutide users and 446 patients (87%) classified as non-semaglutide users. The 2 groups exhibited similar demographic and clinical characteristics. Patients in the semaglutide group were similar in age, with a mean age of 42±12.1 years compared to 45.1±13.6 years in the non-semaglutide group (P=0.075). Ethnic distribution was similar between groups, with no significant differences (P=0.945). The proportion of females was higher in both groups, but slightly lower among semaglutide users compared to non- users (75.4% vs. 82.5%, respectively; P=0.208) A significantly greater proportion of semaglutide users had diabetes mellitus (44.9% vs. 22.4%; P<0.001), while no significant differences were observed for hypertension (52.2% vs. 41%; P=0.107) or obstructive sleep apnea (53.65% vs. 43.5%; P=0.149). Patients on semaglutide were significantly more likely to not require bariatric procedures (66.7% vs. 45.1%; P=0.001) (Table 1).

Outcomes differed significantly between the groups. Patients in the semaglutide group were less likely to proceed with surgery, with only 33.3% undergoing a bariatric procedure compared to 54.9% in the non-semaglutide group (P=0.001) (Table 1). Furthermore, the median time from evaluation to surgery was extended in the semaglutide group, with an average duration of 8.82 months compared to 7.44 months in the non-semaglutide group (P=0.152) (Table 3, Fig. 2). Accordingly, those who underwent surgery were on semaglutide for a longer duration with mean of 12.4 months (standard deviation [SD], 9.97) compared to those that did not undergo surgery who were on semaglutide for mean of 6.26 months (SD, 4.98) (P=0.02) (Table 4). Despite these differences, postoperative weight loss outcomes at 2 and 6 months did not differ significantly between the 2 groups. At 2 months post-surgery, the median weight loss in the semaglutide group was 9.22% (IQR, 5.40, range, 1.08–19.9), and in the non-semaglutide group, it was 10.3% (IQR, 5.71, range, 2.21–77.9) (P=0.496). At 6 months, the weight loss remained similar, with a median of 18.5% in the semaglutide group vs. 20.2% in the non-semaglutide group (P=0.160) (Table 2, Fig. 3).

Notably, 22% of patients in the semaglutide group no longer qualified for bariatric surgery during the evaluation period due to successful weight loss achieved with pharmacologic therapy. Reported adverse events associated with semaglutide were minimal in our patient population.

DISCUSSION

Semaglutide is a GLP-1 receptor agonist that is used in the treatment of type 2 diabetes. The route of entry is a subcutaneous injection once per week. In a 2015 study, Ahrén et al. [4] found that semaglutide was superior to sitagliptin at decreasing body weight and improving glycemic control in type 2 diabetes patients on metformin, thiazolidinediones, or both. In fact, semaglutide appeared to be a much more effective add-on treatment in the patient population studied.

The integration of semaglutide into obesity management raises important considerations for clinical practice. As pharmacologic options like semaglutide become more prevalent, clinicians must carefully evaluate patients for bariatric surgery, ensuring that those who achieve initial success with medical therapy are adequately monitored for sustained weight loss. The potential for reduced surgical demand also has implications for healthcare resource allocation, emphasizing the need for long-term follow-up in patients treated with semaglutide to assess durability and address emerging challenges. In our study, patients in the semaglutide group were less likely to proceed with surgery, with only 33.3% undergoing a bariatric procedure compared to 54.9% in the non-semaglutide group (P=0.001).

The results align with existing evidence on semaglutide’s efficacy in managing obesity. The STEP trials demonstrated its ability to achieve clinically meaningful weight loss, reinforcing its role as a primary pharmacologic treatment for obesity and a complement to surgical strategies [56]. In the STAMPEDE trial, semaglutide was compared with bariatric surgery for weight reduction. Semaglutide showed significant weight loss in patients but was associated with adverse effects such as gastrointestinal issues, increased heart rate, and rare cases of thyroid cancer [3]. Unfortunately, after semaglutide withdrawal, patients reported weight regain and cardiometabolic abnormalities. Conversely, bariatric surgery demonstrated higher efficacy in weight loss and resolution of comorbidities such as diabetes. Patients undergoing RYGB and SG specifically achieved better diabetic targets, such as lowered glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels [7]. Despite this, the pharmacological option of semaglutide provides significant weight reduction and associated improvements in comorbidities, so it should be considered an effective management strategy for obesity [8]. Specifically, reductions in waist circumference, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, and improvements in HbA1c and lipid levels have been observed [5910]. While the long-term sustainability of weight loss with semaglutide is still being evaluated, its potential to serve as a bridging therapy or standalone option is evident.

A cautious approach wherein pharmacologic therapy is tried before surgical options are pursued appears warranted. Our study found that, while not statistically significant, semaglutide prolonged the time to surgery (8.8 months vs. 7.4 months, P=0.152). This delay in surgery likely reflects the time required for patients to undergo adequate trials of pharmacologic therapy. Notably, patients who ultimately proceeded to bariatric surgery were on semaglutide for a longer duration compared with those who did not undergo surgery (12.4 months vs. 6.3 months, P=0.02). This suggests that, in some cases, semaglutide may serve as a bridge therapy, providing symptom or weight control while patients prepare for surgery, whereas in others it may delay or even replace the need for operative intervention. While this extension could be viewed as an opportunity for preoperative optimization, it may also risk exacerbating obesity-related comorbidities for patients requiring more immediate intervention. This necessitates a balance between leveraging semaglutide’s benefits and avoiding delays in definitive treatment. Additionally, the prolonged timeline may influence patient motivation and adherence, both of which are critical in achieving successful outcomes with surgical interventions.

However, while semaglutide offers significant weight loss benefits, bariatric surgery remains the gold standard for managing severe obesity, especially for addressing comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, and gastroesophageal reflux disease, which require durable and multifaceted interventions. The substantial reduction in surgical candidacy among semaglutide users in our study highlights its efficacy as a medical therapy yet raises important clinical questions. Is the reduced surgical demand a reflection of successful pharmacologic management or an unintended barrier to definitive treatment? Further studies are needed to evaluate long-term outcomes in patients who forgo surgery due to semaglutide use, particularly regarding weight maintenance, comorbidity resolution, and quality of life. Additionally, the role of semaglutide in patients with extreme obesity or complex comorbidities, for whom surgery is often the most effective treatment, warrants closer examination.

While this study highlights promising findings, limitations such as its retrospective design and relatively short follow-up period post procedure (6 months) warrant cautious interpretation. Future research should explore the long-term efficacy of semaglutide, its impact on obesity-related comorbidities, and its integration into comprehensive treatment algorithms. Randomized controlled trials comparing semaglutide with bariatric surgery would provide valuable insights into optimizing care for patients with severe obesity.

CONCLUSION

Semaglutide is reshaping obesity management, offering significant benefits both as a standalone therapy and as an adjunct to bariatric surgery. By integrating pharmacologic and surgical approaches, clinicians can provide personalized treatment strategies that address the complex and multifaceted challenges of obesity. This comprehensive approach has the potential to enhance patient outcomes and redefine the standard of obesity care.
