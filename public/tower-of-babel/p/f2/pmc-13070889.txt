Optimizing the diagnosis of primary aldosteronism: The role of adrenal vein sampling and the need for high‐expertise reference centers
Authors: Aura D Herrera‐Martínez, Ángel Rebollo‐Román, Pablo Remón‐Ruiz, María José Picón‐César, Patricia Guirado‐Peláez, Carmen Tenorio‐Jiménez, Carlos López‐Pereira, Manuel Aguilar‐Diosdado, Marta Iturregui‐Guevara, Viyey K Doulatram‐Gamgaram, Maria Eugenia Pérez‐Montilla, Juan José Espejo‐Herrero, Ma Ángeles Galvez‐Moreno, Alfonso Soto‐Moreno, the Neuroendocrine Group of the Andalusian Society of Endocrinology, Diabetes and Nutrition
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdrenal vein sampling (AVS) is the cornerstone diagnostic procedure for subtype differentiation in primary hyperaldosteronism, guiding therapeutic decision‐making and offering the possibility of curative surgery to a subset of patients. However, technical challenges limit its use, and success rates vary substantially across centers, restricting equitable access for all patients with primary hyperaldosteronism. To evaluate AVS success rates in major hospitals across Andalucía and to assess the clinical evolution of patients who underwent surgery, with the goal of identifying referral centers and optimizing patient care. A retrospective observational study was conducted across six centers in Andalucía. A total of 151 procedures were performed in 140 patients, with an overall success rate of 55.62%. Intercenter analysis revealed successful catheterization rates ranging from 25% to 75.5%. Among patients without a computed tomography‐detected adenoma, 27.4% demonstrated unilateral or bilateral aldosterone secretion. In patients with a single adenoma, discordant lateralization was observed in 9.80%, and bilateral secretion in 11.76%. Half of the patients with bilateral adenomas exhibited unilateral secretion. In total, discordant results reached 21.57%. Following surgery, all patients with successful AVS discontinued potassium supplementation, accompanied by a significant reduction in antihypertensive medication requirements (p < .001). There were no significant differences among patients that underwent unilateral adrenalectomy based on imaging or AVS. Surgical management of primary hyperaldosteronism leads to significant clinical improvement, which is most accurately achieved when guided by successful AVS. Identifying and supporting expert centers is essential to improve access to AVS and enhance clinical outcomes for patients with primary hyperaldosteronism.Keywords: adrenal vein sampling, curation, primary aldosteronism, surgery

INTRODUCTION

Primary aldosteronism is a leading cause of secondary hypertension worldwide and has gained growing recognition for its substantial public health impact due to its high prevalence and associated morbidity. Recent studies estimate that primary hyperaldosteronism accounts for 4%–13% of hypertensive cases in general practice, with prevalence reaching up to 20% among individuals with resistant hypertension, depending on diagnostic protocols and clinical settings. 1 , 2 , 3 Once considered a rare entity, primary hyperaldosteronism is now known to be markedly underdiagnosed. 4 Population‐based registries, such as the SPAIN‐ALDO study, have demonstrated significant regional variations in cardiometabolic burden and in the completeness of the diagnostic cascade, both in Spain and across Europe. 5

The prevalence of primary hyperaldosteronism increases with hypertension severity—from approximately 3.9% in stage 1 hypertension to over 11.8% in Stage 3 hypertension. Aldosterone‐producing adenomas (APAs) are more frequently identified in women, whereas idiopathic adrenal hyperplasia appears to be more common in certain populations, including individuals of African descent. Diagnostic rates also vary considerably across studies due to differences in patient selection, assay methodologies, and diagnostic thresholds, which limit cross‐study comparability. 1 , 6

Beyond its prevalence, primary hyperaldosteronism carries a disproportionate risk of cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and renal complications compared to essential hypertension. Patients exhibit a 3‐ to 12‐fold higher risk of stroke, atrial fibrillation, coronary artery disease, and heart failure. 4 Additional comorbidities such as hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, obesity, gout, chronic kidney disease, and depression are common. Delayed or missed diagnosis contributes to irreversible end‐organ damage, greater long‐term morbidity, and increased mortality. 4 , 7

Despite the availability of standardized screening and confirmatory algorithms, primary hyperaldosteronism remains underdiagnosed. Major barriers include limited clinical awareness, restricted access to confirmatory testing, and the technical complexity of key diagnostic procedures. 8 The performance of hormonal assays is influenced by factors such as salt intake, medication withdrawal, and the use of non‐standardized cut‐off values. Among confirmatory and subtype differentiation tests, adrenal vein sampling (AVS) remains the gold standard for distinguishing unilateral from bilateral disease, thereby guiding the choice between surgical and medical treatment. However, AVS is technically demanding, requires substantial operator expertise, and is not uniformly accessible. Reported failure rates range from 5% to 30%, largely due to challenges in right adrenal vein cannulation. 9 Although the use of intra‐procedural adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) stimulation can enhance technical success, it still can cause diagnostic discordance by altering lateralization indices, complicating result interpretation and standardization. 1 , 2

The economic burden of primary hyperaldosteronism is considerable, driven by its cardiovascular and renal complications, lifelong medical monitoring, and the costs associated with complex diagnostic and therapeutic procedures. 5 Surgical removal of a unilateral aldosterone‐producing adenoma typically results in biochemical cure or marked clinical improvement, with substantial reductions in cardiovascular risk and long‐term healthcare expenditures. Conversely, medical therapy with mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists is effective in bilateral forms or when surgery is contraindicated, but it often provides only partial blood pressure control and incomplete reversal of comorbidities. 2 Inequities in diagnostic access—particularly to AVS—may cause differences in care quality, therapeutic outcomes, and costs across regions and healthcare systems. 2 , 10

Given these considerations, accurate diagnosis, subtype classification, and appropriate treatment of primary hyperaldosteronism are essential to reduce the global burden of hypertension‐related morbidity and mortality. In this context, AVS represents a pivotal step in the diagnostic algorithm, directly influencing clinical decision‐making and outcomes. However, its successful implementation depends on the availability of experienced interventional radiologists, standardized protocols, and multidisciplinary collaboration. Establishing and recognizing reference centers with demonstrated expertise in AVS—characterized by high success rates and low complication profiles—is critical to optimize patient care and ensure equitable access to definitive diagnosis and treatment worldwide. 2 , 4

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and according to national and international guidelines. A retrospective study was performed for analyzing the data of the main hospitals of Andalucia in which AVS is performed (Reina Sofía University Hospital, Córdoba [RSUH]; Virgen de la Victoria University Hospital, Málaga [VVUH]; Puerta del Mar University Hospital, Cádiz [PMHU]; Regional University Hospital, Málaga [RUH]; Virgen de las Nieves University Hospital, Granada [VNUH]; Virgen del Rocío University Hospital, Seville [VRHU]). Anonymized internal data from each participating hospital were analyzed to optimize clinical patient care; therefore, approval from the institutional review board was waived. All procedures performed between January 2020 and January 2025 were analyzed. All patients were treated according to the current available guidelines. 8 , 11 In those patients treated with spironolactone, this treatment was suspended at least during 6 weeks before AVS in all cases. Potassium supplementation per day was analyzed; each potassium pill contains 10 mEq of potassium. Data were collected from the clinical histories, and specific protocols were analyzed in each center.

Adrenal venous sampling

Briefly, the technical procedure used at the center with the highest success rate is described. AVS was routinely scheduled as the first procedure of the day. Thirty minutes prior to catheterization, a continuous intravenous infusion of ACTH (50 μg/h) was initiated. In this center, all procedures were performed by two interventional radiologists selected from a team of six, with individual experience ranging from 3 to 18 years. Under local anesthesia and ultrasound guidance, a vascular introducer sheath was placed in each common femoral vein. Subsequently, under fluoroscopic guidance, both adrenal veins were catheterized using a 4‐French catheter. 12 Once both adrenal veins had been successfully catheterized, venography was performed to confirm correct positioning within the adrenal veins, followed immediately by blood sampling. Simultaneous blood samples (3–5 mL) were obtained from both adrenal veins through the previously positioned catheters, as well as from the iliac veins via the introducer sheaths. After reconfirming catheter positioning in both the right and left adrenal veins by manual contrast injection, blood samples were promptly sent to the laboratory for immediate analysis. Cortisol (μg/dL) and aldosterone (ng/dL) concentrations were measured in samples from each adrenal vein and from peripheral blood while the patient remained in the procedure room. Once adrenal vein cortisol concentrations were confirmed to be higher than those in peripheral blood, the ACTH infusion was discontinued and the introducer sheaths were removed, with manual compression applied to achieve vascular hemostasis. If this criterion was not met, the procedure (adrenal vein catheterization and blood sampling) was repeated at least once. 13

The selectivity index (SI) was calculated as the ratio of plasma cortisol concentration in the adrenal vein to that in the inferior vena cava. The relative aldosterone secretion index (RASI) was calculated as follows: ([plasma aldosterone concentration dominant adrenal vein/plasma cortisol concentration dominant adrenal vein]/[plasma aldosterone concentration non‐dominant adrenal vein/plasma cortisol concentration dominant non‐adrenal vein]). The lateralization index (LI) was calculated as the ratio of the aldosterone‐to‐cortisol concentration in the adrenal vein to that in the inferior vena cava. Adequate catheterization was defined as SI ≥5. Unilateral aldosterone production was considered when LI >4, whereas bilateral production was defined by LI <3.

Statistical analysis

Between‐group comparisons were analyzed by the Mann–Whitney U test (nonparametric data). Chi‐squared test was used to compare categorical data. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software version 30 and Graph Pad Prism version 10. Data are expressed as mean with standard deviations and percentages. Absolute differences in some parameters were calculated using mean values. For specific group analysis, the absolute number has been also expressed in brackets. p values <.05 were considered statistically significant.

Technical AVS results

In the overall cohort, successful catheterization was achieved in 55.62% of procedures, with a complication rate of 3.3%. No severe complications occurred; all reported events were limited to local hematomas. Repeat AVS was unsuccessful in only two patients, both of whom declined further attempts (RSUH, RUH). In patients with successful catheterization, 32.1% presented right adrenal secretion (n = 27), 36.9% presented left adrenal secretion (n = 3) and 31% (n = 26) bilateral secretion.

Clinical evolution of patients with primary hyperaldosteronism

Overall, 42.1% (59/137) of patients underwent unilateral adrenalectomy (3 are pending). A single CT‐visible adenoma was identified in 43 cases (72.9%); 11/59 (18.6%) had no CT‐detectable adenoma, and 5/59 (8.5%) had bilateral adenomas.

Of those that underwent surgery, 77.97% (46/59) had a successful AVS, and 87% showed unilateral secretion on AVS (n = 40). Surgery for symptom control was performed in 13% (6/46) despite bilateral aldosterone production on AVS.

Among patients who underwent surgery (n = 59), all discontinued potassium supplementation (p < .001). After adrenalectomy, 69.5% (41/59) still required antihypertensive therapy, with a mean of 1.36 ± 1.27 drugs compared with 3.12 ± 1.45 at baseline (p < .001); only one patient continued on spironolactone.

In patients with successful AVS, 67.4% presented with hypokalemia and 58.7% required more than two antihypertensive drugs. A total of 54.8% (46/84) underwent unilateral adrenalectomy, 40 (86.96%) based on AVS lateralization and 6 (13.04%) for symptom control. Notably, 21.7% (10/46) had no detectable lesion on CT, while 8.7% (4/46) had bilateral lesions. Left adenomas (41.3%) were more frequently observed on CT than right‐sided ones (28.3%). These patients required a mean of 3.06 ± 1.37 antihypertensive agents preoperatively, which decreased to 1.40 ± 1.28 postoperatively (p < .001). Potassium supplementation decreased from 1.15 ± 1.63 pills/day preoperatively to none after surgery (p < .001).

In patients with unsuccessful AVS, 46.2% (6/13) presented with hypokalemia and 76.9% (10/13) required more than two antihypertensive drugs. One patient without an identifiable adenoma on CT underwent surgery targeting the larger adrenal gland. A unilateral adenoma was observed in 84.7% (11/13), and one patient had bilateral adenomas; the gland containing the largest lesion was resected. Patients required a mean of 2 ± 0.96 potassium pills/day preoperatively, decreasing to none postoperatively (p < .001). The mean number of antihypertensive drugs decreased from 3.46 ± 1.19 to 1 ± 1 per day (p < .01).

There were no significant differences in the number of potassium supplements or antihypertensive drugs of patients that underwent surgery based on image or AVS (p = .30 and p = .10, respectively). In patients who did not undergo surgery, the mean potassium supplementation was 1 ± 2.13 pills/day and the mean antihypertensive requirement was 3.42 ± 1.56 drugs/day; all patients were treated with spironolactone or eplerenone (only one case).

DISCUSSION

AVS is the cornerstone diagnostic procedure for subtype differentiation in primary hyperaldosteronism, a determination that critically influences therapeutic decision‐making and offers the possibility of curative surgery to a subset of patients. The clinical need for accurate lateralization stems from the frequent discordance between anatomical findings on cross‐sectional imaging and the functional assessment provided by AVS, which may lead to inappropriate management, unnecessary adrenalectomy, and suboptimal cardiovascular outcomes. 14 , 15 The adrenal vein sampling international study (AVIS‐2) study reported absence of adrenal CT findings in 30.1% of a cohort of 1625 patients and bilateral disease in 8.4%. 16 In our study, the rate of negative imaging findings was 17.85%, while bilateral lesions were observed in 16.4% of cases; the clinical relevance of AVS in these patients is mandatory, in whom the therapeutic approach may change substantially.

Comparative analyses consistently demonstrate the superiority of AVS over CT and MRI for localizing the source of aldosterone excess. Meta‐analyses including more than 4600 individuals show that CT/MRI provides only moderate sensitivity (68%) and specificity (57%) for detecting unilateral primary hyperaldosteronism when compared with AVS, with discordance rates up to 38% 17 , 18 ; in this cohort it reached 21.57%. Similarly, some studies indicate that even among younger patients (≤40 years), nearly one‐fifth would be misclassified as surgical candidates based on imaging alone, underscoring the limitations of noninvasive modalities. 15 , 18 In routine practice, discordance between imaging and AVS ranges from 22% to 28%, with most clinically relevant discrepancies involving right‐sided hypersecretion—an inherently challenging site often missed by imaging. 15 As expected, in this cohort, right‐sided adenomas were significantly less frequent than left‐sided lesions.

Meta‐analyses indicate that AVS‐guided identification of unilateral primary hyperaldosteronism results in biochemical cure rates approaching 94% and clinical improvement or remission of hypertension in 68%–100% of cases. 14 , 19 , 20 In this study, all patients undergoing AVS‐guided adrenalectomy discontinued potassium supplementation and achieved a >50% reduction in antihypertensive requirements. Consistent with previous reports, 21.70% of patients with non‐localizing CT findings benefited from AVS when unilateral secretion was demonstrated. 14 , 20 Unilateral disease accounts for 40%–55% of primary hyperaldosteronism cases evaluated with AVS but is underestimated when management relies solely on imaging. 21 In this cohort, 78.43% presented concordant unilateral secretion.

AVS technical performance varies substantially. Success rates in high‐volume expert centers exceed 85%–90%, but may fall below 70% in low‐volume sites, with right adrenal vein cannulation representing the primary technical challenge. 21 , 22 Evidence suggests that operator experience, intra‐procedural cortisol measurement, ACTH stimulation, and the use of dynaCT significantly enhance procedural success and correct lateralization. 23 , 24 A significant difference in success rates between one center and the remaining centers was observed, likely related to the earlier implementation of AVS and the higher procedural volume at that center, resulting in greater technical expertise. In addition, intra‐procedural cortisol measurement was implemented at this center, which has been shown to improve success rates in larger cohorts. 10 Systematic reviews highlight that ACTH stimulation increases selectivity indices and procedural yield, though it remains a source of controversy regarding protocol standardization. 24 AVS failure may perpetuate inequities in care, 22 , 23 while current guidelines uniformly recommend AVS for subtype diagnosis in all surgical candidates, with rare exceptions such as young patients (<35 years) with a distinct unilateral adrenal adenoma and characteristic biochemical findings. 21 , 22 Referral to experienced high‐volume centers is recommended, as technical success and interpretative accuracy correlate strongly with operator expertise—a position endorsed by the Endocrine Society and other international bodies. 21 , 22 In this line, the largest Spanish cohort of AVS included 153 AVS with a success rate of 44.4%, 25 similar to our study, there were no significant differences between patients who underwent surgery based on image results and those who underwent surgery based on AVS results. 25 Several limitations should be acknowledged, including the retrospective design, potential selection bias, small subgroup sizes, and the absence of absolute hormone measurements. Consequently, patient outcomes may not fully reflect those observed in expert reference centers. The absence of formal biochemical outcome measures. In this study, we aimed to identify centers with the highest success rates in Andalucía to establish regional referral pathways and ensure equitable diagnostic access for all primary hyperaldosteronism patients.

The biochemical diagnosis of primary hyperaldosteronism remains challenging, given the variability in screening and confirmatory cut‐off values. 26 With regard to AVS, the use of hormonal stimulation during the procedure remains controversial. According to the AVIS‐2 study, cosyntropin‐stimulated AVS increases the success rate of bilateral catheterization, reduces steroid hormone fluctuations, and allows for stricter selectivity criteria; however, it may reduce the LI and, consequently, the proportion of patients eligible for surgery. 10 Importantly, in the present study, all centers performed stimulated AVS, which may have resulted in a lower proportion of patients undergoing surgery and deriving clinical benefit.

Untreated or insufficiently managed primary hyperaldosteronism is associated with a two‐ to fourfold increased risk of atrial fibrillation, stroke, coronary artery disease, heart failure, and all‐cause mortality compared with essential hypertension. 6 , 19 , 27 Comorbidity burden is substantial: hypertension in 56%, hypokalemia in 8%, obesity in 31%, diabetes in 25%, hepatic steatosis in 11%, and gout in 12%, all significantly more frequent than in essential hypertension cohorts. 4 , 7 In this cohort, 61.30% of patients required more than two antihypertensive agents, and hypokalemia reached 30%. After AVS‐guided surgery, resolution of hypokalemia occurs in 75%–93% of cases, and regression of left ventricular hypertrophy, albuminuria, and metabolic disturbances has been documented. 28 Major adverse cardiovascular events significantly decrease following adrenalectomy compared with continued medical therapy. 21 Conversely, incorrect lateralization may result in persistent hypertension and ongoing cardiometabolic risk, emphasizing the need for rigorous AVS protocols for all surgical candidates.

Clinically, primary hyperaldosteronism surgery outcomes consensus outcomes show that approximately 37% of patients achieve complete clinical remission of hypertension following adrenalectomy, with 47% achieving partial improvement; these outcomes are observed both in classic adenomas and in AVS‐lateralized surgery with non‐definitive imaging. 14 , 29 Biochemical cure exceeds 90%. 29 However, 15%–30% of patients achieve neither clinical nor biochemical remission, often due to misclassification, small adenoma size, or concomitant essential hypertension. 14

Despite the consensus on AVS as the gold standard for primary hyperaldosteronism lateralization, significant limitations persist. Protocol standardization is needed. Practices regarding baseline versus ACTH‐stimulated AVS, cut‐off thresholds, cosyntropin dosing, and selectivity criteria differ among centers, limiting data comparability and outcome benchmarking. 10 , 22 , 24 Cost‐effectiveness analyses support AVS based on its long‐term reductions in cardiovascular events and medication burden, but these benefits vary geographically due to technical and referral disparities.

Regarding long‐term medical therapy with spironolactone or eplerenone, available data show partial efficacy but persistent cardiovascular risk. Large meta‐analyses demonstrate that patients treated chronically with mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists have a higher risk of major cardiovascular events than those undergoing surgery or essential hypertension controls. 27 Specifically, medically treated primary hyperaldosteronism is associated with twice the cardiovascular risk compared with essential hypertension (OR 2.11, 95% confidence interval: 1.88–2.38), while reduced all‐cause mortality is confined to surgically treated patients. 27 In a large observational cohort, 67% of mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist‐treated patients had suppressed renin, indicating inadequate aldosterone blockade and continued cardiometabolic harm. 21 However, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists therapy does promote regression of vascular and cardiac remodeling, and in some cohorts, carotid intima‐media thickness improves comparably to post‐adrenalectomy patients at 6‐year follow‐up. 30 However, discontinuation due to adverse effects (gynecomastia, menstrual abnormalities, and hyperkalemia) reaches 20%–30%, and dose titration is often suboptima. 31

Beyond blood pressure and potassium normalization, surgical cure confers additional benefits, including >60% reduction in atrial fibrillation risk, decreased incidence of diabetes, slower progression of chronic kidney disease and proteinuria, and regression of target organ damage (left ventricular hypertrophy, albuminuria, and retinal changes). 6 , 28 Nonetheless, residual metabolic and vascular risk persists in some patients, particularly those with advanced preexisting end‐organ damage. 32

In summary, cumulative evidence supports AVS as the definitive diagnostic test for subtype differentiation in primary hyperaldosteronism, yielding superior clinical and biochemical outcomes, reduced cardiovascular risk, and improved survival with AVS‐guided adrenalectomy compared with medical therapy or imaging‐based decision‐making. Efforts to expand AVS availability, improve procedural success, and harmonize protocols should be prioritized to ensure equitable and optimal care for all patients with primary hyperaldosteronism. Overall, this study highlights the importance of nationwide or regional evaluations to identify appropriate reference centers for AVS.
