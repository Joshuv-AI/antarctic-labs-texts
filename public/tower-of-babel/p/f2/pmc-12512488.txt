Long-term symptoms after SARS-CoV-2 infection in a cohort of healthcare workers in Qatar
Authors: Shuja Reagu, Majid Alabdulla, Nisha Kalariparambil Abdul Kader, Nighat Ara Ajmal, Sara Kamal Hassan Abdelgafar, Ovais Wadoo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDuring the Coronavirus Disease 2019 pandemic, healthcare workers faced a significantly elevated risk of exposure and infection compared to the general population due to their frontline roles in patient care. This heightened risk was compounded by the nature of their work environment and prolonged contact with infected individuals. Despite extensive research on the virus’s immediate impact, limited data exists regarding the prevalence of long-term symptoms, commonly referred to as Long COVID, among healthcare workers. In this study, we aimed to investigate the prevalence and profile of long-term symptoms following SARS-CoV-2 infection, along with associated risk factors, among healthcare workers, with a particular focus on regional data from the Middle East, and to contextualize these findings within the global evidence base.MethodsThis cross-sectional, web-based survey was conducted among healthcare workers at Hamad Medical Corporation, the largest healthcare provider in Qatar, between 24 January 2022 and 24 April 2022. Healthcare workers aged 18 years or older, including physicians, nurses, allied healthcare professionals, and administrative staff, were eligible to participate. The survey assessed the prevalence of Long COVID-19 symptoms, and the symptoms of depression, and anxiety. Over a four-week data collection period, we received 783 complete responses, which exceeded the minimum required sample size.ResultsOf the respondents, three-quarters reported long-term symptoms after SARS-CoV-2 infection, and one-third reported more than three symptoms. Fatigue was the most common symptom. Gender disparities were observed, with females experiencing a higher incidence of symptoms. Significant associations were identified between a history of SARS-CoV-2 infection, a psychiatric history, symptoms of depression, and the development of multiple Long COVID-19 symptoms.ConclusionThese findings highlight the significant burden of Long COVID-19 symptoms on healthcare workers well-being in Qatar. The study underscores the need for targeted interventions to support them, especially those at greater risk of adverse outcomes. From a healthcare system perspective, addressing Long COVID-19 symptoms among healthcare workers is critical to maintaining a resilient workforce. This includes implementing access to mental health resources, structured follow-up, and workplace accommodation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11372-w.

Background

Long Coronavirus Disease 2019 (Long COVID-19) represents a complex array of symptoms affecting various bodily systems after Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection. It is the virus responsible for causing Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19). Long COVID can affect anyone exposed to SARS-CoV-2, regardless of age or severity of original symptoms. It can affect multiple organ systems and lead to severe and protracted impairment of function as a result of organ damage. The varied nature of Long COVID, its impact on multiple body systems, and the absence of consistent objective markers make it challenging to clearly define [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), “post COVID-19” condition manifests in individuals with a history of probable or confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection, presenting symptoms persisting for at least 2 months, commencing 3 months post onset, with no alternative diagnosis explaining the symptoms, and typically interfering with daily activities [2, 3]. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services recognizes and uses the term “Long COVID” to describe signs, symptoms, or conditions that persist or emerge after the initial SARS-CoV-2 infection and continue for more than four weeks [4]. UK National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence uses the term “ongoing symptomatic COVID-19” for symptoms that are unexplained by an alternative diagnosis and persist for 4–12 weeks after acute COVID-19 and the term “post-COVID-19 syndrome” for symptoms that are unexplained by an alternative diagnosis and persist for more than 12 weeks after acute COVID-19 [5, 6]. None of them require a positive laboratory or lateral flow test. The absence of a standardized and universally accepted definition of Long COVID poses significant challenges for health policy formulation, clinical research, public health planning, healthcare delivery, and patient support services [7].

Common symptoms of Long COVID-19 can include fatigue, shortness of breath and cognitive dysfunction, but patients can experience a wide range of other symptoms across all bodily systems [8, 9]. The etiology, symptom clusters, clinical profiles, and outcomes of Long COVID-19 patients are less well understood and are thus subject to ongoing research [10, 11]. The EuCARE-POSTCOVID multicentre cohort study is examining the incidence and risk factors of Long COVID, with secondary aims to assess the role of viral variants and long-term organ involvement [12]. Huang et al. reported that symptom patterns evolve over time, with neuropsychiatric symptoms persisting beyond one year and population-level factors playing a greater role in symptom persistence than acute illness severity [13]. Azzam et al.’s meta-analysis found that some long-term symptoms may not be unique to COVID-19, highlighting the importance of appropriate controls [14]. Augustin et al. showed that even mild, non-hospitalised cases can result in prolonged symptoms such as fatigue and anosmia, with implications for defining post-COVID syndrome [15]. Similarly, Mahoney et al. found that 45% of COVID-19 survivors reported ongoing symptoms at four months, regardless of disease severity [16]. Hou et al. further highlighted that Long COVID is globally prevalent among COVID-19-positive populations, with highly variable estimates across regions and populations. Their findings showed a sustained symptom burden up to two years post-infection, underscoring the need for robust, representative, and geographically diverse longitudinal studies [17]. The prevalence of Long COVID-19 is potentially influenced by several factors, including the study population, the duration of the COVID-19 pandemic, and the severity of the disease [18, 19]. Estimating prevalence is challenging due to differences in the definitions used, population denominators, and methods of ascertainment [20]. The estimated prevalence of Long COVID-19 symptoms ranged from 30% to 60% at the time.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, healthcare workers (HCWs) are at an elevated risk of exposure and infection compared with the general population [21, 22]. This was more so for HCWs working directly in COVID-19-designated areas. Additionally, the COVID-19 pandemic overwhelmed the hospital capacity and systems in many countries [23]. HCWs had to work at maximum capacity and in some settings with limited protection and redeployment [24, 25]. Given that Long COVID-19 can affect anyone with SARS-CoV-2 infection, the global burden of Long COVID-19 on HCWs is likely to be large. There are currently limited studies specific to HCWs about its prevalence, symptom profile and impact on health. Limited evidence indicates wide variation in the prevalence of Long COVID-19 and disproportionate impact on HCWs [26].

Qatar, a small country in the Middle East, had high rates of SARS-CoV-2 infection during the pandemic due to its status as a major travel hub and a large population of highly mobile expatriates. During the initial period, 10% of HCWs at Hamad Medical Corporation (HMC) in Qatar tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 [27]. In this study, we aimed to explore long-term symptoms after SARS-CoV-2 infection and associated risk factors among HCWs at HMC. In the Middle East, HMC is one of the leading healthcare providers, is the single largest in Qatar, and oversees 12 hospitals, the National Ambulance Service, and home and residential care services. HCWs are drawn from a multitude of ethnicities and sociodemographic backgrounds due to being a migrant majority country. To the best of our knowledge, no study has been published from Qatar to assess Long COVID-19 symptoms among HCWs [28].

Despite the growing body of global research on Long COVID-19, data specific to HCWs, one of the most affected occupational groups, remain limited. HCWs face unique risks due to repeated exposure, high workload, psychological stress, and, in some settings, inadequate protective measures. These factors may influence not only infection risk but also the development and persistence of long-term symptoms. Furthermore, most published studies to date originate from Europe, North America, and parts of Asia, while there is a significant lack of data from the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region. Given Qatar’s unique population structure, comprising a majority of expatriates with diverse ethnic and occupational backgrounds, and the country’s high SARS-CoV-2 infection rates during the pandemic, there is a critical need for region-specific evidence. Understanding the prevalence and nature of Long COVID-19 symptoms in HCWs within this context is essential for guiding occupational health policies, workforce planning, and support services in Qatar and similar settings.

Methodology

Design, settings, and participants

This study was a cross-sectional, web-based survey conducted on HCWs at HMC. HCWs aged 18 years or older who were associated with HMC were eligible to participate in this survey. These included physicians, nurses, allied healthcare professionals, and administrative staff. The survey was conducted between 24 January 2022 and 24 April 2022. HMC oversees 12 hospitals, the National Ambulance Service, and multiple community and residential care services. Qatar’s healthcare workforce at HMC is highly international, comprising professionals from over 60 nationalities, reflecting the broader expatriate-majority population of the country. This diverse workforce includes a wide range of linguistic, cultural, and professional backgrounds.The survey was distributed using institutional email lists and internal communication platforms accessible to all HCWs, forming an open-access sampling frame. As a result, the exact number of individuals who viewed or received the survey cannot be precisely determined. As the HCWs recruited in this study came from the single largest healthcare provider in Qatar, the conditions of work, infection control procedures, vaccination uptake and available support were naturally controlled. We based our sample size calculations on the estimated prevalence of Long COVID-19 symptoms, which ranged from 30 to 60% at the time. To ensure that the study was adequately powered to detect differences in prevalence, we used the higher estimate of 60%. Based on this estimate, the required sample size was approximately 577 participants to achieve a 95% confidence level with a 4% margin of error. Over a four-week period, a total of 783 responses were received, exceeding the desired sample size, and the study was closed. The response rate cannot be definitively calculated due to the open nature of the sampling frame and broad distribution strategy. The completion rate was 100% for the main questionnaire items, as participants had to complete all sections to submit their responses. The survey was administered in English, which is the primary working language within HMC and widely spoken among healthcare professionals in Qatar.

Main outcomes and measures

The primary objective of the study was to assess the nature and prevalence of long-term symptoms after SARS-CoV-2 infection, as well as the associated risk factors. The primary outcomes of interest in the study were the prevalence of Long COVID-19 symptoms, symptoms of depression, and symptoms of anxiety. These were measured by the Long COVID symptoms questionnaire, Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7) questionnaire, respectively. The operational definition of Long COVID-19 symptoms we used was symptoms and signs that developed during or after an acute infection with SARS-CoV-2 and continued for more than 4 weeks, which is consistent with the current definitions used.

Long COVID-19 symptoms included a self-designed set of questions asked to rate the presence and severity of known Long COVID-19 symptoms [refer to the supplementary data]. There were no validated tools available at the time to capture Long COVID-19 symptoms. The symptoms were drawn from the aggregated evidence available at that time and included symptom lists published by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE). The list included extreme tiredness (fatigue), shortness of breath, chest pain or tightness, problems with memory and concentration (brain fog), difficulty sleeping (insomnia), fast heartbeat/pounding heartbeat, dizziness, pins and needles, joint pain, tinnitus, earaches, feeling sick, diarrhea, stomach aches, loss of appetite, fever, cough, headaches, sore throat, changes to sense of smell or taste and rashes. There were 21 symptoms, and the study participants were asked to identify as many symptoms as were applicable to them. To stratify the severity and burden of Long COVID-19 symptoms, participants were classified into two groups based on their symptom score: those reporting ≤ 3 symptoms and those reporting > 3 symptoms. This stratification was designed to allow comparison of demographic, clinical, and psychosocial factors between individuals with mild versus more severe symptom burdens. By examining these differences, the study aimed to identify patterns and risk factors associated with a higher symptom burden, which could inform targeted interventions and support HCWs experiencing long-term effects of COVID-19. Those who scored ≤ 3 on the symptom scale were compared with respondents who scored > 3 to assess possible associations with sociodemographic variables, PHQ-9 scores, GAD-7 scores, and SARS-CoV-2 infection. This approach aimed to uncover significant relationships between symptom burden and various factors, providing valuable insights into potential predictors of Long COVID severity.

The PHQ-9 is a validated, criterion-based measure for screening depression [29]. The PHQ-9 score ranges from 0 to 27. Scores of 5, 10, 15, and 20 represented cutoff points for mild, moderate, moderately severe, and severe depression, respectively. GAD-7 is a validated, criterion-based measure for screening anxiety [30]. The GAD-7 scores range from 0 to 21. Scores of 5, 10, and 15 were used as the cutoff points for mild, moderate, and severe anxiety, respectively.

The sociodemographic data was self-reported by the participants and collected via a sociodemographic data questionnaire. We collected data on age, sex, marital status, children, living arrangement, job designation, place of work, whether the respondent worked at a COVID-19-designated site, COVID-19 vaccination status, and SARS-CoV-2 infection history.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative and categorical data are presented as the means ± standard deviations and frequencies (percentages). Associations between two or more qualitative variables were assessed using the chi-squared (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses (adjusted for potential predictors and confounders) were performed to determine and assess the predictive value of potential predictors and risk factors (demographic characteristics and parameters related to clinical and other characteristics) associated with outcome variables of Long COVID-19 symptoms. The results of the logistic regression analyses are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Pearson’s correlation coefficients were used to assess the linear relationship between the symptom scores, PHQ-9 scores, and GAD-7 scores. Graphic representations of key results were created using appropriate statistical graphs. All p values presented are two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 27.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and Epi Info (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Atlanta, GA, USA).

Results

Sociodemographic profile of the participants

Most respondents were women (n = 538; 68.7%), less than 45 years of age (n = 616; 78.7%), married (n = 622; 79.4%), living with their families (n = 609; 77.9%), and had children (n = 566; 72.4%). The majority were nurses (n = 614; 78.5%), followed by allied healthcare professionals, administrative staff (n = 128; 16.4%) and doctors (n = 40; 5.1%). The majority of the respondents (n = 541; 69.1%) worked in COVID-19-designated areas. More than 90% of them did not have any past (n = 731) or family (n = 750) history of mental health problems.

At the time of the study, two-thirds of them (n = 487; 62.3%) had already taken the third dose of the COVID-19 vaccine. Interestingly, a similar number of respondents (n = 471; 60.2%) had previously tested positive for SARS-CoV-2. Most (86.8%; n = 408) of them were infected with SARS-CoV-2 once, a minority (11.9%; n = 56) had been infected with SARS-CoV-2 twice, and very few (1.5%; n = 7) had been infected with SARS-CoV-2 thrice. The majority of the participants (70%; n = 312) were infected with SARS-CoV-2 less than 3 months prior. Most of them (86.2%, n = 405) required home quarantine, and only 1.3% (n = 6) required ICU admission.

Baseline sociodemographic characteristics

PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores and symptom scores

The PHQ-9 was used to determine symptoms of depression among the participants. More than half of the participants had only minimal symptoms (n = 437; 55.8%). 27% (n = 212) had mild symptoms, approximately 10% (n = 76) had moderate symptoms, approximately 5% (n = 37) had moderately severe symptoms, and 2.7% (n = 21) had severe symptoms of depression. The GAD-7 was used to determine symptoms of anxiety among the participants. The majority (n = 519; 66.3%) of the respondents had only minimal symptoms of anxiety. The associations between the symptom scores, PHQ-9 scores and GAD-7 scores were analyzed using Pearson’s correlation. According to the PHQ-9, 92% (n = 464) of those who had ≤ 3 symptoms scored minimal to mild symptoms of depression, while 66.3% (n = 185) of those who had > 3 symptoms scored minimal to mild symptoms of depression. On the other hand, 33.7% (n = 94) of those who had > 3 symptoms scored moderate to severe symptoms of depression, compared to 8% (n = 40) of those respondents who had ≤ 3 symptoms (P < 0.0001).

According to the GAD-7, 96% (n = 484) of those who had ≤ 3 symptoms scored minimal to mild symptoms of anxiety, while 78% (n = 218) of those who had > 3 symptoms scored minimal to mild symptoms of anxiety. On the other hand, 21.8% (n = 61) of those who had > 3 symptoms scored moderate to severe symptoms of depression, compared to 4% (n = 20) of those respondents who had ≤ 3 symptoms (P < 0.0001).

Univariate logistic regression of sociodemographic variables and symptom scores > 3

Of the 279 respondents who suffered from more than 3 Long COVID-19 symptoms, 43.4% (n = 121) were in the < 35 years age group, 39.8% (n = 111) were in the 36 to 44 years age group, 14.3% (n = 40) were in the 45 to 54 years age group, and 2.5% (n = 7) were in the > = 55 years age group (P = 0.095). Those in the > = 55 years age group were less likely to experience a greater number of symptoms than were those in the other age groups (unadjusted OR = 0.41; 95% CI: 0.17–0.96; P = 0.041).

The majority of them were females (71%; n = 198). Additionally, 45.2% (n = 66) of the respondents who were single, 73.3% (n = 11) of those who were divorced/widowed, and 32.5% (n = 202) of those who were married experienced more than 3 symptoms (P = < 0.0001). Those who were married were less likely to experience an increased number of symptoms than respondents who were single (unadjusted OR = 0.58; 95% CI: 0.40–0.84; P = 0.004). On the other hand, those who were divorced/widowed were more likely to experience a greater number of symptoms than respondents who were single (unadjusted OR = 3.33; 95% CI: 1.01–10.96; P = 0.047).

Furthermore, 40.7% (n = 88) of the participants without children and 33.7% (n = 191) of those with children experienced more than 3 symptoms (p = 0.068). Of the respondents who were living with family, 34.3% (n = 209) experienced more than 3 symptoms compared to 40.5% (n = 70) of those who were not living with their family (P = 0.137).

Overall, 35% (n = 14) of the doctors, 35.2% (n = 216) of the nurses, and 38.2% (n = 49) of the other professionals experienced more symptoms (P = 0.797).

The majority of those who experienced more symptoms (69.5%; n = 194) worked in the COVID-19-designated area. However, a smaller proportion (35%) of respondents who did not work in COVID-19-designated areas experienced more than 3 symptoms (P = 0.843).

Interestingly, 82.4% (n = 42) of those who suffered from current or past mental health problems experienced longer COVID-19 symptoms than did 32.4% (n = 237) of those who did not have any mental health issues. Those who did not suffer from mental health issues were less likely to suffer from a greater number of symptoms than those who did experience current or past mental health issues (unadjusted OR = 0.10; 95% CI: 0.05–0.22; P < 0.0001).

Predictors of Long COVID-19 symptoms using multivariate logistic regression

The findings derived from the multivariate logistic regression indicated that a history of current or past mental health problems, SARS-CoV-2 infection and symptoms of depression were found to be significantly associated with developing a greater number of long-term COVID-19 symptoms. Multivariate logistic regression indicated that respondents who did not suffer from mental health problems were less likely to experience more than 3 Long COVID-19 symptoms (adjusted OR 0.25; 95% CI 0.08–0.78; P = 0.017). Discussion

This study aimed to explore the prevalence, symptom profile, and associated risk factors of Long COVID among healthcare workers at Hamad Medical Corporation. Understanding these factors is critical to tailoring effective support and management strategies for this vulnerable population. In this cross-sectional study of HCWs, 75% of respondents reported long-term symptoms following SARS-CoV-2 infection. Approximately 37% of the participants had more than three symptoms. Recent systematic reviews and cohort studies have provided comprehensive insights into the global prevalence and long-term characteristics of Long COVID. However, prevalence estimates vary substantially, with several systematic reviews reporting wide variation in the frequency of Long COVID-19 symptoms. One meta-analysis found that 63.2%, 71.9%, and 45.9% of participants reported at least one persistent symptom at 30, 60, and ≥ 90 days after onset or hospitalization, respectively [31]. Another large-scale meta-analysis, which included 442 studies, reported a global pooled prevalence of 36% among individuals with confirmed COVID-19, with fatigue, respiratory issues, and neurological symptoms being the most frequently reported subtypes. These findings underscore the enduring nature of Long COVID and highlight significant geographical heterogeneity in symptom persistence and expression [32]. A one-year longitudinal analysis further demonstrated that although symptom prevalence decreases initially, it plateaus at approximately 50% during one-year follow-up, with neuropsychiatric symptoms demonstrating greater persistence than physical symptoms. Registry-based cohort studies have also contributed robust data on symptom persistence and recovery trajectories, thereby strengthening our understanding of Long COVID outcomes [12, 33]. These findings must be interpreted considering discrepancies often observed between patients’ self-reported symptoms and professional evaluations. Such differences may be influenced by patient-specific factors, including personality traits and sociodemographic variables, highlighting the need for careful interpretation of self-reported data [34]. Regional studies like the large multicenter study from Saudi Arabia has also identified wide range of symptoms, similar to those observed and reported in other countries. The loss of smell, the loss of taste, shortness of breath, and fatigue were the main persistent symptoms [35]. Collectively, these studies contribute to a more nuanced understanding of Long COVID and underscore the necessity for continued research and the development of tailored management strategies to address its diverse and persistent clinical manifestations. Consistent with the existing literature, the most common symptoms in our cohort were fatigue, sleep disorders, headache, pain in joints, low mood, anxiety, brain fog and cough. To move beyond descriptive prevalence estimates, we conducted inferential analyses, such as logistic regression and subgroup comparisons, to identify potential sociodemographic, occupational, and psychological predictors of greater symptom burden. This approach allowed us to differentiate between individuals experiencing mild versus more extensive Long COVID symptoms and to explore which factors might contribute to this variation. Notably, the medical sequelae most frequently reported in HCWs include fatigue, headache, anosmia and ageusia after predominantly mild acute infections [36, 37]. There is speculation regarding the reactivation of latent SARS-CoV-2 within the central nervous system as a potential etiology for neurological manifestations. Sehgal et al. support the hypothesis that SARS-CoV-2 can persist in the central nervous system (CNS), particularly in the olfactory system, potentially leading to chronic inflammation and neurological sequelae [38]. This mechanism may explain prolonged symptoms such as anosmia, cognitive dysfunction, and fatigue. Similarly, Fernández-Castañeda et al. demonstrated that even mild respiratory SARS-CoV-2 infection can trigger neuroinflammation, microglial activation, and myelin loss, suggesting that peripheral infection may have lasting consequences for CNS integrity, thereby contributing to cognitive deficits in Long COVID [39]. Swank et al. identified persistent SARS-CoV-2 spike protein in the plasma of individuals with Long COVID, suggesting ongoing antigen exposure may drive chronic immune activation, including neuroinflammation, which contributes to the condition’s pathophysiology [40]. The findings of Guedj et al., using 18 F-FDG PET imaging, support the hypothesis of SARS-CoV-2 neurotropism via the olfactory bulb, with possible extension to other brain regions [41]. Understanding viral persistence in the CNS is therefore crucial for developing targeted therapeutic strategies to mitigate long-term sequelae. Moreover, symptoms akin to Long COVID-19, particularly fatigue, have been linked to postinfectious syndromes and autoimmune disorders [42–44]. For example, Koedel et al. highlights that neurological symptoms such as cognitive dysfunction and fatigue can persist in Lyme neuroborreliosis despite resolution of the acute infection, likely driven by immune-mediated mechanisms or neuroinflammation rather than active infection. Similar processes are proposed in Long COVID, where persistent symptoms may stem from immune dysregulation, microglial activation, or endothelial dysfunction [42]. The study by Hickie et al. demonstrated that post-infective fatigue syndromes can occur after a variety of viral and non-viral infections, suggesting that Long COVID fatigue may be part of a broader spectrum of post-infectious syndromes [43]. Similarly, while Drent et al. focused on fatigue in sarcoidosis, their findings underscore the role of immune dysregulation and chronic inflammation, mechanisms also implicated in Long COVID pathophysiology [44]. In our study, Long COVID-19 symptoms were notably more prevalent in females than in males, consistent with findings from published studies [45]. In our study, 71% of females reported experiencing more than three symptoms. The prevailing hypothesis posits that the observed sex-based difference in long-term complications stems from potential variations in the immune response between males and females, with females being more susceptible [46]. Moreover, it is acknowledged that women’s increased likelihood of reporting symptoms may also contribute to this phenomenon [47]. Our finding that females were more likely to report a greater number of symptoms is consistent with prior studies, which suggest that female sex is a consistent predictor of Long COVID symptomatology, possibly due to differences in immune response and health-seeking behavior. Both married men and married women were less likely to experience an increased number of symptoms than respondents who were single. Our finding that individuals in the above 55 years of age group were less prone to experiencing a greater number of symptoms contrasts with the literature, which suggests that the sequelae of COVID-19 are more prevalent in older age groups [48]. In contrast to earlier research indicating that older adults may be at greater risk of Long COVID, we found that participants aged ≥ 55 were less likely to report more than three symptoms. This unexpected finding may be related to underreporting, differential symptom perception, or differences in healthcare access. It highlights the importance of contextual and occupational variables when interpreting Long COVID risk profiles. While certain studies have identified age and preexisting conditions as risk factors for Long COVID-19, others have found no significant associations between these factors [49, 50]. Supporting this, a study from the Arab world highlighted that sociodemographic and clinical factors are significant contributors to both the likelihood of developing Long COVID and the number of symptoms experienced [51]. Interestingly, we found that doctors, nursing staff and other staff members had similar prevalences of COVID-19 sequelae. Our data indicate that 60% of respondents were infected with SARS-CoV-2. The incidence of SARS-CoV-2 infection was greater in HCWs than in those in most other professions. One systematic review and meta-analysis reported that 51.7% of HCWs tested positive for SARS-CoV-2, with a 15% hospitalization rate and a 1.5% death rate [21]. Interestingly, in our study, the group of healthcare workers not infected with SARS-CoV-2 reported symptoms akin to Long COVID-19 symptoms. This finding is in line with other reports showing that up to one-fourth of noninfected controls reported these symptoms [52]. Symptom reporting in non-infected individuals resembling Long COVID-19 may be influenced by various psychological, social, and lifestyle factors related to the pandemic. The widespread fear and uncertainty during this period have contributed to heightened health anxiety and stress, leading individuals to misinterpret normal physiological sensations as illness-related symptoms. Prolonged exposure to stressors has also been linked to somatization and disorders such as depression and anxiety, which share overlapping symptoms with Long COVID-19, including fatigue, cognitive difficulties, and sleep disturbances. Social isolation and lifestyle disruptions further compound these effects. The pandemic led to extended periods of lockdown, quarantine, and isolation. Loneliness and confinement can be associated with depression, anxiety, and PTSD. Reduced physical activity during lockdowns can lead to fatigue and physical discomfort, while irregular routines and increased screen time contribute to sleep impairments. Alterations in diet and nutrition may also play a role in decreased energy levels and overall well-being. Financial instability and job loss have exacerbated stress, while a lack of social connections during periods of isolation has deepened psychological strain. Quarantine has been associated with increased rates of suicide, anger, acute stress disorder, depression, and PTSD, with symptoms continuing even years after quarantine ends. Mass psychogenic factors, such as widespread awareness of Long COVID-19 through media coverage, may amplify symptom reporting due to suggestion and shared illness expectations. Similarly, the nocebo effect and cognitive biases, such as confirmation bias, may lead individuals to attribute unrelated symptoms to potential health conditions. Pre-existing chronic illnesses, such as fibromyalgia or chronic fatigue syndrome, could also worsen under the pressures of limited healthcare access and pandemic stressors. These multifaceted influences underscore the importance of considering psychological and social confounders when interpreting symptom reports in non-infected populations. Addressing these factors in research and clinical settings will help disentangle the true impact of SARS-CoV-2 infection from the broader consequences of the pandemic, enabling a more accurate understanding of Long COVID-19 and related conditions [53, 54].

Our data indicate that a history of mental health problems and current symptoms of depression were significantly associated with developing a greater number of Long COVID-19 symptoms. This finding is in line with studies indicating that individuals with preexisting psychological distress, including anxiety and depression, are at an elevated risk of developing Long COVID-19 [55–57]. Consistent with previous research, our study showed that a history of mental health problems and elevated scores on the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scales were significantly associated with reporting a higher symptom burden. COVID-19 is not unique in its association with psychiatric symptoms, as psychiatric symptoms have been reported during previous coronavirus epidemics [58]. A history of mental health problems is linked to a greater number of reported Long COVID-19 symptoms, suggesting a complex interplay between psychological distress and symptom perception. Psychological distress, such as anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), can heighten somatic awareness and amplify the perception of physical symptoms. This heightened awareness may lead individuals to misinterpret normal physiological sensations or minor health issues as more severe, contributing to the experience of Long COVID-19 symptoms. Additionally, stress and mental health disorders disrupt physiological systems, including the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, which can exacerbate fatigue, sleep disturbances, and cognitive difficulties. The nocebo effect, negative expectations about illness, may also amplify symptom reporting in those with pre-existing psychological vulnerabilities. Importantly, these symptoms may not always be a direct consequence of SARS-CoV-2 infection but rather the result of psychological distress and its impact on symptom interpretation. This distinction highlights the need for a biopsychosocial approach in managing Long COVID-19, addressing both physical health and psychological well-being. By recognizing the influence of mental health on symptom perception, clinicians can provide targeted interventions, such as cognitive-behavioral therapy and stress management, to mitigate the burden of Long COVID-19 symptoms in vulnerable populations. The need for integrated care models that address both physical and psychological consequences has been reinforced by similar studies in the region [59]. These Middle East & North Africa (MENA)-focused investigations provide vital context to our findings and emphasize the importance of developing region-specific strategies for Long COVID management.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations inherent to its observational design and reliance on self-reported symptoms. The use of a voluntary web-based survey may have introduced selection bias. Staff experiencing more severe or distressing symptoms may have been more likely to participate, while asymptomatic individuals could be underrepresented. Reliance on self-reported data introduces the possibility of recall bias and reporting inaccuracies, limitations commonly encountered in mental health and symptom-based research. The precise duration of symptom persistence could not be determined, as the survey did not include serial follow-up or clinical evaluations. The cross-sectional design precludes any inference of causality between SARS-CoV-2 infection and the reported long-term symptoms. This study was conducted during an early phase of the pandemic, and data were collected when definitions and clinical understanding of Long COVID-19 were still evolving. Consequently, some symptoms that are now recognized as part of the Long COVID-19 spectrum may not have been captured by the questionnaire used in this study.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings underscore the significant burden of COVID-19 sequelae among healthcare workers, with psychiatric history and symptoms of depression emerging as key risk factors for experiencing a greater number of symptoms. These findings highlight the critical need for comprehensive support systems and targeted interventions to address the multifaceted impact of COVID-19 on HCWs’ well-being. Based on these results, we recommend enhanced occupational health screening and monitoring, particularly for high-risk groups, alongside multidisciplinary support programs including mental health services, rehabilitation, and occupational therapy tailored to Long COVID recovery. Furthermore, workplace policies that facilitate flexible work arrangements, phased return-to-work plans, and accommodations for persistent symptoms are essential to support affected staff. Increased awareness and education for employers and policymakers about the long-term impacts of COVID-19 on healthcare workers will further enable effective resource allocation and planning. Support for patients and their families through education, specialized clinics, and psychosocial interventions is also vital. Effective management of post-COVID conditions requires a holistic approach encompassing preventive strategies, patient education, supportive psychotherapy, symptom management, rehabilitative care, medical interventions, counseling on lifestyle factors, and structured follow-up plans [60]. It is important to note that the findings apply primarily to the cohort studied and may not be generalizable to all HCWs in Qatar or beyond. Further research across broader HCW populations is needed to validate and expand upon these insights.
