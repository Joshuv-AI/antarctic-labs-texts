Applying the consolidated framework for implementation research to guide the development, implementation, and evaluation of a cardiology transitions of care program (RECARD trial)
Authors: Shelley A Wilkinson, Keshia De Guzman, Nazanin Falconer, Centaine Snoswell, Ian Coombes, Kelvin Robertson, Jared Miles, Chariclia Paradissis, Liza Raggatt, William Wang, John J Atherton, Sue Carson, Andrew Jones, Michael Barras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTransition of care (ToC) for patients following acute type 1 myocardial infarction or coronary revascularisation procedures is complex, often resulting in medication-related harm and hospital readmissions. Current ToC models lack comprehensive, system-wide approaches, especially in Australia, and often fail to address patient-centred needs and cultural considerations. This theoretical paper aims to outline a multidimensional implementation science approach that will underpin the design and implementation a pharmacy-led ToC intervention to reduce hospital readmissions and improve medication safety.MethodInformed by Guyatt et al. (2021), the REduce hospital readmissions for high-risk CARDiology patients (RECARD) ToC model, will integrate six key elements: (1) guiding implementation with the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), including a patient domain; (2) co-design with patients and healthcare professionals; (3) understanding local needs using Bradshaw’s taxonomy; (4) designing an evidence-informed intervention based on health behaviour theories; (5) planning and executing implementation using the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) and the Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW); and (6) evaluating and sustaining the intervention using the RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance) framework. This approach will involve a pre-post interventional trial across three quaternary Queensland hospitals, informed by focus groups, semi-structured interviews, and Yarning circles to refine the literature-informed intervention design.ResultsWe present our synthesised RECARD ToC model planned to be delivered as an “Adaptive ToC Pathway,” involving a collaborative team of pharmacists, nurse practitioners, and Indigenous health workers. The intervention will incorporate inpatient and post-discharge activities, tailored to local needs and patient preferences. The implementation plan will address barriers and enablers identified through stakeholder engagement, and RE-AIM will guide the evaluation.ConclusionsOur novel theory-informed approach, integrating CFIR, Bradshaw’s model, ERIC, BCW, and RE-AIM, provides a comprehensive framework for designing and implementing the RECARD ToC model. This will allow comparison of a prospective cohort receiving evidence-informed ToC activities to optimise medication management and patient safety, guided by a risk prediction model to predict unplanned readmissions due to medication harm within 30 days, with a retrospective historical cohort who received usual care. By understanding local needs and engaging stakeholders, this project seeks to create a sustainable and impactful intervention that addresses the gaps in current ToC practices, particularly for Indigenous populations, and enhances patient safety in the Australian healthcare setting.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Theory-informed, Implementation, Co-design, Knowledge translation

Background

Transition of care (ToC) is defined as the movement of a patient between healthcare settings. A typical example is between hospital and the primary care setting [1–3]. From hospital, patients leave an environment with fulltime care, structured plans, access to health advice, and immediate diagnostic and therapeutic intervention. This transition is complex as it involves multiple health professionals across numerous settings, new healthcare plans, and the coordination of carers [2, 4]. As the transition involves the loss of intense support it can be challenging for patients [3, 5].

During ToC, medication management transitions from a highly legislated and structured environment with nursing oversight to one where patients often need to be autonomous in all aspects of medication management. Poor care coordination and communication places patients at risk of medication errors, harm, and hospital readmissions [6–9]. A 2021 scoping review reported that 16% of all hospital readmissions were medication related, of which, 40% were preventable [10]. Another review found that polypharmacy, sub-optimal medication prescribing, and changes to medications were risk factors for hospital readmissions [11].

High risk patient populations for medication harm are the post-acute myocardial infarction (type 1 AMI, as per the 4th Universal Definition [12]) and patients who have undergone coronary revascularisation procedures (including percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI) or coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery), who have at least five new medications initiated in hospital. The medications generally have a high risk of side effects, are complex to dose effectively, and often require post discharge dose adjustment. A 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis found that 30-day readmission rates post AMI was 12%, which was increased in patients with kidney disease, heart failure, and diabetes mellitus [13]. Similarly, post-AMI patients have been regularly shown to be at a high risk of hospital readmissions, especially if they had comorbidities that included renal disease, heart failure, diabetes, and hypertension [14–16]. Furthermore, usual cardiac rehabilitation models have been shown to be ineffective in sustaining medication adherence [17].

The Australian government and international health services have identified that ToC is a critical time that requires more effective management [8, 18]. Ensuring an effective and safe transition necessitates a multidisciplinary and patient centred approach [19–22]. Within the multidisciplinary team, pharmacists play an integral role in ensuring medication safety and optimisation of care [23–26].

Whilst there has been some success in providing a systematic, evidence-based approach to reducing risk of medication related harm at ToC for patients post AMI/PCI/CABG [27–29], many of these models of care are in a US context and gaps still exist in service delivery [20]. Three key limitations in current ToC models include: (i) lack of early post discharge engagement with patients/carers to evaluate and refine their medication needs, adjust medication plans and implement supportive services [30]; (ii) less than half of AMI/PCI/CABG patients discharged are followed up by their general practitioner or primary care doctor within seven days, and 66% within 14 days [30] and only 68% of hospital discharge summaries were completed within 30 days of discharge [30]; and (iii) underutilisation and/or lack of interoperability of hospital and primary care IT systems restricting effective communication [9, 31, 32]. Furthermore, almost one third of Indigenous peoples who do not access health services cite cultural reasons including language problems, discrimination, and cultural appropriateness [33]. There also exists a lack of tailored, person-centred care, with one model not fitting the needs of patients post AMI/ PCI/ CABG [8, 25, 34–36].

While existing literature offers strategies to address barriers and system pressure points in the ToC for post- AMI/ PCI/ CABG patients, it lacks a comprehensive, system-wide approach that considers all stakeholders [8, 18, 20, 25]. To address the shortcomings outlined above, we will apply the multidimensional framework proposed by Guyatt et al. (2021) [35], which integrates patient needs, care providers, system dynamics, and external influences. Their co-design model combines need assessment, behaviour change theories, implementation frameworks, and evaluation tools, all aligned with the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR). This approach offers a novel solution that goes beyond traditional ToC models. We aim to explicitly outline the planning of a pharmacy-led ToC intervention project’s design and implementation underpinned by a theoretical, multidimensional implementation science approach across three quaternary Queensland hospital and health service districts (“The RECARD ToC model”). Incorporating these findings into our model of care will enable a comparison of a prospective cohort receiving evidence-informed ToC activities to optimise medication management and patient safety, guided by a risk prediction model to predict unplanned readmissions due to medication harm within 30 days, with a retrospective historical cohort who received usual care.

Methods

Informed by Guyatt et al. (2021)[35], the ToC model for “The efficacy of a transition of care service to REduce hospital readmissions for high-risk CARDiology patients (RECARD): a pre-post interventional trial” (ACTRN12624001009505p) comprised six key multidimensional implementation science elements (Fig. 1). The first step will involve guiding and articulating the implementation process within a central Determinant framework (CFIR- at the figure’s centre). The processes of engaging with patients and health professionals through co-design (left hand side of figure) and understanding needs in the local setting from different perspectives (“Needs Assessment” top left) will provide unique and valuable insights to ensure tailored and relevant interventions are included. This will be underpinned by combining an evidence informed Intervention design guided by health behaviour theory (“Health Behaviour Theories” top right) and tools to address barriers to success (“Implementation Planning” bottom left). Finally, evaluation will be undertaken(“Evaluation Framework” bottom right) [35]. As noted by Guyatt et al. 2021, this process is non-linear [35, 37–39].

Planning and executing the implementation of the ToC model builds on the findings from the pre-implementation stage of understanding need and can occur alongside the intervention design phase.

Key components of implementation success are the individuals involved (both health care providers and patients) and their actions [51], as well as the teams and systems in which they deliver care. Therefore, we have chosen models and tools that articulate with the CFIR constructs and can be operationalised in our intervention context (illustrated in Fig. 1, top right and bottom left quadrants).

Enablers and barriers to effective organisation and delivery of care will be identified through the ‘understanding needs’ process which will refine what is possible, realistic, and preferable in the local context. The co-design element is vital for stakeholder engagement and contributes to the success and sustainability of the implementation.

We will utilise the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) [50, 52] and the Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW) and Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) [53] to provide a suite of practical evidence-based implementation strategies that can be used to facilitate the implementation process at an individual, team and organisational level.

RE-AIM considers five domains of evaluation:

Reach is a measure of the participation by the target population and in this approach the target population by the absolute number, proportion and representativeness of patients who are included in the RECARD ToC service, such as patients post AMI/ PCI/ CABG.

Efficacy is the effectiveness of the interventions; the impact of the ToC service on important outcomes, including clinical (patient) impact, potential negative effects, and economic outcomes. This will also involve a patient experiences survey.

Adoption is the uptake by health care professionals involved and provides a measure of the acceptability of the implementation process and how changes to the ToC intervention have been actioned. Adoption can be considered at an individual level, between work units or organisation wide and provides an understanding of factors that might contribute to ongoing sustainability. This will be measured by the target staff, settings systems, and communities by the absolute number, proportion and representativeness of settings and staff willing to initiate and deliver the ToC service.

Implementation measures fidelity of implementation components both at the inner setting (i.e. was it implemented into routine care as planned? ) and at the individual patient level. This will be assessed by consistency, costs, and adaptions made during the delivery of the ToC service, both at the setting and individual level.

Maintenance is a measure of sustainability either at an individual patient level (i.e. what is the ongoing effect of the intervention?) or within the implementation setting (i.e. How sustainable is the practice change? What mechanisms are needed to optimise sustainability?). This measure is not currently being measured within this project. However, project outcomes will inform a mechanism for an ongoing cycle of improvements based on responding as feedback is received and needs change (e.g. when new guidelines emerge, or local outcomes are not as expected).

Determinant framework – description and application (CFIR – centre of model in Fig. 1)

The CFIR is a comprehensive meta-framework and the central determinant framework used in our theoretical approach (Fig. 1; Table 1). It consists of five broad domains, which are the intervention, inner setting, outer setting, the individuals involved, and process [40]. In addition to these domains, we included a sixth domain, patients. CFIR recognises that interventions can be locally adapted to fit the inner setting while maintaining a core non-adaptable component. This maintains intervention integrity, while facilitating transferability.

Co-design (Left hand side of Fig. 1)

The second key element and underlying support to research efficacy is the concept of co-design - by meaningfully engaging with the end-users of the proposed intervention [41]. The end-users in this case being both patients (and families/carers) and their health care providers. These end-users are identified as the CFIR domains individuals involved and patients from both the inner and outer settings. We outline below, in ‘Understanding Needs’ and in Table 1, how each of these stakeholders is involved to provide context to how the flowchart/care path is refined, adapted and adopted.

Understanding needs (Top left-hand side of Fig. 1)

A pre-implementation stage, and an essential element of the implementation process, is to understand the local context. This understanding includes identifying current evidence-practice gaps, benchmarking, understanding local stakeholder needs and identifying the enablers and barriers to implementation. A needs assessment is a systematic process that facilitates this understanding of the current state and identifies planning and implementation priorities as well as directing future evaluation [42, 43]. To achieve the multidimensional understanding required, a holistic, stakeholder centred needs assessment that considered individual and community needs within the wider context of service delivery [44] is required. Bradshaw’s model of need allows for this type of assessment and has proven to be an enduring and effective model used in an Australian context including other large, complex health systems [42, 45, 46]. Table 1 outlines how Bradshaw’s multi-layered description of need, using Expert, Expressed, Comparative, and Felt Needs [47], can be applied to our needs assessment. Understanding these needs with the lens of the CFIR constructs gives focus to what elements of a pharmacist-led ToC model are needed, and how these should be implemented.

The process of designing and building an effective intervention will be based on the findings from the previous stage of understanding need to refine the steps in Fig. 2. Understanding the local context and applying that understanding to the intentional planning is important for implementation success and a key component of implementation science models and theories like Implementation Mapping (a stepwise model for implementing health promotion interventions)[55].

Multifaceted ToC services that involved inpatient and post-discharge activities demonstrated statistically significant changes in readmission rates and other patient reported outcomes.

Plan and execute implementation (Bottom left of Fig. 1)

Table 1 outlines considerations in planning and executing the implementation of the ToC model. Key CFIR constructs (modifiable variables) that are consistently associated with implementation success are: (i) the relative advantage of the intervention; (ii) tension for change within the inner setting; and (iii) patients’ needs and resources. In addition, the local implementation planning process, available resources (E.g. staff to deliver the care, including pharmacists, nurse practitioner, Indigenous health worker) and the relative priority within the inner setting are markers of likely implementation fidelity [38].

Evaluate and sustain (Bottom right of Fig. 1)

While the CFIR and companion tools can be used for implementation evaluation, some evaluation frameworks solely focus on assessing process and outcomes. These frameworks provide a structure and external validity to evaluation and investigate the why of implementation success or failure as opposed to the determinant framework like CFIR which is primarily defining what variables can facilitate success (or failure) [56]. RE-AIM has been widely used in health service research [56, 57], provides extensive resources online [58], is a standardised method for reporting implementation success that is understood by researchers, funders, and policy makers [57] and fits best with the multidimensional theoretical approach we are taking. Discussion

Our carefully chosen combination of models, theories, frameworks, and tools, aligned through a CFIR lens adapted to this context, provides a comprehensive theoretical foundation for implementation in this project. In the development and planning phase of an implementation project it is important to understand local needs, hence the inclusion of Bradshaw’s model which provides a broad approach for considering need across the CFIR domains. Elements of the BCW/COM-B will contribute to the evidence-based intervention design by providing methodology for delivering the RECARD ToC model to influence patient outcomes, implementation success, and service sustainability. Curating a cohesive and multidimensional methodology, like the one proposed here is the optimal way to create a theory-informed approach to successfully implement and evlaute a new health service [35]. Through the RECARD ToC implementation process, the CFIR-ERIC matching tool and the BCW / TDF will provide useful methodologies for planning and executing the service creation. In the evaluation phase, the RE-AIM framework offers a comprehensive method for assessing and reporting implementation outcomes. By examining key domains—Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance—this approach facilitates the development of a sustainability plan while ensuring that implementation efforts are both measurable and impactful.

This wider body of research is underway and will be reported in future publications, including evaluation of local needs, program design and outcomes from a pre / post implementation trial.

This paper presents a theoretically grounded approach to designing and implementing a pharmacy-led ToC model, guided by CFIR. By understanding, utilising, and transparently reporting the rationale behind our implementation science approach and subsequent evaluation, we aim to meaningfully improve patient safety within our local healthcare settings in an Australian context. Additionally, this approach aligns with key implementation science research principles, ensuring adherence to established reporting guidelines [59, 60]. Further refinement will occur as the results of the stakeholder engagement, including Yarning, emerge. These findings will inform the local adaptation and delivery at each site.

Acknowledgement of the limitations of taking a pragmatic, service-based approach of embedding a new model of care in a real-world context is required. This encompasses the limitations of the theoretical model as well as the complexity of coordinating multiple frameworks plus the challenges of translating theory into practice across three diverse hospital settings will be a challenge. Potential issues that may impact delivery of the adaptive ToC pathway include the capability of each service to enact each of steps of Fig. 3 which may be impacted by ability to recruit suitably trained pharmacists and the conflict between funding-body timelines and respectful time required for recruitment and engagement with various stakeholders. Further, the draft intervention presented in this paper was unable to specifically link steps to barriers highlighted in the current literature (the Scoping Review), but more broadly show the essential elements in a ToC. Our further qualitative work with Stakeholders will assist in generating evidence-based interventions to target identified barriers and enhance enablers in the final adaptive ToC pathway. Cognisance of these limitations will be included in the fortnightly project meetings with team and site members, with documentation of barriers, enablers and solutions being collected and acted upon as they arise. Sustainability is outside our scope but will be facilitated by co-creation of the model with each of the services, their staff and patients.

Conclusions

This paper presents a theoretically grounded, multidimensional implementation science approach to the design, implementation, and evaluation of the RECARD pharmacy‑led ToC model, developed through the synthesis of complementary theories, frameworks, and tools to support implementation success and sustainability. Integrating CFIR with Bradshaw’s model, ERIC, the BCW, and RE‑AIM, the proposed framework provides a comprehensive and transparent structure for addressing persistent gaps in medication safety, follow‑up care, and provider coordination for high‑risk cardiology patients. The worked example described has enabled a deep, collaborative understanding of patient needs alongside local enablers and barriers to implementation, informing the development of an adaptive, multidisciplinary ToC pathway. This approach has important implications for clinical practice and health system planning, demonstrating how pharmacists can be embedded within context‑responsive, patient‑centred models of care, including culturally appropriate strategies for Indigenous populations within Australian healthcare settings. The next phase of the RECARD program will empirically test this theoretically informed model through a pre–post interventional trial across three quaternary hospitals, incorporating stakeholder engagement, pathway refinement, and evaluation guided by the RE‑AIM framework. Lessons generated through this evaluation will inform local adaptation, support sustainability planning, and contribute evidence to guide future implementation and scaling of pharmacist‑led transitions of care models.
