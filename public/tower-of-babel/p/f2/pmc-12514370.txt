Neurologists' perspectives on SUDEP communication: A comparative study across five European countries
Authors: Seungyoun Moon, Lance Watkins, Johan Zelano, Vicente Villanueva, Nandan Ravichandra, Zsófia Jordán, Anna Kelemen, Paul Bassett, Oliver Johannes Henning, Torbjörn Tomson, Rohit Shankar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe leading cause of epilepsy mortality is Sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP). National and international guidelines recommend that people with epilepsy are counseled on SUDEP risk at the point of diagnosis or at the earliest opportunity. This study compares neurologist views from five European countries on attitudes and relevance of SUDEP communication.MethodsA cross‐sectional online survey of 17 Likert questions using validated themes was circulated via different ILAE and/or equivalent organizations using a non‐discriminatory exponential snowballing technique, leading to a non‐probability sampling. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, Kruskal–Wallis, Chi‐square, and Fisher's exact tests. Significance was accepted at p < 0.05.ResultsThere were 313 responses from neurologists from across five countries (UK, Norway, Sweden, Spain, Hungary). Significant heterogeneity (4%–53%) was found between neurologists of different countries in discussing SUDEP with a person with newly diagnosed epilepsy. There were significant inter‐country differences in the amount of time neurologists spent in epilepsy care generally and SUDEP discussions particularly. Significant inter‐country barriers to SUDEP communication included time constraints, clinical complexity, perceived fear of causing distress to the patient, presumed poor ability of patient/carers to comprehend risk, and a self‐perceived low risk of SUDEP.SignificanceDespite all international guidelines stating the need/importance to discuss SUDEP with all PWE, there remains hesitation, avoidance, and subjectivity in neurologists having SUDEP‐related conversations, more so in mainland European countries (Norway, Sweden, Spain and Hungary) than in the UK. Training and education are required to improve communication, engagement, and decision‐making.Plain Language SummarySudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) is the leading cause of death in people with epilepsy, and international guidelines recommend discussing this risk early after diagnosis. This study surveyed 313 neurologists from the UK, Norway, Sweden, Spain, and Hungary to understand their views on SUDEP communication. Results showed large differences between countries in how often and how openly neurologists discussed SUDEP. Barriers included time constraints, fear of upsetting patients, and doubts about patients' understanding. UK neurologists were more likely to have these conversations than their European peers. More training is needed to support consistent and effective SUDEP communication across countries.

INTRODUCTION

Sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) is a diagnosis reached when there is no other identifiable cause of death found (trauma, drowning, or concurrent medical conditions) following a comprehensive post‐mortem examination in a person with epilepsy. 1 SUDEP predominantly affects individuals for whom seizures are not adequately controlled and is typically characterized by proximity to a seizure. 2 , 3

The underlying pathophysiology of SUDEP remains incompletely understood, though it is believed to involve disturbances in neurotransmitter activity, alterations in respiratory and cardiac function, which may arise during or following a seizure. 2 , 3 SUDEP is considered one of the leading causes of mortality in individuals with epilepsy, particularly among those with frequent and poorly controlled seizures. 2 , 3 , 4

Several factors influence the risk of SUDEP, including seizure frequency, the occurrence of tonic–clonic seizures (generalized or focal to bilateral), and the effectiveness of treatment in achieving seizure control and other psycho‐social factors. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Preventive strategies are primarily focused on optimizing seizure management, providing comprehensive patient education, and raising awareness of SUDEP among healthcare providers, patients, and their families. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12

Despite the lack of evidence‐based prevention strategies for SUDEP, awareness of the condition has increased over the past two decades. 13 , 14 Persons with epilepsy (PWE), along with their families and caregivers, have expressed a strong desire for greater information regarding SUDEP from healthcare professionals. 3 , 15 International guidelines from the American Academy of Neurology (AAN) as well as those from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) in the UK recommend that SUDEP be discussed with every individual living with epilepsy at the earliest appropriate opportunity, 16 , 17 counseling that ideally should be repeated at regular intervals thereafter. 5 , 6 Existing literature highlights a significant gap between what information clinicians provide and the expectations of PWE. 18 , 19 , 20 There is recognized variance and heterogeneity in counseling and attitudes between countries irrespective of economic differences and between different professions. 21 To date, most studies have looked to conduct such inquiries in a single country. 20 Much of the research has emerged from economically developed countries. 20 No study to date has used a validated structured tool to compare a group of economically developed countries that have the same shared values towards the treatment and management of epilepsy. 20

The aim of this investigation was to gather neurologist views on SUDEP Communication across five European countries using a validated survey tool. 20 The views of neurologists were compared by country for the United Kingdom, Norway, Sweden, Spain, and Hungary.

METHODS

The survey tool was developed from validated themes/questions. 20 The survey was circulated to neurologists involved in the care of children and adults with epilepsy across five European countries. This group included subgroups, e.g., pediatric neurologists.

A non‐discriminatory exponential snowballing technique leading to non‐probability sampling was used to disseminate the survey in electronic form. The STROBE guidance for cross‐sectional studies was followed and used to report this study. 22

United Kingdom (UK) (population: 68 million)

The survey was distributed via the International League against Epilepsy (ILAE) British chapter and Epilepsy Specialist Nurses Association. The survey was open from February 6, 2023 to April 2, 2023.

Norway (population: 5.5 million)

The survey was distributed via ILAE Norway chapter, the Epilepsy Nurses National Network, and the National Interdisciplinary Epilepsy Network (Epilepsinet). The survey was open from April 12, 2023 to June 20, 2023.

Sweden (population: 10.5 million)

The survey was distributed via ILAE Sweden and larger epilepsy care providers. The survey was open from February 20, 2024 to April 30, 2024.

Spain (population: 48 million)

The survey was distributed to Spanish Epilepsy Society members, the current chapter of ILAE in Spain from September 24 to December 3, 2023.

Hungary (population: 9.5 million)

The survey was distributed via the ILAE Hungarian chapter, the Hungarian Neurology Society, the Hungarian Society of Child Neurologists, and the National Epilepsy website (epilepszia.hu). The survey was open from October 10, 2024 to December 6, 2024.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables with a natural order were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test to account for the ordinal nature of the data. For categorical variables without a natural ordering, comparisons were conducted using the Chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test when expected frequencies were low. Significance was taken at p < 0.05.

Ethics

In the UK, the survey was reviewed by professional bodies approached to disseminate the survey to neurologists who have a significant role working with people with epilepsy; that is, ILAE (British branch) before dissemination to their members. All participants were advised at the start of the study that participation was voluntary, and informed consent would be presumed if the survey was submitted. If they chose to participate, data would be pooled, anonymized, and analyzed. No participant identifier data were collected. Further, it was to a professional participant group where consent was implicit by participation.

In Norway, the regional ethics board decided that the study did not require permission under the Health Research Act but had no ethical objections (decision number 607647). The local privacy commissioner of the Oslo University Hospital approved the project with case/file/reference number 23/07460.

In Sweden, the Ethics Review Authority decided that the study did not require permission under the Ethical Review Act but had no ethical objections (decision number 2024‐120).

In Hungary, ethics committee approval was not required due to there being no patient data collection and no intervention‐based research. This was confirmed by a senior board member on the Hungarian chapter of the ILAE. In Spain, no ethics committee was required due to there being no patient data collection and no intervention‐based research.

RESULTS

Comparison of neurologists' responses between countries

A total of 313 responses from neurologists were recorded across all countries. This included 75 from the UK, 55 from Norway, 69 from Sweden, 54 from Spain, and 60 from Hungary. Within the broader category of neurologists, various subtypes were represented. A breakdown of the number of responses in each neurologist subcategory is provided in Table 1. There was significant variation in the types of neurologists who responded across the five countries (p < 0.001). In most countries, epileptologists comprised approximately one‐third of the neurologists, with Spain being a notable outlier where epileptologists represented 61% of the responses. Pediatric neurologists made up just over one‐third of the neurologist cohort in the UK and Hungary. General neurologists were most predominant in Norway (60%) and Sweden (52%).

Epilepsy and SUDEP communication experience

Responses from the five participating countries were compared, and Figure 1A,B and Table S1 provides a breakdown of the roles of respondents in the management of epilepsy, how often and time spent discussing SUDEP. Figure 1A,B reflects the number of responses to each question, alongside a summary of the responses categorized by number and percentage.

Epilepsy experience and SUDEP discussion.

The results indicated no significant differences in epilepsy experience of the respondents across the five countries. However, a notable distinction emerged in the percentage of time devoted to epilepsy‐specific work (p < 0.001). In Spain, 57% of respondents reported spending the majority of their time (i.e., more than half) on epilepsy‐related tasks; whereas only 13% of Swedish respondents allocated a similar proportion of their time to epilepsy care.

Figure 2 gives the common reasons and associated percentages of each country respondents of when they would discuss SUDEP. Significant differences in the discussion of SUDEP were observed across most categories, except for instances where patients specifically inquired about SUDEP, for which no country‐based differences were noted.

Reasons for discussing SUDEP.

Neurologists in the UK were significantly more likely to discuss SUDEP with all patients (12%), while this figure was substantially lower in other countries (3% or less) (p = 0.003). In Spain, 85% of respondents reported discussing SUDEP when a change in risk was observed, compared to fewer than half of respondents in Sweden (46%) and Hungary (41%) (p < 0.001). Additionally, 18% of Norwegian and 21% of Hungarian neurologists reported rarely or never discussing SUDEP, in contrast to 1% of respondents from the UK (p < 0.001).

The amount of time spent discussing SUDEP also varied significantly among the five countries (p < 0.001). Spanish neurologists devoted the most time, with 29% reporting that they spent 25% or more of their time discussing SUDEP. In comparison, only 1% of UK respondents and 8% of Swedish neurologists reported spending a similar proportion of their time on SUDEP‐related discussions.

Respondent views on various aspects of SUDEP communication

Significant differences were observed across the five countries on all four themes examined on the relevance of SUDEP communication and patient understanding (Table S2).

There were significant countrywide differences on the importance of SUDEP communication (p = 0.03). Swedish respondents were the least likely to prioritize these topics, with only 37% considering them most important, while nearly 60% of participants from the UK and Spain viewed these matters as highly important. Figure 3A–E provides an illustrative comparison of countrywide respondent comparisons.

Responding neurologists' views on the importance of SUDEP communication.

Most factors influencing the decision not to discuss SUDEP varied significantly between countries. This included time availability (p < 0.001), potential to cause distress to the patient (p = 0.03), clinical perception that the patient or their carers would be unable to comprehend SUDEP (p = 0.01). The factors that did not demonstrate strong country‐based differences were perceived “low clinical risk (p = 0.05)” and “other reasons.” Figure 4 provides an illustrative countrywide comparison of these themes on reasons not to discuss SUDEP.

Reasons for not discussing SUDEP.

Spanish neurologists were more likely to have a problem with time constraints (57%), while 23% of Hungarian neurologists cited this as a reason. Hungarian neurologists were less likely to identify complexity as a barrier to discussing SUDEP. Over 60% of Swedish respondents cited patient comprehension as a barrier for SUDEP discussion, while this was an issue for 31% of Norwegian respondents.

The five countries also exhibited significant variation in their responses to the use of face‐to‐face and/or virtual consultations being suitable to discuss SUDEP. Nearly half (44%) of UK neurologists considered virtual review comparable to face‐to‐face, compared to only 9% of Spanish and 12% of Hungarian neurologists (p < 0.001). UK neurologists were least likely to believe that SUDEP would be less likely to be discussed during a virtual consultation (p < 0.001).

SUDEP experience of participants

Four themes looked to explore countrywide experiences to SUDEP and post SUDEP support (Table 2). These included “participant loss of a patient/s to SUDEP,” “access to bereavement support for families,” “knowledge of available agencies to support bereaved families” and the “overall participant experience to SUDEP counselling.”

There were significant country‐based differences in the experience of having a patient die from SUDEP (p = 0.001). Spanish neurologists were most likely to have experienced a patient's death from SUDEP (63%), while respondents from Norway (36%) and Hungary (38%) were least likely to report such experiences.

Bereavement support was most commonly available in the UK (56%) and Sweden (54%), whereas only 8% of Norwegian respondents reported that such support was available (p < 0.001).

The overall experience with SUDEP‐related communication also varied significantly across the five countries (p = 0.01). Positive experiences with communication were least common in Hungary, where only 19% of respondents reported favorable outcomes, while Spain had the highest proportion of positive experiences, with 43% of respondents reporting satisfactory counseling outcomes.

DISCUSSION

In developed western countries, SUDEP is recognized to be a significant cause of death before the age of 70. 16 , 17 This has been recognized by the focus on this area in the latest international guidance, including those from the American Academy of Neurology 17 and the recent National Institute of Clinical Excellence (NICE). 16 Both guidelines unconditionally and categorically stress the need to inform and discuss SUDEP with people with epilepsy and/or their carers. This has been further supported by patient‐facing organizations and charities that want full and frank discussions on SUDEP. However, despite such overwhelming expressions of need, there has been a continued reticence in the clinical system to provide suitable and satisfactory counseling. 19 , 21 , 23 Currently, there is no legal mandate to provide SUDEP communication in any of the countries involved in this survey. This may be an inevitable next step, ensuring risk is communicated through regulation.

Our paper looks to explore the attitudes, challenges, barriers, and perceived needs of health professionals from five European developed countries having similar economic and health systems. This study uses validated themes on inquiry of SUDEP‐related issues for clinicians that have been derived from a previous paper which pooled all 16 healthcare surveys. 20 Our findings are, in our opinion, noteworthy in highlighting various concerns and potential aspects for improvement. We also believe our findings could be generalizable to an extent to other countries with similar economic and health systems.

Comparing the responses from a diverse range of countries highlights significant variations in approaches to SUDEP communication and offers a critical reflection on the development of an evidence‐based survey tool tailored to better understand these differences. If we compare the responses from this investigation to previous large SUDEP communication surveys of professionals, we observe similar findings, with only limited progress from decades ago (Table S3). 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 The data cannot be compared directly due to differences in population and methodological factors. However, the general proportional responses are similar. A large, broader international survey including lower income countries corresponded with lower SUDEP communication. 19 Therefore, although SUDEP awareness has increased over time, we have not observed a significant impact upon clinical practice and the level of SUDEP communication. This standardized survey provides us with insight into the barriers clinicians may face in approaching SUDEP communication. Further, it also allows for comparison analysis with any future survey following the template evidence‐based methodology. 20

The findings from this study revealed that there were differences in the types of neurologists involved in epilepsy management who responded across the countries. While most participating neurologists in most countries were epileptologists (approximately one‐third), Spain stood out with 61% of responses coming from epileptologists. In contrast, the UK and Hungary had a higher proportion of pediatric neurologists, while no pediatric neurologists participated in the Norwegian responses. The variation in the roles of specialists across countries may reflect differing healthcare structures, specialization availability, and regional practices, which could significantly influence the approaches to SUDEP communication and patient care. The differences may also be related to any bias in approaching potential participants and invitation to the survey in each country.

One notable finding of this survey was the marked variation in the amount of time participating neurologists allocated to epilepsy‐specific care and discussions surrounding SUDEP. Countries with more dedicated specialist time allocated for epilepsy care appear more engaged in SUDEP communication. Furthermore, this aligns with the experience of a participant having personally encountered a SUDEP death, familiarization with bereavement support, and prioritization of SUDEP communication. These differences may reflect varying levels of awareness and resources available for epilepsy care in different countries.

The study highlighted significant differences in how SUDEP is communicated to patients, particularly in terms of frequency and the barriers neurologists face when discussing SUDEP. UK neurologists participating in this study were the most likely to discuss SUDEP with all patients (12%), while responses from other countries were much lower (3% or less). In the UK, SUDEP Action is a charitable organization working on behalf of bereaved families to improve SUDEP awareness that is established and may be influencing UK clinicians' responses. SUDEP Action is the host to the SUDEP and Seizure Safety Checklists for clinicians to offer structured evidence‐based personalized communication of SUDEP. 9 , 10 , 23 , 29 It appears to be used largely by epilepsy nurses, an important professional group who help bridge the doctor‐patient divide in the UK more effectively. 21 There have been over 2000 professionals who have downloaded this Checklist in the UK. Other SUDEP Action resources include EpSMon, a freely available mobile app for people with epilepsy to self‐assess risk, the Epilepsy Death Register, a self‐report register of SUDEPs and epilepsy deaths, commissioning guidelines, bereavement support, and a range of other resources for public and clinicians. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34

SUDEP Action and other epilepsy charities in the UK have also ensured SUDEP research remains a priority and have been instrumental in political lobbying and raising public awareness via media. 34 , 35 , 36

There has also been a systems approach to SUDEP and epilepsy risk in vulnerable special groups, particularly people with intellectual disability and epilepsy in the UK, who are at higher risk of SUDEP; this could be added to the increased awareness. 37 , 38 , 39 This has involved raising a whole system awareness of the vulnerabilities of people with intellectual disability and epilepsy, particularly psychiatrists working with this population. 40 , 41 , 42

There is no data indicating whether systematic additional written or audio‐visual supplemental materials are used regularly by clinicians when discussing SUDEP in other countries. There is a lack of specific organized patient lobby groups particularly for SUDEP such as SUDEP Action and other epilepsy charities in the UK. Nor is there a systems approach to the care of epilepsy in people with intellectual disabilities outside the UK. It could be that these multi‐modal efforts have led to an increased engagement on SUDEP discussion in the UK. There is certainly no standardized approach between or within countries.

We can observe that the role of the specialist epilepsy nurse (where available) may be crucial to supporting SUDEP communication and correlates with rates of discussion. 21 Therefore, we may be missing important information from countries such as Sweden, where specialist epilepsy nurses engage in SUDEP communication similar to UK practice; these are data worth exploring in the future.

Another explanation for low numbers engaging in risk communication (12%) could be the attitude the neurologists might be bringing around risk communication. This is highlighted by the belief that they will discuss SUDEP if they believe the patient is “high risk.” However, given that there are no objective risk measurement tools, this would be a subjective and arbitrary approach to an important clinical area.

For the entire cohort, only 16%–28% of the participating neurologists considered SUDEP communication in the highest category of importance. This is an issue of concern, as SUDEP has been identified by both expert clinical groups and patient representative organizations as one of the most important clinical and research topics for them. 16 , 17 , 30

There was great variation in whether SUDEP is discussed with a newly diagnosed patient (4%–53%) across the countries, which does not align with good practice as outlined by clinical guidelines. 16 , 17

In Norway, Sweden, and Hungary, a significant proportion (18%–24%) of respondents rarely or never discuss SUDEP in general. This would be again an issue of significant concern and sits outside recommended best practice. In the UK, it is best practice as per NICE to discuss SUDEP soon after diagnosis of epilepsy and subsequently at regular intervals based on presenting risk. 16 It is anticipated that 10–15 min is spent by the neurologist introducing SUDEP and is followed up soon by the epilepsy nurses for a more in‐depth discussion. The use of semi‐structured tools such as the Safety Checklists and EpSMon have contributed to more informed engagement and awareness. 9 , 10 , 29 , 31 , 32 In Norway, the first consultation is of 45 min followed by 30‐min appointments, and it is estimated around 10 min is utilized in the first four consultations to raise SUDEP with the patient. In Hungary, treating neurologists spend around 10 min/visit to educate patients of all risks including SUDEP. In Spain, there is no identifiable pattern to discuss SUDEP, but it is expected to be neurology dependent and around 10 min when reviewed. In Sweden, approximately 60 min is dedicated to a first consultation and 20–30 min in follow‐up visits. Epilepsy nurses, where available, offer counseling for some 30 min, of which maybe 10 min will be about risks including SUDEP.

The variance between countries could suggest cultural, systemic, and institutional factors that may influence the SUDEP communication. There is also a need to consider the relative size of the country, economic status, and number of neurologists specializing in epilepsy in each country.

As a whole, neurologists were most likely to discuss SUDEP when they perceive a change in risk, over and above the initiation of the conversation from a person with epilepsy. For every country, less than 50% of respondents rate the importance of the person with epilepsy (or their family/caregiver) understanding SUDEP risk at the highest importance. This remains a paternalistic approach and does not concord with a collaborative approach to SUDEP communication where risk is acknowledged and the responsibility shared, empowering people with epilepsy to develop a personalized approach to risk reduction focusing on modifiable factors. 10 , 11

Another interesting aspect of the study was the varying responses to whether face‐to‐face and virtual consultations were considered similar, particularly in terms of discussing SUDEP. UK neurologists by far were more positive than those in other European countries. The noted discrepancies suggest that cultural attitudes towards virtual care and communication may impact how sensitive topics such as SUDEP are addressed in different regions. As telemedicine continues to expand, it is essential to thoroughly assess its role in delivering effective care and ensuring that crucial conversations, like those about SUDEP, are not overlooked or diminished in quality.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides views from clinicians actively involved in epilepsy care across five European countries based upon a standardized evaluation framework. The respondents from each country are limited and constitute a small fraction of potentially eligible participants. To what extent their opinions are representative for their country is unknown. The method of approach and communication may have been biased towards particular viewpoints. The distribution of respondent subgroups (epileptologists, general neurologists, pediatric neurologists, other) varied significantly between countries. It cannot be excluded that this can impact the apparent differences in responses between countries. The number of responders in each neurology subgroup for this survey did not provide enough statistical power for any meaningful analysis. In future, it would be useful to consider the differences between the subgroups by specifically recruiting responders in each category.

CONCLUSION

Through the use of an evidence‐based standardized survey tool, this investigation was able to compare views on SUDEP communication from neurologists who responded in five different European countries. The methods were transferable and easily replicated in each country. The views on SUDEP communication may be influenced by some of the factors we have already discussed, including the presence of a national SUDEP guidance for that country and the level of advocacy for SUDEP (e.g., SUDEP Action, UK). Despite apparent differences between countries, a consistent finding is that a significant gap remains between the way neurologists in all countries communicate about SUDEP and the lack of following of recommendations in international guidelines. The study data suggest that there may be specific factors that impact views on SUDEP communication, including level of experience and time spent in specialist epilepsy care. In the future, a standardized approach to SUDEP communication with core principles and supportive tools may be an option to provide a consistent approach and minimum standard.

Implications for patients, clinicians and services

Clinician‐reported barriers to SUDEP discussions expose critical gaps in healthcare systems, necessitating reforms that prioritize sustained, supported dialogue from the outset. All clinicians must be equipped to lead person‐centered, holistic conversations, avoiding reductive risk categorizations that undermine patient education, engagement, and informed decision‐making.

Implications for policy

Epilepsy remains overlooked in national health and policy agendas, despite its designation as an urgent priority by the WHO Intersectoral Global Action Plan. With 76% of epilepsy deaths deemed potentially preventable, dismantling clinical barriers to SUDEP and mortality discussions is imperative. Our study demonstrates a clear need to embed structured SUDEP communication practices from diagnosis, even within high‐income, highly literate populations. A four‐point action plan to systematically embed SUDEP communication is proposed in Table S4. 43

Implications for research

Research should focus on overcoming communication barriers, empowering patients in risk self‐management, and integrating digital tools to enhance clinical practice and outcomes.
