‘Fast Ossifier’ in diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis: a sex-modulated, heterogeneous phenotype with accelerated ossification and early trabecular decline
Authors: Emilio Pariente-Rodrigo, Marta Martín-Millán, Giusi Sgaramella, Javier Pardo-Lledías, Patricia Fierro-Andrés, Merelyn Bonome, Sandra Solares, Carmen Ramos-Barrón, José Manuel Olmos-Martínez, Victor Martínez-Taboada, José L Hernández
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH) is considered a slowly progressive condition, typically requiring a decade to achieve full radiographic development. However, some individuals exhibit accelerated ossification. This study aimed to characterise the clinical profile of these patients, referred to as Fast Ossifiers (FO).MethodsStudy nested within the Camargo Cohort, integrating cross-sectional and longitudinal data (baseline (E0), 5 year (E1) and 10 year assessments (E2)). Propensity Score matching was applied. FO was defined as progression of ≥2 grades in Schlapbach’s Scale between consecutive assessments. We evaluated inflammation, insulin resistance (via Triglyceride-Glucose Index (TyG)), Visceral Adiposity Index (VAI), intact parathormone (iPTH), bone turnover markers and Trabecular Bone Score (TBS).ResultsWe analysed 455 DISH cases and 455 matched controls. During follow-up, 61 individuals fulfilled FO criteria (18%<60 years; 49% female; 65.6% obese; 72.1% hypertensive). Compared with controls, FO subjects had higher TyG (8.65±0.9 vs 8.39±0.4; p=0.002), FO-females showed higher visceral adiposity (VAI 2.30±2 vs 1.44±0.1; p=0.024), and both sexes presented elevated iPTH at E2. In multivariable models, FO was associated with high TyG (adjusted OR=9.31; 95% CI: 1.04 to 36; p=0.046), low TBS (adjusted OR=0.002; 95% CI: 0.001 to 0.61) and higher alkaline phosphatase levels (79 vs 69 (U/L); p=0.043).ConclusionsFO represents an active variant that challenges the view of DISH as a quiescent disease affecting older men. Rather than a single entity, FO emerges as a convergent phenotype driven by diverse metabolic pathways and linked to accelerated skeletal changes, including ossification and early trabecular impairment.

Introduction

Diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH) is a systemic condition characterised by progressive ossification of ligaments, entheses and adjacent soft tissues, primarily affecting the axial skeleton. While its pathogenesis remains incompletely understood, genetic predisposition, mechanical loading and metabolic dysfunction have all been implicated as contributing factors.1 2

Recent findings have begun to challenge long-standing assumptions about DISH, particularly the notion that it is a non-inflammatory and slowly progressive degenerative disorder of ageing.1 Traditionally, it has been thought to require at least a decade to reach full radiographic expression.3 However, emerging evidence suggests that the rate of progression may vary considerably between individuals, and that endocrine, inflammatory and metabolic mechanisms may be involved in driving disease activity.1 2 In particular, younger individuals4 and those with elevated Body Mass Index (BMI)5 6 appear more prone to present accelerated ossification.

DISH is strongly associated with metabolic syndrome (MetS), particularly central obesity, hypertension and insulin resistance (IR).7 8 Furthermore, several large population-based studies have demonstrated an increased risk of ischaemic stroke among individuals with DISH, independent of conventional cardiovascular risk factors.9 10 This observation suggests a possible shared pathophysiological substrate between ossification and cerebrovascular damage, potentially mediated by inflammation, endothelial activation or vascular remodelling. In this context, Mader et al proposed that chronic inflammation and angiogenesis may represent a key mechanism underlying both conditions,11 a view supported by imaging and biomarker data indicating a persistent low-grade inflammatory state in DISH.4 5 12

Beyond inflammation, IR—a hallmark of MetS—may accelerate disease progression by promoting osteogenesis, impairing bone resorption and disrupting bone remodelling, in part via hyperglycaemia-induced cytokines and RANKL activation.13

In turn, visceral adiposity, which is closely tied to both IR and inflammation, may further amplify ossification through pro-osteogenic adipokines. An elevated Visceral Adiposity Index (VAI) has been associated with poor bone quality and metabolic impairment,7 suggesting that IR, inflammation or visceral adiposity could lead to a faster or more severe progression of the disease.

From a clinical perspective, early identification of individuals at risk for rapid radiographic progression—here referred to as Fast Ossifiers (FO)—may have prognostic implications. These patients could experience earlier skeletal burden, functional impairment and elevated metabolic risk. However, no formal criteria currently exist to define or detect such cases in the early stages of the disease.

The aim of this study is to identify and characterise individuals with rapid progression of DISH, according to predefined criteria, using longitudinal radiographic, metabolic and biochemical data. Special emphasis is placed on IR, systemic inflammation and bone turnover markers, in order to better delineate the biological profile of the FO phenotype.

Subjects and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective longitudinal study was nested within the Camargo Cohort, a well-characterised population-based cohort initiated in 2006 to investigate the incidence and progression of metabolic bone diseases in postmenopausal women and men aged ≥50 years in Northern Spain. The cohort features periodic assessments—including structured interviews, laboratory evaluations, radiographic imaging and DXA scans—at baseline and every 5 years. Detailed cohort methodology has been previously published.14 Participants with diseases or treatments known to affect bone metabolism in the previous year were excluded, as well as patients with a previous diagnosis of ankylosing spondylitis and chronic liver disease. However, if the diagnosis of a bone metabolic disorder was made as a result of enrolment, the patient was maintained in the cohort.

We implemented a longitudinal design with three endpoints: baseline (termed endpoint-0, E0), +5 years (E1) and +10 years (E2). In addition, a cross-sectional design to compare DISH and non-DISH individuals at the endpoints.

Data from participants were drawn from baseline assessment, second and third cohort evaluations (2006–2010, 2010–2015 and 2015–2019, respectively) (figure 1).

DISH: radiographic classification and FO definition

A diagnosis of DISH relies on the radiographic criteria of Resnick and Niwayama, which require flowing osteophytes over at least four contiguous vertebrae of the thoracic spine, preservation of the intervertebral disc space without extensive degenerative intervertebral disease and the absence of apophyseal and costovertebral joint ankylosis and sacroiliac joint erosion, sclerosis or bony fusion.15 Schlapbach’s Graded Scale16 is a tool usually used in the Camargo Cohort to evaluate DISH images in plain radiographs (figure 2). This scale involves the thoracic spine and distinguishes four grades. Grade 3 (three or more bony bridges) overlaps with the diagnostic criterion of Resnick and Niwayama. Interobserver and intraobserver reliability for this scale has been validated, including by our group (weighted kappa: 0.81–0.89).17

Schlapbach’s Graded Scale applied to DISH radiographs and operational definition of ‘Fast Ossifier’ (FO). DISH, diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (Drawings modified from ref 16)

Taking into account the traditional 10-year timescale for full radiographic development, the views of other authors9 10 and our previous work with this scale,5 16 we arrived at an operational definition of FO. Based on changes in the Schlapbach Graded Scale over time, FO status was defined as ‘patients who advanced by two or more grades on the Schlapbach Scale between two consecutive assessments’ (see figure 2 for details). Although not previously validated in DISH-specific plain radiographic studies, this cut-off is consistent with progression metrics used in CT-based DISH assessments (≥2 new bony bridges).37 Those who progressed ≥2 grades between E0 and E1 were denominated as FO1, and those who completed the progression between E1 and E2 were denominated as FO2.

Two trained readers, blinded to clinical data, independently evaluated the DISH images on digitised lateral thoracic and lumbar plain radiographs. Prior to formal grading, a calibration session was held with a senior radiologist, which included consensus scoring of 20 external radiographs.

Propensity Score matching

The Propensity Score (PS) is the result of a logistic regression including the covariates estimated to be related to the outcome variable and/or the treatment decision. The goal of PS matching (PSM) is to ensure that the treatment groups are comparable with respect to the measured baseline characteristics in order to achieve a comparison with minimised selection bias.18

The PSM was used to match all DISH cases registered in the Camargo Cohort (n=455) to non-DISH controls. Considering variables closely related to DISH, the PSM model included age, sex, obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypertension, waist circumference (WC) and MetS. The model also incorporated the K-nearest neighbour algorithm, a calliper width of 0.05, a 1:1 ratio and sampling without replacement.

All DISH cases within the Camargo Cohort were identified and matched 1:1 to non-DISH individuals using a PS model. The matched control group served as the source population for subsequent analyses. For longitudinal evaluation, we selected the highest PS tertile (T3) as the control group, representing individuals with the greatest baseline likelihood of developing DISH. In contrast, the lowest PS tertile (T1) was selected as control group for cross-sectional comparisons, thereby maximising the contrast in skeletal and metabolic parameters. The primary comparison of interest remained between the FO group and non-DISH controls, who shared highly comparable baseline characteristics due to the robustness of the initial matching.

After excluding subjects with incomplete data, the longitudinal analysis evaluated 238 individuals (non-DISH (T3, n=86), grade 1 (n=103) and grade 2 (n=49)), while the cross-sectional analysis evaluated 117 controls (T1), alongside the overall control group (n=415).

The quality of matching was assessed using standardised mean differences, variance ratio, pseudo-R² and L1 imbalance.18 The results showed that the matching procedure had effectively minimised systematic differences between groups (online supplemental annex 1)

Clinical and laboratory data

Weight, height and WC were assessed with the participants wearing underwear and without shoes. BMI was measured in kg/m2, considering obesity when BMI≥30 kg/m2. Alcohol intake was defined as a daily consumption >20 g. Diagnoses of hypertension, T2DM, ischaemic heart disease, stroke and chronic renal failure were collected from the medical records and were based on clinical guidelines and international standards. MetS was defined according to the NCEP-ATPIII criteria and the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) was estimated with the CKD-EPI formula. We also analysed the Charlson Index as a general measure of comorbidity.

Blood samples were drawn from an antecubital vein in the morning after a requested 12-hour overnight fast. Serum concentrations of 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D), intact parathyroid hormone (iPTH), amino-terminal pro-peptide of type 1 collagen (P1NP) and C-terminal telopeptide of type 1 collagen (CTX) were determined by an automated method of electrochemiluminescence (Elecsys 2010, Roche Diagnostics, GmbH, Mannheim, Germany). Serum concentrations of albumin, total alkaline phosphatase and lipid parameters—including total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL)—were obtained by automated methods in an ADVIA 2400 Chemistry System autoanalyser (Siemens, Germany). Detection limits and normality ranges are shown in online supplemental annex 2.

Regulators, effectors and composite biomarkers

Recent advances in the field of osteometabolism have emphasised the critical role of metabolic and hormonal signals in bone remodelling. Among key regulators, iPTH has been shown to modulate osteoblast differentiation, insulin acts directly on osteoblasts and inflammation reprograms osteoclast metabolism.19 These elements provide a biological rationale for investigating them as interconnected drivers of accelerated ossification and bone trabecular impairment.

TyG Index is a robust surrogate of IR20 and the albumin-to-globulin ratio (AGR) is a recognised biomarker for inflammation. A cut-off point of 1.50 was chosen, with values below this threshold indicating inflammation.21 Intact PTH represents endocrine modulation of bone turnover and shows extensive associations with IR and lipid metabolism.22 In the same context, we also focused on the effectors alkaline phosphatase, P1NP and CTX. Alkaline phosphatase is a classic marker of bone formation and heterotopic calcification.23

VAI, which serves as a surrogate for visceral fat dysfunction and has been linked to inflammation and IR,24 and the Cardiometabolic Index (CMI), were also assessed. A high CMI is associated with the risk of worse diabetic outcomes and higher levels of IR.25 Formulae corresponding to these biomarkers are shown in online supplemental annex 2.

DXA and DXA-derived measurements

Bone mineral density (BMD) was measured by DXA using a Hologic QDR-4500 device. At the lumbar spine, this method is easily affected by heterotopic calcifications; therefore, femoral neck and the total hip were the choice for BMD analysis. The in-vivo precision was 0.4%–1.5%, and the results were expressed in g/cm2.

The Trabecular Bone Score (TBS) is a non-invasive measure of bone quality. It is a grey-scale texture that correlates with the trabecular microarchitecture of the bone, obtained by transforming 2D DXA images into a 3D structure. The score was calculated from DXA images of the lumbar spine (L1–L4) using TBS iNsight software (TBS iNsight V.2.1, Medimaps, Merignac, France). TBS (unitless) values>1.310 have been considered normal, between 1.310 and 1.230 partially degraded and <1.230 degraded trabecular microstructure.26

Integral volumetric BMD (IvBMD) is a DXA-based 3D modelling of the proximal femur that includes cortical and trabecular volumetric BMD and is expressed in g/cm³. It was obtained from DXA scans using 3D-Shaper software (Galgo Medical, Barcelona, Spain).

Statistics

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Descriptive statistics were reported as mean±SD for normally distributed data, or median and IQR for non-normal distributions. Group comparisons were conducted using t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests or χ2 tests, as appropriate. Missing values were handled by multiple imputation using regression-based methods.

The main analytical approaches included Shapley value decomposition and repeated measures analysis. The Shapley method estimates the relative contribution of each independent variable to the total variance explained (R²) in linear models, helping to identify the most influential predictors.27

To model the FO status over time, two approaches were used. First, a repeated measures design was applied, where each participant served as their own control across timepoints. This method increases statistical power and improves estimation precision.28 Within-subject comparisons were evaluated using Wilcoxon signed-rank test and McNemar’s test. Second, to explore predictors of FO status, we used conditional logistic regression via Cox regression modelling.

The analyses were conducted using SPSS V.29 (IBM Corp. Armonk, NY). The quality of PSM was assessed by using Python-based scripts (https://zenodo.org/records/15030430). Bonferroni correction was applied in post-hoc analysis of variance and crosstabs analyses, and significance was set at p<0.05 (two-tailed).

Results

The study results are presented in four sections: (1) description of the overall sample; (2) description of FO group characteristics; (3) cross-sectional analyses: comparisons between the FO group and the control group in the three differentiated metabolic settings and (4) repeated measures and multivariate models. Finally, a summary of findings related to progression of trabecular damage in FO individuals.

Overall sample description

A control group of 455 individuals was selected through 1:1 PSM from the 455 DISH cases identified in the Camargo Cohort (131 with grade 1, 60 with grade 2 and 264 with grade 3). No significant differences were observed between cases and controls with respect to anthropometric measures, laboratory parameters related to general metabolism or bone quality metrics (online supplemental annex 3).

The ‘Fast Ossifiers’ group: clinical characteristics

Sixty-one individuals were classified as FO, 30 females and 31 males (age range: 44–92 years, 18% <60 years). Of them, 14.8% had T2DM, 34.4% had dyslipidaemia (DLP), 65.6% obesity and 72.1% hypertension. Only individuals who were grade 0 (non-DISH) or grade 1 at baseline could reach FO status, and longitudinal analysis revealed that at baseline, 39 FO (63.9%, 26 women and 13 men) were grade 1, while the remaining 22 FO (36.1%, 4 women and 18 men) were grade 0.

Women and men in the FO group showed significant differences: females were younger (63±7 years vs 69±6; p=0.0001), had lower Charlson Comorbidity Index (0.27±0.1 vs 1.2±1; p=0.002), lower WC (99±12 cm vs 106±10 cm) and lower rate of MetS (36.4% vs 63.6%; p=0.003). FO women had higher plasma alkaline phosphatase levels (79±27 vs 66±16; p=0.025) and higher VAI (2.30±0.9 vs 1.74±0.1; p=0.002). There were no differences between women and men in the FO group in plasma levels of P1NP, CTX, CMI or the regulators TyG, AGR and iPTH.

A total of 51 subjects (83.6%) progressed ≥2 grades between E0 and E1 (FO1). The remaining 10, between E1 and E2 (denominated as FO2). FO1 and FO2 subjects also exhibited differences: while both sexes had similar frequency in FO1, within the FO2 group, up to 70% were females. Basal alkaline phosphatase was significantly higher in FO1 (74±21 vs 66±23; p=0.04), and CTX was significantly reduced in FO2 (0.23±0.1 in FO1 vs 0.14±0.1 in FO2; p=0.007). Compared with FO2 women, females in the FO1 group had a significantly higher rate of alkaline phosphatase over the median (69.6% vs 28.6%; p=0.048).

We conducted a further analysis of the six patients who progressed three grades (from non-DISH to grade III) between two evaluations. Termed ‘Fastest’, all of them were FO1, 50% had AGR < 1.50 and 60% were in the lowest McCloskey TBS category (<1.230). As iPTH increased, TBS value in this subgroup declined and reached 1.108±0.1; in contrast, the remaining groups presented TBS values of 1.264±0.1 (FO), 1.297±0.1 (DISH-non-FO) and 1.291±0.1 (controls), with p values of 0.09, 0.016 and 0.022, respectively.

Comparisons between FO individuals and controls at baseline

Compared with the control group, individuals with FO were older and had a higher BMI and WC. They also had higher rates of hypertension, DLP and obesity (table 1). P1NP and CTX plasma values were downregulated, particularly CTX (0.28±0.1 vs 0.40±0.2 in the control group; p=0.0001). The TBS value was lower in the FO group, within the range of partially degraded trabecular structure.

Comparisons between FO individuals and controls at E1 and E2

Up to 83.6% of FO subjects completed their transformation in E1, while the remaining 16.4% started. In this environment between two activated settings, FO individuals differed from controls in three domains (table 3): glucose metabolism (HbA1c); lipid profile (TC) and bone turnover markers (downregulated P1NP and CTX). Alkaline phosphatase remained stable, TBS values were lower and low bone turnover appeared to be consolidated (online supplemental annex 5). Correlation analysis in the relevant subgroups showed no significant associations except for AGR and alkaline phosphatase (r=−0.884; p=0.003) in males <64 years (table 2).

Loss of trabecular bone in FO status

The FO variant also appeared to be associated with rapid deterioration of the trabecular structure, particularly in the early stages and in younger subjects. The findings that could provide support for this argument are summarised as follows: (1) a marked reduction in TBS from baseline (individuals with FO exhibited a significantly lower mean TBS than controls: 1.270±0.1 vs 1.372±0.1; p<0.001); (2) in multivariable analysis restricted to individuals aged under 64, the aforementioned strong negative relationship between TBS and FO status (B coefficient=−6.46); (3) the lowest TBS (TBS=1.108) observed in the ‘fastest’ subgroup and (4) the IR–TBS axis, as shown by correlation and Shapley analyses, suggesting a link between IR, metabolic activation and early trabecular decline.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to define and characterise FO, a heterogeneous subgroup within the DISH spectrum that exhibits significant radiographic changes of ossification and faster bone trabecular loss within a markedly shorter timeframe.

According to our results, an individual develops FO status through intricate metabolic connections and skeletal changes that differ in terms of timing, severity and biomarker profiles.

The three assessments have been shown to correspond to differentiated environments linked to the acquisition of FO status, with each environment having its own characteristics.

These findings are discussed as follows.

Fast Ossifier and age differences by sex

An early, unexpected and robust finding of our study was the comparatively younger age of women within the FO group. While DISH is classically considered a male-predominant disease emerging in late adulthood, our results reveal a more nuanced pattern: women accounted for nearly half of FO cases and were, on average, 6 years younger than their male counterparts. Notably, the FO group emerged from a sample enriched with individuals at high risk for DISH progression. Among these 182 individuals, 60% were women, which may partially explain their overrepresentation among FO.

Sex-specific metabolic pathways appear to underlie these differences: in women, FO status was linked to high visceral adiposity, elevated TyG and overactive alkaline phosphatase; in men, early elevations in iPTH predominated. These findings suggest that ossification in women may commence earlier through distinct metabolic routes, without contradicting the male predominance in end-stage DISH.

Metabolic environments

FO progression occurred in three differentiated metabolic environments. In E0, metabolic activation and trabecular damage were evident, driven by IR and alkaline phosphatase overactivity in women, and by inflammatory-endocrine signals (iPTH) in men. Despite this stimulatory influence, P1NP and CTX levels were paradoxically reduced, likely due to the suppressive effects of IR.13 Low TBS observed suggests that trabecular degradation may have begun insidiously and preceded overt radiographic progression.

E1 represented a quiescent phase with low bone turnover, persistent hyperglycaemia and an inflammation-driven lipid profile. During this phase, trabecular decline seems to be driven by inflammation, as well as by the adverse effects of low turnover. Total cholesterol was reduced, possibly indicative of an inflammation-driven lipid profile29 with full effects in the next stage.

In E2, renewed activity was observed. Inflammation had a prominent role, alongside two features typically associated with inflammatory settings: high iPTH30 and a distinct lipid profile characterised by low TC, LDL and HDL levels.29 In contrast to the previous stage, a high turnover rate was established, indirectly confirming the weaker influence of IR at this phase.

The role of iPTH: a biphasic modulator

In the FO variant, iPTH emerges as a time-dependent and sex-modulated regulator. At E0, a subset of FO males showed elevated iPTH levels, associated with increased alkaline phosphatase and P1NP, suggesting early osteoanabolic signalling. Notably, iPTH levels remained relatively low during the quiescent mid-phase, consistent with the known suppressive effect of hyperinsulinaemia on parathyroid activity31 32 and aligning with the reduced bone turnover observed. Interestingly, the transition to the final phase was marked by a significant rise in P1NP, CTX and iPTH, indicating the reactivation of turnover and suggesting the suppression of insulin signalling.

No significant differences in 25(OH)D levels were observed between FO and controls at any timepoint. This finding suggests that elevated iPTH in FO was not attributable to vitamin D deficiency, as well as the relevance of non-calciotropic mechanisms—particularly those linked to metabolic dysregulation—in modulating parathyroid activity.

Elevated iPTH levels associated with DISH have been previously reported. Horie et al examined 49 male patients (mean age 59.6 years) undergoing surgery for cervical ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (C-OPLL) and observed that patients with concurrent C-OPLL and DISH, compared with controls, exhibited higher BMI, elevated iPTH levels (60.4±4.8 vs 41.5±3.4; p=0.001) and reduced P1NP and TRACP-5b, indicative of a low turnover state.33

These findings suggest that iPTH plays a crucial role in FO progression. It may drive early-stage ossification in males in the FO1 group and reactivate bone turnover during the final acceleration phase in FO2 women and men.

Visceral adiposity: a sex-divergent driver

VAT is a metabolically active tissue that secretes hormones, different adipokines (leptin, adiponectin) and inflammatory molecules, such as the cytokines TNF-α and IL-6. VAT is closely related to MetS, cardiovascular risk and incident cardiovascular disease, as well as diabetogenic, atherogenic and proinflammatory abnormalities.34 Several metabolic factors identified for VAT also strongly relate to DISH, and both conditions have been shown to be associated.4

VAT has also been related to alkaline phosphatase and iPTH. Elevated alkaline phosphatase levels, also found in preadipocytes and likely playing a role in their maturation, have been associated with visceral fat accumulation, IR, endothelial dysfunction and vascular calcification.35 On the other hand, VAT appears to increase iPTH through secretion of higher levels of specific adipokines.36

Consistent with this, FO females with high visceral adiposity (VAI>2.10) exhibited a distinctive biochemical profile characterised by elevated alkaline phosphatase and iPTH, but a notable decline in P1NP, that could be explained by the known suppressive effects of IR on osteoblast function.

Interestingly, this pattern was not consistently seen in males, where VAT-associated bone metabolic disturbances appear to be mediated more prominently by iPTH than by IR. Sex hormones and sex differences in the distribution of total body adipose tissue could explain part of these results.4

A multidimensional variant of DISH

The study findings suggest that the FO variant is likely to be the common end and result of diverse pathophysiological mechanisms rather than a single, uniform clinical entity. In this regard, several differentiated traits can be discerned. For example, females with pronounced IR, men <66 years with an inflammatory load or females with a high VAI will probably experience the metabolic effects observed in E0 (high IR level, alkaline phosphatase overactivity in females and high iPTH in males). This represents a prototype with a moderate clinical and metabolic burden. In contrast, women <66 years with a milder metabolic profile may represent the FO2 group, which potentially reflects a less inflammatory ossification pathway. These individuals will probably experience an inflammatory-driven metabolic acceleration, with overexpression of iPTH and high bone turnover, thus completing FO status.

Strengths and limitations

Some caveats are necessary when interpreting the findings. Although the cohort was followed longitudinally over a decade, we did not apply a time-to-event approach, such as Kaplan-Meier analysis, due to the inherent constraints of the cohort’s structure. The progression to radiographic changes was assessed at prescheduled follow-up visits rather than through continuous monitoring. In relation to the possibility of misclassification bias, due to a non-detected change of status (control transformed into DISH). The study has been conducted on the general population, mostly Caucasian, in the north of Spain, and the results may not be applicable to other ethnicities. The use of stratified analyses and subgroup models—though essential to characterising FO heterogeneity—may have reduced statistical power in some comparisons. This could explain the presence of wide CIs, limited estimate precision and increased risk of type II error.

Despite its limitations, this research highlights several interesting points. Notably, the radiographic and biochemical follow-up over a period of 10 years. It is also worth noting the innovative use of Shapley analysis, which revealed the impact of IR, inflammation and iPTH on each effector and metabolic environment. Multivariable analyses provided consistency and supported the mechanistic interpretation of the results. We consider the application of PSM to be a key methodological strength. In addition to the balance of covariates, the decision to select the highest PS tertile as non-DISH individuals for longitudinal analysis resulted in 22 FO and 17 DISH non-FO cases registered from this group. This outcome highlights the PS robustness to identify individuals with a latent predisposition to fast progression of DISH.

Our proposal of the FO phenotype builds on the important contributions of several research groups that have characterised early structural changes through serial CT imaging, introduced classification criteria for pre-DISH and advanced our understanding of the natural course of ligamentous ossification. Murakami et al used serial CT scans to document ligamentous ossification over a 5 year period and proposed the concept of pre-DISH, characterised by early but incomplete structural changes that may evolve into classic DISH.37 Similarly, Kuperus and colleagues reported that approximately one-third of adult males with early thoracic ossification developed bridging over a decade, suggesting that progression may follow discrete, stepwise patterns.38 In a subsequent effort, the same group proposed formal criteria for early-phase DISH and validated them using radiographic and CT-based features.39 Our findings align with and extend this body of work by demonstrating that a subset of individuals exhibit accelerated progression in terms of structure and metabolic interconnections.

Conclusions

The FO phenotype is a metabolically active, sex-specific variant of DISH that challenges the traditional view of the disease as a slow condition affecting older men.

Clinically characterised by rapid progression, FO status appears to be a multidimensional construct integrating diverse metabolic and skeletal responses. It has been shown to be linked to IR, visceral adiposity and distinctive endocrine and inflammatory profiles, with iPTH emerging as a key factor.

Longitudinal analysis showed that individuals with FO not only underwent rapid ossification but also exhibited cumulative metabolic and skeletal alterations throughout the follow-up period, consistent with a severe, multidimensional disease course. Although the study was not designed to address extra-skeletal outcomes, the results may raise the possibility that FO also portends an aggressive trajectory in vascular or metabolic regulation, particularly in younger patients. This highlights the importance of promptly recognising FO and developing tailored strategies for risk stratification, prevention and clinical management.

Imaging-based definitions alone seem insufficient to capture the complexity and heterogeneity of DISH’s rapid progression. However, the combination of Schlapbach Scale-based criteria with biomarker assessment and advanced statistical modelling allowed characterisation of FO from metabolic and skeletal perspectives.

Future research should focus on validating a clinical score or predictive equation for the detection of FO in diverse populations, enabling a more dynamic and personalised approach to disease assessment and management.
