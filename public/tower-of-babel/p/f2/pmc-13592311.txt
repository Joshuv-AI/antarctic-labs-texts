Molecular Engineering Boosts Photon‐Activated Immunotherapy for Prostate Cancer Through Concurrent Pyroptosis and cGAS‐STING Pathway Activation
Authors: Cheng Zhang, Xiaolan Yin, Jeongyeon Hong, Qixian Chen, Weijie Chi, Jingyun Wang, Juyoung Yoon, Haidong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTProstate cancer therapy is often limited by metastasis, drug resistance, and systemic toxicity. Photodynamic immunotherapy (PDIT) offers a promising alternative, yet its efficacy depends on photosensitizers that can simultaneously generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) and activate antitumor immunity. Herein, three thiophene (T) dyes based on triphenylamine (D) and N‐ethyl‐benzoselenazolium iodide (Se) (DTSe)‐based photosensitizers were molecularly engineered by modulating the π‐conjugated structure. Incorporation of a carbazole unit affords DZTSe with suppressed fluorescence, enlarged Huang–Rhys factor, reduced singlet‐triplet energy gap, and prolonged triplet‐state lifetime, resulting in enhanced ROS generation. Notably, DZTSe exhibits multi‐organelle localization in the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria, concurrently inducing pyroptosis and activating the cGAS‐STING pathway. This dual stress‐immune activation reprograms the tumor microenvironment and enables effective eradication of primary tumors and suppression of distant lesions in vivo, providing a molecular blueprint for immune‐activating photosensitizers.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) remains one of the most prevalent malignancies in men and is frequently associated with metastatic progression that markedly limits long‐term survival and therapeutic outcomes [1, 2]. Despite advances in surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, treatment resistance and systemic toxicity continue to pose significant clinical challenges [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Benefiting from high spatiotemporal controllability, low systemic toxicity, and intrinsic tumor selectivity, photodynamic therapy (PDT) offers unique advantages over conventional treatment strategies [8, 9, 10]. The core mechanism of PDT lies in the following: under specific light exposure, photosensitizers (PSs) generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), which further damage cellular structures (such as proteins and nucleic acids), leading to cell death, especially for immunogenic cell death (ICD) [11, 12]. Subsequently, the release of tumor‐associated antigens and danger signal molecules ultimately activates a systemic antitumor immune response [13, 14, 15]. On this basis, photodynamic immunotherapy (PDIT) has recently attracted increasing attention as a minimally invasive therapeutic modality that integrates localized photodynamic cytotoxicity with antitumor immune activation [16, 17, 18]. At the core of this approach are photosensitizers that function not merely as ROS generators, but as molecular regulators of intracellular stress signaling. Effective PDIT requires photosensitizers capable of both high ROS production and precise subcellular targeting, as the spatial localization of ROS critically determines cell death pathways and subsequent immune responses [19, 20, 21]. Although single‐organelle (e.g., mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, and lysosomes) targeting employs a precision‐based strategy, its therapeutic efficacy is fundamentally limited by the strong compensatory and adaptive abilities of tumor cells [22]. Against this backdrop, multi‐organelle‐targeting photosensitizers that simultaneously disrupt mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum represent a particularly promising yet synthetically formidable frontier, as they may trigger synergistic oxidative stress and amplified danger signaling far beyond what single‐organelle targeting can achieve [23, 24]. However, the rational design of such multifunctional agents remains a formidable challenge.

Recently, the rapid development of donor–π–acceptor (D–π–A) photosensitizers in phototherapy has demonstrated their great potential as ideal phototheranostic agents. In traditional D–π–A photosensitizers, the modulation of properties relies not only on the modification of electron donors and acceptors but also on the precise design of the π‐bridge structure [25, 26, 27, 28]. For instance, incorporating strong electron‐withdrawing groups such as benzothiadiazole into the π‐unit to construct a donor–acceptor–π–acceptor (D–A’–π–A) configuration can effectively modulate the molecular excited‐state properties and significantly enhance ROS generation efficiency [29]. At the functional level, the limitations of the single‐cell death pathway have prompted researchers to explore new strategies that simultaneously activate multiple death mechanisms [30, 31, 32]. Endoplasmic reticulum‐targeted photosensitizers can induce gasdermin protein‐mediated pyroptosis [33, 34, 35, 36], leading to loss of cell membrane integrity and the eventual release of cellular contents, thereby generating intense inflammatory signals that significantly enhance adaptive immune responses primarily driven by T‐cell activation [37, 38, 39, 40]. Furthermore, mitochondrial‐targeting strategies leverage the membrane potential to promote the selective enrichment of cationic photosensitizers. Their photodynamic effects can facilitate the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) into the cytoplasm, which in turn activates the cGAS‐STING pathway, effectively initiating innate immunity centered on type I interferon responses [41, 42, 43, 44, 45]. Theoretically, simultaneous targeting and disruption of both the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria may synergistically induce pyroptosis and activate the cGAS‐STING pathway, thereby achieving coordinated systemic activation of adaptive and innate immunity [46, 47]. However, photosensitizers capable of cooperatively triggering dual immune responses remain extremely scarce.

Herein, we present a rational molecular design strategy for PDIT by systematically modulating the electronic structures within the thiophene (T) dyes based on triphenylamine (D) and N‐ethyl‐benzoselenazolium iodide (Se) (DTSe)‐based π‐conjugated scaffold, affording three photosensitizers, DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe. Among them, incorporation of a carbazole unit in DZTSe effectively narrows the singlet–triplet energy gap, leading to markedly enhanced ROS generation and a prolonged triplet‐state lifetime. Cellular investigations reveal that DZTSe preferentially accumulates in mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum, where localized oxidative stress simultaneously induces pyroptotic cell death and activates the cGAS‐STING signaling pathway. This dual‐organelle‐mediated process establishes a strong link between photodynamic cytotoxicity and both innate and adaptive immune activation. In vivo evaluation in an LNCaP PCa xenograft model demonstrates efficient suppression of primary tumor growth under light irradiation. Notably, in an RM‐1 metastatic model, DZTSe‐based PDT elicits a pronounced systemic antitumor immune response, characterized by reduced regulatory T cells (Tregs) and enhanced infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+ T lymphocytes at both primary and metastatic lesions. By integrating high‐efficiency ROS production with the coordinated activation of both pyroptosis and the cGAS‐STING pathway, DZTSe represents a single‐molecule platform that enables synergistic photodynamic and immunotherapeutic effects, offering a promising strategy for treating metastatic PCa (Scheme 1).

Results and Discussion

Structural Characterizations and Photophysical and Photochemical Properties of Molecules

Triphenylamine (TPA, acts as an electron donor) and N‐ethyl‐benzoselenazolium iodide (acts as the electron acceptor) were respectively installed at both ends of the bithiophene structure to obtain the molecules DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe, which were obtained by systematically replacing thiophene with benzothiadiazole (an electron‐withdrawing group) or carbazole (an electron‐donating group), respectively (Figure 1a; Schemes S1 and S2). To verify the electronic effects of benzothiazole, thiophene, and carbazole, we have calculated the ionization potential (IP) of the three groups at the ωB97X‐D/DEF2SVP level. In principle, a lower IP value indicates a stronger electron‐donating ability, as it reflects a lower energy barrier for electron loss. Our calculated results (Figure S17) show that the IP values of benzothiazole, thiophene, and carbazole are 6.78, 6.65, and 5.87 eV, respectively. These data clearly demonstrate that carbazole exhibits the strongest electron‐donating ability, while benzothiazole shows the weakest. Notably, although thiophene is generally considered an electron‐donating group, its IP (6.65 eV) is significantly higher than that of carbazole (5.87 eV), confirming that carbazole is a stronger donor. Finally, the intermediate and final product structures were fully characterized by nuclear magnetic resonance and high‐resolution mass spectrometry (Figures S1–S15).

(a) Frontier orbital distribution of HOMO and LUMO energy levels of DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe calculated using the ωB97X‐D/DEF2SVP basis set. (b) Excited state relaxation pathways and corresponding energy profiles of S1 and T1 for DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe.

Density functional theory (DFT) and time‐dependent DFT (TDDFT) calculations were carried out to elucidate the photophysical properties of DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe. Geometric structures in both ground and excited states were optimized at the ωB97X‐D/DEF2SVP level, with solvent effects incorporated using the SMD continuum model. All computations were performed with the Gaussian 16 program. Results indicate that the three molecules exhibit similar distributions of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO). It is worth noting that the contributions of benzothiazole, thiophene, and carbazole to the HOMO are 10.9%, 14.6%, and 15.9%, respectively, whereas their contributions to the LUMO are 18.6%, 7.9%, and 6.6%, respectively. These results indicate that, among the three groups, carbazole exhibits the strongest electron‐donating ability, benzothiazole displays the strongest electron‐withdrawing ability, and thiophene functions as a π bridge. Among the three molecules, the thiophene unit leads to the narrowest HOMO–LUMO energy gap, consistent with the longest absorption wavelength observed for DTTSe. In contrast, the carbazole unit yields the largest energy gap and the shortest absorption wavelength in DZTSe (Figure 1a).

The rate of intersystem crossing (ISC) is governed by the energy gap (ΔE S‐T) and spin‐orbit coupling (SOC) between the first singlet (S1) and triplet states (Tn). A small ΔE S‐T combined with a large SOC facilitates a higher ISC rate. Our results indicate that in DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe, the T1 and T2 states are more stable than S1 (Figure 1b). The computed ΔE S‐T2 values for DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe are 0.169, 0.489, and 0.040 eV, respectively, with corresponding SOC values of 1.100, 0.250, and 0.479 cm−1. These computational findings align with experimental observations showing that all three molecules exhibit comparable total ROS generation.

The colors of DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe varied markedly in the dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) solution (Figure S16), which could be attributed to the difference in the respective absorption peaks of the compounds, with the maximum absorption peak observed at 550 nm for DTTSe, 540 nm for DBTSe, and 520 nm for DZTSe (Figure 2a). By altering the π‐bond structure between the electron‐donating and electron‐withdrawing groups to form different configurations (including D–π–π–A, D–A–π–A, and D–D–π–A), it's particularly important to study the photosensitive properties of these dye molecules. As seen in Figure 2d–f, DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe exhibited weak fluorescence in DMSO, a typical behavior of TPA rotors, primarily due to non‐radiative transitions induced by free molecular motion. However, when insoluble solvents (e.g., toluene) were added to DMSO, the fluorescence intensities of these compounds were enhanced due to the formation of aggregates and the restriction of intramolecular movement. Interestingly, the fluorescence intensity increased significantly at a toluene volume fraction of 90%, indicating that all three molecules belong to the aggregation‐induced emission (AIE) family. Additionally, we observed significant differences in the emission spectra of the three molecules. The emission peak of DTTSe was located near 720 nm (Figure 2b,d), the emission peak of DBTSe was located around 650 nm (Figure 2b,e), the emission peak of DZTSe was located around 690 nm, and the fluorescence intensity of DZTSe was low (Figure 2b,f). Calculating their fluorescence quantum yields, we obtained values of 1.5%, 5.1%, and 1.3% for DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe, respectively (Figure 2c). Therefore, we predict that due to DZTSe's lower fluorescence quantum yield, the absorbed energy may generate higher levels of ROS.

To further clarify the origin of the AIE effect, we calculated the Huang–Rhys (HR) factors and reorganization energies in the excited state for these molecules. In general, a large HR factor in the low‐frequency region together with a high reorganization energy promotes a fast non‐radiative transition rate, leading to fluorescence quenching in dilute solution. When such low‐frequency vibrational modes are restricted, such as in the aggregated state, fluorescence emission can be recovered. Our results show that the maximum HR factors in the low‐frequency region (<200 cm−1) are 4.3 for DTTSe, 6.1 for DBTSe, and 80.2 for DZTSe. Vibrations associated with triphenylamine units contribute significantly to the HR factors in DTTSe and DBTSe, whereas in DZTSe, vibrations of the N‐ethyl‐benzoselenazolium iodide also play a major role in enhancing the HR factor. Furthermore, the computed reorganization energies are 3898 cm−1 for DTTSe (Figure 2g), 3380 cm−1 for DBTSe (Figure 2h), and 9447 cm−1 for DZTSe (Figure 2i). Overall, DZTSe exhibits the largest HR factor and reorganization energy among the three compounds, which may account for its relatively low AIE turn‐on ratio.

Next, the total ROS‐generating capacity of DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe was assessed by using 2’,7’‐dichlorodihydrofluorescein (DCFH) as a probe. The fluorescence intensity of DCFH was positively correlated with the exposure time of the PSs under light irradiation (Figures 2j and S18). In contrast, no significant changes in fluorescence were observed in the control group lacking PSs under the same treatment conditions. DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe were able to effectively induce fluorescence changes in DCFH under the same treatment conditions (10 mW•cm−2, 60 s), and the total ROS of DZTSe was significantly higher than that of the other two molecules. To further confirm the type of ROS produced by the three molecules, singlet oxygen (1O2), superoxide anion (O2 •−), and hydroxyl radical (•OH) were detected using 9,10‐anthracenediyl‐bis(methylene)dipropanedioic acid (ABDA), dihydrorhodamine 123 (DHR123), and aminophenylfluorescein (APF), respectively. As shown in Figures S19–S21, DZTSe can effectively generate both type I and type II ROS. Calculations show that the energy gap between T1 and S0 for DZTSe exceeds 0.98 eV, demonstrating that DZTSe can effectively generate 1O2 (Figure 2k). Subsequently, to perform an in‐depth study on the nature of the triplet states of DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe, transient absorption spectra were measured in degassed DCM under 532 nm photoexcitation, and the decay kinetics were measured in degassed DCM under 398 or 680 nm photoexcitation. As observed in Figure S22, DZTSe was found to have a long‐lived triplet excited state (τ(398 nm) = 455.57 ns, τ(680 nm) = 391.09 ns), compared to DTTSe and DBTSe (Figure 2l).

Cell Imaging

The cellular uptake of PSs and their subcellular distribution within organelles play crucial roles in determining overall therapeutic efficacy. Indeed, observations at 4, 12, and 24 h revealed that DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe clearly entered LNCaP cells at 24 h (Figure S23). To investigate the intracellular distribution of the three molecules, co‐staining experiments were performed in LNCaP cells using DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe alongside commercial organelle‐specific dyes, including Mito‐Tracker, Lyso‐Tracker, and ER‐Tracker. Notably, under the same culture conditions, the red fluorescence of DZTSe overlapped with the green fluorescence of the MitoTracker to a slightly greater extent than that of DTTSe and DBTSe, with a co‐localization coefficient of 0.62 (Figure S24). In addition, we also observed that the red fluorescence of DZTSe overlapped with the green fluorescence of LysoTracker (PCCDZTSe = 0.56) and ER‐Tracker (PCCDZTSe = 0.74) (Figure S20), suggesting that DZTSe can distribute to multiple organelles. According to previous reports, when PSs are present in the endoplasmic reticulum, under light irradiation, ROS generation by PSs induces ER stress, leading to pyroptosis in cells [11]. PS distribution in mitochondria, along with light irradiation, can produce ROS that cause mitochondrial damage, leading to mtDNA leakage and activating the cGAS‐STING pathway [20]. Additionally, PSs in lysosomes, along with ROS generation, can disrupt the pyroptosis checkpoint, thereby amplifying pyroptosis [21]. Luckily, by accumulating in various cellular organelles, DZTSe may cause damage to these organelles, thereby inducing multimodal tumor cell death and potentially further enhancing the efficiency of anti‐tumor immunotherapy.

Detection of Intracellular Reactive Oxygen Species and Cytotoxicity

According to the above results, DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe could induce intracellular ROS generation in solution under light conditions. To assess the performance of these agents in cells, DCFH‐DA was used as an indicator of total ROS. LNCaP cells were pretreated with DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe for 24 h. The light‐exposed group was irradiated for 60 s at a power density of 100 mW•cm−2, and DCFH‐DA was added for an additional 1 h. Subsequently, intracellular DCFH‐DA fluorescence was observed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). An obvious green fluorescence signal was observed in cells, demonstrating that these molecules can generate ROS in live cells. Next, intracellular O2 •− production was monitored using the DHE agent. As shown in Figure 3a, as expected, the red fluorescence signal in the nucleus was significantly enhanced after white‐light irradiation, indicating that DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe could also induce intracellular O2 •− production. Additionally, the CCK‐8 assay was used to assess the cytotoxicity of the molecules under dark and light conditions. As illustrated in Figure 3b, under dark conditions, none of the three molecules exhibited significant toxicity. Upon a light dose of 6 J•cm−2, DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe exhibited significant cytotoxicity (Figures 3c and S25). Subsequently, the half‐maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values for DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe were calculated to be 2.65, 3.09, and 2.16 µM, respectively (Figure 3d). Although the cytotoxicity of the three molecules is similar, the therapeutic value of a drug increasingly depends on its ability to reshape the tumor microenvironment. ICD liberates danger signals that first activate dendritic cells (DCs), enhancing antigen presentation and priming tumor‐specific T‐cell responses that culminate in durable antitumor memory. Consequently, evaluating whether photosensitizer‐induced tumor cell death effectively drives DC maturation is a critical determinant of long‐term efficacy. DCs in the tumor microenvironment play a crucial role in enhancing immune responses. Next, RM‐1 cells were treated with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe under light and dark conditions, respectively, and the supernatants were collected and added to DC2.4 cells. Subsequently, DC cell maturation was assessed by flow cytometry. The proportion of mature DCs (CD86+CD80+) in the supernatants treated with DZTSeLight was significantly higher than that of the PBSLight, DTTSeLight, and DBTSeLight‐treated groups (Figure 3e), which were 256.67, 2.25, and 8.77 times higher, respectively (Figure 3f), clearly inferring that DZTSe was capable of boosting antigen presentation and priming tumor‐specific T‐cell responses.

Insights on Photon‐Driven Pyroptosis and cGAS–STING Mechanisms of Photosensitizers

Given that the DZTSeLight‐treated cells could effectively induce the maturation of DC cells. Subsequently, we investigated the mechanism of cell death and further validated it using a biological transmission electron microscope (bio‐TEM) (Figure 4a). The results showed that the disappearance of cilia from the cell membrane, as well as the breakage of the cell membrane under magnification, were clearly detectable compared with the control group. This may be caused by the perforating protein breaking the cell membrane during pyroptosis. The nucleus changes from its original irregular shape to a regular spherical shape, a state of stress produced by the cell in response to changes in the external environment, which is used to reduce the exchange of substances with the cytoplasm. The morphology of the mitochondria also changed, becoming swollen overall and accompanied by the disappearance of the cristae. The endoplasmic reticulum also became swollen. To further verify the damage to mitochondria in DZTSeLight‐treated cells, we used FCM using the JC‐1 (mitochondrial membrane potential) assay kit to demonstrate the damage to intracellular mitochondria. As seen in Figure 4b, the red fluorescence of DZTSeLight‐treated cells was significantly reduced, and the green fluorescence was significantly increased, which demonstrated that DZTSeLight induced a loss of mitochondrial membrane potential. Interestingly, we also observed that DZTSeLight specifically induced rapid cancer cell death with typical pyroptosis features, including pronounced cellular swelling and giant bubbles within 24 h after light treatment (Figure 4c).

It is well established that, upon undergoing pyroptosis, cells release their intracellular contents, thereby eliciting robust immune activation. Then, the DNA‐intercalating green fluorophore YO‐PRO‐1, a standard probe for pyroptosis pores, was used to verify plasma membrane permeability. As illustrated in Figure 5a, the green fluorescence of the cells treated with DTTSeLight, DBTSeLight, and DZTSeLight accumulates in the nucleus, while PBSLight merely produced a faint rim at the plasma membrane. This nuclear accumulation confirms that all‐PSs compromise membrane integrity during light‐triggered cell death.

To further validate the occurrence of pyroptosis, the expression of GSDMD‐N in cells treated with PBS, DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe after light or non‐light treatment was measured by Western blotting (Figure 5b). Intriguingly, the results demonstrated that GSDMD‐N protein was significantly increased in DZTSeLight‐treated cells. And, Caspase‐1 activity in DZTSeLight‐treated cells was also significantly higher than that in the PBSLight, DTTSeLight, and DBTSeLight‐treated groups, with increases in activity of 3.32, 5.44, and 1.48‐fold, respectively (Figure 5c). In addition, cell supernatants treated with PBS, DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe under both light and non‐light conditions were examined using ELISA kits. Expectedly, the results showed significant 4.79, 1.24, and 2.06‐fold increases in LDH secretion (Figure 5g), 14.25, 0.95, and 2.59‐fold increases in ATP secretion (Figure 5d), 3.36, 1.28, and 1.95‐fold increases in CRT secretion (Figure 5e) and 2.67‐, 1.44‐, and 1.75‐fold increases in HMGB‐1 leakage (Figure 5f) in the supernatant of LNCaP cells treated with DZTSeLight, compared to the PBSLight, DTTSeLight, and DBTSeLight‐treated groups, respectively. Therefore, it can be concluded that DZTSeLight can shear the GSDMD‐FL protein by elevating Caspase‐1, producing the GSDMD‐N segment protein aggregates, further forming perforated proteins that pierce the cell membrane, resulting in the leakage of the cellular contents. Notably, DZTSeLight induced cells to undergo pyroptosis but may also lead to the accumulation of mtDNA in the cytoplasm by damaging mitochondria and activating the cGAS–STING pathway through target protein cascades. Activation of the cGAS–STING pathway further contributes to the production and secretion of interferon‐β (IFN‐β), which promotes the maturation of DCs. As seen in Figure 5h, Western blot analysis showed that the expression levels of p‐STING, p‐TBK1, and p‐IRF‐3 were significantly increased in DZTSeLight‐treated cells. In addition, the release of mtDNA from PBS, DTTSe, DBTSe, and DZTSe under both light and dark conditions was then tested. As observed in Figure 5i, the results displayed that the release of mtDNA from DZTSeLight was 1.93, 1.60, and 1.28‐fold higher than that from PBSLight, DTTSeLight, and DBTSeLight, respectively (Figure 5i). The secretion of IFN‐β in the supernatant of DZTSeLight‐treated cells was significantly increased by 21.18, 11.53, and 4.04‐fold compared to that released following treatment with PBSLight, DTTSeLight, and DBTSeLight, respectively (Figure 5j); the secretion of IL‐6 in the supernatant of DZTSeLight‐treated cells was found to be significantly increased by 22.64, 18.66, and 5.43‐fold compared to that following treatment with PBSLight, DTTSeLight, and DBTSeLight, respectively (Figure 5k). The above experimental results collectively suggest that DZTSeLight can kill tumor cells by damaging multiple intracellular organelles and maturing immune cells through both pyroptosis and the cGAS–STING pathway, thereby markedly altering the tumor microenvironment and converting “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors.

In Vivo Therapeutic Efficacy Assessment of the Subcutaneous LNCaP Tumor Model

The significant in vitro antitumor effects of DZTSe prompted further evaluation of its potential in vivo antitumor effects in LNCaP tumors. Therefore, we first assessed the therapeutic effects of DZTSe on subcutaneous LNCaP tumors using the treatment approach (Figure 6a). Initially, the accumulation profile of DZTSe was detected by intratumoral injection of LNCaP Balb/c nude mice. The near‐infrared signal at the tumor site was observed approximately 6 h after injection and gradually increased, reaching a maximum at around 12 h (Figure 6b). Subsequently, the tumors in the mice, including those in the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, were removed. Interestingly, DTTSe agent was observed to be mainly enriched in the tumor as well as in the liver after 12 h, demonstrating that DZTSe was primarily excreted through hepatic metabolism (Figure 6c). Subsequently, LNCaP‐loaded mice were used to assess the antitumor efficacy of DZTSe. Subcutaneous tumors were established through subcutaneous injection of LNCaP into the left subcutaneous of the mice. When the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, phototherapy was performed by white light irradiation (100 mW•cm−2) for 3 min. Tumor volume and mouse weight analyses showed that both PBS and DZTSeDark failed to inhibit tumor growth (Figures 6d,e, and S28), whereas the tumor growth of the DZTSeLight group was significantly suppressed. These results indicate that DZTSeLight can improve clinical therapeutic effects Additionally, a histological analysis was performed after 14 days of treatment using both hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and TUNEL assays (Figure 6f). Apparently, the DZTSeLight treatment group exhibited significant tumor damage; however, the other groups did not. Importantly, major organs, such as the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, in the DZTSeLight group of mice did not exhibit obvious damage, compared to other groups (Figure S29). The TUNEL assay results also showed that green fluorescence (indicating widespread cell death) was significantly enhanced in the DZTSeLight treatment group, whereas no green fluorescence was observed in the other groups, indicating that the DZTSeLight treatment effectively inhibited tumor growth. Therefore, these results demonstrate that DZTSeLight can effectively induce cell damage in tumors and inhibit tumor growth.

Antitumor Mechanisms and Immune Responses

To further assess the influence of DZTSe‐mediated PDT on the immune system within the tumor microenvironment, we established a bilateral tumor model by subcutaneously injecting RM‐1 cells into the subcutaneous region of C57 mice. Subcutaneous tumors were generated by subcutaneously injecting RM‐1 cells into the left subcutaneous region of the mice (Figure 7a). Three days later, an equivalent number of cancer cells were implanted into the right subcutaneous region to simulate a distal tumor. The tumor was then subjected to white‐light irradiation (100 mW∙cm−2, 3 min) to initiate treatment. The results for bilateral tumor growth and mouse body weight revealed that DZTSeLight effectively suppressed tumor progression in both the primary and distal sites (Figures 7b,c and S30). FCM analysis showed significant changes in CD80+CD86+ in CD11c+ mature DCs in the tumor microenvironment, which increased to 10.12% in the DZTSeLight group compared to PBS (6.35%) and DZTSeDark (6.13%) in the tumors generated, and to 8.13% in the distal tumors relative to PBS (5.21%) and DZTSeDark (5.95%) (Figure 7d,h,i). Among the F4/80+‐labeled macrophages, the level of M1 (CD86+CD206−) macrophages in the DZTSeLight treatment group was significantly increased, from 14.6% to 29.1%, relative to the PBS group. In contrast, the level of M2 (CD86−CD206+) was significantly lower, decreasing from 59.9% to 50.8%, compared to the PBS group. The M1 and M2 levels in the DZTSeDark group were 13.5% and 62.4%, respectively. Likewise, in the distal tumors, the M1 macrophages in the DZTSeLight treatment group increased significantly, from 14.8% to 24.3%, whereas the M2 macrophages decreased significantly, from 63.7% to 57.1%, compared to the PBS group. The M1 and M2 levels were 15.2% and 63.5%, respectively, in the DZTSeDark group (Figure 7e). It was also evident from the M1/M2 ratios that there was a 2.33‐ and 2.54‐fold increase in the DZTSeLight group for the in situ tumors compared to the PBS and DZTSeDark groups, respectively; there was a 1.90‐ and 1.74‐fold increase in the DZTSeLight group compared to the PBS and DZTSeDark groups, respectively, in the distal tumors (Figure 7j,k). Since mature DCs are essential for presenting tumor antigens to T lymphocytes, CD3+CD4+ T cell infiltration and CD3+CD8+ T cell infiltration in the tumors were increased to 4.33% and 1.47%, respectively, while in the distal tumors, CD3+CD4+ T cell infiltration and CD3+CD8+ T cell infiltration increased to 3.43% and 1.19%, respectively (Figure 7f,g). By analyzing the ratio of CD8+ T cells and immunosuppressive Tregs in the primary tumors, the DZTSeLight group showed a 6.75‐ and 7.30‐fold increase over the PBS and DZTSeDark groups, respectively. Moreover, in the distal tumors, the DZTSeLight group showed 2.8‐ and 3.35‐fold increases over the PBS and the DZTSeDark groups, respectively (Figures 7l,m, and S31). The above results suggest that the DZTSeLight group effectively remodeled the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, splenic CD3+CD4+ T cells and CD3+CD8+ T cells were analyzed since the spleen plays an important role as an immune organ. The DZTSeLight group demonstrated the greatest increase in splenic CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells, suggesting that this treatment could successfully trigger a systemic immune response (Figures 7n,o, and S32).

Conclusions

Leveraging a rational molecular design strategy, we developed a novel photosensitizer, DZTSe, that functions as a dual‐pathway immune activator by simultaneously triggering pyroptosis and the cGAS–STING pathway upon light irradiation. Theoretical calculations and in vitro ROS assays indicate that DZTSe effectively enhances ROS yield. In addition, DZTSe can be distributed in both mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum, where it induces organelle‐specific damage, leading to the release of mtDNA and activation of the cGAS–STING signaling axis. Concurrently, it initiates Caspase‐1‐mediated cleavage of GSDMD, resulting in pyroptotic cell death and the release of immunostimulatory damage‐associated molecular patterns. This dual‐mode ICD not only promotes dendritic cell maturation and T cell activation, but also reshapes the tumor microenvironment from an immunosuppressive to an immune‐supportive state. In vivo studies demonstrate that DZTSe‐mediated PDT significantly inhibits both primary and distant tumor growth, with enhanced infiltration of cytotoxic T cells and reduced regulatory T cell populations. More importantly, its dual‐pathway immunogenic death mechanism successfully synergistically activated both innate and adaptive immune responses, thereby providing a new paradigm for tumor PDIT that combines high efficiency with systemic effects.
