UBE2C‐Mediated CD147‐CTLA‐4 Axis Promotes T Cell Exhaustion and Immunosuppressive Microenvironment in Bladder Cancer
Authors: Xuwei Hong, Ting Hong, Xinyu Liu, Peixiu Yao, Weiqiang Lin, Guoyuan Liu, Huihong Guan, Yonghai Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDespite immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) having benefited bladder cancer (BCa) patients, their limited response rates urge elucidation of intrinsic resistance mechanisms. In this study, we demonstrated that ubiquitin conjugating enzyme E2C (UBE2C) promotes the proliferation, invasion, and migration of BCa and inhibits DNA damage, accompanied by a decrease in T cell abundance. In syngeneic C57BL/6 BCa models and BCa–CD8+ T‐cell cocultures, UBE2C silencing reduced CD147 and CTLA‐4 abundance, restored IFN‐γ, TNF‐α, and TGF‐β secretion, expanded CD3+CD8+ subsets, and attenuated tumor growth, invasion, and migration. Conversely, UBE2C or CD147/CTLA‐4 overexpression reinstated T‐cell exhaustion and malignant progression. Targeting the UBE2C–CD147–CTLA‐4 axis resensitizes BCa to immune attack, offering a rational strategy to extend the efficacy of current immunotherapies.Keywords: bladder cancer, CD147, CTLA‐4, T cell exhaustion, UBE2C

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BCa) is a common malignant tumor of the urinary system, with its incidence ranking ninth globally [1]. BCa is divided into non‐muscle‐invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) and muscle‐invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) according to the depth of tumor invasion [2]. The treatment of different types of BCa varies; among them, immunotherapy plays a crucial role in both NMIBC and MIBC [3]. For NMIBC, intravesical BCG instillation is often required after transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) [4]. For MIBC or advanced cancer, immunotherapy represented by immune checkpoint inhibitors has achieved satisfactory effects, which has effectively improved the objective response rate of patients and prolonged the progression‐free survival period [5]. However, the overall response rate to immunotherapy in patients with BCa is < 30% [6]. Therefore, further research is needed to clarify the mechanism of the occurrence and development of BCa.

T cell exhaustion is an extremely important phenomenon in the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment [7]. Exhausted T cells exhibit overexpression of inhibitory receptors, reduced cytokine secretion, and reduced cytotoxic activity in the tumor microenvironment, resulting in an inability to effectively kill cancer cells [8]. In the study of breast cancer treatment, researchers found that SMAD3 and TCF1 transcription factors work together to promote the exhaustion of CD8+ T cells, leading to the failure of immunotherapy [9]. CircTRPS1 can regulate BCa cells through the miR141‐3p/GLS1 axis by regulating the balance of T cell exhaustion [10].

UBE2C is involved in cell cycle progression and checkpoint control [11]. UBE2C expression correlates negatively with T cell infiltration and positively with immune checkpoint levels in lung adenocarcinoma, suggesting its potential as an immunomodulator [12]. Moreover, in esophageal cancer, high infiltration of UBE2C+CD8+ T cells is associated with poor response to neoadjuvant immunotherapy [13]. These findings position UBE2C as a promising target for understanding immune evasion mechanisms. In bladder cancer, although UBE2C overexpression is linked to aggressive phenotypes [14], its immunoregulatory functions remain largely unexplored. Therefore, investigating whether UBE2C influences immune checkpoint expression, particularly CTLA‐4, could provide novel insights into BCa immunosuppression and therapeutic resistance.

A growing number of studies have shown the clinical effectiveness of the immune checkpoint CTLA‐4 in a variety of cancer [15]. T cell activation is inhibited and immunological homeostasis is regulated by CTLA‐4, which is mostly expressed on regulatory and activated T cells [16]. It inhibits T cell activation through a variety of mechanisms, including directly competing with CD28 for co‐stimulating ligands, preventing immune synapse formation, promoting ligand localization, and recruiting phosphatases [17]. The high expression of CTLA‐4 is one of the important markers of T cell exhaustion [18]. Anti‐CTLA‐4 may be utilized to treat BCa by preventing CTLA‐4 from attaching to its ligand, which relieves T cell inhibition and improves T cell immunity to malignancies [19]. At the same time, Zhang et al. [20] found that CTLA‐4 knockout could enhance the therapeutic effect of CAR‐T cells in BCa tumors. This study was to explore UBE2C by promoting CTLA‐4 high expression, enhancing T cell exhaustion, which induces an immunosuppression microenvironment in BCa.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

The BCa cell line T24, UM‐UC‐3, MB49, and human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were both purchased from IMMOCELL (China). The T24 cell culture system is a dedicated medium for T24 cells (CM‐0227; Pricella, China); UM‐UC‐3 was cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin; PBMC was cultured using a dedicated medium (IMP‐H022‐1; IMMOCELL, China); and the MB49 cell culture system is a MEM medium (SH30024.01; HyClone, China) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; BL302A; Biosharp, China) and 100 U/mL penicillin streptomycin (C0222; Beyotime, China) added. Cell culture was performed in a constant temperature incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2.

Isolation and Culture of CD8+ T Cells

CD8+ T cells were isolated and purified from human PBMCs using the Dynabeads Untouched Human CD8 T Cell Kit (11348D; Thermo Fisher, USA). The purified T cells were resuspended in RPMI 1640 complete medium (12633012; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 1 μg/mL anti‐CD3 (11‐0032‐82; Thermo Fisher) and 1 μg/mL anti‐CD28 (16‐0289‐38; Thermo Fisher) antibodies to induce T cell activation.

Cell Transfection and Coculture

The siRNA targeting UBE2C (si‐UBE2C), siRNA targeting CD147 (si‐CD147), the plasmid overexpressing CD147 (oe‐CD147), the plasmid overexpressing CTLA‐4 (oe‐CTLA‐4), the shUBE2C, and their corresponding negative controls were all purchased from Sangon (China). The plasmid overexpressing UBE2C (oe‐UBE2C) and the corresponding negative control were all purchased from IGE (China).

We transfected T24 and UM‐UC‐3 cells with si‐UBE2C, si‐CD147, oe‐CD147, oe‐UBE2C, or their respective controls using Lipo3000 reagent (L3000015; Thermo, USA) to achieve knockdown or overexpression of the target genes. We transfected primary CD8+ T cells with the oe‐CTLA‐4 plasmid or oe‐NC using Lipo3000 to overexpress CTLA‐4 specifically in T cells. Following a 48‐h transfection period, the cells were harvested for subsequent coculture experiments or direct analysis.

To establish a stable UBE2C‐knockdown MB49 cell model, the cells were incubated with lentiviral particles carrying shUBE2C or shNC sequences in the presence of polybrene. After 8–12 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium to remove the viral particles. Following an additional 72 h of culture, the cells were selected with puromycin to obtain stably transfected populations.

In the coculture system, the coculture was maintained for 24 h in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, with a T cell‐to‐BCa cell ratio of 10:1. The Transwell inserts (pore size: 0.4 μm) were used to separate T cells (upper chamber) and BCa cells (lower chamber) while allowing cytokine exchange.

Data Collection and Gene Correlation Analysis

The mRNA expression profile and clinical data were downloaded from the TCGA‐BLCA project (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/), including 408 primary bladder urothelial carcinoma samples. Subsequently, a Spearman correlation analysis was performed to explore the potential association between the expression levels of UBE2C and CTLA‐4 genes.

Clinical Tissue Samples Collection

The use of human tissues was approved by the ethics committee of Shantou Central Hospital, with written informed consent obtained from all participants. BCa tissues were collected from 20 patients (5 females and 15 males) undergoing surgical resection at Shantou Central Hospital between 2022 and 2024. For each clinical sample, the histological and pathological types were independently determined by two skilled pathologists. Detailed clinical and pathological information is provided in Table S1.

Animal

Six‐week‐old male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Hubei Provincial Laboratory Animal Research Center. All mice were raised under standard SPF conditions. All procedures are conducted in accordance with internationally recognized animal welfare and ethical standards to minimize animal pain and discomfort. All animal testing procedures in this study were approved by the Experimental Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Shantou Central Hospital (Approval No. STCH2023010). Twelve mice were randomly divided, six mice were subcutaneously inoculated with 1 × 106 MB49 cells transfected with shNC, and six mice were subcutaneously inoculated with 1 × 106 MB49 cells transfected with shUBE2C. Measure the tumor size every 3 days. One to two weeks after inoculation, the mice were randomly grouped as shNC (N = 3), shNC + anti‐CTLA‐4 (N = 3), shUBE2C (N = 3), and shUBE2C + AAV‐CTLA4 (N = 3). shNC + anti‐CTLA‐4 mice were treated with anti‐mouse CTLA‐4 (9D9) Antibody (HY‐P99132, MCE, USA; 200 μg per mouse) The shUBE2C + AAV‐CTLA‐4 group received CD8 promoter‐specific AAV‐CTLA‐4 treatment (Genomeditech; 30 μg per mouse), and the administration method was peritumoral injection at d7, d10, and d13, while the remaining groups of mice were injected with the same dose of sterile PBS solution. All mice were euthanized with an overdose of pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg, i.p.) on the 21st day after inoculation. All tumor tissues for subsequent Western blot, immunohistochemistry, and flow cytometry analyses were collected at this terminal time point (day 21 postinoculation).

Transwell

After wrapping the upper chamber with 100 μL matrix adhesive (356234; Corning, USA) for 30 min and absorbing the excess liquid, the T24 and UM‐UC‐3 (5000 cells/well) of different treatment groups were resuspended in 100 μL of FBS‐free medium and inoculated in the upper chamber. In the lower chamber, 500 μL medium containing 10% FBS was added. After culturing at 37°C for 24 h, the excess cells on the upper surface were scraped off, and the chambers were fixed in methanol (100141190; Sinopharm, China) and stained with 0.2% crystal violet (C0121; Beyotime) for 30 min. Cells were randomly selected using a microscope (CAVTION) to evaluate their ability.

CCK‐8 Proliferation Experiment

The cells were inoculated into 96‐well plates (5 × 103 cells/holes) and cultured for 24 h. The original medium was sucked out and 90 μL of medium and 10 μL of CCK‐8 (BA00208; Bioss, China) were added to each well. After incubation for 1 h, the OD value at 450 nm was measured by SPECTRA MAX 190 enzyme marker (Molecular Devices).

Flow Cytometry

To analyze the infiltration of T cells in the tumor microenvironment after UBE2C treatment, we stained the tumor single‐cell suspension with CD3‐PE (PE‐65077; Proteintech, China), CD8‐FITC (FITC‐98472‐2; Proteintech), and CTLA‐4‐APC (565778; BD, USA). The apoptosis of T cells was detected using the Annexin V‐FITC Apoptosis Kit (C2015M; Beyotime). After incubation for 30 min at 4°C in the dark, centrifugation was added to PBS, and analysis was performed immediately using a flow cytometer (Beckman, USA).

For tumor tissue analysis, freshly harvested tumors were minced and digested with a cocktail of collagenase IV (1 mg/mL) and DNase I (100 μg/mL) at 37°C for 40 min to generate single‐cell suspensions. Red blood cells were lysed using ACK lysis buffer. After staining with the indicated fluorescent antibodies, cells were washed and resuspended in staining buffer containing a viability dye. Lymphocytes were gated based on forward and side scatter, and single cells were selected.

Exosome Isolation and Characterization

Extracellular vesicles were isolated from conditioned medium of T24 cells by differential ultracentrifugation. Briefly, cell culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min to remove cells, followed by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 20 min to remove cell debris. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min to eliminate large vesicles, and the resulting supernatant was ultracentrifuged at 10,000 × g for 90 min at 4°C to pellet exosomes. The exosome pellet was washed with PBS and subjected to another ultracentrifugation step at 10,000 × g for 90 min. The purified exosomes were resuspended in PBS or RIPA buffer for downstream analysis. Exosome identity was confirmed by Western blot detection of exosomal markers CD 63 and CD 9.

Western Blot

BCa cells or mouse tumor tissues treated under different experimental conditions were lysed with RIPA lysate (BF0003; Boerfu, China) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (BL507A; Biosharp, China) to collect total cellular protein. The protein was quantitatively detected by BCA kit (BF0026; Boerfu), and the protein was separated by gel electrophoresis. After transfer to PVDF membrane (IPVH00010; Millipore, USA), 5% BSA (1172; BioFroxx, China) was closed for 1 h. PVDF membranes with UBE2C (1:1000, 12134‐2‐AP), Ki‐67 (1:2000, 28074‐1‐AP), γ‐H2AX (1:5000, 8307‐2‐RR), PD‐1 (1:5000, 66220‐1‐Ig), CTLA‐4 (1:1000, 30648‐1‐AP), LAG‐3 (1:2000, 31722‐1‐AP), TIM‐3 (1:1000, 82836‐1‐RR), CD147 (1:5000, 11989‐1‐AP) or β‐actin (1:5000, 66009‐1‐Ig) antibodies were incubated overnight. All antibodies were purchased from Proteintech. Then, membranes were incubated with secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h, and signals were detected in 4800 chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon) using an ECL kit (NCM Biotech, China). The results were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue sections that had been paraffin‐embedded and fixed with formalin were deparaffinized in xylene and then rehydrated using a series of graded ethanols. The sections were treated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against UBE2C, CD8 (GB15068; Servicebio, China), and CTLA‐4 after endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked. Secondary antibodies were then added, and the sections were incubated for 40 min at room temperature. Hematoxylin was used to backstain the sections, and diaminobenzidine was used to observe the results. A microscope was used for imaging, and a random field of view was chosen. The immunohistochemistry staining findings were examined by two pathologists. Positive cells of CD8 were quantified using QuPath (v0.2.0) software and counted per high‐power field (HPF) at ×200 magnification.

Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Mouse blood samples were collected and centrifuged at 4°C, 3500 rpm for 10 min to separate the serum. The supernatant from cell cultures or the prepared mouse serum was then carefully collected. The levels of IFN‐γ (R‐S‐M0094; Ruisaiqi, China), TNF‐α (ml002859; Mlbio, China), TGF‐β (ml057830; Mlbio), soluble CD147 (sCD147) (CB15406‐Hu; COIBO BIO, China) were measured using their respective ELISA kits. All steps are performed according to the manufacturer's instructions and OD values are measured at 450 nm using an enzyme marker.

Statistical Analysis

All data analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 statistical software. All experiments were replicated at least three times for data analysis. Student's t‐test and Wilcoxon test were used for the significance of differences between groups. All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

UBE2C Knockdown Suppresses Bladder Cancer Progression and CTLA‐4 Expression

To elucidate the role of UBE2C in BCa progression and the T cell infiltration status in the tumor microenvironment, the in vitro functions of T24 and UM‐UC‐3 cells were assessed after knocking down UBE2C expression. First, the results demonstrated that si‐UBE2C‐1 achieved the most significant knockdown (Figure 1A,B and Figure S1A). Hence, si‐UBE2C‐1 was selected for all subsequent functional experiments. The Transwell assay results demonstrated that UBE2C knockdown led to a substantial reduction in the invasion capacity of T24 and UM‐UC‐3 cells (Figure 1C and Figure S1B). Concurrently, UBE2C knockdown resulted in a significant decrease in the viability of both cell lines (Figure 1D and Figure S1C). Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis revealed that T24 and UM‐UC‐3 cells exhibited elevated rates of apoptosis following UBE2C knockdown (Figure 1E and Figure S1D). These findings indicate that UBE2C contributes to the invasion, migration, and proliferation of BCa cells while preventing apoptosis.

To further investigate the relationship between UBE2C and the tumor immune microenvironment, we conducted loss‐of‐function experiments in a xenograft model. MB49 cells were infected with shUBE2C or shNC, and the resulting cells were then injected into C57BL/6 mice. Ki‐67 and γ‐H2AX expression in mouse tumor tissues revealed that Ki‐67 expression was significantly reduced, while γ‐H2AX expression, a DNA damage marker, was significantly increased after UBE2C knockdown (Figure 1F). These results indicate reduced tumor cell proliferation and enhanced DNA damage response in the shUBE2C group at the terminal time point. Furthermore, transcriptomic data from TCGA‐BLCA cohort revealed a positive correlation between UBE2C expression and CTLA‐4 levels (Figure 1G). In addition, IHC staining of clinical specimens demonstrated that high UBE2C expression in BCa tissue was associated with significantly reduced CD8+ T cell infiltration (Figure 1H). Subsequent flow cytometry analysis was conducted to ascertain the proportions of CTLA‐4 and CD3+ CD8+ T cells within the tumor samples. We observed a substantial increase in the proportion of CD3+CD8+ T cells in tumors from the shUBE2C group compared to the shNC group (Figure 1I). Concurrently, the proportion of CTLA‐4+ cells within the tumor immune infiltrate was significantly reduced upon UBE2C knockdown (Figure 1J). In summary, UBE2C has been shown to promote BCa progression and regulate CTLA‐4 expression and T cell levels.

The UBE2C/CTLA‐4 Axis Drives T Cell Exhaustion in the Bladder Cancer Microenvironment

Studies have shown that the efficacy of checkpoint inhibitor therapy depends on its ability to enhance T cell‐mediated killing of cancer cells [21]. Based on this theory, we evaluated the effects of UBE2C on CTLA‐4 and T cell responses using rescue experiments and coculturing T24 with T cells. First, we validated the efficacy of CTLA‐4 overexpression in T cells (Figure 2A). Consistent with the mouse model displaying downregulated UBE2C expression, in cocultured T cells, the expression of CTLA‐4 decreased after knockdown of UBE2C in T24 cells (Figure 2B). Western blot analysis showed that the proportions of T cell exhaustion markers were reduced in the UBE2C knockdown group. Importantly, this effect was rescued by overexpressing CTLA‐4 specifically in T cells (Figure 2C and Figure S1E). Additionally, CCK‐8 and apoptosis assays revealed that T cell proliferation capacity increased and apoptosis levels decreased in the si‐UBE2C group. This trend was partially reversed in the si‐UBE2C + oe‐CTLA‐4 group (Figure 2D,E). To further validate T cell function, we used ELISA to detect the expression levels of the cytokines IFN‐γ, TNF‐α, and TGF‐β in the culture supernatant of cocultured cells. Compared with the si‐NC group, IFN‐γ and TNF‐α expression levels increased, while TGF‐β expression levels decreased after UBE2C knockdown. Similarly, knockdown of UBE2C combined with CTLA‐4 overexpression partially reversed the expression of these factors (Figure 2F–H). These results suggest that UBE2C regulates CTLA‐4 in BCa cells by promoting T cell exhaustion and inhibiting T cell antitumor activity.

CTLA‐4 Overexpression Reverses the Inhibition of Tumor Growth Mediated by UBE2C Knockdown

We investigated the functional roles of UBE2C and CTLA‐4 in bladder cancer progression by establishing a C57BL/6 mouse BCa model. The experimental results showed that reducing the expression of either UBE2C or CTLA‐4 significantly inhibited tumor growth in mice. However, co‐knocking down UBE2C and overexpressing CTLA‐4 via AAV‐CTLA4 promoted tumor growth (Figure 3A,B). Tumor volume and weight measurements were consistent with this trend (Figure 3D,E). Additionally, compared to the shNC group, mice in the shUBE2C group or the anti‐CTLA‐4 treatment group exhibited stable weight gain. However, mice in the shUBE2C combined with AAV‐CTLA4 treatment group experienced significant weight loss during the latter stages of treatment (Figure 3C). The individual tumor growth trajectories for all mice, demonstrating the consistency of the effect within groups, are provided in Figure S2. These results suggest that UBE2C may regulate tumor growth through CTLA‐4‐mediated immune regulatory pathways.

Knocking Down UBE2C Inhibits Bladder Cancer Growth by Alleviating CTLA‐4‐Mediated Immune Suppression

Further investigation was conducted into the mechanism by which UBE2C and CTLA‐4 regulate BCa tumor growth. Immunohistochemical results revealed that CTLA‐4 expression decreased significantly in the shUBE2C and anti‐CTLA‐4 groups. However, the downregulation trend of CTLA‐4 was partially reversed in the shUBE2C combined with AAV‐CTLA‐4 treatment group (Figure 4A). Western blot analysis validated these protein changes (Figure 4B). Additionally, western blot results showed that UBE2C knockdown or CTLA‐4 inhibition significantly alleviated T cell exhaustion within tumors. However, CTLA‐4 overexpression markedly enhanced T cell exhaustion levels (Figure 4C). ELISA detection of cytokine levels in mouse serum revealed that UBE2C or CTLA‐4 knockdown promoted IFN‐γ and TNF‐α release and inhibited TGF‐β expression. However, combined shUBE2C and AAV‐CTLA‐4 treatment reversed these cytokine changes (Figure 4D–F). These results suggest that UBE2C promotes tumor progression in a mouse BCa model by regulating CTLA‐4 expression and inducing T cell exhaustion.

UBE2C Promotes the Expression of CTLA‐4 by Regulating CD147 and Induces T Cell Exhaustion

CD147 is closely associated with immune infiltration, immune checkpoint molecules, and neoantigen levels in the tumor microenvironment [22]. Our results revealed that UBE2C knockdown significantly decreased CD147 expression, while altering CTLA‐4 expression levels had no significant impact on CD147 (Figure 5A,B). Conversely, although CD147 knockdown did not influence UBE2C expression, ectopic expression of UBE2C was sufficient to restore CD147 levels (Figure S3A). These findings suggest that CD147 is a specific downstream target of UBE2C. To investigate whether UBE2C influences CTLA‐4 expression by regulating CD147, we conducted rescue experiments and coculture validation. First, we validated the transfection efficiency of oe‐CD147 and si‐CD147 via Western blot analysis (Figure 5C). CCK‐8 and apoptosis assays revealed that silencing either UBE2C or CD147 promoted T cell viability while attenuating apoptosis. This effect was partially reversed by overexpressing CD147 or UBE2C (Figure 5D–F and Figure S3B,C). Western blot analysis of the coculture system showed that knocking down UBE2C in T24 cells significantly decreased the protein levels of both CD147 (in T24 cells) and CTLA‐4 in T cells. Similarly, knocking down CD147 in T24 cells significantly reduced CTLA‐4 levels in T cells. Notably, the downregulation of CTLA‐4 in T cells caused by UBE2C knockdown was partially reversed by overexpressing CD147 in T24 cells (Figure 5G). Furthermore, Western blot analysis of cocultures of bladder cancer cells from different treatment groups with T cells showed that inhibiting UBE2C or CD147 reduced T cell exhaustion levels. This phenomenon could also be partially reversed by CD147 overexpression (Figure 5H). ELISA results support these findings, showing that silencing UBE2C or CD147 promotes IFN‐γ and TNF‐α release while inhibiting TGF‐β levels. Compared to the single knockdown group, co‐transfection of si‐UBE2C and oe‐CD147 partially reversed these cytokine changes (Figure 5I–K).

To elucidate how tumor cell‐intrinsic UBE2C communicates with T cells, we investigated its effect on CD147 secretion. ELISA analysis demonstrated that UBE2C knockdown significantly reduced the level of soluble CD147 (sCD147) in the conditioned medium (Figure 5L). We next isolated extracellular vesicles. Western blot analysis of the isolated vesicles confirmed the presence of the exosomal marker CD63 and CD9, verifying successful exosome enrichment with minimal cellular contamination (Figure 5M). Notably, the level of CD147 protein packaged within these exosomes was markedly decreased in the shUBE2C group compared to the shNC control (Figure 5N).

In summary, UBE2C likely promotes CTLA‐4 expression in bladder cancer by regulating CD147, thereby inducing T cell exhaustion and accelerating tumor progression.

Discussion

T cells are the primary target of current tumor immunotherapy [23]. T cell exhaustion refers to a state in which the effector function and self‐renewal capacity of T cells continuously decline and has been shown to be closely associated with tumor progression and resistance to immunotherapy [7]. This state of exhaustion is considered one of the key mechanisms leading to resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [24, 25]. The tumor microenvironment (TME) in BCa is associated with its occurrence, development, and resistance [26, 27]. As BCa immunotherapy continues to evolve, elucidating the specific mechanisms of T cell exhaustion in the TME is of great significance for overcoming treatment resistance and improving patient outcomes. This study reveals that UBE2C can promote CTLA‐4 expression by upregulating CD147, thereby inducing T cell exhaustion, shaping an inhibitory immune microenvironment, and driving BCa progression (Figure 6).

UBE2C upregulates cellular CD147 and enhances its release into the tumor microenvironment both as soluble CD147 (sCD147) and packaged within tumor‐derived exosomes. These CD147‐bearing signals induce T‐cell exhaustion, marked by elevated CTLA‐4 expression and reduced secretion of effector cytokines (IFN‐γ, TNF‐α, TGF‐β), ultimately diminishing CD8+ T cell abundance and antitumor immunity.

UBE2C is overexpressed in various malignant tumors and is significantly associated with poor patient outcomes [11, 28]. The UBE2C/CDH1/DEPTOR axis regulates cell cycle progression and autophagy, promoting the progression of nonsmall cell lung cancer [29]. Additional study has shown that UBE2C expression is closely associated with multiple indicators, including tumor mutation burden, microsatellite instability, immune cell infiltration, and drug sensitivity [30]. Our previous research also demonstrated that UBE2C is significantly overexpressed in BCa and predicts poor prognosis, and it can mediate lymph node metastasis by promoting lymphangiogenesis. Mechanistically, UBE2C mediates the monoubiquitination of SNAT2, inhibiting its K63‐site polyubiquitination modification, thereby regulating BCa progression [14]. This study further confirmed through functional experiments that knocking down UBE2C inhibits BCa cell proliferation and invasion while enhancing the DNA damage response. The observation of reduced Ki‐67 and increased γ‐H2AX in shUBE2C tumors at a fixed experimental endpoint supports the notion that UBE2C knockdown impairs proliferation and induces genomic stress in vivo, consistent with the observed tumor growth inhibition.

CTLA‐4 has been widely used as an ICI in the immunotherapy of BCa [20]. However, clinical data indicate that a significant number of patients develop primary or secondary resistance to CTLA‐4 inhibitors [31], with the underlying mechanisms being complex and involving multiple immune components and signaling pathways within the TME [32]. By competitively binding to ligands on antigen‐presenting cells, CTLA‐4 can prevent T cell activation and IL‐2 generation, which will hinder T cell survival and proliferation [33]. High expression of CTLA‐4 also weakens T cell glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation capacity, further suppressing immune function [34]. Although UBE2C has been identified as a key regulatory factor in tumor progression, its role in immune therapy response remains unclear. A study on neoadjuvant therapy for esophageal cancer found that patients who achieved major pathological response (MPR) had significantly fewer UBE2C+CD8+ T cell infiltrates in their tumors compared to those who did not achieve MPR, suggesting that high UBE2C+CD8+ T cell infiltration may be associated with poor treatment response [13]. Additionally, UBE2C expression is positively correlated with the levels of various immune infiltrating cells in thyroid cancer [35]. These studies suggest that UBE2C may be involved in regulating the tumor immune microenvironment. In this study, we confirmed through in vitro and in vivo experiments that CTLA‐4 acts as a downstream effector molecule of UBE2C in BCa, mediating T cell exhaustion, apoptosis, and inhibition of cytotoxicity, thereby promoting tumor progression.

CD147 is a single chain type I transmembrane protein that plays a critical role in various cancers, including involvement in tumor proliferation, metastasis, and tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment [36, 37]. Research has demonstrated that TGF‐β can induce the expression of CD147, and an elevated expression of CD147 can further enhance the synthesis of TGF‐β, thus forming a positive feedback loop between the two [38]. At the same time, TGF‐β can promote the production of Tregs, and Tregs inhibit the biological function of T cells through CTLA‐4. This interaction forms a positive feedback loop that enhances the immunosuppressive state in the tumor microenvironment [39]. Although both UBE2C and CD147 play important roles in tumor progression, it remains unclear whether UBE2C influences CTLA‐4 expression by regulating CD147. This study confirmed through gene knockdown and complementation experiments that knocking down UBE2C causes a synchronous decrease in CD147 and CTLA‐4 protein levels. Notably, knocking down CD147 also reduces CTLA‐4 expression, whereas UBE2C overexpression restores CD147 levels. Functionally, knocking down UBE2C alleviates T cell exhaustion and apoptosis, while exogenous overexpression of CD147 partially reverses this phenotype. Crucially, we found that UBE2C knockdown not only reduces cellular CD147 but also significantly diminishes its release into the microenvironment, both in soluble form (sCD147) and packaged within tumor‐derived exosomes.

Our study reveals that the UBE2C–CD147–CTLA‐4 axis plays a critical role in promoting T cell exhaustion and shaping an immunosuppressive microenvironment in bladder cancer. These findings suggest several clinical translational opportunities. First, UBE2C and CD147 could serve as predictive biomarkers for resistance to CTLA‐4 blockade immunotherapy. Validating their expression levels in patient tumors and correlating with treatment response will be an essential next step. Second, targeting UBE2C or CD147 pharmacologically, possibly through small‐molecule inhibitors or monoclonal antibodies, may synergize with existing immune checkpoint inhibitors to overcome therapeutic resistance. Third, this axis may also be relevant in other immunogenic tumors where CTLA‐4 inhibitors are used. Future studies should focus on: validating our findings in large‐scale clinical cohorts of BCa patients undergoing immunotherapy; elucidating the precise molecular mechanisms by which UBE2C regulates CD147 transcription or protein stability; and developing dual‐targeting strategies that simultaneously inhibit UBE2C/CD147 and CTLA‐4.

This study has preliminarily revealed the important role of the UBE2C‐CD147‐CTLA‐4 axis in regulating T cell exhaustion, but there are still limitations. The direct molecular mechanisms by which UBE2C regulates CD147 transcription or protein stability have not yet been fully elucidated, and whether CD147 acts as a bridge molecule directly involved in CTLA‐4 expression regulation or membrane transport requires further biochemical and cell biological evidence. While our study mechanistically delineates the UBE2C‐CD147‐CTLA‐4 axis using controlled experimental models, future investigations employing single‐cell RNA sequencing of clinical bladder cancer specimens will be invaluable. Such analyses could define the correlation between tumor‐intrinsic UBE2C expression levels and the abundance or exhaustion state of specific T cell subsets within the tumor microenvironment, thereby bridging our mechanistic findings with patient heterogeneity and potentially informing patient stratification strategies for immunotherapy. Future research will further explore these aspects to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the role of this pathway in tumor immune escape and to provide new strategies for combined targeted therapy.
