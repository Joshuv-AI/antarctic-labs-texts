Kidney biopsy during pregnancy or wait postpartum: two decades experience from a single center
Authors: Aisha Batool, Mukoso Ozieh, Alexander J Gallan, Jack Kleinman, Ashraf El-Meanawy, Anna Palatnik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKidney biopsy is the gold standard for diagnosing kidney disease, yet its use during pregnancy remains controversial due to concerns about procedure related complications and limited contemporary evidence. This study evaluates long term kidney outcomes with biopsy timing in pregnant and postpartum females.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study spanning over 22-years including females aged ≥ 18 years who underwent native kidney biopsy during pregnancy or within three months postpartum. Demographic, clinical, histopathological, maternal, fetal, and long-term kidney outcomes were abstracted from electronic health records. Kaplan-Meier analysis evaluated kidney survival, defined as time to dialysis initiation and statistical significance was set at p-value < 0.05.ResultsIn our cohort, 29 females met inclusion criteria: 13 biopsied during pregnancy and 16 postpartum. Long term kidney survival did not differ significantly between groups (log rank p = 0.3). Complication rates were comparable between groups; low, limited to small, and self-limiting hematomas. Lupus nephritis was the most frequent diagnosis in both groups.DiscussionAlthough long term dialysis rates were comparable between the two groups, the postpartum cohort had a shorter duration of follow up. Biopsies performed during pregnancy and postpartum were safe in this cohort and yielded clinically meaningful information that frequently altered acute management. Nephrotic range proteinuria and an eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m² were key predictors of progression to ESKD.ConclusionIn our study, no statistically significant difference in long-term kidney survival was observed by biopsy timing during pregnancy vs. postpartum, even though pregnancy group had much longer follow-up than the postpartum group.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12882-026-05211-6.Keywords: Kidney biopsy, Kidney disease, Kidney histopathology, Pregnancy, Postpartum, Lupus nephritis

Background

Kidney disease affects about 7% females of reproductive age but takes its grim toll by elevated rates of maternal morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Although kidney biopsy is a critical diagnostic tool that provides definitive insights into the etiology of renal dysfunction, its use during pregnancy is fraught with clinical dilemmas [3, 4]. The current literature is based on retrospective studies with heterogeneous data, in which reported complication rates, and the timing of percutaneous kidney biopsy vary across studies [5–10]. Despite risk of procedure related complications, kidney biopsy during pregnancy is indispensable in cases where immediate diagnosis is essential for guiding urgent therapeutic decisions [11]. While the diagnostic yield of kidney biopsy can influence management, the timing of the procedure, whether during pregnancy or in the postpartum period, remains a subject of ongoing debate [11, 12].

The timing of biopsy is frequently influenced by concerns regarding procedure related complications including bleeding, leading many clinicians and patients to defer the procedure until the postpartum period [13]. The primary purpose of a PKB in this setting is to guide specific management, particularly because many immunosuppressive medications pose fetal risks [14, 15]. In some situations, biopsy results may support continuing the pregnancy with a modified, pregnancy safe treatment plan; in others, they may indicate that pregnancy termination or early delivery is necessary to optimize maternal and fetal outcomes [16, 17]. This creates a central dilemma: whether to accept the risk of procedure related complications in order to obtain timely diagnostic information that could significantly influence clinical decisions, or to avoid the procedural risk and defer biopsy until after delivery. Another consideration is the severity of the underlying kidney disease, whether its progression poses an immediate threat to maternal health, fetal wellbeing, or long-term kidney survival, thereby necessitating timely tissue diagnosis [11]. Conversely, certain disease presentations may allow for safe postponement of biopsy until the postpartum period, based on clinical stability or patient and physician preference [18]. Practical considerations, including delayed access to care or late gestational presentation, may also factor in the decision to defer biopsy to the postpartum period [11].

Despite these complex decision pathways, evidence guiding optimal biopsy timing remains limited. To address this gap, we conducted a retrospective cohort study at our institution. The objective of this study was to compare long-term kidney survival among patients who underwent kidney biopsy during pregnancy with those who were biopsied postpartum. We hypothesized that delaying biopsy until after delivery may be associated with poor long-term kidney survival.

Materials and methods

Aims and objectives

The primary aim was to evaluate whether kidney biopsy during pregnancy or waiting postpartum impacted long-term kidney survival. Secondary aims included: (a) Examine the distribution of biopsy timing and procedure related complications across pregnancy trimesters and post-partum, (b) Identify histopathological patterns and kidney disease severity spectrum identified during pregnancy and postpartum, (c) Characterizing how biopsy findings influenced therapeutic interventions, e.g., immunosuppression, pregnancy termination, mode of delivery, etc. (d) Examine maternal and fetal outcomes, e.g., pregnancy outcomes, pre-eclampsia, delivery mode, gestational age at delivery, NICU admission, and birth weight, etc., (e) Investigate predictors or determinants of end stage kidney disease (ESKD) and dialysis needs in both groups.

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Froedtert Memorial Lutheran Hospital (FMLH), a tertiary care academic medical center. Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained from the Medical College of Wisconsin IRB prior to conducting the study (PRO 00046230). The study period spanned from January 1, 2002, to November 25, 2024. The trend of kidney biopsies performed during the study period is illustrated in Supplementary Figure S.A.

Study population

Eligible participants included all females aged 18 and older who underwent a native kidney biopsy (non-transplanted kidney) either during pregnancy or within three months postpartum. Participants were excluded if they underwent a kidney transplant biopsy (i.e., not a native kidney) or had a biopsy performed more than three months postpartum. However, we did scoping search and did not identify any remote post-partum biopsies beyond 3 months postpartum. Kidney biopsies were identified through the hospital’s electronic medical records system using procedural codes related to percutaneous kidney biopsy (CPT: 50200) and pregnancy (ICD10: Z34, Z33, O00-09, ICD9: V22.0, V22.1, V22.2, V23.0-V23.9) [19]. This was further verified through review of the procedure reports and the obstetric records. Please see Fig. 1 detailing the cohort creation flowsheet. A thorough chart review was conducted to confirm pregnancy status at the time of biopsy and to extract demographic and relevant clinical data including maternal age, race, medical comorbidities, indication for kidney biopsy, and kidney biopsy related complications such as bleeding, hematoma and, need for blood transfusions. We also looked in depth at kidney pathology results, management changes following biopsy results, End Stage Kidney Disease (ESKD) needing dialysis, comparison of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) at biopsy and most recent eGFR values. None in our cohort underwent pre-emptive kidney transplant.

Please note that that eGFR values were calculated using the CKD-EPI creatinine equation, as implemented in our institutional laboratory reporting system.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [20]. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on distribution. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons between biopsies performed during pregnancy vs. postpartum were conducted using Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Independent t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables, as appropriate. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was used to compare long term kidney survival (defined as time to dialysis) between the two groups (biopsy during pregnancy vs. biopsy within 3 months postpartum), with time zero being the day of kidney biopsy. The log rank test was used to assess statistical significance and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Determinants of dialysis requirement analysis

To examine clinical factors associated with dialysis requiring ESKD, we performed stratified univariate analyses comparing patients who underwent kidney biopsy during pregnancy versus the postpartum period. For each cohort, we evaluated baseline predictors available at the time of biopsy, including proteinuria severity (UPCR ≥ 3000 mg/g), serum creatinine (≥ 1.3 mg/dL), presence of hypertension or preeclampsia, and an eGFR categorized as ≤ 29, 30–59, and ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m². An eGFR of ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m² was used as the reference category for eGFR comparisons. For each binary predictor, odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals were computed using Fisher exact tests; confidence intervals were corrected using the Haldane-Anscombe method due to sparse events in this small cohort. To maintain interpretability and prevent distortion from extreme ORs, the odds ratio axis was capped at 0.1–80 in the forest plot visualization, with values beyond this range visually squished. Cohort sizes and number of dialysis events were included in facet labels for transparency. To further illustrate the kidney disease severity phenotype at biopsy, we generated a scatterplot of UPCR (log scale) versus e-GFR, stratified by biopsy timing (pregnancy vs. postpartum), with cohort indicated by color (pregnancy = pink; postpartum = purple) and dialysis outcome indicated by point shape. Dashed reference lines were added at UPCR = 3000 mg/g and eGFR = 60 mL/min/1.73 m² to show clinically meaningful thresholds. Management changes documented after biopsy were displayed as point outlines for clinical context but were not used as predictors, avoiding adjustment for post biopsy decision mediators. This analytic strategy was pre-specified as exploratory and hypothesis generating due to limited sample size.

Results

Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of the 29 females included in this study stratified by timing of kidney biopsy. Kidney biopsy was performed during pregnancy in 13 and in the postpartum period in 16 females. The mean age at biopsy was 30.4 ± 7 years in the pregnancy group and 27.7 ± 8 years in the postpartum group with p-value 0.3. Non-Hispanic White females (61.5%) were more likely to undergo biopsy during pregnancy, whereas Black or African American females (68.8%) were more frequently biopsied postpartum with p = 0.02. In this cohort, 53.8% of kidney biopsies performed during pregnancy occurred in the first trimester, with 23% each in the second and third trimester. The majority of biopsies in both the pregnancy and postpartum groups were performed by nephrologists (69% and 75% respectively), and rest by interventional radiology. Procedure related complications were small, self-limiting sub capsular hematomas observed in both groups, among 3 of 13 (23%) pregnant females (two of whom were in second trimester), and among 3 of 16 (18.8%) postpartum females.

Hypertension was more prevalent among females who underwent biopsy during pregnancy 69.2% as compared to postpartum 25% with p = 0.03. Proteinuria was the most common indication for biopsy across both groups, followed by acute kidney injury (AKI). There were no differences between both groups in terms of prothrombin time, international normalized ratio, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, blood urea nitrogen and serum creatinine. The postpartum group did have a higher UPCR and also a higher eGFR at biopsy 69 ml/min/1.73 m2 as compared to 59 ml/min/1.73m2 in the pregnancy group, please see supplementary Figure B and C. Preeclampsia was more prevalent in the postpartum group (50%) compared to the pregnancy group (30.8%). Neither group had cases of gestational diabetes mellitus or gestational hypertension. Preexisting kidney disease was present in 2 of 13 women in the postpartum group, while none in the pregnancy group had known kidney disease prior to biopsy. All biopsies performed during pregnancy led to a change in clinical management, compared to postpartum, where only 50% of kidney biopsy results lead to change in acute management with p = 0.003.

Lupus nephritis emerged as the most common histopathological diagnosis in both groups, identified in 30.8% of the pregnancy group and 43.8% of the postpartum group (please see, Fig. 2 and supplementary Tables 1 and 2). The second most frequent finding was focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), observed in 23% of pregnant and 18.8% of postpartum females. One postpartum female was diagnosed with acute interstitial nephritis (AIN) attributed to aspirin use, which resolved following discontinuation of the medication. IgA nephropathy was found in 12.5% of postpartum females and none of the pregnancy group. Additionally, one patient in the pregnancy group was found to have a renal mass, which was diagnosed as a low-grade spindle cell carcinoma, delivery was term and tumor was resected postpartum.

Maternal fetal outcomes

Three out of 13 pregnancies resulted in elective termination (please see Fig. 3) followed by immunosuppressive therapy, 2/13 resulted in iatrogenic preterm delivery and 5/13 females were started on high dose steroids during pregnancy, among whom 3 pregnancies resulted in term delivery. Mode of delivery differed between groups with spontaneous vaginal delivery (SVD) was more frequent among females biopsied during pregnancy (61.5%), whereas cesarean section was more common in the postpartum group (40%). Fetal outcomes were comparable in terms of NICU admission rates; however, the postpartum group exhibited lower birth weights, and lower gestational age at birth (please see Table 2 and supplementary Figures S.C and S.D).

Kidney outcomes

Progression to ESKD needing dialysis occurred in 5/13 (38.5%) females who had kidney biopsy in pregnancy and 5/16 (31.3%) females in postpartum, p-value 0.3. Figure 4 shows the Kaplan-Meier survival curves comparing the kidney survival between the two groups and there is no statistically significant difference in kidney survival probability (p = 0.3), with average follow up of about 14.5 years for pregnancy group and 9.7 years for post-partum group. Most recent eGFR (please see supplementary Figures D and E) between pregnancy and postpartum group differed widely, with severe kidney dysfunction (≤ 29 mL/min/1.73 m²) was present in 37.5% of postpartum groups as compared to 30% in pregnancy group.

Determinants of long-term dialysis dependence

Among the 29 females in our cohort, dialysis requiring ESKD occurred in 10 participants (5 in each group). Figure 5 shows stratified univariate analyses with higher proteinuria at biopsy (UPCR ≥ 3000 mg/g) was consistently associated with greater odds of subsequent dialysis in both pregnancy and postpartum groups, although confidence intervals were wide due to small sample size. Compared with individuals with preserved kidney function (eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m²), those with severe reduction in eGFR (≤ 29 mL/min/1.73 m²) demonstrated higher unadjusted odds of dialysis in both strata, and a similar direction of effect was observed for moderate eGFR impairment (30–59 mL/min/1.73 m²). Elevated serum creatinine (≥ 1.3 mg/dL) and the presence of hypertension or preeclampsia showed positive associations with dialysis risk, but sample related sparsity limited precision of effect estimates. Scatterplot visualization also demonstrated that dialysis events clustered among patients with simultaneously higher UPCR and lower eGFR.

Discussion

In this single center 22-year retrospective cohort of 29 females, while kidney survival and long term dialysis dependence did not differ between the groups (p = 0.3), however pregnancy group had a much longer follow up as compared with postpartum. In addition, safety of kidney biopsy was comparable in both groups with small, self-limiting hematomas. Lupus nephritis was the most frequent histopathological diagnosis in both groups. The higher rate of management changes following biopsies during pregnancy suggests that, in selected cases, the benefits of timely diagnosis may outweigh the procedural risks. Dialysis events in both the pregnancy and postpartum cohorts occurred among patients who concurrently exhibited markedly elevated UPCR and reduced eGFR. In other words, nephrotic range proteinuria in combination with lower eGFR served as a more reliable predictor of progression to dialysis requiring ESKD in both groups. These findings underscore that, despite inherent risks, timely biopsy during pregnancy can be pivotal for guiding urgent therapeutic decisions and optimizing maternal fetal outcomes, highlighting the need for individualized, multidisciplinary care and further research to refine biopsy timing and reduce reliance on invasive diagnostics. Also, this study highlights the complex interplay between clinical urgency, maternal fetal safety, and diagnostic necessity in the decision to perform a kidney biopsy during pregnancy. The observed racial imbalance between groups may point to potential influence timing of biopsy, and engagement with nephrology care. However, given small sample size, the social disparities are beyond the scope of this study.

Kidney biopsy timing and procedural risk considerations

The distribution of biopsies across all trimesters indicates that kidney disease manifestations can arise at any stage of pregnancy. While two of the three hematomas occurred in the second trimester, the small number of events precludes any statistically meaningful trimester-specific risk conclusions. This finding aligns with previous literature [6–11] and reinforces the need for heightened vigilance and multidisciplinary planning when considering kidney biopsy during this period [11]. Despite the higher procedure related complication rates reported during pregnancy in literature [7–9], our study did not find a statistically significant difference in procedure related complications between the two groups, which can be likely attributed to small sample size. However, this alternatively may reflect improved procedural techniques, skilled biopsy performers, and enhanced peri-procedural monitoring [21]. Over the past two decades, advances in real time imaging, refinement of needle gauge selection, and increased use of interventional radiology have modernized kidney biopsy technique, resulting in improved tissue adequacy and more standardized procedural approaches [21]. Emerging data demonstrate that these technical improvements, such as precision in sampling and optimized procedural imaging, are associated with lower complication rates, including reduced rate of sub capsular hematomas [21]. However comparing percutaneous kidney biopsy techniques and distribution of procedure related complications with different biopsy techniques or performers is beyond the scope of this study.

Role of kidney biopsy in changing clinical management

Kidney biopsy findings did impact clinical decision making, particularly when performed during pregnancy. Among all 13 females who had kidney biopsy performed during pregnancy, biopsy results prompted immediate changes in management, including initiation of high dose corticosteroids in five females, three of whom achieved term delivery, and two required iatrogenic preterm delivery to allow aggressive immunosuppression. In the pregnancy group, 3/13 pregnancies were electively terminated following biopsy confirmation of severe disease, enabling timely initiation of immunosuppressive therapy. In contrast, among postpartum biopsies, only half led to management changes, primarily involving initiation or adjustment of immunosuppressive regimens. These observations underscore that biopsy during pregnancy can alter the trajectory of care, sometimes prolonging pregnancy safely, other times expediting delivery or elective termination, while postpartum biopsy often informed long term kidney disease management.

Clinical severity and kidney outcomes

The higher prevalence of hypertension among those biopsied during pregnancy suggests that these individuals may have presented with more severe or rapidly progressing disease [22, 23]. This clinical severity likely contributed to the decision to proceed with biopsy during pregnancy despite the known procedure related risks [22, 23]. The universal impact of biopsy timing on altering management in this group further supports the rationale that these were high stakes clinical scenarios where empirical treatment alone may have been insufficient or inappropriate. Most recent eGFR data showed that postpartum group had a higher prevalence of severe kidney disease (≤ 29 mL/min/1.73 m²) 37.5% as opposed to 30% in pregnancy group, suggesting potentially an ongoing decline in kidney function during and following delivery. Additionally, only one of the patients in each group in our cohort demonstrated complete resolution of kidney disease, highlighting the chronic nature of these conditions and the need for ongoing surveillance [11]. Longer follow up in pregnancy group may point to different clinical trajectories and severity of disease between the groups. This difference complicates direct comparisons and reinforces that the lack of statistical difference in kidney survival should be interpreted cautiously in light of unequal follow-up duration.

Determinants of dialysis dependence

The observed pattern in this cohort, where dialysis requiring ESKD occurred predominantly among females presenting with both severe proteinuria and reduced eGFR, aligns closely with prior studies in glomerular disorders, demonstrating that high grade proteinuria and impaired kidney function at the time of biopsy are the strongest predictors of progressive kidney disease [24–26]. Although limited by sample size, the stratified associations suggest that these risk relationships are consistent whether biopsy occurs during pregnancy or postpartum, supporting the notion that underlying kidney disease severity, rather than biopsy timing alone, might drive adverse kidney outcomes. This finding parallel prior reports in lupus nephritis, and other glomerulopathies where high UPCR and lower eGFR identified patients at highest risk for ESKD despite treatment [24, 27]. The clustering of dialysis events (please see Fig. 7) among those with UPCR ≥ 3000 mg/g and eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² underscores the potential clinical utility of integrating these measures to identify patients requiring closer monitoring or expedited intervention, particularly in the context of pregnancy where timely decision making regarding biopsy and immunosuppression is critical. This also supports that nephrotic range proteinuria and impaired kidney function at the time of biopsy are the strongest predictors of progressive kidney disease in pregnancy despite treatment. The observed association between more severe baseline kidney parameters (higher UPCR and lower eGFR) and subsequent management changes suggests that clinicians appropriately escalated care in response to disease severity in pregnancy group, which is consistent with existing guidelines recommending early therapeutic intervention in high risk presentations [28, 29].

Strengths, limitations and future directions

Our study’s strengths lie in its comprehensive 22-year retrospective cohort analysis, which provides significant insights into the diagnostic utility and safety of kidney biopsies performed during pregnancy and postpartum. Our study is unique because it offers detailed clinical characterization, including eGFR, proteinuria, detailed maternal fetal outcomes, and long-term kidney outcomes. Our findings, including the high rate of management changes following antenatal biopsies and the comparable complication rates between the two groups, underscore the clinical relevance and safety of the procedure. Our study evaluates not only the need for long term dialysis, but it is also the first study to evaluate predictors of dialysis dependence by kidney disease severity phenotype in the context of the management changes informed by biopsy findings. Our study is inherently limited by its single center design, small sample size, and sparsity of events, which restricts precision of effect estimates and precludes fully adjusted multivariable modeling. The inclusion criteria of 3-month postpartum window represents a stringent definition and we note that broader windows (e.g., 6–12 months) could be explored in future or sensitivity analyses in larger datasets. Although this study provides a 22 year follow up, it is possible that further deterioration in kidney function occurred beyond the observation period, which may underestimate long term risk. As reflected by wide confidence intervals and the need for Haldane-Anscombe corrections, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis generating rather than confirmatory. Because UPCR and eGFR reflect disease severity and also influence clinicians’ decisions to perform biopsy and escalate treatment, there is a meaningful possibility of confounding by indication; patients with more severe disease are more likely to receive urgent interventions that may modify subsequent outcomes. Although we addressed this by restricting predictors to pre-biopsy characteristics and treating management change descriptively rather than as a covariate, residual confounding remains likely.

Given the complexity and risks of procedure related complications of kidney biopsy during pregnancy, shared decision making is essential and coordinated care among nephrology, maternal fetal medicine, and procedure team, are critical to optimize outcomes [11]. Counseling should address potential benefits of timely diagnosis, procedural risks, and the limited evidence guiding management of proteinuria and kidney disease in this population [11]. Larger, multicenter studies with standardized timing and longitudinal follow-up are needed to validate these observations and to refine patient selection criteria. The development and validation of non-invasive biomarkers to diagnose kidney dysfunction in pregnancy could reduce the need for invasive procedures and improve diagnostic accuracy [30]. We advocate for the inclusion of pregnant individuals in studies evaluating novel serum and urine markers, which may offer safer alternatives to kidney biopsy in the future. Additionally, prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate our findings and provide more definitive guidance on biopsy timing.

Conclusion

In this two-decade single center cohort, kidney biopsy performed during pregnancy demonstrated a favorable procedural safety profile and delivered clinically actionable information that frequently altered clinical management. Although long term kidney survival did not differ between pregnancy and postpartum biopsy groups, the higher rate of severe kidney disease at follow up in those biopsied postpartum suggests that diagnostic delay may have implications for kidney disease trajectory. These findings highlight the critical value of timely biopsy in pregnant individuals, particularly when rapid diagnostic clarification can guide urgent therapeutic decisions, prevent irreversible and ongoing kidney damage, and optimize maternal fetal outcomes.
