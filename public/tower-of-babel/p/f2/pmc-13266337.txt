Computationally Derived Spatial Immune Signature Identifies Trastuzumab Responders in HER2+ Breast Cancer: NSABP B-41 Clinical Trial Validation
Authors: Satvika Bharadwaj, Germán Corredor, Hilmi Al-Shakhshir, Sebastian Medina, Sahar N. Almahfouz, Rohan Dhamdhere, Tilak Pathak, Pingfu Fu, Yuan Liu, Shipra Gandhi, Sunil Badve, Anant Madabhushi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose:Trastuzumab-based chemotherapy has improved outcomes in human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)–positive breast cancer, but treatment benefit varies among patients. Predictive signatures are needed to identify patients most likely to respond to these therapies.Experimental Design:We developed Density and Spatial architecture of Tumor-Infiltrating Lymphocytes (DeSTIL), a computational signature derived from hematoxylin and eosin slides. The signature captures spatial organization of immune cells and interactions with nonimmune cells. DeSTIL was trained on HER2+ breast cancer slides from The Cancer Genome Atlas (n = 250) and validated in a phase III National Surgical Adjuvant Breast and Bowel Project (NSABP) B-41 randomized clinical trial (n = 221), which compared chemotherapy plus trastuzumab, lapatinib, or combination. The DeSTIL scores were dichotomized into positive and negative groups, and event-free survival (EFS) was assessed using Cox proportional hazards with interaction terms.Results:In NSABP B-41, DeSTIL-positive patients (n = 61) showed significantly improved event-free survival (EFS) with trastuzumab compared with the combination arm [hazard ratio (HR) = 0.09; 95% confidence interval (CI) = 0.01–0.77; P = 0.006] and a significant signature–treatment interaction (P = 0.024). No EFS difference was observed in DeSTIL-negative patients (n = 160). Gene expression analysis supported the image-derived signature stratifying DeSTIL-positive and DeSTIL-negative tumors. In an exploratory pathologic complete response analysis, a classifier trained on University Hospitals Cleveland slides achieved AUCs of 0.70 in the training cohort and 0.63 in the trastuzumab arm of the NSABP B-41 validation cohort.Conclusions:DeSTIL identifies a subset of HER2+ patients who derive greater benefit from trastuzumab. These findings support the potential of computationally derived immune architecture to inform selection of standard HER2-targeted therapies.

Translational Relevance

Although trastuzumab-based chemotherapy is the standard of care for human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)–positive breast cancer, treatment benefits vary among patients. This study introduces Density and Spatial architecture of Tumor-Infiltrating Lymphocytes (DeSTIL), a computational pathology-based signature that quantifies spatial cellular architecture to identify patients likely to benefit from trastuzumab using hematoxylin and eosin slides. As DeSTIL is derived from routinely acquired diagnostic biopsies with standard staining, it requires no specialized assays and can complement existing clinical workflows. As HER2 categories evolve and treatment regimens expand, explainable computational pathology signatures such as DeSTIL may help refine patient selection and support treatment decisions involving HER2-targeted therapies.

Introduction

Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)–positive breast cancers account for 15% to 20% of new breast cancer diagnoses (1). Before the development of HER2-targeted therapies, these tumors carried a high risk of recurrence and poor survival (2). The development of trastuzumab, pertuzumab, and other HER2-targeting therapies transformed therapy for early HER2+ breast cancer, by improving the 5-year disease-free survival from 75% to approximately 84% (3). As a result, trastuzumab-based chemotherapy has become the established standard of care for stage I to III HER2+ breast cancers (4, 5). Despite these advances, about 11% of patients with early-stage HER2+ breast cancer treated with trastuzumab will experience a recurrence within 5 years (4). Trastuzumab resistance is due to intrinsic changes in the tumor and differences in the host immune response (6). The therapeutic effect of trastuzumab, when administered in combination with chemotherapy, depends in part on antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) mediated by immune cells. Consequently, higher levels of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) are associated with better outcomes in these patients (7, 8). Schlam and colleagues (9) observed that the tumor and its microenvironment further shape sensitivity to therapy. Developing pathology-based signatures that integrate features of both the tumor and its immune microenvironment may help improve therapy selection and outcomes in HER2+ breast cancer (10).

Current evaluation of HER2 status relies on immunohistochemistry (IHC) and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH/ISH). However, these methods remain limited by subjective interpretation, evolving criteria, and variability across laboratories (11, 12). Retrospective analyses from adjuvant trastuzumab trials, including National Surgical Adjuvant Breast and Bowel Project (NSABP) B-31 and NCCTG N9831 (13), found that some patients labeled HER2+ by local laboratories but HER2− on central review still experienced potential benefit from trastuzumab (14). Therefore, the evolving and subjective criteria used in current HER2 testing may fail to identify some patients who may benefit from HER2-targeted therapy and indicate a need for additional methods to guide treatment selection. Moreover, HER2 expression itself is increasingly recognized as a continuum. Clinical trials such as DESTINY-Breast04 (15) and DESTINY-Breast06 trials (16) extend the use of HER2-directed therapies into the HER2-low category (17). Findings from the DAISY trial (18) further suggest that HER2 expression alone may not serve as a biologically meaningful predictor of response. This gap points to the need for quantitative, reproducible, and biologically relevant signatures to better stratify patients for HER2-targeted therapy.

In parallel to HER2 expression, TILs have emerged as an important prognostic and predictive signature for pathologic complete response (pCR) and treatment outcomes in HER2+ breast cancer (9, 19). Although pCR is an established signature for evaluating short-term treatment response, survival outcomes like event-free survival (EFS), breast cancer–specific survival, and overall survival (OS) are needed to assess the long-term impact of treatment beyond the initial response (20). High levels of stromal TILs, assessed from routine hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)–stained images, have consistently correlated with improved prognosis and higher rates of pCR (19). Despite this evidence, clinical adoption of TIL assessment remains limited because of the subjective, labor-intensive nature of manual scoring and the absence of standardized clinical thresholds. The International Immuno-Oncology Biomarker Working Group has recommended reporting stromal TILs as a continuous parameter and suggested a 60% cutoff indicative of high lymphocytic infiltration in HER2+ tumors (21). However, translation to clinical practice is hindered by the lack of prospective clinical validation (21). An objective, reproducible, and automated computational framework that quantifies TILs and captures complex spatial immune cell arrangements not perceptible to the human eye could potentially improve prediction of trastuzumab responsiveness and aid in guiding individualized treatment decisions.

In this study, we introduce Density and Spatial architecture of TILs (DeSTIL), a computational pathology-based signature that quantifies immune architecture from routine H&E-stained histology slides. DeSTIL was evaluated in the NSABP B-41 (22) phase III randomized HER2+ breast cancer clinical trial (Fig. 1). The primary objective was to determine whether DeSTIL identifies subgroups of patients with differential survival benefit from trastuzumab. We further assessed whether DeSTIL captures underlying immune context by integrating immune-related gene expression data. As an exploratory objective, we examined whether DeSTIL-derived features were associated with pCR.

Materials and Methods

Dataset

This study used a training set of 250 patients from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA; ref. 23) with HER2+ or HER2-expressing breast cancer as determined by IHC and FISH with a median follow-up of 2.4 years. The training dataset comprised of varied specimen types to capture sampling heterogeneity, consisting primarily of core biopsies and tumor resections. The primary endpoint in TCGA cohort was disease-specific survival (DSS), defined as the time from diagnosis to death due to breast cancer. As pCR data were not available in TCGA, we incorporated a separate institutional cohort from University Hospitals (UH) Cleveland Medical Center (n = 30), treated with docetaxel, carboplatin, and trastuzumab (TCH), approximating the trastuzumab-containing arm of NSABP B-41. Pretreatment biopsies in this cohort were reliably annotated for pCR, but recurrence data were incomplete and events were few (n = 9); accordingly, this dataset was used exclusively for pCR analysis. Response categories comprised of complete response (CR), partial response (PR), and no response. Cases with PR or no response were grouped as non-pCR, whereas CRs were classified as pCR. All tumors were HER2+, with the majority presenting as early-stage disease (T2 in 43.4% of cases).

Model validation was performed using whole-slide images (WSI) from the phase III NSABP B-41 clinical trial with a median follow-up of 5.1 years. The NSABP B-41 clinical trial evaluated the efficacy of neoadjuvant paclitaxel in combination with trastuzumab, lapatinib, or both in patients with operable HER2+ breast cancer. The treatment regimen consisted of an initial course of doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide followed by paclitaxel with randomized HER2-targeted therapy. Eligible patients had histologically confirmed disease and were stratified by hormone receptor status. EFS was defined as the time from randomization to the first occurrence of local, regional, or distant recurrence; contralateral breast cancer; second primary cancer; or death from any cause. For model training, DSS (TCGA) was considered an equivalent breast cancer–relevant endpoint, consistent with NeoSTEEP guidance (24) for neoadjuvant trials. For the exploratory analysis, pCR status was used as the endpoint in the NSABP B-41 cohort. A total of 221 digitized WSIs and paired clinical data were available for analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1). All studies involving human subjects were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The NSABP B-41 clinical trial and institutional cohort from UH Cleveland Medical Center were approved by their respective institutional review boards, and informed written consent was obtained from all participants in the original studies.

WSI quality control and image processing

WSIs were digitized at 40× magnification (0.25 μm/pixel). Quality control was performed using HistoQC (25), a software tool to assess image integrity and exclude artifacts. Subsequently, nonoverlapping 2,000 × 2,000 pixel H&E patches were extracted, and then nuclei were segmented using a deep learning model (26). Macenko stain normalization was applied to the H&E patches prior to lymphocyte detection to reduce batch effects due to intersite staining variability (27). A pretrained support vector machine was subsequently used to identify lymphocytes based on the texture, shape, and color of the nuclei (28).

Feature extraction

Spatial features were extracted to characterize the organization, density, and colocalization of TILs and non-TILs (nuclei not identified as TILs, primarily cancer cells, as well as macrophages, fibroblasts, and neutrophils, which were not further subclassified and were treated as a single group) within the tumor microenvironment (29). Subgraphs of TILs and non-TILs were built based on spatial proximity, and for each subgraph a cluster was built as the convex hulls containing all the subgraph cells, following previous published work, validated across cancer types (29–31). A total of 369 spatial features were extracted from these clusters, capturing density, architecture, and colocalization (29). Features were computed per patch and averaged to obtain a single feature vector for each patient.

Development of the DeSTIL predictive signature

Features extracted in Section 2.3 from TCGA cohort were selected to construct the DeSTIL predictive signature. The locked model was used to generate risk predictions in the independent NSABP B-41 cohort and to perform subgroup analyses.

Feature selection and DeSTIL model construction

Feature selection was performed using a bootstrapped modeling framework applied to the training cohort (TCGA; ref. 32). In each of the 50 bootstrap iterations, the cohort was partitioned into training (70%) and validation (30%) subsets. A Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO)–regularized Cox proportional hazard regression model (33) was trained (hyperparameters listed in Supplementary Methods) in each iteration to identify the most predictive features associated with patient outcomes. Features were subsequently ranked to select robust features; robustness was defined by a score reflecting both the frequency of feature selection and the aggregate absolute coefficient magnitudes across 50 iterations (32). Following this ranking, 33 top-ranked features emerged as robust predictors. To ensure feature independence and reduce redundancy, low-variance (σ2 < 0.05) and highly correlated (r > 0.95) features were further removed, resulting in a refined set of 17 distinct features (Supplementary Table S1). These final features were used to train a Cox proportional hazards model to obtain the feature coefficients to form the DeSTIL signature (Supplementary Table S2). Risk scores were computed as a weighted combination of the 17 features using the derived coefficients, and the median risk score was used as the threshold to define DeSTIL-positive and DeSTIL-negative subsets (29).

Immune-related gene expression associations with the DeSTIL signature

Gene expression profiling was performed centrally as part of the NSABP B-41 clinical trial from macrodissected formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor tissue (Supplementary Methods). Normalized gene expression data were obtained using the NanoString nCounter Breast Cancer 360 (BC360; Bruker Spatial Biology) panel, which profiled 776 genes, including 758 breast cancer–related genes and 18 housekeeping genes used for normalization. Raw counts were processed using NanoString’s standard pipeline (34), first with technical normalization via positive control probes followed by biological normalization using the geometric mean of the housekeeping genes. Normalized expression values were log2-transformed prior to analysis. Immune-related gene signature scores inferred from the BC360 panel, including CD8 T cells, cytotoxic cells, interferon (IFN) γ, PD-1, and B7-H3, were compared between DeSTIL-positive and DeSTIL-negative tumors using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test across all patients. To evaluate pathway-level differences, differential gene expression analysis was performed between DeSTIL groups across trastuzumab and combination arms. Genes were ranked by a sign (log fold change) × −log10 (P value). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using GSEAPreranked against a custom set of immune cell–tumor interaction pathways curated from MSigDB (35). Enrichment was considered significant at a false discovery rate (FDR) q value < 0.05.

Statistical analysis of the DeSTIL predictive signature

EFS was evaluated in the NSABP B-41 cohort to assess predictive validity across DeSTIL-negative and DeSTIL-positive groups. Survival distributions were estimated by Kaplan–Meier analyses and compared using two-sided log-rank tests (α = 0.05). Treatment effects were quantified with Cox proportional hazards models to obtain hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI), and differences in 5-year EFS with 95% CIs were also calculated. The predictive ability of a signature was defined as a differential treatment effect across signature subgroups with a statistically significant treatment–signature interaction term. To test this, a Cox model with a treatment × DeSTIL interaction term was fit, and subgroup HRs and the interaction P value for each subset analyses are reported. To determine whether the signature modified treatment benefit after adjusting for clinical variables, we fit a multivariable Cox model that included treatment arm (trastuzumab and combination therapy arms), DeSTIL status (DeSTIL-negative and DeSTIL-positive), age (binarized at the median age of 48 years), menopausal status (premenopausal or postmenopausal), nodal status, tumor stage [tumor staging was determined according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer eighth edition tumor–node–metastasis definitions (36) as outlined in the NCCN Breast Cancer Guidelines; ref. 37], and a treatment–DeSTIL interaction term. A statistically significant interaction term was interpreted as evidence of differential treatment effect based on immune spatial architecture and density. All analyses were performed using R version 4.2 with the survival and survminer packages. Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Exploratory analysis of pCR

pCR was evaluated in an exploratory analysis to characterize short-term response in the neoadjuvant setting. pCR was defined as the absence of any invasive component in the resected breast specimen and absence of cancer in all resected lymph nodes after neoadjuvant therapy (38). Analyses used pretreatment core biopsies to reflect baseline tumor biology.

Feature selection and pCR classifier construction

The pCR classifier was constructed using the UH training cohort, which included patients treated with the TCH regimen. Feature extraction and preprocessing followed the workflow detailed in Sections 2.2 to 2.3, and the same panel of 369 image-derived features was considered. Training data were split at the patient level into training and internal test sets at a 70:30 ratio. Features with near-zero variance (σ2 < 0.05) were excluded. Mutual information was computed between each feature and pCR status in the training set, and the number of selected features was capped at approximately 10% of the available training samples (n = 30), resulting in the selection of the top three features. Three classification algorithms were evaluated: support vector machine, logistic regression, and random forest. The classifier that achieved the highest area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) on the held-out 30% internal test set was designated as the locked down classifier.

Statistical analysis of the pCR classifier

The locked pCR classifier was evaluated in the independent NSABP B-41 cohort. As the NSABP B-41 trial did not include a TCH treatment arm, the trastuzumab plus chemotherapy arm was analyzed as the closest comparator to the UH training cohort. However, model performance was also evaluated in the lapatinib-containing arms of B-41 to examine discrimination under tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI)–based therapy and dual HER2 blockade. No additional feature selection or model tuning was performed during external evaluation.

Research Resource Identifiers

WSIs were quality checked using HistoQC (RRID: SCR_025780).

GSEA was performed using GenePattern GSEAPreranked (v6.0.5; RRID: SCR_003201).

Statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.2; RRID: SCR_001905) with the survival (RRID: SCR_021137) and survminer (RRID: SCR_021094) packages.

Results

Clinicopathologic features of the patient cohorts

The clinical and pathologic characteristics of patients from TCGA, NSABP B-41, and UH cohorts are summarized in Table 1. The NSABP B-41 cohort included patients with a median age of 48 years, primarily T2 stage (91.4%), and hormone receptor–positive tumors (65.2%; Supplementary Fig. S1). Similarly, the majority of tumors in TCGA cohort were T2 stage (61.6%), with a median patient age of 61 years. The median follow-up was 2.4 and 5.1 years for TCGA and NSABP B-41, respectively, with 83.1% achieving a 5-year EFS and 89.3% achieving a 5-year DSS, respectively. The UH cohort included 30 patients treated with TCH, with a median age of 50.5 years. Most tumors were T2 stage; 53.3% (16 patients) achieved pCR, whereas 46.7% (14 patients) showed no response.

Trastuzumab benefit prediction over lapatinib-containing therapies

The study design and analytic workflow are summarized in Fig. 1. Among 221 HER2+ patients from the NSABP B-41 clinical trial, 61 (28%) patients were classified as signature-positive (DeSTIL-positive) and 160 (72%) as signature-negative (DeSTIL-negative). In DeSTIL-positive patients, trastuzumab alone was associated with significantly improved EFS compared with the combination therapy (HR = 0.09; 95% CI = 0.01–0.77; P = 0.006; Fig. 2A). Trastuzumab showed an absolute EFS net benefit at 35% (95% CI = 8.8–61.1) over the combination therapy (Supplementary Fig. S2). Within the DeSTIL-negative group, no significant difference in EFS was observed when comparing trastuzumab with combination therapy (HR = 1.33; 95% CI = 0.47–3.75; P = 0.585; Fig. 2B), and the 5-year net benefit was −4.1% (95% CI = −17.9% to 9.8%), with a statistically significant signature–treatment interaction term (P = 0.024; Supplementary Fig. S2). Multivariable analysis was performed to assess whether DeSTIL-modified treatment effect after adjusting for clinical variables also yielded a statistically significant interaction (HR = 0.07; 95% CI = 0.01–0.77; P = 0.030; Supplementary Fig. S4), consistent with the univariable analysis (P = 0.024; Supplementary Fig. S2). DeSTIL status (positive vs. negative) alone was not significantly associated with EFS (HR = 3.17; 95% CI = 0.97–10.34; P = 0.056), consistent with a predictive rather than prognostic signature role.

Genomic pathways associate DeSTIL status and immune response

Gene signatures for CD8 T cells, cytotoxic cells, IFNγ, and PD-1 were significantly higher in DeSTIL-negative tumors compared with DeSTIL-positive tumors across the trastuzumab and combination therapy arms (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P < 0.05), whereas the immunosuppressive checkpoint molecule B7-H3 was elevated in DeSTIL-positive tumors (Fig. 4A). GSEA of differential expression data identified an enrichment of IFN signaling, humoral immune response, cytokine–receptor interaction, and inflammatory pathways in DeSTIL-negative tumors, whereas integrin-mediated adhesion pathways were enriched in DeSTIL-positive tumors (Fig. 4B). Supplementary Figures S9–S11 show transcript-level and pathway-level differences between DeSTIL-negative and DeSTIL-positive tumors, including genome-wide differential expression, clustering of significant genes, and hallmark GSEA.

Exploratory analysis of pCR prediction using spatial TIL features

Multiple classifiers were trained on the UH cohort to predict pCR, including logistic regression, random forest, and support vector machine models. Among these, the support vector machine achieved the highest discrimination (AUC = 0.70) compared with AUCs of 0.45 for logistic regression and 0.57 for the random forest (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Table S4). The UH training cohort consisted of patients treated with the TCH regimen; therefore, performance of the locked classifier was evaluated in the external NSABP B-41 cohort by identifying the trastuzumab plus chemotherapy arm as the closest clinical comparator and by assessing discrimination separately across treatment arms. ROC analyses yielded AUCs of 0.63 for the trastuzumab arm, 0.51 for the lapatinib arm, and 0.49 for the trastuzumab plus lapatinib arm (Fig. 5B). Feature selection within the training cohort identified three spatial immune features contributing to the final classifier (Fig. 5C).

Discussion

Since its approval in 2006, trastuzumab combined with chemotherapy has been the standard of care for HER2+ early breast cancers (39). However, its benefit is not uniform, with a subset of patients experiencing relapse despite receiving targeted therapy (4). Resistance to trastuzumab has been attributed to both tumor-intrinsic alterations and heterogeneity in the immune microenvironment (6, 9). Quantification of this heterogeneity remains subjective and lacks standardized methodology (21). There is a need for signatures that can identify patients who benefit from trastuzumab in early-stage HER2+ breast cancer. As therapeutic strategies evolve, such frameworks are essential for providing objective measures that support individualized response assessment (40, 41).

In this study, we introduced DeSTIL, an automated, image-based predictive signature derived from routine H&E histopathology that captures the immune microenvironment not reflected in HER2 status alone (Fig. 1). Rather than comparing therapies, our objective was to define a quantitative framework linking tumor immune architecture to treatment response. In the NSABP B-41 cohort, the DeSTIL-positive patients demonstrated higher EFS with trastuzumab (Fig. 2), with an absolute 5-year net benefit exceeding 35% relative to combination and 28% relative to lapatinib-containing regimens. In contrast, DeSTIL-negative cases showed no meaningful differences across treatment arms (HR ≈1; Supplementary Figs. S1–S3). These results do not imply the universal superiority of trastuzumab but instead reveal that the DeSTIL-defined group identifies a subset of patients with improved EFS from a particular therapy arm. Trastuzumab seems more effective in tumors with distinct immune spatial architecture captured by DeSTIL, which reflects not only immune cell presence but also their spatial arrangement, and may be relevant to efficient ADCC (42). Through its Fc region, trastuzumab binds Fcγ receptors on immune effector cells, triggering cytotoxic activity and tumor cell apoptosis (42). This immune-mediated mechanism complements HER2 blockade and represents a critical component of trastuzumab’s antitumor effect (42). In contrast, lapatinib, a small-molecule TKI of HER2 and EGFR, lacks an Fc domain and therefore cannot induce ADCC, acting solely through intracellular kinase inhibition (43). The absence of additional benefit with combined lapatinib and trastuzumab in the DeSTIL-positive group may reflect interference with ADCC, potentially through lapatinib-induced alterations in HER2 receptor conformation or reduced surface expression that limit trastuzumab binding and immune engagement (44). Furthermore, the signature–treatment interaction was statistically significant in both univariable (P = 0.024; Supplementary Fig. S2) and multivariable (P = 0.030; Supplementary Fig. S4) models that indicate the discriminatory power of DeSTIL status on treatment effect. A similar trend was observed in which DeSTIL-positive patients exhibited improved EFS with the trastuzumab arm relative to the lapatinib arm (Supplementary Fig. S5). Interestingly, the ALTTO (20) trial with 6,281 patients who received chemotherapy with trastuzumab, lapatinib, or a combination showed no significant survival benefit and increased toxicity with the addition of lapatinib (45). In our study, we found that the immune spatial architecture captured by DeSTIL delineates a subset of patients who experience improved EFS from single-agent trastuzumab.

Beyond predictive performance, DeSTIL also offers interpretable biological correlates (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Across the 17 features comprising the DeSTIL signature, several spatial trends are observed. DeSTIL-negative tumors exhibit larger TIL cluster areas, increased TIL graph density, and greater convex hull coverage by TILs. Furthermore, non-TIL clusters in DeSTIL-negative tumors exhibit lower density and reduced grouping. Features quantifying cluster interactions also show increased intersected areas between TIL and non-TIL clusters and greater variability in the proportion of TIL clusters surrounding non-TIL clusters compared with DeSTIL-positive tumors. These observations suggest that DeSTIL-negative tumors exhibit a spatial trend toward larger areas of TIL infiltration and heterogeneous spatial interactions between TIL and non-TIL compartments compared with DeSTIL-positive tumors. To illustrate these trends, two representative features were visualized using image patches (Fig. 3), with quantitative values shown in Supplementary Fig. S6 (no evident cohort-level separation was observed in the parent image–derived feature space; Supplementary Fig. S7). Transcriptomic profiling showed similar immune signaling patterns with significantly elevated CD8+ T-cell, cytotoxic cell, IFNγ, and PD-1 signatures in DeSTIL-negative tumors (Fig. 4A) and enrichment of IFN signaling, cytokine–receptor interaction, and broader inflammatory pathways (Fig. 4B). In contrast, DeSTIL-positive tumors showed elevated expression of genes involved in immune suppression, cellular proliferative signaling, and matrix remodeling (Supplementary Figs. S9–S11). Correlation of image-derived features with these transcriptomic profiles indicates that the spatial organization of immune cells captured by DeSTIL reflects underlying molecular features of immune activity. Decision curve analysis showed modest gains in net benefit (0.01–0.03) when DeSTIL was added to clinicopathologic models (Supplementary Fig. S8). In addition, workflow timeline and infrastructure considerations for DeSTIL deployment are summarized in Supplementary Table S3.

The NSABP B-41 trial reported comparable pCR rates between trastuzumab and lapatinib, with no statistically significant improvement for dual HER2 blockade. In this context, we explored short-term treatment response through a classifier trained to predict pCR. For this exploratory analysis, the classifier was locked after training on the UH cohort to match the external test arms of NSABP B-41 as closely as possible, consistent with recommended validation practices in pCR modeling (46). The classifier achieved an AUC of 0.63 on the trastuzumab arm of the NSABP B-41 trial (Fig. 5) compared with an internal validation AUC of 0.70 on the UH cohort, using three features describing TIL and non-TIL cluster spatial overlap. In comparison, the NSABP B-41 clinical trial reported that a logistic regression model incorporating 20 genomic markers achieved an AUC of 0.73 for predicting pCR using 10-fold cross-validation (38). Importantly, our image-based pCR classifier is derived from routine histopathology and was evaluated in an independent randomized clinical trial cohort. More broadly, these findings (Supplementary Table S4) should be considered in the context of how pCR relates to long-term outcomes. Although pooled analyses (47, 48) such as CTNeoBC (49) demonstrate that achieving pCR is associated with improved outcomes at the individual patient level, increases in pCR rates at the trial level do not consistently translate into proportional improvements in EFS or OS. Given this modest trial-level association between the two endpoints, the original immune feature set which is strongly associated with survival may only be weakly correlated with pCR, suggesting that alternative morphologic descriptors of the tumor microenvironment (e.g., stromal TILs and collagen architecture on H&E; ref. 50) could be more appropriate for pCR-focused modeling. Comparable performance is seen with HER2DX (46), a transcriptomic assay which integrates immune and tumor-intrinsic features to categorize patients into low and high risk along with predicting pCR. HER2DX reports AUCs of 0.81 internally and 0.77 externally in the trastuzumab-based chemotherapy cohort (46). However, this assay requires gene expression profiling from tumor tissue, which may limit accessibility and adds cost compared with digital image–based signatures.

We acknowledge that this study has several limitations. First, the validation cohort comprised a limited number of patients (n = 221), with fewer events in each treatment arm, which reduced power to assess treatment effects. However, DeSTIL identified a signature-defined subset of patients who did well with single-agent trastuzumab alone as shown by treatment interaction analyses. Second, the exploratory pCR analysis conducted on a smaller subset (n = 30) treated with TCH demonstrated modest discriminatory performance (AUC = 0.63) in an independent validation cohort and will require confirmation in a larger, well-powered study to more robustly assess short-term treatment response using additional morphologic descriptors of the tumor microenvironment. Third, the comparison between trastuzumab and lapatinib arms did not reach statistical significance in DeSTIL-positive patients. However, the trend of improved survival was consistent among those treated with trastuzumab. Lastly, WSIs and complete clinical data were unavailable in all the cohorts, which could introduce selection bias. For example, TCGA includes both biopsy and resection specimens, whereas validation was performed using biopsies from the NSABP B-41 cohort. A larger prospective study, with paired diagnostic biopsies and resection specimens from patients treated with trastuzumab or dual HER2-targeted therapy with pertuzumab, would help assess the predictive ability of the DeSTIL signature in the context of current HER2-targeted therapies that engage immune effector mechanisms. Future work will include independent cohort validation using randomized clinical trials and institutional datasets. This will be followed by extension of the framework based on automated spatial TIL feature extraction to the evolving spectrum of HER2 disease, including HER2-low tumors treated with antibody–drug conjugates. If transcriptomic-derived scores such as HER2DX become available for overlapping cohorts, direct performance comparison would be valuable for understanding how spatial immune features complement established molecular predictors, thereby allowing more individualized therapy while reducing unnecessary toxicities.

In conclusion, DeSTIL represents an immune density and spatial pathomic signature with predictive utility, capable of identifying patients with improved EFS and providing a blueprint for assisting tailored treatment decisions in HER2-positive breast cancer management.
