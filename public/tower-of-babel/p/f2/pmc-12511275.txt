Phase transition driven tough hydrogel ionic thermoelectric cell with giant thermopower
Authors: Xiaofang Shi, Yingjie Li, Nan Shi, Chengyu Ji, Lei Hou, Yingkun Shi, Jianyao Xu, Yarong Lan, Qingcong Wei, Guanglei Ma, Peiyi Wu, Zhiguo Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The application of quasi-solid ionic thermoelectric (i-TE) cells holds great potential for powering ubiquitous wearable electronics without the need for cables or batteries. However, their practical application is restricted by low thermopower. Herein, a temperature-responsive supramolecular hydrogel, P(N-acryloylsemicarbazide-co-acrylic acid) (PNA), has been developed as a i-TE cell that integrates good mechanical and electrochemical properties. The volume phase transition (VPT) of PNA i-TE cell can generate a substantial ion entropy difference, thereby enhancing both the redox reaction efficiency and ionic thermodiffusion rate. A single PNA i-TE cell can generate a thermopower of 2.04 volts with a temperature difference of 50 K. Introduction

The rapid development of wearable systems has raised urgent demand for sustainable energy sources1. Based on the challenge of “dual carbon” goal, the energy supply is facing the onerous task of carbon emission reduction. Thermoelectric conversion of low-grade heat from abundant thermal sources of the environment, such as body heat, solar heat, and industrial waste heat, is crucial to meet the challenge of “carbon-free” society2,3. The traditional thermoelectric materials based on semiconductors are weak in poor thermopower, fragility, and complicated manufacturing process4,5. Eminently, the burgeoning quasi-solid i-TE materials have attracted increasing attention due to their intrinsic adaptable mechanical deformation with movement, mimicking the ionic conduction of biomimetic tissues, and adjustable optical properties6–11. Over the past decade, scholars have conducted systematic research on quasi-solid hydrogel i-TE materials. These cutting-edge research efforts have stimulated the rapid development of i-TE materials in theory and technology12–22. However, it is usually necessary to connect a voltage amplifier or cascade it for use, leading to complicated fabrication and high costs23–25. It is still challenging in improving thermopower of i-TE cells to meet the threshold requirement for practical applications.

The Se, an important criterion for assessing thermoelectric performance of quasi-solid hydrogel i-TE materials, is contributed by the ionic thermodiffusion effect and thermogalvanic effect of redox reaction24. The low thermopower of hydrogel i-TE materials can primarily be attributed to the following factors: On one hand, an adequate concentration of thermoelectric ions is a key factor to ensure efficient thermoelectric conversion of i-TE cell materials. However, a significant challenge lies in the introduction of adequate thermoelectric ions into the hydrogel matrix. Because most hydrogel materials will swell when soaked in thermoelectric ions, leading to poor mechanical properties and ion loss26. Furthermore, the incorporation of thermoelectric ions into the hydrogel network is significantly restricted owing to their weak interaction with the hydrogel matrix. On the other hand, because of the ionic-dipole interaction between the hydrogel matrix and ions, increased stiffness often deteriorates the ionic conductivity and vice versa. High toughness and ionic conductivity are often difficult to achieve simultaneously. Thus, it is challenging to achieve fast ion thermodiffusion for tough hydrogel i-TE materials. Most importantly, the low thermoelectric conversion efficiency of the redox couple significantly influences the performance of i-TE cells. In order to improve the thermoelectric efficiency of i-TE materials, the advancement of electrochemical thermoelectric conversion with molecular technology is highly demanded and imperative27. Some emerging strategies have been proposed, including the in situ photocatalytically enhanced i-TE cells28, crystallization-boosted i-TE cells8,29, and the thermal-responsive VPT process-driven i-TE cells30–32. In particular, the strategy of improving thermoelectric conversion efficiency of hydrogels via its inherent function of VPT holds the advantages of being non-toxic, flexible, and low-cost. But it is still challenging to drive a sharp jump in thermoelectric conversion efficiency. Effective modulation of the interaction between the polymer matrix and electrolyte ions represents a critical approach to achieving significant thermopower enhancement. The Se can achieve 38.2 mV K−1 by establishing robust hydrogen bond hydrogel systems via synergistic coordination and hydration interactions19,33. However, these reported processes necessitate the addition of strong acids or bases, such as hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide, which may make it unsuitable for wearable applications. Alarmingly, there has been no reported study on utilizing the synergies of phase transition and coordination theory to design efficient i-TE materials in an environmentally friendly and cost-effective manner by modulating the interaction between polymer substrates and redox-active ions.

To address the aforementioned challenges, we developed an upper critical solution temperature (UCST) type quasi-solid i-TE cell with good thermoelectric and mechanical properties. In this study, acrylic acid (AA) is selected to construct UCST-type thermal-responsive hydrogel because it is capable of forming hydrogen bonding34 and the temperature-responsive behavior of acrylic hydrogels can be customized by changing their molecular structure composition35,36. N-acryloylsemicarbazide (NASC) with strong inter/intramolecular hydrogen bonding is selected for copolymerization with AA to prepare PNA supramolecular hydrogel with strong mechanical properties and advantageous ionic conductivity pathway37. The primary design principles include: (1) The hydrogel-based i-TE cells exhibit temperature-responsive behavior, and (2) the functional groups within the hydrogel networks can coordinate with one of the redox pairs. (3) The hydrogel networks demonstrate adequate mechanical toughness to accommodate high-concentration electrolytes. (4) A synergistic interaction exists among the ionic complex, ion thermodiffusion, and the VPT of the i-TE cell, which substantially enhances thermoelectric efficiency.

Results

Formation of the tough PNA i-TE hydrogel with high-concentration thermogalvanic ions

For the majority of reported quasi-solid i-TE cells, the concentration of Fe3+/2+ thermoelectric ions typically does not exceed 0.5 M, which may be insufficient for achieving high-performance thermoelectric conversion (Table S1). In this study, the PNA hydrogel exhibits a swelling resistance feature due to its supramolecular hydrogen bond networks37,38. The PNA/Fe3+/2+ hydrogel i-TE cells were prepared by immersing the PNA hydrogel in a saturated Fe3+/2+ aqueous solution with a concentration exceeding 2.0 M. Moreover, the Fe3+/2+ ions can coordinate with the carboxyl groups of PNA hydrogel, thereby ensuring a sufficient supply of ions within the hydrogel matrix.

The preparation process of the PNA supramolecular hydrogel i-TE cell is illustrated in Fig. 1a. The NASC monomer was synthesized according to the published method38. The chemical structure of NASC, as confirmed by 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (Fig. S1), aligns with the literature. A lithium chloride aqueous solution was chosen as the reaction solvent for preparing ionic conductive hydrogels. The addition of lithium chloride enhances the water retention capability of the hydrogels39. The PNA hydrogels were fabricated via one-step ultraviolet irradiation polymerization. Initially, the supramolecular networks of the hydrogel are formed through inter-/intramolecular hydrogen bonding between PNASC chains and PAA chains. Subsequently, the hydrogels are immersed in a saturated FeCl3/FeCl2 aqueous solution to incorporate thermogalvanic ions. In this process, the Fe3+/2+ redox pair not only contributes to the thermogalvanic effect but also serves as ionic crosslinkers, thereby conferring the hydrogels with good mechanical properties (Fig. 1b)40. In addition, the hydrogel network can be further reinforced through volume shrinkage coordinated by thermogalvanic ions. This process enhances the density of hydrogen bonding, thereby facilitating the self-strengthening of the hydrogel network41. As shown in Fig. 1c and Movie S1, the PNA i-TE cell holds excellent stretchability that can adapt to dynamic mechanical changes. The maximum stress of PNA i-TE cell (the molar ratio of AA and NASC is 2:1) significantly improved compared with the hydrogel (Fig. 1d). The rheological profile of PNA hydrogel and i-TE cell is shown in Fig. 1e. An obvious increase in the dynamic storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G′′) of PNA i-TE cell indicates that the strength of PNA i-TE cell is significantly improved compared with PNA hydrogel. The maximum stress of PNA i-TE cells increases with the concentration of reactants. Molecular mechanism of synergistic phase transition and the ion–polymer matrix interaction for fast ion thermodiffusion

The ion–polymer matrix interaction is crucial to control the magnitude of the ionic Se42. It has been proved in the previous work that the inter/intramolecular hydrogen bonding of supramolecular hydrogel can provide desirable channel for ion transport38,43. The abundant hydrogen bonding of PNA/Fe3+/2+ i-TE cell can facilitate the transport of ions, giving rise to high-efficiency ionic thermodiffusion under a temperature gradient33.

The ionic conductivity of PNA hydrogel is optimized from two aspects. On one hand, a series of hydrogel samples are prepared in varied LiCl aqueous solution with different concentrations, while the solid content of reactants is fixed at 40 wt%. The sample prepared in 2 M LiCl aqueous solution possess the optimal conductivity with the change of temperature (7.5–10.1 S m−1) (Fig. 2a). On the other hand, while the concentration of LiCl aqueous solution was fixed at 2 M, the conductivity of 30 wt% solid content hydrogel can reach up to 8.6–11.0 S m−1 with the change of temperature. The conductivity of 50 wt% solid content hydrogel is 7.1–9.8 S m−1 (Fig. 2b). Thus, the solid content change has little effect on the ionic conductivity of PNA hydrogel. The Se of the PNA i-TE cell with different NASC:AA molar ratio is shown in Figs. 2c and S3. The sample with a molar ratio of AA: NASC = 2:1 and 40 wt% solid content obtains a maximal thermopower of 1.56 V with about a 50 °C temperature difference. The Se of PNA i-TE cell increases with the increase of Fe3+/2+ aqueous solution concentration (Fig. S4). On consideration of the optimal comprehensive performance, involving the conductivity, mechanical property, and thermopower, the hydrogel sample with a molar ratio of AA: NASC = 2:1 prepared in 2.0 M LiCl aqueous solution with 50 wt% solid content, and the corresponding i-TE cell prepared in saturated Fe3+/2+ aqueous solution are selected for further investigation unless specifically noted otherwise.

The PNA supramolecular hydrogel exhibits a UCST behavior observed in turbidity measurement (Fig. 2d, e). The volume phase transition temperature (VPTT) of PNA hydrogel is 56 °C. The FTIR signals located at 1800–1565 cm−1 belong to the asymmetric stretching band v(C = O) of the carboxylic acid group. Slight blueshift of the band v(C = O) is observed in the temperature-dependent FTIR spectra, which indicates the intramolecular hydrogen bonding of hydrogel is weakened and the carboxylic acid groups are more associated with water molecules during heating (Fig. 2f)44. The perturbation correlation moving window (PCMW) technique is used to further confirm the phase transition behavior of PNA hydrogel. On the basis of PCMW analysis, two inflection points (42 °C and 64 °C) can be read on the asynchronous spectra; thus, the phase transition interval is determined to be 42–64 °C (Fig. 2g). PCMW synchronous spectra are very helpful to find transition points. Thus, the VPTT of PNA hydrogel can be easily determined to be 56 °C from the synchronous spectra45. This result matches well with the turbidity test.

Two-dimensional correlation spectroscopy (2Dcos) analysis is used to further reveal the molecular mechanisms behind the reversible dynamic phase transition of PNA hydrogel (Note S1)46. On the basis of 2Dcos analysis, the sequence order of PNA hydrogel during the phase transition is described as (→ means prior to or earlier than): 1614 cm−1 v(C = O···HN) (amine II strong H-bonded of PNASC) → 1658 cm−1 v(C = O···HN) (amine I strong H-bond of PNASC) → 1674 cm−1 v(C = O···H2O) (aqueous associated PNASC) → 1685 cm−1 v(C = O) (H-bonded PAA dimer) →1721 cm−1 v(C = O···H2O) (aqueous associated PAA) → 1746 cm−1 v(COOH) (free PAA) (Fig. 2h). This sequence suggest that the thermal sensitivity of strong intramolecular hydrogen bonding is earlier than weak inter-molecular hydrogen bonding of PNASC. The hydrogen bonding of PNASC weakened upon heating. Subsequently, the hydrogen bonds of PAA dissociate from dimeric hydrogen bonds to free PAA upon heating.

Based on the above analysis, the molecular network revolution during the phase transition of the PNA hydrogel upon heating can be proposed as Fig. 2i. At temperatures below VPTT, strong inter-/intramolecular hydrogen bonding exists among the amide groups of PNASC chains, and the carboxyl groups of PAA chains form carboxyl dimers. When the temperature increases to VPTT, the inter-/intramolecular hydrogen bonding among polymer chains weakens initially, followed by the dissociation of carboxyl dimers. This results in an increase in the hydrophilicity of the polymer chains, driven by the association of PNA chains with aqueous molecules. During the phase transition process, a significant release of free carboxyl groups occurs, accelerating H+ and Li+ movement while simultaneously inhibiting anion movement. This dual effect results in an ion entropy difference, which plays a crucial role in the generation of a substantial thermopower.

The hydrophilic increase of polymer chains during the phase transition along with heating was further verified by water contact angle tests (Fig. 2j). The water contact angle of PNA hydrogel upon VPTT (57.7°) is obviously smaller than that below VPTT (89.6°). When the temperature is higher than VPTT, the hydrogel is hydrophilic and transparent. The hydrogel becomes hydrophobic when the temperature is below VPTT, resulting in the inner networks collapse and aggregation, manifesting as a decrease in water contact angle.

Molecular mechanism of the synergistic phase transition and ionic coordination for high-efficiency thermoelectric conversion of redox couple

The PAA aqueous solution has been reported to form hydrogels through the complexation of Fe³⁺ ions with carboxyl groups. However, the PAA–Fe3+ hydrogel undergoes degradation via gel–sol transition when Fe3+ is reduced to Fe2+, due to the de-crosslinking between Fe2+ and carboxyl groups47,48. The binding energies between AA (C3H4O2) and the Fe3+/2+ ions are evaluated by density functional theory computations. The binding energies of Fe2+–C3H4O2 and Fe3+–C3H4O2 are calculated to be −5.86 and −9.44, respectively (Fig. 3a). For the PNA i-TE cell, the free carboxyl group of the PAA chain can coordinate with Fe3+ ions with temperature increase. It is anticipated that the distinct coordination ability with carboxyl groups can enlarge the entropy difference of the Fe3+/2+ redox couple and be beneficial to boost thermopower49. This conjecture is further proved by the temperature-dependent cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests. Figure S5 presents the CV of 0.01 M FeCl3/FeCl2 aqueous solution with 0.6 mol% PAA addition from 20 to 60 °C. The oxidation and reduction peak-to-peak separation is observed to increase with rising temperature. Notably, the increase in equilibrium potential is predominantly attributed to the reduction reaction occurring at the hot side of the cell. Figure 3b, c presents the peak potentials of oxidation and reduction of CV. V1/2 represents the equilibrium potential of oxidation and reduction. The slope of the V1/2 potential is roughly consistent with the measured thermopower value, which is reflected by the Se values50. In the presence of 0.6 mol% PAA additive, the Se value increased from 1.12 to 1.51 mV K−1. This result further proves that the coordination of PAA with Fe3+ contribute to generate large thermopower.

The UCST-type phase transition behaviors can also be observed in the PNA i-TE cell (Fig. 3d). It is worth noting that compared with the hydrogel, the VPTT of i-TE cell decreases from 56 °C to ~30 °C (Fig. 3e). This is mainly because the PNA i-TE cell is constructed by the synergy of ionic coordination and hydrogen bonding; thus, the inter/intramolecular hydrogen bonding among polymer chains is weakened compared to PNA hydrogels, which is manifested as the decrease of VPTT.

The synergy mechanism of PNA i-TE cell with giant thermopower driven by VPT is proposed in Fig. 3f. According to the 2Dcos analysis, the VPT of hydrogel can facilitate the protonation of carboxyl groups, which can provide plenty of binding sites for metal ions. Furthermore, the carboxyl groups coordinate with Fe3+ more easily than with Fe2+. After the VPT of PNA i-TE cells, the coordination ability between carboxyl groups and Fe³⁺ increases with temperature, which enlarges the entropy difference of the redox couple (Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺), thereby significantly improving the redox efficiency (Fig. S6). The mechanism is further validated by the CV analysis of the PNA i-TE cell at varying temperatures (Fig. S7). The CV results at different temperatures indicate that both the redox peak potential and current density increase with an increasing temperature gradient. Notably, a significant enhancement in thermopower is observed when the temperature difference rises from 30 to 40 °C. As shown in Figs. 3g and S8, the thermopower have a sharp increase upon exceeding the optimal temperature for ion coordination (~35 °C, defined as the critical temperature Tc). The thermopower of a single PNA i-TE cell can reach up to 2.05 V at 54 °C. In general, Se increases with the increasing solid content. The Se of PNA i-TE cell (50 wt% solid content) is calculated to be 15.6 mV K⁻¹ below Tc and 40.9 mV K⁻¹ above Tc, respectively (Figs. 3h and S9). Below the Tc, the Se (Se = 15.6 mV K⁻¹) is primarily attributed to the ion entropy difference resulting from ion thermodiffusion. Upon exceeding Tc, the Se (Se = 40.9 mV K⁻¹) originates from the combined effects of ion entropy differences, which are attributed to both ion thermodiffusion and ion coordination.

This mechanism was further validated by additional control experiments utilizing PNA hydrogels accommodated with [FeCN6]4-/3- redox couple. According to the UV–vis spectral analysis, no significant absorbance band shift was observed for either [FeCN6]4- or [FeCN6]3- solutions upon addition of AA, suggesting that the ionic coordination between [FeCN6]4- or [FeCN6]3-, and carboxyl group exhibits no apparent difference (Fig. S10). No sharp increase of thermopower is observed for PNA/[FeCN6]4-/3- i-TE cell, and the Se is calculated to be −5.18 mV K−1 (Fig. S11). This result confirmed the effect of ionic coordination of the PNA i-TE cell with boosting thermopower.

Thermoelectric properties characterization and comprehensive comparison

The thermoelectric properties of PNA i-TE cell below and upon Tc were compared. The current density, power density, and Pmax/(△T)2 of PNA i-TE cell with a temperature difference of 30 K is 102.9 A m−2, 12.6 W m−2, and 14.0 mW m−2 K−2, respectively (Fig. 4a–c). The corresponding results with a temperature difference of 50 K is 148.7 A m−2, 75.8 W m−2, and 30.3 mW m−2 K−2, respectively (Fig. 4a–c), which is much higher than previously reported gel-based quasi-solid i-TE cells (Table S3). It is noticeable that the conductivity of PNA i-TE cell is 1.45–2.58 S m−1 at the temperature range of 10–50 °C, which is much lower than that of the PNA hydrogel (Fig. S12). It is attributed to the ionic crosslink of i-TE cell. With Fe3+/2+ serving as ionic crosslinker, the coordination of Fe3+/2+ ions with PNA hydrogel induces the self-strengthening of the hydrogel network. This results in the ion channel of PNA i-TE cell being narrower than that of the PNA hydrogel, which is reflected by a decrease in ionic conductivity41. The thermal conductivity of PNA i-TE cell decreases from 0.47 to 0.35 W m−1 K−1 while the temperature increases from 25 to 55 °C (Fig. S13 and Table S4). This result indicates that the PNA i-TE cell possesses better thermally insulated ability upon VPTT. Application for powering wearable devices via harvesting body heat

To demonstrate the potential application of PNA i-TE cell for powering wearable devices via harvesting low-grade body heat, we evaluate its stability as a sustainable thermoelectric device. The cyclic charge–discharge at the temperature difference of 10 K was tested (Fig. 5a). The output voltage of PNA i-TE cell reached its maximum of 0.2 V by thermal charging within 35 min. The PNA i-TE cell will discharge to 0 V by connecting to an external circuit and recover back to 0.2 V within 25 s. Benefit from the low thermal conductivity and high ionic conductivity, the duration for one charge–discharge cycle of PNA i-TE cell is much shorter than reported i-TE materials (3–12.5 min)24,55. Five hundred consecutive charge-discharge cycles were conducted over a duration of 3.45 h. During these cycles, the maximum output voltage exhibited no significant variation between the first and the five hundredth cycle (Fig. 5b). The corresponding current density is 3.87 A m−2, and the power density is 2.0 W m−2 (Fig. 5c). According to the literature, heat dissipation from the human skin is approximately in the range of 200–500 W m−2. For a proof-of-concept demonstration, we assembled PNA i-TE cell as a wearable wristband for LED powering. As shown in Fig. S14, a PNA i-TE cell measuring 28.8 × 8.9 × 3.7 mm3 was connected using copper wires at both ends. One end of the copper wire was connected to a 1.5 V LED, while a small iron clip was utilized as a switch to control the circuit’s connection or disconnection. The PNA i-TE wristband was sealed with VHB tape to prevent water loss. The output thermopower of the assembled PNA i-TE wristband can reach up to about 1.8 V with a temperature difference of about 40 K (Fig. 5d and Movie S2). A single PNA i-TE wristband can power an LED by harvesting human thermal energy without the use of a voltage amplifier at an ambient temperature of approximately −5 °C. (Fig. 5e, f and Movie S3). A four-unit PNA i-TE cell array is capable of powering a LED using body heat at an ambient temperature of ~15 °C (Movie S4). These results demonstrate the great potential of the PNA i-TE cell for continuously powering ubiquitous wearable electronics.

Discussion

This work proposes a phase transition-driven mechanism for simultaneous improvement of ionic thermodiffusion and redox efficiency of the quasi-solid supramolecular hydrogel i-TE cell. The PNASC network with strong hydrogen bonding provides robust support for the matrix of PNA i-TE cell. The PAA network in the hydrogel can complex with redox ion couple and boost large thermopower upon Tc. Through the VPT process, a significant ion entropy difference can be generated, thereby enhancing both the ion thermodiffusion rate and the efficiency of redox reactions. The PNA i-TE cell capable of improving ZT by 13.3 times upon heating exceeds Tc. The PNA hydrogel-based i-TE cell is capable of powering a LED independently, without the need for a voltage amplifier or cascading configuration. We propose that leveraging the intrinsic UCST-type VPT behavior and ionic entropy difference to develop high-performance i-TE materials with a low-cost and straightforward preparation process will be a promising innovation. This pioneering research could open avenues for harnessing clean energy from ubiquitous low-grade heat sources.

Methods

Materials

Aminourea hydrochloride was purchased from Aladin (99%, Shanghai, China), acryloyl chloride is provided by J&K Scientific (Beijing, China), diethyl ether and potassium carbonate were purchased from Deen reagent (Tianjin, China), Ferric chloride hexahydrate was provide by Leyan (Shanghai, China), Iron chloride tetrahydrate and potassium hexacyanferrate were purchased from Guangfu (Tianjin, China), acrylic acid and Lithium chloride anhydrous were obtained from Macklin (Shanghai, China), potassium ferrocyanide trihydrate was provided by Kelong (Chengdu, China), 2-Hydroxy-4′-(2-hydroxyethoxy)-2-methylpropiophenon (Irgacure 2959, 98%) was obtained from Energy Chemical (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of NASC monomer

The NASC was synthesized as described in the literature37. In a typical protocol, semicarbazide hydrochloride (12.7 g), deionized water (12 mL), cold K2CO3 solution (2 M, 67.2 mL), and cold diethyl ether (36 mL) were successively added into a 250-mL round-bottom flask. Then, 11.4 g of acryloyl chloride dissolved in 48 mL of diethyl ether was added dropwise to the above solution under stirring at 0 °C for about 30 min. After that, the mixture was stirred continuously at this temperature for another 4 h. Then, the white precipitate formed was collected by filtration. The resulting precipitate was washed with cold water to obtain the crude product. For purification, the crude product was dissolved in deionized water again at room temperature, the undissolved precipitate was removed by centrifuging, and the supernatant was freeze-dried to obtain the final product.

Preparation of PNA hydrogels i-TE materials

NASC and AA monomers were dissolved in LiCl aqueous solution with a range of different molar ratios (1:1, 1:1.5, 1:2, 1:2.5). Then, the photoinitiator IR 2959 (1.0% mol relative to the monomers) was added to the solution. The precursor solution was poured into a tetrafluoroethylene mold immediately, followed by exposure to a UV source (Ergu Photoelectric, RX1K300, 365 nm, 1 kW, the distance between the lamp and reactants was 25 cm) for polymerization. Then, the as-prepared PNA hydrogels were soaked in the saturated FeCl2/FeCl3 solution (1:1 mol) for about 12 h to reach the equilibrium state.

Preparation of PNA i-TE cells

A piece of PNA i-TE cell was sealed by polyacrylate tapes (VHB 4905, 3M Company) and connected to 0.3 mm diameter platinum wires as an electrode.

The cyclic voltammetry at different temperatures to measure the formal potential of redox ions was performed using a three-electrode setup with a platinum tablet electrode as the working electrode, a carbon rod electrode as the auxiliary electrode, and an Ag/AgCl reference electrode. The voltage window range was from 0 to 1 V, and the scan rate was 10 mV s−1. The working and auxiliary electrodes were fully immersed in the cell. The CV of PNA i-TE cell at varying temperatures is record on an electrochemical station (CHI 660E). The hydrogel electrolyte is sealed by two pieces of insulative VHB tapes (3 M 4905). The platinum wires with a diameter of 0.3 mm are used as electrode. The voltage window range was from −1 to 1 V, and the scan rate was 10 mV s−1.
