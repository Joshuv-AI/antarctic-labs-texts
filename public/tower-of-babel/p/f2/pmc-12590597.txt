Delivering optimal weight gain advice to pregnant women by lead maternity carer midwives in a real-world setting to optimise health outcomes (DOT study): a case study protocol
Authors: Kirsten J. Coppell, Nicole McGrath, Beverley Lawton, Kendall Stevenson, H. Mel Rackham, Katrina J. Sharples, Trudy Sullivan, Rosemary M. Hall, Helen Paterson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExcess gestational weight gain (GWG) occurs in up to 70% of women who birth. Excess GWG increases the risk of adverse outcomes in both mother and baby including gestational diabetes, pre-eclampsia, postpartum weight retention, large for gestational age babies, and adiposity among children. Antenatal lifestyle interventions can limit excess GWG. However, successful translation of interventions into routine antenatal care are seldom, and interventions involving midwives are uncommon. Following a successful feasibility study, the main aims of this study are to determine if the Delivering Optimal weighT gain advice in pregnancy (DOT) intervention is acceptable to midwives, acceptable and accessible to pregnant women, leads to improved pregnancy outcomes and reduces health inequities for Māori.MethodsThis is a mixed-methods case study in Te Tai Tokerau (Northland), Aotearoa New Zealand where 37.4% of the total, and half the birthing population are Māori. Independent Lead Maternity Carer midwives (15–20) will be trained to deliver the DOT intervention to 150–200 pregnant women aged ≥ 18 years. Women with pre-existing health conditions requiring specialist obstetric or dietetic care identified at the first antenatal visit will be excluded. The DOT intervention is goal-based and utilises the Starting the Conversation: Diet questionnaire. It is delivered over three visits, the first before 15 weeks gestation then 3–4 weekly. Routinely recorded clinical data including self-reported ethnicity, gravidity and parity, estimated date of delivery, anthropometry, blood pressure, antenatal blood tests, date and mode of delivery and infant birth weight will be collated. Costs associated with intervention delivery will be identified. The primary outcome measures are recruitment and retention. The secondary outcome measures are the cost of intervention delivery, rate of GWG, and birth weight. The acceptability of, and accessibility to, the intervention (enablers and barriers) will be explored qualitatively. Quantitative variables will be summarised using standard statistical methods. The rate of GWG will be described using linear mixed effect models. Qualitative data will be analysed thematically.DiscussionA key goal of this research is to improve the repeatedly documented poor and inequitable health outcomes for Māori. A case study, unlike a RCT, will enable the context of the study to be considered.Trial registrationACTRN12623001228673 with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry: 29 November 2023.

Background

Despite internationally recognised recommendations for gestational weight gain (GWG) for different pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) categories [1], up to 70% of all women who birth gain excess weight during their pregnancy, irrespective of their pre-pregnancy weight [2]. Excess GWG increases the risk of adverse outcomes in both mother and baby, for example, gestational diabetes mellitus, pre-eclampsia, postpartum weight retention, large for gestational age babies, and adiposity among children [3–5]. While less common, lower than recommended GWG or weight loss during pregnancy are associated with increased risks of small for gestational age infants and stillbirth [3]. Being overweight or obese pre-pregnancy further increases the risk of adverse outcomes [6–8]. Unhealthy GWG increases individual costs, healthcare costs and societal costs [9–12]. In Aotearoa New Zealand (NZ), the impact is greater for Māori and Pacific women who have higher average BMI when compared with NZ European/Other women [13].

Pre-pregnancy weight, gestational weight gain and gestational diabetes mellitus among women in Aotearoa New Zealand

In NZ the proportion of women who have a healthy weight at their first antenatal visit declined between 2008 and 2021, while the proportion with a BMI > 30 kg/m2 increased (Fig. 1) [14]. In 2021, more than half of pregnant NZ women with BMI recorded at their first antenatal visit were categorised as overweight (29.1%) or obese (29.8%) and 2.1% were underweight [14]. However, these data have limitations. It is unknown what proportion of women had their weight and height recorded at their first antenatal visit or whether these were measured or self-reported records.

Information about GWG among NZ women is not routinely available. However, a few published studies highlight that unhealthy GWG is common. Among the 264 pregnant NZ women participating in the Screening for Pregnancy Endpoints (SCOPE) study, 21.6% gained their recommended weight, while 16.7% gained insufficient and 61.7% gained excess weight [6]. Of the small number of Pacific women and Māori women (combined n = 21), 14.3% gained their recommended weight [8], which is consistent with a 2014–2016 NZ study in which 7.5% of 240 pregnant Pacific women and 14.2% of 120 pregnant Māori women had healthy GWG [2]. A retrospective cohort study of 534 NZ women having a trial of labour after a previous Caesarean section found 53.4% gained more weight than recommended [15], but this cohort was not representative of the NZ maternity population, as Caesarean section rates are higher among European women compared with Pacific women and Māori women [14]. Although diagnostic criteria and screening regimens for diabetes in pregnancy and study size vary, making comparisons difficult, there is sufficient evidence showing that in parallel with increasing obesity rates, the proportion of NZ women diagnosed with diabetes in pregnancy has increased, for example from around 2% in 1991 to over 12% in 2020 in Auckland, NZ [16].

Intervention studies to limit excess gestational weight gain and translation into real-world settings

Previous intervention studies and meta-analyses show antenatal lifestyle interventions can limit excess GWG [17–20]. However there is little published evidence of interventions being successfully translated into routine maternity care [21], and interventions involving midwives are uncommon [22–28], with many interventions utilising a dietitian to deliver dietary advice [17–20]. Further, the majority of GWG intervention studies only include overweight and obese women [23, 29–33], as they are more likely to have excess GWG than women with a healthy pre-pregnancy weight [6, 8, 34]. However, women with a healthy weight or who are underweight pre-pregnancy can also gain too much weight [7, 35], increasing the risk for postpartum weight retention and an unhealthy pre-pregnancy weight for future pregnancies [36, 37].

The limited evidence from studies in real world settings can be at least partly explained by failure to (i) engage stakeholders, (ii) address barriers and enablers, and (iii) use implementation informed study design [38]. Other considerations and limitations of antenatal lifestyle interventions include the use of absolute weight gain for the primary outcome measure [18, 32, 33, 39] without reporting the proportion of women who gain weight within the Institute of Medicine (IOM) guidelines [1] during their pregnancy. Many studies use self-reported pre-pregnancy weight and height [6, 40–42] yet self-reported pre-pregnancy weight is less reliable than a first trimester measured weight [42]. Further, in many studies interventions begin after the first trimester [23, 29], which reduces the length of time to facilitate healthy lifestyle changes and optimal GWG [43–46]. Ideally interventions to limit nutrition-related and weight-related adverse outcomes for mothers and babies would occur prior to conception [47]. Given most women do not access pre-conceptual lifestyle interventions and more than half of pregnancies and 40% of births are unplanned, at least in NZ [48], early pregnancy is the most practical time to offer antenatal lifestyle interventions aimed at achieving optimal GWG.

Maternity care in Aotearoa New Zealand

Primary maternity care is publicly funded for all eligible women in NZ and is provided by Lead Maternity Carers (LMCs). In 2022, ~ 95% of LMCs were midwives with the others being obstetricians (~ 4%) or general practitioners (< 1%) [14]. Most LMCs are self-employed and are contracted by the NZ Ministry of Health (MoH) to provide ‘primary maternity services’ for pregnant women from the time of first contact at the antenatal booking visit until 6 weeks postnatal, and since 2021 this includes additional care to women with complex needs, and to women living in rural areas [49]. The other LMCs are employed at publicly funded hospitals.

Pregnant women are advised to register with their LMC in the first 10 weeks of pregnancy, but this does not necessarily occur. In 2022, 76.4% booked with their LMC in the first trimester nationally, but this percentage varied by region, ranging from 60.3 to 87.7% [50]. Local initiatives to increase the proportion of women booking with their LMC in the first trimester have been effective in many regions, for example, Te Tai Tokerau (Northland) where there has been an increase from 46.9% in 2009 to 75.9% in 2022 [50]. Most women birth at a publicly funded maternity facility.

In NZ, there are written and online resources to guide safe eating and a healthy weight gain in pregnancy [51, 52], but no specific intervention to optimise gestational weight gain; seeing a publicly funded dietitian is limited to women with specific nutritional requirements such as women with diabetes. Women with a high BMI seldom see a dietitian due to limited dietetic services available.

Feasibility of a midwife-delivered healthy food choice intervention

The Delivering Optimal weighT gain advice in pregnancy (DOT) intervention was developed to inform and guide food choice advice provided by LMC midwives as part of routine antenatal care to increase the proportion of pregnant women gaining their recommended weight according to the IOM guidelines [1]. The tool was adapted from a primary care nurse delivered prediabetes intervention [53]. The feasibility of delivering the intervention as part of routine antenatal care was assessed in Southland, NZ, with eight trained LMC midwives who recruited 27 pregnant women and delivered the intervention. Study participant retention was 100% and 14 of the 27 participating women (51.9%; 95% CI 33.0%, 70.7%) gained their recommended total GWG. An independent qualitative study reported, “Participating midwives spoke very positively about the intervention and enjoyed being able to offer a simple, well-planned and well-developed intervention to guide their women to optimise their weight gain in pregnancy. It was clear they felt empowered by the additional knowledge and resources they acquired.” [54]. The main challenge in the feasibility was the manual paper-based data collection which was considered to be cumbersome and time consuming.

Community-initiated study

The geographical location of this study was initiated by health professional and community concerns about the increasing number of women developing diabetes in pregnancy in Te Tai Tokerau [55] and the need to optimise GWG to improve maternal and perinatal outcomes. Community engagement and consultation, and information sharing and discussions with midwives, diabetes dietitians, and the local Maternity Clinical Governance Committee community representative validated not only community concerns about diabetes but also prioritised equity, cultural and ethical issues and facilitated the establishment of a community-academic partnership [56]. Several people and groups identified GWG as an equity issue for Māori women and said that any intervention needs to be acceptable, accessible and affordable if pregnant women are to optimise their GWG.

Based on the pilot study results we hypothesised that our pragmatic midwife-delivered DOT intervention in a real-world setting would be acceptable to midwives, acceptable and accessible to pregnant women and lead to improved pregnancy outcomes.

Design

In determining the study design we considered that (1) there is evidence that dietary interventions can limit GWG [17–19], and (2) a randomised controlled trial (RCT) was unacceptable to the Te Tai Tokerau community given the level of evidence available and the urgent need for an intervention. Therefore, a case study was deemed to be the most appropriate design [ 57, 58] as it would generate an in-depth, multi-faceted understanding of the acceptability, accessibility and impact of a midwife-delivered GWG management intervention in a real-world community setting, rather than an homogenous clinical trial cohort [ 59]. It would also seek to understand the process of changing clinical practice and the factors that influenced the implementation and delivery of the intervention, specifically its workability and integration into midwifery practice.

This mixed-methods case study [57, 58] is guided by the Medical Research Council (MRC) complex interventions framework [60]. There are two components (i) a quantitative analyses carried out on all study participants (contributing to all aims) and (ii) a Kaupapa Māori qualitative component which will address the acceptability and accessibility of the intervention to midwives and pregnant women. The study is funded by the Health Research Council (HRC) of NZ, has been reviewed and approved by the NZ Northern A Health and Disability Ethics Committee. (Ref: 2023 FULL 17956) and registered on the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trial Registry (Trial ID: ACTRN12623001228673: 29 November 2023). In addition to community engagement, formal Māori consultation has been undertaken with the Northern Region Kaunihera Kaumātua (Council of Elders).

Setting

The study is being conducted in Te Tai Tokerau, NZ (Fig. 2) where there is a regionwide maternity service [61]. In 2023, the population of Te Tai Tokerau was 194,007 of whom 72,612 (37.4%) identified as Māori, 141,594 (73.0%) as European, 9,486 (4.9%) as Pacific and 9,345 (4.8%) as ‘Asian’. Nationally, 17.8% of NZ’s almost 5 million population identify as Māori, 67.8% as European, 8.9% as Pacific and 17.3% as ‘Asian’ [62]. Many areas in Te Tai Tokerau have a high level of socioeconomic deprivation relative to most other areas of NZ [63].

The Te Tai Tokerau region has three primary birthing centres and a secondary care facility in Whangārei, the main city. A Maternity Clinical Governance Committee, which includes consumer representatives, meets regularly to provide oversight of the regional maternity service [61]. About half of the birthing population in Te Tai Tokerau are Māori and almost 60% of babies born are Māori [55, 61]. In 2021, most (79%) pregnant women in Te Tai Tokerau birthed at Whangārei Hospital with other women birthing at a primary birthing unit (13%) or home (7%) [61]. There are approximately 30 LMC midwives in Te Tai Tokerau; the average annual case load per self-employed midwife in NZ is about 40 women [64].

Eligibility criteria and recruitment of pregnant women

The inclusion criteria are: pregnant women aged ≥ 18 years; able to communicate in English; an antenatal registration appointment before 15 weeks gestation (ideally before 10 weeks gestation); a first intervention visit being able to be delivered before 15 weeks gestation; and plans to stay in the study region for the duration of their pregnancy and birth. The exclusion criteria are: women with any pre-existing health condition at antenatal booking who require a ‘transfer of care’ to an antenatal obstetric specialist as defined in the 2023 NZ guidelines [65]; or any health condition requiring specialist dietetic care (e.g. previous bariatric surgery).

All practising LMC midwives in the Te Tai Tokerau region who use Expect Maternity software [www.expectmaternity.co.nz/] will be invited to participate in the study. Participating LMC midwives will be asked to assess the eligibility of each consecutive pregnant women at their antenatal registration appointment, inform those who meet the eligibility criteria about the study and invite them to participate. Eligible women will also be provided with a study information sheet. For those willing to take part, a further appointment will be made within 1–2 weeks of their antenatal registration appointment to obtain written informed consent and undertake the first intervention visit. As part of the consenting process, women will be asked if they would like to participate in the qualitative component of the study. This will enable women to be contacted directly and invited to participate in the qualitative study.

The Delivering Optimal weighT (DOT) gain advice in pregnancy intervention

The overarching goal of the DOT intervention is to provide women (and their whānau (wider family)) with an understanding of the principles of healthy eating to enable good food choices. The intervention is principally dietary-based (as opposed to exercise or both diet and exercise) as diet has been shown to be more effective at improving weight-related outcomes in pregnancy [20]. The intervention is based on a primary care-based prediabetes intervention which was designed to provide individuals with prediabetes and their family with an understanding of healthy eating principles, and to develop and enhance empowerment around good food choices with consideration of sociocultural context [53, 66]. The intervention recognises the personal, interpersonal, organisational, environmental and policy factors that influence dietary change [67]. We have minimally modified the prediabetes intervention for use in the maternity care setting and developed a ‘user-friendly’ written healthy food choice resource with involvement of eight pregnant women using a citizen’s deliberation approach [68]. The DOT intervention comprises three components:

1. Structured dietary intervention tool

The tool was developed using a socio-ecological theoretical framework [67]. It was informed by evidence from clinical trials, principles of health behaviour change and dietary guidelines [51, 69]. The tool uses the Starting the Conversation: Diet (STC: Diet) questionnaire [70], adapted for NZ [53, 66], combined with additional sociocultural questions and a Detailed Dietary Assessment (DDA). The STC: Diet questionnaire has been recommended and promoted as an efficient tool to facilitate nutritional counselling in a range of settings [70]. The DDA, originally developed by a dietitian for the primary care nurse-delivered prediabetes intervention [53, 66], was modified for pregnancy, taking into account foods to be avoided in pregnancy such as cold meats to minimise the risk of listeriosis [52]. The tool considers women’s sociocultural context [71], enables a structured, pragmatic approach to dietary assessment in pregnancy [54], and facilitates establishing individualised smart goals, increasing the likelihood of achievement [67].

2. Evidence-based culturally appropriate training for midwives with dietitian support

Training for midwives is a 5-hour theory and practical course, which includes nutrition principles, familiarisation with the intervention tool, how to accurately measure height and weight and accurate study data documentation including consenting processes. Course content, based on the training that practice nurses received in the prediabetes intervention study [53, 66], will be delivered by three study investigators (KC, HP and HR) and a local dietitian. One hour will be self-directed learning, two hours will be delivered virtually, and two hours will be case study practical in-person training. Training documentation includes an outline of the study, intervention procedures and reference material, including NZ Ministry of Health resources [72, 73]. A local dietitian will be available by email and phone to answer questions or discuss specific cases during the study. While seeking dietitian advice and guidance will be encouraged, this was seldom required in the Southland feasibility study [54].

Five hours for training was considered an appropriate length to provide sufficient knowledge and skills to utilise the tool and undertake the required study procedures. This was confirmed in the independent qualitative component of our feasibility study which reported “All of the midwives felt that the training received at the study day prepared them well for their role. All enjoyed this education session and spoke very positively about the quality and volume of information they received” [54].

3. Dietary assessment and goal setting intervention sessions for pregnant women

Midwives will offer three intervention sessions—the first, a separate 30–45 min visit (at < 15 weeks gestation and ideally before 10 weeks gestation), with two subsequent (15–30 min) sessions at 15–18 weeks and 19–22 weeks gestation, either as separate visits or combined with a usual routine antenatal visit. At the first intervention visit, written consent will be obtained, and the woman’s early pregnancy BMI and recommended GWG goal will be determined according to the IOM guidelines (adopted by NZ) [1, 51]. A dietary assessment using the intervention tool will be undertaken, and three individualised goals that focus on enabling healthy food choices will be established in discussion with the woman. Healthy food guidance will not be prescriptive but will specifically take into account the particular needs and sociocultural context of each woman [67, 71]. This approach has been designed to enable LMC midwives to work with women to establish achievable goals, and to deliver consistent evidence-, and context-based healthy food guidance. Women will be provided with our ‘user-friendly’ written food choice resource [68], along with the NZ Ministry of Primary Industries ‘Food Safety in Pregnancy’ pamphlet [52]. They will also be encouraged to be physically active in accordance with national recommendations [72]. The purpose of the subsequent two intervention visits will be to review progress with goal achievement and set new goals as necessary (if goals were achieved or considered unachievable on review). All other aspects of participants’ antenatal care will remain the same, that is, they will continue their recommended routine antenatal care.

All participating LMC midwives will each receive a standardised set of weighing scales and a stadiometer and will be reimbursed for their time to undertake study procedures and deliver the intervention. Intervention fidelity will be monitored by Assistant Research Fellows who will visit the midwives regularly (about monthly).

Clinical data

LMC midwives in NZ use one of three electronic maternity software systems for their clinical records. At the time of planning this study the maternity software used by the majority of LMC midwives in Te Tai Tokerau was chosen to be modified to facilitate the intervention and study data collection. Routinely collected data will include self-reported ethnicity, date of birth, gravidity and parity, estimated date of delivery, height, weight and blood pressure measurements at each antenatal visit and 6-weeks postnatally, antenatal blood test results, date of delivery, mode of delivery and birth weight of the infant. The length of time to deliver the intervention and the responses to questions asked as part of the intervention, including the contextual questions (e.g. who else lives at home, who mostly cooks, is the food budget limited), the modified brief STC: Diet questionnaire [70] and the dietary goals established with each participant, will also be recorded in the maternity management system on newly designed study pages. An Assistant Research Fellow will regularly visit participating midwifery practices to review and check data collection processes and undertake data checks as needed to minimise missing data and ensure data accuracy. Visiting midwifery practices outside Te Tai Tokerau to follow-up any participant who moves from the region prior to their delivery will not be feasible.

Pre-specified de-identified study data captured through the maternity software system will be encrypted, exported securely to study investigators and checked monthly, and any missing or inaccurate data identified and corrected where possible. Participants will be assigned a unique identifying number at the time of recruitment. As there is an obligation for clinicians to record clinical data accurately, data integrity is likely to be high.

Cost data

Costs for the DOT intervention (i.e. in addition to standard dietary advice) will include all costs associated with training midwives and the time-cost of delivering the intervention (calculated as number of additional hours midwives spend with participating women to deliver the intervention) as well as any additional resources used. Each midwife will be asked to describe and record their antenatal care dietary delivery practice, including the resources used, frequency of weighing women, and time taken, in order to estimate the costs associated with the delivery of DOT intervention.

Quantitative component

Quantitative outcome measures

The primary outcome measures are: (1) recruitment (the proportion of midwives who complete training and recruit at least 10 consecutive eligible women and the proportion of invited women who consent to take part in the study), and (2) retention (the proportion of women who complete the intervention). The secondary outcome measures are (1) the cost of delivering the intervention, (2) the proportion of women achieving recommended GWG and the rate of gestational weight (kg) gain from study entry to end of pregnancy, and (3) infant birth weight.

Quantitative data analysis

Quantitative variables will be summarised using standard statistical methods (e.g. proportions for categorical data, and median and interquartile range for continuous data). Analyses will include the calculation of the following proportions (with 95% confidence intervals): (1) midwives who complete training, (2) midwives who recruit at least 10 consecutive eligible women, (3) invited women who consent to participate in the study, (4) enrolled women who complete each of the three intervention visits, and (5) enrolled women who complete the study to 6-week follow-up post birth. The rate of GWG will be described using linear mixed effect models for Māori and non-Māori. Population percentiles for Te Tai Tokerau will be constructed using birthweights from the National Maternity collection.

All costs associated with delivering ‘usual care’ dietary advice and the study intervention will be summed separately, and the total costs compared.

Sample size for quantitative objectives

We anticipate recruiting 15–20 (60–80%) of approximately 25 LMC midwives practising in the Te Tai Tokerau region who use Expect Maternity software, so the estimated proportion will have a margin of error of approximately 20%. Emphasis is on the qualitative data (see below). For pregnant women, we anticipate retention rates of around 70% and a proportion who achieve recommended GWG of approximately 50% based on our previous pilot study. The design effect needed to account for correlations induced by nesting of women within midwives is unknown, but as an indication of likely precision of estimates, with 150 pregnant women and a design effect of 1 the margin of error is < 3%; for a design effect of 2 the margin of error is < 11%.

Data monitoring

The study will be monitored by an internal data monitoring committee who will review data on study conduct and safety. Given the level of risk is low and the pragmatic nature of the study, early termination for efficacy of the intervention will not be considered. However, a concerning level of adverse effects of the intervention such as GWG below that recommended [1, 51] or a higher than expected proportion of small for gestational age (SGA) babies [74] may lead to early termination. Termination for futility may be considered if recruitment and retention are low or there are unforeseen problems with trial conduct.

Qualitative component

The acceptability of, and accessibility to, the DOT intervention (enablers and barriers) as delivered during the study will be explored qualitatively. By examining how participating LMC midwives incorporated the intervention into their practice (implementation), and describing the factors that influenced delivery of the DOT intervention programme, we will determine how the programme can be optimised in practice to enhance intervention fidelity and incorporation into everyday routine practice [75, 76]. This will be underpinned by a methodology consistent with a Kaupapa Māori Research approach, whereby whakawhanaungatanga (developing relationships), participant safety, and research accountability are paramount.

Kaupapa Māori Research methodologies see being Māori as legitimate and normal and aim to address disparities by examining structural barriers to care and wellbeing [77, 78]. Kaupapa Māori Research respects and prioritises Māori ways of knowing and being, acknowledges Māori as active agents rather than victims, and ultimately benefits the Māori community. Linda Smith’s seven Kaupapa Māori practices will guide our work using the following key concepts: respect/love, accountability/being seen, listening before speaking, consultation/collaboration, generosity/reciprocity, abiding by cultural protocols, and humility [78]. These highlight the importance of relationships for Māori.

In addition, a theoretical and conceptual framework, Normalisation Process Theory (NPT), will be used to understand the implementation of the DOT intervention [79]. This theory hypothesizes that practices become routine or normalised as a result of people working to enact them [80], and is concerned with the social organisation of the work (implementation), of making practices routine elements of everyday life (embedding), and of sustaining embedded practices in their social contexts (integration) [81].

Recruitment and consenting

Participants will be key stakeholders, both LMC midwives involved in delivering the DOT intervention and pregnant women who participated in the intervention. It is anticipated that 16 LMC midwives and 24 women will participate in the qualitative sub-study. All LMC midwives delivering the intervention will be invited to participate in an interview lasting up to 1 h. Interviewing midwives who work together in pairs or small groups will be invited to be interviewed individually, in pairs or small groups [54].

For women, potential eligible participants are those who were recruited for and participated in the DOT study intervention and consented to be contacted and invited to participate in the qualitative study. For these women, an analysis of the study enrolment data will inform a purposeful sample to ensure a range of demographic profiles. We aim to interview 24 women, and it is anticipated that half will be Māori.

Qualitative data collection

Data will include multiple sources and a broad range of data, allowing for a good understanding of each midwifery practice, and the enablers and barriers for accessing the intervention by pregnant women. This will include semi-structured interviews, field notes, and document analysis, and augmented by the Normalisation MeAsure Development (NoMAD) survey tool [82], which is a 23-item instrument based on the constructs of NPT that measures implementation process from the viewpoint of professionals involved in delivering the intervention programme [83].

Interviews will be semi-structured and at a location of participants’ choosing or virtually. Interview guides will be developed and will comprise open-ended questions. The qualitative interviews will be audio-recorded with consent and transcribed verbatim using Kaituhi, an iwi-owned and developed web-based transcription tool that is highly secure (https://kaituhi.nz/). Following transcription, data will be checked for accuracy by the interviewer, and participants provided with the opportunity to check their transcript for accuracy.

Qualitative data analysis

Interview data will be analysed thematically [84], and will occur concurrently with data collection to allow for clarification and further exploration of emerging concepts and themes in subsequent interview [85]. Coding of data will be undertaken and reviewed and discussed prior to data analysis and interpretation. Themes (and sub-themes) will be derived inductively. For the interview data from LMC midwives, deductive codes will be developed using the concepts of the NPT and the research questions, and inductive coding completed as new codes emerge [86]. This will be guided by the coding manual for NPT [87].

The qualitative and quantitative data will be analysed separately, then triangulated to generate a comprehensive overall understanding of how the midwife-delivered DOT intervention was implemented in the study.

Dissemination of results

Key study results will be first presented to study participants, the local Maternity Clinical Governance Committee and the Northern Region Kaunihera Kaumātua (Council of Elders). In addition, a written summary of the key results will be sent to those participating women who indicated they would like to receive a copy as part of the study consenting process. Results will also be presented to key health sector stakeholder groups in Te Tai Tokerau, such as the wider midwifery group, dietitians, the local obstetric and diabetes departments, and then more widely in academic and conference settings including publication in lay, health professional and academic journals.

Discussion

This case study focuses on a community health concern (diabetes in pregnancy and the need to optimise GWG to improve maternal and perinatal outcomes) and equity, in the northern region of NZ where 37% of the population are Māori and 60% of babies born are Māori. A community-research partnership approach involving the community and local health services has been adopted to facilitate opportunities for equitable participation in the antenatal intervention to sustainably improve health outcomes for women, babies and the wider family. This approach also aligns with national efforts to strengthen maternity services in NZ, and to facilitate improvements in the health and wellbeing of women and their babies by addressing an identified gap in health service delivery that has arisen because of high overweight and obesity rates in the NZ population.

A key goal of this research is to improve the repeatedly documented poor and inequitable health outcomes for Māori, e.g. high diabetes rates [13, 88]. As a group, Māori are likely to benefit from this planned research through improved health outcomes for mama (mothers), pēpi (babies) and whānau (wider family). The wāhine (woman) and whānau-centred approach of our intervention is consistent with whānau-based approaches for Māori. Sociocultural context, including culture, food likes and dislikes, household budgets and household membership, is integral to the intervention. The development of goals by wāhine in partnership with their midwife will provide wāhine with opportunities to make food choices that are important and relevant to them. While a key focus is Māori, our novel intervention has the potential to be applicable in different settings with different ethnic groups, both nationally and internationally, as the intervention focuses on a structured process rather than a specific diet.

This research seeks to implement and evaluate a pragmatic, culturally appropriate and safe intervention that will develop and strengthen maternity carer workforce capability and enhance the health and wellbeing of mama and pēpi in Te Tai Tokerau, and across NZ. The incorporation of the intervention into ‘everyday’ midwifery practice, and the examination of how participating midwives incorporate the intervention into their practice and what factors influence the delivery of the intervention will provide an excellent platform to optimise and enhance fidelity when extending the intervention, should it be found to be acceptable and accessible to both midwives and pregnant women in the Te Tai Tokerau community. Knowing what influences delivery of the intervention by LMC midwives and uptake of the intervention by pregnant women, as well as the impact of the intervention on GWG will provide evidence for local and national policy and affect system change. This information will be especially relevant given the national health system transformation occurring in NZ during the study [89]. It will inform effective targeting of resources as well as establishing methods and processes that could be adapted for other high priority groups in NZ including Pacific and Indian women [13].

Our ongoing engagement with the NZ College of Midwives and the MoH nutrition team from the time of planning the feasibility study, and more recently with the MoH maternity team, provides the foundation and avenue for facilitating uptake of our research findings. An Advisory Group comprised of key stakeholders will meet with investigators 6-monthly to ensure a formal process for regular communication of study progress which will facilitate timely knowledge transfer through the sharing of results and planning at a local (Te Tai Tokearu) and national level. The inclusion of consumers, and key groups and organisations at both local and national levels from the outset, will enable partnerships between end-users, academics and policy makers to be established, thereby facilitating effective processes and policy.

Limitations

This case study does not have a comparison group, which may be viewed as a limitation. However, having a control group was not acceptable to the community. A case study will enable the context of the study to be considered. This is currently important in NZ where the health sector is going through structural change and the impact of this on the intervention will be able to be described. The gestation of potential study participants at study entry is required to be less than 15 weeks yet women register with a midwife after the first trimester and as a group these women are likely to be different from those who register later than recommended. In Te Tai Tokerau there are ongoing efforts to increase the proportion of pregnant women registering with a LMC or DHB primary maternity service in the first trimester, which was 75.9% in 2022 [50].

Trial status

This trial began recruiting and training LMC midwives in February 2024, and the recruitment of women began in April 2024.
