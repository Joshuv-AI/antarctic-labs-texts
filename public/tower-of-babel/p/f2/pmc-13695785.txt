Safety and pharmacokinetics of anumigilimab for hidradenitis suppurativa or palmoplantar pustulosis: A phase 1b study
Authors: Johannes S. Kern, Simon Francis Thomsen, Georgios Kokolakis, Maurizio Podda, Gregor B. E. Jemec, Kurt Gebauer, Claus Zachariae, John Frew, Pablo Fernandez‐Peñas, Falk G. Bechara, Stefan Beissert, Sourabh Malandkar, Karen Scalzo‐Inguanti, Partha Nandy, Selvakumar Sukumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe report the first study of anumigilimab, a novel anti‐G‐CSFR antibody, in patients with neutrophil‐driven skin diseases [hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) and palmoplantar pustulosis (PPP)].MethodsThis phase 1b, open‐label, repeat‐dose study (NCT03972280) comprised 15‐week treatment and 9‐week follow‐up periods. Enrolled patients had International HS Severity Score System (IHS4) score ≥4 or PPP Area and Severity Index (ppPASI) score ≥12. After ≥10 patients completed anumigilimab 0.3 mg/kg IV at 21‐day intervals (Cohort 1), pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic modelling determined the Cohort 2 dose (0.6 mg/kg). Primary endpoints were treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs). Secondary endpoints were pharmacokinetics and immunogenicity. Exploratory endpoints were pharmacodynamics and clinical response.ResultsOf 39 patients (HS, n = 22; PPP, n = 17), 33 patients (84.6%) experienced 198 TEAEs. Sixteen patients (41.0%) experienced 70 anumigilimab‐related TEAEs; the most common were fatigue and neutropenia (both n = 7), which resolved spontaneously, and no association between neutropenia and infections was observed. No deaths or serious treatment‐related TEAEs occurred. Presence of antidrug antibodies was negligible. Anumigilimab exposure and absolute neutrophil count reduction (pharmacodynamic endpoint) were dose dependent. In signal‐finding exploratory analyses, 57.9% of patients with HS (n = 11/19 with data at Week 15) achieved ≥50% HS Clinical Response, IHS4 score decreased by 40.3%, and patients with PPP experienced a 40.4% reduction in ppPASI score. These clinical responses were not dose dependent, possibly due to imbalanced baseline characteristics and small sample size.ConclusionsAnumigilimab was generally well tolerated in patients with HS or PPP, with no serious treatment‐related safety concerns, and demonstrated dose‐dependent pharmacology. While clinical responses in HS and PPP were observed, it should be noted that these observations were exploratory and signal‐finding in nature. Importantly, the study was not powered to statistically confirm efficacy and lacked a placebo control, underscoring the need for future randomized, placebo‐controlled trials to validate these preliminary signal‐finding observations.

INTRODUCTION

Significant unmet therapeutic need exists for hidradenitis suppurativa (HS), a chronic, recurrent and painful inflammatory scarring disease that affects the apocrine gland‐bearing skin. HS is characterized by abscesses, nodules and draining tunnels in the skin on the groins, axillae, buttocks, thighs and under the breasts. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Pain, malodorous discharge and scarring contribute to significant morbidity and distress. Comorbidities and psychological impact include metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, axial spondyloarthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, social isolation, sexual dysfunction, loss of employment, anxiety, depression and increased risk of suicide. 5 , 6 The reported prevalence of HS is <0.1–4.1%, and approximately 75% of patients are considered to have moderate‐to‐severe disease. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12

Palmoplantar pustulosis (PPP), another chronic and recurrent inflammatory skin disease with unmet therapeutic need, is a rare condition with flares of pustules covering the hands and feet. 13 , 14 High‐quality data on the treatment of PPP are limited, contributing to uncertainty about therapeutic approaches.

HS and PPP pathogenesis are both characterized by neutrophilic infiltration. 13 , 14 , 15 Neutrophils are central to innate immunity but also promote the inflammatory response. 16 , 17 If neutrophils are not appropriately regulated (e.g. via delayed apoptosis after elimination of pathogens), perpetual inflammation leads to tissue modification and destruction. 18 , 19 Interleukin (IL)‐1, IL‐17, tumour necrosis factor‐alpha (TNFα) and granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF) are reportedly involved in HS. 3 , 20 IL‐17 and TNFα have also been implicated in PPP. 21 High levels of IL‐17‐expressing neutrophils and of G‐CSF have been detected in HS lesions. 20 , 22 IL‐1 and IL‐17 induce G‐CSF production. 20 G‐CSF‐mediated increase in neutrophil activity is considered a key driver of inflammation in HS. 23 Hence, G‐CSF pathway blockade is considered to have therapeutic potential. 20 , 24

Anumigilimab (CSL324) is a novel, fully human, recombinant monoclonal antibody composed of germline variable regions and IgG4 heavy and kappa light constant regions. 25 , 26 Anumigilimab binds to human and non‐human primate G‐CSF receptors (G‐CSFRs) with high affinity, blocking binding of G‐CSF. Single anumigilimab doses prevented G‐CSF‐induced peripheral blood neutrophilia in monkeys, while human ex vivo experiments showed that anumigilimab blocks neutrophil migration markers. 25 , 26 In monkeys, repeat anumigilimab dosing did not result in neutropenia and had no discernible effects on neutrophil function. 25 In a first‐in‐human study in healthy participants, single and three repeat doses of anumigilimab at 21‐day intervals were generally well tolerated, with dose‐dependent reductions in absolute neutrophil counts (ANCs) compared with placebo (unpublished data). Overall, these studies reported promising pharmacodynamic (PD), pharmacokinetic (PK) and safety profiles.

The aim of the reported phase 1b study was to investigate the safety, PK and immunogenicity profiles of repeat‐dose anumigilimab as well as to provide initial exploratory assessments of PD and signal‐finding observations of clinical response in patients with moderate‐to‐severe HS or PPP.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This phase 1b, open‐label, 2‐regimen, repeat‐dose study was conducted at 11 sites in Australia, Denmark and Germany (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03972280). The study comprised 28‐day screening, 15‐week treatment and 9‐week follow‐up periods. Patients with HS applied antiseptic wash (e.g. chlorhexidine 4%) daily.

Patients with moderate‐to‐severe HS or PPP were treated with repeat doses of anumigilimab in Cohorts 1 (0.3 mg/kg) and 2 (0.6 mg/kg). Anumigilimab was administered IV for 15 weeks, at 21‐day intervals. When ≥10 participants with a given indication (HS or PPP) were enrolled in Cohort 1, enrolment for that indication began in Cohort 2.

Doses for this phase 1b study were selected using a PK/PD model that was based on a semi‐mechanistic model of the neutrophil lifecycle 27 and incorporated PK/PD data from a phase 1a study in healthy adults (unpublished data), which indicated that blockade of G‐CSF‐mediated neutrophilia occurred at single doses of 0.3 mg/kg or above. After the first 10 participants had completed 15 weeks of treatment with the 0.3 mg/kg dose in Cohort 1, PK and PD data were incorporated into the PK/PD (ANC) model, and the simulations and safety data were reviewed by the safety review committee (SRC). A dose of 0.6 mg/kg was recommended for Cohort 2 by the SRC to achieve sustained receptor occupancy and minimize the risk of neutropenia.

The trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by institutional review boards. All participants signed informed consent forms and could voluntarily discontinue treatment. Investigators could withdraw treatment for safety, behavioural or administrative reasons.

Participants

Eligible participants were 18–75 years of age with a diagnosis of HS or PPP ≥6 months before screening. Patients with HS had moderate‐to‐severe disease (IHS4 score ≥4), inadequate response or contraindication to a 90‐day course of oral antibiotics, and ≤20 draining tunnels. Patients with PPP had a ppPASI score ≥12 and inadequate response to topical therapy, phototherapy and/or systemic therapy. Detailed eligibility criteria are described in Table S1. Permitted and prohibited therapies are listed in Tables S2 and S3.

Endpoints

The primary endpoints were TEAE incidence, severity and causality. Potential relationships to anumigilimab administration were investigator assessed. TEAE severity (mild, moderate or severe) was determined using the Clinical Data Interchange Standards Consortium (CDISC) Study Data Tabulation Model (SDTM) Severity Intensity Scale for Adverse Event Terminology. Adverse event of special interests (AESIs) were Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) Grades 3 and 4 neutropenia and infections. Severity of neutropenia was assigned using the neutropenia severity scale: mild (<1.5 to ≥1.0 × 10 9 /L), moderate (<1.0 to ≥0.5 × 10 9 /L) and severe (<0.5 × 109/L). The scale correlates with CTCAE Grades 2, 3 and 4 neutrophil count decreased (Table S4). Secondary endpoints were PK parameters and the presence of anti‐anumigilimab antibodies in serum. Exploratory endpoints included PD (serum G‐CSF and ANC profiles) and clinical response: HS Clinical Response (HiSCR), International HS Severity Score System (IHS4), Palmoplantar Pustulosis Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (ppPASI) and Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI). HiSCR50 was defined as a ≥50% reduction from baseline in total abscess and nodule count, with no increase in abscesses or draining tunnel count. 28 IHS4‐55 (defined as a ≥55% reduction from baseline in IHS4 score) and change in the number of draining tunnels were both analysed post hoc. IHS4 scores (calculated from the number of nodules, abscesses and draining tunnels) indicate HS severity: mild (≤3), moderate (4–10) and severe (≥11). 29 Higher ppPASI scores (maximum 72) reflect greater severity (erythema, pustules and desquamation) and greater percentage area affected. 30 Higher DLQI scores (maximum 30) reflect greater impact on quality of life. 31

Treatment and assessment schedules

Participants attended study visits at least weekly throughout the 15‐week treatment period and remained onsite for safety observations for ≥1 h after each anumigilimab infusion (Days 1, 22, 43, 64 and 85). Oral temperature was self‐monitored daily. Blood was collected from the contralateral arm (with respect to anumigilimab infusion) for immunogenicity, PK and PD assessments. Safety, immunogenicity, PK, PD and clinical responses were assessed throughout the treatment period until Day 105. During the 9‐week follow‐up period, further onsite assessments were conducted on Days 126, 147 and at study end (Day 168).

Statistical analysis

No formal sample size calculation was performed, although the potential incidence of neutropenia was considered (Table S5). The Safety Analysis Set comprised all participants who received anumigilimab. The PK and PD Analysis Sets included all participants in the Safety Analysis Set who had a quantifiable concentration of anumigilimab and PD data before and after anumigilimab administration, respectively. The Efficacy Analysis Set comprised all participants in the Safety Analysis Set who had an efficacy assessment at baseline and corresponding assessment(s) after the first anumigilimab administration. The Per‐Protocol Analysis Set comprised all participants in the Efficacy Analysis Set who received at least four anumigilimab infusions, with at least one efficacy assessment after the fourth infusion and without major protocol deviations. The percentages of patients achieving HiSCR50 were calculated based on the number of patients for whom HiSCR data were available on the study day. Data were summarized descriptively.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics and patient disposition

Thirty‐nine patients with HS (n = 22) or PPP (n = 17) were enrolled. First and last visits occurred in July 2019 and October 2022.

Most patients were female (82.1%), White (89.7%) and current (69.2%) or former smokers (17.9%) (Table 1). HS characteristics at baseline were not balanced between the two dosing cohorts, reflecting the non‐randomized study design and small sample size. For patients with HS in Cohorts 1 and 2, the proportions with severe disease were 90.9% and 72.7%; mean [standard deviation (SD)] IHS4 scores were 38.4 (23.3) and 18.6 (11.0); number of draining tunnels were 5.7 (6.2) and 2.3 (1.7); and ANCs were 5.5 × 10 9 /L (2.1) and 5.8 × 109/L (2.4), respectively. For patients with PPP in Cohorts 1 and 2, mean (SD) ppPASI scores were 21.1 (7.0) and 19.2 (5.5), and ANCs were 5.3 × 109/L (1.5) and 4.8 × 109/L (1.9), respectively.

All patients were treated with anumigilimab (Figure S1 and Table S6). Primary endpoints: Safety

Sixteen patients (41.0%) experienced 70 anumigilimab‐related TEAEs, including 7 patients with 14 events of neutropenia [the worst severity was mild (n = 5) and moderate (n = 2)]. No neutropenia was severe, and all neutropenia resolved spontaneously and can be explained by the known pharmacology of anumigilimab. No serious or life‐threatening infection TEAEs were reported, and no association between neutropenia and infections was observed. All neutropenia occurred in Cohort 2; the incidences of other treatment‐related TEAEs were not dose dependent. Other treatment‐related TEAEs occurring in more than 2 patients were fatigue (n = 7), headache (n = 4) and nausea (n = 4).

Moderate neutropenia (four events in two patients) was monitored as an AESI. Another patient experienced moderate pyrexia, which was unrelated to anumigilimab (occurring 12 weeks after the last dose), coincided with an unspecified infection treated with antibiotics and was also considered an AESI.

No anumigilimab‐related serious TEAEs were reported, and there were no deaths. Serious TEAEs, unrelated to anumigilimab, which did not result in treatment discontinuation were experienced by two patients (5.1%). A patient with HS (Cohort 1) experienced two serious TEAEs, breast abscess due to underlying HS on Day 43 (19 days after the second anumigilimab administration) and diverticulitis on Day 125 [40 days after the final (fifth) anumigilimab administration], which resolved on Day 128. A patient with PPP (Cohort 2) experienced a serious TEAE of cholangitis on Day 11 (10 days after the first anumigilimab administration), which resolved on Day 19; the patient had a prior history of recurrent cholangitis, likely due to an iatrogenic injury during a hepaticojejunostomy procedure.

Two patients (diagnosed with HS) discontinued anumigilimab due to one moderate TEAE each (hypersensitivity on Day 22 in Cohort 1, neutropenia on Day 55 in Cohort 2). Both TEAEs were treatment related and resolved. Another three patients, who withdrew consent, had unresolved TEAEs (none related to treatment) at the time of study discontinuation: eczema (moderate) and pruritus (severe) in a patient with PPP (Cohort 1); thrombocytosis and hidradenitis (both mild) in a patient with HS (Cohort 2); and urinary tract infection (mild) in a patient with HS (Cohort 2) (Figure S1). Three patients (7.7%) experienced TEAEs that led to temporary interruption of anumigilimab, all of which resolved: oropharyngeal pain and pyrexia (both mild) in a patient with HS (Cohort 1); COVID‐19 (moderate) in a patient with PPP (Cohort 2); and neutropenia (moderate) in a patient with PPP (Cohort 2).

Secondary endpoints: Pharmacokinetics and immunogenicity

Serum PK parameters were generally similar in patients with HS and PPP (Figure 1). Serum anumigilimab concentrations peaked 1 h after starting the first infusion and decreased to very low or undetectable levels by Day 21. Increase in Cmax was dose‐proportional: after the first dose, mean (SD) Cmax was 8.6 μg/mL (2.7) and 17.6 μg/mL (3.9) in Cohorts 1 and 2, respectively. Increase in AUClast was greater than dose‐proportional: after the first dose, mean (SD) AUClast was 686 h*μg/mL (252) and 2363 h*μg/mL (785) in Cohorts 1 and 2, respectively. Based on AUCtau ratio of the last to first dose, no or minimal accumulation was observed in Cohort 1 (1.05, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.84–1.32) and Cohort 2 (1.61, 95% CI: 1.26–2.05). Presence of antidrug antibodies was negligible.

Exploratory endpoints: Pharmacodynamics and signal‐finding observations of clinical response

Consistent with the PK and pharmacology of anumigilimab, PD activity was dose‐dependent, as measured by serum G‐CSF concentrations and ANC (Figure 2). In Cohort 1, G‐CSF concentrations increased after the first dose and then decreased to close to the baseline value before the second anumigilimab infusion, with a similar pattern after subsequent infusions (Figure 2a,b). In Cohort 2, G‐CSF concentrations followed a similar pattern to Cohort 1, although concentrations were generally higher and more sustained until the next infusion. Since serum G‐CSF at these concentrations is primarily cleared by binding to G‐CSFRs, serum G‐CSF is a good surrogate for receptor occupancy. Notably, the trend of ANC reduction (Figure 2c,d) inversely correlated with increases in serum concentrations of anumigilimab (Figure 1) and G‐CSF (Figure 2a,b). Reductions in ANC were generally greater in magnitude and more sustained until the next infusion in Cohort 2 than in Cohort 1 (Figure 2c,d).

DISCUSSION

In this phase 1b study, no serious safety concerns were observed following anumigilimab administration to patients with HS or PPP. Administration was generally well tolerated. The observed neutropenia TEAEs resolved spontaneously and can be attributed to the mechanism of action of anumigilimab (i.e. inhibition of G‐CSF signalling via antagonistic binding to G‐CSFRs). No neutropenia was severe, no serious or life‐threatening infection TEAEs were reported, and no association between neutropenia and infections was observed. These findings are notable, given that infection risk increases considerably when neutropenia is severe. 32 These safety results in patients with HS or PPP are consistent with the lack of increase in susceptibility to infections, and no impact on neutrophil function, with anumigilimab in the phase 1a placebo‐controlled trial in healthy participants (unpublished data) and in non‐human primates. 25 Taken together, these findings suggest that risk of infection may not be a major safety concern.

Following the first and last doses, mean Cmax increased in a dose‐proportional manner; the increase in mean AUClast was greater than dose proportional. There was no and minimal accumulation of anumigilimab in Cohorts 1 and 2, respectively. The dose‐dependent PD findings, comprising increases in G‐CSF concentrations and decreases in ANC, are consistent with the pharmacology of anumigilimab. The PD and PK profiles of anumigilimab are similar for patients with HS and PPP, and consistent with previous results, including in healthy participants (unpublished data) and non‐human primates. 25 , 26 Furthermore, trends towards improvements in HiSCR50, IHS4 and ppPASI clinical responses were observed; however, no firm conclusions can be reached regarding efficacy, largely due to the signal‐finding exploratory nature of the endpoints, lack of placebo control, small sample size and imbalanced baseline disease characteristics (due to the non‐randomized study design).

In this study, the observed increase in circulating G‐CSF levels is likely due to blockade of the G‐CSFR by anumigilimab, thus inhibiting G‐CSF clearance. 33 , 34 This raises questions regarding potential compensatory myelopoietic feedback over longer treatment durations (15 weeks in the current study) and the potential development of tachyphylaxis, although future studies with longer treatment durations will be needed to explore these questions adequately.

Although the study demonstrated that anumigilimab can significantly reduce circulatory neutrophil levels in HS and PPP, presumably by decreasing the production and egress of neutrophils from bone marrow, 20 , 26 the effect on neutrophils localized to dermatological tissues is not completely clear and requires additional research. Circulatory G‐CSF levels positively correlate with HS severity, and G‐CSF is highly expressed in HS lesions. 26 Anumigilimab inhibits G‐CSF‐mediated increase in genes that promote neutrophil migration, thus G‐CSFR blockade may be expected to reduce neutrophil recruitment in skin lesions. 26

HS and PPP were both investigated in this study, despite their distinct clinical features and partially divergent inflammatory pathways, because both diseases are characterized by prominent neutrophilic infiltration and elevated expression of neutrophil‐active cytokines. 13 , 14 , 15 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 35 Blockade of the G‐CSFR is expected to operate through a shared proximal mechanism in both conditions—namely, attenuation of neutrophil mobilization to and activation in inflamed tissue.

This phase 1b, open‐label, non‐randomized study should be interpreted in relation to several limitations and caveats. More patients with severe HS were included in the lower‐dose cohort than in the higher‐dose cohort, potentially affecting outcomes, particularly clinical responses. Analysis of outcomes by baseline factor, such as disease severity, is beyond the scope of this small study, in which the sample size was based on empirical evidence to evaluate neutropenia risk and not powered to assess efficacy. In addition, the absence of placebo limits comparisons. Thus, efficacy endpoints were exploratory, conducted as a signal‐finding exercise, and should be interpreted with caution. Naturally, safety and efficacy will need to be evaluated in larger placebo‐controlled studies. The dose‐dependent pharmacology reported in this study can support biomarker‐driven adaptive selection of doses for optimizing safety and efficacy in future studies.

Regarding generalizability, the study included predominantly White participants. HS is more prevalent in people of African descent. 8 Moreover, ANCs tend to be lower in people of African descent; 36 , 37 thus, clinical investigation of anumigilimab's safety and efficacy in people of diverse racial and ethnic backgrounds would be beneficial, although this was beyond the scope of this phase 1b study. It is also noteworthy that most study participants were current or former smokers, a risk factor for HS and PPP. 38 , 39 Smoking is associated with neutrophil accumulation in the lungs and disruption to neutrophilic activity. 40 It would be beneficial to determine the degree to which smoking affects HS and PPP pathogenesis and treatment outcomes.

In conclusion, in this phase 1b study, anumigilimab therapy was not associated with serious safety concerns in patients with HS or PPP. All neutropenia resolved spontaneously. Reductions in neutrophil count and increased serum G‐CSF reflect the mechanism of action of anumigilimab and demonstrate the dose‐dependent pharmacology of anumigilimab in patients with HS and PPP. PK parameters indicate dose‐dependent increases in exposure, which correlate with these PD findings. Signal‐finding exploratory analyses of clinical response suggest potential for therapeutic efficacy in HS and PPP. Based on these and previous findings, further investigation of anumigilimab as a treatment for neutrophilic skin diseases in randomized placebo‐controlled studies is warranted.
