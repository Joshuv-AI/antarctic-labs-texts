All hands on DEK: structural insights into a unique histone modifier and chromatin remodeler
Authors: E. J. Peters, K. L. Gardner, L. M. Privette Vinnedge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
DEK is a chromatin-associated, DNA-binding protein with unique properties that place it in its own protein class. First discovered in 1992 as a fusion protein in acute myeloid leukemia, DEK gained further attention in the mid-2000s as a growing number of studies identified its connections to chromatin architecture and subsequent impact on pathologies such as cancer and autoimmune diseases. Current evidence indicates that DEK can alter the topology of nucleic acids in a variety of biological processes, including DNA replication, DNA repair, chromatin organization, epigenetic modification, transcription, and mRNA splicing. Interestingly, DEK is highly evolutionarily conserved among higher eukaryotes, and this, combined with its involvement in such a diverse array of processes, highlights its biological significance. Interest in DEK is further driven by its status as a potent oncogene, as it is transcriptionally upregulated in most solid tumors tested to date, with high DEK expression correlating with poor survival and more aggressive tumors. Recently, advances in cryogenic electron microscopy have made it possible to visualize the structural basis of DEK-nucleosome interactions, providing concrete mechanistic insights into how DEK influences gene regulation and transcription. These findings clarify prior observations and open new avenues for exploring the biological and clinical relevance of DEK.

Background

Over 30 years ago, a novel gene (DEK) was identified as a new fusion protein partner with CAN (now NUP214) in a patient with a t(6;9) translocation-associated acute myeloid leukemia [1]. Since then, gain or loss of the DEK protein has been implicated in several more pathologies, including nearly all solid tumors [2, 3], multiple autoimmune diseases [4–6], asthma [7], and multiple forms of neurodegeneration, including Tau-associated pathology and Huntington’s disease [8, 9]. Aside from chromosomal translocations, t(6;9) in AML and the t(6;X) DEK-AFF2 gene fusion in sinonasal carcinoma [10, 11], disease-associated mutations have not yet been recorded for DEK. Over a decade after the initial identification of DEK, subsequent sequencing with functional assays [12] and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy [13–15] began to elucidate the structure, and DNA-binding activity, of the DEK protein. Only recently it has been revealed how DEK interacts with DNA and the histone core of the nucleosome, as monomers and dimers, using cryogenic electron microscopy (“cryo-EM”) [16, 17]. Here, we report the history of elucidating the structure and function of the chromatin remodeling DEK protein, both in normal tissue and in the context of fusion proteins and cancer. We also briefly review its role in epigenetic modifications, particularly for histone H3.

DEK: functions, fusions, and phenotypes

DEK is a chromatin-bound non-histone protein that plays a significant role in regulating chromatin dynamics, particularly in stem and progenitor cells [18–23]. DEK contains an N-terminal histone binding region (HBR), and two DNA-binding domains: the central pseudo-SAP/SAP (ΨSAP/SAP) domain and the C-terminal DEK-C domain (discussed in more detail below). It is both abundant in expression and highly conserved across vertebrates [22, 24], arthropods [25, 26], and plants [27, 28], and its absence in lower eukaryotes suggests that it evolved out of necessity to meet the demands for more complex genome organization and regulation (Fig. 1A). DEK participates in a variety of cellular processes, including mRNA splicing [29], DNA repair [30, 31], DNA replication [32, 33], and transcription [34, 35], and has shown a preference for supercoiled and cruciform DNA structures when binding DNA [36, 37]. In addition, DEK is involved in signaling pathways such as both p53-dependent and p53-independent senescence pathways [2, 38, 39], regulation of apoptosis [39, 40], and cell proliferation through Wnt/β-catenin [41], PI3K-Akt-mTOR [42–44], and Rho signaling [45]. DEK is most widely expressed in proliferating cells, such as normal progenitor cells in blood, muscle, and epithelial tissues, as well as cancer cells [20, 21, 41, 46–48]. This is due, in part, because it is an E2F target gene that is upregulated following Rb inhibition [2, 49]. It is predominantly bound to chromatin throughout interphase and parts of mitosis [50] although there are conflicting reports on whether or not DEK levels change significantly throughout the cell cycle [50, 51]. Indeed, originally it was reported that the amount and localization of DEK does not change [51] but one later study observed that DEK levels were dramatically reduced in mitotic cell populations compared to the interphase cells of the same cell line [50]. This study also showed that DEK is dissociated from mitotic chromosomes in prophase through anaphase but re-associates during telophase, and that overexpression and incomplete removal of DEK from chromosomes during mitosis resulted in genetically abnormal daughter cells [50]. This dynamic pattern of DEK localization in mitosis resembles that of many DNA damage repair proteins which are removed from chromatin during mitosis such as Rad51, DNA-PK, FANCD2 and Bloom complex components [52]. DEK has been shown to reside in both euchromatin and heterochromatin (especially facultative heterochromatin), reflecting its roles in both transcriptional activation and repression [16, 51, 53–55]. In proliferating cells there are typically 4–6 million DEK molecules per cell, which is equivalent to one DEK molecule for every two to four nucleosomes [17, 51]. In contrast, DEK production is decreased or absent in mitotic cells, differentiated cells, and cells in quiescence, indicating a potential role in promoting cell cycle entry and cellular functions during G1, S, and G2 phases of the cell cycle [20, 21, 26, 56]. Indeed, cell cycle arrest experiments showed DEK protein levels are moderate in G1 phase, highest in S phase, and decrease in mitosis [50]. This is supported by the finding that DEK is a target gene of the pro-proliferative E2F1 and E2F3 transcription factors, which control S phase entry [49]. Furthermore, in solid tissues, DEK expression is tightly controlled and is upregulated in response to environmental cues that induce stem and progenitor cell proliferation, such as damage, stress, or mitogenic signals [26, 33, 57–59].

The association of DEK with chromatin is regulated by a range of post-translational modifications (PTMs) [22, 60]. DEK is a highly modifiable protein, containing 57 potential sites for phosphorylation and over 70 lysine residues with the potential to be poly ADP-ribosylated or ubiquitinated [58, 60–63]. Biochemically, DEK has no known enzymatic activity [55] but does form multimers [12]. Overall, it is an acidic protein, with four separate and distinct long patches of aspartic/glutamic acid residues, although this contrasts with an abundance of basic amino acids in the DNA binding domains [12, 22, 64].

Dysregulation of DEK expression, or structural disruption via the formation of fusion genes, is associated with stem cell dysfunction and cancers, including acute myeloid leukemia. DEK was first identified in 1992 as part of a DEK-NUP214 (nucleoporin 214, previously known as CAN) fusion protein resulting from a t(6;9) chromosomal translocation in a subtype of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [1]. The gene name DEK was conferred based on the patient’s initials (patient “DK”) from which this fusion protein was characterized [1]. DEK has since continued to be implicated in solid tumors [2, 48, 65, 66], neurodegenerative diseases [8, 67], and autoimmune disorders [3, 5, 6, 68]. Today, DEK is a bona fide oncoprotein based on substantial evidence implicating it in tumor-promoting activity. Indeed, the DEK-NUP214 (DEK-CAN) t(6;9) fusion gene/protein induced leukemic transformation in both murine and human hematopoietic stem cells [69, 70]. Although the molecular pathogenesis of DEK-NUP214 is still largely unknown, DEK-NUP214 was shown to increase myeloid cell proliferation through the upregulation of mTOR and constitutive activation of JAK2 and thus STAT3 and STAT5 [69, 71]. Recently, DEK-NUP214 was identified as an XPO1-dependent transcriptional activator of key leukemia drivers [72]. Together, these studies indicate an oncogenic function of DEK-NUP214 in contributing to leukemic phenotypes. DEK is nearly entirely conserved in DEK-NUP214, thus making the DEK portion of the protein responsible for its cellular localization. NUP214, usually associated with nuclear pores in the perinuclear region, was found to be mis-localized in the nucleoplasm as the DEK-NUP214 fusion formed distinct nuclear speckles. While DEK-NUP214 retained the chromatin association of full-length DEK, it lost the ability to interact with histone marker H3K9me3, indicating that DEK-NUP214 interrupts some molecular interactions of DEK [73].

More recently, a second DEK fusion gene was identified, the DEK-AFF2 fusion caused by a chromosomal translocation between chromosomes 6 and X, t(6;X). It results in a fusion that consists of the first seven exons of DEK and exons 9–21 of AFF2 (also known as FMR2, Fragile X/FRAXE-associated Mental Retardation 2 protein, Fig. 1B) [74, 75]. This fusion gene is most frequently found in nonkeratinizing squamous cell carcinomas (SCC), particularly the sinonasal forms of head and neck SCCs [75], but also was reported in SCCs of the middle ear [76] and larynx [77] as well as lung carcinomas [74]. The DEK-AFF2 fusion protein localizes to the nucleus [78], as expected since DEK binds to chromatin and AFF2 is a transcription activator that is also implicated in mRNA splicing [79, 80]. DEK-AFF2 positive SCCs have a 50% recurrence rate, are typically negative for tumor-associated viruses, are associated with papillary growth and have an inflammatory, neutrophil-rich tumor microenvironment [81, 82]. DEK-AFF2 positive tumors are still under-studied and only at the characterization stages of discovery, with little-to-no information known about the oncogenic, and transforming, activity of the fusion protein and its downstream molecular consequences.

The transcriptional overexpression of full-length (non-translocation) DEK is found in a majority of solid tumors, including cervical cancer [83], melanoma [38, 84], colorectal [85, 86], breast cancer [41, 48, 87, 88], and esophageal/oral squamous cell carcinoma [47, 89]. However, DEK overexpression in retinoblastoma often is due to gene amplification on 6p [66, 90]. Specific molecular mechanisms contributing to tumorigenesis vary by tissue type, but generally high DEK expression will inhibit apoptosis and cellular senescence pathways while promoting proliferation, cancer stem cell maintenance, cell mobility and metastasis in vitro [2, 24, 39, 41, 48]. Loss of DEK expression in both xenograft and genetically engineered DEK knockout mouse models of tumor formation and progression have resulted in an inhibition of oncogenic traits in vivo [21, 24, 41, 89]. However, using genetically engineered transgenic mouse models of DEK over-expression, it was shown that high DEK protein levels tend to be tumor-promoting, not tumor initiating, requiring other tumor initiating mutations for its oncogenic activity [21, 89]. Structure-function studies are limited, and none are available that specifically investigate which regions are necessary for the oncogenic functions of DEK. The ΨSAP-SAP region (residues 87–187) are necessary for the ability of DEK to induce supercoils, which is described in more detail below, but how this region contributes to DEK-associated oncogenic cellular phenotypes is unknown [12]. However, the two cancer-associated DEK fusion genes may provide clues for future studies (Fig. 1B). The DEK-NUP214 fusion retains nearly the entire DEK gene, up to the end of exon 9, such that the resulting protein contains amino acid residues 1-349, therefore missing only 26 amino acids [1]. It is fused to amino acids 813–2090 of NUP214 [1, 91]. As previously mentioned, the fusion retains DEK-mediated chromatin localization and induces leukemic transformation [92]. The DEK-AFF2 fusion includes DEK exons 1–7, resulting in the inclusion of DEK amino acid residues 1-254 in the resulting fusion protein [75, 93]. This leaves intact the N-terminal histone binding region (HBR), the ΨSAP-SAP DNA-binding domain, the nuclear localization signal, most of the RNA binding domain, and multiple acidic regions in DEK that are fused to AFF2, while completely losing the DEK-C domain. Combined, this suggests that the N-terminal HBR and the ΨSAP-SAP DNA-binding domain are likely essential for the oncogenic activity of DEK and that the C-terminal DEK-C DNA-binding domain is likely dispensable for oncogenic activity.

DEK is a unique and highly conserved protein with multiple DNA binding regions

DEK is structurally unique to the extent that it constitutes its own protein class. Spanning 375 amino acid residues, human DEK contains three DNA-binding sites in two main regions (Fig. 1). Perhaps the most substantial of these is its first DNA-binding domain, the SAP (SAF-A/B, acinus and PIAS) motif in its central region, which is preceded by a pseudo-SAP (ΨSAP) motif [12, 13, 16, 64, 94]. The ΨSAP motif is structurally similar to the SAP motif but lacks any sequence similarity [13]. Forming a globular ΨSAP/SAP" domain (residues 78–188 [16]) together, this region appears to be the primary driver of the ability of DEK to introduce positive supercoils into DNA and induce intermolecular interactions [94]. Interestingly, DEK appears to fold DNA into supercoils without consuming energy, meaning it is more likely that passive structural interactions are driving this activity [12, 36]. The SAP motif (also known as a “SAF-box”), a highly conserved and founding feature of a diverse group of chromatin-associated proteins [95], is the only domain of DEK that shows homology to other proteins. However, the central location of the SAP domain of DEK differs from other SAP-containing proteins, where the motif is located either in the far N- or C-terminus. The central ΨSAP/SAP combination is also a feature unique to DEK [12, 13, 36]. As seen in other SAP-containing proteins, the ΨSAP/SAP domain (hereafter SAP domain) of DEK binds naked DNA weakly on its own yet gains high-affinity DNA interactions when DEK is part of large nucleo-protein complexes like the nucleosome [16, 36, 64]. ΨSAP and SAP motifs create a unique arrangement of two helix-extended loop-helix (HEH) subdomains [13, 94]. The DEK–DNA interaction is currently thought to be generated through hydrogen bonding between the side chains of DEK and phosphate backbone of DNA [94]. This interaction may be supported by the stabilization of the two HEH motifs [13, 64], which fold together in a distinct globular five-helix bundle structure that has not been reported in other proteins [13, 16]. This helical bundle consists of the ΨSAP (α − helices 1–3) and SAP folds (α − helices 4 and 5), and DNA binding has been shown to occur on the α1–3 of ΨSAP and α5 of SAP [13, 16, 17, 94]. All the α − helices are related by a plane of symmetry that efficiently binds DNA [16, 17]. It is also worth noting that a sixth helix is predicted to be present within this structure in AlphaFold, although this remains to be proven experimentally.

The second DNA-binding region spans residues 309–375 located within DEK’s C-terminal region [12, 16, 17, 96]. This DEK-C domain seems to be more involved in stabilizing DNA interactions and modulating the SAP box’s activity through phosphorylation [12]. Structurally, DEK-C folds into a three-helix bundle with structural homology to the winged-helix motif, although DEK-C binds dsDNA through a different mechanism compared with the winged-helix motif [96]. These interactions between DEK and nuclear DNA are predominantly based on structure rather than sequence [36]. However, there are limited studies claiming that DEK can bind specific DNA sequences. For instance, DEK was observed to bind the HIV-2 peri-ets (pets) site during an HIV-2 infection [97], but no other instances of sequence-specific DNA binding have been reported yet. There is strong sequence conservation across species of both the ΨSAP/SAP and DEK-C domains, as well as no known DEK paralogs [22], indicating that there is tight evolutionary pressure on the unique DNA-binding regions of the DEK gene (Fig. 1A).

It has also been observed that 10% of a given DEK population is associated with cellular RNA [51]. Both the DEK-C domain and a lysine and arginine-rich intrinsically disordered region (IDR) (residues 187–270) have been identified as RNA binding domains that can interact with many RNA biotypes, including rRNA, which links DEK to ribosome assembly [98, 99]. Although the distinction between these two RNA binding regions is still not fully clear, the IDR is implicated in modulating ribosome biosynthesis-related pathways through splicing-related events on coding mRNA [99]. In contrast, the DEK-C domain contains a poly(A)-binding domain, so it may potentially regulate mRNA stability, export, and/or translational initiation [7, 99]. DEK also contains an N-terminal disordered tail (NDT) (residues 1–78) and a linker region connecting the globular SAP and C-terminal domains, both of which are in need of further mechanical dissection [13, 16]. The RNA binding activity of DEK also has led to its identification as an mRNA splicing factor [29, 98, 100–102].

Recently, two groups independently identified a histone-binding region (HBR) within the disordered tail of DEK at residues 52–68, which interacts with the histone octamer surface, including the acidic patch [16, 17]. In humans, DEK isoform 2, is a short splicing isoform lacking residues 49–82, resulting in loss of the HBR, that localizes to the nucleolus rather than the broad nuclear localization of isoform 1 [103, 104]. The overexpression of isoform 2 does not compensate for the loss of isoform 1, especially during DEK-mediated DNA damage responses to doxorubicin [103]. This highlights the important role the HBR plays in chromatin binding, which, by extension, indirectly supports the positive DNA supercoiling activity of the SAP domain of DEK [17, 37]. The HBR segment is conserved from plant to human, indicating a potentially similar mode of interaction across species (Fig. 1) [17]. A study performed in the plant Arabidopsis thaliana on one of its DEK isoforms, AtDEK3, revealed that it has histone H3/H4 binding sites at the N- and C-termini and DNA/nucleosome-binding domains in the SAP domain and at the C-terminus, DEK-C domain, showing considerable homology with human DEK [105]. In summary, the HBR, along with the two DNA-binding modules within the SAP domain and DEK-C domain, are the key regions for normal DEK function. Of these, the most important appears to be the SAP domain, as a small fragment of DEK (residues 87–187) containing the SAP domain was observed to be sufficient to introduce supercoils and induce intermolecular interactions between two or more DNA molecules, whereas DEK fragments without the region between amino acids 87 and 187 could not form these DNA-mediated clusters, meaning the SAP domain is most responsible for the core chromatin architectural activity of DEK. However, it is important to note that only full length DEK is able to bend DNA, highlighting the crucial auxiliary roles of the HBR and DEK-C domain in tandem with the SAP domain that allow DEK to localize to chromatin and bind nucleosomes [16, 60].

Multiple models for how DEK regulates chromatin structure

DEK’s involvement in the regulation of chromatin structure is thought to be able to occur in three different ways. Direct interactions have been confirmed with nucleosomes and there is evidence that supports their indirect interaction as a linker protein with other linker proteins. The third potential model is speculation that the DEK interactions with chromatin are facilitated through RNA-mediated mechanisms by the RNA interacting domain in DEK [94].

In the “linker protein” model, which involves factors binding at the entry/exit site of the nucleosomal DNA, such proteins are involved in keeping the nucleosomal gate in a more “open” and “locked” conformation. When compared to other linker proteins, DEK structure and/or nucleosome binding properties seem to resemble High-Mobility Group (HMG) proteins, a class of well-characterized chromatin architectural proteins, and the linker histone H1. Indeed, both HMG proteins and DEK prefer binding to distorted and cruciform DNA structures [36], and both can introduce supercoils into DNA. Additionally, they can both directly interact with nucleosomal DNA and appear to be similarly abundant within a cell. However, DEK (43 KDa) is larger than the canonical HMG proteins (10–30 kDa), separating it from the “high mobility group” of proteins that migrate faster in gel electrophoresis. Furthermore, the central SAP domain that serves as a primary DNA-binding module and defining structural characteristic of DEK differentiates it from all three major families of HMG proteins, which each have their own distinct DNA-binding and structural motifs [106]. Interestingly, one of these families, the HMGB family, contains long rows of acidic amino acids in their carboxy-terminal regions, just as DEK has similar acidic regions [107, 108]. Taken together, the behavioral similarities between DEK and HMG proteins imply that DEK could at least partly function as a linker protein that binds at the entry/exit site of nucleosomal DNA, regulating chromatin structure in a similar, yet distinctly different way than HMG proteins. Future interactome studies could continue to reveal which transcription factors serve as interaction partners for each family of chromatin architectural proteins to further clarify similarities and differences in functional roles [16, 55, 109].

In the “direct interaction” model, the nucleosome can form a complex with either one or two DEK molecules (DEK1-nucleosome and DEK2-nucleosome respectively), and these two formations differ slightly from each other in function due to their different structures [16, 17]. In both DEK-nucleosome complexes, DEK’s ΨSAP/SAP domain binds DNA in a tripartite form, interacting with the dyad DNA and two linker DNAs of the nucleosome (Fig. 2A, B, top) [16]. In the DEK2-nucleosome structure, the two ΨSAP/SAP domains in a DEK dimer sit nearly symmetrically on the nucleosomal dyad DNA and adopt a dual-tripartite DNA-binding form (Fig. 2C) [16]. These two DEK SAP domains do contact each other, but Kujirai et al. found that these interactions do not appear to be critical for DEK nucleosome binding, as a mobility shift assay showed that replacing residues at the major DEK-DEK contact site between SAP domains (residues 102–105) with alanine did not reduce DEK’s efficiency in forming DEK-nucleosome complexes compared to wild-type DEK [16].

Using similar methods, DEK mutants lacking the HBR (residues 52–68) were substantially deficient in nucleosome binding ability [16, 17]. Likewise, DEK mutants lacking SAP domain residues exhibited highly reduced nucleosome binding. In contrast, deleting the DEK-C domain (residues 309–375) did not affect nucleosome binding, suggesting a more auxiliary role [17]. Strikingly, a DEK mutant containing only the HBR and SAP domain (residues 49–199) was unable to stably associate with the nucleosome [17]. This suggests that additional regions within DEK contribute to nucleosome binding.

The segment immediately following the ΨSAP/SAP domain (residues 195–222) was recently identified to contain a lysine-rich motif (LRM), which is made up of 43% basic lysine and arginine residues. The LRM segment likely interacts with the positively charged residues in linker DNA, as DEK ΔLRM exhibited reduced nucleosome complex formation and DEK ΔHBR + LRM could not form a complex at all [17]. Fluorescence polarization assays also showed that DEK ΔLRM had substantially reduced affinity for double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) compared to wild-type DEK, whereas the binding affinity for dsDNA of a shortened DEK fragment (residues 1–222, containing HBR, SAP domain, and LRM segment) was less altered. Taken together, these data indicate that DEK strongly associates with the nucleosome through interactions mainly involving the HBR, SAP domain, and LRM segment [17].

Certain residues of DEK have been identified as being especially important in binding the nucleosome and are highly conserved across species. Specifically, there are three points at which DEK binds to DNA. Within SAP, the K124 and K125 residues interact with one linker DNA and the R153, K158, and R168 residues interact with the other. These interactions are the same in both DEKs in a DEK2-nucleosome structure. The R107, K111, R116, and K144 residues of DEK interact with the DNA backbone on the dyad DNA, and the other DEK binds to the opposite DNA strand in the same manner in a DEK2-nucleosome structure [16]. Although the DEK-DNA interactions are mostly conserved in the DEK1-nucleosome complex, the DEK molecule bound to the dyad DNA in the DEK2-nucleosome complex is tilted by 22º relative to its orientation in the DEK1-nucleosome complex [16].

DEK bends linker DNA to increase chromatin compaction

DEK is not the only protein that adopts tripartite DNA binding in the nucleosome. Linker histone H1, another major nucleosome-binding protein, also specifically binds to the nucleosome through tripartite DNA binding (Fig. 2A, B, bottom) [110, 111]. However, what makes DEK truly unique is that it binds the acidic patch simultaneously with the dyad and two linker DNAs, which is a nucleosome-binding mode not observed in any other nucleosome-binding protein. The DEK2-nucleosome complex can compact the chromatin formation by substantially bending linker DNA in a way that has not been reported in any other protein-nucleosome complex, making this another remarkable trait unique to DEK (Fig. 2C) [16, 17]. Linker histones like H1 also mediate chromatin compaction by bending the linker DNA (Fig. 2B, bottom), but the DEK2-nucleosome complex bends linker DNAs at a sharper angle and in the opposite direction of the bending by the H1-nucleosome complex (Fig. 2B, C). The DEK1-nucleosome complex orients linker DNA orientation at an intermediate angle between those of the H1-nucleosome and DEK2-nucleosome complexes [16].

Linker DNA is thought to have inherent flexibility in a free nucleosome, as its structure has been observed in multiple orientations. In contrast, DEK-nucleosome complexes have not been observed to take on multiple orientations, suggesting that DEK reduces the flexibility of linker DNA [16]. Additionally, DEK binding reportedly induces positive supercoils to chromatin [55], which, along with how dramatically two DEK molecules bend linker DNA, implies that topological changes in the nucleosomal DNA may be occurring because of DEK binding. It is also worth mentioning there are approximately 50–130% more linker histones than there are nucleosomes within a cell, so it is possible that DEK may be working in cooperation, or competition, with these linker histones [16, 112, 113].

DEK influences transcription through epigenetic changes in the chromatin

Epigenetic mechanism to control chromatin organization and accessibility include DNA methylation, histone post-translational modifications, and alternative histone usage. DEK has been reported to restrict incorporation of the histone H3 variant, H3.3, in a process that is thought to require transcriptionally active chromatin [114–116]. This implies DEK could be able to suppress transcription by promoting a suppressive chromatin conformation in certain scenarios. The role of DEK in H3.3 deposition differs depending on cellular and genomic contexts. In non-telomeric chromatin, DEK prevents HIRA from broadly depositing H3.3 in human mesenchymal stem cells (Fig. 3A, right). Upon siRNA knockdown of DEK, DNA topology was more relaxed and HIRA-dependent H3.3 deposition was observed to be promoted. However, using mouse embryonic stem cells, DEK was found to be essential in guiding ATRX and DAXX to load H3.3 onto the telomeric nucleosomes through promyelocytic leukemia (PML) nuclear bodies (NBs; Fig. 3A left). DEK was also found to physically interact with DAXX [114, 117]. Loss of DEK in this scenario results in the displacement of ATRX and PML NBs and impairment of H3.3 loading, creating a fragile telomere phenotype [114]. DEK functioning as a histone H3.3 chaperone has also been reported in Arabidopsis and Drosophila [115, 116].

There is evidence to suggest that DEK influences transcription by maintaining histone marks that stabilize the chromatin state at its binding sites. Several reports have indicated that DEK helps to regulate histone H3 post-translational modification. For example, DEK was shown to support histone H3 trimethylation on residue lysine 9 (H3K9me3) in Drosophila [25]. Similarly, DEK was reported to colocalize with, or promote, histone H3 lysine 4 trimethylation (H3K4me3) and the histone H3 lysine acetylation marks H3K9ac and H3K27ac [17, 118, 119], which are associated with gene activation and euchromatin. Despite this link with H3K27ac, several genome-wide analyses have revealed that DEK colocalizes with, or promotes the deposition of, facultative heterochromatin marks histone H2A lysine 119 ubiquitination (H2AK119ub), which primes the nucleosome for histone H3 lysine 27 trimethylation (H3K27me3) [16, 17, 21], which are associated with gene silencing. This suggests that DEK may broadly support H3 post-translational modifications, not just a single epigenetic mark. DEK could theoretically be suppressing gene transcription by preserving histone marks like H3K27me3 at certain loci and forming a suppressive chromatin confirmation with less accessibility for transcription machinery. This is supported by the discovery that DEK is a modifier of EZH2 methyltransferase activity, which upregulates H3K27me3 levels as a part of the Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2), and DEK can interact with PRC2 complex members as determined by immunoprecipitation techniques (Fig. 3B) [16, 17, 21]. This was further supported by showing a substantial decrease in H3K27me3 with DEK loss in both human MCF10A cells and mammary glands from Dek knockout mice while H3K27me3 was upregulated in Dek transgenic mice. This was correlated with differential expression of genes controlling the cell cycle and mammary stem and progenitor cells [21]. Similarly, DEK promoting H3K27me3 epigenetic marks was shown using cell free systems, co-localization by ChIP-seq at development- and differentiation-related genes in murine neural progenitor and ES cell models [16, 17]. Collectively, these studies indicate that DEK modulates PRC2 activity and H3K27me3 deposition to regulate expression of cell cycle, cell fate, and differentiation genes in stem and progenitor cells. It is also worth noting that histone methylation, especially on histone H3, is both dynamic and tightly regulated during development, playing an important role in establishing cell lineages, body patterning, and the development of specific organs [120]. Questions regarding the mechanisms by which DEK promotes histone H3 methylation, and the biological processes wherein this is important, have not been investigated extensively enough.

Conversely, DEK may assist in activating the transcription of certain genes by fostering a more accessible chromatin conformation through histone acetylation. Shen and colleagues conducted a chromatin profiling assay on genes observed to be downregulated upon Dek knockout (Dek-KO) in mouse embryonic stem cells and found that H3K9ac levels were reduced at these genetic loci, suggesting that DEK can sustain activation of certain genes by supporting acetylation [17]. Similarly, another study quantified chromatin marker levels in mouse and human breast cancer stem cells and reported that H3K9ac levels decreased after DEK knockdown and increased after DEK rescue [56]. However, there are also reports that DEK restrains acetylation in other contexts, such as a report that DEK can inhibit histone acetylation in mouse hematopoietic stem cells by recruiting the co-repressor NCoR1 and inhibiting the histone acetyltransferases p300 and PCAF [44]. In another report that investigated the role of DEK in neuronal function, DEK deficient primary mouse neurons cultured in vitro demonstrated an increase in acetylation of lysine 36 of histone H3 (H3K36ac), increasing activity-induced transcription and neuronal excitability [67]. This indicated that DEK suppresses histone H3 acetylation, leaving these residues available for methylation. Collectively, the impact of DEK on nucleosome topology and histone PTMs resulted in changes to transcription factor binding, such as blocking RelA/p65 [121, 122] and promoting C/EBPα binding [123].

Together, there are conflicting reports about DEK and the methylation versus acetylation status of histone H3, particularly at K9 and K27 (Fig. 3B). Notably, each of the reports was conducted in different animal models and/or tissues. This could indicate that DEK either (1) facilitates the accessibility of the histone H3 N-terminal tail for any epigenetic modification, (2) (de)stabilizes the existing PTMs at a genetic locus, (3) functions as a co-factor to locally regulate methyltransferase or acetylase enzymatic activity, or (4) functions in an organism- and tissue-specific manner to direct the expression of cell cycle and differentiation genes. Higher resolution studies that identify specific genes with altered transcription, in the presence or absence of DEK, and comparisons of how DEK modulates histone PTMs in different tissue types and organisms, are needed to understand this variance in DEK behavior. Perhaps there are tissue-specific functions of DEK that relate to the presence and concentration of various cofactors, such as NCoR1 and EZH2, in different tissues. Additional biochemical analyses are also needed to elucidate how DEK interacts with H3 and various demethylases, methyltransferases, acetyltransferases, and deacetylases to understand the mechanisms driving DEK-mediated epigenetic modifications. Importantly, this will support a fuller comprehension of how DEK impacts epigenetic modifications and transcriptional outcomes in a cell.

Conclusions and future directions

Future studies providing a comprehensive functional characterization of the conserved DEK-C domain and the functional distinctions and similarities between DEK and H1 in heterochromatin formation are essential to better understand the role of DEK in oncogenesis. Molecular details of how DEK interacts with cellular RNA also remain yet understudied. It is possible that DEK functions may show broad impacts on coding mRNA with regards to splicing, intron retention, alternative exon usage, and on non-coding RNAs for functions like ribosome biogenesis.

As for most of the targets, PTMs of DEK like phosphorylation, poly ADP-ribosylation, and ubiquitination alter its ability to bind chromatin, re-localize, and carry out specific biological functions, especially under stress conditions. This impact remains still poorly understood as primarily studied in cancer cells, where DEK is over-expressed. Additional work is therefore needed to better understand how these PTMs influence the chromatin-binding and DNA-bending properties of DEK, its stability, and its non-chromatin remodeling functions. The subsequent impact of post-translationally modified DEK on cellular functions is also under-studied.

A common observation across the literature is that DEK, through its broad associations with chromatin, plays some sort of role in regulating stem cell fate decisions in various tissues. In one study, hematopoietic stem cells with DEK deleted displayed an impaired self-renewal capacity, and there was evidence to suggest that DEK promotes self-renewal through modulating chromatin accessibility and histone PTMs [44]. In another study, quiescent breast cancer stem cells were shown to become reactivated through a DEK-dependent mechanism in which DEK facilitates genome-wide accessibility of chromatin [56]. Interestingly, in cases such as this one, where DEK seems to promote exit from quiescence and activation of the cell cycle, it would be essential that the cell is able to upregulate the production of nucleotides and amino acids to double their mass in preparation for mitosis. In line with this, some studies have connected DEK to upregulated expression of metabolic genes, such as a report that DEK overexpression in epithelial cells reprograms metabolism to support macromolecule synthesis [124]. Understanding the relationship between DEK expression and metabolism remains an exciting new area of research, especially in the context of cancer.

There appears to be a greater biological requirement for DEK in proliferating cells that is not yet fully understood. This phenomenon was exemplified in several studies where upon the loss of DEK in rapidly proliferating cancer cells increased cellular susceptibility to genotoxic and cytotoxic chemotherapies, as well as endocrine therapies [31, 38, 88, 125, 126], which suggests that dividing cancer cells are dependent upon high levels of DEK expression. Noncancerous proliferating progenitor cells, such as muscle satellite cells, exhibit similar behavior, such that DEK-deficient cells have a limited ability to exit quiescence [20, 26]. Thus, DEK seems to be essential for supporting proliferation, potentially through its epigenetic functions to regulate gene expression.

Collectively, the literature on DEK portrays it as a uniquely versatile chromatin architectural protein that plays its own role in shaping the epigenetic landscape. By forming complexes with the nucleosome and bending linker DNA while interacting with histones, DEK can alter nucleosome topology and regulate the physical accessibility of chromatin. Its mechanism of action also appears to be intricate and fine-tuned, as it has been observed modulating various histone PTMs under different biological contexts and can be heavily modified itself. Most cells require different chromatin states that are reinforced depending on their current needs, and chromatin remodeling proteins like DEK allow for transcriptional activation or repression to be sustained at different times. This activity explains why DEK is involved in so many processes, and its evolutionary conservation is evidence of its essentiality in more complex organisms like metazoans and plants. Future work that further clarifies the context-dependent functionality of DEK will be the next step in understanding the true nature and extent of its influence on chromatin.
