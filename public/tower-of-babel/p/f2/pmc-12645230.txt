Biocontrol of Root‐Knot Nematodes via siRNA‐Loaded Extracellular Vesicles From a Nematophagous Fungus Arthrobotrys oligospora
Authors: Xinyi Huang, Wenliang Zhou, Xiaoying Liu, Ke‐Qin Zhang, Juan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRoot‐knot nematodes (Meloidogyne spp.) represent a major threat to global crop production, and current chemical nematicides pose serious environmental and health risks. RNA interference (RNAi) offers a promising gene‐specific strategy for nematode control. However, the efficient and sustainable delivery of RNA molecules into nematodes remains a significant challenge. In this study, we developed an innovative RNA delivery platform using extracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from the nematode‐trapping fungus Arthrobotrys oligospora. EVs were either exogenously loaded with synthetic siRNAs targeting the Mi‐flp‐18 gene of M. incognita or harvested from engineered fungal strains expressing short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) or double‐stranded RNAs (dsRNAs) against multiple nematode neuropeptide genes (flp and nlp families). The engineered EVs efficiently delivered RNA cargos into nematodes, leading to significant downregulation of target gene expression. Functional assays and greenhouse experiments revealed the biocontrol potential of the engineered fungal strains, with reductions in nematode motility, root invasion and infectivity. This is the first demonstration in a nematophagous fungus that EVs can serve as effective RNA delivery vehicles for the control of root‐knot nematodes. The use of engineered A. oligospora strains provides a scalable, eco‐friendly alternative to synthetic delivery systems and transgenic crops. Our findings establish fungal EVs as a powerful tool in cross‐kingdom RNAi applications and open new avenues for sustainable pest management in agriculture.Keywords: Arthrobotrys oligospora, biocontrol, cross‐kingdom communication, extracellular vesicles, Meloidogyne incognita, neuropeptides, RNA interference

Introduction

Root‐knot nematodes (Meloidogyne spp.), one of the highly adaptive obligate plant parasitic nematodes (PPNs), cause a global production loss of US$100 billion every year (Zhao et al. 2020). They penetrate plant roots using stylets and secrete cell wall–degrading enzymes such as cellulases and pectinases to form specialised feeding sites (giant cells) (Coyne et al. 2018; De Waele and Elsen 2007; Pulavarty et al. 2021; Tileubayeva et al. 2021), thereby disrupting host metabolism and facilitating nutrient uptake (Rutter et al. 2022; Siddique et al. 2015). In addition, the wounds they create increase plant susceptibility to secondary pathogens, aggravating yield losses (Zou et al. 2024). Although chemical nematicides remain the most effective management in PPNs control, the long‐term overuse of chemical nematicides not only resulted in serious resistance and low control efficacy, but also posed safety risks to food and the environment. Therefore, exploring and applying novel eco‐friendly and efficient nematode management approaches to prevent excessive chemical pesticide residues and ensure agricultural product safety has become a critical task in modern agricultural development (Dalakouras et al. 2024; Li et al. 2015).

In recent years, RNA interference (RNAi), especially host‐induced gene silencing (HIGS), has emerged as a promising tool by silencing essential nematode genes via dsRNA expressed in transgenic plants (Nowara et al. 2010). HIGS functions by expressing double‐stranded RNA (dsRNA) sequences in transgenic plants that are specifically designed to target essential genes in pests or pathogens (Huang et al. 2006; Mani et al. 2020; Moreira et al. 2022; Pan et al. 2019). For instance, Huang et al. synthesized and expressed a hairpin RNA structure of the M. incognita gene 16D10 in Arabidopsis thaliana which markedly reduced the infectivity of four phytopathogenic nematode species (Huang et al. 2006). Host‐delivered RNAi has also been utilized to silence Meloidogyne genes such as flp‐14 and flp‐18 (Papolu et al. 2013). However, the commercialization of HIGS crops faces technical, regulatory and public acceptance challenges (Atkinson et al. 2012), while RNAi efficiency varies among plant‐nematode systems (Lisei‐de‐Sá et al. 2021).

Compared to transgenic approaches, recent advances in nanotechnology have introduced innovative strategies for the safe and efficient delivery of RNA molecules.

Nanoparticle‐based nucleic acid pesticides, in which dsRNA is encapsulated within nanocarriers, have garnered increasing attention. The nanoparticle shell acts as a physical barrier that protects nucleic acids from degradation. For instance, Peng et al. utilised a structurally simple, cost‐effective and environmentally friendly cationic star polymer (SPc) to form a stable nanocomplex with fluopyram via hydrogen bonding, significantly reducing particle size to the nanoscale (Peng et al. 2023). These nanoformulations enhanced the expression of genes related to transport and energy metabolism in Mucuna pruriens root‐knot nematodes, induced a reactive oxygen species (ROS) burst, inhibited ATP synthesis and succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) activity, and ultimately accelerated nematode mortality (Peng et al. 2023). These findings underscore the potential of nanocarriers in nematode control. Nevertheless, compared to synthetic nanomaterials, EVs offer several advantages for nucleic acid delivery: exceptional biocompatibility, low immunogenicity and broad biological distribution, as they are naturally derived from organisms including animals, plants, fungi and bacteria (Munhoz da Rocha et al. 2020). Moreover, they provide comparable stability and loading efficiency. Currently, EV‐mediated RNA delivery has been primarily studied in the biomedical field, where endogenous genetic engineering and electroporation remain the predominant loading strategies (EL Andaloussi et al. 2013; Jiang et al. 2017).

In this study, we propose an innovative biological control strategy that integrates RNAi and fungal EVs. We exogenously loaded siRNAs targeting nematode neuropeptide genes (Mi‐flp‐18) into EVs derived from the nematode‐trapping fungus A. oligospora , generating Mi‐flp‐18‐siRNA@EVs that markedly suppressed Meloidogyne incognita infectivity. Furthermore, we engineered A. oligospora strains to endogenously produce shRNA‐ or dsRNA‐containing EVs, enabling continuous in vivo RNAi delivery. The nematode‐suppressive activity of these EVs was verified through both in vitro and in vivo assays. Our findings highlight fungal EVs as a natural and sustainable RNA delivery platform for effective nematode management.

Materials and Methods

Extraction of EVs From A. oligospora

A. oligospora was cultured in TG liquid medium at 28°C with 180 rpm for 5 days. The mycelium was filtered through 12 layers of lens paper, and the resulting filtrate was centrifuged at 10,000×g for 30 min to remove cellular debris. Subsequently, the filtrate was passed through a 0.22‐μm membrane filter to eliminate residual macroproteins and larger vesicles. The liquid was concentrated using a 100‐kDa ultrafiltration tube. The concentrate was then centrifuged at 10,000×g for 1 h. The resulting pellet was subjected to ultracentrifugation at 120,000×g for 70 min. Following supernatant removal, the EV‐containing pellet was resuspended in RNase‐free PBS and stored at −80°C. EV concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime) (He et al. 2023).

TEM and NTA Characterisation of EVs

EV samples (10 μL) were fixed overnight at 4°C in 2.5% glutaraldehyde/0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB, pH 7.2), then rinsed three times (7 min each) with 0.1 M PB. Post‐fixation was performed with 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO4) at 4°C for 2 h, followed by three ddH2O rinses (7 min each). Samples were gradually dehydrated through an ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 90% and 100%), transitioned to absolute acetone (5 min), and embedded in SPI‐Pon 812 resin. Polymerisation occurred at 60°C for 48 h. Sections were prepared using a Leica EM UC7 ultramicrotome: 800 nm semithin sections for orientation and 60 nm ultrathin sections for imaging. Ultrathin sections were mounted on 200‐mesh copper grids and double‐stained with 2% uranyl acetate (15 min) and lead citrate (5 min) before imaging with a JEM‐1400 Plus TEM at 80 kV.

EV samples were analysed using a NanoAnalyzer (NanoFCM, Xiamen, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Particle concentration and size distribution were calculated using the NanoFCM software (NanoFCM Profession V1.0) and normalized for starting tissue weight, conditioned media volume, volume of EV resuspension and dilution necessary for adequate NanoFCM reading.

Methods of Exogenous siRNA Loading in A. oligospora EVs

Co‐Incubation Method

1 μg of chemically synthesized Mi‐flp‐18‐siRNA was mixed with 200 μg EVs and incubated at 28°C, 37°C or 42°C for 1, 1.5 or 2 h, respectively.

Saponin‐Mediated Loading

EVs (200 μg) were treated with 0.1%–0.5% saponin (5 min) to enhance membrane permeability. After adding 10 mL PBS, saponins were removed by ultrafiltration (100 kDa; 5000 × g, 20 min). Mi‐flp‐18‐siRNA (1 μg) was then mixed with the treated EVs and incubated at 25°C for 1 h.

Ultrasonic‐Mediated Loading

A mixture of Mi‐flp‐18‐siRNA (1 μg) and EVs (200 μg) was sonicated at 10%–50% amplitude for 6 cycles (3 min/cycle; 30‐s pulse/pause intervals). Membrane integrity was restored by incubation at 37°C for 1 h.

Freeze–Thaw Cycling

Mi‐flp‐18‐siRNA (1 μg) and EVs (200 μg) were incubated at 25°C for 30 min, flash‐frozen in liquid nitrogen, then thawed at 25°C. This freeze–thaw cycle was repeated one to nine.

PEI‐Mediated Transfection

Mi‐flp‐18‐siRNA (1 μg) in PBS (40 μL) was mixed with PEI 40K transfection reagent (2–6 μL) and incubated (25°C, 15 min) to form complexes. These were added to EVs (200 μg) and incubated at 37°C for 12 h.

Calcium Chloride Heat Shock

EVs (200 μg) and Mi‐flp‐18‐siRNA (1 μg) in PBS containing 0.1 M CaCl₂ were ice‐bathed (30 min), heat‐shocked (42°C; 45–120 s), then ice‐bathed again (5 min).

In the control group, 1 μg of chemically synthesised Mi‐flp‐18‐siRNA was mixed with 200 μg of EVs without any treatment.

Production of an Absolute Quantitative Standard Curve of Nucleic Acid and Calculation of siRNA Loading Efficiency by EVs

cDNA was synthesised from 1 μg synthetic Mi‐flp‐18‐siRNA using the miRNA/mRNA First‐Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Accurate). Serial dilutions (10−2–10−7) were prepared from the cDNA. qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix (Accurate) on a Light Cycler 480 (Roche) with the following thermal cycling conditions: 95°C for 30 s; then 55 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s. A universal reverse primer and gene‐specific forward primer (Mi‐flp‐18‐F: 5′‐GCCGATTTAATGAACAATTTTGCCGCGC‐3′) were used. Following loading, RNase A (1 μg/mL) was added to degrade unencapsulated RNA (37°C, 1 h). siRNA was extracted using the miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). A series of logarithmic values of Mi‐flp‐18‐siRNA dilutions (0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1, 5, 10 and 50 nM) was utilised to construct the standard calibration curve. Encapsulation efficiency, loading capacity and loading concentration of EVs were calculated with the equations as followed.

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

Tomato seeds were surface‐sterilised by soaking in 1% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite for 30 min, followed by rinsing in sterile water and incubation at 50°C for 30 min. Sterilised seeds were transferred using sterile tweezers to 9‐cm plates containing moistened filter paper and cultured at 28°C until radicles reached 1.5 cm (Li et al. 2023).

Acquisition of M. incognita J2

M. incognita egg masses were aseptically transferred to glass plates containing sterile water using sterile tweezers. Eggs were washed with 1% sodium hypochlorite (1 min), thoroughly rinsed with sterile water, then placed on a 200‐μm nylon mesh overlying sterile water. After hatching at 28°C for 72 h, J2 larvae were collected for experiments.

EVs or siRNA Uptake by Root‐Knot Nematode

J2 larvae (10,000) were incubated in stimulation solution (Lu et al. 2022) for 12 h, followed by three washes with sterile water. Following treatment with A. oligospora EVs (1 mg) or target siRNA (150 nM) for 4 h, nematodes were washed five times with sterile water before subsequent experiments.

Nematode Motility Assay

A 23% Pluronic F‐127 solution (Sigma) was dispensed into 6‐cm plates and allowed to solidify at 28°C. Tomato seedlings were positioned parallel to one plate edge. Prepared J2 larvae (200 nematodes) were inoculated 1.5 cm from each root tip. Nematode accumulation near root tips was quantified at 2, 4 and 8 h post‐inoculation (Zhao et al. 2023).

Nematode Invasion Assay

Seedling roots were put on 23% Pluronic F‐127 gel. ~200J2 larvae were inoculated 1.5 cm from each root tip. Plants were maintained at 28°C for 24 h. After acid fuchsin staining, invading nematodes were counted (Topalovi et al. 2019). The relative decrease ratio of the number of nematodes inside/around the root in the treatment group was calculated with the equations as followed.

Real‐Time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from 20,000J2 larvae using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). cDNA was synthesized from 500 ng RNA (miRNA/mRNA First‐Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit; Accurate). qPCR used SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix (Accurate) on a LightCycler 480 (Roche) under: 95°C for 30 s; 55 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s. Mi‐actin served as the endogenous reference, with relative expression quantified via the 2−ΔΔCt method (primers: Table S1). Three biological replicates included technical duplicates/triplicates (Nakagami et al. 2023).

Construction of shRNA and dsRNA Expressing Strains

The Mi‐flp‐18‐shRNA sequence (Tsingke) was chemically synthesized and ligated into NcoI/SpeI‐digested pCAMBIA‐Hyg‐1303 using T4 DNA ligase (Thermo Fisher). The resulting plasmid (PCA‐flp‐18‐shRNA) was transformed into A. oligospora protoplasts. Constructs were transformed into A. oligospora protoplasts following established protocols (Xie et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2018).

In addition, using M. incognita genomic DNA as a template, we amplified the forward and reverse fragments of several flp and nlp genes using specific primers (Table S1). These fragments were ligated into the XhoI‐ and HindIII‐linearized PSilent‐1 vector via T4 DNA ligase. The resulting dsRNA expression plasmids were then introduced into A. oligospora protoplasts, leading to the construction of fungal strains expressing Mi‐flp‐7‐dsRNA, Mi‐flp‐12‐dsRNA, Mi‐flp‐14‐dsRNA, Mi‐flp‐16‐dsRNA, Mi‐flp‐18‐dsRNA and Mi‐nlp‐12‐dsRNA, respectively.

Preparation of A. oligospora Spore, Mycelium and Fermentation Fluid

A. oligospora was cultured on CMY agar plates at 28°C for 14 days. Conidia were harvested by flooding plates with CMY medium, and concentrations were determined using a haemocytometer (Liu et al. 2024).

For mycelial cultures, fresh hyphae were inoculated into TG medium and incubated at 28°C for 7 days with shaking (180 rpm). The culture was filtered through a sterile funnel lined with 12 layers of lens paper to separate mycelia from the fermentation broth (Ding et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2024).

Tomato Pot Inoculation Experiment

Four 3–5 cm deep holes were made 1.5 cm from 20‐day‐old tomato plants. EV‐treated nematodes (1000/seedling) were introduced into each hole. For fungal colonisation assays, spore suspensions (108 spores/pot) were applied to soil 7 days before nematode inoculation. In fermentation broth and mycelium (FB/M complex) treatments, materials were applied 12 h before adding 1000 nematodes per pot. After 2 weeks, 3 plant seedlings in each treatment were collected, and the severity of root galling was recorded using a 0–4 rating scale according to the percentage of galled tissues (0 = 0%, 1 = 1%–25%, 2 = 25%–50%, 3 = 50%–75%, and 4 = 75%–100%).

Moreover, the EVs isolated from the four engineered strains expressing flp‐18‐shRNA, flp‐12‐dsRNA, flp‐18‐dsRNA and nlp‐12‐dsRNA were also used to conduct the pot experiments. 10,000 nematodes were pre‐incubated with 1 mg/mL engineered EVs for 24 h before being applied to potted tomato plants. In each tomato potting plant, 3000 treated nematodes and 300 μg EVs were added for each infection observation. Root‐knot formation was assessed 14 days post‐inoculation. Wild‐type EVs (Ao‐EVs) served as a negative control, and PBS treatment served as a blank control.

The fresh weight, plant height, and the number of root knots of all plants in each plot were measured and recorded to calculate the relative increase/decrease ratio of the treatment group. The disease index (DI) and relative control effect (CE) were calculated with the equations as follows.

The relative increase/decrease ratio of fresh weight, plant height and the number of root knots in treatment group were calculated with the equations as followed.

Results

Evaluation of the Efficiency of A. oligospora EVs Loaded With Exogenous siRNA

To investigate the potential of EVs as nucleic acid drug delivery vehicles for the control of root‐knot nematodes, EVs derived from A. oligospora were isolated and purified using ultracentrifugation. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed that the EVs exhibited a typical cup‐shaped morphology (Figure 1A), and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) showed that their size predominantly ranged from 60 to 80 nm, consistent with typical exosome profiles (Figure S1A). The siRNA sequence targeting the Mi‐flp‐18 gene of M. incognita was synthesized according to the protocol described by Dalzell (Dalzell et al. 2010).

EVs Loaded With Exogenous siRNA Reduced the Pathogenicity of Root‐Knot Nematodes

The second‐stage juveniles (J2s) of M. incognita were soaked in the Mi‐flp‐18‐siRNA@EVs solution for 4 h. The bioactivity of the engineered EVs Mi‐flp‐18‐siRNA@EVs was then preliminarily assessed using a Pluronic F‐127 gel‐based assay (Figure 1B). Results showed that, compared to the water‐treated control, the number of nematodes surrounding tomato roots was reduced by 50.55% following treatment with engineered EVs Mi‐flp‐18‐siRNA@EVs after inoculation for 8 h (Figure 1C), while the number of nematodes within root tissues decreased by 48.25% (Figure 1D). Notably, the group treated with synthetic Mi‐flp‐18 siRNA alone exhibited a similar impact on nematode behaviour as the group treated with Mi‐flp‐18‐siRNA@EVs with no statistically significant difference. Nevertheless, the EV‐encapsulated siRNA possesses inherent advantages over free siRNA. Owing to the protective lipid bilayer of EVs, the encapsulated siRNA is shielded from nuclease degradation, thereby enhancing its stability and persistence under extracellular conditions. In addition, EVs can facilitate cellular internalisation and targeted intracellular delivery, allowing efficient gene silencing even at lower nucleic acid concentrations. This indicates that the engineered EVs mediate efficient gene knockdown primarily through their superior biocompatibility, structural protection, and delivery efficiency compared with free siRNA. Furthermore, as a natural nanocarrier, EVs provide a biologically compatible and efficient platform for siRNA delivery in root‐knot nematodes. RT‐PCR analysis further confirmed that treatment with Mi‐flp‐18‐siRNA@EVs led to a significant reduction in Mi‐flp‐18 gene expression in M. incognita J2s (Figure 1E).

The Construction and Verification of Engineered Strains of A. oligospora That Produce EVs Loaded With Target siRNAs

To further simplify the production process and enhance nucleic acid loading efficiency of engineered EVs, we employed A. oligospora as a basic organism to generate large quantities of EVs endogenously loaded with nucleic acids by constructing shRNA‐ and dsRNA‐expressing fungal strains targeting neuropeptide genes in root‐knot nematodes. As illustrated in Figure 2A, the Mi‐flp‐18 shRNA fragment was ligated into the PCA‐1303 vector (PCA‐flp‐18‐shRNA) and transformed into A. oligospora protoplasts for the generation of a shRNA‐expressing strain, Ao‐gpdA::Mi‐flp‐18‐shRNA. PCR analysis confirmed successful expression of Mi‐flp‐18‐shRNA in the engineered strain (Figure S1B,C).

Similarly, we constructed dsRNA expression plasmids and fungal strains expressing Mi‐flp‐7‐dsRNA, Mi‐flp‐12‐dsRNA, Mi‐flp‐14‐dsRNA, Mi‐flp‐16‐dsRNA, Mi‐flp‐18‐dsRNA and Mi‐nlp‐12‐dsRNA, respectively (Figure 2B). PCR verification confirmed the expression of corresponding dsRNA fragments in these engineered strains (Figure S1D). To evaluate the efficacy of these endogenously dsRNA/shRNA‐loaded EVs in nematode control, EVs were extracted from the culture supernatants of the engineered strains via ultracentrifugation (Figure 2C), and the presence of shRNA or dsRNA within the EVs was validated (Figure S1C,E). As shown in Figure S1E, the expected dsRNA fragments were successfully detected within the EVs secreted by the engineered strains, demonstrating that both shRNA and dsRNA molecules can be efficiently packaged and exported via fungal EVs.

To assess the gene‐silencing effects of these EVs on M. incognita, nematodes were co‐incubated with the dsRNA/shRNA‐loaded EVs for 4 h, followed by RT‐PCR to examine the expression levels of the target genes. The results revealed that EVs endogenously loaded with nucleic acid molecules effectively suppressed the expression of the corresponding nematode genes. Among the tested constructs, EVs‐Mi‐flp‐18‐dsRNA achieved the most potent gene silencing, reducing target gene expression by 5.41‐fold (Figure 2D). Even the construct with the lowest silencing efficiency, EVs‐Mi‐flp‐7‐dsRNA, resulted in a 2.16‐fold reduction in target gene expression (Figure 2D). To confirm that gene silencing was specifically mediated by the EVs, the EV secretion inhibitor GW4869 was added during the cultivation of A. oligospora . The resultant culture supernatant, after EV suppression and ultracentrifugation, lost the ability to interfere with nematode gene expression, indicating that the observed gene silencing was indeed dependent on EV‐mediated delivery of nucleic acids (Figure 2E).

Engineered A. oligospora Strains and Their Secreted EVs Effectively Suppress Nematode Infectivity

To further evaluate the biocontrol potential of engineered A. oligospora ‐derived EVs, M. incognita juveniles pre‐incubated with EVs containing dsRNA or shRNA constructs were subjected to tomato plant infection assays. The results demonstrated that EV‐mediated delivery of Mi‐flp‐18‐shRNA, Mi‐flp‐7‐dsRNA, Mi‐flp‐12‐dsRNA, Mi‐flp‐14‐dsRNA, Mi‐flp‐16‐dsRNA, Mi‐flp‐18‐dsRNA and Mi‐nlp‐12‐dsRNA reduced nematode migration toward tomato roots by 44.61%, 40.00%, 55.38%, 55.38%, 26.15%, 49.23% and 58.46%, respectively after inoculation for 4 h (Figure 2F). Interestingly, treatment of nematodes with EV‐depleted fractions—obtained by culturing the engineered strains in the presence of the EV secretion inhibitor GW4869—did not impair their ability to recognise and migrate toward host roots (Figure 2F), indicating that EVs are essential for the observed phenotypic effects.

To quantify nematode invasion into plant tissues, root systems were stained with acid fuchsin 24 h post‐inoculation and the number of nematodes within the roots was counted. As shown in Figure 2G,H, engineered EVs significantly reduced nematode penetration into tomato roots. Notably, EVs carrying Mi‐flp‐18‐dsRNA and Mi‐flp‐18‐shRNA exhibited the strongest inhibitory effects, decreasing internal nematode numbers by 62.50% and 55.36%, respectively (Figure 2G,H). In contrast, the GW4869 treatment group showed no significant difference from the water‐treated control, further confirming that the nematode phenotype was specifically mediated by EVs derived from engineered fungal strains. These findings establish engineered EVs as the primary factor contributing to the nematode‐suppressive effect of the modified A. oligospora strains.

The Engineered Strain of A. oligospora Reduces the Infection of Root Knot Nematodes in Tomato Pot

To further investigate the efficacy of engineered strains in controlling root‐knot nematodes under more realistic conditions, we conducted pot experiments using tomato plants treated with the A. oligospora strain engineered to express Mi‐flp‐18‐shRNA. Since EVs were isolated from fermentation broth, and to reduce costs, we used the fermentation broth to treat with the tomato seedling. Tomato seedlings were treated with several formulations: 108 engineered fungal strain spores, engineered fungal strain fermentation broth (FB), or in combination with its fermentation broth and mycelia (FB/M complex). Also, similar formulations from the wild‐type A. oligospora were used as control. The number of root knots on the tomato roots and the fresh weight and plant height were also recorded to evaluate the nematode control efficacy. Results showed that the group treated with engineered fungal spores led to a 43.14% reduction in root knots (Figure 3A,B), an 18.13% increase in fresh weight (Figure S2A), a 12.13% increase in plant height (Figure S2B), and a control efficacy of 45.48% (Table 2). The group treated with fermentation broth of the engineered Mi‐flp‐18‐shRNA strain results in a 31.92% reduction in root knots (Figure 3A,B), a 6.86% increase in plant height (Figure S2B), an 10.68% increase in fresh weight (Figure S2A), and a control efficacy of 27.12% (Table 2). Notably, the combined application of engineered fermentation broth and mycelia (FB/M complex) exhibited the highest protective effect against nematode infestation with the number of root knots was reduced by 60.34% (Figure 3A,B). Moreover, the plant height and fresh weight increased by 12.62% and 21.15% (Figure S2A,B), respectively, resulting in an overall control efficacy of 54.50% (Table 2).

Discussion

Our study presents a novel biocontrol strategy leveraging EVs derived from the nematode‐trapping fungus A. oligospora to deliver RNA interference (RNAi) agents targeting the parasitic nematode M. incognita . We successfully loaded Mi‐flp‐18 siRNA into EVs derived from the nematode‐trapping fungus A. oligospora using a calcium chloride‐mediated method, achieving 3.40% loading efficiency. These engineered EVs reduced nematode infectivity by 50.54% in gel‐based assays, accompanied by a significant downregulation of the Mi‐flp‐18 transcript (Figure 1C–E). Compared with controls treated with free siRNA, the EV‐carried siRNA showed equivalent efficacy despite significantly lower quantities, highlighting EVs' potential for enhancing RNAi delivery through their inherent biocompatibility and protection of cargo (Cai et al. 2019; Hagiwara et al. 2014; Jiang et al. 2017).

Engineering A. oligospora strains to endogenously express nematode‐targeting shRNA/dsRNA allowed constant generation of RNA‐loaded EVs. Among tested constructs, EVs carrying Mi‐flp‐18 dsRNA achieved the strongest gene silencing (5.4‐fold transcript reduction), and all engineered EVs suppressed target gene expression, while inhibition of EV secretion with GW4869 abolished silencing, demonstrating RNA delivery is EV‐dependent (Figure 2D,E). These findings are consistent with recent studies demonstrating EV‐mediated cross‐kingdom RNA transfer (Munhoz da Rocha et al. 2020; Samuel et al. 2015; Urzi et al. 2022).

Importantly, engineered EVs significantly reduced nematode attraction (~26%–59% reduction) and root penetration (~55%–62%) in tomato infection assays (Figure 2F,G), confirming their effective suppression of root‐knot nematode (RKN) infectivity. In greenhouse pot experiments, treatments with Mi‐flp‐18 shRNA/dsRNA strains and fermentation broth reduced root galling by ~60% and increased plant biomass by ~20%, resulting in a control efficacy of ~55% (Figure 3, Tables 2 and 3). Similarly, the direct application of engineered EVs alone significantly decreased root gall numbers (Figure 4A,B) and promoted tomato growth (Figure 4C,D). Collectively, these results demonstrate that fungal EVs represent a robust and sustainable platform for RNA delivery and nematode suppression.

Compared with conventional host‐induced gene silencing (HIGS) approaches, this EV‐based RNAi system offers a non‐GMO alternative that can be applied topically or via soil amendment. The platform enables continuous in situ RNA production and release, thus circumventing the regulatory and public concerns associated with transgenic crops. Despite these promising findings, several limitations remain. First, the yield of EVs and the loading efficiency of RNA molecules need to be improved to facilitate broader agricultural applications. Emerging studies suggest that the incorporation of RNA‐binding proteins (RBPs) such as ALIX, YBX1 or HNRNPA2B1 can enhance cargo loading into EVs (Fabbiano et al. 2020; He et al. 2021), and similar strategies could be applied to fungal systems. Second, the precise uptake mechanisms by which fungal EVs enter nematode cells remain unclear. It is unknown whether this process involves clathrin‐mediated endocytosis, lipid raft domains or lectin–glycan recognition (Abels and Breakefield 2016; He et al. 2023). In addition, functional redundancy within nematode gene families may attenuate RNAi efficacy; thus, high‐throughput RNAi screening or infection‐responsive transcriptome profiling could help identify synergistic targets. Moreover, although EV‐mediated RNAi performed effectively under controlled conditions, its stability and efficacy under field environments remain to be validated.

While this study focuses on root‐knot nematodes, the underlying EV‐based RNA delivery framework is broadly applicable to other plant pathogens and pests. Unlike synthetic nanoparticles, fungal EVs naturally protect RNA from nuclease degradation, enable stable and biocompatible delivery without auxiliary excipients, and can be mass‐produced through fermentation. By achieving potent gene silencing at substantially lower doses, EV‐encapsulated siRNAs minimize material costs and off‐target risks, providing a scalable, environmentally friendly approach for RNAi‐based crop protection.

Conclusions

Collectively, we present a pioneering EV‐based RNAi approach against root‐knot nematodes, merging fungal biology and RNA delivery into a biocompatible and sustainable pest control strategy. Our work clearly differentiates itself from existing HIGS and synthetic nanocarrier methods by showcasing continuous RNA production, environmental adaptability and significant nematode control efficacy. This platform holds promise to transform nematode management in agroecosystems. In the future, this technology is expected to be applied to the control of more types of plant‐parasitic nematodes and other agricultural pests.
