APOC1⁺IFI30⁺ TAMs orchestrate colorectal cancer liver metastasis by coupling cholesterol biosynthesis with secretion of immunosuppressive factors
Authors: Zheyou Cai, Yibing Chen, Lei Cui, Jine Yao, Jianghua Qiao, Weihong Sun, Haisheng Liu, Linhao Yang, Zhuang Yang, Quentin Liu, Benxin Hou, Zhengzhi Zou, Dongming Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) play a crucial role in colorectal cancer (CRC) metastasis, particularly in the establishment of the liver metastatic niche. TAMs facilitate metastasis by enabling epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and suppressing anti-tumor immunity. While their roles in primary tumors have been extensively investigated, their functions at metastatic sites remain poorly understood. In this study, we analyzed the single-cell RNA sequencing data from 46 CRC patients, encompassing 402,972 cells, to identify macrophage subsets associated with liver metastasis. A distinct population of APOC1+ IFI30+ TAMs was identified and found to be significantly enriched in metastatic liver lesions. Compared to primary tumors (PT), the number of APOC1+ IFI30+ TAMs significantly increased at liver metastatic sites after CRC cells metastasized to the liver. We employed spatial transcriptomics to validate the above conclusions. These TAMs exhibited significantly enhanced immunosuppressive capacity and cholesterol synthesis metabolism. Further investigation showed that TAMs within metastatic niches reprogram the mevalonate pathway to enhance cholesterol synthesis, characterized by upregulation of key genes such as HMGCR and ACAT1/2. These TAMs act on surrounding tumor cells by secreting cholesterol and cytokines such as TNFSF12, TGFB1, MIF and LGALS9, thereby enhancing tumor adhesion and proliferation and creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment to continuously support tumor growth. Our findings provided new insights into the complex interactions among TAMs, immune cells, and tumor cells within CRC metastatic microenvironment, highlighting potential therapeutic targets to disrupt TAMs-mediated immune evasion and metastatic progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06338-3.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Metastatic colonization, APOC1, IFI30, Tumor-associated macrophages

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) represents a significant global health burden and is one of leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. While substantial advancements have been achieved in the treatment of early-stage disease, achieving 5-year survival rates as high as 91%, the prognosis for patients with metastatic CRC (mCRC) remains poor. A primary contributor to this elevated mortality is liver metastasis, which dramatically reduces the 5-year survival rate to approximately 13% [2, 3]. Recent studies have showed various mechanisms contributing to CRC metastasis. For instance, activation of oncogene such as KRAS or BRAF mutations drives metastatic progression [4]. Beyond intrinsic tumor cell properties, the tumor micro-environment (TME) in both primary and metastatic sites have emerged as a critical determinant of metastatic dissemination and colonization. Components of the TME, including cancer-associated fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells, can remodel the extracellular matrix, promote angiogenesis, and create an immunosuppressive niche that favors tumor cell survival and growth in the liver [5–7]. Among these, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are key regulators. Macrophages are highly plastic innate immune cells traditionally classified along a polarization continuum into pro-inflammatory, anti-tumor M1 and anti-inflammatory, pro-tumor M2 phenotypes [8–10]. Among M2 subtypes, M2c and M2d exhibit particularly potent pro-tumorigenic activities and are considered key functional drivers of tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) behavior in established tumors [11]. Nevertheless, the canonical M1/M2 dichotomy is increasingly recognized as an oversimplification that inadequately reflects the biological complexity and functional diversity of TAMs. Recent single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) studies have revealed extensive heterogeneity within the TAM compartment, identifying multiple transcriptionally distinct, intermediate polarization states whose phenotypic identity and functional output are dynamically shaped by spatial cues and local signals within the tumor microenvironment [12, 13]. In colorectal cancer (CRC), spatially distinct and anatomically defined tumor-associated macrophage TAM subsets, localized within the primary tumor, invasive margins, or metastatic niches, play non-redundant roles in primary tumor progression and liver metastasis. To date, several such transcriptionally unique TAM subsets have been rigorously characterized in CRC models and patient samples [14, 15]. In the primary tumor microenvironment, TAMs have been shown to facilitate metastatic spread through various mechanisms, such as promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [8], stimulating angiogenesis [9, 10], and degrading the extracellular matrix to enable tumor cell intravasation [11]. Notably, studies indicate that CD163+ TAMs located at the invasive front correlate with EMT, an increased ratio of mesenchymal circulating tumor cells (CTCs), and poor prognosis for CRC patients [12]. Additionally, TAMs may contribute to the generation and release of CTCs, which are regarded as key initiators of metastatic dissemination [13]. The stark disparity in survival rates underscores the limitations of a tumor cell-centric approach alone and emphasizes the urgent need to shift research and clinical attention toward the unique ecology of metastatic sites, particularly the hepatic tumor microenvironment.

Once tumor cells reach distant organs, the microenvironment of the metastatic site plays a crucial role in their survival, colonization, and growth. In the context of liver metastasis, TAMs at these secondary sites significantly contribute to a pro-metastatic environment in CRC and other cancers. M2-like TAMs, in particular, are abundant in liver mCRC and have been associated with poor prognosis as well as reduced efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade [14, 15]. Accumulating evidence suggests that M2-TAMs contribute to the establishment and progression of metastatic lesions through multiple mechanisms [16–18]. In the context of CRC liver metastasis, TAMs are recruited to the liver and create a supportive microenvironment. They facilitate colonization by secreting factors like inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) and extracellular matrix-remodeling enzymes (e.g., MMP9) that help arriving cancer cells survive and adapt to the new milieu [19]. Furthermore, TAMs directly support metastatic tumor growth by producing trophic factors (e.g., EGF) that stimulate cell proliferation and by suppressing anti-tumor immunity [20]. Studies have demonstrated that SPP1+ TAMs may activate fibroblasts through SPP1-CD44 interaction and induce T cell exhaustion through CD155-CD226 signaling, thereby providing mechanistic insights into microenvironmental reprogramming that supports metastatic growth [21, 22]. Yin et al. further discovered that TAMs promote the formation of spheroids, facilitating the early stages of cancer metastasis and colonization [23]. These findings highlight the critical role of TAMs in supporting the proliferation and colonization of CTCs in metastatic tumors. However, the precise mechanisms through which specific subsets of TAMs modulate the liver microenvironment to facilitate these processes in CRC liver metastasis remain unclear. Identifying and targeting these specific TAM populations could offer novel therapeutic avenues.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has emerged as a powerful tool for elucidating subpopulations within the tumor immune microenvironment. This technique enables the detection of the transcriptome at a single-cell level and facilitates the analysis of relevant functional states. In this study, we conducted scRNA-seq analyses and identified a novel APOC1⁺IFI30⁺ TAM subset enriched in colorectal cancer liver metastases. These TAMs were found to present in higher proportions and actively contribute to the growth and colonization of CRC with liver metastasis. In particular, they engaged in cellular communication that leads to immunosuppression, thereby promoting tumor growth and metastasis. Furthermore, we found that APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs enhanced cholesterol metabolism, which consequently increased tumor cell adhesion and colonization. Our study provided new insights into the role of TAMs in CRC liver metastasis, suggesting potential therapeutic strategies focused on targeting TAMs.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

The chemicals and their sources are as follows: protease inhibitors (P8340) and phosphatase inhibitors (P0044) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. The antibodies for immunoblot (IB) were provided as follows: Anti-β-actin (ab8226); Anti-Apolipoprotein CI/Apo-CI [EPR16813] (ab198288); Anti-ACAT2/Acetyl CoA acetyltransferase [EPR8416(2)] (ab191431); Anti-SOAT1/ACAT1 [EPR26952-40] (ab307597; 1:1,000); Anti-HMGCR [CL0260] (ab242315).

Cell lines and cell culture

Human colorectal cancer cell lines HCT-116 and SW620 were obtained from the Chinese Type Culture Collection (Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in DMEM (11965092; Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% FBS (10099141 C; Gibco) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Cell adhesion assay

The 96-well flat-bottom plates were coated with extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins (10 µg/mL; Life Technologies, Inc.) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) overnight at 4 °C. Following the removal of the coating solution, wells were blocked with 1% (w/v) heat-denatured bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS (100 µL/well) for 1 h at 37 °C, and then washed three times with PBS. Cancer cells (5 × 10⁴ cells/well) were subsequently seeded onto the ECM-coated plates and incubated at 37 °C for 2–3 h to facilitate cell adhesion. Non-adherent cells were eliminated through three washes with PBS, while adherent cells were fixed using crystal violet staining solution for 10 min. After staining, the dye was solubilized in a solution of 33% acetic acid for 5 min, and absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 570 nm to quantify cell adhesion.

In vitro cell proliferation assay

HCT-116 and SW620 cells were dissociated into individual cells, and the viable cells (negative for Trypan Blue) were enumerated using a hemocytometer. The cells were subsequently plated in a 24-well plate with 1 mL of culture medium at a density of 5,000 cells per well, and viable cell counts for each well were performed daily for five consecutive days using a hemocytometer [24].

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (containing 1 mM PMSF and 0.25 mM DTT) on ice. After centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected for protein quantification using a BCA protein assay kit (P0009; Beyotime). Protein samples were mixed with 5× SDS loading buffer (containing 0.25 M Tris-HCl, pH 6.8, 50% glycerol, 10% SDS, 0.5% BPB, and 5% β-mercaptoethanol) and denatured by heating. SDS-PAGE was performed, and proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (IPVH00010; Millipore) at a rate of 1 kDa per minute or in transfer buffer (25 mM Tris, 192 mM glycine) [25]. Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA in TBST (0.02 M Tris, 0.15 M NaCl, pH 7.4, 0.15% Tween-20) at 37 °C for 2 h. Primary antibodies were incubated on a shaker at 4 °C for 16 h, followed by three washes with TBST. Secondary antibodies were applied at room temperature for 2 h, and membranes were washed again. Protein bands were detected using ECL substrate (P0018; Beyotime or GCP1001-500-ES; GENECYCLER) and visualized with a digital imaging system (Tanon 5200) [26].

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent (R1000; Lablead), followed by reverse transcription into cDNA with Hifair III Reverse Transcriptase (11297ES09; YEASEN). The resulting cDNA products were analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (11201ES08; YEASEN) [27]. Gene-specific primers were used for amplification, and their sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S5.

Plasmid construction

The pLKO.1 lentiviral shRNA constructs targeting APOC1 were designed in accordance with the protocol available on the Addgene website (https://www.addgene.org/protocols/plko/). All sh-RNA Primers sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S6.

Cholesteryl ester/free‑cholesterol quantitation assay

Based on the instruction provided in the Cholesterol/Cholesteryl Ester Quantitation Assay kit (Abcam, UK), HCT-116 and SW620 cells were harvested at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells and washed with cold PBS. Subsequently, lipids were extracted by resuspending the cell pellets in 200 µL of Chloroform: Isopropanol: NP-40 (7:11:0.1) using a micro-homogenizer. The lipid extract was then centrifuged for 5–10 min at 15,000 × g to facilitate separation. The resulting extract was air-dried at 50 °C to remove chloroform. Once dried, the lipids were reconstituted in 200 µL of Assay Buffer II/Assay Buffer. Relative reaction mixes were prepared and added to each sample according to the established protocol, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 60 min while protected for light. Fluorescence measurements were performed using excitation/emission wavelengths of Ex/Em = 535/587 nm for fluorometric assay analysis.

Quality control, integration and clustering of single cell sequencing

We obtained the original single-cell expression data matrices (GSE164522 [28], GSE178318 [29], GSE225857 [30], GSE245552 [31], GSE203608 [32] and data from https://dna-discovery.stanford.edu/research/datasets [33]), along with corresponding sample and patient information from previously published CRC research. Seurat objects were constructed for each dataset using Seurat (version 4.3.0) [34]. DoubletFinder (version 2.0.3) [35] identified and removed doublets from the datasets. We implemented cell quality control measures by excluding cells that: (1) expressed fewer than 500 genes, (2) had a mitochondrial gene content exceeding 25%, and (3) had UMI counts below 800. To correct for batch effects, the FindIntegrationAnchors and IntegrateData functions were employed to perform reciprocal PCA (RPCA). Dimensionality reduction for the UMAP was achieved using the first 50 principal components. Cell clustering was performed using the FindClusters function, and clusters were annotated based on the expression of classic marker genes. We supplemented manual curation with computational methods that suggest cell type identities by interpreting complex gene expression signatures.

Single-cell distance analysis

We conducted a single-cell distance analysis utilizing the scDist R package (version 1.1.1) [36] to evaluate cellular heterogeneity. The analysis quantifies variations in gene expression profiles between primary and metastatic site TAMs cell populations. Specifically, the scDIST function employs default parameters to assess differences among TAMs derived from various tissues by using the standardized counting data and metadata obtained from the Seurat object. The DistPlot function was utilized to visualize intercellular distances, while the DistGenes function focus on comparing TAMs between primary and metastatic site, identifying the top 20 genes with the highest absolute beta values.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis

Monocle (version 2.26.0) [37] was utilized for pseudo-time analysis to elucidate the trajectories of macrophage differentiation and cell-state transitions. Dimensionality reduction was achieved using the DDRTree function, and cells were mapped onto pseudo-time trajectories through the orderCells function. The plot_cell_trajectory function was employed to visualize these differentiation trajectories. Additionally, CytoTRACE (version 0.3.3) [38] was utilized to confirm the stemness of cell populations. To illustrate the expression patterns of developmental regulatory marker genes, we generated pseudo-heatmaps using the plot_pseudotime_heatmap function from Monocle. Differential expression analyses for genes along the pseudotime trajectory were conducted with the differential GeneTest function in Monocle.

ST data preprocessing and analysis

Spatial transcriptomics data was downloaded sourced from GSE206552 [39], E-GEAD-579 [40], E-GEAD-619 [41], E-GEAD-622 [41], and the dataset available at http://www.cancerdiversity.asia/scCRLM/ [42]. Seurat was employed for quality control, normalization, and the identification of highly variable genes. The spatial transcriptomics data, comprising both coordinates and gene expression levels, were extracted from Seurat and subsequently analyzed in Python. The coordinates represented cell locations in a two-dimensional space, while the expression data illustrated cellular transcriptomic profiles. Data was converted from CSV to ESRI Shapefile format using geopandas (version 1.0.1) for GIS compatibility. Spatial autocorrelation was assessed using libpysal (version 4.12.1) to create Queen contiguity matrices and esda (version 2.6.1) for Moran’s I calculations, examining global and local patterns of gene expression spatial clustering [43].

Spatially resolved ligand-receptor (LR) interaction analysis was conducted via SpaGene (version 0.1.0) R package [44]. A k-nearest neighbor graph was built based on spatial transcriptomics coordinates, with Earth Mover’s Distance applied to quantify spatial co-aggregation of LR pairs from a curated species-specific database. Significant LR co-localization and co-expression pairs were identified by permutation tests with false discovery rate correction, and their spatial distribution was visualized to display in situ co-expression patterns and interaction strength.

Cellular communication

To investigate cell–cell interactions between TAMs and other cell types in primary and metastatic CRC, we utilized CellChat (version 2.1.2) [45] to infer these interactions based on the expression of known ligand–receptor pairs across different cell types within scRNA-seq datasets. The Seurat object and CellChatDB of human were used to create cellChat object. Subsequently, differential cell–cell communication pathways were calculated by comparing two distinct groups. To predict significant communications, CellChat identifies differentially over-expressed ligands and receptors for each group. The quantification of communications between two cell groups mediated by these signaling genes, CellChat associates each interaction with a probability value. Additionally, we used CytoSig (version 0.0.3) [46] and FlowSig (version 0.1.0) [47] to predict cytokine signal activity, which guided the selection of differentially over-expressed ligands in TAMs within CellChat.

Metabolite-sensor cell-cell communication

MEBOSCOST (Version 1.0.3) was applied to predict metabolite-mediated cell-cell communications derived from scRNA-seq data [48]. It estimated metabolite abundance and calculate communication score for each condition. Subsequently, the results of two conditions were integrated to facilitate a comparative analysis of the communications. The differences in communication scores between the two conditions were calculated. Communication events involving in sender-metabolite-sensor-receiver were predicted and visualized using bar plots, flow plots, and circle plots. Violin plot illustrated both metabolite abundance and sensor expression levels. The Index of dispersion (IOD) was calculated using communication scores across conditions as described in the MEBOCOST publication. The top 100 most variable communications were selected for further investigation. We utilized scFEA (version 1.1.2) [49] to estimate metabolic fluxes in TAMs from primary and metastatic CRC based on scRNA-seq data, and applied scMetabolism (version 0.2.1) [42] for metabolic pathway enrichment analysis to validate the reliability of cellular metabolic communication.

Single-cell drug susceptibility assessment

The potential sensitive drugs were identified by the Beyondcell (version 1.2.1) [50] for Perturbation Sensitivity (PSc) analysis. The PSc scores were calculated through the bcScore function based on scRNA-seq. Cellular relationships were visualized following the PSc analysis via the bcUMAP function with UMAP. To further characterize the dataset and identify potential therapeutic targets, both condition-based statistics and cell type-based statistics were employed. Differential analysis between groups was conducted using the bcRanks function, adhering to default settings. The bc4Squares function presented the analysis outcomes, highlighting the top three drugs exhibiting the greatest average variability. Drug target prediction was carried out using PharmMapper (https://lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/) [51], and target visualization was implemented with the bcSignatures function.

Pathway enrichment analysis

To investigate the heterogeneous expression of TAMs, we conducted Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using the fgsea (version 1.28.0) and clusterprofiler (version 4.8.0) R package [52]. The pathway enrichment analyses were based on the C2 (curated gene sets) from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) [53]. To compare signaling pathway enrichment between epithelial cells derived from primary and metastatic tissues, we performed gene ontology and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses using the KOBAS tool (http://bioinfo.org/kobas) [54]. For protein function prediction, we used DeepFRI (https://beta.deepfri.flatironinstitute.org/) to analyze protein structures and generate function predictions [55].

Bulk deconvolution of bulk RNA-Seq

The merge scRNA-seq dataset was used for the purposes of single-cell reference mapping and bulk deconvolution. Initially, the bulk count matrices were obtained from the GEO (GSE17536, GSE17537 [56], GSE29623 [57], GSE39582 [58]). The bulk count matrices were converted into an ExpressionSet object using the ExpressionSet function from the Biobase (version 2.62.0). To identify markers of each class, we applied the RunPrestoAll function from the SeuratWrappers (version 0.3.1) with default parameters. For each bulk RNA-seq sample, we performed deconvolution to inferred cell class proportion using the MarkerBasedDecomposition function from the BisqueRNA (version 1.0.5) with default parameters [59].

Survival analysis

Patient samples were classified into four distinct groups based on the proportions of specific cell types inferred through deconvolution: a high proportion of CXCL8+ TAMs, a low proportion of CXCL8+ TAMs, a high proportion of APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs, and a low proportion of APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs. The overall survival and disease-free survival rates for each group were visualized using Kaplan-Meier survival curves, which were generated utilizing R packages survival (version 3.5–7) and survminer (version 0.4.9). The prognostic significance of the identified genes in CRC patients was evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier Plotter database (http://kmplot.com/analysis/) [60].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the ggpubr R package (version 0.6.0) and Graphpad Prism 9. The significance of the ratio of TAMs cell subtypes was evaluated by Mann-Whitney test. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 (*P < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001; ns, not significant). Box plot data are presented as median with 25% and 75% percentile. Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank test was used for survival difference between groups.

Results

Analysis of primary and metastatic CRC microenvironments via single-cell RNA sequencing profiling

To systematically characterize the immunological divergence between primary CRC tumors and liver metastatic colorectal tumors, we integrated five publicly available scRNA-seq datasets involving a total of 46 patients. This cohort comprised 27 primary tumors (PTs), 19 adjacent non-neoplastic tissues (NTs), and 44 metastatic tumors (MTs) (Fig. 1A and Table S1). Following quality control and the removal of doublets (Supplementary Fig. S1), we established a harmonized expression matrix encompassing 18,606 genes across 402,972 high-quality cells. The subsequent analyses included merging, normalization, batch correction, and clustering to identify the principal cell types. These identified cell types encompassed lymphoid cells (T, natural killer (NK), innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), B and plasma cells), myeloid cells (dendritic cells (DCs), neutrophils, macrophages, and monocytes), mast cells, stromal cells (endothelial and fibroblasts), and epithelial cells (Fig. 1B and Fig. S2A-S2B). The identity of these major cell populations was confirmed by the expression of established canonical marker genes (Fig. 1A-B and Table S2). The investigation into cell abundance and composition in both primary and metastatic samples revealed substantial differences in immune infiltration. Notably, compared with PTs, MTs exhibited a higher overall proportion of immune cells, accompanied by altered abundances of non-immune cell populations (Fig. 1E and Fig. S2C). This finding indicated that the immune microenvironment underwent substantial changes between MTs and PTs. This finding indicated that the immune microenvironment underwent substantial changes between MTs and PTs. Specifically, our analysis revealed that the proportion of B cells and plasma cells in PTs was significantly higher than that observed in MTs; conversely, T cells and macrophages proportions were found to be elevated in MTs relative to those observed in PTs (Fig. S3).

APOC1, IFI30, and CXCL8 distinguish metastatic CRC and predict disease progression

TAMs are abundant immune cells correlated with poor prognosis in CRC liver metastases, and their heterogeneity within the unique liver niche remains largely unexplored compared to other cell populations. To differentiate TAMs in primary and metastatic tumors, we analyzed scRNA-seq data for TAMs in both PTs and MTs, utilizing the scDist algorithm to prioritize gene importance [36]. Our analysis revealed that 15 genes exhibited elevated expression in PTs, while 5 genes were predominantly expressed in MTs (Fig. 2A). Further differential expression analysis of the external bulk RNA-seq dataset GSE213402 revealed that only APOC1, IFI30, and CXCL8 exhibited statistically significant differential expression (Fig. 2B).

APOC1 and IFI30 gene exhibited elevated expression in MTs, while CXCL8 was upregulated in PTs (Fig. 2A-B). Gene expression profiling revealed that these three genes were high expressed within TAMs (Fig. 2D). We evaluated the diagnostic efficacy of combining APOC1, IFI30, and CXCL8 combined with macrophage differentiation markers CXCL9 and SPP1 for colorectal cancer metastasis through ROC curve analysis [61]. The integrated model comprising APOC1, IFI30, and CXCL8 demonstrated significantly superior performance compared to the single marker SPP1 or any of the other genes alone when distinguishing MTs. This highlighted its potential clinical application value for assessing liver metastasis risk in colorectal cancer patients (Fig. 2E). Additionally, we further validated our findings using an additional bulk RNA-seq dataset (GSE49355) as well as an independent single-cell RNA sequencing dataset (GSE203608) (Fig. 2F-G). In the GSE17538 cohort, increased expression levels of APOC1 and IFI30 were significantly associated with poor prognosis, whereas CXCL8 expression showed no statistically significant association with patient survival (Fig. 2H). These results indicate that APOC1 and IFI30 expression levels are associated with patient prognosis.

Metastasis-enriched APOC1⁺IFI30⁺ TAMs predict adverse clinical outcomes

Pseudo-time analysis of TAMs derived from scRNA-seq data of PTs and MTs was performed (Fig. 3A). The differentiation trajectories revealed an increased expression of APOC1 and IFI30 during the later stages, while CXCL8 expression was elevated in the early stages and subsequently decreased as differentiation progressed (Fig. 3B-C and Fig. S4A). In comparison to previously established macrophage differentiation markers such as CXCL9 and SPP1 [61], our novel markers provided a more comprehensive representation of macrophage polarization throughout the metastatic transition. Notably, there was a significant increase in TAMs within MTs compared to those in PTs during the late stage of differentiation (Fig. 3D). In states 5–7, TAMs exhibited a pronounced, highly concordant co-expression pattern of APOC1 and IFI30 (Fig. S4B). The vast majority of cells co-expressed both genes, whereas only a minor fraction displayed mutually exclusive expression patterns (Fig. S4C), highlighting the robust transcriptional coupling between these two markers. Notably, APOC1-single-positive cells constituted a significant fraction (21.3%) of this subset (Fig. S4C). To investigate whether the APOC1-single-positive cells represented a distinct subpopulation, we performed differential gene expression analysis against the double-positive (APOC1⁺IFI30⁺) cells. However, this comparison yielded only a sparse set of differentially expressed genes, which were mainly restricted to mitochondrial and ribosomal-associated functions (Fig. S4D and Table S3). Therefore, our data indicated that states 5–7 comprised a relatively homogeneous TAM population, primarily characterized by the co-expression of APOC1 and IFI30.

We designated the TAMs in the early phase (states 1–4) of differentiation as CXCL8+ TAMs and those in the later phase (states 5–7) as APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs. The KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated that APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs were predominately enriched with the two pathways: antigen processing and presentation, and lysosome function (Fig. S4E). Notably, the identified marker genes were also predicted to exhibit these functions (Fig. S4F). The later phase gene IFI30 had been reported to be correlated with the expression of lysosome-related proteins and play a role in antigen presentation processes [62]. APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs exhibited a mixed polarization phenotype with concurrent features of both M1 and M2 macrophages, whereas CXCL8+ TAMs did not show significant polarization (Fig. S4G). Specifically, this subset showed M2-like immunosuppressive and pro-tumor functional properties, along with M1-like enhanced phagocytic capacity and upregulated antigen processing and presentation pathways (Fig. S4E-S4G). Survival analyses across multiple validation cohorts revealed that patients with high infiltration of APOC1⁺IFI30⁺ TAMs had significantly poorer overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) compared to those with low infiltration. In contrast, high infiltration of CXCL8⁺ TAMs was associated with a more favorable prognosis (Fig. 3E). Additionally, spatial transcriptome datasets employed Bivariate Moran’s I and LISA for spatial correlation analysis, showing that there was a pronounced positive spatial co-localization between IFI30 expression and TAMs density within MTs; conversely, there was negative or absent spatial coupling in the primary site of early-stage cancer (Fig. 3F-G). Notably, the Bivariate Moran’s I of CXCL8 in the primary site exceeded APOC1 and IFI30 values; however, the difference did not reach statistical significance. Conversely, in MTs, the Bivariate Moran’s I for CXCL8 were found to be lower when compared to those for IFI30 and APOC1 (Fig. S4H). These findings suggested a potential role of the IFI30 and APOC1 genes in tumor progression and metastasis. The spatial relationship between these markers further revealed a complex association between APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs and tumor progression as well as metastatic dissemination.

APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs promote immunosuppression and tumor growth in metastasis site via MIF/LGALS9 and TNFSF12/TGFB1 signaling

To elucidate the intricate cell-cell communication networks between TAMs and other cell-types in metastatic CRC, we conducted an analysis of cellular interactions in both PTs and MTs (Fig. 4A-B). Notably, compared with PTs, MTs exhibited a reduced total number of cell-cell interactions but a significantly increased overall interaction strength (Fig. 4A). This likely reflects a functional reorganization of the metastatic TME, where broad pleiotropic signaling is replaced by selective reinforcement of high-intensity interactions driving immune evasion and metastatic colonization. Our findings revealed that the differential interaction strength of macrophages in MTs exhibited the most significant variation among all cell types when compared to PTs (Fig. S5A). We identified three highly active ligands associated with TAMs in metastatic lesions: GALECTIN, SPP1, and MIF (Fig. 4C). Notably, relative to primary tumors, metastatic tumors displayed upregulated MIF and GALECTIN (include LGALS9 and other galectin protein) signaling pathways specifically within macrophages, which were predominantly transmitted to immune cells. While the SPP1 signaling pathway mainly derived from macrophages, no significant differences in its activity were observed between primary and metastatic sites (Fig. S5B-S5D). Subsequently, we conducted a comprehensive investigation into the specific intercellular interactions mediated by these ligands between TAMs cells and other cell types. Our analysis confirmed that ligand-receptor pairs associated with MIF and LGALS9 (also named galectin-9) specifically mediated the interactions between metastatic TAMs and immune cells (Fig. 4D). Specifically, MIF released by TAMs interacted with CD74, CXCR4, CXCR2, and CD44 on immune cells. In addition, LGALS9 released by TAMs interacted with HAVCR2, CD45, and CD44 on these immune cells. It had been reported that both MIF and LGALS9 not only enhance the polarization and chemotaxis of myeloid cells, but also orchestrate CD8+ T cell exhaustion and CD4+ T cell activation [63–65]. Upon assessment of CD8+ and CD4+ T cell populations, we confirmed that compared to PTs, CD8+ T cells in metastatic lesions exhibited a higher degree of exhaustion coupled with reduced cytotoxicity. In contrast, CD4+ T cells showed a decreased naive score alongside an increased regulatory T cell (Treg) score (Fig. S5E). Furthermore, we identified that TNFSF13 and RETN, two ligands derived from TAMs in metastatic lesions, were significantly activated and mediated extensive pan-cellular interactions with nearly all cell types in the metastatic TME (Fig. S5F). These two ligands have been previously reported to be associated with inflammatory signal amplification and immunosuppressive niche maintenance in solid tumors, representing an auxiliary mechanism by which TAMs remodel the liver metastatic microenvironment.

We observed a significantly enhanced communication between epithelial cell and macrophages in metastatic lesions, which markedly outnumbered the interactions of epithelial cells with other cell types (Fig. 4E). The activities of macrophage ligands at both primary and metastatic sites were examined, confirming the critical role of several ligands in metastatic sites through previously established pseudo-time analysis (Fig. 4F and Fig. S6A). These ligands are particularly associated with APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs in the late differentiation stage. Utilizing the FlowSig algorithm [47], we identified that TGFB1 and TWEAK (also named TNFSF12) exhibited significant activation during cell communication between TAMs and epithelial cells (Fig. 4G). Meanwhile, we observed a significant increase in communication between TAMs and epithelial cells at the metastatic site. This interaction was primarily mediated through the TNFSF12-TNFRSF12A and TGFB1-(TGFBR1 + TGFBR2) ligand-receptor signaling pathways (Fig. 4H-I). Furthermore, we employed spatial transcriptome to verify these findings (Fig. S6B-S6C). Previous studies indicated that TAMs facilitate tumor growth and proliferation via NFSF12-TNFRSF12A and TGF-beta signaling [66, 67]. The above results revealed that TAMs within MTs exerted immunosuppressive functions and promoted tumor growth through cell-cell communication. Notably, APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs were found to play an essential role in this process.

Cholesterol metabolism mediated crosstalk between TAMs and tumor epithelial cells to promote tumor metastasis and colonization

To investigate the impact of specific cellular metabolites on tumor metastasis, we conducted an analysis of metabolic communication in CRC, focusing specifically on both PTs and MTs (Fig. S7A-S7B). Our analysis revealed significant alterations in the signaling pathways of sender and receiver cell populations comparing pre- and post-metastatic states. Notably, the role of TAMs as senders was significantly amplified in MTs (Fig. 5A), characterized by enhanced metabolic communication with epithelial cells (Fig. 5B). Among the top 5 metabolites involved in this communication, cholesterol emerged as the most abundant. We observed a significant increase in cholesterol abundance levels in TAMs, as calculated by MEBOCOST, following metastasis compared to pre-metastatic states (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, the cholesterol receptor LDLR emerged as one of the top five metabolite receptors identified in our microenvironmental screen, with high expression observed in epithelial cells (Fig. 5D). Metabolic communication elucidated the interaction between metabolites and their receptors across different cell types, and we found that TAMs interacted with epithelial cells via the cholesterol-LDLR pathway (Fig. S7C). To validate these findings, we generated TAMs with either APOC1 overexpression or knockdown. The TAMs were co-cultured with the colorectal cancer cells HCT-116 and SW620 respectively (Fig. 5E). Subsequently, we then performed a quantification assay for cholesteryl ester/free cholesterol in both TAMs co-cultured with HCT-116 and SW620 cells. Our results indicated that APOC1 overexpression increased free cholesterol levels, while APOC1 knockdown resulted in a reduction of cholesterol levels (Fig. 5F). Metabolic enrichment analysis revealed that TAMs located within metastatic lesions significantly up-regulated cholesterol-related metabolism pathways (Fig. S7D). Furthermore, we observed that TAMs in the metastatic site displayed a metabolic flux from (E, E)-Farnesyl-PP to cholesterol (Fig. S7E). The findings suggested that TAMs within the metastatic niche reprogramed the mevalonate pathway to enhance cholesterol synthesis. To further investigate this metabolic reprogramming, we evaluated the protein and mRNA expression levels of key genes involved in the mevalonate pathway using Western blot analysis and quantitative RT-PCR. We observed the mRNA and protein levels upregulation of key genes (ACAT1/2, HMGCR) in TAMs overexpressing APOC1, whereas their expression was significantly diminished in APOC1-deficient TAMs (Fig. 5G-H). Meanwhile, we found elevated expression of key genes (ACAT1/2, HMGCR) in both metastatic and APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs (Fig. S7F).

We investigated the alterations in downstream pathways of paired epithelial cells influenced by primary and metastatic TAMs. Our findings indicated that molecular pathways associated with cell adhesion and actin cytoskeleton regulation were upregulated at metastatic site compared to the primary sites (Fig. 5I). Previous studies have indicated that tumor cells regulate adhesion through cholesterol metabolism [68], which suggested that TAMs played a significant role in promoting tumor metastasis by cholesterol-mediated colonization of epithelial cells, as demonstrated by our analysis. To evaluate the impact of APOC1 expression on tumor cell functional properties, we measured the adhesion and proliferation of HCT-116 and SW620 cells co-cultured with TAMs. Co-culturing with APOC1-overexpressing TAMs significantly enhanced the adhesion and proliferation of cancer cell, while APOC1 knockdown exhibited an opposing effect (Fig. 5J). Furthermore, we observed that APOC1 overexpression in TAMs led to upregulation of proliferation-related genes MYC and CCND1 in both HCT-116 and SW620 cells, whereas silencing APOC1 resulted in their downregulation (Fig. 5K). We conducted an analysis of pathway enrichment in epithelial cells within metastatic lesions and confirmed that several tumor colonization pathways were significantly upregulated in these lesions compared to primary lesions. These pathways include cell adhesion and WNT signaling pathway (Fig. 5L and Fig. S7G). Additionally, we observed that the copy number variation score of epithelial cells was elevated in metastatic lesions relative to PTs (Fig. S7H). It underscored the heightened genomic instability and ongoing clonal evolution occurring in these cells. This increased genetic heterogeneity likely represented an adaptation to the stringent selection pressures encountered during metastasis and colonization, potentially enabling the acquisition of advantageous traits for survival and proliferation within the distant niche. Collectively, these results indicated that TAMs at metastatic sites modulated the tumor microenvironment through cholesterol metabolites, interacting with tumor cells via the LDLR pathway. This interaction enhanced the adhesion function of tumor cells and activated pathways regulating the actin cytoskeleton, thereby promoting metastasis and colonization of tumor cells.

Targeting TAMs in metastatic lesions with DC-45-A2 and BACCATIN-III for therapeutic intervention

To investigate the therapeutic approaches for preventing colorectal cancer liver metastasis by targeting TAMs in MTs, we re-clustered TAMs and annotated their tissue origin and subtypes in UMAP plot (Fig. 6A). Subsequently, we used BeyondCell algorithm [50] to analyze the drug sensitivity of metastatic TAMs and APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs. This analysis identified Trioxacarcin DC-45-A2 and BACCATIN-III as high-sensitive drugs (Fig. 6B-C). Trioxacarcin DC-45-A2 is an antitumor antibiotic from the trioxacarcin class, known to intercalate DNA and cause double-strand breaks. BACCATIN-III is a taxane precursor and microtubule-interacting agent, serving as a key intermediate for clinically important chemotherapy drugs like paclitaxel. UMAP visualization confirmed that the BC scores for these two drugs were elevated in metastatic TAMs and APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs (Fig. 6D). Additionally, we depicted the sensitivity of TAMs from primary and metastatic sites, CXCL8+ TAMs and APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs to these two drugs using histograms (Fig. S8A-S8B). The results indicated that metastatic TAMs and APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs were more sensitive to DC-45-A2 and BACCATIN-III. Notably, Baccatin-III has been shown to impede tumor progression by impeding the accumulation and functions of MDSCs [69]. The subsequent investigation aimed to elucidate the potential mechanism of DC-45-A2 affecting TAMs. Utilizing the Pharmmapper tool for target prediction, we identified APEX1, CLK4 and HIF1A as the top three putative targets of DC-45-A2 (Table S4) [51]. The expression of APEX1 and HIF1A was found to be elevated in metastatic site and APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs, while CLK4 expression was not found in TAMs (Fig. 6E and Fig. S8C). Analysis of the survival rate associated with APEX1 and HIF1A showed that APEX1 had no significant effect on the prognostic of CRC, while the survival outcomes of patients with high levels of HIF1A exhibited markedly worse (Fig. 6F-G and Fig. S8D-S8E). As these survival analyses were derived from bulk tumor transcriptomic data, the prognostic association of HIF1A cannot be directly attributed to TAM-specific HIF1A expression. We stratified patients according to both bulk HIF1A expression and the inferred proportion of APOC1⁺IFI30⁺ TAMs in the CRC cohorts. Patients with both high HIF1A expression and high APOC1⁺IFI30⁺ TAM proportion showed poorer overall survival than those with low HIF1A expression and low APOC1⁺IFI30⁺ TAM proportion (Fig. S8F–G). These results suggest that the adverse prognostic association of HIF1A is more evident in APOC1⁺IFI30⁺ TAM-enriched CRC tumors. In summary, our findings revealed that metastatic and APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs in CRC exhibited heightened sensitivity to DC-45-A2 and BACCATIN-III, with potential therapeutic implications. We further identified that APEX1 and HIF1A as the key targets of DC-45-A2, with HIF1A specifically associated with poor survival rate in CRC patients.

Discussion

In this study, we utilized scRNA-seq to identify the subset of TAMs names APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs, specifically enriched within the liver metastatic lesions of CRC. These TAMs were significantly elevated in metastatic liver tumors compared to primary CRC and played a key role in promoting tumor proliferation and metastatic colonization in the liver. They facilitated cellular interactions that result in immunosuppression, thereby promoting tumor growth and metastasis. The liver presents a unique immunological environment where resident macrophages, such as Kupffer cells, along with recruited monocyte-derived macrophages, are influenced by tumor-derived factors to establish a pre-metastatic niche (PMN) [42]. While our multi-omics approach robustly identified the spatial enrichment and functional state of this specific APOC1+IFI30+ subset, it remains an open question whether these cells originate from the reprogramming of resident Kupffer cells or represent recruited monocyte-derived macrophages that have acquired a unique transcriptional signature upon entering the metastatic niche. Our identification of the specific APOC1+IFI30+ subset reveals an additional layer of macrophage heterogeneity, suggesting specialized pro-tumoral functions at distinct stages of metastatic colonization. Furthermore, we also found that APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs enhanced cholesterol metabolism, which subsequently increased tumor cell adhesion and colonization. Our findings provided valuable into the role of TAMs in CRC liver metastasis and underscore their potential as therapeutic targets. A schematic illustrating this mechanism diagram was presented in Fig. 7.

Model of APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs promoting CRC liver metastasis. Upon liver metastasis of colorectal cancer, the intra-metastatic niche exhibits a marked expansion of APOC1⁺IFI30⁺ TAMs relative to the corresponding primary tumor. These TAMs display markedly enhanced immunosuppressive capacity along with a robust upregulation of de novo cholesterol biosynthesis. Through the secretion of cholesterol and multiple soluble cell factors including TNFSF12, TGFB1, MIF, and LGALS9, the APOC1⁺IFI30⁺ TAM subset potentiates tumor cell adhesion and proliferation, while concurrently fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment that collectively supports sustained metastatic outgrowth

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the distinct role of APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs in CRC liver metastasis, specifically focusing on their effects on cancer cell growth and colonization rather than migration. Our strategy integrated high-throughput single-cell RNA sequencing with spatial transcriptomics to achieve multi-dimensional tumor-microenvironment mapping. This cell type-specific heterogeneity is not restricted to macrophages. Previous reports have documented significant differences in the composition and functional states of other immune cells between primary and metastatic CRC tumors. For instance, in the context of T cells, Liu et al. classified exhausted CD8+ T cells (Tex) and activated regulatory T cells as M-type (malignancy-associated), while natural killer and mucosal-associated invariant T cells were defined as N-type (niche-associated) [28]. Similarly, Xu et al. ‘s scRNA-seq study on B cells identified distinct subpopulations in CRC, revealing that activated B cells were significantly depleted in primary tumors of patients with liver metastasis. Importantly, they discovered a metastasis-associated B cell subtype derived from activated B cells, highlighting the functional heterogeneity of tumor-infiltrating B cells in CRC metastasis [70]. Unlike most studies focusing on TAMs derived from the primary tumor as drivers of cancer metastasis [71–74], our work revealed that the APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs subset arising within the metastatic niche orchestrated tumor colonization through integrated immunosuppressive functions and metabolic reprogramming. Angiogenesis is a rate-limiting step for the sustained growth of metastatic lesions following colonization [9, 10]. The pro-angiogenic properties of this TAM subset promote neovascularization within the liver metastatic niche, providing oxygen and nutrients for tumor proliferation while establishing routes for further dissemination. Furthermore, these angiogenic effects remodel the microenvironment and facilitate the recruitment of additional immunosuppressive cells. This synergy amplifies the pro-tumor milieu, contributing to the poor prognosis associated with high APOC1⁺IFI30⁺ TAMs infiltration. Collectively, these findings further delineate the multidimensional pro-metastatic mechanisms of this TAM subset.

These results were in line with previous studies that emphasized the immunosuppressive characteristics of TAMs within the tumor microenvironment [22]. Wang et al. indicated that primary CRC cells stimulated TAMs to produce CXCL1, which attracted CXCR2-positive MDSCs to the liver, thereby promoting PMN formation and facilitating liver metastasis development [75]. Similarly, Zhao et al. illustrated that M2 TAMs secreted CXCL13, activating a feedback loop that induced PMN formation in CRC cells, underscoring the pivotal role of TAMs in both PMN development and CRC metastasis [74]. Our study characterized the APOC1+IFI30+ TAM subset, demonstrating its enrichment in liver-metastatic CRC and its dual role in promoting tumor progression through immunosuppressive cytokines (including TGFB1, MIF, TNFSF12, and LGALS9) and enhanced cholesterol synthesis. Importantly, our cell experiments confirmed increased cholesterol biosynthesis in colorectal cancer cell-induced TAMs. Previous studies have reported that cholesterol metabolic reprogramming suppresses the cGAS-STING pathway in T cells, thereby promoting tumor immune escape [76]. Notably, Goossens et al. showing that ABC transporter-mediated cholesterol efflux promoted TAM reprogramming, may represent another important axis in TAM-mediated tumor progression [77]. Together, these findings highlighted the complex interplay between cholesterol metabolism pathways in shaping TAM phenotypes and their tumor-promoting functions.

Notably, APOC1, a pivotal protein in lipoprotein metabolism, had been implicated in various cancers, including CRC. Its expression was found to be elevated in CRC and associated with cancer progression, immune cell infiltration, and macrophage polarization [78–81]. Specifically, within the context of macrophages, APOC1 had been demonstrated to promote an M2-like polarization phenotype by modulating lipid raft formation and subsequent inflammatory signaling [80]. This reprogramming of macrophage function by APOC1 fostered a tumor-supportive and immunosuppressive microenvironment. Similarly, IFI30, also known as interferon-gamma-inducible protein 30 and recognized for its role in antigen processing, and immune regulation, has been implicated in immune cell infiltration and immunosuppressive phenotypes within the tumor microenvironment [82, 83]. Consistent with these established roles, our study demonstrated that the co-expression of APOC1 and IFI30 defined a TAM subset enriched in liver-metastatic CRC. This subset played a pivotal role in tumor growth and metastasis through the TNFSF12-TNFRSF12A and TGFB1-(TGFBR1 + TGFBR2) ligand-receptor signaling pathways, as confirmed by spatial transcriptomic analysis demonstrating their co-localization patterns within the metastatic tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, our findings demonstrated that APOC1-mediated dysregulation of cholesterol metabolism promotes resistance to ferroptosis, potentially through mechanisms involving GPX4 upregulation and 27HC-induced M2 macrophage polarization, thereby enhancing tumorigenicity and metastasis [84–86].

Based on our findings, our computational drug prediction analysis identified two compounds, Baccatin III and DC-45-A2, as potential agents to target the pro-metastatic activity of APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs. Although the MVA/cholesterol biosynthesis pathway is activated in APOC1⁺IFI30⁺ TAMs, cholesterol-targeting agents, such as statins or ACAT/SOAT1 inhibitors, were not prioritized under our current drug-screening criteria and therefore were not included among the top candidates. Nevertheless, recent CRC studies support the therapeutic relevance of targeting cholesterol metabolism: statins have been reported to suppress CRC tumor growth and modulate Wnt/β-catenin signaling, while pitavastatin and atorvastatin can enhance 5-FU cytotoxicity and overcome chemoresistance in CRC models [87–89]. In addition, ACAT1/SOAT1 inhibition with avasimibe showed marked antiproliferative synergy with 5-FU in human CRC cells [90]. These findings suggest that cholesterol-metabolism-targeting agents may represent a biologically relevant therapeutic direction in CRC, although their specific efficacy in APOC1⁺IFI30⁺ TAM-enriched tumors require further validation. Targeting immunosuppressive myeloid cells in the PMN is a promising therapeutic strategy [91]. Baccatin III, a natural diterpene best known as a key precursor for the chemotherapy drug paclitaxel, has demonstrated significant immunomodulatory and anticancer activities of its own. Research has shown that Baccatin III can suppress tumor growth in models of colon cancer by inhibiting the accumulation and suppressive function of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), which share an immunosuppressive role with TAMs [69, 92]. This suggests it could directly counter the pro-tumoral microenvironment created by the APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs we identified. DC-45-A2 belongs to the trioxacarcins, a class of potent natural antineoplastic products that are understood to function by binding to and damaging DNA, leading to cancer cell death [93, 94]. Notably, PharmMapper-based target prediction identified APEX1, CLK4, and HIF1A as the top three putative targets of DC-45-A2. HIF1A was further prioritized because it was enriched in APOC1⁺IFI30⁺ TAMs in our single-cell analysis, and high HIF1A expression was associated with poorer prognosis in bulk CRC cohorts. Stratified survival analysis further supported this finding, showing a stronger association in tumors with a higher inferred abundance of APOC1⁺IFI30⁺ TAMs. However, as the survival analyses were based on bulk transcriptomic data, the observed prognostic association cannot be taken as direct evidence of macrophage-specific HIF1A activity. Instead, our data supported a more cautious interpretation: given that APOC1⁺IFI30⁺ TAMs exhibited high HIF1A expression, elevated bulk HIF1A levels likely reflected a substantial infiltration of these TAM cells. This hinted that high HIF1A expression within APOC1⁺IFI30⁺ TAMs was possibly associated with adverse clinical outcomes, although direct validation of this specific contribution remained to be established. While further investigation is required, the combined targeting approach utilizing both Baccatin III and DC-45-A2 presents a promising dual-pronged strategy to dismantle the PMN orchestrated by immunosuppressive APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs.

Our findings add to the current knowledge of tumor microenvironment dynamics, suggesting potential avenues for developing combination therapies against metastasis-associated immune and tumor cells. Several key directions merit further exploration to refine these findings. First, the use of CD68 as a pan-macrophage marker precludes the differentiation between resident Kupffer cells and recruited monocytes as the origin of APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs. Distinguishing these origins is clinically significant because therapeutic strategies differ substantially, ranging from blocking recruitment to modulating resident populations. Future studies should employ lineage-tracing or spatial multi-omics with Kupffer-specific markers such as TIMD4. Second, the molecular mechanisms through which APOC1 regulates cholesterol metabolic reprogramming and promotes the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment require systematic investigation. This may entail comprehensive lipidomic analyses and metabolic flux assays to precisely identify the specific pathways influenced by APOC1. Moreover, as IFI30 is another defining marker of this subset, exploring its role in antigen processing and MHC-II-mediated immune regulation would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the functional identity of these TAMs, which remains a subject for future study. Third, while our study focused on tumor-immune cell crosstalk, it did not fully address how metastatic CRC cells actively remodel normal hepatic parenchyma. Successful liver colonization relies on active modification of the local liver microenvironment, especially normal hepatocytes. Although we cannot definitively trace APOC1⁺IFI30⁺ TAMs to a resident Kupffer cell origin, we postulate these reprogrammed TAMs are key intermediaries in this process. CellChat analysis showed APOC1⁺IFI30⁺ TAMs are potent secretors of TGFB1, which drives EMT, apoptosis resistance and ECM remodeling in adjacent hepatocytes; their aberrant cholesterol biosynthesis may also disrupt hepatocyte lipid homeostasis to create a metastasis-permissive metabolic niche, in addition to fueling tumor cells via the LDLR pathway. Future studies with 3D co-culture or liver organoid models are required to define how this TAM subset regulates hepatocyte fate during metastatic niche formation. Furthermore, translational medicine research focusing on therapies targeting APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs represents a crucial frontier for clinical application. Addressing these scientific inquiries will enhance the theoretical framework regarding tumor metastasis mechanisms while providing novel insights for the prevention and treatment of CRC liver metastases [95].

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study has elucidated a novel subset of TAMs involved in CRC liver metastasis, highlighting the critical roles of APOC1 and IFI30 in tumor progression and immune evasion. These findings not only enhance our understanding of the immunobiology underlying CRC but also pave the way for the development of targeted therapies aimed at APOC1+IFI30+ TAMs to effectively combat CRC liver metastasis.
