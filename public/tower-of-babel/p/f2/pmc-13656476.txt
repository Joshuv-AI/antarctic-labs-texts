Advanced herbal self-assembled nanoplatforms: targeted, stimuli-responsive, and precision therapeutics for human diseases
Authors: Xiao Zou, Jianxin Liu, Xuecong Ren, Yuxin Hu, Qian Feng, Jianing Mi, Xiaogang Zhou, Jianming Wu, Jing Wei, Anguo Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHerbal self-assembled nanoplatforms (H-SANs) represent an emerging strategy in herbal nanomedicine by integrating the intrinsic bioactivity of phytochemicals with supramolecular nanoscale organization. Unlike conventional nanocarriers that may be limited by low drug loading, excipient burden, premature release, and scale-up complexity, H-SANs are carrier-free or predominantly bioactive systems formed through noncovalent interactions, including π–π stacking, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic forces, electrostatic interactions, and metal coordination, among polyphenols, flavonoids, alkaloids, terpenoids, saponins, and related phytochemical components. Reported assemblies can achieve high drug loading, exhibit stimuli-responsive structural changes under disease-associated cues such as acidic pH, redox imbalance, or enzyme overexpression, and can be further engineered with targeting motifs. Preclinical studies have explored their applications in oncology, inflammation, neurodegeneration, metabolic disorders, cardiovascular disease, antimicrobial therapy, wound repair, kidney injury, and bone disorders, where H-SANs may act as bioactive scaffolds as well as delivery systems. However, their translational development remains constrained by dilution-dependent instability, incomplete understanding of in vivo disassembly and parent-drug regeneration, protein corona formation, tumor transport heterogeneity, raw-material variability, manufacturing reproducibility, regulatory classification, and limited human-relevant pharmacokinetic and safety evidence. Emerging strategies, including AI-assisted modeling, rational multicomponent co-assembly, continuous manufacturing, and improved biological validation, may help address these challenges. Overall, H-SANs provide a promising but still largely preclinical framework for high-payload herbal nanomedicine, and their clinical value will require rigorous physicochemical characterization, reproducible manufacturing, and cautious translational validation.Graphical Abstract Herbal bioactive molecules and phytochemicals can spontaneously organize into diverse nanoscale architectures through cooperative noncovalent and coordination interactions, including hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, π–π stacking, electrostatic forces, and metal coordination. These interactions enable the formation of micelles, metal–phenolic networks, nanofibers, vesicles, core–shell structures, nanorods, nanospheres, and hydrogels. By improving solubility, stability, bioavailability, targeted delivery, and stimuli-responsive release, herbal self-assembled nanoplatforms provide versatile therapeutic opportunities for cardiovascular diseases, neurological diseases, kidney injury, inflammatory diseases, cancers, metabolic diseases, bone disorders, and skin diseases. Introduction

Nanocarrier technology has significantly advanced pharmacotherapy in recent years [1, 2]. However, the gap between laboratory performance and clinical outcomes remains substantial, with meta-analyses indicating that a median of only ~ 0.7% of intravenously administered nanoparticles actually reach solid tumors in vivo [3]. Conventional delivery systems, including liposomes, polymeric micelles, and inorganic nanoparticles, typically encapsulate less than 10% (w/w) of active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs), necessitating large amounts of inert excipients that may trigger infusion-related reactions or accumulate in organs [4]. Even when drug loading is adequate, payloads are often weakly retained, leading to rapid and uncontrolled release in circulation, high plasma concentrations, and dose-limiting toxicities [5, 6]. Additional limitations, such as rapid clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system, off-target accumulation in the liver and spleen, and complex multi-step fabrication processes that increase production costs and batch-to-batch variability, continue to hinder scalability and regulatory approval. As a result, many phytochemicals and small-molecule therapeutics remain without clinically viable formulation strategies [7, 8].

Recent studies have revealed that a distinct group of amphiphilic plant-derived compounds can act simultaneously as therapeutic agents and structural units for nanoparticle formation [9–12]. These “structural drugs,” which include saponins, alkaloids, lignans, terpenoids, and polyphenols, undergo spontaneous self-assembly through π–π stacking, hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and metal coordination, yielding nanostructures composed predominantly of bioactive materials [13, 14]. Such herbal self-assembled nanoplatforms (H-SANs) achieve nearly complete drug loading, avoid excipient-associated toxicity, and often demonstrate synergistic efficacy when multiple active monomers co-assemble in defined stoichiometries [15]. Their assemblies are stabilized by noncovalent bonds that respond to pathological signals such as acidic pH, elevated glutathione, or enzyme overexpression, thereby enabling controlled and site-specific drug release [16]. Furthermore, surface-exposed glycosidic or cationic groups enhance interactions with nutrient transporters or lectin receptors, and structural modifications allow ligand conjugation for receptor-mediated targeting [17]. Preclinical studies have already shown that saponin-based assemblies can cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and modulate immune responses, reinforcing their translational potential [18].

In this review, H-SANs are defined as nanoscale systems in which herbal bioactive molecules or phytochemical-rich components make a major structural and functional contribution to self-assembly. Within this definition, core H-SANs refer to carrier-free or predominantly bioactive assemblies formed mainly through interactions among phytochemical molecules, including aromatic stacking, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic interactions, and metal coordination. These systems differ from conventional nanocarriers because the herbal components are not merely encapsulated payloads, but also serve as essential structural and functional units. By contrast, herbal–polymer hybrids, herbal–peptide or herbal–nucleic acid hybrids, conventional liposomes, phytosomes, transfersomes, ethosomes, hybrid vesicles, and plant-derived extracellular vesicle-like nanoparticles are discussed as extended or adjacent platforms when their architecture depends substantially on additional carrier components, synthetic materials, phospholipid or ethanol-rich membranes, polymers, or biological membranes. These systems are included for context, comparison, and design inspiration because they illustrate principles relevant to herbal nanomedicine, but they should not be interpreted as equivalent to carrier-free or predominantly bioactive H-SANs. This distinction is maintained throughout the classification, fabrication, targeting, and translational sections [9, 13, 19–33].

This review systematically explores the conceptual foundation and technological evolution of H-SANs. We synthesize current knowledge on structure–assembly relationships, categorize scalable fabrication strategies, and analyze the roles of intrinsic or engineered motifs in enabling precise targeting and multi-stimuli responsiveness. In parallel, we highlight their broad therapeutic potential, including oncology, inflammation, neurodegeneration, metabolic disorders, cardiovascular disease, antimicrobial therapy, and wound repair (Fig. 1). Drawing on recent advances in carrier-free nanomedicine [19] and smart, multi-triggered delivery platforms [34], we also discuss emerging research frontiers, including AI-assisted prediction of self-assembly behavior, rational design of multicomponent co-assemblies for combination therapy, integration with immunomodulatory agents, and strategies to meet regulatory standards for bioactive nanomaterials [20]. By bridging pharmacognosy, supramolecular chemistry, and nanomedicine, this review provides both a critical appraisal of current achievements and a forward-looking roadmap to accelerate the translation of herbal self-assemblies into clinically relevant therapeutics.

Molecular basis of herbal self-assembly

Polyphenols

Polyphenols are among the most abundant bioactive constituents in herbal medicine and possess structural motifs that make them archetypal building blocks for self-assembly. Their extended π-conjugated backbones favor aromatic stacking, while multiple phenolic hydroxyl groups enable dense hydrogen-bonding networks and dynamic redox chemistry. In aqueous environments, the balance of hydrophobic collapse, π–π stacking, and hydrogen bonding drives dehydration of local domains and nucleation of nanostructures with high thermodynamic stability. Molecular dynamics simulations have quantified stacking free energies in the range of –20 to –30 kJ/mol, consistent with experimental evidence that curcumin and related flavonoids spontaneously aggregate into ordered nanoparticles held together without additional carriers [35]. Beyond π-stacking and hydrogen bonding, polyphenols exhibit interaction diversity that extends the scope of self-assembly. Catechol- and galloyl-containing units provide strong metal-chelation sites, and their coordination with Fe3+ or other multivalent cations leads to the formation of metal–phenolic networks (MPNs). These architectures exhibit high stability under neutral pH but disassemble under acidic or reductive conditions, reflecting the reversible nature of coordination bonds [36]. Redox-active o-diphenol groups undergo facile oxidation to quinones, which can participate in Michael addition or Schiff base reactions, introducing covalent “locks” that stabilize assemblies yet allow responsiveness to intracellular glutathione [37]. Such dynamic covalent chemistry endows polyphenol assemblies with self-healing and stimuli-sensitive properties. Hydrogen-bond donors or acceptors introduced by chemical modification can further tune lattice organization. For example, phosphorylation introduces P═O···H–O interactions that strengthen packing and enhance ordered π-stacking. In addition, amphiphilic substitution of polyphenols alters solvation and nucleation pathways, producing nanostructures with distinct sizes and morphologies. The spatial distribution of hydroxyl groups across polyphenolic scaffolds also dictates assembly geometry, where symmetric arrangements promote spherical aggregates while asymmetric motifs favor fibrillar or layered architectures [38]. Collectively, these features make polyphenols exceptionally versatile for herbal self-assembly. Their molecular toolkit includes π–π stacking, hydrophobic collapse, extensive hydrogen bonding, reversible metal coordination, redox-mediated covalent sealing, and tunable amphiphilic interactions. This combinatorial set of weak yet cooperative forces enables precise control over nucleation, stability, morphology, and stimuli-responsiveness, establishing polyphenols as foundational monomers for the construction of H-SANs.

Flavonoids

Flavonoids constitute a large class of herbal metabolites characterized by a planar C6–C3–C6 scaffold. Their extended π-conjugated systems favor face-to-face stacking, while multiple hydroxyl substituents and a carbonyl group on the central pyrone establish dense hydrogen-bonding networks. The complementarity of π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding lowers the nucleation barrier, enabling many flavonoids to spontaneously aggregate in aqueous environments without external carriers [21]. The supramolecular code is highly sensitive to substitution patterns. Aglycones with free hydroxyl groups, such as myricetin, readily form colloidally stable nanoparticles, whereas glycosylation or methylation can disrupt lattice packing and abolish self-assembly [39]. Beyond aromatic stacking and hydrogen bonding, flavonoids also engage in metal coordination. Catechol- and galloyl-rich structures provide chelation motifs at 3,4-dihydroxyl or 3-hydroxyl-4-carbonyl positions, allowing cross-linking with multivalent ions such as Fe3+. These interactions rigidify the aromatic stacks into metal–flavonoid networks that are both structurally stable and stimuli-responsive. For example, baicalin self-organizes with Fe3+ into nanoscale spheres whose coordination imparts near-infrared (NIR) absorption and pH sensitivity, reflecting the coupling of π–π stacking with reversible metal binding [40]. A further cohesion mechanism arises from redox-active o-diphenol units. Under oxidative conditions, catechol-bearing flavonoids such as quercetin undergo conversion to o-quinone intermediates that participate in dynamic covalent bonding. The resulting oligomers re-assemble noncovalently into nanoparticles yet depolymerize in reductive environments such as glutathione-rich cytosol [41, 42]. This reversible interplay between covalent and noncovalent interactions adds a redox-sensitive tier to flavonoid assembly. In addition, polyhydroxylated flavonoids often propagate extensive hydrogen bonding and π–π contacts into higher-order fibrillar networks, producing hydrogels capable of encapsulating or slowly releasing associated molecules [43]. Taken together, the molecular basis of flavonoid self-assembly rests on the interplay of planar aromatic stacking, directional hydrogen bonding, metal coordination, and redox-driven covalent chemistry. These multivalent forces provide flavonoids with a versatile supramolecular toolkit that allows high payload incorporation, structural adaptability, and responsiveness to physiological stimuli, establishing them as foundational components for H-SANs.

Alkaloids

Alkaloids are a structurally diverse group of herbal metabolites whose rigid heteroaromatic skeletons provide natural templates for supramolecular stacking. Isoquinoline, benzophenanthridine, and related polycyclic frameworks present extended π-systems that align face-to-face, while nitrogen atoms, methoxy groups, and carbonyl substituents contribute hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors as well as local dipoles. This combination of π–π attraction, hydrophobic collapse, and directional hydrogen bonding constitutes the primary nucleation mechanism for carrier-free alkaloid assemblies [21]. Cationic scaffolds such as berberine (BBR) exemplify the multivalent nature of these interactions. The positively charged quaternary nitrogen enables electrostatic pairing with anionic herbal acids or co-drugs, while its methoxy-decorated aromatic rings interdigitate through π-stacking. Such cooperative forces drive the formation of stable nanoparticles when BBR is combined with partners like glycyrrhetinic acid (GRA) or artesunate, yielding assemblies with high loading and well-defined sizes [44]. Substituting the counter-ion with photosensitive molecules such as indocyanine green demonstrates how electrostatics and π-stacking can be extended to build hybrid nanostructures [45]. Certain alkaloids, including sanguinarine and chelerythrine, require no partners. Spectroscopic and calorimetric studies show that they undergo spontaneous dimerization in aqueous solution, driven primarily by π–π stacking of their tetracyclic iminium frameworks, with free energy changes characteristic of strong noncovalent cohesion [46]. Alkaloids also exhibit higher-order organization beyond nanoparticles. BBR, for instance, forms injectable supramolecular hydrogels when combined with triterpenoid co-assemblers such as glycyrrhizic acid (GA). Here, hydrogen bonding, π-stacking, and electrostatic pairing cooperate to generate fibrillar networks with macroscopic integrity, exemplifying how molecular-level affinities propagate into bulk-scale materials [47]. Overall, alkaloid self-assembly is governed by three convergent structural motifs, namely rigid polycyclic aromatics that promote π-stacking, cationic nitrogens that attract counter-ions through electrostatics, and peripheral heteroatoms that form hydrogen bonds or, under oxidative conditions, stabilize assemblies via quinonoid covalent interactions. These complementary forces enable alkaloids to form stable, high-payload, and stimulus-responsive supramolecular systems, positioning them as indispensable building blocks in the molecular design of H-SANs.

Terpenoids

Terpenoids constitute a structurally diverse class of herbal metabolites ranging from simple monoterpenes to complex pentacyclic triterpenoids, all derived from isoprene units. Their rigid, densely packed aliphatic backbones favor strong van der Waals contacts and hydrophobic collapse in aqueous media, while peripheral hydroxyl and carboxyl groups introduce directional hydrogen-bonding interactions. The interplay of these forces lowers the nucleation barrier, allowing many terpenoids to spontaneously assemble into ordered nanostructures in water or saline without additional excipients [13]. Small, highly hydrophobic terpenoids illustrate a hydrophobe-dominated mode of self-assembly. Paclitaxel, for instance, forms stable nanoparticles when conjugated with small molecular fragments, as disulfide-linked paclitaxel–tetramethylpyrazine constructs assemble into carrier-free particles that remain colloidally stable and undergo reductive cleavage in the cytosol [48]. Similarly, fatty-acid–paclitaxel conjugates exploit amphiphilic balance to form nanoparticles in which the lipophilic chains drive core packing and simultaneously shield the drug from solvent exposure [49]. Larger triterpenoid scaffolds also exhibit intrinsic self-assembly. Ursolic acid, bearing a carboxyl and multiple hydroxyl groups on a C30 pentacyclic framework, organizes into nanovesicles through the combined action of hydrophobic ring stacking and hydrogen bonding at polar nodes [50]. Betulinic acid, structurally similar but with a hydroxymethyl substitution, follows the same assembly principle to yield nanoscale aggregates with high colloidal stability [51]. Collectively, terpenoid self-assembly is governed by extensive hydrophobic surfaces that promote van der Waals collapse, strategically placed polar groups that provide hydrogen-bonding capacity, and glycosyl or fatty-acid substitutions that introduce amphiphilicity akin to synthetic surfactants. These molecular features endow terpenoid assemblies with high payload capacity, serum stability, and environment-responsive disassembly, establishing them as versatile building blocks for H-SANs.

Saponins

Saponins are amphiphilic metabolites characterized by a hydrophobic pentacyclic triterpenoid or steroidal aglycone covalently attached to one or more hydrophilic oligosaccharide chains. This dual architecture functions as a natural surfactant, with aglycone backbones packing through van der Waals forces and hydrophobic collapse, while sugar chains extend into the aqueous phase and interlock via hydrogen bonding. These cooperative interactions markedly lower the critical aggregation concentration, with reported values typically in the 0.01–0.05% range, thereby promoting spontaneous nucleation of micelles, vesicles, fibrils, or higher-order assemblies in saline environments [18]. GA, the major saponin in licorice, exemplifies this assembly principle. In aqueous solution, GA molecules stack tail-to-tail into tubular motifs that further fuse into fibrils. These fibrils entangle into pH-responsive networks with viscoelastic properties, yielding injectable hydrogels or shear-thinning dressings. The architecture is governed primarily by hydrophobic packing of the triterpenoid core and hydrogen bonding among the sugar moieties, demonstrating how the amphiphilic balance of saponins alone can produce robust supramolecular scaffolds [52, 53]. Similarly, dammarane-type ginsenosides such as Rg3 and Rb1 rely on a triterpenoid aglycone capped by several sugars. Their tapered molecular geometry favors face-to-face stacking into columns that curl into spherical nanoparticles, often around 100 nm in diameter, in the absence of any excipients. Co-assembly between complementary ginsenosides, or between ginsenosides and hydrophobic phytochemicals, further stabilizes these aggregates through lattice-like hydrogen-bond and van der Waals interactions [54–56]. Beyond simple self-aggregation, saponins readily engage in cooperative assembly with lipids and other biomolecules. Quillaja saponins, for example, interact with cholesterol and phospholipids to form cage-like immunostimulating complexes (ISCOMs) of ~ 40 nm. Here, the hydrophobic aglycone domains anchor into cholesterol-rich cores, while the sugar chains orient toward the solvent, producing highly ordered architectures stabilized by steric and hydrogen-bonding interactions [57, 58]. These examples highlight how the interplay of hydrophobic collapse, hydrogen bonding of oligosaccharides, and auxiliary ionic or steric interactions with lipids, metals, or partner phytochemicals confer structural diversity to saponin assemblies. In summary, the molecular basis of saponin self-assembly arises from the large hydrophobic surfaces of the aglycone that promote van der Waals collapse, the pendant oligosaccharides that enhance hydration and hydrogen bonding, and the capacity to engage in cooperative interactions with lipids or other biomolecules. Acting together, these multivalent forces enable the formation of assemblies with high drug loading, colloidal stability, and sensitivity to environmental cues, establishing saponins as versatile scaffolds within H-SANs.

Driving forces of self-assembly

H-SANs are governed by a set of fundamental noncovalent and coordination interactions that collectively dictate their nucleation, stability, and morphology. These forces not only determine whether a molecule can self-organize in aqueous environments but also influence responsiveness to pH, redox conditions, and biological interfaces. Across the reported H-SAN systems, several provisional design principles can be extracted, although direct head-to-head comparisons remain limited. First, successful self-assembly is most consistently favored by five molecular features: extended aromatic surfaces that support π–π stacking; balanced hydrophilicity and hydrophobicity that enable dehydration-driven nucleation without irreversible precipitation; multiple hydrogen-bonding donors or acceptors that provide directional cohesion; ionizable groups that allow electrostatic pairing and pH responsiveness; and catechol, galloyl, carboxyl, or related motifs that support metal coordination. Molecules containing only one of these features may form aggregates, but ordered and reproducible H-SANs usually require cooperative interactions among several motifs [20, 21, 59–71]. Second, the interaction modes differ substantially in their expected physiological robustness. Hydrophobic packing and aromatic stacking can generate compact high-loading assemblies, but these systems may be vulnerable to dilution, serum protein adsorption, and competitive hydrophobic binding in vivo. Hydrogen-bonded networks provide adaptability and reversible release, but they are sensitive to pH, ionic strength, hydration, and competing biological solutes. Electrostatic assemblies are simple and efficient, especially for cationic alkaloids and anionic herbal acids, but may be weakened in salt-rich biological fluids or after protein-corona formation. Metal coordination can improve structural integrity and stimuli responsiveness, but it also introduces concerns related to metal speciation, ligand exchange, catalytic activity, long-term tissue retention, and regulatory control. Therefore, translational potential should be judged not only by drug loading or morphology, but also by reproducibility, dilution stability, serum stability, controlled disassembly, manufacturability, safety, and regulatory feasibility [20, 21, 59–63, 71–81].

π–π stacking

π–π stacking is one of the fundamental driving forces for the self-assembly of herbal-derived nanomaterials. Planar natural products with extended conjugation, such as curcumin, rhein, quercetin, icariin, and isoquinoline alkaloids, readily align in a face-to-face manner through π–π interactions [82]. Molecular dynamics simulations and UV–Vis spectroscopy provide strong evidence for this mechanism. For example, epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) can co-assemble with curcumin, with a redshift in UV–Vis absorption (425 to 435 nm) confirming aromatic stacking and explaining the observed stability and high retention of curcumin in aqueous environments [83]. The strength of π–π stacking depends strongly on molecular planarity. Fundamental analyses show that introducing sp3 carbons or torsional strain reduces stacking energies, whereas fully conjugated, coplanar aromatics maximize cohesive forces [59, 84]. Anthraquinones such as rhein, with rigid planar frameworks, exploit strong π–π interactions to nucleate tightly packed nanofibers (~ 30 nm) that cross-link into hydrogels or carrier-free nanoparticles with drug-loading efficiencies approaching 100% [60, 85]. In contrast, partial hydrogenation (e.g., rhein to dihydrorhein) disrupts conjugation, weakens stacking, and produces unstable aggregates with markedly lower colloidal stability [59, 84]. Among flavonoids, strict coplanarity between the A and B rings promotes columnar stacking and compact nanoparticle formation. When bulky glycosidic substituents distort planarity, π overlap decreases and assemblies shift toward ribbon-like or fibrillar morphologies, which disassemble more slowly and provide sustained release [64, 65]. These cases illustrate how subtle changes in conjugation and stereochemistry dictate stacking strength, assembly geometry, and stability. Overall, π–π stacking constitutes the primary organizing force for many herbal self-assemblies. While often complemented by hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatics, it is the extent of aromatic conjugation and molecular planarity that most strongly determines the nucleation, morphology, and environmental responsiveness of H-SANs.

Hydrogen bonding

Hydrogen bonding is another fundamental force governing the self-assembly of herbal-derived nanomaterials. Functional groups such as phenolic hydroxyls, carboxyls, carbonyls, and peptide-backbone amides serve as directional hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, enabling the organization of flavonoids, anthraquinones, saponins, and peptide amphiphiles into diverse supramolecular architectures [21]. Extensive N–H···O and O–H···O interactions stabilize assemblies ranging from nanoparticles to fibrillar hydrogels, as seen in carrier-free Cur/EGCG-PVP nanoparticles and rhein-based hydrogels, both of which maintain integrity even in serum-rich environments [60, 66]. Spectroscopic and calorimetric analyses consistently highlight hydrogen bonding as a dominant stabilizing interaction. In baicalin–aluminum assemblies, hydrogen bonds between –OH and –COOH groups act in concert with coordination bonds to yield spherical nanoparticles (~ 130 nm) with negative zeta potentials, protecting encapsulated insulin from gastric degradation and enabling controlled release at intestinal pH [67]. Similarly, peptide–flavonoid hybrids illustrate the versatility of hydrogen bonding. Baicalin-loaded peony seed peptide nanoparticles remain amorphous and stable under pH, thermal, and ionic stress, and FTIR, XRD, and DSC analyses confirm that this stability is governed by hydrogen-bond interactions [68]. The stability of hydrogen-bonded frameworks is counterbalanced by their responsiveness. Environmental cues including acidic pH, reductive microenvironments, and competitive hydrogen-bond donors can induce disassembly, thereby enabling site-specific payload release [60, 69]. This property is particularly advantageous for oral, colon-targeted, or macrophage-mediated delivery, where controlled release is essential [70]. Overall, hydrogen bonding provides H-SANs with both cohesion and adaptability. By tuning the density and spatial distribution of donors and acceptors, assemblies can be designed to balance stability with stimuli-responsiveness, establishing hydrogen bonding as a central molecular driving force in precision herbal nanomedicine.

Hydrophobic interactions

Hydrophobic interactions are central to the assembly of amphiphilic herbal molecules, as aggregation minimizes the exposure of hydrophobic domains to water and drives the formation of ordered nanostructures such as micelles, vesicles, and fibrils. This behavior is exemplified by saponins like ginsenoside Rg1, which display a defined critical micellar concentration above which monomers spontaneously organize into micelles. The size and morphology of these aggregates are concentration-dependent, reflecting the delicate balance between hydrophobic collapse of the aglycone core and hydration of appended sugar chains [61]. Advances in cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) further demonstrate that modulation of packing parameters in amphiphilic molecules induces predictable structural transitions, from linear wormlike micelles to branched networks and ultimately to vesicular forms such as stomatosomes. These morphological transformations highlight how hydrophobic forces, coupled with entropic effects, dictate nanoscale organization and provide a framework for rationally tuning particle morphology in natural product assemblies [86]. Hydrophobic triterpenoids such as ursolic acid follow a similar principle. Despite their poor solubility, their rigid pentacyclic scaffolds collapse through van der Waals forces, nucleating nanoparticles that can be further stabilized by hydrogen bonding or by co-assembly with amphiphilic partners [87]. Co-assembled systems illustrate how hydrophobic collapse enhances stability and drug loading. For example, baicalin–peony seed peptide nanoparticles form through pH-driven assembly, with hydrophobic interactions protecting flavonoid domains and ensuring robust encapsulation efficiencies. These structures maintain integrity against gastrointestinal stresses while enabling controlled release, demonstrating the importance of balancing hydrophobic and hydrophilic domains in achieving colloidal stability [68]. In summary, hydrophobic interactions provide the cohesive driving force that underpins micellization, vesicle formation, and higher-order architectures in herbal self-assemblies. By modulating amphiphilic balance, chain length, and steric packing, it is possible to fine-tune particle morphology and responsiveness, making hydrophobic interactions a key determinant of stability and functionality in herbal nanoplatforms.

Metal coordination

Metal coordination represents a distinctive driving force in herbal self-assembly, where polyphenolic groups, such as catechol, galloyl, and carboxylate moieties, chelate metal cations to stabilize supramolecular architectures. These coordination bonds introduce directionality and reversibility, effectively “locking” assemblies into robust frameworks while retaining sensitivity to environmental stimuli. Tannic acid (TA), with its dense galloyl groups, rapidly coordinates with Fe3+ to generate tris-catecholate networks, yielding metal–polyphenol coatings or bulk assemblies with strong structural stability and pH responsiveness [71]. Such metal–polyphenol networks serve as versatile supramolecular platforms. Their stability, redox activity, and therapeutic performance can be tuned by varying the coordinating metal ions, including Fe3+, Zn2+, and Cu2+ [62]. For instance, TA–Fe3+ coatings on mesoporous silica nanoparticles enable doxorubicin (DOX) release under acidic conditions and support combined chemo–photothermal therapy. Likewise, rhein, EGCG, and other catechol-rich phytochemicals form coordination-driven nanostructures that integrate drug delivery with catalytic or photothermal functions, underscoring the broad applicability of this interaction mode. Single-molecule coordination also contributes to functional assemblies. Baicalin coordinates with Al3+, forming BA–Al nanoparticles stabilized by hydrogen bonding and metal–ligand interactions. These assemblies encapsulate insulin, shield it from gastric degradation, and enable controlled release at intestinal pH, achieving oral bioavailability significantly higher than free insulin [67]. Similarly, TA–Fe3+ nanoparticles have been employed for iron supplementation, where enteric-coated formulations enhance systemic absorption, reduce gastrointestinal toxicity, and provide antioxidant activity while maintaining biocompatibility [88]. In summary, metal coordination acts as a molecular “fuse” in herbal assemblies, imparting mechanical strength, environmental responsiveness, and multifunctionality. The interplay between polyphenolic ligands and transition metal ions not only stabilizes nanostructures but also enables stimulus-responsive behaviors such as pH-triggered drug release, redox-mediated catalytic activity, and ferroptosis induction, thereby expanding the design space of H-SANs.

Electrostatic forces

Electrostatic interactions represent a fundamental driving force in the self-assembly of many herbal-derived nanoplatforms, particularly in carrier-free systems formed by charged natural molecules. Cationic alkaloids such as BBR exemplify ionic complementarity, as the quaternary ammonium of BBR readily pairs with carboxylate- or acid-bearing herbal partners to form stable nanoparticles. Simple aqueous mixing of BBR with GRA or artesunate produces uniform nanoparticles (~ 120–150 nm), where electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged groups serves as the primary cohesive mechanism [44, 89]. Spectroscopic shifts in carbonyl and proton resonances, together with calorimetric binding profiles, confirm that ionic interactions govern the assembly process. The structural outcomes of such assemblies depend sensitively on the charge distribution of the partner molecule. For instance, baicalin, with its free carboxyl group, forms compact spherical nanoparticles with BBR, whereas wogonoside, lacking ionizable acid groups, assembles into nanofibers [89]. These examples highlight how subtle differences in ionizable substituents dictate supramolecular geometry. Electrostatic complementarity not only stabilizes nanostructures but also influences their colloidal stability and interaction with negatively charged biological membranes. Electrostatic forces are also evident in single-component systems. Ursolic acid, despite being uncharged in bulk form, undergoes partial deprotonation in aqueous environments to generate localized anionic sites. These are bridged by protonated ursolic acid dimers through ionic pairing combined with hydrophobic collapse, producing nanospheres or vesicles that remain stable at physiological pH yet disassemble under acidic conditions [50, 90]. Such pH-dependent ionization underscores the role of electrostatics in imparting stimuli-responsiveness. In summary, electrostatic interactions provide herbal assemblies with both cohesion and tunability. By balancing positive and negative charges within or between molecules, herbal compounds can be directed into nanoparticles, vesicles, or fibrillar structures that maintain stability in neutral media while disassembling under acidic or competitive ionic conditions. This dual role of stabilizing structure and enabling environmental responsiveness positions electrostatic forces as a key molecular driving force in the design of H-SANs.

Classification of nanostructures

Nanofiber

Nanofibers are one-dimensional assemblies with diameters typically ranging from 10 to 500 nm, characterized by high surface-to-volume ratios, abundant functional groups, and structural resemblance to extracellular matrix (ECM) fibrils [91]. In H-SANs, they can be generated through two main strategies. In bottom-up supramolecular assembly, phytochemicals with planar or amphiphilic structures organize into fibrillar networks via π–π stacking, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic collapse, and electrostatic interactions. Rhein, for example, spontaneously forms entangled nanofiber hydrogels, while phenylalanine derivatives co-assembled with short cationic peptides yield ordered helical or aligned fibrillar architectures [60, 92]. In top-down fabrication, electrospinning provides a scalable method to construct nanofiber mats with tunable diameter, alignment, and porosity. Polymer–phytochemical blends, such as chitosan/poly(vinyl alcohol) with ursolic acid or poly(lactic acid) with curcumin, demonstrate how natural products can be incorporated into continuous fibers, conferring both structural functionality and controlled release capacity [93, 94]. Overall, nanofibers represent a versatile structural class within herbal nanoplatforms. Whether arising spontaneously through molecular self-assembly or engineered by electrospinning, they offer controllable morphology, high loading potential, and responsiveness to environmental cues, making them an important category of herbal-derived nanostructures.

Micelles

Micelles are core–shell nanostructures typically 5–100 nm in diameter, formed when amphiphilic herbal molecules exceed their critical micelle concentration in aqueous environments [95]. Their architecture features hydrophobic cores that sequester poorly soluble phytochemicals and hydrophilic shells that confer colloidal stability, enabling both solubilization and responsive release. Two general construction routes are observed in herbal nanoplatforms. In carrier-free assemblies, natural saponins and related amphiphiles spontaneously form micelles without auxiliary excipients. For instance, ginsenoside Rb1 generates ultrasmall micelles (~ 8 nm), highlighting the ability of glycosylated triterpenoids to self-organize into nanoscale aggregates [96, 97]. In conjugation or co-assembly strategies, hydrophobic herbal scaffolds are covalently linked to hydrophilic segments or combined with block copolymers, thereby tuning micellar size, charge, and responsiveness. Representative systems include ginsenoside Rg3 with Pluronic F127 yielding uniform micelles (~ 49 nm), paclitaxel–baicalein prodrug micelles (~ 92 nm, narrow dispersity), and hyaluronic acid (HA)–decorated ginsenoside–chitosan micelles (~ 126 nm, positively charged). Polyethylene glycol (PEG)-based micelles carrying oleanolic acid further demonstrate long-term colloidal stability and efficient tissue penetration [98, 99]. Overall, herbal micelles constitute a versatile class of nanostructures that can be formed directly from natural amphiphiles or engineered through conjugation and co-assembly. By adjusting molecular composition and assembly conditions, micelles offer controllable dimensions, surface properties, and release profiles, positioning them as an important platform for herbal-derived nanomedicine.

A key limitation of micelle-like and amphiphilic carrier-free H-SANs is that many assemblies are maintained by dynamic noncovalent equilibria rather than by permanent covalent architectures. After intravenous administration, a low-volume formulation is rapidly diluted in the circulatory volume and exposed to serum proteins, salts, shear stress, and competing hydrophobic or electrostatic interactions. If the local concentration of self-assembling phytochemical components falls below the critical aggregation concentration or critical micelle concentration, hydrophobic packing, hydrogen bonding, aromatic stacking, and electrostatic cohesion may weaken, leading to partial disassembly and premature payload release before sufficient accumulation at the target site. Therefore, apparent colloidal stability in storage buffer should not be interpreted as evidence of systemic stability. Future studies should routinely report CAC or CMC values together with dilution stability, serum stability, release kinetics, and structural integrity in biologically relevant media. Existing reports on ginsenoside and saponin micelles provide representative CAC/CMC-related examples, but these values should be interpreted according to molecular composition, assay method, ionic strength, protein content, temperature, and formulation context rather than treated as universal thresholds [18, 61, 96, 97]. Stabilization strategies including metal coordination, covalent prodrug dimerization, reversible crosslinking, and sterically stabilized surface modification may help preserve particle integrity below the thermodynamic dilution limit. However, these strategies should be evaluated together with release efficiency, biodegradability, parent-drug regeneration, and safety because excessive stabilization may prevent timely disassembly or active-compound release at the intended pathological site [48, 62, 71, 100, 101].

Vesicles

Vesicles are bilayer nanostructures formed by the spontaneous assembly of amphiphilic molecules into closed shells that enclose an aqueous lumen [22]. Their architecture allows simultaneous encapsulation of hydrophilic agents within the core and lipophilic agents within the bilayer, providing protection to sensitive compounds and enabling controlled release. In herbal nanoplatforms, vesicles are constructed through several routes. Conventional liposomes and phytosomes incorporate phytochemicals, such as curcumin, silybin, or baicalin, into phospholipid bilayers, improving solubility and stability. Ultra-deformable transfersomes introduce edge activators or polymer coronas to enhance flexibility and penetration across biological barriers. Hybrid vesicles combining lipid bilayers with polymer matrices expand structural stability and functional tuning. In parallel, plant-derived exosome-like nanoparticles from edible sources (e.g., ginger, grape, broccoli) represent naturally secreted vesicular carriers that package phytochemicals and nucleic acids with intrinsic tissue homing and gastrointestinal stability [23, 24]. For example, baicalin has been encapsulated in proteovesicles derived from ATP synthase (~ 120 nm, ζ ≈ –27 mV, stable for > 90 days) and in polymer-stabilized gellan-based transfersomes (~ 107 nm, PDI ≤ 0.16) that increase dispersion viscosity and enhance dermal deposition [25, 26]. Ginger-derived exosome-like nanoparticles similarly carry 6-shogaol and small RNAs, tolerate gastrointestinal transit, and exhibit tissue-specific accumulation, underscoring the potential of edible-plant vesicles as cell-free oral delivery systems [23, 24, 27]. Overall, vesicular systems are included in this review with a defined conceptual boundary rather than treated as a uniform subclass of H-SANs. Vesicle-like assemblies may be considered closer to core H-SANs only when herbal bioactive molecules themselves make a major structural contribution to bilayer or membrane-like self-assembly. By contrast, conventional liposomes, phytosomes, ultra-deformable transfersomes, ethosomes, polymer-stabilized vesicles, and hybrid vesicles are better regarded as functionally analogous or adjacent delivery formats when their architectures are mainly supported by phospholipids, surfactants, ethanol-rich membranes, polymers, or other carrier materials. Plant-derived extracellular vesicle-like nanoparticles should also be discussed separately because they originate from biological membrane structures and contain endogenous lipids, proteins, RNAs, and phytochemical cargos, rather than being formed by direct supramolecular assembly of purified phytochemicals. This distinction allows vesicular platforms to be compared with H-SANs while preserving the carrier-free or predominantly bioactive concept emphasized in this review [22–30].

Within this adjacent vesicular context, ethosomes deserve brief mention as representative transdermal delivery systems for herbal and phytochemical bioactives. These ethanol-containing vesicles can enhance skin permeation and have been used to address poor aqueous solubility, limited stability, and delivery inefficiency of selected phytochemicals. However, ethosomes are not carrier-free H-SANs in the strict sense because their vesicular structure is primarily supported by phospholipids and ethanol-rich membrane organization. They are therefore included here only as comparative transdermal delivery formats that may inform the design of herbal nanoplatforms, rather than as core subclasses of H-SANs [31–33].

Spheres, rods, and core–shells

Nanoparticles represent zero-dimensional assemblies generated by the supramolecular organization of phytochemicals, typically ranging from tens to a few hundred nanometers in size [102]. Their geometry can be tuned from isotropic spheres to anisotropic rods and hierarchical core–shell structures, and particle shape is closely linked to biodistribution, cellular uptake, and responsiveness to stimuli. Carrier-free assemblies illustrate the versatility of herbal compounds in forming spherical nanoparticles. BBR, for instance, undergoes counterion exchange with GRA to yield monodisperse spheres (~ 110 nm) with complete loading efficiency and enhanced intrinsic fluorescence. Similar electrostatic co-assembly of BBR with chlorogenic acid (CGA) produces nanoparticles around 90 nm, while TA templating adds adhesive properties at the particle surface, facilitating interactions with bacterial envelopes while retaining biocompatibility [103–105]. Anisotropic structures can be accessed by modulating molecular planarity or supersaturation kinetics. A notable example is the co-precipitation of 10-hydroxycamptothecin with chlorin e6, producing uniform nanorods (~ 165 nm) that enhance cellular internalization and photodynamic response under visible light, demonstrating how directional growth yields functional anisotropy [106]. Core–shell designs further expand structural tunability by decoupling mechanical integrity from responsiveness. Amphiphilic IR-780–lactobionic acid conjugates co-assembled with a redox-cleavable camptothecin dimer create a NIR absorbing corona for targeting and photothermal conversion, while the inner core ensures reduction-sensitive drug release. Likewise, mesoporous silica nanoparticles cloaked with Fe3+–TA shells (~ 200 nm) exhibit stable photothermal conversion and pH-triggered disassembly for controlled drug delivery [71, 107]. Overall, herbal-derived nanoparticles demonstrate how supramolecular forces can be leveraged to control size, shape, and interfacial chemistry. By fine-tuning counterion pairing, templating effects, and co-assembly pathways, isotropic spheres, anisotropic rods, and core–shell hybrids can be rationally engineered, establishing nanoparticles as a highly adaptable class within H-SANs.

Metal–phenolic networks

MPNs are coordination-driven supramolecular frameworks formed by polyphenols bearing catechol or galloyl groups chelating with multivalent metal ions such as Fe3+, Zn2+, Mn2+, and Hf4+ [72]. Each polyphenol provides multiple binding sites, enabling rapid interfacial deposition and the construction of highly cross-linked lattices. Depending on assembly conditions, MPNs can be engineered into ultrathin films, hollow capsules, core–shell nanoparticles, or two-dimensional nanosheets. Their morphology and porosity are strongly influenced by parameters such as pH, metal-to-ligand ratio, ionic strength, and deposition time. Importantly, the coordination bonds are dynamic, weakening under acidic conditions, during Fe3+/Fe2+ redox transitions, or in the presence of competing chelators, which imparts reversible adhesion, tunable permeability, and stimulus-responsive disassembly [73]. Representative examples highlight the versatility of MPNs in herbal-inspired platforms. A TA–Fe3+ shell deposited on DOX-loaded mesoporous silica nanoparticles produces ~ 200 nm core–shell constructs with pH-gated release and stable photothermal conversion under NIR irradiation [71]. Zn2+–TA coatings on titanium generate conformal interfacial layers that co-immobilize peptide drugs such as abaloparatide and sustain their release for extended durations [74]. MPNs can also serve as substrates for hybrid architectures, as Fe3+/TA assemblies enable in situ growth of Prussian blue, yielding hybrids with peroxidase-like activity and photothermal conversion that are suitable for ultrasensitive toxin detection [75]. Similarly, Mn-based Prussian blue analogues etched with TA form double-shelled nanoboxes that combine strong peroxidase-like activity with NIR photothermal effects [76]. At the nanosheet level, HfSe₂ coated with a TA–Fe3+ network yields multifunctional composites that integrate radiosensitization, Fenton-like chemodynamic reactivity, photothermal conversion, and Se-mediated radioprotection [77]. Overall, MPNs provide a modular and tunable structural platform in herbal self-assembled nanotechnology. Their dynamic coordination chemistry enables precise control of crosslinking density, composition, and surface charge, while reversible bonding ensures multi-stimuli responsiveness. These features position MPNs as a versatile category of herbal-derived nanostructures with applications in controlled encapsulation, catalytic activity, and responsive therapeutic delivery.

Hydrogels

Hydrogels are three-dimensional, water-rich networks stabilized by reversible noncovalent interactions or dynamic coordination, resulting in soft, ECM-like mechanics and tunable transport properties [108]. In herbal self-assembled systems, they arise when small phytochemicals or polyphenol–metal complexes form percolating nanofiber meshes, with gelation controlled by molecular planarity, hydrogen-bonding topology, ionization state, and coordination stoichiometry. Environmental conditions such as pH, redox status, and ionic strength further modulate network integrity, enabling injectable, self-healing, or adhesive formats with adjustable porosity and mesh size [60, 109]. One major route is supramolecular gelation in which a single herbal molecule serves as both drug and matrix. Rhein, for example, organizes into nanofibrils through π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding, producing robust hydrogels, while its lysinate derivative yields injectable variants with improved stability [110]. GA, a saponin from licorice, forms helical nanotubes and fibrils that entangle into concentration-dependent hydrogels. These GA-based systems display long-term colloidal stability and tunable mechanical strength, and newer formulations demonstrate fully carrier-free, injectable, and adhesive gels derived entirely from natural small molecules [111, 112]. A second route exploits metal–phenolic coordination. Polyphenols such as TA, EGCG, and rhein chelate multivalent ions (Fe3+, Zn2+, Mn2+, Al3+), generating dynamic crosslinks that reinforce the hydrogel yet disassemble under acidic pH, redox transitions, or competitive chelation. TA–based hydrogels exemplify this principle with strong wet-tissue adhesion and reversible bonding, while broader MPNs allow fine control of crosslink density, porosity, and responsiveness. Hybrid systems such as EGCG-grafted silk fibroin further integrate antioxidant capacity with bulk mechanical reinforcement from the polymer scaffold [113–115]. Furthermore, herbal hydrogels can be cast as continuous films, injected for in situ gelation, or fragmented into microparticles for sprayable dressings and modular drug loading. GA hydrogel microparticles encapsulating stem-cell exosomes illustrate how particulate forms extend utility in minimally invasive delivery [116]. Overall, herbal hydrogels combine reversible supramolecular cohesion with tunable mechanics, enabling adaptable, biocompatible scaffolds. Their ability to integrate structural, adhesive, and responsive features positions them as a distinct and widely applicable class within herbal self-assembled nanostructures.

Taken together, different H-SAN nanostructures show distinct strengths and translational liabilities rather than representing equivalent formulation choices. Micelles and nanospheres are attractive for poorly soluble phytochemicals because they offer high loading, simple preparation, and favorable injectability, but their clinical value depends strongly on dilution stability, serum stability, and prevention of premature disassembly [18, 96, 97]. Nanofibers and hydrogels are more suitable for local retention, wound repair, tissue regeneration, and inflammatory microenvironments, but their translation requires reproducible gelation, mechanical control, sterilization compatibility, and predictable degradation [60, 108–116]. Metal–phenolic networks and core–shell structures provide stronger modularity, surface tunability, and multi-stimuli responsiveness, but their advantages must be balanced against metal release, ligand exchange, manufacturing complexity, and long-term biodistribution [71–77]. Vesicular and vesicle-like systems can improve barrier transport, oral delivery, or transdermal delivery, yet many of them are adjacent carrier-based formats rather than strict carrier-free H-SANs, and therefore require careful classification and standardization [22–33]. These comparisons suggest that the most translationally promising platform cannot be defined by morphology alone. Instead, platform selection should match the intended route of administration, target tissue, required circulation time, trigger accessibility, release window, manufacturability, and safety profile [78–80, 117, 118].

Fabrication strategies of H-SANs

Fabrication strategies are crucial to the development of H-SANs, as they determine the structural features, loading efficiency, and biological performance (Table 2). In contrast to conventional nanocarriers that heavily depend on synthetic excipients, these systems utilize the amphiphilicity, hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, and coordination potential of phytochemicals to drive spontaneous organization in aqueous or mixed environments. Such processes produce nanoparticles with high payloads, reduced excipient content, and improved compatibility. The design space is broad, including single-component carrier-free assemblies, dual or multicomponent herbal co-assemblies, herbal–drug hybrids, and higher-order constructs that encode multiple interactions within one lattice. When polymers, peptides, nucleic acids, lipids, surfactants, or biological membranes dominate the structural framework, these systems are better interpreted as extended or adjacent platforms inspired by H-SAN principles rather than strict core H-SANs. They are retained in this review only when herbal bioactives or phytochemical-rich components contribute substantially to assembly behavior, therapeutic function, targeting, or stimuli responsiveness. Fabrication approaches also enable the incorporation of stimulus responsiveness, allowing assemblies to respond selectively to endogenous cues such as pH gradients, redox imbalance, or enzymatic activity, together with exogenous inputs including light, heat, and magnetic fields. Recent advances in green and scalable preparation techniques, including microfluidics, high-gravity mixing, and supercritical carbon dioxide processing, are accelerating the translation of laboratory methods into reproducible and sustainable manufacturing (Fig. 2) [119]. Taken together, these strategies illustrate how careful control over fabrication provides a bridge between molecular design and therapeutic application, establishing versatile routes for tailoring herbal nanoplatforms toward precision medicine. From a critical design perspective, fabrication should be evaluated by whether it preserves the intended supramolecular interaction while remaining reproducible after scale-up. Methods that provide precise control over solvent exchange, mixing rate, supersaturation, pH, ionic strength, temperature, and aging are more likely to generate transferable H-SAN products than manually adjusted protocols that only perform well at a small laboratory scale [119–126].

Herbal-derived molecules with intrinsic amphiphilicity and supramolecular interaction capacity (e.g., π–π stacking, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interactions) can undergo simple self-assembly to form ordered nanostructures. Beyond single-component systems, multicomponent co-assembly strategies integrate herbal–herbal combinations, herbal–drug synergies, herbal–polymer hybrids, herbal–metal coordination networks, and herbal–biopolymer conjugates, thereby enhancing structural stability, functional diversity, and therapeutic efficacy. Environmentally friendly green fabrication technologies such as antisolvent precipitation, supercritical CO2 processing, microfluidics, nano-spray drying, and high-gravity mixing further enable scalable and sustainable production. Moreover, stimuli-responsive assemblies are engineered to achieve controlled and precise drug delivery by responding to pathological cues, including pH, redox potential, enzymes, light, temperature, and magnetic fields, with possibilities for multi-responsive designs

Simple self-assembly

Simple self-assembly refers to carrier-free nanoplatforms formed from a single herbal constituent whose intrinsic amphiphilicity, π–π stacking, or hydrogen-bonding capacity drives spontaneous organization in aqueous or mixed solvents. Hydrophobic and poorly water-soluble phytochemicals crystallize into nanoparticles when the solvent environment is rapidly shifted, whereas amphiphilic saponins and polyphenols collapse into stable colloids or soft nanoparticles through dehydration and hydrophobic clustering. In these monocomponent systems, the building block functions as both reservoir and scaffold, which yields high payloads and avoids excipient-related artifacts [127]. Fabrication typically relies on gentle solvent evaporation or antisolvent precipitation. Curcumin, 10-hydroxycamptothecin, and paclitaxel form carrier-free nanocrystals under such conditions, and near-quantitative drug loading is routinely achieved [127]. Antisolvent precipitation performed under a quality-by-design framework has produced resveratrol nanosuspensions with a mean diameter of about 46 nm and a zeta potential near − 38 mV, demonstrating tight control over nucleation and growth [128]. Polyphenolic antioxidants such as EGCG assemble in one step into compact colloids when aromatic rings cluster and hydroxyl groups establish intra- and intermolecular hydrogen bonds, and the resulting particles exhibit improved dispersion stability and cellular uptake compared with the free molecule [129]. Ginsenoside monomers exhibit similar behavior, with the triterpenoid backbone acting as a hydrophobic anchor while multiple glycosyl residues remain hydrated, yielding micelle-like aggregates with enhanced aqueous stability [97]. Process variables strongly influence particle size, shape, and dispersity. High-pressure homogenization and media milling reduce large crystals to nanorods or nanoplates with narrow size distributions, while bottom-up antisolvent precipitation tunes nucleation–growth kinetics through solvent ratio, mixing intensity, and temperature [127]. Continuous-flow microfluidics further refines fabrication by providing sub-second mixing and well-defined laminar interfaces that limit Ostwald ripening, and gram-scale production of monodisperse nanoparticles can be achieved by adjusting flow-rate ratios and channel geometry [130]. Data-driven predictive models built from flow parameters and solute concentrations have been used to forecast particle size and polydispersity, which shortens optimization cycles and reduces material consumption [131]. In summary, simple self-assembly uses the intrinsic amphiphilicity and stacking of a single herbal constituent to form high-payload nanoparticles through water-based steps. Reproducible outcomes depend on setting solvent polarity and saturation, keeping total concentration just above the aggregation threshold, and controlling antisolvent addition, mixing rate, and temperature to fix nucleation and growth. An additional short aging stage can be introduced to consolidate nuclei, after which aggregates are removed by brief sonication and low-speed clarification. Tune size and solid state through dilution rate and thermal profile, choose drying routes that preserve redispersion, and include light stabilizers or cryoprotectants only when needed. With these controls, monocomponent platforms achieve consistent size, solubility, and colloidal stability while keeping excipients minimal and remaining compatible with microfluidic or continuous processing.

Dual/multicomponent co-assembly

Herbal–herbal synergistic assemblies

Herbal–herbal assemblies are carrier-free nanoparticles produced by pairing two phytochemicals that interact through complementary noncovalent forces in aqueous or mixed solvents. Fabrication follows a simple co-precipitation or solvent-exchange workflow in which separately prepared stock solutions are combined under controlled stoichiometry, pH, and ionic strength, followed by short aging to stabilize nuclei and removal of unbound solutes by centrifugation or dialysis. Key levers include the molar ratio of the two components, total concentration, mixing order, and the polarity gradient between solvent and antisolvent. These parameters govern nucleation and growth, and thereby set hydrodynamic size, ζ potential, and dispersity while avoiding auxiliary surfactants or polymeric carriers. The BBR–baicalin pair provides a charge-guided route, as mixing cationic BBR with anionic baicalin at near-equimolar ratios induces rapid electrostatic condensation into ~ 150 nm nanoparticles that remain stable without surfactant, with particle size and ζ potential tunable by adjusting pH and salt during mixing [132]. Replacing baicalin with CGA preserves ionic pairing while introducing stronger hydrogen bonding and π–π contacts, and at a 1:1 molar ratio the two components self-assemble into ~ 90 nm particles with a negative ζ potential and pH-responsive dissolution obtained by direct mixing or water–alcohol solvent displacement [104]. Substituting cinnamic acid shifts the balance toward hydrophobic π–π interactions, and solvent exchange followed by mild stirring yields nanospheres close to 100 nm with surface-exposed aromatic domains that promote adhesion to bacterial envelopes [133]. Introducing curcumin moves the system from an anionic partner to a neutral hydrophobic polyphenol, and controlled addition of an ethanolic curcumin solution into an aqueous BBR phase promotes uniform nucleation so that the resulting hybrids retain the optical responsiveness of curcumin [134]. In summary, fabrication of herbal–herbal synergistic assemblies relies on matching complementary interactions, balancing charge or hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, and working at total solute concentrations just above the aggregation threshold. Define a clear mixing order with short mixing times to promote uniform nucleation and limit secondary growth. Adjust solvent polarity, pH, ionic strength, and temperature to tune size, ζ potential, and dispersity. After assembly, allow a short aging step, remove oversized species by low-speed centrifugation, and apply gentle sonication only as needed. For storage, use lyophilization with volatile buffers or simple cryoprotectants to preserve redispersion. With these controls, carrier-free herbal co-assemblies are produced reproducibly with predictable size and interfacial properties while keeping excipients minimal and remaining compatible with scale-up.

Herbal–drug hybrids

Herbal–drug hybrids are carrier-free nanoparticles formed by co-assembling a bioactive plant constituent with a synthetic or semisynthetic drug through hydrophobic association, π–π stacking, hydrogen bonding, and, when present, complementary ionic pairing. Fabrication emphasizes control of molecular complementarity and mixing kinetics so that two otherwise incompatible solutes nucleate a single lattice with near-quantitative payload [17, 135]. Practical levers include the molar ratio of partners, total solute concentration above the aggregation threshold, solvent–antisolvent polarity gap, order and rate of addition, pH and ionic strength, brief post-mixing aging to consolidate nuclei, and removal of unincorporated species by centrifugation or dialysis. These parameters, together, determine the hydrodynamic size, ζ potential, and dispersity while avoiding the use of auxiliary carriers. Ion-pairing followed by antisolvent precipitation provides a straightforward route. The irinotecan and curcumin nanocomplex is obtained by combining equimolar stock solutions and injecting the mixed organic phase into water under vigorous stirring, which yields spheres around 180 nm stabilized by electrostatic contacts and π–π interactions with loading that approaches completeness [136]. One-step reprecipitation offers a closely related workflow. Cyanine–curcumin nanoparticles are prepared by rapid injection of a dye and curcumin solution into water under controlled temperature and mixing, producing 90–110 nm particles that maintain colloidal stability even at high dye fractions [137]. Hydrophobic templating is effective for taxane hybrids. Ursolic acid co-nucleates with paclitaxel during solvent shift or thin-film hydration followed by aqueous dispersion, giving ~ 100 nm particles stabilized by hydrogen bonding and van der Waals contacts, and the same strategy applies to paclitaxel with betulonic acid, yielding high loading with minimal excipients [21]. Amphiphilic saponins enable mixed micellar or co-crystallized formats. Ginsenoside Rg3 is combined with paclitaxel in aqueous ethanol and then diluted into water while controlling pH and salt to balance charge screening, which produces uniform hybrids and leverages Rg3 as both structural amphiphile and permeability enhancer [138]. When Rg3 is introduced alongside DOX within a Pluronic matrix, the herbal component serves as a co-assembly partner that modulates interfacial properties and drug partitioning, illustrating a variant in which a minimal amount of polymer aids processability without displacing the herbal–drug pairing [97]. In summary, the fabrication of herbal–drug hybrids is best approached through a set of rational design principles that match formulation strategies with molecular properties. For systems in which one partner is ionizable at the working pH, ion pairing combined with antisolvent precipitation provides an efficient route to nanoparticle formation. When both partners are neutral yet soluble in a volatile organic solvent, rapid reprecipitation becomes the method of choice. In cases dominated by strong π–π interactions and non-overlapping solubility profiles, thin-film hydration or hydrophobic templating offers more effective assembly. Regardless of the method, maintaining near-equimolar stoichiometry with a slight excess of the weaker binder enhances co-incorporation efficiency. Particle size can then be controlled by modulating solvent fraction, temperature, and mixing rate while keeping total concentrations just above the aggregation threshold. A short aging step further consolidates nuclei, and low-speed centrifugation removes aggregates, together ensuring reproducible production of hybrids with well-defined size, composition, and minimal reliance on excipients.

For chemically conjugated phytochemical prodrugs and hybrid assemblies, stimuli responsiveness should be interpreted as a kinetic chemical process rather than as a simple on/off trigger. Linkers including disulfide, diselenide, thioether, ester, or other cleavable motifs can improve self-assembly or introduce microenvironment-responsive release, but they also generate a modified chemical entity whose biological activity depends on efficient regeneration of the parent molecule or an intended active metabolite. In dense supramolecular lattices, steric hindrance, local hydrophobicity, and restricted hydration may limit the access of glutathione, enzymes, or other intracellular triggers to the cleavable bond. Suboptimal cleavage may leave partially modified intermediates or linker-bearing fragments that require evaluation for receptor affinity, intracellular retention, metabolism, and toxicity. Therefore, prodrug H-SANs should be characterized by linker stability in circulation, cleavage rate under disease-relevant conditions, parent-drug regeneration, identification of intermediates, and cytotoxicity or safety profiles of both intact and cleaved species [48, 49, 100, 101, 139–141].

Herbal–polymer/biopolymer hybrids

Many phytochemicals can self-assemble, yet their native colloids often lack the mechanical integrity, size uniformity, or long-term stability needed for systemic use. Hybridization with natural or synthetic polymers provides a fabrication route that stabilizes nuclei, controls interfacial properties, and adds targeting or mucoadhesive functions while keeping excipients minimal. The principal assembly modes are ionic gelation and polyelectrolyte complexation, solvent or antisolvent displacement combined with hydrophobic association, and coordination assembly using metal–polyphenol chemistry. Process control relies on charge ratio, polymer and cross-linker concentration, pH and ionic strength, mixing sequence and rate, and brief post-mixing aging to consolidate particles. Ionic gelation of chitosan with tripolyphosphate (TPP) is a widely used workflow. A HA–modified ginsenoside Rb1 and chitosan system optimized with Design-Expert software identified an operating window at 2 mg/mL chitosan, 2.3 mg/mL TPP, and a chitosan to TPP mass ratio of 1.5 to 1, which produced narrowly dispersed nanoparticles around 126 nm with a surface potential near + 37 mV and sustained release in simulated gastrointestinal fluids [142]. Polyelectrolyte complexation extends this strategy when an anionic polymer such as HA or alginate is introduced. Curcumin-loaded nanoparticles prepared from N-isopropylacrylamide-grafted chitosan acquire an HA corona by electrostatic self-anchoring after primary particle formation, yielding pH-responsive CUR@CS-NP@HA hybrids with improved endosomal escape and prolonged circulation [143]. Lecithin–chitosan nanoparticles carrying BBR are obtained by mixing a lecithin dispersion with acidic chitosan under controlled addition and mild shear, and subsequent incorporation into topical gels preserves particle integrity for cutaneous delivery [144]. A related alginate and chitosan BBR nanocomposite close to 71 nm with a ζ-potential near + 22 mV was produced by Box–Behnken optimization, highlighting how design of experiments maps formulation variables to particle size, charge, and entrapment efficiency [145]. Coordination assembly with MPNs provides an adhesive and redox-active shell while improving the dispersibility of hydrophobic cargos. Curcumin embedded in an Fe3+ and TA framework forms by simple mixing near mildly acidic pH, followed by dilution into water, which increases apparent solubility and supports gastrointestinal retention without extensive surfactant use [146]. Blended polymer matrices can also be processed into fibrous hybrids when a film or dressing format is desired. Chitosan with poly(vinyl alcohol) improves solution spinnability and mechanical robustness, enabling electrospun fibers between 100 and 200 nm that deliver ursolic acid with controlled release while preserving the bioactive contributions of the polysaccharide blend [93]. In summary, fabrication of herbal–polymer and herbal–biopolymer hybrids is guided by matching interaction profiles to process roles. HA provides CD44 affinity and a negatively charged corona, chitosan supplies cationic amines for ionic gelation with antimicrobial and mucoadhesive function, and alginate or plant-derived gums enable mild gelation and ROS scavenging. Practical control comes from polymer ratios, cross-linker level, and pH during ionic gelation or polyelectrolyte complexation, with solvent displacement or metal–phenolic coordination used when appropriate. Design-of-experiments maps these variables to particle size, ζ potential, loading, and release. With a defined mixing order, short aging, and low-speed clarification, hybrids are produced reproducibly as stable, monodisperse nanoparticles that retain the bioactivity of both the phytochemical and the scaffold and are suitable for scale-up.

Herbal–metal coordination networks

Polyphenolic phytochemicals that present catechol or galloyl groups chelate multivalent metal cations and form either amorphous MPNs or discrete coordination-polymer nanoparticles. Coordination complements π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding and enables rapid, aqueous, carrier-free assembly in which the herbal ligand and the metal center become the only structural components [147]. Fabrication is typically a one-pot, room-temperature process that involves preparing separate aqueous solutions of the polyphenol and the metal salt, adjusting pH and ionic strength, and combining the two under vigorous mixing. The ligand-to-metal ratio, total concentration, order of addition, and mixing time control nucleation and growth and determine whether the product is a conformal coating, hollow capsule after template removal, or a dispersed nanocolloid. Protonation suppresses bis and tris ligation, whereas higher ionic strength screens electrostatic repulsion and favors extended coordination, which provides practical handles to program particle size and film thickness. The same environmental variables also enable stimulus-triggered disassembly and cargo release in acidic or reductive microenvironments [148, 149]. For example, instantaneous coordination of ferric ions with TA through direct mixing yields Fe–TA nanoaggregates that absorb across the NIR region and achieve high photothermal conversion efficiencies, with the outcome determined by ligand–metal ratio, concentration, and quenching effects upon dilution [150]. Formaldehyde-assisted prepolymerization of TA followed by Cu2+ crosslinking produces dually cross-linked Cu–poly(TA) particles with a rigid scaffold, where the network traps copper during fabrication but releases it in reactive-oxygen-rich settings, a behavior governed by the crosslink density established in the second step [151]. Replacing the metal node with Fe3+ enables in-situ encapsulation of curcumin during coordination to form Cur@Fe–TA core–shell nanoparticles. A simple sequence of dissolving curcumin, introducing TA, and adding Fe3+ under controlled pH improves apparent solubility and yields sustained gastrointestinal release without extensive surfactant use [146]. For imaging-enabled constructs, coordinating gadolinium or iron within sub-50-nm MPNs increases longitudinal relaxivity and preserves photothermal absorption, which supports the fabrication of T1-weighted probes that can be produced by the same aqueous protocol with only minor adjustments to stoichiometry and pH [152, 153]. In summary, herbal–metal coordination networks are built by aqueous, kinetically mild steps in which pH and ionic strength, ligand-to-metal ratio, total concentration, and the order and rate of addition set particle size, crosslink density, and stimulus responsiveness. Short aging consolidates nuclei, and subsequent low-speed centrifugation removes oversized species, while trigger behavior and coordination integrity are verified under defined buffer conditions. Near-term priorities are standardized links between formulation variables and ligand–metal stoichiometry, in-line monitoring of chelation during continuous mixing, and clear dose windows for therapeutic ions that balance efficacy with long-term safety. With these controls, coordination networks provide carrier-lean, high-payload nanoparticles that are well-suited for scale-up.

Herbal–peptide/nucleic-acid hybrids

Peptides and nucleic acids provide programmable secondary structure, enzyme responsiveness, and receptor affinity that complement the self-assembly of herbal small molecules. Co-assembly relies on π–π stacking, hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction, and metal coordination in water or buffered aqueous–organic mixtures. The aim is to nucleate a single lattice that packages both components at high payload under mild conditions and with minimal excipients. Sequence design and charge balance guide peptide systems, while N/P ratio and ionizable headgroup chemistry guide nucleic-acid systems [154]. For peptide hybrids, concise aqueous workflows are effective. A cyclic L-histidine–D-histidine dimer is dissolved in buffer, Zn2+ is introduced to chelate the imidazole rings, and an epirubicin solution is added under gentle stirring. This one-pot route yields ≈30 nm Cyclo-HH·Zn particles that retain intrinsic fluorescence and release cargo as intracellular acidity weakens the coordination bonds [155]. An octapeptide bearing two RGD motifs is processed in the same manner. Zn2+ cross-linking promotes rapid nucleation, and epirubicin is loaded during or after assembly to give ≈28 nm nanospheres with NIR fluorescence and αᵥβ3 affinity encoded by the peptide sequence [156]. These examples highlight practical levers for peptide–herbal fabrication, namely ring closure to stiffen the template, placement of aromatic or histidine residues to promote stacking or coordination, and control of metal-to-ligand ratio, pH, and ionic strength to set size and stability. For nucleic-acid hybrids, two complementary routes are common. BBR-inspired ionizable lipidoids are synthesized and then formulated with siRNA by ethanol injection or microfluidics. Mixing an ethanolic lipid phase with an aqueous siRNA phase at a controlled flow-rate ratio yields ≈64 nm LNPs with high nucleic-acid loading and colloidal stability [157]. Alternatively, catechin or tannin scaffolds act as polyphenolic chaperones. EGCG condenses siRNA by simple mixing at a defined N/P ratio to form compact nanocomplexes, and TA adsorbs onto preformed lipid nanoparticles to create a phenolic shell that prolongs circulation and enables redox-responsive release in inflamed tissue [158, 159]. The same phenolic chemistry supports modular bioconjugation. EGCG can tether antibodies or enzymes through multiple hydroxyl groups, and controlled “TANNylation” of proteins introduces adhesive and matrix-binding features without harsh reagents [160, 161]. In summary, reproducible herbal–peptide and herbal–nucleic-acid hybrids are obtained by combining stock solutions in a defined order at concentrations just above the aggregation threshold, allowing a short aging step to consolidate nuclei, and tuning pH and ionic strength to set coordination and electrostatics. For siRNA complexes, choose an N/P ratio and buffer that support efficient condensation and endosomal escape. In microfluidic formats, fix particle size by setting the ethanol fraction and the flow-rate ratio to control supersaturation. After assembly, remove aggregates with brief sonication and low-speed centrifugation, and include suitable cryoprotectants when lyophilization is planned. These controls yield carrier-lean hybrids with consistent size and interfacial chemistry ready for integration into H-SANs.

Ternary and higher-order architectures

Ternary and higher-order herbal nanoplatforms are built when three or more components co-assemble into a single particle. A common scaffold is the MPN, where multivalent cations such as Fe3+, Cu2+, or Zn2+ coordinate galloyl or catechol-rich phytochemicals to form a pH-labile framework that can host additional guests through coordination, π–π stacking, or hydrophobic association. Fabrication is typically aqueous and room temperature. Separate stock solutions of the polyphenol, the metal salt, and the third payload are prepared, pH and ionic strength are adjusted, and mixing is performed in a defined order. The ligand to metal ratio, total concentration, order and rate of addition, and short aging after mixing determine whether the outcome is a dispersed nanocolloid or a conformal coating on a preformed core. Protonation lowers coordination multiplicity and favors smaller particles. Increased ionic strength screens charge and promotes extended networks. These handles allow programming of size, crosslink density, and shell thickness while keeping excipients minimal. Representative workflows highlight how composition and mixing sequence encode structure. Direct mixing of TA, Fe3+, and DOX produces TA–Fe/DOX spheres around 120 nm. The DOX is introduced before or during coordination so that it partitions into the forming network, and the acidic sensitivity of the shell is set by the TA to Fe3+ ratio [162]. Substituting cisplatin (DDP) for DOX yields a tricomposite commonly referred to as TA–Fe–DDP, in which the drug is introduced as an aqueous solution into a precomplexed TA–Fe precursor, and an additional photothermal input at 808 nm is achieved by tuning the network thickness to preserve NIR absorption [163]. Moving to quadripartite designs, an EGCG–Fe3+ framework can be assembled first, followed by co-loading of L-arginine and chlorin e6 through ionic pairing and π–π interactions. The result is a single particle that integrates a coordination shell, a gaseous therapy donor, and a photosensitizer, with size determined by the EGCG-to-metal ratio and the sequence of guest addition [164, 165]. Antiangiogenic constructs follow the same design principles, as pemetrexed, Fe3+, and hyoscine B can be combined under controlled pH to generate a self-targeting network traceable by magnetic resonance. The hyoscine component is incorporated through π–π and ionic contacts after the initial coordination step, a strategy that prevents precipitation during nucleation [166]. Recent variants introduce oxygen management or additional metal nodes. One example co-assembles a metal phenolic oxygen economizer with an NO donor by sequential dosing under constant pH, which preserves network integrity while positioning the donor in the outer shell [167]. Another approach involves the deposition of a polydopamine–Mn coordination layer onto gold nanoclusters through stepwise coating. In this process, polydopamine is first generated on the cluster surface via oxidative polymerization, after which Mn2+ coordinates with catechol groups to produce a compact hybrid with tunable shell thickness [168]. In summary, the fabrication of ternary and higher-order architectures can be rationally guided by a small set of programmable steps. The first step is to match solubility windows and working pH, and when all components are compatible in the same medium, a one-pot route can be employed. When a labile drug is involved, a stepwise assembly strategy is preferable, whereby the coordination network is first allowed to preform and the drug is subsequently introduced so that the nascent shell protects sensitive motifs. To promote uniform nucleation, total concentrations should be maintained just above the aggregation threshold, and a short aging step can be applied to restrict secondary growth. Particle size, crosslink density, and shell thickness can be tuned by adjusting ligand-to-metal stoichiometry, ionic strength, and mixing energy, after which oversized species are removed by low-speed centrifugation. For core–shell formats, a drug core can be prepared and subsequently coated with a metal–phenolic layer through alternating exposure to ligand and metal solutions under controlled pH, followed by a final rinse to quench unbound ions. Under these conditions, ternary and higher-order assemblies can be reproducibly fabricated as carrier-free nanoparticles with defined composition while accommodating multiple functional guests.

Stimuli-responsive assemblies

To better align the main text with the concepts of targeted, stimuli-responsive, and precision therapeutics, H-SAN design can be organized into four overlapping but progressively more complex levels: passive self-assembly, targeted self-assembly, stimuli-responsive self-assembly, and multifunctional precision-oriented design (Table 3). Passive self-assembly refers to phytochemical molecules or phytochemical-rich components that form nanostructures mainly through π–π stacking, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic forces, or metal coordination. These systems primarily improve solubility, stability, local concentration, or bioavailability without relying on a defined targeting ligand or disease-triggered release mechanism. Targeted self-assembly incorporates surface ligands, endogenous affinity motifs, cell-type recognition elements, or organelle-directed components to bias biodistribution, cellular uptake, or intracellular trafficking. Stimuli-responsive self-assembly introduces endogenous or exogenous triggers, including pH, redox state, enzymes, light, temperature, magnetic fields, or combined stimuli, to regulate structural transformation, cargo release, imaging activation, or therapeutic amplification. Multifunctional precision-oriented design further integrates assembly principle, targeting motif, trigger response, therapeutic payload, and disease microenvironment into a rationally matched system. These categories are not mutually exclusive, but they provide a useful framework for understanding how H-SANs progress from simple nanoscale organization toward precision therapeutic design. At the same time, most reported systems remain preclinical, and precision claims require validation of in vivo stability, targeting specificity, trigger accessibility, therapeutic index, and safety [17, 19, 34, 100, 101, 139–141, 169–181].

pH-responsive assemblies

Endogenous pH gradients, from near-neutral blood and stroma (≈7.2–7.5) to mildly acidic tumor interstitium (≈6.5–6.9) and endo-lysosomal compartments (≈5.0–5.5), offer a reliable internal trigger for fabrication and release control in herbal self-assembled systems. Protonation weakens π–π stacking, hydrogen bonding, and ionic crosslinks, and acid-labile Fe3+–polyphenol coordination dissociates in weak acidity. These features enable assemblies that are built in water under mild conditions and are programmed to disassemble after cellular uptake [114, 182, 183]. Among these, MPNs are especially amenable to process control because coordination equilibria can be set by pH during assembly, which supports on-demand release and modular combination with other functions [62, 114]. For MPN-shelled hybrids, fabrication typically begins with a drug-loaded core and proceeds by depositing a TA and Fe3+ layer at a preset pH. Adjusting the ligand-to-metal ratio and ionic strength during coating reduces the initial burst at pH 7.4 and accelerates release at pH 5.0, which has been demonstrated on PEG-PLGA cores where the coating cut the burst nearly two-fold at pH 7.4 and increased cumulative release by about 40% at pH 5.0 [149]. Porous hosts provide an alternative route, as MIL-101(Fe) can be loaded with BBR by diffusion and capped with FA-PEG to limit premature leakage and confer targeting, while the framework releases approximately 70% of cargo within 96 h at pH 5.0 compared with about 32% at pH 7.4 [184]. pH-responsive micelles are obtained by solvent displacement using protonatable backbones such as PBAE blended with HA or PEG segments. The protonation that occurs in endosomes increases chain solvation and drives payload liberation while HA confers CD44 affinity and stabilizes the corona [169–171]. Stronger intracellular selectivity can be introduced by coupling acidity with redox sensitivity. Disulfide-bridged PEtOx-SS-PLA micelles are prepared by standard solvent exchange and show coordinated disassembly under endosomal acidity and cytosolic glutathione, which yields rapid curcumin release confirmed by dual-trigger assays and live-cell imaging [172]. Carrier-free herbal matrices can also be tuned by pH during gelation. Rhein solutions form nanofibrous hydrogels at pH 8.0–9.4 and collapse below pH 6.8, with gel–sol transitions achieved through carboxylate titration that enables reversible, on-demand dosing [60]. In summary, pH-responsive fabrication spans Fe3+–polyphenol coordination shells, porous MOF hosts, ionizable micelles, and all-herbal gels. Reproducible construction depends on setting assembly pH and ionic strength, defining ligand-to-metal ratios, and controlling solvent displacement or gelation paths to fix size, loading, and stability. Near-term priorities include establishing quantitative structure–pH–release maps for Fe3+–polyphenol and related motifs, coupling pH with redox or enzyme triggers to enhance spatial selectivity, and implementing one-pot or stepwise co-assembly that is ready for scale-up with in-line control of size and release behavior. Standardized reporting of trigger thresholds, buffer conditions, and pharmacokinetic consequences in the acidic tumor microenvironment will help harmonize methodologies and facilitate clinical translation [62, 114, 182].

Redox-responsive assemblies

Redox heterogeneity across biological environments provides an internal switch for fabrication and release control. Extracellular fluids are weakly oxidizing at micromolar thiol levels, whereas the cytosol of cancer and inflamed cells contains glutathione at roughly 1–10 mM, which is two to three orders of magnitude higher than outside the cell [173]. This gradient is used during design and testing to program assemblies that remain intact in circulation yet disassemble after uptake. The chemistries most frequently employed in herbal systems include disulfides that undergo rapid thiol–disulfide exchange, mono-thioethers that are cleaved through glutathione-mediated thiolysis, diselenides that respond at lower redox thresholds than disulfides, and thioketals that fragment in reactive-oxygen-rich environments. These distinct chemistries provide tunable responsiveness to different redox conditions and enable precise control of drug release [174–177]. The fabrication of these assemblies follows simple aqueous or solvent-displacement workflows with the redox motif built into the payload or the matrix. Carrier-free herbal prodrugs illustrate this approach clearly, as a curcumin dimer bridged by a mono-thioether self-assembles into ~ 100 nm nanoparticles with a high drug content of approximately 78 wt%. The dimer is dissolved in a volatile organic solvent and injected into water under stirring so that hydrophobic association nucleates the particles, and intracellular glutathione later cleaves the thioether to trigger disassembly and release [100]. Dual pH and redox logic can be encoded in a single construct. A glycogen amphiphile grafted with urocanic acid as a pH-responsive imidazole and α-lipoic acid as a disulfide source forms ~ 129 nm nanoparticles that encapsulate ginsenoside Rh2 upon mixing in water under controlled ionic strength, while acidic conditions accelerate graft hydration and glutathione reduces disulfides to promote release [178]. Sense-and-release designs combine assembly with self-reporting, exemplified by a dual-drug conjugate of camptothecin and curcumin linked through a disulfide that forms ~ 100 nm particles in the presence of TA as a physical crosslinker. Stock solutions are combined in a defined order so that a Förster pair is embedded in the lattice, and cleavage of the disulfide in tumor-relevant glutathione separates donor and acceptor to report release in real time [101]. Oxidation-responsive hybrids complement thiol-driven systems. Constructs that carry herbal actives within redox-active inorganic or polymer matrices are assembled by dispersing a precursor such as MnO2 and loading the drug from an aqueous phase. ROS drive matrix degradation and drug release while the matrix consumes peroxide and modulates local redox balance [179]. In summary, reproducible redox-responsive fabrication hinges on selecting a reducible linker or coordination motif that matches intracellular thiol levels, keeping total concentrations just above the aggregation threshold to promote uniform nucleation, and fixing particle size by adjusting solvent fraction, mixing rate, and ionic strength. Validate responses with standardized surrogates that reflect cytosolic chemistry, typically 1–10 mM glutathione with reported GSH:GSSG ratios, and confirm stability in protein-rich media to limit premature breakdown. Near-term priorities should move redox responsiveness from qualitative trigger design to quantitative chemical-kinetic validation. Disulfide, diselenide, thioether, and thioketal motifs differ in bond energy, steric accessibility, hydrophobicity, oxidation or reduction sensitivity, and susceptibility to competing biological nucleophiles. Therefore, intracellular glutathione or ROS levels should not be treated as binary activation signals. Instead, redox-responsive H-SANs should report time-dependent cleavage rates, parent-drug regeneration efficiency, intermediate profiles, and release kinetics under physiologically relevant redox gradients. Matrix design is equally important because dense hydrophobic cores, aromatic packing, metal coordination, or poor hydration may restrict GSH diffusion and slow bond scission even when the linker is chemically labile in solution. Quantitative LC–MS/MS, HPLC, NMR when appropriate, redox-gradient release assays, intracellular metabolite tracking, and comparison of bioactivity among the parent molecule, intact prodrug, and cleavage products will be necessary to determine whether the intended active phytoconstituent is fully regenerated at the target site. With these controls, redox cues can be encoded at the linker level, amplified at the matrix level, and validated at the pharmacological level rather than assumed from the presence of a cleavable bond [139–141].

Enzyme-responsive assemblies

Enzyme-rich niches in tumors and inflamed tissues provide reaction-specific triggers that can be encoded during fabrication. Design focuses on building particles that remain intact during circulation and unlock after exposure to matrix metalloproteinases, lysosomal cathepsins, or stromal glycosidases. This is achieved by incorporating cleavable peptide motifs or glycan shells into the building blocks, or by using prodrug linkers that are part of the self-assembled lattice, so activation coincides with bond scission at the target site [185–187]. Protease-responsive peptide systems are prepared by dissolving an amphiphilic peptide that contains an enzyme-cleavable segment and then co-assembling it with the herbal payload through solvent displacement or direct aqueous mixing. In MMP-2 designs, the peptide is allowed to self-assemble at a set pH and ionic strength, the drug is added during the nucleation window, and the reaction is aged briefly to consolidate particles before purification. Cleavage of the peptide segment triggers a controlled transition in topology that can be tuned through sequence length, hydrophobic tail identity, and peptide-to-drug ratio [188, 189]. Cathepsin-B-responsive nanoassemblies follow a similarly simple workflow. A DOX conjugate bearing the minimal FRRG motif is synthesized, dispersed in water or buffered saline at concentrations just above the aggregation threshold, and allowed to self-assemble into nanoparticles around 200 nm without auxiliary carriers. Particle size and stability are adjusted by ionic strength, degree of conjugation, and mixing rate. Variants that compare FRRG with related motifs such as FRRL or FRRLG use the same steps and demonstrate that minor sequence changes modulate dispersity, uptake, and assembly robustness [190–192]. Glycosidase-triggered systems are built by layering a degradable corona onto preformed herbal or polymer–herbal cores. pH-responsive PEG-PBAE micelles are first generated by solvent displacement, then HA is adsorbed electrostatically to create an HA shell that both targets CD44 and responds to hyaluronidase. The HA sets surface coverage to amine ratio, incubation time, and ionic strength. The resulting hybrids exhibit pH-accelerated release in endosomes and enzyme-accelerated shell removal, which together improve intracellular transport without increasing excipient load [169, 192]. In summary, reproducible enzyme-responsive fabrication hinges on keeping total concentrations just above the aggregation threshold, controlling pH and ionic strength to set electrostatics and enzyme access, and selecting linker or motif sequences with documented cleavage kinetics in the intended compartment. Use short aging to limit secondary growth, remove oversized species by low-speed centrifugation, and verify trigger behavior with standardized enzyme assays that report activity ranges and buffer composition. Near-term priorities are to standardize enzyme surrogates and assay conditions, map sequence variants to cleavage rates and particle responses, and implement in-line size and trigger checks in continuous mixers so that carrier-lean nanoparticles activate as programmed by peptide, glycan, or prodrug chemistry embedded during assembly [185, 193].

Photo-responsive assemblies

Photo-responsive herbal assemblies are fabricated so that particles remain quiescent during transport and activate only at the site of irradiation. Design centers on photosensitizers or photothermal dyes whose photophysics are preserved by self-assembly. Practical workflows use aqueous nanoprecipitation, solvent displacement, protein-assisted co-loading, or coordination-enabled co-assembly. Key variables include solvent to antisolvent ratio, total concentration set just above the aggregation threshold, order and rate of addition, pH and ionic strength for coordination systems, and a short aging step that consolidates nuclei while limiting secondary growth. Aggregation behavior is engineered during fabrication. Chromophores that suffer aggregation-caused quenching are formulated to de-quench after an intracellular trigger, whereas aggregation-induced emission dyes are allowed to pack more tightly to increase emission or reactive oxygen generation. Selection of the excitation window guides the formulation. Visible light supports efficient photochemistry but limited penetration, while NIR light penetrates deeper and often benefits from local concentration effects created by assembly [194–200]. Peptide-free workflows exemplify the underlying fabrication principles. In one approach, hypericin is dissolved in a volatile organic solvent and then injected into water under stirring with light protection, leading to the formation of stable nanoaggregates. Particle size is set by solvent fraction and mixing energy, and the same route permits self-illumination by co-loading a luciferase substrate so that bioluminescence resonance energy transfer activates hypericin in situ. Process control focuses on chromophore to substrate ratio and oxygen exposure during mixing [201, 202]. Matrix-assisted designs use a quenched-to-on strategy. Pheophorbide a is first conjugated to glycol chitosan through reducible disulfides, and the amphiphile is dialyzed from organic solvent into buffer to self-assemble. Aggregation keeps the photosensitizer dark in blood, then intracellular reduction restores fluorescence and phototoxicity. Degree of substitution, dialysis rate, and buffer ionic strength tune size, dispersity, and the de-quenching threshold [203]. Protein-assisted co-loading enables combined photothermal and photodynamic effects. Indocyanine green and curcumin are incorporated into albumin by desolvation or solvent displacement, followed by mild cross-linking or high-pressure homogenization. The drug-to-protein ratio, desolvation solvent fraction, and homogenization pressure collectively regulate loading efficiency, NIR absorbance, and photothermal response, while simultaneously maintaining colloidal stability [204]. Carrier-free coordination co-assemblies can be fabricated through a one-pot route in which sorafenib and chlorin e6 are combined at a defined stoichiometry and titrated with ferric ions under controlled pH to nucleate coordination-stabilized nanoparticles. Particle size and NIR absorption are determined by the ligand-to-metal ratio, total concentration, and sequence of addition, while oversized species are removed by brief low-speed centrifugation without the need for surfactants [205]. In summary, photo-responsive herbal assemblies are produced by simple water-based steps that preserve chromophore function and confine activation to the irradiated site. Key levers are dye selection and packing state, solvent–antisolvent ratio and mixing sequence, and pH and ionic strength for coordination shells, which together set nucleation, particle size, and the balance between quenching and de-quenching. Current gaps include batch variability, aggregation-caused quenching, oxygen-dependent PDT efficacy, limited tissue penetration for visible dyes, and incomplete reporting of process conditions, with scale-up further constrained by unintended light exposure and thermal load [206–208]. Looking ahead, key priorities include establishing standardized links between formulation variables and optical metrics, developing NIR-II or two-photon active herbal chromophores and co-assemblies, and incorporating internal light sources to enable treatment at deep tissue sites. Equally important are advances in continuous or microfluidic production with in-line optical quality control, together with storage-stable designs that allow rapid reconstitution [209–213].

Thermo-responsive assemblies

Thermo-responsive assemblies use temperature-dependent solvation and permeability to stabilize herbal actives during transport and to switch release under mild hyperthermia (about 40–43 °C). Fabrication relies on two material logics. The first uses LCST or UCST polymers that change hydration near physiological temperature. The second uses bilayers or cross-linked networks whose permeability rises across a phase transition. Formulations are prepared in water or aqueous–organic mixtures under gentle mixing, with total concentration kept just above the aggregation threshold and with brief aging to consolidate nuclei. Transition temperatures are tuned by copolymer composition, molecular weight, salt, and pH, which together set particle size, stability, and on–off release behavior [214, 215]. Carrier-free or minimally formulated systems show how LCST physics supports herbal loading. Ionic derivatives of hydroxypropyl cellulose co-assemble through polycation–polyanion interactions into 150–250 nm spheres that entrap curcumin and expand upon heating, with temperature elevation subsequently accelerating drug release without the need for additional excipients [216]. HA-g-PNIPAM nanogels are prepared by grafting PNIPAM onto HA and dialyzing into buffer to induce self-assembly, with the LCST tuned near body temperature so that heating promotes curcumin release while the hydrated corona enhances dispersion and cellular uptake [217]. Polymer–surfactant architectures introduce a controllable size-change mechanism, as Pluronic F127 mixed with chitosan forms nanoparticles that shrink from ~ 300 nm at 22 °C to ~ 22 nm at 37 °C, thereby enhancing cellular entry. Exposure to 43 °C during the uptake window further accelerates intracellular accumulation of encapsulated curcumin [218]. Thermosensitive liposomes extend the same stimulus to bilayer systems, with DPPC-rich vesicles loaded with the turmeric analogue bisdemethoxycurcumin prepared by thin-film hydration and extrusion. Glycyrrhizin is co-loaded during fabrication to enhance solubilization. Heating across the lipid melting point increases permeability and drug release while preserving a narrow size distribution, offering a route to on-demand dosing that can pair with image-guided hyperthermia devices such as MR-HIFU [215, 219]. Hybrid capsules extend thermal control to coordination shells, where PNIPAM chains bearing biscatechol anchors are coordinated with Fe3+ or Al3+ on sacrificial templates, and the core is subsequently removed to generate MPN capsules that contract reversibly above the LCST. The extent of contraction and the associated temperature-dependent permeability are governed by the choice of metal and the length of the PNIPAM chains [220, 221]. In parallel, elastin-like polypeptides (ELPs) provide a genetically encodable option, in which block architecture and sequence define the LCST and simple solvent displacement yields micelles or nanoassemblies that encapsulate phytochemicals and respond within a narrow thermal window [222, 223]. In summary, thermo-responsive herbal assemblies are produced by simple water-based steps that align material transition temperatures with mild hyperthermia and confine release to the heated region. Key levers are polymer composition and molecular weight, salt and pH, solvent–antisolvent ratio and mixing sequence, and the heating profile used during characterization, which together set nucleation, particle size, LCST or UCST position, and membrane or network permeability. Current gaps include batch variability in transition temperature, serum-induced shifts in permeability, nonuniform tissue heating, structural fatigue after repeated cycles, and incomplete reporting of process variables, with scale-up further constrained by shear sensitivity and unintended warming during manufacture. Looking ahead, priorities include establishing standardized links between formulation variables and thermal metrics, integrating thermal control with image-guided hyperthermia such as MR-HIFU, and coupling thermal cues with secondary triggers to achieve narrower activation windows. Equally important are advances in continuous or microfluidic production with in-line monitoring of thermal and size parameters, together with storage-stable designs that can be rapidly reconstituted [215, 220, 224].

Magnetic-responsive systems

Magnetic-responsive assemblies are engineered so that an external field guides localization, modulates release, or supplies mild hyperthermia, while the herbal component provides the payload or the surface chemistry. Fabrication of herbal–SPION hybrids typically follows three practical routes. The first involves in situ coprecipitation of Fe2+ and Fe3+ in alkaline aqueous media to generate Fe3O4 cores, with catechol- or galloyl-rich phytochemicals serving as capping ligands. These plant-derived ligands regulate nucleation and prevent aggregation, allowing particle size and saturation magnetization to be tuned by the Fe2+/Fe3+ ratio, base addition rate, temperature, and capping agent concentration [225–227]. The second route relies on post-synthesis surface engineering, in which herbal ligands are anchored onto preformed SPIONs. Ligand exchange with polyphenols, polydopamine deposition under mildly basic conditions, and subsequent TA coordination generate stable shells that accommodate additional cargos and confer pH or redox sensitivity. The thickness and permeability of these coatings are determined by reaction time, pH, and metal–phenolic stoichiometry [228]. The third strategy involves hybrid particle formation, where SPIONs are embedded within polymeric or lipid matrices that simultaneously incorporate phytochemicals. Emulsion and solvent-displacement methods can disperse curcumin or related actives together with SPIONs into PLA, HA-bearing copolymers, or thermosensitive lipids. The polymer-to-SPION ratio, organic fraction, mixing rate, and extrusion or homogenization conditions dictate hydrodynamic size, magnetic content, and release profiles [229]. Process add-ons convert magnetic guidance into on-demand release. MPN overcoats applied by alternating exposure to TA and Fe3+ add pH-labile shells around SPION cores or SPION–drug hybrids, with ligand-to-metal ratio setting shell integrity and gating in acidic endosomes. For magnetothermal control, alternating magnetic fields are specified during development and testing, and field amplitude and frequency are matched to carrier composition to reach target temperatures without loss of colloidal stability. Microfluidic mixers can improve batch reproducibility by fixing mixing energy and residence time during nanoprecipitation or polymer hybridization. Green syntheses replace synthetic reductants with plant extracts that serve as both reducers and capping agents, streamlining SPION formation and introducing antioxidant functionality [227]. Magnetically driven swarms are built by coating SPIONs with polydopamine and TA, then assembling chains or clusters under rotating fields. The coating conditions define surface chemistry and the collective response to steering fields [228]. In summary, water-based coprecipitation, surface functionalization with herbal ligands, and polymer or lipid hybridization provide modular paths to magnetic herbal assemblies with controllable size, magnetization, and stimulus-responsive shells. Key gaps include batch variability in SPION size and magnetic loading, incomplete reporting of field parameters during testing, and sensitivity of coatings to serum and shear. Near-term priorities include establishing standardized links between formulation variables and magnetic metrics, integrating image-guided targeting and magnetothermal release with pH- or redox-responsive shells, and advancing continuous or microfluidic manufacturing equipped with in-line control of particle size, magnetic content, and drug loading. These measures support reproducible, magnetically addressable herbal nanoplatforms suitable for deep-tissue targeting and programmed release.

Multi-stimuli responsive systems

Multi-stimuli responsive herbal self-assembled nanoparticles are fabricated to read a sequence of cues in the disease milieu, remain stable in circulation, and then unlock in stages once they encounter acidity, glutathione, ROS, enzymes, hypoxia, or external light. The fabrication focus is to encode these triggers into the material architecture rather than to add separate excipients. A practical workflow starts with a base assembly formed reproducibly in water or aqueous–organic mixtures at concentrations just above the aggregation threshold. The primary trigger is placed at the shell or interface, secondary triggers are introduced into the core or corona, and short aging consolidates nuclei while limiting secondary growth. Stoichiometry, order of addition, pH, ionic strength, and solvent fraction set particle size, surface charge, and the response profile [221, 230, 231]. Metal–phenolic coordination offers a versatile strategy for constructing responsive shells. TA combined with multivalent ions at a preset pH forms a dynamic network that is sensitive to acidity and redox state and can additionally respond to oxidants. Coatings can be generated either by alternating exposure to ligand and metal solutions or by one-pot mixing when all components are mutually soluble. The ligand-to-metal ratio, overall concentration, and deposition time govern coating thickness and permeability, thereby enabling pH-gated opening, oxidase-like catalytic activity, and tunable relaxivity when Mn nodes are incorporated [221, 231, 232]. Redox- and ROS-active hosts based on MnO2 are prepared by soft-template growth or by oxidizing a sacrificial core, followed by diffusion-driven loading of herbal actives. The matrix degrades under acidic and high-glutathione conditions, couples cargo release with H2O2 catalysis, and produces Mn2+ for T1 MRI. Precursor concentration, oxidant strength, and templating time fix morphology and pore structure, and polyphenols such as TA can direct pore formation and add pH-dependent enzyme-mimetic activity [232, 233]. Carrier-free co-assemblies hard-wire orthogonal stimuli to different components of a single lattice. A disulfide-linked paclitaxel prodrug is dissolved with IR-808 and subjected to solvent displacement to nucleate an IR-808 core that self-coats with the prodrug. Prodrug to dye ratio, polarity gap, and mixing rate determine core size, shell thickness, and loading. The glutathione-cleavable linker programs cytosolic release while NIR light drives photothermal conversion [234]. Likewise, BBR hydrochloride and indocyanine green co-assemble through electrostatics and π–π stacking by direct mixing in aqueous ethanol, followed by dilution into water. NIR irradiation perturbs packing and increases permeability, which is useful in infection models where external light supplements local cues [45]. In summary, fabrication of multi-stimuli responsive herbal nanoparticles begins with a stable base particle prepared in water at concentrations just above the aggregation threshold, the primary trigger is encoded at the shell while secondary triggers are positioned in the core or corona, and the response window is fixed by composition, pH, ionic strength, and mixing sequence. Remaining gaps include incomplete definition of trigger thresholds and kinetics, batch-to-batch variation in shell thickness and porosity, and limited alignment between in-vitro trigger settings and human tissue ranges. Key priorities include establishing quantitative maps that link composition to pH, glutathione, and oxidant windows, developing modular linker and coordination libraries compatible with herbal actives, and advancing quality-by-design scale-up with in-line monitoring of particle size, shell integrity, and trigger responsiveness.

Green and scalable preparation techniques

Green fabrication minimizes hazardous solvents, energy input, and waste, and scalable methods preserve particle quality from bench to continuous or pilot production. The emphasis is on workflows that assemble herbal actives in water or recyclable media and that keep excipients low while maintaining control of size, solid state, and redispersibility. Antisolvent precipitation, supercritical CO2 processing, and microfluidic or other continuous mixers form the core toolkit and align with quality-by-design practices that define design spaces and reduce development waste [235]. Antisolvent precipitation is a first-line, low-energy route in which stock solutions of the herbal active and the antisolvent are combined under high-shear or impinging-jet mixing at concentrations just above the aggregation threshold. The solvent–antisolvent ratio, addition rate, temperature, and mixing energy govern nucleation and growth, while the identity and concentration of stabilizers determine size distribution and redispersion after drying. In the case of curcumin, the process generates amorphous nanoparticles with high drug loading and sizes in the range of 100–200 nm, while the incorporation of gelatin and D-sorbitol modulates particle dimensions and redispersibility, resulting in multi-month stability and faster dissolution relative to the bulk drug [235]. Excipient-free nanodrugs are feasible when the active possesses sufficient surface activity and when mixing and dilution conditions are matched to its logP, resulting in particles with predictable sizes [236]. Supercritical CO2 processing provides solvent-lean and recyclable alternatives. In one approach, drug solutions are loaded into nanoporous starch aerogels and subsequently dried with scCO2, where pore confinement reduces crystallinity without leaving organic residues [237]. In another, gas-antisolvent precipitation expands an organic solution with scCO2 under controlled pressure and temperature to induce rapid supersaturation, and Box–Behnken optimization yields curcumin nanoparticles of 81–230 nm with improved dissolution while clarifying the variables that govern scale-up and solvent recovery [238]. Microfluidic and other continuous mixers provide sub-millisecond mixing with minimal solvent consumption, where flow-rate ratio, Reynolds number, and residence time determine supersaturation and thereby control particle size and dispersity. These devices are compatible with both herbal–inorganic hybrids and purely organic systems, and they can be scaled by numbering-up while incorporating in-line DLS or NIR to monitor particle size and concentration in real time [239–242]. For conversion into dry products or inhalable formulations, spray drying and nano-spray drying can transform nanosuspensions into respirable powders with tunable aerosol performance, while scCO2-assisted variants further reduce solvent use [243, 244]. Combinative sequences, such as spray or freeze drying before high-pressure homogenization, improve millability and allow smaller particle size minima by pre-tuning morphology and solid state [245, 246]. High-gravity rotating packed-bed mixers provide an additional continuous, low-solvent strategy for producing carrier-free multidrug herbal nanoparticles with uniform sizes of approximately 130 nm and improved oral bioavailability [247]. In summary, controllable fabrication hinges on solvent and antisolvent selection, flow and mixing energy, temperature, stabilizer level, and drying strategy, with solid-state control treated as a scale-dependent critical quality attribute. Near-term priorities include developing integrated continuous lines that couple flash or impinging-jet nanoprecipitation with scCO2 steps and in-line monitoring, establishing standardized control over crystallinity and amorphous fraction in herbal actives, validating numbered-up microreactors capable of gram-to-multigram-per-hour outputs, and codifying DoE/QbD playbooks that link process parameters to particle size, loading, and stability. Combining antisolvent precipitation and scCO2 with microfluidic or high-gravity mixers offers a practical route to greener and reproducible scale-up, while aligning manufacturing practices with regulatory frameworks for nanocrystals and nanoparticulate systems.

Industrial translation of H-SANs requires coupling scalable preparation methods with strict control of plant-derived input variability. Herbal extracts and purified phytochemical fractions may vary according to botanical source, cultivar, geography, season, storage, extraction solvent, and purification protocol. Because H-SAN formation depends on a narrow balance of aromatic stacking, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic forces, and coordination chemistry, even low-abundance constituents, oxidation products, salts, residual solvents, or impurity profiles may alter nucleation, growth, morphology, particle size distribution, PDI, zeta potential, loading, release kinetics, and long-term stability. Therefore, plant-derived input variability should be treated as a critical material attribute rather than as a background feature of herbal formulation [78, 120, 248, 249]. A QbD-oriented workflow should connect critical material attributes, critical process parameters, and critical quality attributes within one control strategy. Critical material attributes may include botanical authentication, chromatographic or spectroscopic fingerprints, marker-compound ranges, total phenolic or saponin content, impurity profiles, moisture content, residual solvent, oxidation state, ionizable groups, and metal-chelating capacity. Critical process parameters include solvent/antisolvent ratio, flow-rate ratio, mixing intensity, supersaturation, pH, ionic strength, temperature, residence time, shear environment, aging time, solvent removal, drying, and redispersion conditions. Critical quality attributes should include particle size distribution, PDI, zeta potential, morphology, association state, loading, encapsulation or co-assembly efficiency, residual solvent, sterility, dilution stability, serum stability, release behavior, and biological activity [120–126]. Continuous manufacturing technologies should therefore be viewed not only as scale-up tools, but also as control platforms for reproducible self-assembly kinetics. Microfluidic mixing, flash or impinging-jet precipitation, high-gravity rotating packed-bed reactors, and supercritical-fluid processes can reduce batch heterogeneity by controlling supersaturation, mixing, residence time, and solvent removal. However, their translational value depends on integration with in-line or at-line process analytical technologies, including DLS, Raman or NIR spectroscopy, UV–Vis, conductivity, turbidity, pH, and zeta-potential monitoring. Such systems can detect deviations caused by raw-material variability and support feedback control before changes propagate into final CQAs. This integrated raw-material fingerprinting–continuous manufacturing–PAT strategy provides a practical route toward GMP-compatible and regulatory-comparable H-SAN production [120–126].

Targeting strategies in H-SANs

Passive targeting

Passive targeting in H-SANs should be interpreted as a context-dependent transport process rather than as a direct consequence of uniformly leaky tumor vasculature. In solid tumors, the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect has traditionally been used to explain nanoparticle accumulation through abnormal neovasculature and impaired lymphatic drainage. However, contemporary nanomedicine studies show that endothelial gaps are highly heterogeneous across tumor types, anatomical sites, disease stages, stromal densities, and host species, and may be limited or absent in many human tumors. Quantitative analyses also indicate that average intratumoral delivery after intravenous dosing remains low and variable, which argues against assuming that nanoscale size alone is sufficient for tumor accumulation [250–252]. Tumor entry of nanoparticles may involve multiple vascular routes. In addition to passive extravasation through permissive vascular regions, active and energy-dependent trans-endothelial transport, including endothelial transcytosis, can contribute substantially to nanoparticle entry into tumor tissues. Once particles cross the endothelium, dense extracellular matrix, stromal compression, heterogeneous vascular perfusion, and elevated interstitial fluid pressure can oppose inward diffusion and restrict penetration beyond perivascular regions. These mechanical barriers are particularly relevant for carrier-free herbal nanocrystals and supramolecular assemblies because their delivery depends not only on particle size and circulation time, but also on endothelial crossing, serum stability, protein-corona formation, interstitial transport, and controlled disassembly within the tumor microenvironment [80, 251–255]. To address these constraints, H-SANs should be engineered to balance circulation, vascular access, endothelial transport, and tissue penetration. Hydrodynamic diameters in the range of 30–150 nm may support longer circulation and size-selective access to permissive tumor vessels, whereas particles smaller than approximately 5–6 nm are rapidly cleared and rarely accumulate [256]. Surfaces that limit opsonization reduce uptake by the mononuclear phagocyte system and extend circulation, increasing the probability of tumor exposure [257]. Even with optimized size and surface properties, a substantial fraction of dose is typically sequestered by liver and spleen macrophages, which can hinder tumor delivery or be deliberately exploited for liver and spleen indications [258, 259]. For carrier-free herbal self-assembled nanoparticles, the assembly parameters that determine passive behavior are intrinsic to fabrication. Solvent and antisolvent choice, concentration near the aggregation threshold, and mixing energy determine size and dispersity, while polyphenol-rich or saponin-based interfaces shape the nascent protein corona and may prolong circulation without heavy synthetic coatings. Curcumin nanoformulations based on polymeric, lipid, and inorganic platforms enhance bioavailability and achieve higher tumor exposure through passive targeting in breast, colorectal, and pancreatic models, provided that size distribution and solid-state properties are tightly controlled [260, 261]. Ginsenosides offer a complementary route because they function as bioactives and assembly-competent surfactants. They form micelles or reinforce liposomal bilayers, raise loading, extend circulation, and increase the probability of passive capture, with cholesterol-like motifs helping to stabilize membranes in blood [262]. Passive mechanisms also operate in inflammation through the ELVIS paradigm, which has been validated in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and related conditions, and herbal self-assembled nanoparticles tuned to the same 30–150 nm window can accumulate at inflamed sites without added ligands [263, 264]. Lymphatic drainage supplies an additional passive pathway, as nanoparticles in the range of 10–100 nm can reach draining lymph nodes and localize within defined niches, a property particularly relevant to immuno-oncology and vaccine-like applications of H-SANs [265]. Implementation benefits from EPR-plus strategies that account for vascular heterogeneity and temporal dynamics. Short-term vessel normalization can enhance perfusion and reduce interstitial pressure, while synchronizing dosing with transient permeability windows increases exposure without requiring formulation changes. Stimuli-adaptive herbal self-assembled nanoparticles that shrink or soften after extravasation, or that respond to acidic pH and matrix-remodeling enzymes, further penetrate into tissue under otherwise restrictive passive conditions [252, 266]. In summary, passive targeting remains a practical baseline for H-SANs, but it should be defined by tumor-transport physiology rather than by the classical EPR model alone. Vascular permeability, endothelial transcytosis, interstitial fluid pressure, extracellular-matrix density, protein-corona formation, and hepatic or splenic sequestration collectively determine whether carrier-free herbal nanocrystals and self-assembled phytochemical nanoparticles can reach and penetrate tumor tissues. Therefore, future studies should explicitly couple fabrication parameters to in vivo performance by reporting size, ζ-potential, solid state, serum stability, and corona composition alongside delivery outcomes. Intravital imaging, endothelial transport assays, three-dimensional tumor spheroids or organoids, stromal-rich tumor models, and spatial biodistribution analysis should be incorporated to define model- or patient-specific transport windows. Integrating passive capture with modest microenvironmental modulation or adaptive mechanics may offer a route to deeper and more uniform tumor distribution while preserving the carrier-lean and bioactive nature of herbal self-assemblies [80, 252–255, 266].

Active targeting

Active targeting in H-SANs adds selective recognition to the baseline of passive accumulation. Ligands are presented on the particle surface to bind receptors that are enriched on diseased cells or on the surrounding microenvironment. Plant-derived building blocks and polysaccharides can be conjugated to small molecules such as folic acid, to peptides such as RGD, and to sugars such as 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) and mannose, or they can be employed directly as glycan-rich biopolymers such as fucoidan. Such ligand choices bias uptake toward receptor-mediated endocytosis while maintaining the intrinsic advantages of self-assembly and high drug loading. Increasingly, dual-ligand formats are being employed to address receptor heterogeneity in tumors and other pathological settings [180, 181]. Folate receptor targeting remains common in herbal platforms because FRα is upregulated on many epithelial cancers and binds folate with high affinity. In a fucoidan–poloxamer copolymer system, folic acid was installed to produce self-assembled nanogels that co-loaded curcumin and paclitaxel. The particles showed pH-responsive release and lower IC50 in FR-positive cells, indicating receptor-mediated uptake layered onto a bioactive polysaccharide scaffold [180]. Fucoidan itself binds selectins, so folate on fucoidan creates a dual axis that addresses tumor cells and tumor endothelium in one carrier [180]. Peptide ligands provide a complementary strategy, with RGD motifs that recognize αvβ3 or αvβ5 integrins grafted onto amphiphiles or protein nanoparticles to consistently enhance internalization and in vivo efficacy. Of particular relevance to phytochemicals, conjugation of UA with RGDS produces a small-molecule pro-assembly that forms 16–156 nm particles and exhibits pronounced tissue selectivity in vivo, demonstrating how the addition of a short peptide to a herbal monomer can simultaneously promote assembly and confer site-specific recognition [181, 267]. In parallel, glycan-guided targeting offers a natural extension, as fucoidan binds P-selectin on tumor endothelium and certain cancer cells and has also been investigated as a mediator of BBB transit, thereby supporting the concept that a single natural ligand can enable both vascular adhesion and transcytosis [180, 268, 269]. Nutrient-transporter targeting provides access to otherwise privileged sites. A glucose-mimetic amphiphile composed of DSPE-PEG and 2-DG self-assembles into nanoparticles that engage GLUTs, with uptake scaling with ligand density and being competitively inhibited by free glucose or 2-DG. This design also enhances transport across in vitro BBB models.[270]. The immune niche can also be targeted, as mannosylated HA nanocapsules enhance recognition by CD206-positive tumor-associated macrophages and increase tumor accumulation in a fibrosarcoma model. Pre-dosing with a liver-occupancy primer further boosted tumor delivery, showing how receptor targeting and systemic biodistribution management can be combined in self-assembled carriers [271]. However, the in vivo performance of active targeting depends not only on ligand–receptor affinity, but also on whether the targeting motif remains accessible after exposure to plasma proteins. For H-SANs bearing folate, RGD, transferrin, mannose, glycan, or peptide ligands, serum-induced corona formation may reduce ligand exposure and weaken receptor-mediated uptake. Future studies should therefore verify ligand accessibility and receptor engagement after serum or plasma pre-incubation, rather than relying only on targeting assays performed in buffer or serum-free cell culture [80, 248, 249, 253, 272–274]. Transferrin-receptor (TfR) targeting is frequently applied to herbal payloads, with transferrin-modified PLGA nanoparticles enhancing the delivery of asiatic acid in TfR-positive glioblastoma models and transferrin-decorated genistein nanocrystals improving solubility and pharmacokinetics. These findings are consistent with broader evidence that transferrin conjugation increases brain and tumor exposure for poorly soluble agents [275–277]. In summary, active targeting in H-SANs is most effective when ligands are encoded during assembly and their density, spacer length, and orientation are tuned to the receptor context while preserving colloidal stability. Credible evaluation of active targeting requires competitive blocking and receptor quantification linked to uptake measured in serum, ensuring that the influence of the protein corona is considered and that binding is demonstrated to mediate internalization rather than surface adhesion. Since many targeted pathways deliver cargo to endo-lysosomes, coupling receptor recognition with pH- or enzyme-responsive components, or with endosomal-escape motifs, can help convert binding into cytosolic exposure. Dual-address formats and transporter-focused ligands provide strategies to accommodate receptor heterogeneity and extend access to tissues such as the brain, while keeping the overall process aqueous and materials-lean. To improve comparability and facilitate translation, studies should consistently report ligand density per particle, apparent affinity in both buffer and serum, in vivo receptor engagement, and standard physicochemical attributes such as size and ζ-potential.

Organelle-specific targeting

Organelle targeting increases precision by releasing herbal components where they take effects. Mitochondria are targeted to modulate bioenergetics and initiate apoptosis, lysosomes to enable pH-triggered release and autophagy regulation, the nucleus to influence transcription and induce DNA damage, and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to rewire the unfolded protein response. Delocalized lipophilic cations such as triphenylphosphonium exploit the large negative potential across the inner mitochondrial membrane and give strong enrichment, while native cationic aromatics such as BBR can serve as mitochondria-seeking handles built into the herbal component. Lysosomal delivery often uses weak bases such as morpholine that become protonated and trapped in the acidic lumen. Nuclear entry relies on cell-penetrating peptides combined with nuclear localization sequences that recruit importin pathways, and ER targeting draws on KDEL-type motifs and ER-tropic lipids. These vectors can be installed on small molecules or positioned at the corona of self-assembled carriers without losing the advantages of herbal assembly [278, 279]. A triphenylphosphonium–hydroxycamptothecin conjugate self-assembled into nanoaggregates and also co-assembled with mPEG-PLGA into smaller nanoparticles. Both formats showed stronger mitochondrial localization and cytotoxicity than the parent drug, and the co-assembled particles displayed the highest uptake and tumor inhibition. This provides a practical route in which the organelle vector is grafted before assembly and the subsequent self-assembly preserves targeting while improving dispersion [278]. In a nutrition-inspired design, glycosylated oat protein was modified with triphenylphosphonium and used to encapsulate curcumin. The particles resisted digestion, escaped lysosomes, accumulated in mitochondria, preserved membrane potential, and enhanced antioxidant defenses, showing that biopolymer–herbal co-assemblies can be retargeted to mitochondria by appending cationic handles during fabrication [280]. For lysosomes, morpholine has been attached to supramolecular scaffolds and organic nanoparticles to bias trafficking to acidic compartments and to support pH-gated photodynamic action under two-photon excitation. Performance depends on whole-molecule charge and lipophilicity, and the strong mitochondrial potential can out-compete lysosomal accumulation for highly cationic constructs, which means selectivity must be tuned during design [281–283]. Nuclear delivery has been achieved by co-displaying cell-penetrating peptides with nuclear localization sequences on polymersomes or micelles, enabling engagement of import machinery and passage through nuclear pores while maintaining particle morphology. A nucleus-addressed WT1-antagonistic peptide formulated in nanomicelles suppressed refractory CML growth, indicating that peptide or herbal combinations can be adapted to this route [284, 285]. ER targeting uses KDEL or KKXX retrieval signals and ER-tropic lipids together with herbal actives that modulate proteostasis or oxidative stress. These designs engage PERK–ATF4–CHOP signaling or immunogenic cell death readouts and have been demonstrated with peptide and lipid carriers that can be translated to herbal assemblies [286–292]. Beyond oncology, organelle-directed nanoparticles are being generalized to inflammatory models, which broadens the scope for herbal payloads [293]. In summary, organelle-specific H-SANs are most effective when the targeting vector is matched to the charge and hydrophobicity of the payload and incorporated during assembly to preserve ligand presentation beyond the protein corona. Barrier navigation remains central, since mitochondrial and nuclear formats require endosomal escape, lysosomal systems must balance entrapment with timely release, and nuclear carriers must respect size and rigidity limits for pore transport. Validation should incorporate organelle-level readouts, including mitochondrial potential, lysosomal pH, nuclear accumulation kinetics, and ER-stress markers alongside disease endpoints. Within this framework, diverse targeting ligands and responsive elements can be rationally combined to guide carriers to specific organelles while maintaining aqueous, materials-lean fabrication that preserves the advantages of herbal self-assembly [278, 281, 285].

Therapeutic applications in human diseases

By leveraging the inherent bioactivity of phytochemicals and the capacity for spontaneous nanoscale organization, H-SANs have been employed to address a wide spectrum of human diseases, including cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, cardiovascular diseases, inflammatory and infectious conditions, and metabolic syndromes. In each case, the integration of phytochemical pharmacology with tailored nanostructures enhances stability, delivery, and efficacy. Anti-cancer effect

Cancer remains one of the most formidable challenges in modern medicine, with conventional treatments often constrained by poor selectivity, systemic toxicity, multidrug resistance, and limited therapeutic indices [384–388]. H-SANs have emerged as a promising strategy to overcome these barriers by leveraging the intrinsic bioactivity and self-assembly propensity of natural phytochemicals. Through noncovalent interactions, phytochemicals can spontaneously organize into well-defined nanostructures with high drug-loading capacity and minimal excipient requirements. These assemblies improve solubility, stability, and bioavailability, while enabling tumor-targeted accumulation and microenvironment-responsive release. Moreover, many herbal compounds provide additional therapeutic benefits, including direct induction of apoptosis, ferroptosis, or immunogenic cell death, and modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment. Such dual functionality, where structural design couples drug delivery with inherent pharmacological action, positions H-SANs as innovative candidates for next-generation anticancer therapeutics [13, 266, 389, 390].

A range of herbal self-assembled systems illustrates how H-SANs dictate anticancer efficacy. Carrier-free co-assemblies of phytochemicals with synergistic partners have been employed to improve solubility, stability, and tumor selectivity. For instance, a rod-shaped nanoplatform formed from luteolin and GRA improves aqueous dispersibility and hepatic accumulation. Mechanistic studies show suppression of ESR1 and CDK1, resulting in cell cycle arrest and reduced tumor burden, an effect further reinforced by the intrinsic hepatotropism of GRA [294]. Similarly, excipient-free nanoparticles composed of rhein and DOX (RD NPs) achieve high drug loading through π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding, markedly increasing intracellular retention of DOX, promoting apoptosis in 4T1 cells, and suppressing both primary tumor growth and lung metastasis with reduced systemic toxicity compared to free drug [295]. Recent advances further highlight the potential of MPN–mediated assembly in anticancer nanomedicine. Using phytochemicals such as EGCG and anticancer drugs (e.g., DOX, venetoclax), functional small molecule–MPN nanoparticles (FSM-MPN NPs) can be constructed under mild aqueous conditions, achieving exceptionally high drug loading (up to ~ 80%) and tunable release profiles. These assemblies are stabilized predominantly by π–π and hydrophobic interactions, which not only enhance drug retention and reduce premature leakage but also facilitate targeted modifications, such as antibody coatings for CD44-overexpressing tumor cells, thereby improving tumor selectivity and therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 3A) [296]. MPNs also exemplify the impact of architecture, as TA–Fe3+ shells encapsulating DOX initiate combined apoptosis–ferroptosis, improve nuclear influx, and circumvent P-gp–mediated efflux, while enabling multimodal imaging-guided chemo/chemodynamic/immune therapy [297]. Building on this concept, EGCG–Fe3+ MPNs with polydopamine coatings undergo acidic and GSH-mediated degradation to intensify chemodynamic and photothermal effects, thereby deepening oxidative stress within tumors [298]. In parallel, full–active-ingredient designs show that natural macromolecules and small molecules can be co-assembled with iron to orchestrate chemo–chemodynamic–immunotherapy in a single platform. A representative system combines isoliquiritigenin (ISL) with Rosa laevigata polysaccharide (RLP) and Fe3+ to form ISL@RLP–Fe nano–chelates. These nano–chelates are pH/GSH-responsive, deplete intracellular GSH, generate ferrous ions, and catalyze Fenton reactions to trigger ferroptosis, while the flavonoid component simultaneously induces apoptosis. In addition, the polysaccharide component promotes dendritic cell maturation and elevates IL-12/IFN-γ, thereby amplifying antitumor immunity and suppressing metastasis in vivo [301]. Beyond small-molecule assemblies, natural product–based photosensitizers and hybrid systems expand the therapeutic landscape. Aloe-emodin coordinated with copper ions and surface-modified with PEG–DSPE–FA accumulates selectively in tumors and exerts dual action under 450 nm irradiation. Aloe-emodin induces photodynamic ROS generation and immunogenic cell death, while copper overload triggers cuproptosis through disruption of the TCA cycle and Fe–S cluster biogenesis, collectively converting “cold” tumors into “hot” ones and enhancing the efficacy of PD-L1 checkpoint blockade [303] (Fig. 3B). Carrier-free photo(immuno)therapy illustrates how photophysics can be coupled with immune modulation. Co-assembly of chlorin e6 with baicalein reduces intratumoral glutathione, sensitizes ferroptosis, and promotes macrophage polarization, while related Ce6 assemblies enable sonodynamic–chemotherapy synergy and tumor-associated macrophage reprogramming when combined with the CSF1R inhibitor BLZ945 (Fig. 3C) [300, 302]. Hybrid constructs such as folate-targeted PLGA nanoparticles co-delivering ginsenoside Rg3 and the TLR7 agonist imiquimod (IMQ) integrate NIR–triggered release with durable antitumor immunity, providing protection against recurrence [55, 299, 391]. Finally, carrier-free assemblies based on alkaloids and triterpenoids broaden the design space, with BBR-based nanoparticles demonstrating synergistic anticancer activity across models, and betulinic-acid-centered assemblies advancing apoptosis priming in resistant tumors [44, 392].

Addressing tumor heterogeneity will require multifunctional designs that integrate direct cytotoxic effects with immune modulation and tumor microenvironment remodeling. Future progress depends on engineering programmable assemblies with precise spatiotemporal control, alongside rigorous evaluation of safety and manufacturability, to bridge the gap between preclinical promise and clinical application.

Anti-inflammatory effect

Inflammation underlies a wide spectrum of acute and chronic diseases, ranging from autoimmune disorders and metabolic syndromes to neurodegenerative and cardiovascular pathologies [393–396]. Conventional anti-inflammatory agents often suffer from limitations, such as short half-life, systemic side effects, and insufficient targeting of inflamed tissues [397]. H-SANs provide a distinctive opportunity to address these challenges by integrating the intrinsic anti-inflammatory activity of phytochemicals with the advantages of nanoscale delivery. Through self-assembly, bioactive compounds, such as flavonoids, saponins, and terpenoids, can form stable nanostructures that enhance solubility, protect labile constituents, and prolong systemic circulation. Importantly, these platforms selectively accumulate at inflamed sites, where microenvironmental cues such as oxidative stress, enzymatic activity, and acidic pH initiate controlled drug release. In addition to attenuating pro-inflammatory mediators such as NF-κB, NLRP3, and COX-2, many phytochemical-based assemblies also promote resolution of inflammation by modulating macrophage polarization and restoring immune homeostasis. This dual capacity for suppression and immunomodulation highlights H-SANs as versatile candidates for next-generation anti-inflammatory therapies [13, 230, 398].

A series of experimental models has demonstrated the diverse strategies by which herbal self-assemblies achieve disease-specific protection. Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a leading model to evaluate H-SANs. Carrier-free binary assemblies that pair isoquinoline alkaloids with polyphenols or phenylpropanoids reinforce epithelial barriers, suppress cytokine cascades, and modulate the dysbiotic microbiome. For example, BBR–hesperetin nanoparticles formed by electrostatic and π–π interactions reduced disease activity, downregulated TNF-α and IL-6, and restored tight junctions (TJs) in murine colitis beyond the effect of either monomer (Fig. 4A) [304]. BBR–magnolol assemblies produced superior control of colitis, lowered myeloperoxidase activity, and promoted epithelial repair, supported by spectroscopic mapping of their noncovalent assembly code [305]. Colon-targeted BBR double salt nanoparticles improved barrier function and corrected microbial imbalance [306], while costunolide–GA nanostructures reduced colonic IL-1β, TNF-α, and oxidative stress [307]. More complex “nanoarmors” integrating EGCG and TA within polycation–hyaluronan shells suppressed NF-κB activation, activated Nrf2 antioxidant defenses, and inhibited caspase-1–dependent pyroptosis [308]. Hybrid constructs such as rhubarb polysaccharide–BBR nanoparticles relieved colitis while rebalancing the intestinal flora [309], and HA functionalization further enhanced colon targeting [310]. In addition, L-arginine–induced hesperetin self-assemblies function as nitric oxide–releasing systems that provide protective effects in ulcerative colitis (UC) [311]. A recent study expanded this paradigm using an oral metal–organic framework (MOF-818) with intrinsic catalase (CAT)- and superoxide dismutase (SOD)–like activity. This nanoplatform selectively promoted the growth of Roseburia intestinalis, restored redox balance, enhanced TJs, increased short-chain fatty acids (SCFA) levels, and markedly reduced IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. In DSS-induced UC mice, MOF-818 alleviated weight loss, improved colon length, and repaired mucosal barrier function, with its therapeutic effect shown to be microbiota-dependent [312]. Together, these systems demonstrate how herbal self-assemblies can achieve multi-layered control of inflammation, barrier repair, and microbiota homeostasis in IBD.

Musculoskeletal inflammation provides a second platform where metal–polyphenol assemblies and macrophage-modulatory designs have shown consistent benefits. EGCG coordinated with divalent metals forms nanosheets and amorphous networks that temper synovial inflammation, promote M2 polarization, and preserve chondrocyte phenotype. In osteoarthritis models, Cu–EGCG nanoformulations suppressed TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and iNOS while activating Nrf2 and inhibiting NF-κB, ultimately preserving cartilage matrix integrity [381]. Macrophage membrane–camouflaged EGCG–cerium nanoparticles enhanced joint retention and scavenged ROS, showing how immune-inspired coatings reinforce herbal nanocores (Fig. 4B) [314]. These results align with broader principles of MPNs, which generate redox-active, inflammation-responsive interfaces suited for joint disease [399]. Since macrophage polarization is now recognized as a causal driver of synovitis and cartilage loss, herbal assemblies that bias macrophages toward reparative phenotypes directly address disease mechanisms in osteoarthritis [400]. A recent advance involves the development of sinomenine (SIN)–GA (S-G) self-assembled hydrogels, which stabilize SIN, extend its half-life, and mitigate gastrointestinal side effects. These hydrogels significantly suppressed NF-κB and MAPK signaling, reduced neutrophil recruitment, and reversed apoptosis delay, thereby alleviating joint swelling, protecting cartilage, and reducing neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation in RA models. Importantly, the sustained release and synergistic immunomodulatory effect of SIN and GA enhanced efficacy compared with either compound alone, while minimizing systemic toxicity [315].

Cutaneous and mucosal inflammatory disorders further highlight the versatility of these materials. GA self-assembles into fibrillar hydrogels and dual-network matrices with inherent anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial activity. Injectable GA hydrogels reduced neutrophil burden, lowered pro-inflammatory cytokines, and accelerated wound closure in infected injuries [53]. GA hydrogel microparticles carrying stem-cell exosomes enhanced re-epithelialization and angiogenesis, showing how herbal scaffolds can be combined with biological cues while retaining safety [116]. At the nanoscale, GA-based assemblies improved survival in infected mice by combining antiviral and anti-inflammatory effect (Fig. 4C) [316]. Polyphenol-derived platforms, including TA–mineral nanoparticles embedded in wound hydrogels, added redox buffering and immune suppression, while being fabricated from food-compatible natural inputs [376]. Psoriasis provides another example where nanotechnology-enabled herbal assemblies show distinct advantages. Self-assembled HA nanoparticles conjugated with methotrexate (HA-MTX NPs), delivered via dissolvable microneedle patches, achieved targeted accumulation in psoriatic lesions through CD44-mediated uptake. These NPs provided sustained drug release for over seven days, markedly reduced epidermal hyperplasia, and suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-23, IL-17, and TNF-α, in IMQ-induced mouse models. Compared with free methotrexate, this system improved biocompatibility, alleviated splenomegaly, and prevented recurrence, offering a minimally invasive and long-acting therapeutic alternative [319]. Collectively, these examples emphasize how H-SANs can orchestrate antimicrobial defense, immune regulation, and tissue repair in cutaneous and mucosal inflammation.

Despite substantial progress, several challenges limit the clinical translation of H-SANs for inflammatory diseases. Variability in phytochemical purity, differences in assembly stability under gastrointestinal or systemic conditions, and incomplete understanding of pharmacokinetics remain obstacles to reproducibility and safety. Moreover, inflammatory pathologies are heterogeneous, requiring systems that integrate multiple functions such as barrier protection, immune modulation, microbiota regulation, and oxidative stress control. Future advances are likely to focus on programmable architectures that respond to specific inflammatory cues, the incorporation of imaging for real-time disease monitoring, and scalable manufacturing strategies that preserve natural compound activity. Such innovations will be essential to harness the full therapeutic potential of herbal self-assemblies in diverse inflammatory disorders.

Antioxidative effect

Oxidative stress plays a pivotal role in the onset and progression of diverse disorders, including neurodegenerative diseases, metabolic syndromes, and cardiovascular dysfunction [401–403]. Conventional antioxidants are often hampered by poor stability, low bioavailability, and rapid clearance, limiting their therapeutic efficacy [129, 404, 405]. H-SANs provide a viable solution by harnessing the intrinsic redox-regulating properties of phytochemicals while improving their pharmacokinetic performance. Through spontaneous self-assembly into nanoscale architectures, polyphenols, flavonoids, and terpenoids achieve enhanced solubility and sustained release, enabling effective scavenging of ROS and protection of cellular redox balance. This integration of delivery function with inherent antioxidant activity underscores their promise in combating oxidative damage and restoring physiological homeostasis [13, 230].

Building on the general principles of redox regulation, a wide range of H-SANs exemplify how structural design enhances antioxidant performance. EGCG demonstrates this concept clearly, as its controlled autoxidation yields stable nanoparticles that scavenge ROS in vitro and restore hepatic redox homeostasis in vivo [318]. PEGylated EGCG assemblies further improve cellular uptake, reduce DNA oxidative damage, and restrain pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization [129], while red-blood-cell (RBC)–camouflaged EGCG nanoparticles prolong circulation and mitigate diquat-induced renal oxidative stress and inflammasome activation [368]. Extending to ophthalmic applications, EGCG coordinated with Zn2+ forms metal–phenolic antioxidant nanoparticles that delay lens turbidity under oxidative challenge, suggesting potential in cataract prevention (Fig. 5A) [317]. TA and related polyphenols provide another versatile platform, as their MPNs combine intrinsic radical-quenching and protein adhesion with Nrf2 activation to reinforce endogenous antioxidant defenses. Co-assembly of TA with BBR yields robust carrier-free nanoparticles with enhanced stability, while TA–iron complexes act as antioxidant nanoplatforms in their own right (Fig. 5B) [320, 406, 407]. Complementing these polyphenol systems, saponin-based assemblies extend antioxidant effects to tissue repair, exemplified by GA hydrogels that self-assemble into injectable, self-healing matrices capable of accelerating wound closure. Updated GA constructs that incorporate polyphenols or transition metals create enzyme-mimetic, ROS-responsive scaffolds that quench oxidative bursts and simultaneously support re-epithelialization and angiogenesis [53, 111, 349, 350, 408]. Flavonoid–triterpenoid pairings further illustrate this multifunctionality, as costunolide–GA nanoparticles alleviate oxidative stress in UC, thereby demonstrating how antioxidant outcomes can be co-optimized within a single supramolecular entity [307]. Similarly, resveratrol self-assemblies not only improve solubility and antioxidant potential but also achieve enhanced radical-scavenging and photostability when coated with metal–polyphenol shells [321, 409]. Beyond direct ROS neutralization, herbal assemblies increasingly aim to modulate redox-sensitive signaling pathways, as shown by baicalin–peptide supramolecular nanofibers that inhibit ferroptosis and attenuate DOX cardiotoxicity through preservation of lipid redox balance (Fig. 5C) [64]. In parallel, quercetin and related flavonoid constructs activate Nrf2-dependent genes and strengthen endogenous antioxidant enzyme systems in vitro and in vivo [313, 410, 411]. Extending to the central nervous system (CNS), self-assembled UA nanoparticles cross the neurovascular barrier and engage Nrf2/GPX4 signaling to attenuate oxidative brain injury [322]. Collectively, these examples highlight how herbal self-assemblies can combine direct radical scavenging with pathway-level regulation, offering versatile tools for the prevention and treatment of oxidative stress–driven diseases.

Although H-SANs display considerable promise in combating oxidative stress, important barriers remain before clinical application can be realized. Variability in natural product composition, limited stability of assemblies under physiological conditions, and incomplete characterization of pharmacokinetics hinder reproducibility and standardization. Moreover, oxidative stress contributes to diverse pathologies in tissue- and context-specific ways, demanding designs that integrate radical scavenging with targeted delivery, controlled release, and pathway-level modulation. Future research should prioritize programmable assemblies capable of responding to spatiotemporal redox cues, multifunctional systems that combine antioxidant activity with anti-inflammatory or regenerative effects, and integrated diagnostic–therapeutic platforms to monitor oxidative states in real-time. Addressing these challenges will be essential for translating the strong preclinical potential of herbal self-assemblies into effective interventions against oxidative stress–driven diseases.

Neuroprotective effect

Neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD) are strongly associated with oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction [412–417]. Conventional neuroprotective strategies are often constrained by poor BBB penetration and systemic side effects [418]. H-SANs offer a promising approach by enhancing the solubility, stability, and brain delivery of neuroactive phytochemicals. Through nanoscale organization, compounds including alkaloids, flavonoids, and terpenoids can sustain release, protect neurons from apoptosis, and modulate key pathways involved in synaptic plasticity and mitochondrial homeostasis [419, 420]. This dual capacity for efficient delivery and intrinsic neuroprotective action highlights their potential in delaying or mitigating neurodegenerative progression [13, 230].

Preclinical investigations exemplify how herbal self-assembled systems function as both delivery vehicles and active agents in neuroprotection. Rhein, an anthraquinone from Rheum species, self-assembles into nanofibrous hydrogels that provide localized and prolonged release, suppress TLR4/NF-κB signaling, and attenuate microglial activation, outperforming the free compound in neuroinflammation models (Fig. 6A) [60]. Extending this concept, rhein-lysinate (RHL) forms injectable hydrogels via π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding, and an RHL depot loaded with a synapse-protective agent improved functional recovery in post-stroke settings, highlighting the adaptability of drug-as-matrix designs (Fig. 6B) [110]. Polyphenols extend this paradigm by targeting oxidative stress and ferroptosis. Curcumin has been engineered into a self-assembled hydrogel that exhibits a “double” ROS-scavenging effect. The network neutralizes extracellular ROS and then releases curcumin in response to oxidative cues, which further quenches intracellular ROS and preserves neuronal viability in ischemic settings [323]. UA formulated as HA-PEG–UA nanoparticles selectively accumulate at CD44-rich lesions after subarachnoid hemorrhage, improve neurological scores, alleviate edema, and activate the Nrf2/SLC7A11/GPX4 axis to inhibit neuronal ferroptosis (Fig. 6C) [322]. Baicalin has been formulated as PEG-PLGA redox-responsive nanoparticles that enhance brain exposure and attenuate cerebral ischemia injury through anti-oxidation and anti-apoptosis, showing that flavonoid self-assemblies can convert pleiotropic mechanisms into measurable functional benefit [324]. Beyond these examples, Aloin-based nanoparticles (Aloin NPs) have recently demonstrated a novel immunomodulatory mechanism by inducing conformational changes in protein coronas. Aloin NPs specifically bind to myotrophin (MPTN), triggering a “finger-unclasping” rearrangement that alters its interactome and releases peroxiredoxin 6 (PRDX6). This event protects mitochondrial membranes, reduces ROS accumulation, and blocks cGAS–STING–NF-κB signaling, thereby reprogramming microglia from pro-inflammatory M1 to neuroprotective M2 states. In vivo, Aloin NPs alleviated neuroinflammation and improved functional outcomes in PD models without notable toxicity [325]. In parallel, ultrasmall iron–gallic acid coordination polymer nanodots (Fe–GA CPNs) have emerged as potent neuroprotective assemblies. These nanodots not only scavenge ROS but also activate Akt and Nrf2/HO-1 pathways, reduce neuronal apoptosis, and restore glucose metabolism in ischemic brain tissue. In MCAO rat models, Fe–GA CPNs significantly improved neurological recovery as validated by PET/MR imaging, while maintaining excellent biocompatibility and biosafety, underscoring their translational promise for ischemia–reperfusion (I/R) brain injury [332]. Complementary evidence comes from TA–Fe complex-based nanozymes, which exhibit CAT- and SOD-mimicking activities. By efficiently relieving oxidative stress and modulating α-synuclein aggregation, TA–Fe nanozymes attenuated dopaminergic neuronal loss, rescued mitochondrial function, and improved behavioral outcomes in PD models, highlighting how polyphenol–metal assemblies extend herbal self-assembly principles into biomimetic nanozyme designs with strong neuroprotective potential [326]. More recently, TA–based nanoplatforms have been engineered to specifically traverse the injured BBB. By leveraging TA’s strong metal-binding and protein-interactive properties, these assemblies achieve selective accumulation in lesioned regions, enhance brain delivery of therapeutic payloads, and mitigate neuroinflammation while sparing healthy tissue. Such targeted BBB penetration underscores the translational significance of herbal polyphenol-based designs in CNS therapy, complementing their intrinsic antioxidant and anti-inflammatory actions [331]. Carrier-free herbal assemblies provide additional therapeutic avenues. Amygdalin and GA spontaneously co-assemble into a full-natural hydrogel (AG-gel) that matches brain tissue modulus and ensures biocompatibility. When injected after traumatic brain injury (TBI) debridement, AG-gel exerts dual actions by preserving the BBB and directly binding complement C3, thereby suppressing glial-driven inflammatory cascades and enhancing neuronal survival. In TBI models, AG-gel significantly reduced apoptosis, preserved NeuN-positive neurons, and improved neurological outcomes (Fig. 6D) [327]. Similarly, ginsenoside Rg1 nanoparticles have demonstrated BBB penetration and neuroprotection against ischemia-induced barrier disruption, supporting self-assembled ginsenosides as intrinsic neuroprotectants with delivery capability [328]. The versatility of these assemblies is further illustrated by hybrid or co-assembled systems. Tanshinone IIA–GA nanoparticles, when embedded into immunoexosomes, improve brain targeting and immune modulation, coupling intrinsic pharmacology with biologically derived carriers [329]. Finally, EGCG–selenium nanoparticles mitigate spinal cord injury (SCI) through ROS scavenging and inflammasome suppression, leading to measurable functional recovery [330]. Collectively, these studies highlight that H-SANs not only enhance solubility, stability, and brain delivery of phytochemicals but also integrate multi-dimensional mechanisms, including ROS clearance, ferroptosis inhibition, BBB protection, inflammasome suppression, and synapse preservation. Such dual-function systems blur the boundary between carriers and drugs, providing a new paradigm for neuroprotection across diverse CNS injuries.

Despite encouraging progress, the translation of H-SANs for neuroprotection still faces critical challenges. BBB penetration remains a major hurdle, as many assemblies achieve strong efficacy in peripheral models but lack systematic evaluation of brain delivery. The long-term safety and stability of these supramolecular systems in the CNS also require deeper investigation, particularly given the chronic nature of neurodegenerative diseases. Moreover, the heterogeneity of pathological mechanisms, ranging from oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction to neuroinflammation and protein aggregation, calls for multifunctional designs capable of engaging several pathways simultaneously. Future advances may focus on programmable nanoplatforms that integrate targeted delivery, spatiotemporal control of release, and real-time monitoring, while also aligning with clinically relevant endpoints. Such strategies will be pivotal to translate the promising neuroprotective effects of herbal self-assemblies into viable interventions for complex neurological disorders [63].

Metabolic diseases

Metabolic disorders, including diabetes, obesity, and dyslipidemia, are major global health concerns characterized by chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and impaired energy homeostasis [421–425]. Current pharmacological approaches are often limited by poor bioavailability, adverse effects, and suboptimal patient compliance. H-SANs provide a promising alternative by improving the stability, solubility, and controlled release of bioactive phytochemicals with known metabolic regulatory properties. Through nanoscale assembly, compounds such as saponins, flavonoids, and terpenoids can enhance glucose uptake, modulate lipid metabolism, and restore insulin sensitivity, while reducing systemic toxicity. This integration of delivery efficiency with intrinsic metabolic regulation underscores their potential in addressing complex metabolic diseases.

The therapeutic promise of these platforms is illustrated by a growing body of case studies across different metabolic models. In diabetes and insulin resistance, coordination-driven assembly of baicalin with AlCl3 produces baicalin–aluminum nanoparticles capable of loading insulin (INS@BA–Al). These minimal-excipient particles remain stable in gastric fluid, release in the intestine, adhere to the mucosa, and transiently loosen TJs (claudin-1, ZO-1, ZO-2) to enhance paracellular transport. As a result, they achieve approximately 8.9% relative oral bioavailability and prolong glycemic control in diabetic rats, far exceeding the performance of free oral insulin (Fig. 7A) [67]. At the same time, edible plant-derived exosome-like nanoparticles offer a natural, carrier-free route to metabolic reprogramming along the gut–liver axis. Ginger-derived nanoparticles prevent high-fat diet (HFD)–induced insulin resistance and obesity by protecting Foxa2 from Akt-1–mediated phosphorylation in intestinal epithelium, thereby reshaping downstream metabolic programs (Fig. 7B) [337, 426]. Garlic-derived vesicles reinforce this approach by reversing HFD-induced obesity and suppressing neuroinflammation via preferential microglial uptake and inhibition of IDO1–AHR and c-Myc–cGAS/STING signaling, linking dietary vesicles to central control of energy balance (Fig. 7C) [23, 336]. Liver-related metabolic disorders have also been addressed using edible vesicles. Blueberry-derived nanoparticles accumulate in the liver, reduce oxidative stress, and ameliorate lipid deposition through mitochondria-centric mechanisms [333]. Pomegranate-derived vesicles further strengthen intestinal barrier function, lower circulating endotoxin, restore mitochondrial activity, and suppress fibrosis in MASLD, thereby acting simultaneously on both intestinal and hepatic compartments [335]. Beyond vesicles, ionic co-assembly of BBR with sodium taurocholate yields STC@BBR-SANPs that overcome solubility and absorption limits of BBR, lowering serum urate and reducing renal inflammation in hyperuricemic mice through improved intestinal uptake and enzymatic pathway regulation [334]. Herbal assemblies are also effective in metabolic complications of diabetes. Puerarin-based self-healing hydrogels accelerate wound closure, stimulate angiogenesis, and rebalance macrophage polarization while maintaining antioxidant tone in diabetic mice, exemplifying how a single monomer can act simultaneously as a therapeutic agent and as a structural matrix [356, 358]. In addition, carrier-free curcumin nanocrystals attenuate renal ferroptosis and injury in diabetic nephropathy by restoring GPX4 activity and correcting lipid and renal indices, thereby providing a redox-centered and excipient-minimal strategy for kidney protection [338]. Collectively, these studies highlight the versatility of herbal self-assemblies in addressing primary metabolic disorders as well as their complications through multi-level regulation of absorption, signaling, and tissue repair.

Although H-SANs show considerable potential in treating metabolic disorders, several challenges remain before they can be translated into clinical practice. Oral delivery systems, although appealing, still face obstacles related to reproducibility, scalability, and long-term stability within the gastrointestinal environment. The heterogeneity of metabolic diseases, which include diabetes, obesity, fatty liver disease, and hyperuricemia, requires multifunctional assemblies capable of acting across the gut–liver–brain axis while regulating oxidative stress, inflammation, and energy balance. At present, most findings are restricted to animal models with limited validation in clinically relevant settings. Future progress will depend on programmable and biocompatible designs that integrate targeted delivery, real-time monitoring, and multi-pathway modulation, and this must be supported by rigorous pharmacokinetic profiling and comprehensive safety evaluations. These advances will be essential to move H-SANs from experimental studies to effective therapeutic options for metabolic syndromes and their systemic complications.

Cardiovascular diseases

Cardiovascular diseases remain the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, driven by pathological processes such as oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and dysregulated lipid metabolism [427–431]. Conventional therapies, although effective in symptom management, are often constrained by poor bioavailability, drug resistance, and long-term side effects. H-SANs provide a novel avenue to overcome these barriers by enhancing the pharmacokinetics, tissue distribution, and therapeutic precision of cardioprotective phytochemicals. Through nanoscale self-assembly, flavonoids, polyphenols, and saponins can achieve improved solubility and sustained release while exerting intrinsic activities such as antioxidation, anti-inflammation, and lipid regulation. This dual function positions H-SANs as promising candidates for preventing and treating cardiovascular disorders [432, 433].

Evidence from specific nanoplatform designs demonstrates how herbal self-assemblies integrate structural stability with biological activity to achieve cardioprotection. In myocardial I/R injury, a hydrogel co-assembled from EGCG and a rhein-peptide network (EGCG@Rh-gel) provides a mechanistic and in vivo proof-of-concept. This supramolecular network scavenges ROS and suppresses TLR4-driven inflammation, thereby interrupting the vicious cycle of oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling. Injection of EGCG@Rh-gel into the infarct border zone improved cardiac function and reduced scar formation in mice, illustrating how herbal active components used directly as matrix materials can remodel the lesion microenvironment (Fig. 8A) [343, 432]. A related strategy employs puerarin, an isoflavone from Pueraria, formulated as an in situ self-assembling hydrogel with chitosan. When combined with degradable mesoporous silica, this composite suppressed M1 macrophage polarization and pro-inflammatory cytokine production while simultaneously promoting endothelial migration and angiogenic gene expression, thereby addressing both inflammation and revascularization in the early post-infarction period [340]. Systemic approaches also show considerable promise. Dual-drug microspheres composed solely of nicotinamide riboside (NR) and resveratrol form carrier-free nanocrystals that enhance oral NAD+ bioavailability and attenuate myocardial IR injury more effectively than the free agents, providing a clear example of drug-as-material self-assembly that delivers organ-level benefit (Fig. 8B) [342]. Complementary advances include self-healing peptide hydrogels delivering salvianolic acid B (SaB), which sustain drug release, tolerate cyclic mechanical strain, and promote cardiac repair (Fig. 8C) [339, 434].

Beyond acute salvage, strategies have also been designed to address post-infarction remodeling. A TA-modified MnO2 nanozyme (MnO2@TA) integrates the coating and adhesion properties of polyphenols with intrinsic antioxidant activity, conferring combined SOD and CAT-mimicking functions. In murine myocardial infarction models, MnO2@TA reduced fibroblast activation, tempered fibrosis, and improved ventricular function without impairing physiological healing, illustrating how natural phenolic shells can endow inorganic catalytic cores with cardiac specificity and redox balance [341]. Herbal self-assemblies are increasingly being explored in vascular disease. In atherosclerosis, such systems enhance bioavailability, regulate macrophage phenotypes, and mitigate oxidative stress within plaques, and recent studies highlight carrier-free constructs as an emerging class for plaque stabilization [435, 436]. Among them, BBR co-assembled with sodium taurocholate forms STC@BBR nanoparticles that markedly enhance solubility and absorption, offering superior in vivo efficacy over free BBR in hyperuricemic models. This pharmacokinetic advantage supports direct extension to cardio-metabolic atheroprotection, where BBR is already recognized for lipid-lowering and anti-inflammatory effects [334]. A recent study further illustrates this concept by co-assembling ginsenoside Rb1 with the lipid-lowering drug probucol into stable carrier-free nanoparticles. When camouflaged with macrophage-derived microvesicles, these biomimetic particles selectively accumulated at atherosclerotic lesions and synergistically reduced oxidative stress, inflammation, and lipid deposition, thereby slowing plaque progression without detectable toxicity (Fig. 8D) [346]. At the blood–device interface, herbal chemistries have been integrated into stent coatings to reduce thrombosis and accelerate endothelialization. A TA–based coating incorporating the antithrombin peptide bivalirudin suppressed inflammation, reduced thrombosis, and accelerated re-endothelialization with diminished neointimal hyperplasia in vivo [344, 345].

Despite these encouraging advances, the translation of H-SANs into cardiovascular medicine remains at an early stage. Many designs show efficacy in IR injury, myocardial remodeling, or atherosclerosis, yet challenges persist in achieving targeted delivery, long-term stability, and safety within the dynamic cardiovascular environment. The integration of herbal assemblies into implantable devices highlights new opportunities, but their durability, hemocompatibility, and regulatory pathways require further clarification. Moreover, the multifactorial nature of cardiovascular diseases demands multifunctional systems that can combine redox regulation, anti-inflammatory activity, and tissue regeneration in a coordinated manner. Future progress will rely on programmable assemblies capable of responding to biochemical and mechanical cues in the heart and vasculature, together with rigorous pharmacokinetic and safety evaluations in large-animal models. Such innovations will be essential to transform H-SANs from experimental constructs into clinically viable strategies for cardiovascular therapy.

Antimicrobial effects

The rising prevalence of multidrug-resistant pathogens poses a serious global health threat, underscoring the urgent need for novel antimicrobial strategies [437–439]. Conventional antibiotics are limited by declining efficacy, off-target toxicity, and the inability to modulate host–pathogen interactions. H-SANs represent a promising approach by integrating the intrinsic antimicrobial activities of phytochemicals with the advantages of nanoscale delivery. Through self-assembly, alkaloids, polyphenols, and terpenoids can form stable nanostructures that enhance solubility, protect labile compounds, and enable controlled release at infection sites. Beyond direct bactericidal and fungicidal effects, many of these assemblies also disrupt biofilms and modulate immune responses, highlighting their potential to address antimicrobial resistance and improve therapeutic outcomes [72, 221, 440].

Various studies in antimicrobial applications demonstrate how H-SANs integrate infection control with immune modulation and tissue repair. GA readily self-assembles into injectable and self-healing hydrogels with intrinsic antibacterial activity. These bioactive hydrogels adhere to irregular wound surfaces, inhibit common pathogens such as S. aureus and E. coli, and exhibit good biocompatibility, thereby supporting wound repair. Structural modifications and composite designs further expand their therapeutic potential. For example, a GA/carboxymethyl-chitosan dual-network hydrogel accelerated healing in S. aureus–infected wounds by enhancing granulation tissue, collagen deposition, and suppressing inflammatory cytokine expression in vivo. More recently, an oxidized GA/sericin dual-network hydrogel incorporating desferrioxamine was developed to address chronic wounds. This system scavenged ROS, promoted macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, and activated HIF-1α/VEGF signaling to stimulate angiogenesis, ultimately accelerating closure and improving vascularization in diabetic rats (Fig. 9A) [53, 357]. Building on this chemistry, FeS/GA hydrogels eradicated multidrug-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) and promoted repair in diabetic wound models, while co-assemblies pairing GA with monoterpenoid phenolics such as carvacrol or thymol produced injectable hydrogels with potent anti-MRSA activity and enhanced wound closure in rodents [348, 354]. GA matrices have also been combined with biological vesicles, as shown by GA hydrogel microparticles carrying stem-cell exosomes that provided both antibacterial action and regenerative cues in chronic wounds [116]. Polyphenol-centered assemblies provide powerful tools against biofilm-associated infections. EGCG not only disrupts amyloid fiber formation within bacterial biofilms but also serves as a building block for functional nanoplatforms. For instance, EGCG–metal nanoparticles combine intrinsic antibacterial and antioxidant activity with NIR photothermal effects, enabling eradication of MRSA, suppression of biofilm maturation, and promotion of infected wound healing without systemic toxicity (Fig. 9B) [353, 360, 441]. Furthermore, EGCG/Cu and TA/Ca/Cu, as well as other MPN composites, generate durable antibiofilm surfaces and broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, making them particularly relevant for device-associated infections and chronic wounds (Fig. 9B, C) [72, 221, 359, 442]. Binary carrier-free assemblies illustrate how pairing herbal monomers can outperform physical mixtures. BBR–TA nanoparticles display stronger antibacterial activity than simple BBR + TA combinations owing to enhanced membrane binding and redox stress [105]. BBR–EGCG nanocomplexes accelerate healing of MRSA-infected wounds [347], and BBR–CGA nanoparticles suppress MRSA while reducing persistence-associated phenotypes [104]. In addition, NIR–responsive BBR–indocyanine nanoparticles enable light-triggered bacterial clearance both in vitro and in vivo, demonstrating how herbal self-assemblies can be engineered for spatiotemporally controlled antimicrobial therapy [45].

Although H-SANs display strong antimicrobial potential across infected wounds, biofilm-associated infections, and device-related complications, several challenges remain before clinical translation can be achieved. The majority of studies are confined to rodent models, and their scalability, stability, and pharmacokinetic behavior in larger organisms are not yet well defined. Moreover, antimicrobial resistance is highly dynamic, requiring assemblies that can adapt to evolving pathogens while maintaining long-term safety. The complexity of biofilm environments and chronic wounds further calls for multifunctional systems that integrate antimicrobial effects with immune modulation and tissue regeneration. Future research should therefore prioritize programmable and stimuli-responsive assemblies capable of spatiotemporal drug release, the development of biofilm-penetrating designs, and rigorous evaluations in clinically relevant infection models. These advances will be essential to translate the promising antimicrobial efficacy of herbal self-assemblies into reliable therapeutic options for resistant and persistent infections.

Wound healing effects

Effective wound healing requires a coordinated sequence of processes, including inflammation resolution, oxidative stress control, angiogenesis, and tissue remodeling [443–445]. Conventional dressings and pharmacological agents often provide limited protection or insufficient stimulation of these biological responses, leading to delayed recovery or chronic wounds. H-SANs offer unique advantages by improving the stability, bioavailability, and targeted delivery of phytochemicals with intrinsic wound-healing activities. Through spontaneous nanoscale organization, flavonoids, saponins, and polysaccharides can enhance antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, promote collagen deposition, and stimulate vascular regeneration. This multifunctional capacity highlights their potential to accelerate wound closure and restore tissue integrity in acute and chronic injury settings [446, 447].

Practical evidence from wound repair demonstrates that herbal self-assembled systems can integrate antimicrobial protection, redox modulation, and regenerative support within a single therapeutic platform. One representative example is GA, which forms injectable, self-healing hydrogels with inherent antibacterial and anti-inflammatory activity. GA-based scaffolds accelerate closure of full-thickness and infected wounds, reduce inflammatory burden, and, in variants such as FeS/GA hydrogels, eradicate MRSA in diabetic wounds while simultaneously promoting tissue regeneration [53, 352]. Building on this foundation, co-assemblies of GA with monoterpenoid phenolics such as carvacrol or thymol further enhance anti-MRSA efficacy and improve wound healing [348]. Additional GA formats include hydrogel microparticles carrying stem-cell exosomes, which promote granulation, angiogenesis, and re-epithelialization, as well as GA–asiaticoside hydrogels that provide combined antimicrobial and pro-repair effects (Fig. 10A) [116, 367]. Polyphenol-centered assemblies add complementary functionality. For instance, TA–based systems have recently advanced from conventional hydrogels to in situ self-gelling coacervate powders composed of TA, chitosan, and PEG. These powders can rapidly absorb interfacial water and transform into robust adhesive gels with strong wet adhesion, hemostatic efficacy, and inherent antibacterial and antioxidant properties. Notably, when loaded with platelet-rich plasma and deferoxamine, the coacervate gels sustained the release of growth factors, promoted angiogenesis, and significantly accelerated the healing of normal, diabetic, and infected wounds, while maintaining long-term storage stability and scalable production potential (Fig. 10B) [448]. A parallel approach leverages ROS-responsive nanoassemblies of CGA and copper (PTC@Cu NPs), which selectively release their payload in oxidative wound environments. These nanoplatforms combine CGA-driven ROS scavenging and M2 macrophage polarization with Cu2+-mediated antibacterial and pro-angiogenic effects, thereby suppressing apoptosis, enhancing cell migration, and markedly accelerating closure of infected burn wounds in vivo [363]. Similarly, TA hydrogels exhibit rapid gelation, strong tissue adhesion, and accelerated healing, supported by evidence of hemostatic, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory activity in contaminated or bleeding wounds (Fig. 10C) [113, 365, 449]. Within the same category, EGCG gains greater stability and therapeutic efficacy when organized into self-assemblies, where it scavenges ROS, supports re-epithelialization, and promotes collagen deposition. Variants such as curdlan–EGCG composites contribute hemostasis and antibacterial activity, while nanoscale EGCG particles improve cellular uptake and redox control in chronic wounds [129, 351, 366]. EGCG can also be coordinated with copper to reprogram macrophage metabolism and accelerate burn healing, or incorporated into poloxamer gels with recombinant EGF to promote re-epithelialization and collagen remodeling (Fig. 10D) [362, 364]. Expanding the design space further, cinnamaldehyde–folic acid hydrogels provide broad antibacterial and pro-healing activity with favorable injectability [361], and binary nanodrugs composed of BBR and CGA outperform physical mixtures by accelerating inflammatory resolution, suppressing bacterial persistence, and improving wound closure through combined membrane disruption and phenolic redox chemistry [104]. More recently, SaB has emerged as another promising scaffold, forming robust supramolecular or double-network hydrogels that integrate antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and angiogenic properties. These SAB-based systems enhance immune modulation and vascular regeneration in infected and chronic wounds, highlighting their potential as multifunctional scaffolds for difficult-to-heal injuries [355]. Together, these studies underscore how herbal self-assemblies can act as multifunctional scaffolds that coordinate infection control, oxidative balance, and tissue regeneration, thereby offering an integrated strategy for effective wound management.

Despite these promising advances, several challenges must be addressed before H-SANs can be translated into clinical wound management. Chronic wounds are characterized by complex microenvironments involving persistent infection, hypoxia, impaired angiogenesis, and dysregulated inflammation, which require multifunctional systems capable of responding dynamically to diverse pathological cues. Current designs demonstrate encouraging efficacy in preclinical models, but questions remain about long-term safety, large-scale manufacturing, and the reproducibility of supramolecular assemblies under physiological conditions. Moreover, the integration of antimicrobial activity with regenerative signaling highlights the need for platforms that can deliver spatiotemporally controlled release while preserving the bioactivity of fragile natural components. Future efforts should focus on programmable scaffolds that combine anti-infective, antioxidant, and pro-angiogenic functions, alongside rigorous evaluation in chronic and diabetic wound models and eventual validation in clinical trials. Such innovations will be pivotal to realizing the potential of herbal self-assemblies as next-generation wound dressings that not only accelerate closure but also restore tissue integrity and functional recovery.

Kidney injury

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a severe clinical condition marked by abrupt declines in renal function, often progressing to chronic kidney disease (CKD) and increasing the risk of end-stage renal disease. Oxidative stress, inflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction are recognized as core drivers of tubular injury, apoptosis, and fibrosis, yet effective pharmacological interventions remain limited [450–453]. Conventional antioxidants such as N-acetylcysteine (NAC) offer modest benefit but are hindered by low bioavailability and systemic toxicity. In this context, H-SANs present an innovative therapeutic paradigm, capable of simultaneously enhancing solubility, stability, and kidney accumulation of bioactive phytochemicals while exploiting their inherent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [371, 373, 454].

Evidence from recent studies highlights the versatility of these systems. PEGylated gambogic acid (GBA) nanoparticles with ultrasmall size achieved preferential renal uptake in AKI, significantly improving renal function and reducing tubular injury. The protective effects were linked to Nrf2 activation and NF-κB inhibition, which together mitigated oxidative stress and inflammation [371]. A complementary approach employed rosmarinic acid (RosA) encapsulated in dendrimer-based nanoparticles, surface-modified with serine residues to target Kim-1 on injured renal tubules. This strategy resulted in selective accumulation within damaged kidneys, robust antioxidative and antiapoptotic activity, and marked recovery of renal function in I/R injury (Fig. 11A) [373]. Building on biomimetic designs, erythrocyte-membrane-coated nanoparticles encapsulating EGCG achieved long-circulating, stable delivery and, in diquat-induced renal injury, markedly attenuated ROS generation, tubular cell apoptosis, and inflammasome activation [368]. Additional work explored metal–phenolic nanozymes combining baicalein with iron coordination, which displayed CAT- and SOD–like activities. These constructs scavenged ROS, prevented lipid peroxidation, preserved glutathione metabolism, and ultimately blocked ferroptosis, leading to improved survival in cisplatin-induced AKI [370]. Expanding beyond redox regulation, curcumin-loaded nanoplatforms demonstrated the ability to rebalance lipid metabolism through FABP4/PPARγ signaling while maintaining antioxidant capacity (Fig. 11B) [374]. Similarly, polymers derived from ursolic acid acted as self-therapeutic carriers, synergizing with resveratrol to deliver dual antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects [372]. Moreover, herbal nanoparticles enriched from Scutellaria barbata and Scleromitrion diffusum show strong antioxidant activity, activation of Nrf2 pathways, and simultaneous suppression of ferroptosis and inflammation in AKI models (Fig. 11C) [369]. Taken together, these findings reveal how self-assembly enables precise renal targeting while amplifying the intrinsic therapeutic actions of natural compounds.

Overall, H-SANs offer a transformative route for kidney protection by integrating drug delivery with self-therapeutic functions. By tailoring size, surface chemistry, and biomimetic features, these assemblies not only overcome solubility and bioavailability bottlenecks but also achieve sustained release and microenvironment-responsive action within injured kidneys. While most findings remain at the preclinical stage, the convergence of nanotechnology and traditional medicine provides a strong rationale for advancing these systems toward clinical translation. Future work should prioritize safety evaluation, large-scale synthesis, and validation in clinically relevant AKI and CKD models, ultimately positioning herbal self-assemblies as next-generation nephroprotective therapeutics.

Bone disorders

Bone disorders such as osteoporosis, critical-size defects, and inflammatory bone loss remain major clinical challenges due to the imbalance between bone resorption and formation, insufficient local delivery of therapeutic agents, and limited regenerative capacity [455–457]. Current pharmacological agents, while effective in part, are hindered by poor bone-targeting ability, systemic toxicity, and a lack of spatiotemporal control. H-SANs provide new strategies to overcome these limitations. By exploiting the inherent bioactivities of flavonoids, polyphenols, and terpenoids, and organizing them into nanoscale assemblies or hybrid systems, these platforms simultaneously improve solubility, stability, and tissue specificity, while exerting intrinsic antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and osteogenic functions. This dual functionality makes them promising candidates for addressing both metabolic bone diseases and defect repair.

Accumulating experimental evidence demonstrates how structural designs of herbal self-assemblies translate into bone protection. Quercetin-capped selenium nanoparticles (Qu-SeNPs) showed superior stability and bioactivity compared to free quercetin, enhancing alkaline phosphatase activity, mineralized nodule formation, and osteogenic marker expression, while downregulating osteoclast-related genes. Mechanistically, Qu-SeNPs activated the miR-206/Connexin43 axis and WNT/BMP pathways, leading to robust bone regeneration in vivo [375]. Building on quercetin chemistry, phosphorylated quercetin (p-Que) was synthesized to improve solubility and confer hydroxyapatite-binding affinity, thereby achieving bone-targeting. In ovariectomized mice, p-Que significantly suppressed osteoclastogenesis, reduced trabecular bone loss, and preserved bone microarchitecture [380]. Flavonoid icariin has been investigated using diverse nanoplatform formats. A representative approach employed nanoparticle–hydrogel hybrids for sequential drug delivery. In this system, IL-10 was rapidly released to induce macrophage M2 polarization and establish a pro-regenerative immune microenvironment, while icariin was subsequently released in a sustained manner to promote osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells. This strategy resulted in efficient closure and high-quality remodeling of critical-size cranial defects in rats (Fig. 12A) [378]. Complementarily, silk fibroin/chitosan/nano-hydroxyapatite scaffolds loaded with icariin microspheres enhanced cell adhesion, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation in vitro, while in rabbit radial defect models they significantly improved new bone formation and vascularization, with upregulated expression of osteocalcin, VEGF, and type I collagen [377]. For implant applications, TA–strontium metal–phenolic nanocoatings provided a multifunctional surface that facilitated stem cell adhesion, accelerated osteogenic differentiation, and polarized macrophages toward an M2 phenotype. In vivo, these coatings enhanced peri-implant bone formation and osseointegration (Fig. 12B) [383]. Beyond localized applications, systemic MPN nanoparticles composed of strontium and EGCG degraded under osteoporotic microenvironments to release Sr2+ ions, stimulating osteogenesis, and EGCG, inhibiting osteoclast activity. Intravenous administration in ovariectomized mice prevented trabecular bone loss in femoral head and vertebrae, restored bone volume, and improved bone strength [379]. Finally, in inflammatory bone loss, a multifunctional EGCG–Cu hydrogel not only stabilized EGCG through metal coordination but also provided antibacterial effects against periodontal pathogens, suppressed M1 macrophage polarization, promoted M2 polarization, and facilitated alveolar bone regeneration in periodontitis models (Fig. 12C) [382]. Together, these studies highlight that herbal self-assemblies can act as both delivery systems and active therapeutic agents, bridging antioxidant, immunomodulatory, and osteogenic functions.

Although these findings are promising, translation into clinical bone therapeutics still faces challenges. Large-scale and reproducible preparation of herbal assemblies must be standardized to ensure consistent performance. Long-term biosafety, particularly for systemic osteoporosis therapy and implant applications, remains insufficiently characterized. Moreover, while individual studies demonstrate benefits in osteogenesis, angiogenesis, or osteoclast inhibition, future systems should integrate these functions within a single programmable platform. Designing nanoplatforms that respond dynamically to bone microenvironmental cues such as oxidative stress, acidic pH, or inflammatory mediators will enable spatiotemporally controlled release of bioactive phytochemicals and ions. By combining osteoimmunomodulation, angiogenic stimulation, and bone-targeted delivery, H-SANs have the potential to transform from experimental systems into clinically applicable therapies for osteoporosis management, defect repair, and implant osseointegration.

Challenges and perspectives

The translational barriers of H-SANs differ across platform types and should not be generalized as a single development pathway. Monocomponent carrier-free assemblies may offer high bioactive content and simpler formulation composition, but they are often vulnerable to dilution, serum-induced restructuring, and limited control over release kinetics. Multicomponent co-assemblies and herbal–drug hybrids can improve functional diversity, but they introduce additional complexity in composition, stoichiometry, interaction stability, and quality control. Metal-coordination networks may enhance structural robustness and stimuli responsiveness, but require careful evaluation of metal ion safety, ligand exchange, degradation products, and regulatory classification. Vesicular and plant-derived vesicle-like systems may provide biological compatibility, oral delivery, or transdermal delivery advantages, but they often rely on carrier components or biological membrane sources that complicate standardization, scalability, and comparability. Hydrogels and local depot systems provide tissue retention and high local bioactive density, but require rigorous control over gelation, mechanics, degradation, sterilization, and local safety. Across all categories, translation requires reproducible batch manufacture, characterization in serum-containing or disease-relevant media, evidence of in vivo integrity before target-site release, validated disassembly or clearance pathways, and regulatory definitions that distinguish active ingredient, carrier, excipient, metal component, and biological source material [17, 19, 28–30, 78–80, 117, 118, 120–126, 253, 458–466].

Platform-specific translational barriers

Different H-SAN-related platforms face distinct translational barriers, and their development should be evaluated by considering both conceptual status and platform-specific risk rather than through a single generalized framework. Core H-SANs should be assessed as carrier-free or predominantly bioactive self-assemblies in which herbal bioactive molecules or phytochemical-rich components make a major structural and functional contribution. By contrast, extended or adjacent systems, including polymer hybrids, peptide or nucleic acid hybrids, vesicular carriers, and plant-derived extracellular vesicle-like nanoparticles, require additional evaluation of carrier contribution, compositional complexity, batch comparability, and regulatory classification. A comparative view across platforms indicates that the field should move from simply reporting whether a nanostructure can form toward defining why it forms, when it remains stable, where it disassembles, and which therapeutic setting its properties best fit. Monocomponent carrier-free assemblies have clear advantages, including high drug loading, low excipient burden, and relatively simple formulation composition. However, because they are mainly stabilized by reversible noncovalent interactions, they may be sensitive to dilution, serum proteins, salts, and shear stress after administration. Future studies should routinely evaluate CAC or CMC values, dilution stability, serum stability, release kinetics, and structural integrity in biologically relevant media, as discussed above for micelle-like assemblies [18, 48, 61, 62, 71, 96, 97, 100, 101]. Multicomponent co-assemblies and herbal–drug hybrids provide greater functional diversity and may support synergistic or combination therapy. Their main challenges are stoichiometric control, batch-to-batch reproducibility, interaction stability, and the need to distinguish true co-assembly from simple physical mixing or co-precipitation. Orthogonal characterization, including particle analysis, spectroscopy, chromatography, and release profiling, is required to define their critical quality attributes and improve chemistry, manufacturing, and controls comparability [19, 44, 89, 103–106, 120–126, 135, 136]. Metal-coordination networks can enhance structural stability and stimuli responsiveness, but they introduce additional safety and regulatory concerns. Metal speciation, valence state, ligand exchange, metal release, catalytic activity, biodistribution, tissue retention, and interference with endogenous metal homeostasis should be carefully assessed. These systems may also require classification as inorganic–organic hybrid nanomaterials rather than simple herbal nanoplatforms [62, 71–77, 221, 231, 440]. Vesicular and vesicle-like systems require clear conceptual boundaries. Liposomes, phytosomes, transfersomes, ethosomes, and polymer-stabilized vesicles should be regarded as adjacent delivery formats when their structures are dominated by phospholipids, surfactants, ethanol-rich membranes, or polymers. Plant-derived extracellular vesicle-like nanoparticles should also be evaluated separately because they originate from biological membranes rather than direct phytochemical self-assembly. Their key translational issues include source variability, purification, cargo heterogeneity, sterilization, dose normalization, and standardized reporting [22–33, 78, 117, 467]. Hydrogels and local depot systems are promising for wound repair, inflammatory disease, tissue regeneration, and post-surgical delivery. However, their clinical translation requires reproducible gelation, injectability, mechanical strength, degradation kinetics, sterilization compatibility, local safety, and clarification of the biological fate of degraded fragments. Rheological properties, in situ gelation time, swelling behavior, erosion profile, and payload release under clinically relevant conditions should be treated as major quality attributes [60, 108–116, 446–449]. Taken together, monocomponent assemblies are compositionally simple but require stronger proof of integrity under dilution and serum exposure; multicomponent co-assemblies offer therapeutic synergy but require stricter stoichiometric and interaction control; metal-coordination networks provide improved robustness and responsiveness but raise metal-safety and ligand-exchange concerns; vesicular and vesicle-like systems can support barrier delivery but require clear classification and batch comparability; and hydrogels are promising for local therapy but depend on reproducible mechanics and degradation.

Manufacturing robustness, scalability, and storage stability

Manufacturing robustness remains one of the central barriers to H-SAN translation because plant-derived inputs are intrinsically heterogeneous. Herbal compounds, extracts, and phytochemical-rich fractions may vary with botanical source, cultivar, geography, season, harvest period, storage, extraction solvent, and purification protocol. Because H-SAN formation depends on a narrow balance of weak molecular interactions, even minor changes in low-abundance constituents, oxidation products, salts, residual solvents, or impurity profiles may influence nucleation, growth, morphology, particle size distribution, PDI, zeta potential, loading, release behavior, and long-term stability. Therefore, clinical development should begin with authenticated supply chains, standardized extraction procedures, chemical fingerprinting, marker-compound control, impurity profiling, and batch-to-batch comparability testing. In this context, plant-derived input variability should be treated as a critical material attribute rather than as a background feature of herbal formulation [78, 120]. A QbD-oriented workflow should explicitly connect critical material attributes, critical process parameters, and critical quality attributes within one product-specific control strategy. Critical material attributes may include botanical authentication, chromatographic or spectroscopic fingerprints, marker-compound ranges, total phenolic or saponin content, impurity profiles, moisture content, residual solvents, oxidation state, ionizable groups, and metal-chelating capacity. Critical process parameters include solvent/antisolvent ratio, flow-rate ratio, mixing intensity, supersaturation, pH, ionic strength, temperature, residence time, shear environment, aging time, solvent removal, drying, and redispersion conditions. Critical quality attributes should include particle size distribution, PDI, zeta potential, morphology, association state, loading, encapsulation or co-assembly efficiency, residual solvent, sterility, dilution stability, serum stability, release behavior, and biological activity. Such CMA–CPP–CQA mapping is particularly important for multicomponent herbal active ingredients, where a single marker compound is insufficient to capture the relationship between chemical composition and self-assembly behavior [120–126]. Transitioning from bench-scale preparation to industrial manufacturing introduces additional complexity. Manual operations, including dropwise nanoprecipitation, pH titration, solvent evaporation, and uncontrolled sonication, can generate residence-time gradients, shear heterogeneity, and batch-dependent supersaturation when production volumes increase. Continuous microfluidic mixing, flash or impinging-jet precipitation, high-gravity reactors, supercritical-fluid processing, and controlled antisolvent precipitation may improve reproducibility by controlling mixing rate, residence time, supersaturation, and solvent removal. However, these technologies should not be regarded as scale-up solutions alone. Their translational value depends on integration with in-line or at-line process analytical technologies, including DLS, Raman, NIR, UV–Vis, conductivity, pH, turbidity, and zeta-potential monitoring. These tools can help define process windows, detect raw-material-driven deviations, and support feedback control before changes propagate into final CQAs. This integrated raw-material fingerprinting–continuous manufacturing–PAT strategy provides a practical route toward GMP-compatible and regulatory-comparable H-SAN production [120–126]. Storage stability should also be evaluated in a platform-specific manner. Polyphenol-rich systems may undergo oxidation, metal-coordination systems may experience ligand exchange or metal leakage, micelle-like systems may coarsen or disassemble after dilution, and vesicular systems may fuse, leak cargo, or change membrane composition. Lyophilization, spray drying, spray-freeze-drying, cryoprotectants, antioxidant protection, moisture control, and redispersibility testing should be optimized according to the dominant assembly mechanism. Long-term and accelerated stability testing should include particle size, PDI, zeta potential, morphology, chemical degradation, residual solvent, release behavior, and bioactivity retention [123–126, 235, 245, 246, 468].

Structural characterization in biological media

A major limitation of current H-SAN studies is that most structural characterization is performed in water, PBS, or storage buffer, whereas the biological environment is far more complex. Serum proteins, lipoproteins, salts, enzymes, extracellular matrix components, mucus, gastrointestinal fluid, lysosomal acidity, and disease-specific redox conditions can substantially alter nanostructure integrity. Thus, H-SAN characterization should move from formulation-only analysis to biointerface analysis [80, 118, 253]. For monocomponent and micelle-like assemblies, dilution stability and CAC/CMC behavior should be tested after realistic dilution into serum-containing media. For multicomponent assemblies, investigators should determine whether each component remains in the same particle after exposure to biological fluids. For metal-coordination networks, metal–ligand stability, ligand exchange, and metal release should be monitored under acidic, reductive, and chelator-rich conditions. For vesicles and plant-derived vesicle-like nanoparticles, membrane integrity, cargo leakage, particle fusion, and time-dependent protein corona formation should be evaluated. Corona analysis should distinguish the rapidly exchangeable soft corona from the more stable hard corona, because these layers may have different effects on particle size, surface charge, ligand exposure, macrophage uptake, complement activation, and biological identity [80, 248, 249, 253, 272–274]. Useful methods include DLS, NTA, cryo-TEM, TEM, AFM, SAXS/SANS, FRET-based integrity assays, fluorescence quenching, LC–MS/MS, ICP–MS, gel electrophoresis, proteomics, lipidomics, and release profiling [80, 118, 253, 467]. The balance between in vivo integrity and desired disassembly is particularly important. A clinically useful H-SAN should remain sufficiently intact during circulation or barrier transit but disassemble or reorganize at the intended pathological site. This requires time-resolved evaluation of structural fate, not only endpoint biodistribution. Future studies should incorporate dual-label or multi-label tracking strategies to distinguish intact particle accumulation from free-drug distribution, degraded fragments, or adsorbed surface components. Without such evidence, targeting efficiency and stimulus-responsive release may be overinterpreted [80, 253–255].

Biosafety, biocompatibility, and long-term fate

Although many herbal compounds are considered biocompatible, nanoscale organization can reshape their safety profile. Particle size, surface charge, complement activation, immune-cell uptake, organ accumulation, and clearance route may differ substantially from those of the free compound. Phenolic, glycosidic, cationic, or metal-rich H-SAN surfaces can recruit serum proteins and reshape immune recognition, but this protein corona should not be viewed as a single static coating. After entry into the bloodstream, abundant plasma proteins with rapid association kinetics may first form a loosely bound and exchangeable soft corona. These proteins can then be gradually replaced by lower-abundance proteins with higher surface affinity, generating a more stable hard corona through a Vroman-like process. The hard corona can redefine the biological identity of H-SANs by altering hydrodynamic size, surface charge, ligand accessibility, complement activation, macrophage recognition, and clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system in the liver and spleen [78–81, 248, 249, 272–274].

This corona maturation is particularly important for actively targeted H-SANs. A stable hard corona may attenuate the exposure of folate, peptide, transferrin, glycan, mannose, or other receptor-binding ligands, thereby reducing receptor-mediated uptake under physiological conditions. In addition, enrichment of opsonins, immunoglobulins, complement proteins, apolipoproteins, or coagulation-related proteins may promote hepatic and splenic sequestration or increase immunological risk. Surface engineering should therefore be optimized under serum-containing conditions, with attention to ligand density, spacer length, ligand orientation, hydrophilic or zwitterionic shielding, and environment-responsive corona-modulating coatings. Time-resolved corona proteomics, ligand-accessibility assays, complement activation tests, macrophage uptake assays, and serum-conditioned receptor-blocking experiments will be important for evaluating whether targeting motifs remain functional after corona maturation [248, 249, 272–274]. Therefore, biocompatibility should be assessed with attention to nano-specific risks rather than inferred from the traditional use of the parent herb. Key safety evaluations should include hemolysis, complement activation, cytokine release, coagulation effects, macrophage uptake, oxidative stress, mitochondrial toxicity, genotoxicity, reproductive toxicity when relevant, repeated-dose toxicity, and time-resolved corona profiling under physiologically relevant serum or plasma conditions. For long-circulating, metal-containing, or inorganic–organic hybrid systems, liver and spleen retention should be evaluated over extended periods. For metal–phenolic networks and magnetic or catalytic hybrids, metal accumulation and disruption of endogenous metal homeostasis should be carefully monitored. For plant-derived vesicle-like systems, microbiome effects, immunogenic cargo, and batch-dependent biological activity need additional attention [78–81, 117, 272]. Clearance mechanisms should be matched to platform design. Ultrasmall particles may undergo renal excretion, while larger or opsonized particles are commonly cleared by the mononuclear phagocyte system. Because hard-corona composition strongly influences opsonization and phagocyte recognition, corona-resolved biodistribution studies are needed to distinguish intrinsic nanoparticle clearance from clearance driven by adsorbed plasma proteins. Hydrogels may degrade locally, while vesicular systems may be metabolized through membrane-trafficking pathways. Repeated dosing is particularly important for chronic diseases, including neurodegeneration, metabolic disorders, inflammatory disease, and cardiovascular disease. Safety studies should therefore include both acute and chronic exposure windows [78–81, 248, 249, 272–274].

Human-relevant pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics, IVIV translation, and herb–drug interactions

The gap between animal models and human exposure remains a major challenge. Nanoparticle delivery efficiency varies across species, disease models, vascular phenotypes, immune states, and administration routes. For tumor therapy, the EPR effect is highly heterogeneous and often overestimated in preclinical models. For oral delivery, rodent gastrointestinal transit, mucus properties, and microbiota do not fully represent human physiology. For CNS applications, BBB integrity varies with disease stage and model type. These differences limit simple extrapolation from mouse efficacy to human benefit [80, 251–255]. Future studies should integrate human-relevant pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics approaches earlier in development. Physiologically based pharmacokinetic modeling, human serum-corona data, organ-on-chip systems, intestinal models, endothelial barrier models, macrophage uptake assays, humanized immune models, and ex vivo tissue systems can help predict absorption, distribution, metabolism, clearance, and tissue exposure. Microdose or microtracer studies may be useful once sufficient safety evidence is available. For active targeting systems, ligand accessibility should be tested after corona formation because receptor binding observed in serum-free cell culture may not persist in vivo [248, 255, 273, 469–471]. Herb–drug interactions also require systematic evaluation. Many phytochemicals affect CYP enzymes, phase II enzymes, P-glycoprotein, OATP, BCRP, and other transporters. Nanoformulation may increase solubility, alter tissue distribution, prolong circulation, or change intestinal exposure, thereby amplifying interaction risk. This is especially relevant because patients receiving herbal nanomedicine may also use anticancer agents, anticoagulants, antidiabetic drugs, lipid-lowering drugs, or anti-inflammatory therapies. A staged workflow combining CYP/transporter screening, PBPK modeling, and co-medication risk assessment should be incorporated into translational planning [248, 273, 469].

Regulatory, chemistry, manufacturing, and controls comparability, and data standards

H-SANs occupy an overlapping regulatory space between botanical drug products, nanomedicines, complex generics, biologically derived products, and combination products. Regulatory classification will differ according to platform type. Monocomponent carrier-free assemblies may be closer to nano-enabled active ingredients. Multicomponent herbal–drug hybrids may be treated as fixed-combination or complex nanomedicine products. Metal-coordination networks may require additional assessment of metal components and inorganic–organic hybrid behavior. Vesicular systems may fall under liposomal, phytosomal, transdermal, or biological-vesicle frameworks depending on composition and source. Plant-derived vesicle-like nanoparticles may face the most uncertain classification because they combine botanical source, biological membrane structure, and nanoscale delivery behavior [78–80, 117, 467]. Chemistry, manufacturing, and controls requirements should therefore be defined early and revised as the formulation evolves. For H-SANs, chemistry, manufacturing, and controls control should explicitly bridge botanical-drug quality control and nano-specific characterization. For botanical inputs, chemical fingerprinting, botanical authentication, impurity profiling, marker-compound control, and batch-to-batch comparability remain essential. However, for multicomponent herbal inputs, regulatory management should not rely solely on one or two marker compounds because trace constituents, impurity profiles, oxidation state, and component ratios may influence self-assembly behavior. For nanostructures, critical quality attributes should include particle size distribution, PDI, morphology, surface charge, composition, loading, encapsulation or association state, aggregation threshold, in vitro release, dilution stability, serum stability, sterility, endotoxin, residual solvent, long-term stability, and biological activity [133–139]. For chemically conjugated prodrug assemblies or hybrid motifs, additional CQAs should include linker identity, linker stability, trigger-dependent cleavage kinetics, parent-drug regeneration efficiency, intermediate identification, and safety profiles of intact and cleaved species [48, 49, 139–141]. For metal-coordination networks, metal content, oxidation state, metal release, ligand exchange, and competing chelation should be included. For vesicular systems, membrane composition, particle number, cargo profile, isolation-related impurities, and source-related variability should be reported [79, 117, 120–126, 467]. Comparability is especially important during scale-up, process optimization, or manufacturing-site transfer. A formulation that appears equivalent by total active content or marker-compound level may differ in self-assembly kinetics, particle size distribution, PDI, internal organization, zeta potential, release profile, protein-corona behavior, serum stability, biodistribution, or biological potency. Therefore, comparability packages should include orthogonal physicochemical characterization, release testing, stability studies, biological potency assays, and, when relevant, serum-conditioned biointerface evaluation. For H-SANs prepared from multicomponent herbal active ingredients, comparability should link raw-material fingerprints, process parameters, and nano-CQA specifications within one product-specific control strategy. Harmonized reporting standards and FAIR data repositories would improve reproducibility and allow cross-study comparison. For plant-derived vesicles, extracellular-vesicle-oriented reporting standards should be adapted to include plant species, cultivar, tissue source, isolation method, particle quantification, cargo profile, purity assessment, and biological assay conditions [79, 117, 121–126, 467, 472].

AI-driven design, digital process control, and predictive translation

Artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted design may help address the high-dimensional complexity of H-SANs, but its current value is limited by sparse, heterogeneous, and poorly annotated datasets. Reliable models require curated information linking molecular structure, assembly conditions, particle attributes, biological-media stability, protein corona, release behavior, and pharmacological outcomes. Generative models and molecular simulations can help identify phytochemical pairs, amphiphilic derivatives, metal–ligand combinations, and responsive linkers, but predictions must be validated experimentally under realistic conditions [249, 274, 472]. In manufacturing, AI-enabled process control may be more immediately useful. Self-driving microfluidic platforms, reinforcement-learning controllers, and digital twins can optimize solvent ratio, flow rate, pH, mixing energy, temperature, and aging time to maintain particle size and PDI within the design space. Digital twins should extend beyond particle formation to include solvent removal, drying, redispersion, storage, and shipment. For polyphenol, saponin, and metal-coordination systems, stability-aware models should incorporate oxidation, hydrolysis, ligand exchange, moisture sensitivity, and Ostwald ripening. Explainable models, audit trails, and data provenance will be necessary for regulatory acceptance [249, 274, 468, 472].

Ethical sourcing, ABS/DSI compliance, and sustainable translation

The clinical translation of H-SANs also depends on reliable and ethical access to plant-derived resources. Botanical materials should be sourced through traceable supply chains that document species identity, geographic origin, cultivation or collection conditions, harvest time, processing, storage, and extraction. Good Agricultural and Collection Practices, benefit-sharing agreements, and biodiversity protection should be integrated into chemistry, manufacturing, and controls strategies rather than treated as separate administrative issues [473]. Sustainability is especially important for herbal nanomedicine because increased clinical demand may intensify pressure on medicinal plant resources. For rare, slow-growing, or wild-harvested species, semi-synthesis, biosynthesis, plant cell culture, or agricultural standardization may reduce ecological risk. Provenance records should be linked to batch release, quality control, and regulatory documentation. For sequence-enabled discovery, datasets should be tagged with origin metadata and governed by FAIR and CARE principles to improve transparency, reuse, and benefit-sharing. In the long term, responsible sourcing will improve not only ethical compliance but also product reproducibility, patient confidence, and global acceptability [472–477]. Overall, the future of H-SANs will depend on whether the field can move from elegant laboratory assemblies to rigorously defined, reproducible, and clinically testable products. Platform-specific translational strategies are essential. Monocomponent assemblies require proof of integrity under dilution and biological exposure. Multicomponent hybrids require stoichiometric and mechanistic control. Metal-coordination networks require metal-safety and ligand-exchange evaluation. Vesicular and plant-derived vesicle-like systems require clear classification and standardized isolation. Hydrogels require mechanical, degradation, and local-safety validation. Across all categories, successful translation will require integrated characterization in biological media, QbD-guided manufacturing, validated safety and pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics models, regulatory clarity, ethical sourcing, and transparent reporting. With these advances, H-SANs may evolve from promising preclinical constructs into a practical framework for high-payload, multifunctional, and precision herbal nanomedicine.

Conclusion

Advanced H-SANs represent an emerging interface between herbal therapeutics and nanomedicine rather than an already established clinical platform. Reported systems suggest that phytochemical self-assembly can improve solubility, increase bioactive loading, enable stimulus-associated release, and support targeted or multifunctional design in preclinical models. However, these advantages must be interpreted cautiously because translational development remains constrained by dilution-dependent colloidal instability, incomplete knowledge of in vivo disassembly and parent-drug regeneration, protein corona formation, tumor transport heterogeneity, raw-material variability, manufacturing reproducibility, regulatory classification, and limited human-relevant pharmacokinetic and safety evidence. Future work should combine rigorous physicochemical characterization, validated biological models, reproducible manufacturing controls, and careful assessment of safety, biodistribution, and therapeutic benefit. With such validation, H-SANs may provide a rational framework for developing high-payload herbal nanomedicine, but their clinical utility remains to be established through systematic translational studies.
