Voice recognition enhancement by genetic algorithm
Authors: Mohamed Salah Salhi, Ali Hamdan Alenezi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research paper investigates the application of Genetic Algorithms (GA) in optimizing Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) for phoneme recognition. The study examines the formalism of GA, their parameters, and operators, and describes the genetic strategy adopted for phoneme recognition using the TIMIT sound database. The paper presents the outcomes of experiments conducted on the phonemes of the test base and the DR1 dialect learning base of the TIMIT base, and compares the recognition rates obtained by learning and testing with those guided by Self-Organizing Map (SOM) experiments. The findings suggest that the GA algorithm provides improved recognition rates and extends the search space to a global optimum, but can sometimes produce unacceptable results due to rapid and premature convergence and the overfitting problem. The study proposes a hybrid model between SOM and GA to take advantage of each of their properties and improve recognition rates. The results show that the proposed approach achieves an average recognition rate of over 95 %.Keywords: Neural map SOM, Hybrid model, Genetic algorithm GA, Phonemes recognition

Introduction

In the field of speech recognition, the accuracy rate typically fluctuates based on the techniques and environmental factors used. By employing a dynamic approach that seeks a globally optimal solution, recognition rates can be enhanced, albeit at the cost of a substantial increase in computational power.

Some research papers collectively explore the utilization of advanced computational techniques such as genetic algorithms, artificial neural networks, and optimization procedures to address complex nonlinear problems in various scientific and biological contexts. The paper “Design of stochastic solvers based on genetic algorithms for solving nonlinear equations”, by Muhammad Asif Zahoor Raja et al., 2014, delves into the design of stochastic solvers employing genetic algorithms for nonlinear equations [1]. The paper “Gudermannian neural networks using the optimization procedures of genetic algorithm and active set approach for the three-species food chain nonlinear model” in 2022, discusses the use of Gudermannian neural networks optimized via genetic algorithms and active set approaches to model a three-species food chain. The paper “Evolutionary computing for nonlinear singular boundary value problems using neural network, genetic algorithm and active-set algorithm”, examines evolutionary computing methodologies integrating neural networks, genetic algorithms, and active-set algorithms to tackle nonlinear singular boundary value problems. The paper “Fractional Meyer neural network procedures optimized by the genetic algorithm to solve the Bagley-Torvik model”, highlights the application of fractional Meyer neural network procedures, optimized by genetic algorithms, to the Bagley-Torvik model. The paper “Numerical computing to solve the nonlinear corneal system of eye surgery using the capability of Morlet wavelet artificial neural networks”, in 2021, employs Morlet wavelet artificial neural networks for numerical computing to address nonlinear issues in corneal surgery systems. These studies demonstrate the effectiveness of combining neural networks with genetic algorithms and other optimization techniques to solve complex nonlinear equations and models in diverse domains.

Our suggestion involves establishing a platform that uses the K-means paradigm in conjunction with the Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC) of the speech signal. This platform will serve to explore the most effective method for incorporating genetic algorithms and construct a phoneme recognition system using evolutionary algorithms.

The K-means algorithm, also called the moving centers algorithm, was at the origin of the neural SOM model which subsequently became widely used in phonemic classification. The mobile centers are replaced by self-organizing neurons.

The variations of the so-called mobile centers (neurons) adapt well to the evaluation of the variability of speech, and consequently to its classification. This characteristic of having diverse winning neurons at each recognition iteration aligns well with the need for a varied population in a genetic algorithm. This diversity is crucial for optimizing the results of classification and phonemic recognition, following the principle that the best individual is the one that survives.

Our goals are as follows:

Develop a phoneme recognition system that is founded on genetic algorithms.

Embrace a novel, non-linear format that can be adjusted to the genetic algorithm for the determination of recognition rates, regardless of the context or speakers.

Confirm the results using phoneme samples from the TIMIT learning database and compare them with the neural map (SOM) classifier.

Literal review on the genetic algorithm GA

Genetic considerations

The Genetic Algorithm (GA), developed by John Holland at the University of Michigan in 1970, is inspired by biological evolution to understand adaptive processes in natural systems and create resilient software. GA is based on evolution through generations, reproduction with genetic recombination and mutations, and natural selection [2,3,and4]]. The main goal of GA is to mimic how life adapts to its environment, viewing adaptation as searching for the optimum of a survival function. The algorithm starts by generating many solutions, evolving them through reproduction, recombination, and mutation.

Individuals (solutions) are represented as chromosomes composed of alleles. This process combines the survival of the fittest with random gene selection to determine traits. The search ends when a stopping criterion is met. Reproduction involves copying chromosomes based on their fitness function values, favoring higher-valued chromosomes for the next generation. The crossover operator then creates new chromosomes by exchanging segments between pairs of randomly selected chromosomes [5,6,and7]]. Mutation preserves genetic diversity with a high mutation probability [8,9].

GA can be classified into “Binary Entity GA” and “Real Code GA.” Binary Entity GA uses chromosomes as strings of “0″ or “1″ for optimizing combinatorial problems, while Real Code GA uses real values for function optimization in continuous space. The choice depends on specific needs [10,11].

Formalism and emergence of the GA algorithm

To broaden research in phoneme recognition, genetic algorithms (GAs) can be used as learning algorithms based on evolutionary processes guided by efficiency functions, also known as objective functions or ‘fitness.’ GAs are optimization tools that require a well-defined problem, carefully selecting the model to be reconstructed and its parameters.

Our goal is to combine the strengths of individual solutions to create more effective ones. To develop the model using GAs, we must define the model's chromosomes, objective functions for scoring, and the specific language used by the algorithm.

A chromosome is a vector of parameters representing an individual solution, whose effectiveness is gauged by how well the model's outcomes align with the input data. The genome is the set of parameters characterizing an individual, while a gene is a specific parameter. A population consists of a set of individuals, and a generation represents the population at a given time.

The formalism of a GA involves selecting a population of individuals represented by chromosomes and creating a new generation from this population. Similar to living beings, generating a member of the next generation requires two parents whose genomes are crossed. Parents are chosen based on their effectiveness, and to introduce genetic diversity, mutations occur on the individual's genes stored in its genome. GA operates on a population of individuals, each characterized by a genetic fingerprint represented by its chromosome. Evolution in GA is driven by natural selection and reproduction. Natural selection ensures survival and reproduction of the best-adapted individuals. Reproduction, akin to sexual reproduction, recombines solutions to produce well-adapted descendants over several generations [12,13,and14]].

The GA parameters

Population Size: The size of the population impacts the convergence of a genetic algorithm (GA). Larger populations enhance diversity but may slow convergence and reduce efficiency due to longer execution times. Smaller populations risk getting stuck in local minima. Typically, population sizes range from 25 to 150 individuals, depending on the problem and computational resources [15,16].

Initialization: Chromosome representation encodes problem solutions as individuals using bit strings or real vectors. Bit strings simplify crossover and mutation but may be inefficient in high-dimensional spaces. Real vector GA preserves problem structure and reduces search space, ensuring new individuals respect problem constraints.

Crossover Rate: The crossover rate determines the proportion of individuals undergoing crossover. A rate between 0.25 and 0.70 is generally effective, but the optimal rate depends on the problem. Too high a rate can destroy strong structures, while too low a rate can slow population change [17].

Mutation Rate: Mutation introduces diversity and prevents premature convergence. A rate of 0.001, or one change per 1000 bits, is a good starting point. The optimal rate depends on the problem, balancing between randomness and stagnation [18].

Evaluation: The evaluation function assigns scores to individuals, guiding the GA towards the optimum solution. It should balance complexity and constraint satisfaction, providing implicit selection pressure without excessive computational demands while avoiding excessive complexity.

Classification techniques by SOM and GA

Mikhaïl Crucianu's 1997 paper “Evolution Algorithms for Neural Networks” [19]. discusses the combined use of Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) and Genetic Algorithms (GAs). Specifically, Kohonen Self-Organizing Maps (SOMs) have numerous parameters that GAs can optimize, often focusing on connection weights. In some cases, like in Maniezzo's 1993 work, the GA also optimizes the precision of these weights, which is derived from a differential characteristic equation and influences the network's complexity and generalization capacity.

In 1999, Kim et al. proposed a method for adapting Self-Organizing Maps (SOMs) using evolution and learning for image classification. Their approach used a chromosomal structure of successive real numbers to randomly generate the initial population based on a regular distribution. This structure included several prototype vectors, the map's dimensions, and the number of components in each prototype vector.

In 1994, Mcinerney and Dhawan introduced a hybrid GA algorithm combined with Kohonen learning. Their method involves a chromosome composed of concatenated prototype vectors and learning parameters like cycle and iteration numbers, map size, update equations, and map topology. Simultaneously optimizing prototypes and learning parameters can be challenging. The connectivity of the Kohonen map with a specified number of units can be optimized using GA, acting as another control parameter for complexity. Reducing this parameter leads to faster learning, especially for evolutionary algorithms, and simplifies knowledge extraction post-learning from the network.

In 1995, Hamalanian introduced a technique for modeling Kohonen maps with GA. His method uses a binary string as the chromosome, derived from a connection matrix where 1 indicates a connection between neurons and 0 indicates no connection. The chromosome is represented by the upper triangle of the matrix, as shown in figure (1).

In the year 1998, Dasdan presented innovative unsupervised learning algorithms, which were created using a genetic method. These algorithms encode the learning or updating regulations of the prototype vectors into a chain-like structure. Subsequently, Genetic Algorithm (GA) is employed to seek out the most effective unsupervised learning rules, based on a specific strength metric. Through his research, Dasdan discovered multiple learning rules that outperformed Kohonen's rule in the examined applications. His findings suggest that for certain applications, there exist several algorithms that result in a similar organization to the one generated by Kohonen's algorithm [20].

It is important to note that the best individuals are generally those who minimize the objective function (fitness). In other words, these individuals are the closest in terms of Euclidean distance to the real components of the input space.

Stop Criterion: The selection and reproduction assessment steps are applied iteratively to the population, until a stopping condition is satisfied. This condition will be defined by the upper size of generations, or a lower fitness value, to have convergence towards the best solution.

During assessment, the population tends to converge so that individuals tend to become more and more similar to each other. When the population is mainly normalized, the individuals give a good approximation of an optimum of the problem. If this optimum is not always total, it is generally a local optimum close (in quality) to this one. For this we defined this condition with a maximum number of generations, considered in our case, equal to 300 iterations.

This work therefore consists of extracting, from the TIMIT database, certain samples of phonemes under bases corresponding to dialects of the same DR1 testing and learning area. This specification increases the chances of improving recognition rates. Then determine the MFCC coefficient matrices representing a specific property of each phoneme sample. Following which, we proceed to the vertical concatenation of the matrices of the same phoneme samples. We associate the Kmeans algorithm with the equivalent matrix obtained in order to establish a reference vector which can itself be used subsequently for a comparison model with possible solutions of the AG algorithm.

In recent years, various alternative metaheuristic algorithms have been developed, each inspired by different natural phenomena or processes. For instance, the Social-Spider Algorithm (SSA) mimics the social behavior of spiders, where individuals (spiders) communicate and cooperate to find optimal solutions. Similarly, the States of Matter Method (SMM) draws inspiration from the physical states of matter (solid, liquid, and gas) to guide the search process, with particles transitioning between states to explore the solution space more effectively. These algorithms, like GA, aim to balance exploration and exploitation to avoid local optima and find global solutions. They bring unique perspectives and mechanisms to the optimization landscape, expanding the toolkit available for solving complex problems.

SSA might be more efficient for problems where social interaction analogies fit well. GA is relatively straightforward to implement and understand, especially in voice recognition, which might make it easier to use compared to more complex algorithms like SMM. Likewise, GA can be computationally intensive, especially with large populations and many generations. Other algorithms might offer faster convergence in certain scenarios.

Evaluation of proposed approach

We used different values of K in the selection of the reference vector that fits the individuals of the population randomly generated by the GA. This choice ensures convergence of the algorithm towards the optimum and is applied to determine both self-consistency rates and generalization rates.

This approach was used on samples of certain target phonemes, such as ‘aa’, ‘ow’, ‘iy’, as well as on the entire base of each of the phonemes from the DR1 repertoire. We studied the bases of vowels_mel, semi-vowels_mel, nasals_mel, fricatives_mel, affricates_mel, others_mel, and stops_mel to compare the results obtained by the GA associated with K-means with each other and with those established by the Kohonen SOM map.

During experiments on the phonemes of the test base and the DR1 dialect learning base of the TIMIT base, it was observed that the recognition rate improved as the number k of the k-means algorithm associated with the GA increased. This is due to the increased diversity of the population.

The recognition rate obtained by learning was often better than that obtained for the test. This can be attributed to the higher number of samples of the same phoneme in the learning bases than in the test bases. A higher number of samples of the same class of phonemes results in an improved recognition rate.

However, there were a few exceptions where the rate of generalization exceeded that of self-consistency. This indicates that although the GA algorithm provides improved recognition rates and extends the search space to a global optimum, it can sometimes produce unacceptable results due to its property of rapid and premature convergence and the overfitting problem.

It was also observed that the more the probability of selection “Ps” decreased and that of mutation “Pm” increased, the more the diversity of the population was ensured across generations, thereby limiting the problem of rapid convergence of the GA.

The recognition rates by the GA algorithm were higher than the learning and testing rates guided by the SOM experiments. The adopted strategy resulted in an average recognition rate that exceeded 95 %.

Figure (4) shows that the SOM model has the property of ensuring the genetic diversity of a population, leading to the consideration of a hybrid model between the SOM and the GA, taking advantage of each of them.

When studying separate phoneme elements such as aa, ow, iy, it was found that the recognition rate improved as the study was limited to a few samples of each phoneme. This is because the algorithm specializes in recognizing the phoneme under consideration as the work space becomes smaller. The rate also increased slightly with the increase in the number of classes k of the associated K-means algorithm, with k = 11 yielding good results.

However, in some cases, generalization rates were slightly higher than self-consistency rates due to insufficient genetic diversity translated by premature convergence of the GA algorithm. This genetic diversity must be ensured by the variation of k of the k-means algorithm, with diversity increasing with the increasing value of k. It is accompanied by an improvement in the recognition rate and stability of the curves relating to the elites.

Any variation on the curve representative of the objective function (fitness) resulted in a proportional variation on the scores of the elites and ultimately on their recognition rates. Conclusion

The variability of the voice signal can limit the robustness of phonemic recognition tools like the Kohonen SOM map. However, recognition rates can be improved by expanding the search space with genetic algorithms GA. This paper presents the formalism of GAs, their parameters, and operators, along with the genetic strategy for phonemic recognition using the TIMIT voice dataset. By applying GA, where population diversity is ensured by the K-means algorithm, which is at the origin of SOM, good results can be achieved due to the extended search space. However, exclusive use of GA or a decreasing value of the moving center K can cause rapid, premature convergence to a potentially unacceptable global solution.

Phoneme recognition using exclusively the SOM model often achieves limited results, constrained by network parameters and phoneme sample sizes. To address these limitations, the paper proposes enhancing phoneme recognition by hybridizing GA with the SOM map to leverage both models. The GA is relatively straightforward to implement and understand in voice recognition, making it easier to use compared to more complex algorithms like the States of Matter Method SMM. However, it can be computationally intensive, especially with large populations and many generations.
