Pilot study of machine learning in the task of distinguishing high and low-grade pediatric hydronephrosis on ultrasound
Authors: Matthew Sloan, Hui Li, Hernan A. Lescay, Clark Judge, Li Lan, Parviz Hajiyev, Maryellen L. Giger, Mohan S. Gundeti
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
PurposeHydronephrosis is a common pediatric urological condition, characterized by dilation of the renal collecting system. Accurate identification of the severity of hydronephrosis is crucial in clinical management, as high-grade hydronephrosis can cause significant damage to the kidney. In this pilot study, we demonstrate the feasibility of machine learning in differentiating between high and low-grade hydronephrosis in pediatric patients.Materials and MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 592 images from 90 unique patients ages 0–8 years diagnosed with hydronephrosis at the University of Chicago’s Pediatric Urology Clinic. The study included 74 high-grade hydronephrosis (145 images) and 227 low-grade hydronephrosis (447 images). Patients were excluded if they had less than 2 studies prior to surgical intervention or had structural abnormalities. We developed a radiomic-based artificial intelligence algorithm incorporating computerized texture analysis and machine learning (support-vector machine) to yield a predictor of hydronephrosis grade.ResultsReceiver operating characteristic analysis of the classifier output yielded an area under the curve value of 0.86 (95% CI 0.81–0.92) in the task of distinguishing between low and high-grade hydronephrosis using a five-fold cross-validation by kidney. In addition, a Mann–Kendall trend test between computer output and clinical hydronephrosis grade yielded a statistically significant upward trend (p<0.001).ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate the potential of machine learning in the differentiation between low and high-grade hydronephrosis. Further studies are warranted to validate our findings and their generalizability for use in clinical practice as a means to predict clinical outcomes and the resolution of hydronephrosis.

INTRODUCTION

Hydronephrosis, defined as the dilation of the renal collecting system, is diagnosed in 1–5% of all pregnancies in the United States, making it one of the most frequently detected anomalies in pediatric urology [123]. This can be translated to an estimated 40,000–80,000 children diagnosed annually at a cost of over $90 million [4]. Pediatric hydronephrosis can represent a spectrum of etiologies – from a benign transient finding to a serious structural issue requiring surgical intervention, which if left untreated can cause urinary tract infections, sepsis, and worsening renal function [3]. One of the current challenges is determining which children will have clinically significant hydronephrosis. Unfortunately, the natural history of this pathology remains unknown, leading to a lack of established guidelines and limiting providers’ ability to counsel patients and their families [5].

Currently, ultrasound (US) is the modality of choice for both diagnosis and follow-up of pediatric hydronephrosis [6]. It provides valuable information about the size and structure of the kidneys, parenchymal thickness, and presence/severity of hydronephrosis. Additionally, US is noninvasive, does not emit ionizing radiation, does not require an anesthetic, and is low cost.

Pediatric hydronephrosis is typically diagnosed on routine prenatal US and is then further evaluated after birth by postnatal renal US. It is at this time that the grade of a patient’s hydronephrosis is determined. Appropriate and accurate grading of pediatric hydronephrosis is crucial, as further management and treatment are dependent upon this assignment. Such additional management may consist of more invasive imaging studies, including those which emit ionizing radiation and potentially require anesthesia, prolonged prophylactic antibiotic regimens, repeated surveillance US imaging over the span of months to years, and potential surgical intervention.

Numerous grading systems have been developed to aid in standardizing the grading of hydronephrosis [478]. The grading systems equip providers using US for surveillance to have a means to unambiguously classify hydronephrosis. Three of the most used grading systems are the Society for Fetal Urology (SFU), Anterior Posterior Diameter (APD), and Urinary Tract Dilation (UTD) systems [47]. The SFU system considers kidney morphology, degree of dilatation, and presence of renal cortical thinning when determining grade assignment. The APD measures the maximal distance from the anterior to the posterior renal pelvis at the level of the parenchyma. The UTD system includes similar subjective parameters, such as degree of dilation, parenchymal appearance and thickness, ureteral/bladder appearance, and anterior-posterior renal pelvic diameter [6]. Unfortunately, these systems are limited by their inherent subjectivity and reliance on the experience of the sonographer and interpreting provider. The renal pelvis and APD can change significantly depending on the degree of hydration, bladder filling, patient position, and respiration. The subjectivity and variability of the grading systems have been further demonstrated by their relatively low inter-rater reliability and propensity to rely on subjective interpretation of US images [9]. Additionally, these systems provide little prognostic value. While children with lower grades of hydronephrosis typically resolve at an increased frequency than those with higher grades, clinicians are currently unable to reliably correlate the imaging findings of an individual’s hydronephrosis with their eventual clinical outcome and time to resolution [10]. Given these limitations, a more objective and accurate method of determining the degree of hydronephrosis is needed.

There are various studies in the diagnosis of hydronephrosis using artificial intelligence (AI)/machine learning (ML). Both deep learning and conventional radiomics analysis have been employed in these studies to detect renal abnormalities and predict hydronephrosis grade and severity [11121314151617]. Cerrolaza et al. [11] performed quantitative imaging analysis on US in predicting obstructive severity in children with hydronephrosis. The improved performance was observed by using these sonographic biomarkers in grade prediction compared to the SFU grading system [11]. Song et al. [14] performed a deep learning segmentation on US images and measured the ratio of the hydronephrosis area to renal parenchyma. This ratio was used as a biomarker for hydronephrosis severity prediction with high accuracy [14]. Ostrowski et al. [15] developed an automated convolutional neural network model based on a VGG16 model pre-trained on ImageNet to classify hydronephrosis grade. The proposed ML model achieved an overall accuracy of 82.0% with three-fold cross-validation on 710 patients [15]. Lien et al. [16] built three deep-learning models for hydronephrosis detection. The Res-UNet algorithm achieved high accuracy in the detection of moderate/severe hydronephrosis [16].

In this pilot study, we developed a ML algorithm based on radiomic texture features to interrogate the interior of the kidneys to differentiate high-grade from low-grade pediatric hydronephrosis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Dataset

The primary endpoint of this pilot study was to demonstrate that ML can determine low-grade from high-grade hydronephrosis. The research conducted for this manuscript has been carried out in compliance with all applicable laws, regulations, and institutional guidelines. Prior approval was obtained from the relevant Ethics Committee/Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the University of Chicago (approval number: IRB20-0997). We retrospectively reviewed 592 deidentified US images from 90 unique patients ages 0–8 years diagnosed with hydronephrosis at the University of Chicago’s Pediatric Urology Clinic. Informed consent was waived as approved by the IRB committee. Patients were excluded if they had less than 2 studies prior to surgical intervention, or if they had structural abnormalities including ectopic ureter, duplicated system, ureterocele, multicystic dysplastic kidney, posterior urethral valves, neurogenic bladder, and prune belly syndrome. US studies were graded according to the SFU system by a pediatric radiologist. Low-grade and high-grade hydronephrosis were defined as SFU grades I–II and III–IV, respectively. US images were acquired at the University of Chicago Medical Center, with the main US system being a General Electric (GE) US machine, models LOGIQE10 and LOGIQE9, and 8-bit image quantization level. For the analyses, the dataset included 74 high-grade kidneys (145 images) and 227 low-grade kidneys (447 images) (Table 1).

2. Radiomic feature extraction

Each kidney was outlined by a urology resident with 4 years of experience and reviewed by a fellowship-trained pediatric urologist with 20 years of experience with consensus yielding the delineated kidney in the transverse and longitudinal views (Fig. 1). The selection of images was guided by criteria that included factors such as US image quality, absence of annotations or measurements, and the ability to fully visualize the entire kidney parenchyma, collecting system, and renal pelvis both in the transverse and longitudinal views. Approximately only 5% of images were re-contoured after the initial outlines were reviewed by the fellowship-trained pediatric urologist. Twenty-five radiomic features, morphological and texture features, were automatically extracted from these outlines (Table 2) [1819] to characterize the texture, shape, and size [202122]. These features were subsequently input to a support-vector machine (SVM) classifier in the task of distinguishing low-grade from high-grade hydronephrosis (Fig. 2).

3. Classifier design

Five-fold cross-validation was conducted in the task of distinguishing high-grade from low-grade hydronephrosis. The dataset was partitioned in the way that in each fold the high-grade hydronephrosis to low-grade hydronephrosis ratio was kept the same and the same patient was kept in the same fold, i.e., either in the training or test fold but not in both to prevent data leakage. Stepwise feature selection using linear discriminant analysis was performed during each training fold. During each training fold, the radiomic features were iteratively added and removed from the group of selected features using Wilks’ lambda as the feature selection criterion. Linear discriminant analysis was used to calculate the discriminant scores, and these discriminant scores were then used to compute the Wilks’ lambda during each iteration. The F-statistic was applied to determine whether a particular radiomic feature contributed significantly (p<0.05) to the linear discriminant analysis performance in each iteration step [232425]. A subset of radiomic features was selected during each training fold. And within each training fold, a SVM classifier with a linear kernel was trained on these selected features in the task of distinguishing high-grade from low-grade hydronephrosis and then tested on the test fold.

4. Statistical evaluation

Receiver operating characteristic analysis (ROC) was conducted to assess the performance using the SVM classifier output as the decision variable, with the area under the ROC curve (AUC) serving as the figure of merit in the classification task. The evaluation was performed on a by-kidney basis. To evaluate the SVM output trend over the hydronephrosis grade, a nonparametric Mann–Kendall test was performed to determine if there was a positive trend between classifier output and hydronephrosis grade [2627]. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered a statistically significant positive trend. Sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and F1 score for the SVM classifier output were calculated by using a metric cutoff value that minimizes m=(1-sensitivity)2+(1-specificity)2 [2829].

RESULTS

Feature subsets selected from stepwise feature selection for the classification task were mostly texture features. The selected features from each fold were between 7 and 11 features. The majority of features selected were gray-level cooccurrence matrix features that were used to characterize the texture within the kidney. From ROC analysis, an AUC value of 0.86 (95% CI 0.81–0.92) was obtained by using SVM classifier output as a decision variable in the task of distinguishing between low-grade and high-grade hydronephrosis on a five-fold cross-validation by kidney (Fig. 2, Table 3). The sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV, NPV, and F1 score of 75.7%, 86.3%, 83.7%, 64.4%, 91.6%, and 69.6% were obtained by using the cutoff value that minimize (1-sensitivity)2+(1-specificity)2 (Table 3). In addition, a Mann–Kendall trend test between SVM classifier output and clinical hydronephrosis grade yielded a statistically significant upward trend (p<0.001) (Fig. 3). Four examples of hydronephrotic kidneys, each with the human-delineated kidney outline overlaid on the original US image (Fig. 4) [30]. Fig. 4A was from a hydronephrosis patient with an SFU grade of 4, and Fig. 4B was from a hydronephrosis patient with an SFU grade of 1. The predictions of SFU grades agreed with the clinical assessment for both patients. Some cases were classified as false negative and false positive by the classifier, Fig. 4C was a false negative example and Fig. 4D was a false positive example. A hypothesis that could have contributed to the misclassification is that the model could have misinterpreted certain features that may have had a higher correlation with both low-grade and high-grade hydronephrosis, thus making an incorrect prediction. Another explanation was that more intricacies were seen in contrasting SFU grades 2 and 3 as opposed to comparing SFU grades 1 and 4.

DISCUSSION

Hydronephrosis is one of the most frequently detected anomalies in pediatric urology [123]. Many patients with this condition are exposed to multiple imaging studies and interventions, some of which are potentially invasive and associated with potential morbidity. The renal US is cost-effective, non-invasive, and does not expose patients to ionizing radiation, and thus the modality of choice for ongoing surveillance.

The primary goal of providers and their patients with hydronephrosis, whether undergoing surveillance or possible intervention, is to preserve renal function. The hydronephrosis grading systems can help create a standardized classification of the degree of hydronephrosis. As was detailed previously, these grading systems are inherently subjective and dependent on multiple dynamic factors within the urinary tract. The goal of this study was to provide objectivity in the way we grade hydronephrosis by developing an ML algorithm and comparing its accuracy to a trained pediatric radiologist as a reference.

ML has been applied in many different contexts within urology from predicting stone clearance rates in endourology as well as predicting oncological outcomes [313233]. It is also a novel and exciting frontier in pediatric urology, being used in hydronephrosis, hypospadias, posterior urethral valve, detrusor overactivity, vesicoureteral reflux, urinary tract infections, and pyeloplasty [31]. Smail et al. [13] demonstrated an average accuracy of 71% when differentiating between moderate grades (SFU II and III) of hydronephrosis. Their study particularly used deep learning from sagittal renal US images and generated SFU grades and specifically evaluated the comparability to physicians in patients with SFU grades II/III hydronephrosis [13]. Our study found an accuracy of 83.7% in distinguishing high-grade (SFU III and IV) from low-grade (SFU I and II) hydronephrosis.

Our study demonstrates the feasibility and accuracy in differentiating between low- and high-grade hydronephrosis, although there are limitations to discuss. Firstly, the retrospective nature of this study is a clear limitation as it limits our ability to standardize follow-ups for all patients and account for those which are potentially lost to follow-up. However, most AI diagnostic systems have their start with retrospective data. Note that, the US studies from these patients provided data for the development of our ML algorithm and allowed us to pursue our hypothesis that such an algorithm more accurately differentiates between high-grade and low-grade hydronephrosis. Secondly, our study population excluded patients with structural abnormalities (including ectopic ureter, duplicated system, ureterocele, multicystic dysplastic kidney, posterior urethral valves, neurogenic bladder, and prune belly syndrome) and thus could limit the application of our model to the general pediatric population. Thirdly, there is subjectivity within the manual tracing of each renal unit as well as the APD. We acknowledge the varied impact of different sectionals, the potential for image selection bias, and its effect on radiomic features. This was mitigated by reviewing each image that was manually traced by the urology resident with one fellowship-trained pediatric urologist, thus providing a standard way of manually outlining and measuring. Future studies should include consensus amongst multiple providers. Fourthly, although GE US systems were the main scanners for image acquisition in this study, different machine models or scanners from different manufacturers may affect the radiomic feature values, thus affecting the ML model performance. Feature harmonization will need to be considered to ensure the robustness and generalizability of the prediction model in future studies. Fifthly, five-fold cross-validation was performed in this study, as typical in an initial study. The dataset was partitioned in such a way that the same patient was either in the training fold or validation fold, but not in both to avoid data leakage. Our future studies will entail gathering an external dataset, which we will employ for model validation. Concurrently, we will be procuring the outcome data for the study; however, it is difficult to pull such outcome data for all patients. Furthermore, we will collect more cases and will incorporate deep learning and perform analysis regarding the actual clinical treatment decision to assess the effectiveness of the model in our future studies. Lastly, our study was limited by the use and evaluation of one grading system, SFU. Further directions should include incorporating the UTD system.

Our study establishes the potential for ML to accurately correlate SFU grade in patients with low- and high-grade hydronephrosis. Although the integration of ML into clinical practice is beyond the scope of this paper, it introduces the possibility of standardization by adhering to established algorithms and guidelines. The utilization of an AI tool holds the promise of enforcing consistent methodologies for diagnosing hydronephrosis. Envisaging a future practice, we see the automation of hydronephrosis grading and predictive calculations during US scans, seamlessly populating the patient’s US report. Moreover, personalized treatment plans could be developed, leveraging patient-specific data to propose tailored strategies based on the severity of hydronephrosis. Notably, such an AI system could prove invaluable in regions lacking expert pediatric radiologists, ensuring accurate hydronephrosis staging and enhancing patient care. Our future goal is to perform a longitudinal study of ML algorithms to predict clinical outcomes via a combined temporal analysis of US images obtained over time.

CONCLUSIONS

This pilot study from a single institution exhibits ML potential in discerning between low-grade and high-grade hydronephrosis as well as showing a statistically significant upward trend between computer output and SFU grade. Further aims should include further refinement of our ML algorithm and applying it across all grades of hydronephrosis. In the future, we plan to assess and confirm the effectiveness of our ML algorithm in predicting clinical outcomes using a longitudinal AI predictive model.
