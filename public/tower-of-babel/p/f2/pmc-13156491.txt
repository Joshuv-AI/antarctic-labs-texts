Evaluation of the use of segmentation-based and deconvolution-based three-dimensional joint space width analysis of the hand and wrist
Authors: Jilmen Quintiens, Tadiwa H Waungana, Michael T Kuczynski, Sarah L Manske, G Harry van Lenthe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThree-dimensional morphometric analyses of joint space from CT scans can provide a quantitative and unbiased evaluation of radiographic damage from arthritis. This potentially offers greater sensitivity for disease assessment and monitoring than traditional ordinal scoring systems. Previously established methods for 3-dimensional joint space analysis from CT scans can be categorized into segmentation-based and grayscale deconvolution-based methods. However, segmentation-based methods have not been employed for joints other than metacarpophalangeal joints, nor have they been compared to deconvolution-based methods. This study compared the joint space width (JSW) distribution from various hand and wrist joints in photon-counting CT images, using both measurement approaches. From these distributions, the mean (JSW.Mean), standard deviation (JSW.SD), maximum (JSW.Max), minimum (JSW.Min), 95th percentile (JSW.95%), and fifth percentile (JSW.5%) values were calculated. For each morphometry parameter, agreement between the 2 methods was assessed by calculating the coefficient of determination (R2), and the mean and SD of the individual biases. General estimating equations were used to assess the relationship between morphometry and an equivalent/modified Sharp/van der Heijde (SvdH) ordinal radiographic score. In general, agreement between the 2 methods was low: R2 ranged between 0.42 and 0.87; biases up to 0.18 mm \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\pm$\end{document} 0.33 mm. For both methods, an increased degree of joint space narrowing as assessed with SvdH was associated with reduced JSW.Min and increased JSW.SD; for the deconvolution method, it was also associated with decreased JSW.Mean and increased JSW.Max. Interactions between the methods showed a weaker association to SvdH score for JSW.Min and a stronger association for JSW.SD when measured with the deconvolution method, compared to the segmentation method. We conclude that both methods lead to fundamentally different measurement outcomes, and that the deconvolution method might be more sensitive to radiographic damage.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and osteoarthritis (OA) are degenerative joint diseases of which the characteristics observed radiographically include joint space narrowing,1,2 that is, a decrease of the joint space width (JSW) due to a loss of cartilage, and/or joint subluxation. Radiographic damage in RA and OA is typically evaluated using 2-dimensional radiographs and disease-specific ordinal scales. For RA of the hand and wrist, the Sharp/van der Heijde (SvdH) method is the most commonly accepted scoring system.3 This method returns a single score for joint space narrowing and a single score for bone erosion damage. For OA of hand and wrist, multiple scoring systems are available that simultaneously incorporate joint space narrowing, erosion damage, and osteophyte formation; yet, there appears to be no wide consensus on which to use.4 However, these semi-quantitative approaches generally lack sensitivity when monitoring disease progression,5 in part because an ordinal increase in score can reflect a fundamentally altered joint condition and function. Moreover, overlaying features in the 2-dimensional projection of the joint confound the joint space definition.6 Improved sensitivity in monitoring could lead to a better optimized patient treatment and improved outcome of joint function. For the assessment of joint space narrowing, 3-dimensional thickness distributions of the JSW assessed with CT scans provide an alternative approach, which enables an unbiased and continuous evaluation of joint space narrowing.

Current methods for 3-dimensional JSW evaluation can be categorized into grayscale deconvolution methods and binary joint space segmentation methods. The former samples intensity profiles across the joint space, and deconvolves them into a summation of step functions; JSW is then defined as the distance between the 2 peaks present in the deconvolved intensity profile.7 The latter generates a joint space mask from the binary bone masks using a set of morphological operations. Within this joint space mask, JSW is calculated with a sphere-fitting distance transform that calculates the local thickness throughout the masked volume.8

Overall, grayscale deconvolution methods provide a robust technique to assess JSW at spatial resolutions typically acquired with clinical multi-detector CT.7 Yet, while grayscale deconvolution provides a more sensitive approach to estimate JSW from CT images compared to 2-dimensional radiographs,9 this approach has not been compared to other segmentation-based measurement approaches. Furthermore, deconvolution methods have primarily been applied to larger joints, such as the hip, knee, and ankle.7 In comparison, the hand and wrist joints are smaller in size, which may pose challenges for the technique: estimating JSW through deconvolution of the grayscale image data requires sampling of a bimodal grayscale intensity profile across the joint space, where each peak represents one of the articulating bone surfaces. In scenarios, where the JSW is exceptionally low, there may be insufficient contrast across the joint space to adequately distinguish between these 2 peaks.10

Different approaches for segmentation-based JSW measurements have been proposed. However, all of them are primarily suited for high-resolution images, because method accuracy relies upon accurate segmentation. In 2020, the Study grouP for eXtreme Computed Tomography in Rheumatoid Arthritis (SPECTRA) collaboration proposed a consensus method to measure JSW in segmented joint space masks.11 Compared to other similar segmentation methods, their method was less sensitive to boundary effects and joint alignment. However, the SPECTRA method has some limitations. First, the method was exclusively developed for HR-pQCT, an in vivo imaging modality with a small field of view diameter that attains resolutions unachievable by conventional clinical CT. Second, the method depends on the size of the morphological operations used to identify the joint space. Finally, the method has only been used at the second and third metacarpophalangeal (MCP2 and MCP3) joints. While some studies suggest that reduced minimal JSW at the MCP2 and MCP3 joint can be used as an early indicator of RA,12,13 recent work has demonstrated that for these joints, the morphology is not significantly different between RA patients and healthy controls.14–16 Indeed, JSW of MCP joints is not always gravely affected by RA and other joints that show the highest progression of joint space narrowing include the radiolunate (RL), scaphotrapeziotrapezoid (STT), and capitolunate (CL) joints.1 For OA, the scapholunate (SL) and the trapeziometacarpal (TMC) joints are also heavily affected.2,17,18

Generally, the lower resolution of clinical CT does not allow an accurate and precise segmentation of the joint space; however, with the advent of photon-counting CT (PCCT), clinical CT at a markedly increased spatial resolution and quality has become possible.19 This increased resolution could potentially enable both a segmentation of small joint spaces with sufficient detail, as well as a grayscale deconvolution across these small joints. Should both methods be possible, this raises the question which method is most sensitive to reflect progressive radiographic damage. Therefore, the goals of this study were (1) to extend the SPECTRA consensus method for joint space morphometry to PCCT images, for both the MCP2 and MCP3 joints, as well as for other wrist joints; (2) to compare outcome metrics between the segmentation method and the grayscale deconvolution method; and (3) to assess how morphometry parameters from both methods reflect changes in degrading joint quality.

Materials and methods

Image data were taken from our previously published multimodal CT dataset of cadaveric wrists joints.20 This dataset contained PCCT images of 8 cadaveric forearms (White, 3 male, 5 female, age between 75 and 88 yr old), scanned from elbow to hand (Belgian National Council for Bioethics NH019 2023-01-02). Specimens were stored at −20 °C and thawed before imaging.

Image acquisition

Photon-counting CT images were acquired with a state-of-the-art scanner (Naeotom Alpha, Siemens Healthineers, voltage 120 kV, tube current 225 mA, and exposure 588 ms) with a slice thickness of 0.2 mm, an increment of 0.1 mm, and an in-plane pixel size of 0.11 mm. This pixel size corresponded to the 10% MTF as reported by the manufacturer (42.9 lp/cm). Images were reconstructed with a sharp reconstruction kernel (Qr84) and an iterative reconstruction algorithm.

Segmentation-based JSW measurement

Joint space segmentation

Joint space masks were calculated for the MCP2, MCP3, CL, RL, SL, TMC, and STT joints, with the full bone masks as input. Full bone masks were created through image binarization (threshold of 320 mgHA/cm3), after which a closing operation (10 voxels) was performed to create a continuous cortical shell. This shell was then filled. Masks were manually examined and corrected if necessary. The resulting bone masks (mask value 255) were smoothed by using a Gaussian kernel (support = 5, sigma = 2) and re-binarized with a threshold of 147/255.

Joint space masks were acquired after dilation and erosion of the full bone masks (Figures 1 and 2). The consensus method described a dilation of 20 voxels and an erosion of 26 voxels.11 These values were scaled with the ratio of HR-pQCT’s voxel size (0.061 mm) and PCCT’s voxel size, resulting in a dilation of 12 voxels and an erosion of 16 voxels. To ensure accurate processing of the distance transform at the edges of the joint space mask, a dilated joint space mask was also created. Specifically, the joint space mask was obtained by subtracting the full bone masks from the eroded mask. The dilated joint space mask was created by dilating the joint space mask with 16 voxels, and subtracting the full bone masks again.

For the STT joint specifically, an individual joint space mask for both the scaphoid-trapezium and for the scaphoid-trapezoid articulation was made, and these 2 masks were merged into the STT joint space mask (Figure 2E). This way, the larger space at the distal pole of the STT joint was not included in the JSW mask, as this would be a non-relevant zone skewing the JSW distribution.

Distance transform

The dilated joint space masks were upsampled by a factor of 2, and within these upsampled masks, a sphere-fitting procedure was performed. First, a Euclidean distance transform calculated the shortest distance for each masked voxel to a non-masked voxel. Next, the Euclidean distances were sorted in ascending order, and for each value, all voxels within a spherical region were given the same distance value. Hence, at the end of the iterative procedure, all masked voxels were assigned the distance value from the largest sphere in which they could fit. Finally, the distance map in this dilated joint space was masked with the original joint space mask, after which the JSW distribution was calculated (Figure 3A and B). From this distribution, joint space morphometry parameters were extracted. More specifically, the minimal JSW (JSW.Min), maximal JSW (JSW.Max), mean JSW (JSW.Mean), and JSW standard deviation (JSW.SD) were calculated. Additionally, to account for outliers that could distort the JSW.Min and JSW.Max metric, also the fifth percentile and 95th percentile were calculated from the distribution (JSW.5% and JSW.95%, respectively).

The distance transform calculation of the JSW distribution were performed using the ORMIR_XCT Python package (v.1.0), a package that reimplemented standardized pipelines for HR-pQCT analyses.21

Deconvolution-based JSW measurement

Mesh generation and refinement of analysis region

Triangulated, 3-dimensional bone surface meshes, with uniformly sized triangles were generated using the full bone masks obtained prior to segmentation of the joint space. For each joint, a reference bone mesh was selected from which JSW was measured. From the vertices of each reference mesh, rays perpendicular to the surface were projected into the volumetric image space. For rays that intersected with the adjacent bone mesh, their vertex of origin on the reference bone mesh was considered for inclusion in the region of analysis.

In order to account for differences in the region of analysis between the segmentation and the deconvolution method, the joint space mask was meshed as well. The final region of analysis was defined as the set of vertices with rays that intersected both the adjacent bone mesh and the joint space mesh (Figure 3C).

Deconvolution of grayscale data

Methods described by Turmezei et al. to estimate JSW from high-resolution image data were used.7,22,23 From each vertex included in the region of analysis, the grayscale image was sampled along rays (approximately 5 mm long) perpendicular to the surface of the reference bone mesh. Joint space width was estimated as the full-width at half-maximum distance between the 2 peaks with the largest intensity drop between them (Figure 4). The JSW estimates were then mapped back onto the reference bone mesh to visualize the distribution of JSW measurements and verify that the analyzed region corresponded to the region included in the original joint space mask (Figure 3D).

Deconvolution of an intensity profile. (A) Single ray perpendicular to the articulating surface. (B) Intensity profile along the projected ray (red) and the deconvoluted full-width-half-maximum model (blue). The joint space width (JSW) along the projection is measured as the distance between the 2 deconvolutions.

The deconvolution procedure was performed in Python (v.3.8.5) using SimpleITK (v.2.0.2), VTK (v.9.3.1), and PyVista (v.0.44.1) libraries.24–26

Sharp/van der Heijde scoring

From visual inspection of the 3D CT images, each articulation was given an equivalent SvdH score by a single rater. Joints were given a score ranging from 0 to 4, where 4 equals the highest degree of radiographic damage27 (Table 1).

To address the potential influence of strict ordinal cutoffs on samples whose SvdH score was questionable, along with the limited samples per group, all analyses were repeated with an adjusted score (SvdHadj). This adjusted scale had 3 levels: original baseline scores of SvdH 0 were unaltered; SvdH scores 1 and 2 were grouped to an adjusted score of 1; and SvdH scores 3 and 4 were grouped to an adjusted score of 2.

Statistical analysis

Agreement of the paired measurements was investigated. First, goodness of fit (R2) between the segmentation and deconvolution method was calculated for JSW.Mean, JSW.SD, JSW.Max, JSW.Min, JSW.95%, and JSW.5%. Next, for each paired measurement, the individual differences were calculated. From these, the mean bias and SD were calculated. From the SD, also the 95% limits of agreement were calculated.

Generalizing estimating equations (GEE) were used to calculate the relation between the joint space morphology parameters and the ordinal SvdH score, accounting for the correlation between multiple joints within the same specimen. Three categories of GEE models were constructed. First, a GEE model assessed for both measurement techniques individually if joint space morphometry parameters were sensitive to progressive joint space narrowing, by estimating each parameter based on the joint’s SvdH score. Next a GEE model was constructed that assessed which of the 2 techniques was more sensitive to progressive joint space narrowing, by including an interaction term between the measurement technique and the SvdH score. Significance was assumed at a p-value of p < .05.

All statistical analysis were performed in R (v.4.2.1) and GEE models were constructed with the Geepack library.28

Discussion

We found fundamental differences between results from the 2 measurement methods used to quantify the JSW distribution, and their associations with progressive radiographic joint damage. In cadaveric specimens, JSW.Mean, JSW.SD, JSW.Max, and JSW.Min measured with the deconvolution method were significantly related to radiographic damage. In contrast, when using a segmentation method, JSW.Mean and JSW.Max were not significantly related to radiographic damage; however, segmentation-based JSW.Min showed a higher sensitivity to radiographic damage than when measured with the deconvolution method.

These results demonstrate that in general, there is a low agreement between the segmentation and the deconvolution method. Differences can be explained by their respective measurement principles. The segmentation method uses a sphere-fitting approach; the local thickness at a voxel location equals the radius of the largest sphere that fits the segmentation and encompasses the voxel. The deconvolution method only considers distances in a perpendicular direction. For congruent surfaces, these 2 methods lead to identical definitions of local thickness; however, for non-congruent or rough and uneven surfaces, the 2 methods lead to 2 principally different concepts of local thickness. For the segmentation method, the assignment of JSW based on the largest sphere that encloses a voxel could produce larger JSW values than those estimated along the surface normal traversing that voxel. While this is true at some locations, this effect is offset at other locations, where the sphere-fitting algorithm assigns JSW values that are smaller than those estimated using the surface normals. Consequently, agreement between the 2 methods would be better for global JSW metrics that summarize the entire joint, rather than local metrics that reflect specific locations. This is evident in our results where R2 values decreased as the metrics transitioned from global metrics (eg, JSW.Mean), through percentile metrics (JSW.95% and JSW.5%), to more local metrics (eg, JSW.Min and JSW.Max). Additionally, there is a ray length dependency in the deconvolution method. Even though we used the joint space mask to ensure that the same joint space region was being measured with both methods, the joint space region that is identified by the intersection of rays and bone meshes, can still be influenced by the ray length. Different ray lengths may lead to different joint space analysis regions in the same joint, especially in non-congruent joints, where the opposing joint surfaces may have different radii of curvature or pathological features such as osteophytes. Visually, it appeared that most disagreement originated from the edges of the joint space maps: at some edge regions, but not all of them, the segmentation-based JSW increased heavily, whereas this was not the case for the deconvolution-based JSW (Figure 3).

The underlying principles of each method, and their accuracy, are worth considering in the context of the agreement between the deconvolution and segmentation methods. The deconvolution approach is an extension of the cortical bone mapping technique, which allows reliable cortical bone thickness estimation for regions, where the bone is thin relative to the imaging resolution.29,30 Bone thickness estimates are obtained by modeling the blurring behavior of the scanner, fitting the blurred model to the grayscale image intensity profile sampled across the cortical bone, then removing the blur component of the model to obtain a thickness estimate at the sampled location that is minimally affected by image blur. While the FWHM method used in this study does not explicitly estimate a model parameter that accounts for the blurring properties of the CT scanner, this has a negligible impact on JSW estimation as JSW is typically much larger (~10x) than the thin bone segments (eg, subchondral bone plate) for which the original blurred model was designed. Importantly, the PCCT voxel sizes used in this study were smaller than those obtainable using conventional clinical CT and were more comparable to HR-pQCT; thereby reducing the influence of image blur on JSW estimation accuracy.

Results from theses segmentation-based JSW measurements demonstrate strong agreement with previous studies. Using in vivo MCP joints, Manske et al. found significant relations between SvdH scores and both JSW.Min and JSW.SD; also in their results, neither JSW.Mean nor JSW.Max were significantly correlated to SvdH.13 In contrast, to the best of our knowledge, the deconvolution method has not been used to quantify joint space morphology of the hand and wrist before.

This study has some limitations. First, SvdH scoring was performed by a non-expert, and for individual articulations that are typically not considered for SvdH scoring. While the relative comparison of progressive narrowing among different articulations remains valid, it is possible that their actual SvdH score is incorrect. Moreover, we expect that comparing other qualitative joint space narrowing scores than SvdH would lead to comparable results, these were not investigated. Second, it is unclear, if the amount of dilation and erosion to construct the joint space segmentations is appropriate for articulations other than MCP joints. Visually, this seemed to capture the joint space for the joints considered here, the exact definition of the joint space is not straightforward to define from a single CT image; even more so for joints with some degree of subluxation. It seems reasonable that if the SPECTRA consensus method would be extended to other articulations, the amount of dilation and erosion should be tailored to each articulation, and also take into account the subluxation as well as the overall dimensions of the joint. Third, we did not evaluate how both methods are affected by different image reconstruction kernels. The segmentation approach requires sharp images to accurately delineate the periosteal bone surfaces and subsequent joint space mask. Conversely, the deconvolution method assumes that the point spread function responsible for image blur follows a Gaussian distribution29,30; however, the application of sharp reconstruction filters may result in a point spread function that deviates from Gaussian characteristics. While we did not assess the influence of different reconstruction kernels in this study, PCCT will likely have a range of reconstruction kernels and future work should explore their influence on JSW estimation.

Finally, this study is limited by the low sample size of the used dataset, along with the limited number of articulations that had high SvdH scores and the lack of in vivo data. While these results are indicative of differences that can be expected when measuring JSW with the segmentation method as compared to the deconvolution method, they should not be over-interpreted as definitively generalized conclusions regarding the precise relationships between JSW and disease progression across all patient populations. For the comparison between the 2 methods, no formal multiple comparisons correction was applied to the p-value. This carries an increased risk of Type I errors (false positives), meaning that some observed significant differences could be coincidental. For example, the relation between JSW.Max and SvdH score showed opposing effects for both measurement methods, of which only the deconvolution-based measurement was significant. Yet, there was no significant interaction between the methods, and the low p-value from the deconvolution method changed drastically when adjusting the SvdH groups. To address this risk of Type I errors, we reported exact p-values, not just significance thresholds; hence, these exact p-values should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, it is plausible that factors beyond joint quality (SvdH score) could influence the comparison between the 2 measurement methods as well. For instance, interactions involving joint shape or type could explain baseline differences. Similarly, joint asymmetry (ie, the ratio between JSW.Max and JSW.Min), could reflect the degree of subluxation, or could reflect differences between radiographic damage associated with OA and RA,31 and this could also have a moderating effect on the agreement or differences between the two JSW measurement methods. However, to account for these interactions, the current sample size does not provide enough statistical power. Therefore, large in vivo cross-sectional and prospective data of patients with RA and OA, along with healthy controls, is lacking. Such data could fully clarify whether the deconvolution-based or segmentation-based method is better at discerning diseased patients from healthy controls, and which method is more sensitive to progressive radiographic damage.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that the 2 methods of measuring JSW on high-resolution clinical CT lead to fundamentally different results, and that morphology parameters measured with the deconvolution method might be more sensitive to changes in bone quality than those measured with the segmentation method. As more high-resolution CT modalities like PCCT become increasingly available, this offers new perspectives in early detection and improved monitoring of joint degenerative diseases, enabling more conservative and patient-centered treatments. Future work should perform more extensive and in vivo comparisons in large patient cohorts to fully identify the sensitivity to progressive damage, and all moderating effects.
