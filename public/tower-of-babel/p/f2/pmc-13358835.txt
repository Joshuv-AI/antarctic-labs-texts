Introduction

The global use of vaping devices, also known as electronic cigarettes (‘e-cigarettes’) or electronic nicotine delivery systems (ENDS), has increased substantially in recent years, particularly amongst younger adults and children (1). In the United Kingdom (UK), an estimated 800,000 people (1.7% of the population) were actively vaping in 2012, rising to 5.6 million people (10.7% of the population) in 2024 (2). Although initially promoted as a viable aid to smoking cessation and a safer alternative to conventional cigarettes, there is growing recognition that vaping devices may be associated with a range of pulmonary and extra-pulmonary complications (3,4). Notably, in 2019 the term e-cigarette or vaping product use-associated lung injury (EVALI) was coined to describe an outbreak of acute respiratory illness in the United States (US) linked to vitamin E acetate (VEA) found in e-cigarettes (5), marking an important time-point in our understanding of the potential harm caused by these devices. While stricter regulations in the UK may have limited the occurrence of EVALI specifically (6), a number of case reports and case series have subsequently been reported in the literature highlighting a spectrum of acute pathology linked with vaping (7-9).

Despite these emerging safety concerns, public opinion regarding the use of vaping devices remains divided, though perceptions of harm have gradually increased in both the UK and US (10,11). Many proponents argue that the potential for harm reduction afforded by e-cigarettes [considered 95% safer than conventional cigarettes, based on their relative chemical composition (12)] is central to tackling the impact of smoking-related disease in society. Importantly, a recent meta-analysis emphasised the potential utility of nicotine-containing e-cigarettes in promoting smoking cessation compared to other forms of nicotine replacement therapy, as well as nicotine-free devices (13). Nonetheless, a number of professional bodies have cautioned against the widespread adoption of e-cigarettes (14,15), especially in individuals under the age of 18 years, reflecting the relative infancy of these products and the paucity of evidence relating to their long-term health impacts. Understanding the scope of acute and long-term respiratory complications associated with vaping would help to inform more robust decisions about the continued use and promotion of these devices.

In this narrative review, we summarise the current evidence surrounding the epidemiology, pathophysiological mechanisms of lung injury, and major clinical manifestations linked to e-cigarette use. Our aim is to provide a comprehensive overview of vaping-related lung disease that will appeal to a range of healthcare professionals, not only respiratory specialists. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0515/rc).

Methods

We searched PubMed from inception to 30th January 2026. Keywords included ‘vaping’ OR ‘e-cigarette’ AND ‘lung injury’, with additional free-text terms including ‘EVALI’, ‘pneumothorax’, ‘pneumonia’, ‘lung function’, ‘airways’, ‘pathophysiology’, ‘mechanism’, ‘health impacts’, ‘epidemiology’. We identified additional sources by reviewing the list of references of relevant articles, and through public policy resources and website searches (e.g., Office for National Statistics). As a narrative review, the search strategy was not designed to assess the quality or risk of bias of included studies, and did not adopt the same screening processes as a systematic review. Search terms were chosen to identify a broad range of literature concerning the health impacts of vaping. Articles were selected based on their perceived relevance, and excluded if they were not written in English or full texts were not available. Epidemiology of vaping

The origins of vaping stem from the 1930s with the patenting of an ‘electronic vaporiser’ intended for medicinal use (16). However, the first modern-day vaping device was developed in China in 2003 and subsequently marketed in the US in 2007 (16,17). Since then, the manufacture and availability of vaping products have proliferated, driven largely by significant investment from the tobacco industry (18). There is a degree of irony in the prominent role these organisations have played in the development and promotion of vaping devices, positioning them as a solution to the widespread consumption of tobacco products to which they have historically contributed. Nonetheless, given the substantial health and societal implications of continued cigarette smoking, it is relevant to understand the current usage of these devices as potential smoking cessation tools.

Recent estimates suggest over 100 million people vape globally, with the highest prevalence in Europe and North America (19). In the UK, an estimated 5.5 million people currently vape (daily, or occasionally), comprising 3 million former cigarette smokers and 2.2 million current cigarette smokers (often referred to as ‘dual users’) (2). Notably, the number of people vaping has now surpassed the number of tobacco smokers in the UK (estimated at 4.9 million people in 2025) for the first time (20). This downtrend in conventional cigarette smoking is encouraging and may reflect, at least in part, the utility of vaping devices to support successful attempts at smoking cessation (13). Worryingly, however, over the past decade there has been a gradual rise in the proportion of people using vaping devices who have never previously smoked (2,20). This is a particular concern amongst children and young adults: data from Action on Smoking and Health (ASH)—a UK-based public health charity that aims to reduce the harmful effects of tobacco—suggest that around 400,000 11- to 17-year-olds in the UK routinely vape, with more than 2,000 (1.6%) having never smoked conventional cigarettes (2). In a previous survey conducted in 11- to 15-year-olds (21), a significant proportion of respondents were either current users (9%) or had tried vaping in the past (25%), despite the sale of vaping products to children less than 18 years old being illegal in the UK (22).

These patterns of youth vaping seen in the UK are mirrored globally, with similar incidence reported in the US, Europe and Western Pacific regions (19,23-25), although recent regulations in Australia may substantially alter uptake (26,27). Importantly, a large meta-analysis concluded that adolescents based in Europe and North America were approximately four times more likely to initiate cigarette smoking if they had previously vaped (28). Such findings have fuelled concerns that the marketing of vaping devices may serve as a ‘gateway’ to conventional cigarette smoking, especially among young people (29,30), although this interpretation has been contested in other studies (31,32). In the UK, the government has banned all media advertising of vaping devices, including product placement (33). However, as of 2024, 74 countries had no regulations concerning e-cigarettes, and 88 countries had imposed no minimum age restriction on their purchase (34). Combined with the broad heterogeneity of vaping devices available on the market, this variation in global regulatory practice presents challenges in characterising their potential health impacts.

Overview of vaping devices

A myriad of vaping products now exist, ranging from simple ‘pre-filled’ disposable devices to more complex refillable ‘pods’ or ‘mods’ that permit a greater degree of customisation (35). As these devices have evolved, so too has the terminology used to describe the various products in use (Table 2). Disposable devices were previously dominant in the UK market, particularly among 18–24 year-olds (2). However, the UK government has recently prohibited the sale of single-use products (36), largely in an effort to curb their increasing use by children and young adults.

Despite inherent differences in design, all vaping devices share a common purpose, which is to enable heating of a liquid to produce an aerosol that can be inhaled (or ‘vaped’) (37). This is achieved through four core components: a battery, a heating element, a liquid reservoir, and a mouthpiece (Figure 1). There is considerable variation in the composition of devices, with some components (e.g., heating coils) being linked to the formation of harmful toxins (38,39). However, the main difference between devices lies in the respective chemical composition of the liquid component, referred to as ‘e-liquid’. There are a multitude of e-liquid constituents available, though these typically comprise a solvent carrier (usually propylene glycol and glycerol, also known as vegetable glycerine), alongside other additive compounds [such as nicotine, tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), cannabidiol, and various flavourings] (38).

When negative pressure is applied to the mouthpiece during inhalation, the flow sensor is activated. This induces the battery to provide an electric current to the heating element, or atomizer (usually a metal coil), which heats the liquid within the vapourising chamber. The resultant aerosol, or vapour, is then inhaled by the user (37).

Once heated inside a vaping device, these e-liquid chemicals can all be inhaled as an aerosol. Importantly, while propylene glycol and glycerol are both considered safe for gastrointestinal ingestion by the US Food and Drug Administration, this is not true for inhalation (40). The thermal degradation of these and other e-liquid constituents has been linked to the aerosolization of various toxins, including formaldehyde, tobacco-specific nitrosamines, heavy metals, volatile organic compounds, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, some of which are recognised carcinogens (38,41). The long-term impact of inhaling these compounds remains poorly understood.

Although many countries have now introduced strict regulations regarding the sale of e-cigarettes (27,42), it is important to recognise that vaping devices can be readily modified—either by the user once purchased, or by the seller prior to purchase. This permits the inhalation of different substances and/or higher concentrations of constituent chemicals than initially intended by the manufacturer (43,44). In the UK, around a third of vaping devices are thought to be non-compliant with current legislation (45), with over a million illegal products seized by the National Trading Standards in 2024 (46). Through a process known as ‘dripping’, it is possible to achieve higher concentrations of compounds such as nicotine or THC by directly heating the e-liquid on the electric coil of the device, resulting in higher temperatures compared to conventional use (47). This technique has been associated with increased exposure to carcinogenic vapours such as formaldehyde and acetaldehyde (48).

Mechanisms of lung injury

Given the wide variety of devices and e-liquid compositions that exist, determining the pathophysiological basis for vaping-related lung disease presents challenges. Fundamentally, vaping represents a type of chemical inhalation injury occurring as a byproduct of exposure to specific device components, including nicotine, solvents, flavourings, bioactive thermal degradation products, or illicit and unregulated additives (16). Importantly, an individual’s pattern of e-cigarette usage (known as ‘puff topography’) can vary considerably, both internally (e.g., relating to the number and depth of inhalations performed) and between users, which deviates from conventional smoking patterns (49). The depth and duration of inhalation not only affect the heating coil temperature and site of aerosol deposition within the lungs (50) but may also underpin the development of pressure-driven air leaks (see “Clinical manifestations” section, below) (9). Moreover, coil temperature can vary tenfold between devices, altering the intrinsic chemical composition of e-liquids (including their potential toxicities) through pyrolysis (the process of thermochemical decomposition by applying heat) (51).

Despite these inconsistencies in device make-up and individual use, an impression of the principal harms caused by vaping is gradually emerging through work in human cell lines, murine experimentation and human studies.

Inflammatory and cytotoxic effects

Exposure to e-cigarette vapour can induce direct cytotoxic effects within the airways, spanning broncho-alveolar epithelial cells, interstitial cells, and innate immune cells (52-54).

In vitro cultures of primary human bronchial epithelial cells reveal a pro-inflammatory response to aerosolised nicotine-containing e-liquids, with a dose-dependent cytotoxicity that is enhanced by added flavourings (55). These effects appear to be mediated by the pro-inflammatory cytokines interleukin (IL)-6, IL-8 and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)α, inducing oxidative stress through both the aerosol itself and enhanced production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (55,56). The degree of cytotoxicity is likely to vary between the more than 15,000 recognised flavourings that exist (57), though studies indicate menthol, cinnamon, and crème brûlée (which contains high concentrations of the respiratory irritant benzoic acid) are particularly harmful (58-60). There is a paucity of real-world data concerning the cytotoxic effects of combining flavoured e-liquids, or their dual use with cigarette smoking. However, recent prospective studies suggest this pattern of use may be more harmful (61).

Nicotine-free e-liquid vapour has broad effects on both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines, with documented upregulation of IL-10 and IL-1β in human broncho-alveolar models (62). However, even aerosolization of the solvents alone can induce IL-6 and IL-8 production (56). The resulting oxidative stress disrupts the lung’s epithelial barrier, promoting alveolar oedema, as demonstrated in populations of rats after controlled exposure to e-cigarette vapour (63). This alveolar-capillary barrier damage is compounded by altered expression and activity of local matrix metalloproteases, suggesting dysregulated extracellular membrane remodelling may also be an important contributor (37,64).

The oxidative stress induced by vaping is inherently related to the flavourings and nicotine concentrations present. Notably, diacetyl (linked to the development of obliterative bronchiolitis, see below) produces higher levels of formaldehyde and acrolein than cinnamon, though both cause significant cytotoxicity to human bronchial epithelial cell cultures (65). In human volunteers, proteomics of bronchial brush biopsies show further alterations with 191 proteins significantly up- or down-regulated compared to non-smokers, with 113 of these unique to vapers (66). The potential carcinogenic effects of vaping, stemming from persistent oxidative stress and DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) damage, have previously been demonstrated in murine models (67), though larger epidemiological studies are necessary to confirm this association in humans.

Dysregulated immune response

Murine studies have provided additional insights into the complex alteration to local innate immunity in response to vaping. Notably, aerosolised e-liquid provokes pro-inflammatory signalling in alveolar macrophages (AMs) and an influx of pro-inflammatory neutrophils, yet a dysfunctional response to viral and bacterial pathogens is observed (68,69). With chronic vaping, AMs become lipid-laden, at least in part due to dysregulated surfactant turnover (68,70). Deficiency of key opsonins, surfactant proteins-A and D, and reduced AM response to interferon delay both innate immune response to influenza inoculation and recovery from sublethal influenza (68). Human airway models of viral and bacterial infection validate many of these biological changes after exposure to nicotine-based and nicotine-free e-liquids (68), though high nicotine concentrations may have additional deleterious effects such as blunting the clearance of rhinovirus and influenza (71,72). The cytotoxic effects of various flavourings on respiratory innate immune cells may also contribute to this impaired host immune response to pathogens (73,74). Such impacts on innate immunity have previously been linked to a worsening of symptoms relating to coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) infection (75). However, a large epidemiological cohort study found no compelling evidence to support an increased risk of hospitalisation, intensive care unit admission, or death from COVID-19 in e-cigarette users compared to non-users (although, interestingly, smoking conventional cigarettes appears to have a protective effect) (76).

A key function of AMs is efferocytosis (the process of clearing dead or apoptotic cells), enabling the resolution of inflammation and restoration of lung homeostasis. Both acute and chronic e-cigarette use is associated with increased lung neutrophil populations in in vivo models and broncho-alveolar lavage samples from vapers (77,78), influenced by e-liquid composition and overall dose exposure (79,80). Defective clearance of neutrophils and apoptotic epithelial cells, through reduced expression of target receptors for AM efferocytosis, may therefore lead to persistent lung inflammation (81,82). Moreover, e-liquid vapour is known to be toxic to pure populations of AMs, resulting in an excess of ROS and pro-inflammatory cytokines, but a fall in phagocytosis rates (83). Together, these studies suggest that vapers exhibit an increased susceptibility to respiratory infection and acute and chronic inflammation driven by a dysregulated immune response.

Impaired mucociliary function

Both nicotine and non-nicotine-containing e-cigarettes cause mucin overproduction and mucus thickening, mediated principally by increased MUC5AC expression and goblet cell hyperplasia within the human bronchial epithelium (55,84). However, this effect appears to be increased in the presence of nicotine and certain flavourings, particularly tobacco and cinnamaldehyde (85,86). MUC5AC and MUC4 overexpression both produce viscous mucus, most prominent in the distribution of aerosol deposition, while cytokine alterations match those seen in primary human cell lines (66). Induced sputum from human subjects suggests that this compositional change to airway mucins is comparable to that seen with conventional cigarette smokers (87).

The effects on mucin production are exacerbated by defective ion transport via cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator and sodium channels, leading to airway dehydration and reduced clearance of excess mucins (88). Several independent studies have reported that aerosolised flavourings can alter transcriptomic profiles and reduce cilia biogenesis within human bronchial epithelial cells, further impairing mucus clearance (89). Notably, e-liquids have been shown to exert more significant effects on primary human cell lines in aerosol rather than liquid form (88,90); this mirrors findings in surfactant models, whereby conventional e-liquid solvents increase minimum surface tension as an aerosol but not in their liquid state (68,91). Such studies indicate that the products of thermal degradation inherent to vaping represent key bioactive effectors, ultimately contributing to pulmonary insult.

Clinical manifestations

Although most of the literature concerning vaping-related lung disease stems from EVALI, it is increasingly recognised that there is a spectrum of respiratory pathology linked to e-cigarette use (Table 3). Here, we discuss the main pulmonary manifestations reported in the literature, as well as providing a summary of the wider extra-pulmonary complications associated with vaping. EVALI

EVALI was first described as a clinical entity in 2019 following an outbreak of acute respiratory illness in the US states of Wisconsin and Illinois, swiftly linked to the inhalation of THC-containing e-cigarettes (5). The initial report described a cluster of 98 cases, the majority (79%) male with a median age of 21 years, though over a quarter were less than 18-years-old. Primary respiratory symptoms (present in 97% of cases) included dyspnoea, cough and chest pain, and a substantial proportion (77%) had co-existent gastrointestinal disturbance (nausea, vomiting and/or diarrhoea) and/or subjective fever (84%). Most patients presented to hospital within 7 days of symptom onset, with 95% requiring hospital admission. Notably, up to half of patients developed acute respiratory failure requiring intensive care level support, with ~25% requiring intubation and mechanical ventilation. All had used vaping devices within the preceding 90 days, with the majority volunteering that they had vaped in the week leading up to admission.

In a subsequent review, Krishnasamy et al. evaluated 2,668 cases of EVALI reported up to January 2020 (92). Two-thirds of cases were male, with a median age of 24 years. The majority of hospitalised individuals (82% of 2,022 patients) had used e-cigarettes containing THC within 3 months of symptom onset, with a third of these containing THC alone (669/2,022 cases). A minority (274/2,022) reported using only nicotine-containing vaping products, while 41% (834/2,022 patients) reported using devices containing both THC and nicotine.

Several other case series have been reported in the literature (7,93,94), highlighting both the potential variability in presentation of EVALI as well as common features (Table 4). The radiographic abnormalities associated with EVALI are broad, but are typically bronchiolocentric with features of diffuse alveolar damage, airway inflammation and organising pneumonia (97,98); sub-pleural and lobular parenchymal sparing are commonly reported (3,99). Importantly, there are now established criteria that can be used to diagnose EVALI (Table 5) (95), although this largely remains a diagnosis of exclusion based on the shared clinical and radiological patterns observed in other respiratory diseases (e.g., infection).

Mass spectrometry of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid from 51 patients with EVALI identified VEA as the most probable toxicant linked to the development of acute lung injury, being present in 48/51 cases but not in a group of healthy controls (101). VEA is used in THC-based e-liquids as a condensing agent, diluting THC without visually altering the consistency of the product (95). Various mechanisms have been proposed to explain the pathological role of VEA in these findings: as a fat-soluble vitamin, VEA is capable of penetrating and ‘softening’ surfactant within the lungs, impeding the capacity to manage high surface pressures (101,102), such as may be present during vaping. This effect is also seen with propylene glycol and glycerol (91), though is less pronounced and immediate. Pyrolysis of VEA also produces carcinogens, especially alkenes and benzene, though it is thought that lung injury most likely stems from the liberation of ketene from the acetate group (51). Even at low concentrations, ketene is a potent respiratory irritant leading to tissue damage and alveolar oedema, contributing to the ‘chemical pneumonitis’ that characterises EVALI.

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid from patients with EVALI is typically neutrophilic and features lipid-laden AMs (‘foam cells’) with Oil Red O positive staining, likely arising from a combination of directly inhaled lipids and dysregulated lipid homeostasis (99). However, these are not specific for EVALI, as lipid-laden macrophages are also elevated in asymptomatic vapers without THC use (78,103). Previous animal models have shown AMs to be proinflammatory with dysregulated efferocytosis after combined VEA exposure and immunological challenge (104). This suggests that the diffuse epithelial damage seen in EVALI may be linked both to AM activation and thereafter the failure of these innate defence cells to resolve inflammation.

The management of EVALI is primarily supportive in the form of oxygen therapy, alongside immediate cessation of vaping; however, given that a high proportion of patients will require mechanical ventilation, even if initially stable, early discussion with intensive care colleagues is advised (99). In severe cases, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation has been employed (105). Most patients appear to respond well to corticosteroids (94,105), though the exact dose and duration is not well defined and there is a lack of randomised controlled trial data upon which to guide decisions (99).

Diffuse parenchymal lung disease

Early case reports of e-cigarette-related illnesses included presentations of bronchiolitis obliterans (8), hypersensitivity pneumonitis (106), respiratory bronchiolitis-associated interstitial lung disease (107), lipoid pneumonia (108), eosinophilic pneumonia (109), organising pneumonia (110), and diffuse alveolar haemorrhage (111). Such diverse patterns of lung injury likely reflect the varied impacts of exposure to specific e-liquid components and the resultant host response to these. However, whether these presentations represent distinct clinical entities or simply broader manifestations of EVALI remains unclear.

The association between diacetyl inhalation and the development of bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome was first described in a group of microwave popcorn factory workers towards the start of the 21st century, with subsequent coining of the term ‘popcorn lung’ (112). Exposed workers were more than twice as likely to experience symptoms of cough, dyspnoea, or wheeze compared to non-exposed workers, with an associated decline in spirometric lung function. Of relevance, the volatile α-dicarbonyl compounds 2,3-butanedione (diacetyl) and 2,3-pentanedione can both be used as additives in e-cigarettes, contributing to the buttery, fruity or alcoholic flavours of various devices (113,114). In one analysis, diacetyl was present above the laboratory limit for detection in the vapour of 39/51 brand flavourings tested (114). Importantly, the UK and European Union have both banned the inclusion of diacetyl and 2,3-pentanedione in e-liquids (115), which may go some way to reducing risk, though many countries including the US have not imposed such legislation.

Vaping-associated pneumothorax

Both tobacco smoking and cannabis use are strongly associated with the development of pneumothorax (116,117). However, the relationship between vaping and pneumothorax is less clear. Several case reports and small case series have recently emerged suggesting a possible association (118-120), in addition to other air-leak syndromes such as pneumomediastinum (121), subcutaneous emphysema (122) and even pneumorrhacis (123). Skertich et al. (124) reported the first cases of spontaneous pneumothorax attributable to vaping in two adolescents (aged 15 and 16 years old). Neither had smoked conventional cigarettes or cannabis, but later admitted to using e-cigarettes (although the type of device was not specified). Each was managed initially with chest drain insertion, with successful resolution of the pneumothorax; however, both subsequently experienced recurrence after continued vaping, necessitating definitive surgical intervention.

In a later small case series, Ashraf et al. (118) described four patients (aged 18 to 35 years old) with ‘vaping-associated spontaneous pneumothorax’ (VASP), all of whom had used THC-containing devices prior to the onset of symptoms. 3/4 patients denied a previous history of conventional cigarette smoking or cannabis use, though one reported smoking cannabis alongside vaping; this patient also had a prior history of spontaneous pneumothorax, and subsequently underwent elective video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) for definitive management. One patient was managed conservatively without complication. The remaining two patients were managed initially with chest drain insertion: one subsequently underwent VATS pleurodesis in the context of persistent air leak after 48 hours, while one developed a recurrent pneumothorax after 1 week, necessitating further chest drainage and talc pleurodesis.

In their recent review of seven case reports and four case series (comprising a total of sixteen patients), Al-Taj et al. (9) highlight that the majority of patients were young males, with predominant symptoms of chest pain and breathlessness akin to primary spontaneous pneumothorax. Notably, several patients had a history of concurrent smoking and/or cannabis use, making it difficult to fully establish a direct causal relationship with vaping. Nonetheless, many were sole e-cigarette users, supporting other published reports concerning the development of pneumothorax in the absence of co-existent conventional smoking (124,125).

The established effects of e-liquid constituents on surfactant production and function offer a tangible link to the development of VASP and other air-leak syndromes (91,114). Around 1 in 6 patients in the 2019 EVALI outbreak suffered pneumothorax or pneumomediastinum (5,7), suggesting disruption of normal lung parenchymal integrity and alveolar damage through exposure to toxic chemicals, particularly THC and VEA, may increase risk of barotrauma (126). Although based on relatively small numbers, patients presenting with VASP appear to demonstrate less parenchymal ‘blebs’ (presumed small structural defects predisposing to air-leak) compared to other spontaneous pneumothoraces (9), indicating other mechanisms may be at play. Notably, intrathoracic pressure changes are more dynamic in vapers, who often favour deeper inhalations, longer breath-holds and longer exhalations compared to the puff topography of conventional cigarette smokers (49,127). Alongside increased airflow resistance that is inherent to vaping devices, this may predispose individuals to barotrauma and alveolar rupture from abnormally high airway pressures (9), an effect which is further compounded by surfactant dysfunction and inflammation caused by inhaled toxins (128). As such, pneumothorax, as well as other air-leak syndromes (depending on the site of alveolar rupture), may occur even in the absence of pre-existing structural damage.

Importantly, patients with VASP appear to be at particular risk of recurrence and may require early intervention rather than a conservative approach to management (9). In a recent retrospective case series of 15 patients (129), 46% were treated with initial chest tube drainage, yet 54% (8/15 patients) subsequently required surgical intervention. None of these patients had previously smoked conventional cigarettes or cannabis, and none had any evidence of structural pathology on CT imaging. Larger-scale clinical studies are warranted to establish the true incidence of VASP and its optimal management.

Chronic respiratory symptoms and airways disease

Epidemiological studies indicate that individuals who vape report increased respiratory symptoms, even in the absence of underlying lung disease. In a large cross-sectional survey of over 45,000 adolescents in Hong Kong, use of e-cigarettes within the preceding month was associated with more frequent cough and sputum production, regardless of pre-existing smoking history (130). Similarly, a large survey of smoking behaviour among 16–19-year-olds living in Canada, England and the US, found that individuals who had vaped in the past 30 days were more likely to experience at least one symptom of breathlessness, cough, phlegm, wheeze or chest pain compared to those who had never vaped (131). These data support an exposure-related effect of e-cigarettes in the development of respiratory symptoms, which may extend to frank respiratory infection in conjunction with impaired mucociliary clearance and immune dysfunction (61,132,133). Associations between second-hand vape exposure and respiratory symptoms have also been reported (134), although these remain poorly characterised and the effects are likely to be less pronounced compared to second-hand tobacco smoke exposure (135).

Various studies have reported a worsening of asthma symptoms in response to e-cigarette use. In a recent meta-analysis involving over 480,000 high school teenagers, both prior and current vaping use were associated with a higher incidence of asthma, independent of conventional cigarette smoking behaviour (136). Similar findings have been reported in the adult population, where the association between vaping and asthma symptoms appears to be augmented in dual-cigarette users (137). These population-based studies build on pre-clinical models demonstrating that exposure to vapourised e-liquid causes airway hyper-responsiveness and increased bronchoconstriction in response to methacholine (138-140), possibly through stimulation of vagal bronchopulmonary C-fibres (141). Importantly, nicotine-containing e-cigarettes can further exacerbate airflow obstruction through increased airway hyper-reactivity and mucin production (138). Together, these data suggest that vaping is an important environmental trigger for airway hyper-responsiveness and asthma exacerbation, akin to conventional cigarette smoking (139). Further research is necessary to establish the impact of specific flavourings and other e-liquid constituents on mediating these effects (37).

The association between vaping and the development of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) remains uncertain, reflecting both the relative infancy of these devices and the lengthy timeframe over which COPD evolves. To date, no human studies have demonstrated that vaping alone is linked to the development of COPD or emphysema. Nonetheless, it is plausible that prolonged exposure to e-cigarettes will have long-term detrimental effects owing to persisting airway inflammation and structural remodelling (142), particularly given the young age at which many begin vaping. Previous animal models have revealed histological features consistent with emphysema following several weeks’ exposure to e-liquid vapour, especially nicotine-containing aerosol (138,143). By contrast, one study found no evidence of emphysema after 4-month exposure, likely reflecting differences in aerosol dose and/or composition (68). Such discrepancies underscore the need for robustly designed longitudinal human studies to determine whether a true association exists. Emerging epidemiological data suggest that the prevalence of chronic bronchitis and COPD is higher in both current and former vapers compared with at-risk individuals who do not vape (144,145), though this risk appears less compared to conventional cigarette use. Increased rates of COPD exacerbations have also been reported in association with e-cigarettes (146), but causation remains challenging owing to the observational nature of these studies and the frequent history of co-existent tobacco smoking.

It is recognised that people with COPD who vape may have accelerated declines in lung function, even when controlling for conventional smoking history (146). However, the long-term impact in individuals without established respiratory disease remains uncertain. In one study, significantly lower lung function parameters were observed in a group of 30 daily e-cigarette users compared to 30 non-users (none of whom had smoked conventional cigarettes), suggestive of a pattern of peripheral airways obstruction (147); yet, the small sample size and lack of standardisation of vaping devices limits the generalisability of these results. A more recent systematic review (148), comprising 273 participants across eight studies, found insufficient evidence that vaping causes acute changes in spirometric lung function, though may cause alteration to airflow resistance and conductance. In the one longitudinal study included in this review, no differences in lung function were observed among vapers and non-vapers over a 3.5-year timeframe (149). Importantly, substantial heterogeneity exists between reported studies, including (but not limited to) individual vaping history and type of vaping device used, such that it is difficult to draw any firm conclusions about the possible impacts of vaping on lung function. This may be exacerbated by the limited sensitivity of spirometry to detect early small airways dysfunction (150). The use of novel physiological techniques, such as impulse oscillometry and functional magnetic resonance imaging, may facilitate improved detection of subtle airway and/or parenchymal disease associated with vaping (151-153), though at present these modalities remain largely in development.

Lung cancer

While the association between conventional cigarette smoking and lung cancer is well established (154), this is less certain with regard to vaping exposure. Emerging epidemiological data suggest that the combination of conventional cigarette smoking and vaping (i.e., ‘dual use’) may lead to an increased risk of developing lung cancer, particularly adenocarcinoma, compared to smoking alone (155-157). However, there is currently a lack of longitudinal evidence to support a direct causal link between vaping exposure and risk of lung cancer development in humans, reflecting the relative infancy of these devices. In one pre-clinical study (158), a higher incidence of lung adenocarcinoma was reported in mice exposed to electronic cigarette smoke (ECS) over 54 weeks, compared to mice exposed to either filtered ambient air or an isopropylene glycol/glycerol mixture. Specifically, lung adenocarcinoma was observed in 9/40 ECS-exposed mice, but only 1/18 ambient air-exposed mice and 0/18 solvent-exposed mice, respectively. The authors propose that deep penetration of ECS into the lungs may induce harmful metabolites and impaired DNA repair, similar to the damaging effects of vape-exposure reported in previous murine studies (67). Given the increased concentrations of carcinogenic chemicals—such as formaldehyde, acetaldehyde, acrolein and benzene—found in the exhaled breath of e-cigarette users (159,160), a mechanistic link is plausible, though further research is necessary to establish whether a true association between vaping and lung cancer exists.

Extrapulmonary complications

Although not the primary focus for this review, it is worth noting that vaping has been linked to a number of health impacts beyond the respiratory system (Figure 3). These range from acute constitutional symptoms that accompany presentations of EVALI (96), to various cardiovascular, immune, and neurological sequelae (161).

The acute cardiovascular effects of increased heart rate and blood pressure seen with e-cigarettes are typically milder than conventional cigarette smoking but can intensify with increasing nicotine concentration (166,167). In conjunction with oxidative stress associated with increased sympathetic tone and aortic stiffness, individuals may be at greater risk of developing chronic cardiovascular disease, such as atherosclerosis and ischaemic heart disease (168-170). However, there is currently a lack of robust trial data to support these associations definitively.

Various adverse impacts of vaping on periodontal health have been reported in the literature, including nicotine stomatitis, black hairy tongue, periodontitis, gingivitis, oral mucosal lesions, uvulitis, tonsillitis and laryngitis (162). It has been postulated that these effects may relate to changes in the oral microbiome, alongside an increased inflammatory cell response in saliva (171,172). However, the exact mechanisms are not fully established. Notably, the use of e-cigarettes has been linked to the development of oral cancers in a similar manner as conventional cigarettes, though more detailed epidemiological data is necessary to confirm whether a true association exists (173).

Chronic nicotine use has adverse effects on cognition and the increase in neuronal activity via nicotinic acetylcholine receptors is more pronounced in adolescents and young adults (163). Although difficult to attribute causation, the use of e-cigarettes in young people has been associated with increased rates of eating disorder, depression and suicidal thoughts (164,174). This is particularly relevant for societies such as the UK, where vaping device use is high in this age group.

The perception of vaping as a safe alternative to smoking has been implicated as a reason for increased use of e-cigarettes in pregnancy, despite evidence that nicotine has a multitude of long-term adverse effects on the foetus (175). In one meta-analysis concerning the impact of animal and human exposure to nicotine and cannabis (176), deficits in cognitive, emotional and neurodevelopmental domains of offspring were consistently reported. However, only one human study involving e-cigarettes was included in this analysis, such that further research on the impact and safety of vaping in pregnancy is needed.

Future directions

While much progress has been made, the long-term health effects of vaping are currently largely unknown. Establishing if there is a relationship between e-cigarette use and the subsequent development of chronic respiratory diseases, especially amongst young people, represents a major area for future research. A 10-year Adolescent Health Study based in the UK has recently commenced (177), enabling a longitudinal evaluation of the health impacts of vaping in 100,000 adolescents aged 8–18 years old. Additionally, the EVALUATE study (also led by researchers in the UK) is currently in process, aiming to provide a detailed assessment of the underlying inflammatory and immune effects of vaping on the lungs over the course of 1 year (178). Studies such as these are vital in improving our understanding of the pathophysiological impacts and subsequent health consequences of vaping, which will ultimately help to inform policies regarding the continued use and promotion of these devices. In tandem with this, interventions that focus on the promotion of vaping-cessation in children and young people represents a key area for future research (179,180). The application of new and emerging technologies to assess and monitor the effects of vaping on lung function also holds potential to reveal early vaping-related abnormalities that may be hidden to existing clinical measures (150-153). This extends to evaluating second-hand exposure to vaping, the effects of which are not yet understood. Finally, addressing the environmental impact of vaping remains a key priority, particularly relating to disposable devices and the hazardous effects of battery and electronic components (181).

Conclusions

Vaping remains a relatively new phenomenon, where the long-term impacts on lung health are not fully established. Although e-cigarettes may be beneficial as smoking cessation aids for some individuals, there are very real concerns regarding the widespread adoption of these devices, particularly among children and young adults who have never previously smoked. As our understanding of the pathophysiological effects of e-cigarettes has evolved, so too has our recognition of the spectrum of clinical diseases linked to vaping, extending beyond EVALI alone. Establishing the relationship between vaping and the development of chronic respiratory diseases such as COPD remains challenging, particularly given the marked heterogeneity of vaping devices, their constituent chemicals, and individual patterns of use. Nonetheless, current evidence indicates that vaping is far from ‘safe’, with the potential to cause significant long-term harm. This has important implications at both the individual and population level. As such, it is essential that healthcare professionals remain up to date with the literature base surrounding vaping, enabling meaningful and informed discussions with patients and the wider public about the risks and uncertainties regarding ongoing use.
