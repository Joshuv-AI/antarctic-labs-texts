Netherlands Heart Registration-based multicentre retrospective cohort study on primary PCI for ST-elevation myocardial infarction: comparing patient relevant outcomes in on- vs. off-hour presentations
Authors: Lineke Derks, Marijke J C Timmermans, Daniel M F Claassens, Dennis van Veghel, Krischan D Sjauw, Peter Danse, Karin Arkenbout, Dirk J van der Heijden, Salvatore De Rosa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsOver recent decades, numerous measures have been implemented to improve treatment and timely intervention for ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). For deeper insights into the current state of care, this study investigates whether patient outcomes differ based on the timing of presentation (on-hours vs. off-hours) for primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for STEMI.Methods and resultsData from STEMI PCIs performed from 2017 to October 2020, as registered within the Netherlands Heart Registration (NHR), were analysed. Off-hours presentation was defined as arrival at the catheterization laboratory (cath lab) on weekends, during working days between 17.00 and 08.00, or Monday between midnight and 08.00. Short-term outcomes included 30-day all-cause mortality and acute MI within 30 days. Long-term outcomes included all-cause mortality rates up till 5 years after PCI, target vessel revascularization within 1 year, and repeat revascularization with elective or non-STEMI PCI. The study included 19 090 STEMI patients from 17 centres, with 11 719 (61.4%) PCIs performed on-hours. No significant difference in 30-day mortality was observed between on-hours and off-hours patients (5.7% vs. 5.8%). On-hours patients had a longer time from symptom onset to cath lab arrival (≤6 h: 80.2% vs. 84.4%, P < 0.001) and were less likely to present with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (7.6% vs. 9.5%, P < 0.001). No statistically significant differences in long-term outcomes were observed after adjusting for confounders.ConclusionOutcomes after primary PCI for STEMI are comparable between on-hours and off-hours presentations. The quality of care appears to be independent of time of arrival at the cath lab.Keywords: After-hours care, Myocardial infarction, Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), STEMI, Treatment outcome, Quality of care

Introduction

A percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is the primary treatment for patients presenting with ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI).1 However, its effectiveness can be significantly influenced by numerous factors, such as timeliness of presentation, infarct location, and the presence of multivessel disease. The timeliness of the intervention depends on both the promptness of patient presentation and the responsiveness of the healthcare system.2 In case of multivessel disease, complete revascularization is recommended within 45 days of the index PCI in the latest ESC guidelines.3 However, prior STEMI guidelines provided different recommendations for immediate and/or staged revascularization.4,5 Therefore, some centres choose for complete revascularization during index procedure of hospitalization, while others defer treatment of the non-infarct–related artery to a later moment. Both of aforementioned factors have been associated with elevated short-term mortality rates.6,7

Recognizing the critical nature of time in the management of STEMI patients, current guidelines emphasize the importance of minimizing the first medical contact-to-balloon time, setting a maximum expected delay of 120 min.5,8 This urgency is particularly crucial for patients presenting with cardiogenic shock or an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA).4

In response to the sudden onset of STEMI, the establishment of an organized network has become imperative. In the Netherlands, there are 29 PCI centres, including 14 centres without on-site cardiac surgery. All operate on a 24 h/7-day basis to ensure timely access to revascularization through PCI for all Dutch patients.9–11 Also, to increase the early detection of STEMI and to facilitate direct transferal to the catheterization laboratory (cath lab) by the emergency medical services, all ambulances have telemetric electrocardiography (ECG) available to perform local registration.12,13 After confirmation of the STEMI diagnosis, the PCI centre is informed, and the cath lab personnel are required to be present within 30 min, irrespective of timing of the day,9 resulting in a highly responsive system ensuring timely reperfusion throughout the day.

However, there have been previous studies investigating the timing of STEMI presentation and outcomes,14,15 in which some reveal higher (periprocedural) mortality for patients treated during off-hours.16,17 Nevertheless, most were either single-centre studies focused on short-term outcomes or of much older date, in which primary PCI was not always performed in practice. This might have contributed to the conflicting results and differences in outcomes, for instance, due to longer door-to-balloon times or local preferences for treatment.

To gain more insight into the care provided in this contemporary era of organized STEMI networks and primary PCI, we conducted a registry-based multicentre study in the Netherlands. The aim of the study was to assess the effect of off-hours presentation on both short- and long-term outcomes, including mortality and repeat revascularization rates, after PCI for STEMI patients.

Methods

Study design

This is an observational, retrospective multicentre cohort study of data registered within the Netherlands Heart Registration (NHR). The study complies with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The use of the data was approved by the institutional review board MEC-U (W19.270), which issued a waiver for informed consent for analysis of the NHR data.

Data collection

The Netherlands Heart Registration

The NHR is a nationwide, physician-driven, and patient-focused quality registry that contains data of all heart-related procedures from all Dutch hospitals. The aim of the NHR is to contribute to the maintenance and improvement of the quality and transparency of care for cardiac patients.18

Since 2015, a standardized set of patient characteristics, procedural data, and outcome data has been collected for all PCI procedures by PCI centres and submitted to the NHR. The set of mandatory variables is over 95% complete.19,20 Detailed information on the process of data acquisition, completeness, data quality, and analysis of the NHR has been published previously.19,21

On- and off-hours presentation

Facultative supplemental data was obtained from the registry to elucidate time of arrival for off-hours presentation. From the 29 PCI centres, information about the time of arrival at the cath lab was available for 17 centres, and information about the start of symptoms was available for 12 centres. Off-hours presentation was defined as patient arrival at the cath lab on Saturdays or Sundays, or during working days between 17.00 and 08.00 o’clock, or on Monday between midnight and 08.00 o’clock. All other PCIs were classified as on-hours.

The data underlying this article were provided by the NHR by the permission of the participating hospitals. Data are available upon reasonable request to the corresponding author and with permission of the NHR.

Study population

For the present study, we included all adult patients (≥18 years) who underwent a PCI for STEMI from 2017 to October 2020, with known time of arrival at the cath lab. If patients were admitted multiple times for PCI during the study period, only data from the initial procedure were selected for inclusion in the cohort.

Study variables

The baseline characteristics include information about medical history such as diabetes, renal insufficiency, multivessel disease, history of MI, cardiogenic shock, OHCA, previous PCI, and previous coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), but also procedural information such as access site and treated vessels.

Patient relevant outcomes comprised both short-term and long-term outcomes. Short-term outcomes were all-cause mortality and acute MI within 30 days of the procedure. Long-term outcome measures were all-cause mortality rates up to 5 years after the procedure, target vessel revascularization (TVR), and repeat revascularization during follow-up up to 1 year after the procedure. Repeat revascularization was defined as a repeat PCI with the indication elective or non-STEMI within 90 days and 1 year after the initial PCI. For each initial procedure included in the analysis, all repeat PCIs were matched using a unique individual patient identifier (NHRpersonID). Time between repeat and initial PCI was calculated in days. Mortality data were obtained by checking the regional municipal administration registration [Personal Records Database (in Dutch: Basisregistratie Personen) (BRP)]. Follow-up data were completed through 31 December 2021.

Statistical analysis

Data on baseline characteristics and outcomes between on- and off-hours were compared using descriptive statistics. Continuous data is presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared by performing a Student’s t-test. Categorical data is presented as number and percentages and compared by χ2 test.

The relation between time of presentation at the cath lab and outcomes was evaluated using binary logistic regression analysis. Before multivariable analysis, the data was imputed under the assumption of missing at random, creating 20 imputed datasets using a 20-iteration method. Besides potential confounding variables such as gender, age, diabetes, renal insufficiency, multivessel disease, presentation with cardiogenic shock, OHCA, previous CABG, previous PCI, history of MI, and number of treated vessels, other variables were also included in the imputation model but were not imputed: outcomes (30-day mortality, MI within 30 days, TVR within 1 year), PCI centre (anonymized), multivessel PCI, and arrival time at the cath lab.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis with backward selection using the likelihood ratio test was performed to correct for aforementioned confounding factors. Odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding confidence intervals (CIs) and P-values are presented. Long-term survival was evaluated by including the same confounding variables as identified in the multivariable logistic regression analysis in the Cox regression model. Hazard rates with corresponding CIs and P-values are presented. Year of intervention and an interaction term between year of intervention and arrival time at the cath lab were added to the multivariable model to investigate potential changes over time in the relation between time of arrival at the cath lab and outcomes. Sensitivity analyses were performed comparing STEMI patients undergoing primary PCI during daytime vs. nighttime presentation, with an alternative definition of on-and off-hours, i.e. PCIs performed on Monday through Sunday with time of arrival at the cath lab between 08:00–17:00 and 17.00–08.00, respectively (see Supplementary material S1). Additionally, the potential variation in the relation between on-hours presentation in the morning (08.00–12.00) or afternoon (12.00–17.00) and outcomes was assessed using binary logistic regression. Furthermore, crude outcomes were compared for patients suffering from STEMI with cardiogenic shock using χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Two-tailed P-values of <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk NY, USA).

Results

From 2017 to October 2020, a total of 39 652 STEMI patients underwent primary PCI. Time of arrival at the cath lab was known in 19 090 (48.1%) of the procedures. Short-term outcomes

Crude 30-day mortality rates were comparable between on-hours (5.7%) and off-hours (5.8%) patients (Table 2). After adjusting for potential confounders (i.e. age, gender, diabetes, renal insufficiency, previous CABG, presentation with OHCA, and cardiogenic shock), no statistically significant differences in 30-day mortality rates were observed between the two groups (OR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.89–1.19; P = 0.727). Similarly, there were no significant differences in the occurrence of MI within 30 days (OR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.64–1.28; P = 0.572).

Long-term outcomes

Analysis of long-term outcomes, including 1-year mortality and survival rates beyond, revealed no statistically significant differences between patients who presented on- or off-hours (Table 2). Adjusted 1-year mortality rates remained similar between both groups (OR, 1.04; 95% CI, 0.92–1.17; P = 0.545). Furthermore, the Kaplan–Meier curve demonstrated comparable long-term survival trends (Figure 2). After correcting for potential confounders, the hazard ratio was 1.03 (95% CI 0.94–1.12) with a P-value of 0.572 and a median follow-up time of 34.8 months (95% CI: 34.5–35.1 months). In addition, the crude repeat revascularization rates with PCI within 90 days and 1 year, with the indication ‘elective’ or ‘non-STEMI’ procedures, were comparable between patients with on-hours presentation (15.7% and 16.4%, respectively) and those presented off-hours (18.1% and 18.9%, respectively), with P-values of 0.183 and 0.167, respectively.

Temporal analysis

The conducted sensitivity analyses for daytime vs. nighttime yielded comparable results (see Supplementary material online, Tables S1 and S2 and Figure S1). Patients treated in the cath lab during nighttime (between 17.00 and 08.00 o’clock) had similar outcomes to those treated outside this time frame, regardless of the day of the week (alternative on-hours presentation). Furthermore, the analysis investigating potential changes over time in the relation between the timing of the procedure and outcomes did not reveal any significant evidence that the observed association varied over the years. Specifically, the interaction term between the year of intervention and arrival time at the cath lab was not statistically significant for any of the outcomes examined.

Most STEMI patients with a delay between symptom onset and presentation at the cath lab of more than 6 h underwent a procedure in the morning and on-hours (Figure 1A), with no indication of a circadian rhythm (Figure 1B). Outcomes after PCIs performed on-hours in the morning (08.00–12.00) were compared with those conducted in the afternoon (12.00–17.00). For all outcome measures, crude rates did not demonstrate statistically significant differences, including 30-day mortality (P = 0.641), 1-year mortality (P = 0.661), acute MI within 30 days (P = 0.550), TVR within 1 year (P = 0.323), 90-day repeat revascularization (P = 0.702), and 1-year repeat revascularization (P = 0.263). In total, 1425 STEMI patients with cardiogenic shock were included cohort, of whom 526 treated during on-hours and 899 during off-hours. Both crude short- and long-term outcomes did not statistically significantly differ between the groups (data not shown).

Discussion

In our registry-based study, we observed no differences in crude or risk-adjusted outcomes depending on the timing of presentation for PCI in STEMI patients. This included similar 30-day, 1-year, and long-term mortality rates. Moreover, comparable rates of acute MI and (un)planned repeat revascularization after the initial PCI were observed.

In contrast to our findings, some studies have reported differences in outcomes depending on timing of presentation for PCI,16,17 with increased mortality among patients presenting off-hours, which is potentially explained by longer door-to-balloon times,7,16,22 or increased time to reperfusion,6,23 which is reasonable given the era in which these studies were performed. However, in our multicentre registry study, we observed no difference between outcomes during on- and off-hours presentation, which supports the theory as proposed in a meta-analysis performed by Dharma et al.24 Based on their analysis, they suggest that the outcomes after PCI are independent of timing of presentation for PCI if the healthcare system is organized in a way that it ensures timely PCI access.

Fortunately, in the past decades, as mentioned in the introduction, many additional measures to improve STEMI healthcare worldwide have been taken. In the Netherlands, all patients can reach a PCI centre within 120 min,25 and the Dutch Healthcare Inspectorate requires a minimal number of 600 PCIs performed per year by each centre to ensure high treatment quality.26 Additionally, procedural and outcome data are collected by the centres within the NHR and are voluntarily made publicly available for quality and safety monitoring. All these measures together result in a tight network guaranteeing high-quality and optimal treatment throughout the day. This includes treatment for patients suffering from STEMI with cardiogenic shock.

For patients presenting with STEMI, it is known that there is a significant circadian pattern in symptom onset.27 We observed that patients with longer delay between symptoms and presentation often reported a symptom onset early in the morning (24.00–04.00 o’clock). This association has also been shown by Holmes et al.,28 and when accounting for the delay and other risk factors, they observed no increased risk for mortality depending on the specific time of onset of symptoms, which is in line with our study. However, more research is necessary to identify the reasons why presentation at the cath lab was delayed in our study, since it might be caused by alternative decision-making after first medical contact by medical personnel, operator decision-making, or patients/caregivers are inadequate in recognizing symptoms or more hesitant in seeking medical help in the early morning.

In patients with multivessel disease, the optimal treatment strategy, immediate or staged complete revascularization, remains debatable,4,5 and there is variation between PCI centres in which strategy is most frequently used in the Netherlands.19 However, rates of multivessel PCI and repeat revascularization with either elective or non-STEMI PCI in the period after the initial STEMI presentation were comparable, indicating no significant differences in the chosen treatment strategy depending on the time of a patient’s presentation.

For our analysis, reliable real-world data from multiple hospitals was obtained from the PCI registry of the NHR. However, there are several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, the registry includes a limited set of variables, in which critical time-points in the treatment of STEMI patients, such as time of first medical contact or time of arrival at the PCI centre, were not readily available. This gap in time data restricted our ability to fully account for potential treatment delays, which could significantly influence patient outcomes. A more granular approach, incorporating detailed time-delay data, would provide a clearer understanding of the nuances of patient care and response efficiency. Secondly, for only half of the patients in the registry, the necessary data were available. Even though the baseline characteristics and outcome rates of our cohort are comparable with nationwide numbers suggesting that our cohort is representative, it might limit extrapolation of our conclusion outside of the Netherlands. Additionally, the data did not include information on operator experience, e.g. fellow or not, or their supervision. Information on antiplatelet therapy and use of temporary mechanical support devices in cardiogenic shock patients were also unavailable, although these factors could potentially influence patient outcomes. Thirdly, patients treated with CABG, medically treated patients with acute MI, and pre-hospital coronary deaths were not included, and treatment strategies depending on timing of presentation could not be evaluated. Lastly, because of the retrospective observational design, no causality can be drawn from our study.

In conclusion, in this large retrospective cohort study, outcomes after PCI in STEMI patients presenting off-hours were comparable with those presenting on-hours. Therefore, the quality of the provided care in a tight and high-quality STEMI network appears to be independent of time of arrival at the cath lab.
