Radiological evaluation and complications of percutaneous vertebroplasty and fenestrated pedicle screw fixation with bone cement augmentation: propensity-score matched cohort study
Authors: Wen-Yen Tsai, Yueh-Ying Hsieh, Ying-Hsuan Tai, Chang-Jung Chiang, Chia-Hsien Chen, Ting-Shuo Hsu, Tin Van Huynh, Lien-Chen Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteoporotic vertebral fractures affect many elderly patients, and complications after common treatments such as vertebroplasty (VP) may reduce treatment satisfaction and radiographic stability. This study investigated whether the addition of posterior instrumentation with cement-augmented fenestrated pedicle screws (VP + PI) was associated with improved short-term radiographic outcomes compared with VP alone.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 153 patients with osteoporotic vertebral fractures treated between 2017 and 2022. Patients underwent either VP alone or VP + PI. To reduce treatment-selection bias, propensity score matching was performed. Vertebral collapse was assessed on serial radiographs at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively, and statistical analyses were performed using SAS software.ResultsAfter propensity score matching, the VP + PI group had significantly lower anterior column collapse rates at 3 and 6 months postoperatively. Multivariable logistic regression confirmed that VP + PI was independently associated with a reduced risk of anterior column collapse at 6 months (adjusted OR: 0.147, 95% CI: 0.043–0.502, p = 0.0022). At 6 months, screw loosening was observed in 6 of 75 patients in the VP + PI group, and none required revision surgery during the follow-up period.ConclusionsVP + PI may provide better short-term radiographic stability than VP alone in selected patients with osteoporotic vertebral fractures. However, given the longer operation time and hospital stay, lack of functional outcome assessment, and limited follow-up duration, the clinical benefit and long-term safety of VP + PI require further investigation.Trial registrationNot applicable.

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a widespread health concern, affecting approximately 21.7% of the elderly population worldwide [1]. With global population aging, more individuals are suffering from osteoporosis and subsequent osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (VCF) [2, 3]. These vertebral fractures can lead to substantial back pain, local kyphosis, impaired physical function, and diminished quality of life [4]. When non-surgical treatments fail, surgical intervention is often required.

Percutaneous vertebroplasty (VP) with polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) is commonly used to treat osteoporotic vertebral fractures due to its quick execution and proven effectiveness in alleviating pain and restoring vertebral body height [5]. However, achieving stable fixation is challenging in osteoporotic patients because of poor bone quality. When VP alone does not provide sufficient stability, patients may experience subsequent complications, such as vertebral re-collapse or nonunion [6–8].

To address these issues, some surgeons combine VP with pedicle screw fixation spanning one level above and below the fractured vertebra to provide additional stability and reduce postoperative complications. While numerous studies have examined the efficacy of combining VP and pedicle screw fixation or pedicle screw fixation alone, only a limited number of studies have directly compared these combined procedures with VP alone [6, 9–13]. Furthermore, screw loosening is a significant concern in osteoporotic patients due to weakened cancellous bone, which makes stabilization of the fractured segment difficult [14]. To address this problem, fenestrated pedicle screws were developed to improve anchoring strength through bone cement augmentation. Bone cement is injected through the screw head and disperses through the side holes into the surrounding cancellous bone, thereby enhancing screw fixation, increasing construct stiffness, and reducing motion at the instrumented segment, which may facilitate fracture healing [15]. Although many biomechanical studies have focused on pullout strength, screw loosening in actual clinical practice is influenced by multiple factors beyond simple mechanical pullout resistance [16–23].

In this study, we aimed to compare VP combined with fenestrated pedicle screw fixation (VP + PI group) with VP alone (VP group) in patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures(VCF). The primary objective was to evaluate short-term postoperative radiographic outcomes, particularly anterior column collapse, in the VP + PI group compared with the VP group. The secondary objective was to explore possible contributing factors associated with secondary vertebral collapse after surgery and to determine the potential role of VP + PI in patients at higher risk of postoperative re-collapse. Based on the current literature [6, 9–13], we hypothesized that VP + PI would be associated with a lower rate of postoperative vertebral re-collapse than VP alone.

Materials and methods

Setting and patient selection

This study proposal was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Medical University, Taipei, Taiwan (approval number: TMU-JIRB-N202309054; date of approval:2023-10-02). Written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of this study. All methods were performed following the standards of the Helsinki Declaration.

In this retrospective observational study, we used the electronic medical record database of Shuang Ho Hospital, Taipei Medical University to include patients who underwent either VP or VP + PI for osteoporotic VCF from January 1, 2017 to December 31, 2022. Patients were excluded if they (1) had prior history of spinal surgery, (2) had vertebral fractures due to non-osteoporotic causes such as pathological fractures or spinal infections, (3) exhibited multi-level vertebral fractures, (4) possessed a bone mineral density (T-score) > -2.5, or (5) presented with neurological deficits on physical examination or MRI findings were deemed to require spinal decompression, and lastly, (6) had incomplete imaging data, which was deemed essential for a comprehensive analysis. This included any omission in the anteroposterior (AP) and lateral view of both preoperative and postoperative spinal X-ray images, as well as follow-up imaging 1, 3, and 6 months after operation. After meeting the selection criteria, a total of 153 patients were included for analysis (Fig. 1). The included patients were divided into the VP group and VP + PI group based on the procedure they received for managing their problems.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was any anterior column collapse at sixth month after surgery. The secondary outcomes were anterior column collapse at first and third month after surgery, collapse rates of anterior column height at 1st, 3rd, and 6th months after surgery. We evaluated the serial spine plain radiographs of the included patients in the preoperative, postoperative immediate, postoperative 1st month, 3rd month, and 6th month periods. The percentage of anterior column collapse after fracture was measured on a lateral view of spine plain radiographs. The percentage of compression of anterior height of injured body was calculated as follows: ((‾H adjacent – H injured) × 100%)/(‾H adjacent) (‾Hadjacent: the mean height of adjacent anterior column below and above anterior height of the injured vertebra; Hinjured: anterior column height of injured vertebra) The percentage of re-collapse anterior body height was calculated as follows: ((H PostOP – H Follow-up) × 100%)/(H PostOP ) (HPostOP: postoperative anterior body height; HFollow-up: follow-up anterior body height.) Collapse of anterior column was defined as a collapse rate of anterior column height ≥ 25% during the postoperative follow-up period [9]. Loosening of pedicle screw was evaluated on postoperative 6th month image of VP + PI group. Loosening of pedicle screw was defined as osteolysis (radiolucent rim) > 1 mm around the screw.

To mitigate measurement bias, each radiograph was evaluated by three independent orthopedic spine surgeons. Discrepancies exceeding 5 mm in measurements were jointly addressed, with discussions continuing until a unanimous consensus was reached. The final recorded measurement was the average of the three evaluations.

Surgical procedures

Percutaneous vertebroplasty group (VP group)

In our institution, all patients in both groups underwent general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation according to routine surgical practice during the study period. The patient was placed on a radiolucent spinal table in a prone knee-chest configuration, and a posterior approach was chosen. The precise fracture level was confirmed using intraoperative C-arm fluoroscopy. Closed reduction was attempted by gentle manual compression over the affected level to restore vertebral body height through ligamentotaxis. Following this, under C-arm fluoroscopic guidance, a trocar was inserted through the pedicle into the anterior column of the fractured vertebra. Bone cement was then injected into the vertebral body, with the goal of predominantly occupying the anterior column. Continuous fluoroscopic monitoring was used during cement injection to reduce the risk of cement leakage. The ideal cement distribution was defined as filling the anterior and middle columns on the lateral view and extending at least across the midline on the anteroposterior view. After cement solidification, the trocar was carefully removed and the incision was closed.

Percutaneous vertebroplasty and fenestrated pedicle screw fixation with cement augmentation group (VP + PI group)

Percutaneous vertebroplasty at the index level was performed using the same technique as in the VP group. For posterior fixation, fenestrated pedicle screws were inserted percutaneously following the technique previously described by F.-Y. Tsuang et al. [24]. After confirmation of optimal screw positioning, cement was injected through the cannulated screws, dispersing into the surrounding cancellous bone through the side holes. Subsequently, appropriately sized rods were connected to the screw heads bilaterally and secured with nuts. No additional bone graft fusion was performed in this cohort; the procedure was intended primarily to provide immediate mechanical stabilization. The surgical site was then irrigated and closed.

Postoperative care

Patients in both groups were instructed to ambulate with the aid of a four-legged crutch initially and to avoid lifting heavy objects, attending intensive exercise, or bending at the waist. Additionally, they were instructed to wear a thoracolumbar sacral orthosis during daily activities for at least three months following the operation. They received regular follow-up at our out-patient clinic until fracture union was accomplished.

Data collection

To adjust for potential confounding factors, the following patient and clinical covariates were collected based on the available data and physiological plausibility. Demographic attributes were age, sex, and body mass index. The recorded preoperative clinical variables were bone mineral density (BMD), preoperative blood tests (hemoglobin, hematocrit, and creatinine, and anterior height collapse rate. Intraoperative factors included the amount of cement used in vertebroplasty and pedicle screw augmentation, as well as the operation time. Additionally, the length of hospital stay was compared between the VP group and the VP + PI group.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro-Wilk test and Kolmogorov-Smirnov test were used to check normality of the included variables. Non-normally distributed variables were log-transformed to reduce skewness in the distribution. These variables included preoperative blood test results, preoperative anterior height collapse rates, the amount of cement used in VP, and length of hospital stay. We used the propensity score matching to reduce potential confounding effects, as follows. First, a non-parsimonious multivariable logistic regression model was used to obtain a propensity score for the patients who did and did not receive VP + PI. The subjects who underwent VP + PI were matched to those who did not in a 1:1 ratio using the nearest neighbor matching algorithm. This algorithm utilized a caliper width of 0.05 standard deviations of the log odds of the calculated propensity score and was applied without replacement. This matching adjusted for factors including age, sex, body mass index, bone mineral density, preoperative blood tests, vertebral level, anterior height collapse rate, and the amount of cement used in VP. Baseline patient characteristics were compared between the patients who did and did not underwent VP + PI using the absolute standardized mean difference (ASMD). Covariate balance between groups was defined as an ASMD less than 0.1 [25]. The distribution of study outcomes was compared between the two groups using either chi-squared tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and Mann-Whitney tests for continuous variables, as appropriate. Conditional logistic regression analyses were used to evaluate associations between the use of VPPI and study outcomes. The variables with a p-value < 0.20 in univariate analysis were adjusted for in multivariable models to calculate adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Subgroup analyses were conducted based on the following criteria: age (≥ 65 years or < 65 years), sex (male or female), body mass index (≥ 25 kg·m− 2 or < 25 kg·m− 2), T-score of bone mineral density (≥ -3.0 or < -3.0), spine level (thoracic or lumbar, thoracolumbar junction or other levels), and preoperative anterior height collapse rate (≥ 50% or < 50%). A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was used to define a statistically significant difference. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics

Table 1 shows the baseline patient characteristics of the VP and VP + PI groups before and after propensity score matching. In the original cohort, there was no difference in the demographic features, body mass index, T score of bone mineral density, or preoperative blood test results except for age (absolute standardized mean difference, ASMD = 0.1234). The distribution of surgical vertebral levels was also different between the two groups (ASMD = 0.2811). In addition, the preoperative anterior height collapse rate was lower in the VP group compared with the VP + PI group, median 39.4% (interquartile range (IQR): 26.3–50.4) and 55.5% (43.2–68.6; ASMD = 0.9122), respectively. The operation time and length of hospital stay were shorter in the VP group compared to the VP + PI group. It should be noted that all the matched patient characteristics were well balanced after the matching process.

Anterior column height and vertebral body collapse

The preoperative and postoperative immediate anterior column height was significantly higher in the VP group (median: 1.66, IQR: 1.33–1.90 and 2.15, 1.96–2.44) com-pared with the VP + PI group (median: 1.19, IQR: 0.81–1.63 and 1.99, 1.74–2.35) in the original cohort (Table 2). However, the difference disappeared after propensity score matching. After matching, the collapse rate of anterior column height was significantly reduced in the VP + PI group compared with the VP group at the postoperative 3 and 6 months.

After propensity score matching, the risk of anterior column collapse was significantly lower in the VP + PI group than that of the VP group, 0 vs. 14.3%, 6.4% vs. 29.2%, and 8.0% vs. 34.0% at the postoperative 1st, 3rd, and 6th months, respectively (Table 2; Fig. 2). Table 3 shows the results of univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses. After adjusting for covariates, VP + PI was significantly associated with a lower rate of postoperative 6-month anterior column collapse (adjusted OR: 0.147, 95% CI: 0.043–0.502, p = 0.0022).

Screw loosening of VP + PI

At postoperative 6 months, 6 of 75 patients in the VP + PI group demonstrated screw loosening, defined as a radiolucent rim > 1 mm around the screw. The mean bone mineral density T-score of these patients was − 3.63. None of these patients developed neurological deficits or underwent revision surgery during the 6-month follow-up period.

Discussion

In this study, we compared VP alone with VP + PI in patients with osteoporotic VCF. Our results demonstrated that the VP + PI group was associated with a significantly lower rate of anterior column collapse during the 3- and 6-month postoperative follow-up after propensity score matching. These findings suggest that the addition of posterior instrumentation may provide improved short-term mechanical stability in selected patients.

In the multivariable analysis, VP + PI remained independently associated with a reduced risk of anterior column collapse at 6 months. From a biomechanical perspective, this observation is consistent with the rationale that supplemental posterior fixation can reduce motion at the fractured segment and share axial load, thereby decreasing the likelihood of progressive vertebral height loss after cement augmentation.

In this retrospective cohort study, patients were divided into two groups based on the surgical procedure performed. There was a significant difference in preoperative anterior column height between the two groups, with the VP + PI group demonstrating more severe vertebral collapse. This finding suggests that surgeons tended to select a more aggressive intervention for patients with more advanced or unstable fractures. This reflects an inherent indication bias in the original cohort. It should be noted that this difference disappeared after propensity score matching, indicating improved comparability between the groups.

After propensity score matching, VP + PI was significantly associated with a reduced risk of anterior column collapse at 1, at 3 and 6 months postoperatively. In multivariable analysis, VP + PI remained the only variable significantly associated with a lower rate of anterior column collapse at 6 months (adjusted OR: 0.147, 95% CI: 0.043–0.502, p = 0.0022). These findings suggest that VP + PI may contribute to improved short-term radiographic stability. Therefore, the current findings should be interpreted as evidence of structural stabilization rather than clinical superiority.

Subgroup analyses further demonstrated that VP + PI was associated with a reduced risk of postoperative anterior column collapse across multiple patient groups, including those aged ≥ 65 years, female patients, individuals with body mass index ≥ 25 kg·m^-2 or < 25 kg·m^-2, those with T-score < -3.0, thoracic vertebral fractures, and patients with preoperative anterior height collapse ≥ 50% or < 50%. These findings suggest that VP + PI may provide consistent mechanical stabilization across a range of patient characteristics. However, these subgroup results should be interpreted cautiously due to the relatively small sample sizes in certain subgroups.

In subgroups of patients aged < 65 years and male patients, the number of cases was limited (12 and 23 patients, respectively), which may have reduced the statistical power to detect meaningful differences. Therefore, a larger sample size would be required to more accurately evaluate the effectiveness of VP + PI in these populations. Regarding BMD, our findings suggest that VP + PI may offer greater protection against anterior column collapse in patients with more severe osteoporosis.

With respect to implant-related complications, 6 out of 75 patients in the VP + PI group demonstrated radiographic screw loosening during the 6-month follow-up period, with a mean BMD T-score of -3.63. None of these patients developed neurological deficits or required revision surgery. Although no revision was required during the follow-up period, these findings may underestimate the true incidence of clinically significant implant-related complications over time.

Our results are largely consistent with previous studies. Li et al. and Gu et al. reported improved maintenance of anterior vertebral height following VP combined with pedicle screw fixation compared with VP alone [6, 9]. However, Marie-Hardy et al. reported a screw loosening rate of 40.4% in patients undergoing pedicle screw fixation, identifying osteoporosis as a major risk factor [26, 27]. Löffler et al. similarly demonstrated an association between low bone mineral density and screw loosening [14]. These findings highlight the importance of bone quality in determining fixation stability.

Several studies have demonstrated favorable outcomes with cement-augmented pedicle screw fixation. Pawar et al. reported that short-segment stabilization with cement-augmented fenestrated pedicle screws combined with VP was associated with good maintenance of anterior vertebral height during an average follow-up of 18 months [28]. Fiore et al. reported similar findings using percutaneous cement-augmented screw fixation for osteoporotic vertebral burst fractures [12]. However, direct comparative studies between VP + PI and VP alone remain limited, and current evidence is insufficient to establish clear treatment indications.

Regarding the correlation between bone mineral density (BMD) and secondary fracture of cemented vertebrae, several in vitro studies have demonstrated that lower BMD is associated with decreased pullout strength of pedicle screws [29–32]. Although the relationship between low BMD and secondary VCF remains controversial, Ma et al., Lu et al., and other studies have identified low BMD as a potential risk factor for secondary vertebral fracture after VP [33–36]. In our study, all patients met the inclusion criterion of BMD T-score ≤ -2.5, which may have limited the variability in bone quality across the cohort. As a result, the association between BMD level and secondary vertebral fracture may have been underestimated.

From a biomechanical perspective, screw loosening in osteoporotic bone is closely related to trabecular microstructural deterioration, resulting in reduced screw purchase [37]. Krishnan et al. and Varghese et al. demonstrated that BMD plays a more critical role in fixation strength than other technical factors such as insertion angle or depth [38, 39]. Numerous in vitro studies have shown that cement augmentation improves the pullout strength of pedicle screws. Elder et al. reported that PMMA and calcium-based augmentation materials enhance fixation strength in osteoporotic models [40], while Kwak et al. demonstrated that cement distribution patterns influence pullout resistance [41]. These biomechanical findings support the rationale for cement-augmented fixation, although clinical outcomes and long-term complication profiles remain to be fully clarified.

In our study, VP + PI was associated with improved short-term radiographic outcomes, particularly in patients with more severe osteoporosis. However, this should not be interpreted as evidence that VP + PI is superior or should replace VP as a routine treatment. The issue of perioperative risk also deserves consideration. Compared with VP alone, VP + PI is a more invasive procedure and was associated with longer operative time and hospital stay in our study. Therefore, any potential benefit in mechanical stability should be carefully weighed against these factors.

Another important consideration is the role of fusion. In this study, no bone graft fusion was performed in the VP + PI group, as the construct was intended to provide immediate mechanical stabilization rather than long-term biological fusion. While this approach may be sufficient to prevent early collapse, the fixation system remains subject to ongoing mechanical loading, which may predispose to delayed complications such as screw loosening, rod failure, or adjacent segment disease.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, due to the retrospective design, treatment allocation was not randomized, and indication bias was present in the original cohort. Although propensity score matching was applied, residual confounding may still exist. Second, we did not include clinical outcome measures such as visual analog scale (VAS), Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), mobility, or quality-of-life assessments. Therefore, the observed radiographic improvement should be interpreted as evidence of structural stabilization rather than clinical superiority. Third, Cobb angle was not evaluated. Local kyphosis is an important parameter in osteoporotic vertebral fractures and is associated with pain, functional impairment, and sagittal imbalance. As a result, the impact of VP + PI on spinal alignment remains unclear, and future studies should include comprehensive sagittal alignment evaluation. Fourth, all procedures were performed under general anesthesia according to institutional practice. This approach ensured procedural stability in elderly patients with severe pain and limited tolerance for positioning. It differs from many centers where VP is performed under local anesthesia, which may limit generalizability. Fifth, no additional bone graft fusion was performed in the VP + PI group, and our 6-month follow-up period is insufficient to determine long-term implant durability, adjacent segment complications, or delayed deformity progression. Finally, the sample size was relatively limited, especially in some subgroup analyses, and this may have reduced statistical power to detect other contributing factors associated with postoperative collapse. Finally, the follow-up duration was limited to 6 months, which is insufficient to assess long-term implant durability, adjacent segment complications, or delayed deformity progression.

Despite these limitations, our findings provide clinically relevant insights. In patients with severe vertebral collapse or markedly reduced BMD, the addition of cement-augmented pedicle screw fixation may help reduce early postoperative recollapse. From a practical perspective, this approach may be considered in carefully selected high-risk patients where immediate structural stability is a primary concern. Further large-scale prospective studies with longer follow-up, functional outcome assessment, and comprehensive complication analysis are required to better define the optimal role and timing of VP + PI in the management of osteoporotic VCF.

Conclusions

In conclusion, VP + PI may provide improved short-term radiographic stability compared with VP alone in patients with osteoporotic VCF. VP + PI may be considered in selected patients at higher risk of postoperative vertebral collapse, but its clinical benefit, optimal indications, and long-term safety remain to be determined. At present, this approach should not be considered a routine treatment strategy but rather a selective option in appropriately chosen patients. Further well-designed prospective studies are needed to clarify its role in clinical practice.
