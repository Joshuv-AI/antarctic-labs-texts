The impact of mixed reality training method on novice trainees of dental implants: an in vitro study
Authors: Xiaoyu Wang, Miaosheng Guan, Lin Liu, Rongchen Xu, Zhongliang Yang, Zhen Liu, Deyu Li, Chuhua Tang, Ning Wen, Hongbo Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesA novel dental implant training method based on mixed reality (MR) navigation technology was developed. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the learning effect of this method in novices.MethodsWe designed and developed an MR-based training method that provides real-time supervision, teaching, and guidance. Thirty novices who had passed a baseline test and had no prior experience with implant surgery were randomly divided into two groups: the MR Group (n = 15) was trained using the MR training method, while the traditional group (n = 15) received training using conventional methods. Each group involved the following steps: (I) pretest; (II) immediate post-test; (III) dental implant training and (IV) retention test. Implant deviations, including platform points, middle points, apex points and axial angles, were measured. Subsequently, learning curves were analyzed, and the usability of the system was evaluated using the System Usability Scale (SUS).ResultsAfter training with both methods, the implantation deviations in both groups were significantly reduced compared with the pretest (P < 0.001). Except for the axial angle deviation in the immediate post-test, all deviations in the MR group on the immediate post-test and retention test were significantly lower than those in the traditional group (P < 0.05). The learning curve results showed that the learning time and learning effect of the MR group (Y = -95.62X + 1499) during the training process were better than those of the traditional group (Y = -77.36X + 1367). The MR group (80.83 ± 4.88) had significantly greater SUS scores than did the traditional group (69.50 ± 5.36) (P < 0.0001).ConclusionsThis MR-training method demonstrates excellent usability and can significantly improve the implantation learning outcomes for novices. Compared to traditional methods, it exhibits better immediate and retention learning effects. This approach offers a novel perspective and strategy for developing personalized and efficient implant placement teaching methods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06366-8.

Introduction

The effect of dental implants is largely dependent upon the precise position of the implant placement [1]. Due to the complicated anatomical structure of jaw bones, inaccurate implant positioning may lead to a high risk of placement failure [2–4]. The experience and skill of the operator are key factors in successful implantation. For most students, mastery of ability requires a substantial commitment of time and effort. Like other surgical procedures, special attention is given to the cultivation of operating ability in implantology [5–7]. The essence of mastering dental implant surgery lies in transforming theoretical knowledge into practical skills through the imagination and judgment of the three-dimensional (3D) structure of the implant site. However, due to space constraints in the mouth, students cannot visually observe the actual operation site from the view of teaching instructors in the traditional teaching methods, this situation results in what is described as “visual error”. Furthermore, traditional implant training relies on plastic models and animal jaws, which lack realistic haptic feedback and fail to adequately simulate complex anatomical structures, thereby impeding the effective translation of theoretical knowledge into clinical competence. Novices with limited experience may cause serious complications (such as nerve damage, sinus perforation, and prosthesis failure). Therefore, there are urgent demands for more effective methods for teaching implantology [8–10].

Simulators are effective training tools in modern oral teaching education, helping trainees develop skills in safe, controlled environments and reducing concerns about patient harm [11, 12]. In recent years, novel dental training simulators combined with virtual reality (VR) technology, augmented reality (AR) technology, and force feedback technology have ushered in a new era of dental operation teaching and training [13–15]. However, while AR simulators use superimposed virtual implants to guide drilling procedures on the jawbone, the position of the virtual model cannot be adjusted [16]. Another method projects traditional two-dimensional (2D) navigation screen content into spatial dimensions via AR glasses, yet it struggles with challenges such as “2D-to-3D conversion” and “hand-eye separation” [17]. While VR simulators offer immersive environments, they fail to replicate the stability derived from fulcrum-based tactile feedback [18–20]. Overall, the application of these methods has not achieved the ability to enhance the surgeon’s correct observation perspective during the implantation process.

A proper observation perspective is vital for establishing an accurate implant position. Dynamic navigation system-based optical real-time tracking technology, which can guide students to obtain the right angle of vision, placement angle, and implant site by real-time observation of the deviation of the drill or implant from the virtual planned position, has been employed in dental implant education [21]. However, the current dynamic system uses a 2D display to guide the implanted path in a fixed interface, which may cause distraction when users switch between the display and oral cavity.

Mixed reality (MR) technology integrates the advantages of VR and AR, merging virtual guidance with real-world operations. Specifically, MR technology enhances sensory feedback through head-mounted displays (HMDs), achieving high fidelity and visual teaching requirements, thereby producing more effective teaching outcomes [22, 23]. In our previous research, we developed an MR-guided implant surgery system and initially applied it in clinical implant surgery [24]. Consequently, based on the characteristics and benefits of navigation and MR technology, we established an implant teaching and training method. It not only enhances sensory feedback and resolves the challenges of integrating 3D virtuality with reality, but also improves students’ operational experience through the “perspective” that incorporates the real environment. This study evaluated the effectiveness of the MR-based dental implant training method compared to the traditional training method in enhancing the accuracy of freehand implant placement among novice trainees, and analyzed the learning curves of skill acquisition at the same time. Therefore, this study tested the null hypothesis that there would be no significant differences in dental implant placement accuracy using the freehand approach, learning curves, and subjective scale scores between the two teaching methods. The study hypothesized that MR training would result in greater accuracy of implant placement, faster learning curves, and higher subjective scale scores compared to traditional methods.

Methods

Establishment of MR navigation dental implant training system

The MR training system employed in this study consists of relevant hardware components and software modules.

Hardware components of the MR navigation dental implant training system

The training simulator (Fig. 1a) included a navigation server, a HoloLens 2 headset (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA), an optical tracking system (including an infrared stereo camera, a navigation and positioning frame and a dental handpiece with an optical positioning ball), an electro-implant motor (KaVo, Biberach, Germany) and dentition defect models (Xingxing, Shandong, China) (Fig. 3a-d). The teaching simulator was placed in the optimal scope identified by images taken with the infrared camera. The data were transmitted between the navigation server, the optical tracking system, and the HoloLens via the network.

Software modules of the MR navigation dental implant training system

To ensure a standardized structure for the MR-based dental implant navigation training, the module was divided into three key components: the foundational knowledge module, the operation training module, and the evaluation module.

Foundational Knowledge Module: This module included basic implant knowledge about basic principles, implant tools and operation procedures (Fig. 1d and e).

Operational Training Module: In the operation training module, the cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) and standard tessellation language (STL) data of the dentition defect model were obtained by CBCT (Newtom 5G, Italy, slice thickness: 0.15 mm) and a lab model scanner (3Shape D2000, Denmark). Then, 3D reconstruction, volume rendering, registration point marking, and implantation path planning were performed (Fig. 1g). After calibrating the implant tools (Fig. 1h) and registering the model teeth, the virtual models and tools could be observed in the HoloLens, with their relative positions matching the real situation (Fig. 1b).

Evaluation Module: A subjective evaluation scale (System Usability Scale, SUS) [25] was created in the evaluation module (Fig. 1f). After completing all operations, students needed to complete a subjective rating scale to measure the usability of the training method.

Participants

Participants with the same clinical experience background from the novice trainees of the PLA General Hospital were selected, who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria and volunteered to participate in this study (Additional file 1).

The sample size was calculated with Pass15.0 software (NCSS, USA) considering a power of 0.80 and an alpha error of 0.05, based on a previous study [21, 24]. Baseline testing was performed on all participants using questionnaires on demographic characteristics (age, gender, and educational background) before the experiment, and knowledge level of oral implants was tested. To ensure that all participants are comparable. A total of 30 participants were recruited for this study. For this controlled in vitro study, participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the MR training group or the traditional training group. A statistical specialist who was not involved in the training generated the random allocation sequence using computer software. Group assignments were concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque sealed envelopes to ensure allocation concealment.

Study design

This study was divided into four steps: (I) pretest; (II) immediate post-test; (III) dental implant training(MR method/ traditional method); and (IV) 4-week retention test. The flow diagram of the experiment is shown in Fig. 2. In addition, this study was an in vitro model study; the need for approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Chinese PLA General Hospital was waived. The in vitro study protocol followed the CRIS reporting guidelines. Informed consent was signed by all study participants.

Sample size calculation and preoperative planning

To ensure equal practice opportunities for each tooth position, we designed two models (A and B) in which the tooth roots can be blocked in the CBCT. Each model included 14 implant sites, and Models A and B together covered 28 sites in the maxillary and mandibular dentition (Fig. 3). During the pretest, immediate post-test, and retention test phases, each participant was tested 5 times (involving 5 dental implant sites) in each phase and 15 times (involving 15 dental implants) during the practice phase. Therefore, a total of 900 implant sites (34 sets each for Models A and B) were required for this experiment.

To ensure consistency, all the placement routes were performed by an experienced professor based on commonly accepted principles (Additional file 2), using commercial implants (Nobel Active, KAVO, SE). The appropriate implant type was chosen based on the edentulous alveolar ridge condition: φ3.5 × 13 mm implants for incisors, canines, and premolars; φ4.3 × 13 mm implants for molars.

Pretest

Both groups of the 30 novice trainees received training in the fundamentals of dental implant surgery. Then, the specialist explained in detail the drilling path and placement procedure of the dental implants. Each participant was randomly assigned to five implant sites in either model A or model B. In both groups, implants were placed using a freehand approach following the planned oral implant path.

The detailed procedure was as follows: the gingiva of implant sites was cut with a tissue punch; a round bur was utilized to precisely define the entry point prior to sequential drilling, and the teeth were drilled step by step following to the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, a periodontal probe, measuring scale, and direction indicator were used to measure and observe the implant path.

Dental implant training

The dental implant training lasted three weeks. Thirty novice trainees were divided into the MR group (15 participants) and the traditional group (15 participants). Each group followed a distinct training protocol.

The MR Group Training Protocol: Participants studied theoretical knowledge via a foundational knowledge module (2 h). They subsequently utilized the MR training system to observe the implant surgery process demonstrated by experts, enabling them to visualize in real time the target implant path, as well as the correct implant position and angle (1 h). Over a three-week training, they completed exercises at five designated implant sites each week, with each training phase lasting 90 min. During the practice process, HMDs could display position and angle deviations in real time, providing immediate visual feedback for participants enhancing spatial cognition, and improving the accuracy of the surgery (Fig. 1b).

The Traditional Group Training Protocol: Participants acquired knowledge of dental implants through lectures, instructional videos, and equipment manual (2 h). They observed a live demonstration of the standard manual drilling procedure performed by experts (1 h). Similarly, a three-week training process was implemented, with five implants placed each week, each session lasting 90 min. Participants practiced on simulators while receiving hands-on guidance and supervision from experts to learn implant surgery skills (Fig. 1c).

Learning curve experiment

The operation time for each of the 15 implant exercises per novice was recorded over the dental implant training stage in both groups. Additionally, the deviations in platform point, middle point, apex point, and axial angle for each implant were measured. All measurements were conducted blindly by the same experienced surveyor.

Immediate post-test

Immediately after the 15th dental implant training, both groups completed five implant tests by freehand approach. The traditional group continued to use the freehand method, while the MR group switched to the freehand method. Both groups were randomly assigned to five implant sites, and the test procedure for both groups was consistent with the pretest stage.

Retention test

Four weeks after the dental implant training, both the MR group and the traditional group performed a retention test by freehand. Additionally, neither group received any additional implant training between the completion of the implantation training and the subsequent retention testing.

Implants deviations ( platform point, middle point, apex point, and axial angle) were measured at three test stages: pretest, immediate post-test, and 4-week retention test.

Performance evaluation

After each drilling operation, the models were scanned by a scanner fixed with an optical scanning rod post operation. Implant placement, a CBCT scan, and an optical scanning rod scan were performed by a colleague who was not familiar with the group assignment beforehand. The coordinates of the preoperative and postoperative 3D models were aligned by manually selecting the crown and other nonshiftable features in the models in cooperation with the global ICP algorithm. The relative position between implant placement and planned implant location was subsequently observed via the software Geomagic Control. The placed implant location was determined in reverse order according to scan rod information and coordinates of both placed and planned implants were extracted. The positional deviation of the plan and actual implants was calculated, including platform point deviation, middle point deviation, and angle deviation (Fig. 4). Deviations were measured by a professional staff member who was blinded to the other procedures used in this study. Investigators and participants were blinded to route planning, tooth position assignments, novice trainees, and statistical measurements. Participants completed a subjective questionnaire survey based on the SUS [25].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Baseline measurements were analyzed using Student’s t-test (continuous variables) and chi-square test (categorical variables). Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was applied to deviations of platform, middle, apex points, and axial angles across pretest, immediate test, and retention test phases, with Tukey’s or Šídák’s post hoc tests for multiple comparisons. Learning curves for operation time and accuracy were evaluated through linear regression. The SUS scores of the two groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test. The interaction between the groups and the scale items was analyzed using two-way ANOVA, followed by Šídák’s test for intergroup comparisons of individual items. Ethics approval was not required for this in-vitro study. This study has been followed strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology (STROBE) statement (Appendix Research Reporting Checklists file).

Results of the pretest, immediate post-test, and retention test

For the traditional group and the MR group, at each of the three test points (pretest, immediate post-test, and retention test), the deviations in the platform, middle, apex point, and axial angle were measured. Each test involved 30 novice trainees and 150 implants. The accuracy of the measurements showed that the outcome variables were significantly influenced by both group (P = 0.0005) and the three tests (P < 0.0001) (Two-way ANOVA).

Comparing the outcome variables between the traditional group and MR group, the accuracy of the two groups showed no statistically significant differences in platform, middle or apex deviation or angle deviation in the pretest (P > 0.05). In the immediate post-test, apart from angle deviation, the MR group exhibited higher accuracy in platform, middle, and apex deviations (P < 0.05). In the retention test, the MR group demonstrated superior accuracy across all outcome variables (P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Additionally, comparing the accuracy of each training method at three tests (pretest, immediate post-test, and retention test) showed statistically significant differences (P < 0.0001) for almost all outcome variables in each group (Table 3). The results indicated that the accuracy of the immediate post-test was significantly higher than that of the pretest and retention test in both the MR and traditional groups. Although the accuracy of the retention test was lower than that of the immediate post-test, it remained higher than that of the pretest (Fig. 5).

The MR group (80.83 ± 4.88) had significantly greater SUS scores than did the traditional group (69.50 ± 5.36) (P < 0.0001) according to the post-use evaluation of the two teaching methods using the SUS scale for both groups of novice trainees, indicating good operability and efficient learnability of the MR-based navigational implant teaching and training system (Fig. 7, Additional files 3 and 4). Specifically, in three SUS items—“I think that I would like to use this system frequently,” “I thought this system was easy to use,” and “I found the various functions in this system were well integrated”—the MR group scored significantly higher than the traditional group (P < 0.05) (Table 4).

Discussion

Dental implantation has been one of the most challenging medical education projects because it requires students to have a rich imagination to transfer theoretical knowledge to clinical practice effectively. Moreover, it is difficult for novice trainees to find the ideal implant position in dental education training because of the lack of visibility. Effective preclinical simulation training is an essential part of dental implant education [26, 27]. This study proposed a novel MR-based navigation implant teaching and training method. This system allows novice trainees to view both the actual resin model and the virtual 3D model simultaneously. It is also possible to modify the position and size of the 3D virtual model displayed in the MR headset during training and to place it within each trainee’s personalized field of view during training. The headset allows visualization of the 3D model merged into the real surgical area, 360° rotation of the virtual model and real-time observation of the model. Therefore, trainees can fully visualize anatomical structures of the soft and hard tissues, as well as the relative position relation with the target implant.

In recent years, the rapid development of VR and AR technologies has changed traditional dental education [28, 29]. De Boer et al. [30] confirmed that Simodent, a VR immersive dental teaching system, can effectively improve hand flexibility and allow trainees to repeat exercises anytime, saving economic and time costs. However, the tactile feedback of the virtual model is significantly different from that of the actual clinical situation, and there is no fulcrum [31]. In our study, the drilling process was monitored through an MR headset, which enables correction of nonstandard operation procedures with a fulcrum in real time. In addition, MR can improve individuals’ understanding of the ideal placement position and feelings about the operation.

Accuracy is one of the essential indicators used to evaluate the effectiveness of dental implant placement training. Our study revealed that the MR-based navigation implant placement teaching and training method effectively improved the accuracy of the platform point, midpoint, apex point, and axial angle compared to traditional teaching methods in freehand surgical approach. Several studies have reported on the application of VR/AR technology in training for dental implant surgical procedures. Casap et al. [32] demonstrated that a VR navigation system improved the accuracy of implant placement among final-year dental students. Vincent et al. [33] also assessed the effectiveness of VR in dental implant training, revealing that VR training reduced the absolute value of angle deviation, significantly decreased the risk of perforation, and demonstrated a learning curve through repeated training sessions. Schneider et al. [34] investigated the usefulness of a smartphone-based AR dental implant training system for dental students and found that the AR training method significantly improved implant angulation. Similar to VR/AR training systems, MR training method has also significantly enhanced implant angulation accuracy. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, due to the significant differences among these systems, few studies have directly compared the effectiveness of MR, AR, and VR in dental implant training, which remains to be explored in future research. In addition, excellent immediate learning and good post-learning maintenance were measured when novice trainees used this system to learn about the surgical intervention. These findings demonstrated that the MR navigation-based dental implant placement training method still performed well even one month after training. Especially, the angle deviation demonstrates a more pronounced skill retention effect. Consistent with the results of other researchers [21, 35–37], both traditional education methods and other digital methods have emerged in recent years and can improve the accuracy of student performance. However, the performance in the retention test was only evaluated in our study. In addition, unlike 2D dynamic education methods, the MR method significantly improved all the deviation outcomes [21] and performed better than traditional methods did [36]. Although the tracking delay, calibration error, and fitting error of the MR training system may introduce systematic errors, all of these errors are within acceptable limits.

The learning curve is also a crucial objective metric for evaluating a teaching system when introducing a new technology [38]. Breaux et al. demonstrated the significance of establishing a learning curve as physicians engage in an increasing number of clinical practice activities, noting that surgical operative time decreases substantially with accumulated experience [39]. Due to the linear model has the advantage of having fewer parameters, it facilitates the observation of slope changes in practical applications. Consequently, this model is widely utilized, and it was also employed in this study. In this study, we analyzed the learning curve of 15 implant training exercises for novice trainees using the MR method and those who trained using the freehand method. The results of objective evaluation revealed that the learning time and learning effect were significantly better in the MR group than in the conventional group. The findings indicated that the MR-based dental implant placement teaching and training method is more effective, simple to master, and safe.

A subjective research study demonstrated that the system exhibited both high learnability and usability. Further analysis of the SUS items revealed that novices were more inclined to use the system frequently, perceived it as easy to use, and considered its various functions to be well integrated. This may be attributed to the system’s capacity to stimulate novices’ interest in learning more effectively than traditional methods. Additionally, its immersive and intuitive interactive design reduces cognitive load, thereby facilitating comprehension and enhancing novices’ confidence in their learning process. To some extent, psychological factors may influence the effectiveness of learning dentistry procedures [40]. With the help of technical guidance and feedback, MR-based dental implant teaching and training methods can improve trainees’ self-confidence, allowing young dentists to devote themselves to training and accumulating experience. This approach allows young dentists to master clinical skills in the shortest possible time, which suggests that this MR-based approach is a promising new method for teaching and training dental implant placement. In addition, the novel dental implant training method proposed in this study frees the instructor’s hands and reduces the pressure of leading the teaching. For students, the instructor’s review and guidance may lead to feelings of nervousness, anxiety, and lack of confidence in some cases. As if there is a virtual teacher beside the student in real time, students have a sense of “hands on” experience in which they are guided in the process of learning procedures. This helps students quickly translate theoretical knowledge into practical skills. Students receive timely feedback and correction at every moment in which they make mistakes. Notably, one student with high myopia reported experiencing vertigo during the study, which may be a common issue associated with HMDs.

Although this study confirmed the usability and effectiveness of the MR training method among novices, it still had some limitations. Firstly, the study design was short-term with a relatively limited sample size, and the performance differences among participants were not thoroughly evaluated. Secondly, similar to all navigation systems, this system exhibited inevitable systematic errors. Although the system operation has undergone strict calibration and evaluation, there may still be calibration errors and visual fatigue caused by long-term wearing. Furthermore, the skill learning of the MR training method was mainly limited to the in vitro stage. Whether the skills learned through this system can be directly applied in actual surgeries still requires further research. Finally, due to challenges such as equipment costs, technical maintenance, and course arrangements, the full integration of the MR training system into the dental training program still needs to be further improved.

In future work, we plan to expand the sample size and conduct long-term follow-up studies to assess the sustained impact of MR training on participants’ practical skills and knowledge. Subgroup analyzes of individual characteristics (e.g., gender, educational level, personality) will be performed to evaluate their influence on training outcomes. Software algorithms and image display functions will be optimized to improve calibration accuracy and reduce visual fatigue. Meanwhile, we will integrate the MR training module into the preclinical formal courses and evaluate its effectiveness in improving the actual surgical performance. And we will establish a dedicated MR simulation laboratory for pilot teaching, as well as combine artificial intelligence technology with the MR System, which is expected to provide a brand-new solution for intelligent training.

Conclusion

First, the results showed that novice trainees who were trained using the MR navigation-based dental implant teaching and training method learned better than those who were trained using the traditional method, both in terms of implantation site and implantation accuracy of the axial angle. Second, the analysis of the 15 operative training exercises in this experiment revealed that both training modes improved the operative precision and that the operative time, learning speed, and learning effect were better in the MR group during implant placement training than in the traditional teaching group. Third, participants in the subjective survey study were more satisfied with the dental implant teaching and training methods based on MR navigation technology than with the traditional method.
