ADAR1 regulates dsRNA formation in nuclear and mitochondrial transcripts through editing-dependent and -independent mechanisms
Authors: Heegwon Shin, Tyler J. Dorrity, Justin Aruda, Kenenni A. Wiegand, Jung Seung Nam, Jiping Yang, Aidan S. Jones, Jake A. Gertie, Meera K. Singh, Yuanjun Yin, Keer He, Rafan Sarker, Rajesh K. Soni, Yousin Suh, Iok In Christine Chio, Silvi Rouskin, Hachung Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYEndogenous (self) double-stranded RNAs (dsRNAs) in human cells can activate innate immune responses. ADAR1, an A-to-I editing enzyme of dsRNAs, suppresses aberrant immune activation by self-dsRNAs. However, how ADAR1 influences the cellular dsRNA landscape remains unclear. We show that human ADAR1 downregulates self-dsRNA abundance through editing-dependent and editing-independent mechanisms. We further conducted quantitative dsRNA sequencing on wild-type and ADAR1-deficient cells. dsRNAs are enriched in protein-coding mRNAs—especially those with repetitive elements and elongated 3′ UTRs—and mitochondrial RNAs. ADAR1-regulated dsRNA transcripts consist of nuclear-encoded mRNAs and, unexpectedly, mitochondria-encoded RNAs rarely edited by ADAR1. Accordingly, dsRNAs accumulate to high levels within the mitochondria of ADAR1-deficient cells. Mass spectrometry and biochemical assays can detect ADAR1p150 in mitochondrial fractions. Notably, ADAR1 loss sensitizes cells to inflammation under mitochondrial stress (e.g., herniation and X-ray irradiation). Hence, we show that dsRNAs regulated by ADAR1 go beyond A-to-I edited transcripts and that ADAR1 can control mitochondrial dsRNAs.

INTRODUCTION

Double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) is recognized as a potent pathogen-associated molecular pattern that plays a central role in initiating innate immune responses. During viral replication, long dsRNA molecules are produced, which are detected by the host innate immune system through pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs). Specifically, long viral dsRNAs can activate PRRs such as RIG-I-like receptors (e.g., MDA5 and RIG-I), which in turn induce the production of cytokines including type I interferon (IFN). Type I IFN binding to its cognate receptor then results in the induction of hundreds of IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) with potent antiviral and proinflammatory properties. Additionally, PRRs such as protein kinase R (PKR) and 2′,5′-oligoadenylate synthetase 1 (OAS1) exert direct antiviral function upon detecting viral dsRNAs by halting translation or inducing RNA degradation, respectively. Through these diverse dsRNA sensing pathways, the mammalian innate immune system retains high sensitivity for invading viruses and can effectively mount immune responses against viruses.1

Recent studies suggest that the mammalian host also expresses an abundance of long dsRNAs that can activate PRRs. It is vital to prevent these endogenous (“self”) dsRNAs from being sensed by PRRs to prevent pathologic autoinflammation.2 The RNA-editing enzyme adenosine deaminase acting on RNA 1 (ADAR1) plays a critical role in suppressing the immune response against self-dsRNAs.

ADAR1 binds to dsRNA structures and converts adenosine (A) to inosine (I) through a deamination reaction. ADAR1 is expressed in all cells and exists in two isoforms: a 110-kDa protein (ADAR1p110, p110), which localizes to the nucleus, and the 150-kDa protein (ADAR1p150, p150) that shuttles between the nucleus and cytoplasm.3 Both isoforms are constitutively expressed, but ADAR1p150 expression is further induced upon IFN exposure. We and others have shown that ADAR1p150 has a unique and more expansive A-to-I editome than ADAR1p110.4,5 Millions of A-to-I edits have been identified in the human transcriptome, most of which lie in Alu elements located in non-coding regions of mRNAs (e.g., 3′ UTRs and introns).6–8 Alu elements are primate-specific repetitive elements and comprise 11% of the human genome.9 When two nearby Alu elements are present in an inverse orientation, these inverted repeat Alus (IRAlus) can bind to each other and form long dsRNA structures ideal for ADAR1 editing.10 ADAR1 editing of such long dsRNAs is predicted to disrupt or destabilize dsRNA structures, allowing edited dsRNAs to evade detection by dsRNA-sensing PRRs.

Notably, loss-of-function ADAR1 mutations result in Aicardi-Goutières syndrome (AGS), a fatal autoinflammatory disorder and type I interferonopathy characterized by high levels of type I IFN in the brain and neurologic sequelae.11–13 Prior studies have used human cell lines (e.g., HEK-293T, HeLa, and A549) to understand how ADAR1 deficiency causes AGS. These studies collectively suggest that in the absence of ADAR1, various dsRNA-sensing PRRs (e.g., MDA5, PKR, and OAS1) cause dysregulated innate immune responses and cytotoxicity.14–17 Additionally, we utilized pluripotent human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) to investigate ADAR1 function in different cell lineages. We found that human neurons are particularly susceptible to ADAR1 loss and subsequent MDA5 and PKR activation due to their intrinsically elevated dsRNA levels, which may explain why the brain is especially susceptible to inflammation in AGS.18

In line with reports in humans, ADAR1 null mice (ADAR1−/−), ADAR1p150 null mice (ADAR1p150−/−), and ADAR1-editing-deficient transgenic mice (ADAR1E861A/E861A) all exhibit embryonic lethality caused by dysregulated activation of the MDA5-MAVS pathway.19–24 In addition, recent reports showed that activation of PKR, as well as ZBP1, a Z-DNA/RNA sensor that can trigger cell death, contributes to lethality in ADAR1-deficient mice.25–31 Taken together, both human and mouse studies have given rise to the prevailing model that ADAR1 prevents self-dsRNAs from activating PRRs.

As described above, it has been extensively demonstrated that ADAR1 loss causes activation of PRRs. In stark contrast, it is still unclear how ADAR1 influences self-dsRNA levels and identity. ADAR1 has been presumed to downregulate self-dsRNA levels in cells, since dsRNA-sensing PRRs become activated upon ADAR1 loss.14,17,20,25 Additionally, computational tools or in vitro biochemical studies on RNAs have predicted that A-to-I editing can reduce dsRNA abundance by disrupting or destabilizing dsRNA structures (e.g., IRAlus), as A-to-I conversion would remove A-U base pairing and result in bulges in dsRNA structures.17,32–34 Moreover, the identity of self-dsRNAs regulated by ADAR1 has been mainly predicted by identifying RNA species with ADAR1-specific edit sites.

Here, we applied a strategy unbiased by A-to-I edit sites. By applying custom quantitative dsRNA sequencing (dsRNA-seq) analysis and dsRNA imaging to ADAR1-deficient human cells, we demonstrate that ADAR1 actually regulates cellular dsRNA levels via editing-dependent and -independent mechanisms. Importantly, we found that ADAR1 not only regulates dsRNA formation in nuclear-encoded mRNAs but also regulates dsRNA formation in mitochondria-encoded RNAs within the mitochondria. In sum, we uncover new modes by which ADAR1 controls dsRNAs and also identify new dsRNA species regulated by ADAR1.

RESULTS

ADAR1 is a global regulator of long cellular dsRNAs across different human cell types

Besides in vitro-based biochemical methods, it has been challenging to assess how ADAR1 influences self-dsRNA levels and identity, owing to a lack of methods to quantitatively measure dsRNAs within cells. The J2 antibody, which was developed originally to detect viral dsRNAs, presents itself as a valuable tool to study self-dsRNAs due to its ability to bind dsRNAs longer than 40 base pairs in a non-sequence-specific manner.35,36 The J2 antibody has high specificity for dsRNAs, and recently it has been shown that the J2 antibody can also detect host dsRNAs.37–40

Using the J2 antibody, we first addressed whether ADAR1 could broadly regulate cellular dsRNA levels across different human cell types or if there were only certain cell types where ADAR1 regulated dsRNA levels. We analyzed various ADAR1-deficient cell types, including HEK-293T, Hap1, HeLa, and hESCs. After confirming the deletion of ADAR1 in each cell type by western blot (Figure S1A), we imaged dsRNAs in wild-type (WT) and ADAR1 knockout (ADAR1 KO) cells by staining with the J2 antibody. We found that loss of ADAR1 led to 2- to 3-fold higher levels of J2 signal in all cell types analyzed (Figures 1A and 1B). Furthermore, long dsRNAs were mainly located in the cytoplasm, and ADAR1 deficiency led to an increase in cytoplasmic and nuclear dsRNAs (Figures 1C and 1D). To validate that the J2 antibody specifically detects dsRNAs, we pre-treated WT and ADAR1 KO cells with various nucleases before J2 staining. J2 signal was abolished in both WT and ADAR1 KO cells pre-treated with dsRNase but not in cells pre-treated with single-stranded RNase (ssRNAse), DNase, or RNase H (Figures S1C–S1E). These findings confirm that the J2 antibody specifically detects dsRNAs, and not ssRNAs, DNA, or RNA/DNA hybrids. Moreover, we used the K1 antibody, another dsRNA-sensing antibody, which consistently revealed increased dsRNA levels in ADAR1 KO cells (Figure S1F). This orthogonal approach further supports our conclusion that ADAR1 loss leads to elevated dsRNA. In addition, we found that this phenotype is conserved in human dermal fibroblast primary cells (Figures S1G–S1I), indicating that the increase in dsRNAs upon ADAR1 depletion also occurs in human primary cells. In line with these findings, prior studies demonstrated that ADAR1 negatively regulates cellular dsRNA levels in human neurons, Jurkat T-ALL cell lines, and mouse embryonic fibroblasts.18,29,41 Combining our data with these previous observations, we conclude that ADAR1 is a universal regulator of cellular dsRNA levels across diverse human cell types.

Importantly, we observed no basal IFN signaling in ADAR1 KO HEK-293T, Hap1, HeLa, and hES cells analyzed in Figure 1A, underscoring that the elevated dsRNA levels in these cells are tolerated (Figure S1B). However, upon treatment with IFN-β (a type I IFN subtype), we observed robust type I IFN production in ADAR1 KO cells but not in WT cells (Figure S1B), consistent with previous studies. Prior studies demonstrated that this IFN-induced IFN production was mediated by MDA5, which is an ISG and a PRR that detects dsRNAs and induces type I IFN production.14,17,25,42 Hence, the elevated dsRNA levels in ADAR1 KO cells can be tolerated at baseline (mock conditions), but stimuli such as type I IFN can induce MDA5 expression to instigate dsRNA-mediated immune responses.

ADAR1p150 uses editing-dependent and -independent mechanisms to regulate dsRNA formation and inflammation

Past studies have shown that ADAR1p150, but not ADAR1p110, plays a major role in suppressing PRR activation,19,25 but whether this immune regulation occurs by directly modulating cellular dsRNA levels remains to be determined. We sought to determine which ADAR1 isoform is required to control dsRNA burden. Using ADAR1 KO HEK-293T cells, we exogenously expressed either ADAR1p110 or ADAR1p150 and analyzed dsRNA levels. We observed that ADAR1p110 slightly decreased dsRNA levels, while ADAR1p150 expression markedly reduced dsRNA levels, similar to that of levels in WT cells (Figures 2A and 2B). This indicates that ADAR1p150 is much more effective at regulating dsRNA levels than ADAR1p110.

Next, we investigated whether ADAR1-mediated regulation of dsRNA is editing dependent. ADAR1p150 is composed of an N-terminal Z-DNA/RNA-binding domain, three double-stranded RNA-binding domains (dsRBDs), and a C-terminal deaminase domain required for ADAR1 catalytic activity (Figure S2A).43 Mutating the KKXXK motif to EAXXA in all three dsRBDs generates dsRBDs defective in binding to dsRNAs (EAA).44 Introducing an E912A point mutation in the deaminase domain results in an editing-deficient ADAR1 that still maintains dsRNA-binding activity (E912A) (Figure S2A).45 We exogenously expressed EAA or E912A mutant ADAR1p150 in ADAR1 KO cells. While both mutant proteins were expressed efficiently, they were only able to partially reduce dsRNA levels compared to WT ADAR1p150, which fully reduced dsRNA levels to the level of ADAR1-intact cells (Figures 2A and 2B). It was interesting to observe that the EAA mutant could still partially suppress dsRNA signal. The ADAR1-EAA mutant is likely still binding RNA, albeit suboptimally, via its Z-DNA/RNA-binding domain or the catalytic deaminase domain that can also directly bind dsRNAs.29,46 Additionally, a double mutant containing both the EAA and E912A mutations had no effect on observed dsRNA levels. Hence, loss of RNA-binding and -editing activity additively increased dsRNA burden (Figures 2A and 2B). This indicates that both the dsRBDs and the catalytic deaminase domain of ADAR1 are required to fully regulate dsRNA levels, and that the dsRBD and deaminase domain of ADAR1 can employ independent mechanisms to control self-dsRNAs.

To distinguish ADAR1-editing-dependent or -independent mechanisms of dsRNA regulation in a more physiologic setting, we applied CRISPR-Cas9 genome engineering to knock in the E912A mutation into the endogenous ADAR1 locus in Hap1 cells (E912A KI cells). Hap1 cells have a haploid genome, which facilitates genetic manipulation. We confirmed successful knockin (KI) of E912A in the genomic DNA (Figure S2B) and also confirmed that the E912A mutation minimally alters ADAR1 expression levels (Figure 2F). As expected, E912A KI cells lacked editing activity, both at single-gene and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) resolutions (Figures S2C, 6A, and 6B), demonstrating abolishment of the RNA-editing function.

We imaged dsRNA levels in WT, E912A KI, and ADAR1 KO Hap1 cells and observed that E912A KI cells express intermediate levels of dsRNA between WT and ADAR1 KO cells (Figures 2C and 2D). We further confirmed that these differences in dsRNA levels did not result from changes in total RNA levels (Figure S2D). As editing-dead ADAR1-E912A can still bind dsRNAs, we tested whether ADAR1-E912A prevented the J2 antibody from detecting dsRNAs by “masking” dsRNA regions. To test this possibility, we “unmasked” dsRNAs prior to J2 staining by pre-treating cells with proteinase K, which was sufficient to degrade control protein GAPDH (Figures S2E and S2G) and ADAR1 (Figures S2H and S2J). However, despite proteinase K treatment, we observed that E912A KI cells exhibited intermediate levels of J2 signal between WT and ADAR1 KO cells (Figures S2E, S2F, S2H, and S2I). These results suggest that ADAR1-E912A is not blocking J2 antibody access to dsRNA but is rather suppressing dsRNA abundance via an editing-independent mechanism. Taken together, we conclude that both the editing-dependent and -independent capabilities of ADAR1 are important for controlling dsRNA levels.

In addition to dsRNA levels, we interrogated the innate immune activation in E912A KI cells. Similar to ADAR1 KO cells, E912A KI cell lines do not exhibit IFN production at baseline (Figure 2E). However, IFN-β treatment robustly stimulated type I IFN induction in E912A KI cells to levels comparable with those observed in ADAR1 KO cells (Figure 2E), suggesting that the editing-dead ADAR1-E912A fails to suppress IFN production. Interestingly, ADAR1-E912A fully inhibited PKR activation at 24 h post IFN treatment and still partially suppressed PKR activation at 48 h post IFN treatment (Figure 2F), suggesting that ADAR1 can suppress PKR activity via an editing-independent mechanism. A previous study demonstrated that endogenous ADAR1 inhibited PKR activation through direct protein-protein interaction.47 To examine whether ADAR1-E912A could directly bind to PKR, we immunoprecipitated PKR in the presence of nuclease and probed for co-precipitated ADAR1 in WT and E912A KI cells (Figure S2K). We observed a direct protein-protein interaction between endogenous PKR and ADAR1 proteins (ADAR1p110 and ADAR1p150), and the ADAR1-E912A mutation did not interfere with this interaction. These findings support that ADAR1-E912A can reduce PKR activity through a direct protein-protein interaction with PKR. Taken together, we conclude that elevated dsRNA levels in E912A KI cells can induce IFN production and PKR activation. Additionally, ADAR1 fully depends on its editing activity to suppress IFN induction, but it employs a combination of editing-dependent and -independent mechanisms to suppress PKR activity.

dsRNA-seq reveals the conserved identity of dsRNA-containing transcripts

We next conducted dsRNA-seq on WT, E912A KI, and ADAR1 KO Hap1 cells to determine the identity of dsRNAs. In dsRNA-seq, cell lysates were incubated with the J2 antibody to immunoprecipitate dsRNA-containing transcripts, which were then submitted for RNA-seq.37 In parallel, RNA was extracted from input lysates and submitted for total RNA-seq (Figure 3A and Table S1).

Analysis of total RNA-seq libraries revealed minimal differential gene expression between WT and ADAR1-deficient cells, and there was no upregulation of innate immune genes observed in ADAR1-deficient cells (Figures S3A and S3B; Table S2). This is consistent with prior knowledge that many ADAR1 KO cell types do not induce innate immune responses unless they are stimulated with IFN, as demonstrated in Figure S1B. Additionally, total RNA-seq libraries or dsRNA-seq libraries in Figure 3A were analyzed to profile transcriptome-wide A-to-I edit sites as previously described.48–52 As expected, A-to-I editing events were markedly reduced in E912A KI and ADAR1 KO cells (Figures 6A and 6B). We examined whether A-to-I editing influenced transcript abundance, but edited transcripts did not significantly differ in abundance between WT and ADAR1-deficient cells (Figures S3A and S3B). Collectively, these results demonstrate that E912A KI and ADAR1 KO cells provide editing-deficient conditions where dsRNA levels are increased (Figure 2C) while minimally altering overall gene expression.

We validated our dsRNA-seq workflow by confirming that dsRNA-seq enriched for known or expected dsRNA-forming transcripts, including repetitive elements such as SINEs, LINEs, and LTRs (Figure 3B).38,39,53 Sequencing reads of repeat elements could map to multiple regions of the genome due to their repetitive nature. Repeat-element-derived reads were highly enriched in dsRNA-seq, even when accounting for these multiple-mapped reads (Figure S3C). Additionally, we confirmed that transcripts expected to form dsRNA structures, such as NDUFS1 and EIF2AK2—both of which harbor IRAlus and are heavily edited by ADAR1—were enriched in dsRNA-seq datasets (Figure 3C). Similarly, MT-ND6, a mitochondrial transcript previously shown to form dsRNAs, was enriched in dsRNA-seq datasets (Figure 3C).37,38 In contrast, genes lacking Alu repeats and editing, such as RPS11 and FBL, were depleted from dsRNA-seq samples. Enrichment and depletion patterns of representative genes in the dsRNA-seq datasets were also validated by qPCR (Figure 3D), which further highlights the robustness of the dsRNA-seq assay applied.

We examined which genes are enriched in dsRNA-seq datasets after normalizing to total RNA-seq libraries. In total, 2,535, 2,390, and 2,379 genes were enriched in WT, E912A KI, and ADAR1 KO cells, respectively, while 3,224, 3,405, and 3,506 genes were depleted (Figure 3E and Table S3). The analysis of enriched (dsRNA-prone) and depleted (ssRNA-prone) genes showed a high degree of overlap among WT, E912A KI, and ADAR1 KO cells (Figure 3F). Notably, more than 83% of both dsRNA-prone and ssRNA-prone transcripts were conserved across genotypes. This importantly shows that the loss of ADAR1 or its editing activity does not drastically alter dsRNA transcript identity across genotypes. This is likely because A-to-I editing frequency per site is generally low (mostly below 20%),6,14 so even in ADAR1-intact WT cells, unedited transcripts can undergo dsRNA formation as in ADAR1-deficient cells.

The characteristics of the 2,120 co-enriched genes in the dsRNA-seq datasets provide insights into the features of cellular dsRNA sources (Table S3). These genes were predominantly derived from protein-coding mRNAs (85%) (Figure 3G). This finding aligns well with the observation that repetitive elements in intronic regions or 3′ UTRs of pre-mRNAs or mRNAs are the primary dsRNA substrates for ADAR1 editing.4,14

We also found that transcript isoforms detected in dsRNA-seq tend to have longer 3′ UTRs than those identified in total RNA-seq (Figure 3H). The majority of human genes generate alternative mRNA isoforms that encode the same protein but differ in their 3′ UTR length.54 3′ UTRs harbor many repetitive elements with dsRNA-forming potential, and we previously observed that in human neurons, longer 3′ UTRs lead to increased dsRNA levels.18 We applied DaPars2 analysis to compare the transcriptome-wide 3′ UTR length between dsRNA-seq and total RNA-seq datasets (Table S4). DaPars2 uses RNA-seq data to detect alternative polyadenylation (APA) sites and produce a percentage of distal poly(A) sites usage index (PDUI) value for each gene.55,56 DaPars2 analysis in Hap1 cells revealed that dsRNA-seq datasets are enriched for gene isoforms with longer 3′ UTRs compared to total RNA-seq datasets across all genotypes (Figure 3H). In contrast, comparison between WT and ADAR1 KO total RNA-seq datasets showed no substantial difference in 3′ UTR length, indicating that ADAR1 does not influence APA (Figure 3I). Collectively, elongated 3′ UTRs are a distinct and conserved feature of dsRNA-forming transcripts.

Additionally, we found that dsRNA-seq co-enriched transcripts are associated with stress granule localization (Figure S3D). Stress granules are non-membrane-bound complexes composed of RNA and proteins that form in response to various cellular stresses. A previous study described the stress granule transcriptome through RNA-seq analysis of purified stress granules, identifying 1,841 and 2,539 transcripts present inside and outside granules, respectively.57 Interestingly, 23% (429/1,841) of transcripts in stress granules were found in dsRNA-prone transcripts in our datasets, whereas 36% (915/2,539) of transcripts outside of stress granules were ssRNA-prone transcripts from dsRNA-seq datasets (Figure S3D). This suggests a strong association between dsRNA structure and its localization to stress granules. Consistent with this idea, ADAR1-deficient cells have been shown to be susceptible to stress granule formation.58,59 In addition to stress granules, recent studies have shown that dsRNAs can induce dsRNA-induced foci (dRIFs), another type of condensate containing various dsRNA-binding proteins in response to poly(I:C) transfection.60–62 Taken together, the dsRNA-prone transcripts we identified are major constituents of stress granules and could serve as a valuable resource for future condensate studies.

After protein-coding mRNAs, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs)—RNAs longer than 200 nucleotides that do not encode proteins—constituted the second major group of dsRNA-forming transcripts (Figure 3G). Mitochondrial RNA represents another interesting source of dsRNAs. Among the 37 mitochondrial genes, over 20% (eight genes) were present in our dsRNA-seq co-enriched datasets (Figure 3G). Mitochondria-encoded dsRNAs (mt-dsRNAs) are known to be generated through bidirectional transcription of the mitochondrial genome.63,64 Despite the small number of mitochondrial genes (only 37), mt-dsRNAs comprise a major fraction of cellular dsRNAs, totaling 40%–90% of cellular dsRNAs depending on cell type.18,37

Taken together, cellular dsRNAs are mainly composed of protein-coding mRNAs, lncRNAs, and mitochondrial RNAs. Importantly, since over 83% of dsRNA-prone transcripts were conserved across genotypes (Figure 3F), we conclude that ADAR1 does not drastically shift the identity of dsRNA species present in cells but rather modulates the quantity of dsRNA species.

dsRNA-seq reveals mitochondria-encoded RNAs as a key contributor to elevated dsRNAs in ADAR1-defective cells

Previously published dsRNA-seq protocols can effectively determine dsRNA species but not dsRNA quantity. We devised a new strategy to assess dsRNA identity and quantity simultaneously by normalizing dsRNA-seq datasets to External RNA Controls Consortium (ERCC)-RNA controls (Figure 3A).65,66 In brief, identical amounts of synthetic ERCC-RNAs were added to dsRNA pools obtained from the same number of cells, and normalized reads were calculated using ERCC-RNA counts during DESeq2-based library normalization.67 The resulting normalized read count values (nFPM; Table S5) allow us to compare relative quantities of dsRNA-containing transcripts across genotypes.

To compare dsRNA levels between WT and ADAR1-defective cells, we examined the fold change in dsRNA-containing reads. To ensure our analysis targeted dsRNA changes driven by RNA structural modifications rather than transcript-level changes, we excluded genes from the analysis when dsRNA-level differences correlated with differences in total RNA levels in the total RNA-seq libraries (Figures S4A and S4B). The resulting analysis demonstrates a striking accumulation of dsRNA-containing transcripts in ADAR1 KO cells compared to WT cells (Figure 4A). In contrast, the ERCC-RNA control group remains consistent between genotypes as expected (Figure 4A). Importantly, we observed that the increase in dsRNA-containing transcript levels in ADAR1 KO cells is driven by the combined effects of widespread, albeit mild, increases in dsRNA structures across a broad range of transcripts. If dsRNA accumulation were confined to specific transcripts, we would expect to observe a pattern with high fold changes in a limited subset of genes while most transcripts remain unchanged. However, Figure 4A demonstrates a consistent upward trend in dsRNA levels in a broad range of transcripts, indicating that these cumulative changes contribute to the overall dsRNA accumulation in ADAR1 KO and E912A KI conditions (Figure 4A). When analyzing statistically significant changes, 1,088 genes showed increased dsRNA levels in ADAR1 KO cells compared to WT cells, while 165 genes exhibited increases in E912A KI cells compared to WT cells (Figure 4A). Although the overall trend is similar between ADAR1 KO and E912A KI cells, the changes in E912A KI are milder, consistent with patterns observed in the dsRNA staining results (Figure 2C).

To identify the key contributors to the difference in dsRNA levels between ADAR1 KO and WT cells, we noted that fold-change analysis alone is insufficient due to wide variance in gene-expression levels: a 2-fold change in low-abundance transcripts does not have the same impact on overall dsRNA levels as a 2-fold change in high-abundance transcripts. To address this, we performed a subtraction analysis of the normalized read count in fragments per million (nFPM) values from dsRNA-seq datasets of ADAR1 KO and WT cells, enabling us to compare the absolute differences in dsRNA-containing transcript levels across genotypes. As expected, there is no difference in the amount of ERCC-RNAs between WT and ADAR1 KO cells, serving as a negative control. Strikingly, the top four genes driving the most significant dsRNA transcript increases in ADAR1 KO cells were mitochondrial RNAs: MT-RNR1, MT-RNR2, MT-CO1, and MT-ND5 (Figure 4B). Additionally, all other mitochondrial RNAs displayed increased dsRNA levels in ADAR1 KO cells, underscoring the mitochondria’s critical role in the observed dsRNA accumulation in ADAR1 KO cells. In E912A KI cells, mt-dsRNA upregulation was observed but to a much lesser extent (Figure 4B). These results highlight that ADAR1 can regulate dsRNA levels not only in nuclear encoded transcripts but also in mitochondria-derived RNAs. Of note, the mitochondrial rRNA genes MT-RNR1 and MT-RNR2 are not in regions of sense-antisense transcription yet are present in dsRNA-seq libraries. Furthermore, MT-RNR1 is classified as a dsRNA-prone gene in all genotypes (Figure 3E). It is important to mention that while most prior dsRNA-seq protocols depleted nuclear and mitochondrial rRNA, we used a specific rRNA-depletion kit that preserves mitochondrial rRNAs while depleting nuclear rRNAs. We took this unbiased approach to include mitochondrial rRNAs because mitochondria-derived RNAs have much fewer types and lower overall abundance of RNA modifications, which could lead to more stable intramolecular structures.68

Recent studies have shown that certain cellular stresses can increase total mitochondrial transcript levels, leading to mt-dsRNA accumulation.69,70 However, ADAR1-deficient cells do not exhibit changes in mitochondrial transcript expression (Figure 4C), which was further corroborated by qPCR data (Figure 4D). Collectively, these findings indicate that increased mt-dsRNAs in ADAR1-defective cells arise primarily from enhanced RNA duplex abundance rather than increased mitochondrial transcript abundance.

ADAR1 regulates dsRNA levels within the mitochondria and cytoplasm

Our dsRNA-seq results unexpectedly showed that ADAR1 suppresses mt-dsRNA levels, so we decided to further validate this finding. We co-stained cells for dsRNA and COX IV, a mitochondrial marker, and applied super-resolution Airyscan confocal microscopy (Figure 5A). We observed that dsRNA and COX IV co-localization increased in E912A KI and ADAR1 KO cells compared to WT cells, suggesting that mt-dsRNA levels increase in ADAR1-defective conditions (Figures 5A and 5B). To more conclusively determine whether dsRNA is increasing from within the mitochondria, we applied Imaris 3D image analysis. Mitochondrial volume was defined by COX IV staining and the nuclear volume by DAPI staining. 3D image analysis indicates that ADAR1 loss leads to increased dsRNAs from within the mitochondria (Figures 5C and 5D). As expected, we also confirmed that dsRNA levels increase outside of the mitochondria in ADAR1-deficient conditions via 2D image analysis (Figures 5A and 5B) as well as Imaris 3D image analysis (Figure S5A). Importantly, ADAR1 loss did not affect the total mitochondrial levels (Figures S5B and S5C). Additionally, a Seahorse assay demonstrated that depletion of ADAR1 does not noticeably affect mitochondrial respiratory capacity and glycolysis (Figure S5D). These data indicate that ADAR1 affects dsRNA levels within the mitochondria rather than the total abundance of mitochondria.

dsRNA-seq suggests that the mt-dsRNAs regulated by ADAR1 are transcripts encoded by the mitochondrial genome rather than nuclear-encoded transcripts entering the mitochondria (Figure 4B). As an alternative approach to confirm the origins of mt-dsRNAs, we treated cells with different RNA polymerase inhibitors prior to J2 staining to test how inhibiting the production of certain classes of RNA polymerases impacted dsRNA levels in WT, E912A KI, and ADAR1 KO Hap1 cells. As previously demonstrated,18,37 cells were treated with actinomycin D (ActD), which inhibits both RNA polymerase II (POLII) and mitochondrial transcription; α-amanitin, a POLII inhibitor; and IMT1, a mitochondrial RNA polymerase (POLRMT) specific inhibitor.71 All drug inhibitors were given transiently for only 12 h. We confirmed the specificity of the inhibitors by validating that ActD downregulated both POLII- and POLRMT-derived RNAs, while α-amanitin and IMT1 effectively downregulated levels of POLII RNAs and POLRMT RNAs, respectively (Figure S5E).

A 12-h treatment with ActD was sufficient to abrogate all dsRNA signal in WT and ADAR1-deficient cells, indicating that cellular dsRNA structures are relatively transient and persist for no longer than 12 h (Figure 5E). Upon α-amanitin treatment, we observed a moderate reduction in dsRNA signal in ADAR1 KO and E912A KI cells, but the remaining dsRNA levels were still higher than in WT cells (Figures 5E and 5F). This suggests that POLII-derived transcripts partially contribute to the increased dsRNA signal in ADAR1-deficient cells. Supporting this interpretation, when we treated cells with IMT1, there was a notable reduction in dsRNAs in ADAR1-defective cells, indicating that POLRMT-derived transcripts make up a major portion of increased dsRNAs in ADAR1-deficient cells (Figures 5E and 5F). Importantly, dual treatment with α-amanitin and IMT1 almost completely depleted all dsRNA signals, indicating that cellular dsRNAs mainly consist of POLII- and POLRMT-derived transcripts (Figures 5E and 5F). To quantify the contribution of POLII- and POLRMT-derived dsRNAs across genotypes, we calculated the reduction in dsRNA signal upon single treatment with α-amanitin or IMT1, respectively. Our analysis shows that loss of ADAR1 results in increased levels of dsRNAs derived from both POLII and POLRMT (Figure 5G). These results indicate that ADAR1 regulates dsRNA formation in POLII-derived transcripts and in POLRMT-derived transcripts, which further corroborates our findings from dsRNA-seq (Figure 4B) and image analysis (Figures 5A–5D).

To determine which ADAR1 isoform plays a role in mt-dsRNA regulation, we utilized ADAR1p150 KO HeLa cells, which express p110 but not p150, as previously shown.16 We treated WT, ADAR1p150 KO, and ADAR1 KO HeLa cells with α-amanitin or IMT1 to determine which types of dsRNAs are regulated specifically by ADAR1p150. In the absence of any polymerase inhibitor treatment, dsRNA levels were highest in ADAR1 KO cells, followed by ADAR1p150 KO and then WT cells (Figures S5G and S5H). Thus, ADAR1p110 expression in ADAR1p150 KO cells can partially suppress dsRNA levels. A 12-h treatment with α-amanitin or IMT1 efficiently reduced POLII- and POLRMT-derived transcripts, respectively (Figure S5F). In the α-amanitin-treated condition where mitochondrial RNAs are enriched, we observed similarly high levels of dsRNAs in both ADAR1 KO and ADAR1p150 KO cells; indicating that suppression of mitochondrial dsRNAs depends on ADAR1p150 (Figures S5G and S5H). In the IMT1-treated condition that enriches for POLII RNAs, dsRNA levels were highest in ADAR1 KO cells, followed by ADAR1p150 cells and then WT cells (Figures S5G and S5H). This indicates that both ADAR1p150 and ADAR1p110 isoforms can negatively regulate POLII-derived dsRNAs. When we quantified the contribution of POLII- and POLRMT-derived dsRNAs across genotypes, results indicated that ADAR1p150 regulates dsRNAs derived from both POLII and POLRMT (Figure S5I). Taken together, ADAR1p150, but not ADAR1p110, is required for mt-dsRNA regulation. Additionally, experiments in HeLa cells show that ADAR1 control of mt-dsRNAs is not limited to Hap1 cells but can be observed across different human cell types.

In conclusion, three orthogonal approaches—(1) dsRNA-seq (Figure 4), (2) dsRNA and mitochondria imaging (Figures 5A and 5C), and (3) RNA polymerase inhibition experiments (Figures 5E and S5G)—all suggest that ADAR1, specifically ADAR1p150, negatively regulates dsRNA formation in POLII-derived transcripts (e.g., mRNAs) and mitochondrial RNAs derived by POLRMT.

A-to-I edits were rarely detected in mitochondrial RNAs

How does ADAR1 control dsRNAs within the mitochondria? One possibility is that ADAR1 may directly edit mitochondrial RNAs, which can introduce mismatches that disrupt dsRNAs. To identify A-to-I edits in our sequencing dataset, we used a traditional editing-detection method based on analyzing mismatches between the reference genome and the sequenced reads (Figure S6A). Reads were first aligned using STAR, and stringent quality filters were applied to remove low-confidence data (non-unique alignments, reads with <95% unambiguous base calls, or base calls with Phred scores <25). Potential editing sites were defined as positions covered by at least five reads, where at least 5% of reads differed from the reference base with at minimum two supporting reads. Using these criteria, we detected an average of 44,103 A-to-I edits (A-to-G mismatches) per replicate in WT total RNA-seq and 107,211 edits per replicate in WT dsRNA-seq (Figures 6A and 6B). Importantly, other types of mismatches were detected only at very low levels compared with A-to-G mismatches, indicating that filtering effectively reduced background mismatches (Figures 6A and 6B). Moreover, the observation that A-to-I edits were reduced to background levels in E912A KI and ADAR1 KO cells further supports the reliability of our editing analysis (Figures 6A and 6B). Finally, consistent with previous reports, a high proportion of A-to-I edits (90.42%) were found in Alu elements, which are well-known endogenous sources of dsRNAs, further validating the accuracy of our approach (Figure S6B).6–8

Interestingly, we detected ∼2.5-fold more edit sites in dsRNA-seq than in total RNA-seq, likely because dsRNA-seq enriches low-abundance dsRNAs that would otherwise be undetectable (Figures 6A and 6B). Altogether, after discarding A-to-G mutations present in ADAR1 KO or E912A cells, we identified in total 105,210 ADAR1-associated A-to-I edits from total RNA-seq and dsRNA-seq datasets (Figure 6C and Table S6). Interestingly, only a single A-to-G mutation was detected in the mitochondrial genome, which was supported by only a single total RNA-seq replicate (Figure 6C). Because classical pipelines may miss hyper-edited reads due to alignment stringency, we next examined whether mitochondrial RNAs undergo hyper-editing. Hyper-editing occurs when multiple adjacent adenosines are edited, creating dense A-to-G mismatches. These sites may not be detected by classical editing analysis methods if extensive mismatches to the genome are not tolerated in the alignment step, causing hyper-edited reads to be discarded.73 We applied a specialized pipeline optimized for detecting hyper-edited sites in our RNA-seq datasets and identified a total of 14,896 hyper-edited sites (Figures 6D and S6C; Table S7). Notably, none of these edits were found in mitochondrial genes. The low frequency of editing in mitochondrial genes raises the possibility that ADAR1 can regulate mt-dsRNA independently of its editing activity. Supporting this possibility, dsRNA-seq (Figure 4B) and image analysis (Figures 5C and 5D) of the ADAR1-E912A KI cells indicate that the editing-dead ADAR1-E912A can partially suppress mt-dsRNA levels.

ADAR1 does not influence mt-dsRNA stability

To further explore how ADAR1 influences mt-dsRNA levels, we next compared the stability of mt-dsRNAs between WT and ADAR1 KO cells to determine whether impaired degradation of mt-dsRNAs causes accumulation of mt-dsRNAs in ADAR1 KO cells (Figure S7A). To block de novo mitochondrial transcription, cells were treated with IMT1 and harvested at 0, 2, 4, and 8 h post treatment. dsRNAs were then purified by J2 immunoprecipitation, and transcript levels were quantified by qPCR. As expected, the level of NDUFS1 (negative control), a POLII-derived abundant dsRNA source, remained unchanged in the dsRNA pool of both WT and ADAR1 KO cells following IMT1 treatment (Figure S7B). Strikingly, within 2 h of IMT1 treatment, MT-RNR1 and MT-ND6 dsRNA levels dropped to less than half, indicating rapid turnover of mt-dsRNA (Figure S7B). Importantly, we observed no difference in MT-RNR1 and MT-ND6 dsRNA stability between WT and ADAR1 KO cells (Figure S7B). Further corroborating this, ADAR1 deficiency did not affect the activity of key mitochondrial dsRNA decay factors, PNPT1 (an exoribonuclease), and SUV3 (a helicase). These proteins are encoded in the nucleus but translocate to mitochondria, where they degrade mt-dsRNAs. One possibility is that ADAR1 deficiency disrupts the mitochondrial localization of PNPT1 or SUV3. However, we observed no difference in PNPT1 and SUV3 subcellular localization between WT and ADAR1 KO cells (Figures S8A–S8D). Moreover, knockdown of either PNPT1 or SUV3 increased dsRNA levels in ADAR1 KO cells, similar to their effects in WT cells, indicating that ADAR1-mediated regulation of dsRNA occurs independently of PNPT1 and SUV3 activity (Figures S8E–S8H). Taken together, these results confirm that ADAR1 does not influence mt-dsRNA stability and is more likely to regulate dsRNA formation.

Mass spectrometry analysis of WT and ADAR1 KO cell mitochondria

To uncover additional factors potentially mediating ADAR1’s regulation of mt-dsRNAs, we compared proteomes of WT and ADAR1 KO mitochondria. Mass spectrometry is regarded as one of the most sensitive methods for identifying proteins even at very low concentrations. We purified the mitochondria using differential centrifugation as previously shown74 and applied mass spectrometry to input total cell lysates and isolated mitochondrial fractions (Figure S9A and Table S8). We confirmed that mitochondrial proteins annotated in the MitoCarta3.0 database were well enriched in our mitochondrial fractions, validating our mitochondria mass spectrometry analysis (Figure S9B).75 Interestingly, ADAR1 depletion caused only minimal changes in steady-state protein abundance across both total and mitochondrial fractions (Figures S9C and S9D). However, a few proteins, including PTGS2 (the gene that encodes COX2) and ISG15, showed statistically significant differences between WT and ADAR1 KO mitochondria. Both PTGS2 and ISG15 have been previously reported to localize within mitochondria, and, interestingly, both genes play major roles in immunity.76,77 However, overexpression of either PTGS2 or ISG15 in WT or ADAR1 KO cells did not alter dsRNA levels (Figure S9E), indicating that ADAR1 regulates mt-dsRNAs through a mechanism distinct from these mitochondrial proteins.

Intriguingly, our mass spectrometry data suggest that a small fraction of ADAR1 may reside within mitochondria. Although high-resolution co-staining of ADAR1 and the mitochondrial marker COX IV showed minimal ADAR1 co-localization with the mitochondria (Figure 6E), mass spectrometry identified a significant presence of ADAR1 peptides in mitochondrial fractions isolated from WT HeLa cells (Figure 6F). Notably, a recent proteomic study provided independent evidence supporting this possibility.72 In this study, the authors defined the mitochondrial proteome by first isolating “crude mitochondria” via differential centrifugation and then purifying “pure mitochondria” through sucrose gradient fractionation. Comparison between the two fractions revealed that MitoCarta annotated mitochondrial proteins were strongly enriched in the pure mitochondrial fraction, as expected (Figure 6G). Strikingly, ADAR1 exhibited marked fold enrichment in the pure mitochondrial fraction, a level comparable to that of validated mitochondrial proteins, leading the authors to classify ADAR1 as a high-confidence mitochondrial protein (Figure 6G). Additionally, genome-wide analyses of ADAR1 ligands have reported interactions between ADAR1 and mitochondrial RNAs.78 These observations further support the possibility that a small subset of ADAR1 exists within mitochondria.

To determine whether ADAR1 may localize within the mitochondria, we treated purified mitochondria with proteinase K (Figures S9F and S9G). Proteinase K treatment selectively degrades proteins exposed on the mitochondrial surface, while proteins protected by the mitochondrial outer membranes, such as those located in the intermembrane space or matrix, remain intact. As anticipated, impurities introduced during the purification and outer membrane proteins were efficiently degraded, whereas COX IV, an inner membrane protein, was protected (Figures S9F and S9G). Consistent with the mass spectrometry results, we detected a substantial amount of ADAR1 in the mitochondrial fraction. Notably, the ratio of ADAR1p150 to ADAR1p110 increased in mitochondrial fractions compared to total lysates. Furthermore, while ADAR1p110 degraded with proteinase K treatment, a portion of the ADAR1p150 signal persisted even after proteinase K treatment (Figures S9F and S9G). These results support the possibility that a very small population of ADAR1p150 may reside within the mitochondria. This is very interesting because we found that ADAR1p150, and not ADAR1p110, mainly contributes to mt-dsRNA suppression (Figures S5G–S5I).

Based on our editing analysis, mass spectrometry data, and mitochondria proteinase K treatment assays, we speculate that ADAR1 can regulate mt-dsRNA formation through an editing-independent mechanism, potentially mediated by a small pool of ADAR1p150 that localizes to the mitochondria.

Loss of ADAR1 sensitizes cells to inflammation upon mitochondrial stress

Next, what is the biological significance of elevated mt-dsRNAs in ADAR1 KO cells? Although dsRNA accumulates in the mitochondria of ADAR1-deficient cells, it has little effect on inflammatory status at baseline (Figure S1B); likely because mt-dsRNAs are contained within the mitochondria and not exposed to cytoplasmic PRRs. We employed a strategy to herniate the mitochondria to examine the role of ADAR1 in mitochondrial RNA-mediated inflammation. S63845, an MCL-1 inhibitor, has been shown to induce mitochondrial herniation by promoting BAX and BAK pore formation,79 allowing mitochondrial nucleic acids to leak into the cytoplasm. In a prior study, co-treatment of S63845 with the pan-caspase inhibitor emricasan was shown to stimulate potent mt-dsRNA-mediated type I IFN induction.80 Caspases are silencers of innate immune responses and are activated by mitochondrial herniation.81,82 Therefore, it is necessary to co-treat cells with S63845 and emricasan to detect the inflammatory response to mitochondrial herniation.80

We applied the S63845 and emricasan drug combination to WT and ADAR1 KO HeLa cells. We utilized HeLa cells, as they have an especially high proportion of dsRNAs located in the mitochondria.18,37 As expected, single treatments with either S63845 or emricasan did not induce robust IFN-β, but upon dual treatment, we observed an approximately 10-fold induction of IFN-β in WT HeLa cells. Strikingly, dual treatment stimulated much stronger IFN-β induction (about 90-fold) in ADAR1 KO cells (Figure 7A). Consistently, we observed higher phosphorylation of IRF3 (p-IRF3), the upstream step of IFN-β transcription, in ADAR1 KO cells compared to WT cells (Figure 7B). These data suggest that ADAR1 KO cells are more sensitive to mitochondria herniation-induced inflammation.

Next, we examined whether herniation-induced type I IFN induction is dependent on mt-dsRNA. Prior to mitochondrial herniation, we treated WT and ADAR1 KO cells with the RNA polymerase inhibitors α-amanitin or IMT1 to deplete RNAs derived from POLII or POLRMT, respectively (Figure S10A). We could not directly measure IFN-β transcript levels, since it would be affected by α-amanitin treatment that inhibits POLII transcription. Instead, we measured p-IRF3 levels to determine the effect of RNA polymerase inhibitor pre-treatment on type I IFN induction. Inhibition of POLII transcription by α-amanitin did not affect p-IRF3 in WT or ADAR1 KO cells. However, IMT1 treatment completely suppressed the elevated p-IRF3 expression in ADAR1 KO cells, underscoring the strong contribution of mt-dsRNA in herniation-induced type I IFN (Figures 7C and 7D).

ADAR1 inhibition has been shown to sensitize murine tumors to cancer therapies including immune checkpoint blockade and radiation,42 making ADAR1 an attractive target for treating cancer. Radiation therapy can increase type I IFN in the tumor microenvironment, and it is also known to induce mitochondrial stress and membrane damage.83 We asked two novel questions. First, could ADAR1 loss sensitize human cancer cells to X-ray irradiation, and second, could mt-dsRNAs play a role in this context? Interestingly, we observed that ADAR1 KO HeLa cells exhibited much higher levels of IFN-β and PKR activation in response to low (10 Gy) and high (20 Gy) radiation doses compared to WT cells (Figures 7E and S10B). Furthermore, inhibition of mitochondrial transcription by IMT1 treatment (Figure S10C) downregulated IFN-β induction in ADAR1 KO cells at day 3 post irradiation at both radiation doses and at day 5 post irradiation at the higher radiation dose (Figure 7E). Compared to IFN-β induction, IMT1 treatment only mildly reduced PKR activation (Figure S10B), indicating that mt-dsRNAs differentially drive IFN-β or PKR activation. Hence, ADAR1 loss can potentiate inflammation upon X-ray irradiation, and mt-dsRNAs contribute to radiation-mediated inflammation in ADAR1-deficient cells. The mitochondrial herniation experiments and irradiation experiments together demonstrate that loss of ADAR1 sensitizes cells to innate immune responses upon mitochondrial stress, and depletion of mitochondrial RNA can mitigate this inflammation.

DISCUSSION

Here, we demonstrate that ADAR1 is a bona fide universal regulator of cellular dsRNAs across different human cell types and that it employs editing-dependent and -independent mechanisms to suppress dsRNA formation. We additionally developed a dsRNA-seq pipeline to simultaneously compare dsRNA identity and quantity between WT and ADAR1-deficient cells. This revealed that ADAR1 shapes dsRNA quantity rather than dsRNA identity. Interestingly, dsRNA-seq datasets (from both WT and ADAR1-deficient cells) were enriched for elongated 3′ UTRs compared to total RNA-seq datasets, suggesting that long 3′ UTRs could be major contributors to dsRNAs. dsRNA-seq also demonstrated that, as expected, ADAR1 can suppress dsRNAs in cytoplasmic mRNAs as well as, unexpectedly, mt-dsRNAs transcribed in the mitochondria. Surprisingly, we found that mt-dsRNAs are the biggest contributor to the increased dsRNA pool in ADAR1 KO cells. Additionally, consistent with dsRNA-seq datasets, other orthogonal approaches also indicated that mt-dsRNAs accumulate to high levels “within” the mitochondria of ADAR1-deficient cells. Furthermore, ADAR1 loss sensitized cells to inflammatory responses under mitochondrial stress conditions, such as mitochondrial herniation and X-ray irradiation. Hence, we identify new modes by which ADAR1 controls self-dsRNAs and also identify new dsRNA species regulated by ADAR1. Our study also highlights that identifying self-dsRNA species by annotating A-to-I edited regions—which has been a common way to predict cellular dsRNAs—does not fully capture the landscape of self-dsRNAs, as we identify dsRNA-prone transcripts that lack detectable editing.

The dsRNA accumulation in our editing-deficient cells (E912A KI cells) clarified that ADAR1-mediated editing downregulates cellular dsRNA levels. Modifying adenosines to inosines in RNA duplexes has been predicted to disrupt or destabilize dsRNA structures by computational tools and in vitro biochemical methods,17,32–34 but until now it has been unclear how editing impacts dsRNAs in a living cell. By measuring dsRNA levels directly in cells expressing an editing-dead form of endogenous ADAR1, we confirmed that ADAR1 editing is required to decrease double-strandedness in cellular RNAs, confirming a long-held model. It is important to mention that we cannot differentiate whether A-to-I edits just introduce mismatches in otherwise intact RNA duplexes or whether A-to-I edits completely destabilize RNA duplexes, as both scenarios would reduce J2 binding to edited RNAs. More specific RNA-structure-probing studies are required to differentiate these two models.

We also demonstrate that ADAR1 can suppress dsRNA formation in an editing-independent manner. How ADAR1 reduces dsRNA burden without editing dsRNAs warrants further investigation. Both in vivo and in vitro studies have shown that ADAR1 can bind individual Alu elements.25,78,84 We speculate that such binding may prevent individual Alus from pairing with their inverted counterparts, thereby limiting the formation of long dsRNA-producing inverted Alu repeat duplexes. Alternatively, since ADAR1 is known to interact with helicases,85–87 it is possible that ADAR1 recruits helicases to unwind dsRNAs. Importantly, it is also possible that the Z-DNA/RNA binding domain on the N terminus of ADAR1p150 keeps dsRNAs in Z-RNA form, which would reduce the levels of typical right-handed A-RNA that can be detected by the J2 antibody and dsRNA-sensing PRRs. Lastly, ADAR1-editing-independent control of dsRNAs provides valuable insight for boosting cancer immunotherapy. Since ADAR1 inhibition activates various PRRs in tumors and in blood cells, ADAR1 is considered an attractive therapeutic target for potentiating cancer immunotherapy.42,88,89 Many ADAR1 inhibitors in development aim to target ADAR1-editing activity,90 but our findings suggest that inhibitors that can block both ADAR1-editing-dependent and -independent modes of action may be most effective in cancer immunotherapy.

By applying dsRNA-seq to WT and ADAR1-deficient cells, we were able to define the dsRNA transcripts specifically regulated by ADAR1. Our dsRNA-seq analysis confirmed that Alu elements in mRNA non-coding regions, which are major sites for ADAR1 editing, indeed constitute a major fraction of ADAR1-regulated dsRNAs, as has been predicted.91,92 Surprisingly, dsRNA-seq also demonstrated that mt-dsRNAs are major classes of RNAs regulated by ADAR1. When mt-dsRNA fold change is analyzed in WT vs. ADAR1 KO cells, there is a mild (less than 2-fold), statistically significant increase in mt-dsRNAs in ADAR1 KO cells (Figure 4A). However, when dsRNA total transcript level differences are compared between WT and ADAR1 KO cells (Figure 4B), due to the sheer abundance of mt-dsRNAs compared to other dsRNA-harboring transcripts it becomes evident that mt-dsRNAs are major contributors to the increased dsRNA levels in ADAR1 KO cells. In the past, ADAR1-regulated dsRNAs were mainly predicted by identifying sites of A-to-I editing. ADAR1 regulation of mt-dsRNAs may have been overlooked due to the lack of edits in mt-dsRNAs.

We demonstrate that ADAR1p150, and not ADAR1p110, is required for mt-dsRNA regulation (Figures S5G–S5I). We were not able to detect any clear A-to-I editing events in mitochondrial RNAs, but, surprisingly, our mass spectrometry analysis and an independent high-confidence human mitochondrial proteome study both suggest that ADAR1 can localize to the mitochondria.72 Based on these observations, we carefully speculate that a small fraction of ADAR1p150 localizes to the mitochondria and can regulate mt-dsRNA formation through an editing-independent mechanism. How ADAR1 localizes to the mitochondria and how ADAR1 controls mt-dsRNAs warrants further investigation. Since ADAR1p150 downregulates mt-dsRNAs, it is possible that the N-terminal Z-DNA/RNA domain unique to ADAR1p150 plays a role in mt-dsRNA regulation.

We also evaluated the functional significance of elevated mt-dsRNAs in ADAR1 KO cells. Notably, we found that ADAR1 deficiency in human cells causes higher sensitivity to X-ray irradiation, suggesting that ADAR1 inhibition can potentiate cancer radiation therapy. Moreover, mt-dsRNAs contributed to inflammation in irradiated ADAR1 KO cells, indicating that mitochondrial stress combined with ADAR1 inhibition can further potentiate cancer immunotherapy.

In conclusion, ADAR1 regulation of self-dsRNAs appears to be much more complex and far reaching than previously thought. We reveal that ADAR1 uses editing-dependent and -independent mechanisms to regulate self-dsRNAs and also uncover a novel connection between ADAR1 and the mitochondria. These findings open new questions regarding ADAR1’s mechanism and function in regulating cellular dsRNAs and the immune landscape.

Limitations of the study

While the J2 antibody is regarded as a gold standard reagent for detecting dsRNAs, it is limited to binding right-handed A form RNA and likely does not bind left-handed Z-form RNA.40 Since A-RNA is the predominant default configuration of dsRNA, the J2 antibody is still a great tool for assessing overall dsRNA levels. However, it is important to note that a more conservative interpretation of our data is that ADAR1 suppresses the abundance of A-form RNA.

While our study reveals that ADAR1p150 suppresses mt-dsRNAs, the precise mechanism underlying this regulation remains unclear. Although our mass spectrometry and mitochondrial purification assays detected a small fraction of ADAR1p150 within mitochondrial fractions, we cannot fully rule out that cytosolic ADAR1 is contaminating mitochondrial fractions. Further experimentation, including high-resolution microscopy, will help confirm ADAR1p150 localization to the mitochondria. It is also important to elucidate how ADAR1p150 may access the mitochondria. The mitochondrial targeting sequences (MTS) are cleavable short peptide sequences on the N terminus of proteins—often composed of a stretch of positively charged amino acids—that help proteins locate and enter the mitochondria. We could not detect a robust MTS in ADAR1p150. However, many mitochondrial proteins are not synthesized with a cleavable MTS but rather contain mitochondria-targeting information within regions of the mature protein. Intriguingly, there is a relatively strong internal MTS (iMTS) on the N terminus of ADAR1p150 that is present in ADAR1p150 but not in ADAR1p110 when analyzed by the iMTS-L prediction tool.93 Further experimentation will be required to test whether this region facilitates ADARp150 import into the mitochondria. Lastly, if ADAR1p150 localizes to the mitochondria, why can we not detect A-to-I edits in mt-dsRNAs? This is an unresolved question. It is possible that edited mt-dsRNAs are rapidly degraded, or the mitochondrial local environment may not support efficient editing. For instance, ADAR1 concentration in the mitochondria may be too low to promote the ADAR1 dimerization required for efficient editing. Alternatively, the mitochondrial environment may not support sufficient amounts of co-factors, such as inositol hexakisphosphate, which has been shown to be required for ADAR editing.94

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Cell lines and culture conditions

ADAR1 KO HEK-293T cells, ADAR1 KO HUES8 hESCs, and their corresponding WT controls were generated as previously described.14 WT, ADAR1p150 KO, and ADAR1 KO HeLa cells were kindly provided by R. Cattaneo (Mayo Clinic). For Hap1 cells, genome editing was performed on haploid cells. After genetic engineering, diploid cells that emerged through subculture were selected and expanded to establish cell stocks, which were subsequently used in experiments. ADAR1 KO Hap1 cells were generated using CRISPR-Cas9 and the following guide RNA sequences: For clone #1: CAT GAG ATG TGA CTG CAC AG and GGA ATC CAT GAT GCC CAA CA, for clone #2: TTC TTG TAG GGT GAA CAC CG and TGA TTG GTT TTG GCC GTA CG. E912A KI Hap1 cells were generated using CRISPR-Cas9 with the following guide RNA sequence: GCC ATG CAG AAA TAA TCT CC. For clone #1 of the E912A KI Hap1 cells, a donor with a non-mutated PAM site was used in order to preserve the native ADAR1 sequence: AAG GAG ATT CTC TCA GCC TAA AAG GAG AAA CTG TCA ATG ACT GCC ATG CAG CCA TAA TCT CCC GGA GAG GCT TCA TCA GGT GAG CGA GGT CAG AGC TGT GGC CCG GCT GCC CGG. For clone #2, a donor with a mutated PAM site was used, which increased knock-in efficiency while preserving amino acid sequence: TCT CAG CCT AAA AGG AGA AAC TGT CAA TGA CTG CCA TGC AGC CAT AAT CTC CAG AAG AGG CTT CAT CAG GTG AGC GAG GTC AGA GCT GTG GCC CGG CTG CCC GG. Human Dermal Fibroblasts (HDF) were purchased from ATCC. HEK-293T and HeLa cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% nonessential amino acids. Hap1 cells were cultured in IMDM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% nonessential amino acids. HUES8 hESCs were cultured in mTESR1 with its supplement (Stem Cell Technologies) and were passaged using ReLeSR (Stem Cell Technologies). HDF were cultured in Fibroblast Basal Media supplemented with the Fibroblast Growth Kit- Low serum (ATCC). All cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. All cell lines were routinely tested and found to be free of mycoplasma contamination.

METHOD DETAILS

Cell treatments

For IFN-β stimulation, cells were treated with 0.5 nM IFN-β (PBL Assay Science). Ectopic expression of ADAR1 and the indicated ADAR1 mutants was performed using the pcDNA3.1+ backbone. siRNAs were purchased from Dharmacon (ON-TARGETplus siRNA targeting ADAR1, PNPT1, or SUV3). Cells were transfected with 100ng of the indicated plasmid or siRNA using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 48 h, cells were either harvested for downstream analysis or fixed for imaging. For polymerase inhibitor treatments, cells were treated for 12 h prior to harvest with either ActD (10μg/mL, Sigma), α-amanitin (25μg/mL, Sigma), or IMT1 (1μM, MedChemExpress). For mitochondrial herniation experiments, cells were treated for 24 h with emricasan (10μM, Selleckchem) and S63845 (2μM, Selleckchem), as previously demonstrated.80 For combined polymerase inhibition and mitochondrial herniation treatments, cells were treated with the indicated polymerase inhibitor (α-amanitin, 8 μg/mL, Sigma, or IMT1, 1μM, MedChemExpress) for 24 h, followed by co-treatment with emricasan (10μM, Selleckchem) and S63845 (2μM, Selleckchem) for an additional 24 h. For X-ray irradiation, WT and ADAR1 KO HeLa cells were seeded 48 h prior to irradiation. IMT1 (1μM, MedChemExpress) was added 12 h before irradiation by media change, and cells were exposed to X-rays using an X-RAD320 instrument (Precision). After irradiation, cells underwent a half media change with fresh medium containing IMT1, followed by an additional media refresh at day 3. Samples were collected at 3 or 6 days post-irradiation.

Sanger sequencing

E912A KI Hap1 cells were confirmed via Sanger sequencing with the following primer: ACA AAT AGC CTT CAT CCT GTT TTC CAT CGG TT. Loss of editing in E912A KI and ADAR1 KO cells was confirmed by sequencing a frequently edited region of ATM kinase using the following primer: GGC CCA CAG CAA CCT TCA CCT CCC AG.

Immunofluorescent microscopy

Cells grown on glass coverslips were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at 4°C. Samples were then blocked and permeabilized in PBTG (PBS, 1% bovine serum albumin, 0.1% Triton X-100, 10% goat serum) for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies were diluted in PBTG and stained overnight at 4°C. Secondary antibodies [either goat anti-mouse immunoglobulin G (IgG) Alexa Fluor 488 or goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 555, Invitrogen (A11001 and A21428)] and DAPI (40,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, Sigma) were diluted in PBTG and stained for 1 h at room temperature before mounting. Samples were imaged on an LSM-900 Airyscan confocal microscope. Where indicated, super-resolution images were taken with the Airyscan function as a z stack, and have been shown as maximum-intensity projections. The following primary antibodies were used: J2 (ExAlpha, 10010500), K1 (ExAlpha, 10020200), ADAR1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 81284), COX IV (abcam, ab33985), PNPT1 (abcam, ab96176), SUV3 (Bethyl, A303–055A), and GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology, 5174). RNASelect stain was obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific. For samples treated with nucleases, after fixation, there was a permeabilization step in 0.1% Triton X-100 for 1 h. After, samples were incubated for 30 min at 37°C with either ssRNase (RNase T1, 100U/ml, Thermo Fisher Scientific), dsRNase (RNase III, 40U/ml, New England Biolabs), DNase (RQ1, 40U/ml, Promega), or RNaseH (60U/mL, New England Biolabs) diluted in PBS containing 5mM magnesium chloride. Samples were then stained as described above. For samples treated with protease, after fixation, there was a permeabilization step in 0.1% Triton X-100 for 1 h. Samples were then incubated for 30 min at 37°C with protease (proteinase K, 0.004U/mL, Zymo Research) diluted in PBS before continuing with staining as described above.29

Image analysis

Images were processed and analyzed using ImageJ. Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of individual cells was determined by tracing cell outlines. MFI data depict 12 individual cells taken from at least three independent images. Colocalization of normal-resolution images was assessed using the JACop plugin. Colocalization analysis of super-resolution images was completed using Imaris. Additionally, Imaris was used to generate three-dimensional images from Z-stacks, with dsRNA objects being considered inside the mitochondria if they exhibited greater than 80% co-localization by volume.

Mitochondria isolation and proteinase K protection assay

Mitochondria isolation was performed as previously described using freshly prepared mitochondrial isolation buffer (IBc; 10mM Tris-MOPS, pH 7.4, 1mM Tris-EGTA, 200mM sucrose) was used.74 Confluent cells grown in a 150mm culture dish were rinsed with 10mL of PBS and detached by adding 7mL of PBS, followed by scraping with a cell scraper on ice. The cell suspension was transferred to a 15mL tube, and an additional 3mL of PBS was used to collect any remaining cells. One milliliter of the cell suspension was set aside for total lysate preparation, while the remaining cell suspension was centrifuged at 600g for 5 min at 4°C. After removing the PBS, the cell pellets were resuspended in 3mL cold IBc buffer and transferred to a homogenizer. Cells were lysed by 30 strokes with a Teflon pestle at 4°C. The cell lysates were centrifuged at 600g for 10 min at 4°C, and the resulting supernatants were further centrifuged at 7,000g for 10 min at 4°C. Mitochondrial pellets obtained at this step were resuspended in 300 μL of IBc buffer and divided into four tubes. Each tube was centrifuged at 7,000g for 10 min at 4°C to obtain mitochondrial pellets. For the proteinase K protection assay, mitochondrial pellets were resuspended in proteinase K buffer (30mM Tris-Cl, pH 7.4, 150mM NaCl) and treated with proteinase K (5 μg/mL or 50 μg/mL, Roche) for 20 min at 4°C. The reaction was terminated by adding 2mM PMSF (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The protein samples were prepared using NuPAGE LDS Sample Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 400mM dithiothreitol (Sigma) and directly subjected to Western blotting.

qRT-PCR

RNA was extracted directly from cell samples using the Direct-zol RNA Miniprep kit (Zymo Research). cDNA conversion was completed using the ProtoScript II First Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (New England Biolabs). PowerUp SYBR Green (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System were used for qRT-PCR, and all primers are listed in Table S9.

PKR co-immunoprecipitation

PKR immunoprecipitation (IP) was performed using Dynabeads Protein A (Thermo Fisher Scientific) conjugated with anti-PKR antibody (abcam). Confluent Hap1 cells (WT or E912A KI, mock- or IFN-β-treated) grown in four 15 cm dishes were washed once with ice-cold PBS, collected by scraping, counted, and lysed on ice in NP-40 lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-Cl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, 10% glycerol, 0.15% BSA) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). A total of 15 mL of cell suspension (13 × 106 cells/mL in lysis buffer) was incubated on ice for 30 min with vortexing every 10 min, followed by brief sonication (2 s ON/10 s OFF cycles; total ON time = 20 s). Insoluble debris was removed by centrifugation at 8,000 g for 10 min at 4°C, and the resulting supernatants were collected as total cell lysates. To exclude RNA-mediated interactions, 5 U/mL Universal Nuclease (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 25 μL of IgG-conjugated Protein A beads were added to 1 mL of lysate and incubated for 20 min at 4°C with rotation. After removal of IgG-conjugated beads, 50 μL of anti-PKR–conjugated Protein A beads were added to the lysates and incubated for 4 h at 4°C with rotation. Following incubation, beads were washed five times with cold PBS-T (PBS containing 0.02% Tween 20). Bound proteins were eluted by adding 70 μL of NuPAGE LDS Sample Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 400 mM dithiothreitol (Sigma) and incubating at 95°C for 10 min. Input samples were prepared in parallel from total cell lysates. All eluates were subjected to SDS-PAGE followed by immunoblotting analysis.

Western blot

Cell samples were lysed directly using NuPAGE LDS Sample Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 400 mM dithiothreitol (Sigma). Lysates were homogenized by passing through a 26-gauge needle and were denatured by boiling for 10 min. The protein samples were run on SUREPAGE BIS-TRIS, 4–12% gels (GenScript), and were blotted onto a nitrocellulose membrane. The following proteins were probed: ADAR1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 81284S), p-PKR (phospho-T451, abcam, ab227983), PKR (abcam ab32506), MAVS (Cell Signaling Technology, 3993S), TUBB (abcam, ab6046), COX IV (abcam, ab33985), Lamin A/C (abcam, ab108595), p-IRF3 (phospho-S386, abcam, ab76493), IRF3 (Cell Signaling Technology, 11904), and β-actin (Sigma, A3854). Blots were developed using a chemiluminescent method.

Extracellular acidification rates measurement (ECAR)

Cells were plated in XF96 cell culture microplates (Seahorse Biosciences) in appropriate growth media. After 24 h, growth media were changed to bicarbonate-free assay media (XF assay medium, Seahorse Biosciences, Cat# 102365–100) and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) was measured using an XF96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Seahorse Biosciences) under basal conditions and following addition of glucose (10mM), Oligomycin (1μM), and 2-deoxyglucose, 2DG (50mM), according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Oxygen consumption rates measurement (OCR)

Cells were plated in XF96 cell culture microplates (Seahorse Biosciences) in appropriate growth media. After 24 h, growth media were changed to bicarbonate-free assay media (XF assay medium, Seahorse Biosciences) and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured using an XF96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Seahorse Biosciences) under basal conditions or following the addition of Oligomycin (1μM), the uncoupling agent FCCP (0.5μM), and the electron transport inhibitor Rotenone (0.5μM), according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

RNA-seq library preparation

dsRNA preparation

Immunoprecipitation of dsRNA was performed with modifications based on a previous study.37 Freshly prepared buffers included NET-2 buffer (50 mM Tris-Cl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 0.5% NP-40), NP-40 lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-Cl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40), and high salt wash buffer (50 mM Tris-Cl, pH 7.4, 1 M NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS). Protein G Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were washed and resuspended in NET-2 buffer. J2 antibody (15 μg) was added to 100 μL of beads and incubated for 1 h at room temperature on a rotator. Hap1 cells (1 × 107; WT, E912A KI_clone #1, ADAR1 KO_clone #1) were lysed with 300 μL of NP-40 lysis buffer supplemented with RNasin Plus (80 mU/μL, Promega) and cOmplete protease inhibitor (Roche), then incubated on ice for 5 min. Lysates were centrifuged at 17,000g for 5 min at 4°C. RNA for total RNA-seq was extracted from 50 μL of lysate using Trizol reagent. For dsRNA immunoprecipitation, 250 μL of lysate was diluted with 750 μL of NET-2 buffer containing 12 U of RQ1 RNase-free DNase (Promega) and 10 mM MgCl2. 100 μL of J2-Dynabeads were added and rotated for 2 h at 4°C. Beads were washed twice with high salt wash buffer and twice with NET-2 buffer, and dsRNA was extracted using Trizol reagent.

Total RNA-seq library preparation

For total RNA-seq library preparation, RNA was extracted with the Direct-zol RNA MiniPrep (Zymo Research) from Trizol solution dissolving total cell lysate. To remove cytoplasmic rRNAs (5S, 5.8S, 18S, and 28S), the RiboMinus Eukaryote Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used, while mitochondrial ribosomal RNAs (12S and 16S) were retained. Libraries were prepared with the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs) using multiplex oligos (New England Biolabs).

dsRNA-seq library preparation

For dsRNA-seq library preparation, RNA was extracted from the Trizol solution in which the bead complexes of the above dsRNA preparations were dissolved. RNA was purified using ethanol precipitation with GlycoBlue Coprecipitant (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The resulting blue RNA pellets were reconstituted in nuclease-free water and spiked with ERCC RNA Spike-In Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs) with multiplex oligos (New England Biolabs).

Sequencing

The final libraries were pooled and sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform with 150nt paired-end reads.

Sequencing data analysis

Preprocessing and alignment

After quality control using FastQC v0.11.5,98 adaptor sequences and low-quality reads were trimmed from FASTQ files with fastp v0.20.1.99 Preprocessed reads were aligned to the human genome (hg38) using STAR v2.7.3a to generate BAM files.100

Gene expression comparison

Read quantification was performed using featureCounts v1.6.4 with either the GENCODE v38 primary assembly or the RepeatMasker annotation file.101,102 For normalization and identification of differentially expressed genes, analyses were conducted using the DESeq2 package in Bioconductor with log2 fold change shrinkage via the ashr method.67,106 Normalized reads were converted to FPM values using the fpm() function in DESeq2. Genes with a mean FPM value less than 1 in total RNA-seq across all genotypes were considered low-count genes and excluded from further analysis. For total RNA-seq gene expression comparisons, genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2(Fold Change in FPM)| > 1 were considered differentially expressed genes (DEG). For enrichment analysis of dsRNA-seq compared to total RNA-seq, genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2(Fold Change in FPM)| > 1 were classified as enriched genes (Fold Change in FPM >2) or depleted genes (Fold Change in FPM <0.5).

dsRNA quantity comparison

In the dsRNA-seq library, ERCC RNA reads were separately counted and used to calculate the normalization factor with the estimateSizeFactors() function in the DESeq2 package. nFPM values were generated by normalizing read counts using the ERCC RNA-derived size factor. Genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered differential genes.

Alternative polyadenylation site analysis

Uniquely mapped reads in BAM files were converted to bedgraph files using bedtools v2.30.0.103 DaPars v2.1 was used to infer alternative polyadenylation sites from the bedgraph files and to calculate the percentage of distal polyadenylation usage index (PDUI).55,56 Only genes with PDUI values measured in all replicate samples were included in the statistical analysis. Significant 3′UTR changes were defined as alterations with false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2(Fold Change in PDUI)| > 0.59, with FDR values determined using a two-way ANOVA with Tukey corrected multiple comparisons.

Editing analysis

Preprocessing and alignment

FASTQ files were processed with cutadapt v4.9 to trim low-quality reads, adapter sequences, and truncated reads.104

Standard editing detection

To facilitate parallel processing, the human reference genome (hg38) was divided into chunks spanning roughly 100,000 nucleotides, with care being taken to avoid placing chunk boundaries within annotated genes. Reads were first aligned to the reference using STAR v2.7.9a.100 Afterward, non-unique alignments were discarded, reads were separated by strand orientation, and reverse reads were realigned to the antisense genome for stranded mutation quantification. Read:reference mismatches were then identified using SEISMIC-RNA v0.24.3.52 Base calls were excluded if they fell within 4 nucleotides of the 5′ end or 6 nucleotides of the 3′ end of a read. All remaining read:reference mismatches for base calls with a Phred score ≥25 were kept for downstream filtering. Reads with fewer than 95% of base calls unambiguously aligned and positions covered by fewer than 5 reads were then discarded. Following this, the final read:reference mismatches were tabulated. Potential edit sites were defined as those where at least 5% of base calls differed from the reference base with a minimum of 2 supporting reads. ADAR1-associated edit sites were called when an editing event was present in at least one WT replicate but absent in all editing-deficient replicates (ADAR1 KO or E912A KI).

Hyper-editing detection

For hyper-editing detection, reads were aligned using STAR v2.7.9a.100 against a standard, non-chunked human reference genome (hg38). Unaligned reads were then processed as previously reported.73 The hyper-editing detection pipeline is available on GitHub (https://github.com/hagitpt/Hyper-editing). In brief, the reference genome was transformed to mask each of the 12 types of substitutions (A-to-G, A-to-C, C-to-A, C-to-T, C-to-G…) using TransformIndexBWA_genome.sh. Then, unaligned reads were realigned against the corresponding masked reference using BWA 0.7.18-r1243-dirty.105 For each set of alignments, the substitutions were then unmasked, revealing read:reference mismatches. ADAR1-associated hyper-edited positions were called if they appeared in at least one WT replicate and no editing deficient replicate (ADAR1 KO or E912A KI).

Mass spectrometry

Sample preparation

Total cell pellets and mitochondrial fractions from WT and ADAR1 KO cells were prepared using the procedures described above and submitted to the Columbia Proteomics and Structural Biology Core. Mass spectrometry sample preparation was carried out as previously reported.107,108

Liquid Chromatography with Tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)

Two hundred nanograms of each peptide sample were analyzed on a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics) coupled to a nanoElute LC system (Bruker Daltonics). Peptides were separated on a reversed-phase C18 column with an integrated CaptiveSpray emitter (IonOpticks, 25 cm ×75 μm, 1.6 μm) using a 60-min gradient from 25% to 40% solvent B (ionopticks_25cm_60-min_25–40B_Short_v1.m) at a flow rate of 400 nL/min. Mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B was 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile.

diaPASEF acquisition

Mass spectrometry data were acquired in diaPASEF mode (dia-pasef-standard_DemoLab_NarrowWindow_50ms), with 32 isolation windows covering m/z 100–1700 and an accumulation/ramp time of 50 ms.

Protein identification and data analysis

Acquired diaPASEF raw files were analyzed using DIA-NN (v1.9) in library-free mode. Searches were performed against the reviewed human reference proteome (UniProt, UP000005640) supplemented with a contaminant database (cRAP). Trypsin/P digestion was specified with up to one missed cleavage. The precursor m/z range was set to 300–1300 and the fragment m/z range to 200–1800. Peptides with lengths between 7 and 30 amino acids and precursor charge states from 1 to 5 were included. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine residues (+57.021 Da) was set as a fixed modification, while oxidation of methionine (+15.995 Da) and protein N-terminal acetylation (+42.011 Da) were set as variable modifications. The false discovery rate (FDR) at both peptide precursor and protein levels was controlled at 1%. In protein group tables generated by DIA-NN, proteins lacking at least two quantitative values within each condition were excluded from downstream analysis to minimize low-intensity artifacts. To account for systematic variation, normalized by quantile normalization using NormalizerDE.97 Remaining protein abundances were normalized by quantile normalization, and differential expression analysis was performed on normalized intensity values.97

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Except for RNA sequencing and mass spectrometry data analyses, all other statistical analyses were performed using Graphpad Prism 10 software. The data represent the mean ± SD, and the type of statistical test is indicated in the corresponding legend. The differences were considered significant by Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test, or one- or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.
