Single-cell analysis of intermediate horseshoe bat (Rhinolophus affinis) organs reveals viral infections and antiviral immune signatures
Authors: Tianhang Lv, Jianhui Zhang, Zhiwan Zheng, Xiaohui Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBats are recognized for harboring a diverse array of viruses without manifesting disease symptoms. In this study, we explored the single-cell atlas of wild Rhinolophus affinis (R. affinis) using Viral-Track and identified transcript expression of eight viral species in the R. affinis lung and three viral species in the kidney. Within the R. affinis lung, these viruses were detected across all cell types except ciliated cells. Compared to uninfected cells, virus-infected cells exhibited activation of pathways associated with protein synthesis, tissue repair, and immune responses, as evidenced by increased expression of corresponding genes. Ligand-receptor based analysis revealed that viral infection reshapes intercellular communication networks in the bat lung, with infected fibroblasts and infected proliferative T cells exhibiting enhanced signaling linked to tissue remodeling and immune activation. Through gene module analysis, we identified an immune cell activation-related module by high expression of CD14, CD74, and MRC1, as well as an antiviral related module by elevated expression of SAMHD1, SLC11A1, TYROBP, and IL18 in R. affinis pulmonary macrophages. Additionally, a cross-species single-cell transcriptomic comparative analysis demonstrated that R. affinis pulmonary macrophages exhibit elevated expression of pro-inflammatory genes (IRF9, DDX5, IL6ST and ITGA4), which are associated with antiviral activity and immune activation, and anti-inflammatory genes (IRF2, PTPRE and GPR65), which play critical roles in mitigating excessive immune responses. Compared to the other species, R. affinis pulmonary macrophages exhibited upregulation of genes enriched in pathways related to vacuolar acidification and negative regulation of response to external stimulus. These findings suggest that the R. affinis lung possesses a unique immune system that enables it to balance immune responses during viral infections, thereby preventing excessive immune damage and maintaining lung tissue homeostasis. Introduction

Bats are recognized as hosts for an extensive array of viruses, including numerous highly pathogenic strains such as coronaviruses [1], lyssaviruses [2], and filoviruses [3]. Despite this, bats exhibit a remarkable resilience to these viruses, rarely manifesting symptoms [4]. This phenomenon has attracted considerable attention, as uncovering the immune mechanisms that enable bats to tolerate high viral loads without developing disease could yield valuable insights into viral pathogenesis and antiviral immunity [5]. The unique immune system of bats, characterized by their ability to modulate antiviral responses while preventing excessive inflammation, is believed to play a pivotal role in their coexistence with viruses [6]. This immune tolerance is hypothesized to arise from an interplay of molecular and cellular adaptations that collectively regulate the antiviral response while preventing immunopathology. Significantly, bats exhibit constitutive expression of type I interferons (IFNs), allowing them to maintain an “always-on” antiviral state without the harmful effects typically associated with prolonged IFN activation [7]. In addition, bats exhibit dampened activation of inflammasomes such as NLRP3, thereby avoiding excessive inflammation and pyroptosis [8]. They also display modifications in DNA sensing pathways, including altered STING signaling, which reduces the inflammatory response to cytosolic DNA while preserving antiviral defense [9]. Furthermore, adaptations in natural killer (NK) cell receptors and reduced expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines have been reported, collectively contributing to an immune system that balances viral control with inflammation suppression [5]. These unique features suggest that bats have evolved specialized mechanisms to tolerate high viral loads without developing severe pathology, offering valuable insights into host-virus coevolution and immune homeostasis [6].

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology, characterized by its capacity to profile gene expression at the single-cell resolution, represents a robust tool for elucidating complex biological systems [10]. By harnessing the distinctive advantages of this technology, researchers have successfully constructed cell atlases for various bat species, such as R. affinis [11] and Rhinolophus sinicus (R. sinicus) [12], thereby facilitating an in-depth analysis of the diverse cell types within these organisms. The resulting bat cell atlases serve as crucial reference resources for investigating antiviral immunity in bats. Recent studies have further utilized scRNA-seq technology to explore the immune landscape of bats, revealing distinct patterns of immune-related gene expression and cell types. For instance, a study employed scRNA-seq to analyze the immune response in Eonycteris spelaea (E. spelaea) lungs during infection with Pteropine orthoreovirus, identifying key myeloid cell population post-infection [13]. Another study conducted a comparative single-cell transcriptomic analysis to generate atlases of Artibeus jamaicensis (A. jamaicensis) intestine and mesentery, demonstrating that infection with the H18N11 virus elicits a moderate immune response in various leukocytes, such as macrophages, B cells, and NK/T cells, and highlighting the zoonotic potential of the virus [14]. Collectively, these studies provide insights into the cellular mechanisms underlying antiviral immunity in bats.

In addition to characterizing gene expression across diverse cell types, single-cell RNA-seq data can be utilized to identify viral infections through the application of Viral-Track [15], a computational tool designed to detect viral RNA and classify infected cells based on their transcriptomic profiles. In contrast to conventional scRNA-seq pipelines that typically discard unmapped reads, Viral-Track recovers these sequences and realigns them to a curated database of viral genomes. Following quality filtering and quantification at the single-cell level, the method differentiates infected from uninfected cells in an unsupervised manner. Viral-Track has exhibited high sensitivity across various multiple infection models, including COVID-19 [15], chronic hepatitis B [16], and γ-herpesvirus [17]. Its ability to recover viral transcripts renders it especially valuable for studies involving bats. Inspired by its versatility, we applied Viral-Track to the single-cell atlas of wild R. affinis to investigate potential viral expression.

R. affinis, an insectivorous species belonging to the family Rhinolophidae, has previously been studied in the context of coronavirus ecology and immune response, rendering it a suitable, though not fully comprehensive, model for studying bat-specific antiviral features [11, 18]. To investigate the host response to viral infection, we performed comparative analyses of infected and uninfected cells within R. affinis samples. Furthermore, we conducted a cross-species comparison using single-cell transcriptomic data from R. affinis, human (Homo sapiens), mouse (Mus musculus), and cynomolgus monkey (Macaca fascicularis). These species were selected based on their immunological and evolutionary relevance: human as the primary concern for zoonotic transmission, mouse as a widely used model organism for studying immune function, and monkey (non-human primates) as closer phylogenetic relatives to human. This comparative approach enabled us to identify R. affinis-specific immune adaptations by contrasting immune cell states and gene expression profiles across diverse mammalian lineages. Materials and methods

Data resource

We obtained the single-cell atlas data of R. affinis (including raw FASTQ files and gene expression matrices with cell type annotations) from the China National GeneBank database (accession number: CNP0001406). The dataset comprises seven organs: brain, heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and stomach, with 4–6 libraries generated for each organ. Of note, the tissue samples were derived from a single wild-caught individual collected from a cave in Guangdong Province, China. The host reference genome used for alignment was GCA_001888835.1. High-quality viral genomes and their corresponding annotation data were retrieved from the viruSITE database [19]. Single-cell lung atlases for Mus musculus [20], Macaca fascicularis [21] and Homo sapiens [22] were also obtained to enable comparative analysis. To better contextualize our study in R. affinis and evaluate whether analogous immune features are present in other bat lineages, we further integrated published scRNA-seq datasets from two additional bat species: R. sinicus [23] and E. spelaea [13].

All scRNA-seq data underwent standard quality control and preprocessing steps, including filtering of low-quality cells. While these steps may result in limited cell loss, the retained data showed sufficient coverage and quality for robust downstream analysis. No substantial data loss or batch effects were detected in the core lung dataset used for downstream analysis, ensuring the reliability of the results.

de novo detection of viral expression sequences

We utilized Viral-Track to process the raw FASTQ files from the scRNA-seq dataset. Viral-Track identifies reads that fail to align to the host reference genome and subsequently maps them to viral genomes. By employing built-in statistical methods, Viral-Track determines the species origin of these reads based on viral genome coverage and sequence complexity metrics.

Viral sequence classification using Kraken2

To cross-validate the viral detection results obtained using Viral-Track, we also employed Kraken2 [24], a k-mer-based taxonomic classification tool capable of profiling taxa across bacteria, archaea, viruses, and eukaryotes. The pre-built viral genome database provided by Kraken2, which is derived from NCBI RefSeq viral sequences, was used as the reference. In contrast to Viral-Track, which identifies infections based on the abundance and complexity of aligned viral transcripts, Kraken2 classifies all sequencing reads through exact k-mer matches to the reference database, thereby enabling the detection of even low-abundance viral sequences. Kraken2 was executed with the following key parameters: “–confidence 0.2” to ensure stringent taxonomic assignment, and “–minimum-base-quality 20” to minimize classification errors caused by sequencing artifacts. FASTQ files from each sample were used as input. Subsequently, Kraken2 produced comprehensive taxonomic classification reports detailing the identity, abundance, and hierarchical lineage of identified viral sequences.

scRNA-seq data analysis

Seurat [25] was utilized for scRNA-seq data processing. For each organ, data integration across libraries was conducted using the reciprocal PCA method implemented in Seurat. Dimensionality reduction was performed using RunPCA function, followed by application of Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was applied for visualization purposes. The preprocessing workflow adhered to established protocols [26, 27], ensuring reliable integration and clustering of single-cell data from multiple libraries.

Differentially expressed gene (DEG) and gene ontology (GO) enrichment

DEGs were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with significance thresholds set at adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log₂FC|> 1. GO enrichment analysis was performed using Metascape [28] and the clusterProfiler R package [29], both of which apply multiple hypothesis correction (Benjamini–Hochberg method) to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Only terms with adjusted p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Enrichment terms were filtered using adjusted p-values, and only those with FDR < 0.05 were retained.

Cell–cell interaction (CCI) analysis

CellPhoneDB [30] was employed to perform CCI analysis based on the ligand-receptor expression levels among cell types, with a minimum cell number threshold of 10 for each cell type to be included in the analysis.

Gene module analysis

Hotspot [31] was used to perform gene module analysis based on gene–gene pairwise correlation with default parameters. The distribution of gene modules across different cell types was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with adjusted p-values < 0.05 as the significance threshold. In addition, to assess the cell type specificity of module informative genes, we conducted statistical evaluations using multiple approaches, including the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, MAST, and negative binomial models. This method combination was adopted to ensure robustness across genes with varying expression levels, as the Wilcoxon test alone may lack sensitivity for lowly expressed genes due to data sparsity. Genes with adjusted p-values < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched and defined as cell type-specific.

Cross-species comparison analysis of mammalian lung tissues

To enhance signal robustness and minimize dropout effects in single-cell RNA-seq data, we applied hdWGCNA [32] to aggregate cells into meta-cells within each annotated cluster. This approach generates transcriptomic profiles by averaging neighboring cells with similar gene expression across neighboring cells with similar molecular signatures, thereby improving the stability and reliability of expression patterns. For cross-species comparisons, meta-cell aggregation serves to reduce technical noise and harmonize expression scales across datasets derived from different sequencing platforms. This meta-cell approach has been increasingly utilized in recent comparative single-cell studies across species and platforms, where it enhances the detection of conserved transcriptional programs while reducing confounding factors such as batch effects and variations in sequencing depth [22, 33]. By implementing this strategy, we achieved more robust interspecies comparisons and identify both conserved and R. affinis-specific gene co-expression modules associated with antiviral immunity. Homologous genes across species were determined using the biomaRt package [34]. Following normalization and scaling of gene expression data, only one-to-one orthologous genes were included in the cross-species analysis to ensure biological relevance and comparability. Comparative scRNA-seq analysis of homologous genes was subsequently conducted using the SCP framework [35], based on the meta-cell representation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance is denoted by asterisks as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, and "ns" denotes no significance.

Visualization of analysis results

Data visualization was performed using plotting functions from Seurat, SCP, and ggplot2 [36].

Results

Transcripts from eight viral species were found in the lung and kidney of R. affinis

We processed raw scRNA-seq FASTQ data from seven organs of R. affinis using Viral-Track. Viral-Track initially aligned the reads to the bat reference genome (Fig. S1). Reads that failed to align to the host genome were subsequently mapped to the high-quality viral reference genomes. Based on viral genome coverage and sequence complexity of the viral-aligned reads, Viral-Track assesses whether individual cells are infected by specific viruses. We quantified the viral load across various organs by calculating the proportion of viral reads relative to the total number of mapped reads in each tissue. As shown in Fig. 2a, viral transcripts were predominantly detected in the lung, which exhibited a viral load of 3.199%, significantly higher than that observed in other organs. The kidney displayed moderate viral loads (0.399%), whereas the brain, liver, stomach, heart, and spleen showed only minimal viral signals (< 0.2%). These results indicate that the lung represents the primary site of viral transcriptional activity among the analyzed tissues.

Using the built-in statistical algorithms of Viral-Track, we further analyzed sequencing reads aligned to viral genomes. In the lung, Viral-Track identified expression sequences corresponding to eight viral species (Fig. 2b): Human endogenous retrovirus K113 (HERV-K113), Baboon endogenous virus M7 (BEV-M7), RD114 retrovirus (RD114), Simian virus 40 (SV40), Mus musculus mobilized endogenous polytropic provirus (MmEPPV), Woolly monkey sarcoma virus (WMSV), Abelson murine leukemia virus (AMLV), and Snyder-Theilen virus (STV) (Fig. 2c and Table S1). Among these, seven were classified as retroviruses (except for SV40), including four endogenous retroviruses: BEV-M7, HERV-K113, MMPV, and RD114 (Table 1). In the kidney, expression sequences from HERV-K113, BEV-M7 and SV40 were detected (Fig. 2d).

Overview of detected viruses in R. affinis scRNA-seq data

We analyzed the genomic coverage of viral RNA in the lung. The RNA expression of HERV-K113 spanned the entire genome, with predominant enrichment observed in the gag-pro-pol region (Fig. 2e, S2g). Similarly, RD114, BEV-M7 and WMEV exhibited primary enrichment in the gag-pro-pol and gag-pol regions, which are crucial for viral assembly, maturation, and replication (Fig. 2f-h). Furthermore, SV40 RNA expression in the lung predominantly localized to the gp6 region (Fig. 2i), which is associated with the viral antigenicity and infectivity. The gp6 region resides within the coding sequence of the large T antigen [37], a multifunctional protein that facilitates cell cycle progression through interactions with key regulatory proteins in host cells, thereby promoting viral replication [38]. The genomic distribution patterns of other viruses in the lung, along with the unique reads distributions and genomic coverage profiles in kidney libraries, are shown in Fig. S2a-f.

In a recent study by Li et al. [39], a comprehensive virome survey of R. affinis was performed using metagenomic sequencing across multiple tissues, identifying 17 distinct virus families, including Astroviridae, Flaviviridae, Anelloviridae, Herpesviridae, Parvoviridae, Picornaviridae, Papillomaviridae, Coronaviridae, Caliciviridae, Picobirnaviridae, Retroviridae, Circoviridae, Hepeviridae, Poxviridae, Reoviridae, Polyomaviridae, and Paramyxoviridae. Their methodology, based on total DNA extraction and deep sequencing, enabled detection of low-abundance or non-transcribing viruses, thereby revealing an extensive viral reservoir within R. affinis. However, upon comparison of our findings with those reported by Li et al., we found only limited overlap among the 17 virus families identified in their metagenomic data, only Retroviridae and Polyomaviridae were detected in our study using Viral-Track.

To further assess the viral content in the scRNA-seq data from R. affinis, we supplemented the Kraken2 [24], a high-performance k-mer based metagenomic classifier that utilizes the complete NCBI RefSeq viral database. In contrast to Viral-Track, which relies on the abundance and complexity of viral transcripts for detection, Kraken2 classifies all sequencing reads irrespective of their expression status, thereby enabling the identification of viral sequences even at low abundance. This feature renders Kraken2 particularly suitable for family-level taxonomic analysis.

Using Kraken2, we reanalyzed the raw sequencing data from the same seven organs. In total, Kraken2 identified viral sequences from 10 virus families across tissues, with Retroviridae, Polyomaviridae, and Herpesviridae overlapping with the 17 virus families identified in the metagenomic study by Li et al. (Fig. S3a). The lung samples exhibited the highest cumulative viral read count, consistent with the organ distribution observed in the Viral-Track results.

To evaluate the robustness and generalizability of viral detection in bat single-cell datasets, we extended the application of Kraken2 to two publicly available bat scRNA-seq datasets: E. spelaea lung from Gamage et al. [13], R. sinicus lung from Wang et al. [23]. In the E. spelaea lung dataset, Kraken2 identified reads corresponding to Polydnaviriformidae, Retroviridae, and Steitzviridae (Fig. S3b). In the R. sinicus lung dataset, four virus families were detected, including Herpesviridae, Polydnaviriformidae, Poxviridae, and Retroviridae (Fig. S3c). Notably, all virus families detected in these datasets were also present in R. affinis data, highlighting the consistency of viral community profiles across different bat species.

To provide contextualization for our findings, we compared the virus families identified in our study with those reported in Li et al.’s metagenomic analysis of R. affinis, as well as with the viral profiles detected in two additional single-cell RNA-seq datasets from Gamage et al. and Wang et al. As shown in the Venn diagram (Fig. S3d), three virus families (Retroviridae, Herpesviridae, and Polyomaviridae) were common to both our study and Li et al.’s study.

Nevertheless, the consistent detection of multiple virus families across different methods and datasets enhances the biological validity of our findings. This observation indicates that, despite methodological variations, both active viral expression (identified by Viral-Track) and genomic signatures (detected by Kraken2) converge on a shared subset of virus families that may hold biological significance in the context of bat virome research.

Characteristics of infected cells in the R. affinis lung

Analysis results from Viral-Track suggest that R. affinis lung harbors multiple viruses. To further investigate the distribution of viruses across pulmonary cell types and virus-host interactions, we quantified infected cells from all lung libraries, identifying a total of 2,534 infected cells (Table S2). These cells were then intersected to single cell data obtained from the scRNA-seq analysis pipeline, revealing 359 shared infected cells. The distribution of these 359 infected cells is visualized on the UMAP 2D space (Fig. 3a-b). Infected cells were detected across all major pulmonary cell types except ciliated cells. Among these, alveolar type 2 (AT2) cells and macrophages exhibited the highest levels of infection, with each cell type containing more than 75 infected cells (Fig. 3c). However, infected cells did not form a distinct transcriptional cluster but were instead distributed across multiple cell types. Consequently, we did not pursue the identification or analysis of bystander cells in this study.

We further performed a comparison analysis between infected and uninfected cells. We identified DEGs in the infected cells across individual cell types using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (adjusted p < 0.05) compared to uninfected cells (Table S3). Notably, proliferative T cells, mesothelial cells, and club cells exhibited a significant number of elevated expressed genes in infected cells compared to uninfected cells (Fig. 3d). In infected proliferative T cells, immune-related genes such as IL7R and MRC1 were highly expressed. SFTA3, coding a surfactant protein which is critical for immune defense and antibacterial activity in the lung [40], was also highly expressed in infected club cells (Fig. 3d). This finding aligns with a study on COVID-19, which reported elevated SFTA3 expression in SARS-CoV-2-infected alveolar type 1 (AT1) cells and AT2 cells [41]. Additionally, OTUD4, which protects airway epithelial cells against cigarette smoke-induced damage by inhibiting apoptosis, regulating immune responses, and stabilizing PAI-1 (a key cytokine in fibrinolysis regulation) [42], was highly expressed in infected club cells.

Furthermore, we conducted GO enrichment analysis on these highly expressed genes in infected cells. In infected mesothelial cells, enriched pathways included integrin-mediated signaling, apoptosis, and the ERK1/2 cascade (Fig. 3e), suggesting activation of tissue repair and apoptotic pathways. In infected proliferative T cells, pathways associated with T cell proliferation and immune responses, such as interleukin signaling and TCR signaling, were significantly enriched (Fig. 3f). Meanwhile, in infected club cells, pathways related to immune responses, translation regulation, and protein processing and degradation were enriched (Fig. 3g).

Viral infection reshapes CCI networks in R. affinis lung

To investigate the impact of viral infection on intercellular signaling in the lung of R. affinis, we performed a ligand-receptor based CCI analysis using CellPhoneDB, with cells stratified according to their infection status (Infected vs. Uninfected).

Our results reveal that CCI is markedly enhanced among infected cell types, whereas interactions between infected and uninfected cells, as well as among uninfected cells themselves, remain comparatively limited in both frequency and strength (Fig. 4a, Table S4). We observed that viral infection resulted in a substantial increase in ligand-receptor interactions compared to those in uninfected cells, with infected fibroblasts, AT1 cells, mesothelial cells, and proliferating T cells exhibiting particularly strong connectivity with other cell types (Fig. 4b). These results indicate a comprehensive reorganization of intercellular communication networks in response to viral infection.

Arcs represent the number of significant ligand-receptor pairs between cell-type pairs; thicker and more saturated arcs indicate stronger communication. c Dot plot of ligand-receptor CCI between infected fibroblast and other infected cell types

We further examined the interaction landscape of infected fibroblast, which exhibited highly active and diverse signaling with both immune and structural cell types. Extracellular matrix (ECM)-related interactions such as PDGFA-PDGFRA, were significantly enriched in signaling from infected fibroblasts to both infected AT1 cells and infected mesothelial cells. These signaling pathways are strongly associated with fibroblast activation, adhesion, and matrix deposition, and are known to contribute to processes such as wound healing or fibrosis. The PDGFA-PDGFRA pathway, in particular, has been shown to promote fibroblast proliferation and fibrosis following viral lung infection [43]. Additionally, infected fibroblast participated in prominent collagen-integrin interactions, including COL1A1 and COL6A1 ligands binding to various integrin complexes. These interactions are essential for are critical for cell–matrix adhesion, mechanosensing, and ECM remodeling.

Infected proliferative T cells exhibited significantly enhanced interactions with epithelial cell types, including AT1, AT2, and Club cells, with enrichment observed in multiple immune-related pathways (Fig. S4). For example, JAG1-NOTCH1 interaction represents Notch signaling, a pathway involved in the regulation of T cell differentiation, activation, and epithelial homeostasis. The upregulation of this pathway following infection suggests a dual functional role for T cells in maintaining immune barrier integrity and facilitating epithelial repair [44]. Additionally, the LGALS9-CD44 interaction implies that infected proliferative T cells may adopt a regulated or immunosuppressive state, indicating that the immune response is tightly modulated to prevent excessive tissue damage [45].

Gene module and cross-species comparative analysis reveal antiviral immune signatures of macrophages in the R. affinis lung

Genes with similar functions or involvement in shared signaling pathways often exhibit coordinated expression patterns. To explore functional modules in the bat lung, particularly those related to antiviral immunity, we performed gene module analysis using the Hotspot pipeline. This analysis identified 18 distinct modules, among which Module 3 and Module 8 were significantly associated with immune-related functions (Fig. 5a-b and Fig S5a-b). Visualization of modules 3 and 8 on UMAP revealed their predominant enrichment in macrophages (Fig. 5a-b and Fig. S5a-d).

GO enrichment analysis of in Module 3 highlighted its association with pathways related to immune cell activation, inflammation, and immune responses (Fig. 5c). Key genes in Module 3, such as CD14 and CD74, are involved in pathogen recognition and T cell activation [46, 47], while MRC1 is associated with immune defense and inflammatory responses (Fig. 5d, Table S5) [48]. In contrast, Module 8 includes genes with established antiviral functions, such as SAMHD1, SLC11A1, TYROBP, IL18 (Fig. 5e, Table S5). Notably, increased expression of SAMHD1 has been observed in patients with COVID-19, suggesting its potential role in modulating immune responses and influencing disease progression and recovery [49]. To access whether the identified co-expression modules were associated with viral infection status, we compared module score distributions between infected and uninfected groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. This analysis revealed that none of the modules exhibited statistically significant global enrichment in infected cells after correction for multiple testing (Fig. S6). Only Module 11 showed nominal significance (p = 0.028), which did not remain significant following FDR correction. These results suggest that viral infection does not broadly alter transcriptional programs at the level of entire co-expression modules.

However, upon examining gene-level expression patterns within each module, we found that several module-informative genes, were significantly upregulated in infected cells (FDR < 0.05, |log₂FC|> 0.5) (Table S6). These genes were primarily located in Module 3, and included SAT1 (an interferon-stimulated gene involved in polyamine metabolism) [50] and EIF5B (components of RNA processing and translation machinery often exploited by viruses) [51]. Although infection status does not reshape overall module structure, it induces targeted upregulation of specific genes within certain functionally relevant modules.

Given the central role of macrophages in immune responses, we investigated whether their functions in the R. affinis lung are species-specific and contribute to unique antiviral immunity. We collected single-cell pulmonary atlas data from multiple mammalian species (R. affinis, Homo sapiens, Macaca fascicularis, and Mus musculus) and performed comparative analysis with homologous gene expression. To mitigate the effects of technical variability and single-cell data sparsity, we aggregated individual cells of the same type into meta cells using hdWGCNA (Fig. S7a-d). We then identified macrophage marker genes across the four species and conducted cross-species GO enrichment analysis (Table S7). Compared to the other species, R. affinis macrophages were enriched in pathways related to vacuolar acidification and negative regulation of responses to external stimuli (Fig. 5f). GO network analysis further indicated that these pathways occupy critical nodes in the network (Fig. 5g). Comparative gene expression analysis revealed that pro-inflammatory genes (IRF9, DDX5, IL6ST, ITGA4, and CSF1) and anti-inflammatory genes (IRF2, PTPRE and GPR65) were highly expressed in R. affinis macrophages but showed lower expression in the other species (Fig. 5h). IRF2 acts as an immune suppressor, inhibiting antiviral responses under certain conditions by competing with other transcription factors such as IRF1 for DNA binding sites, thereby regulating cytokine production [52]. In contrast, IRF9 plays a pivotal role in antiviral immunity, particularly in interferon responses, by synergizing with IRF1 or IRF3 to promote the transcription of antiviral genes such as OAS1 and PKR [53].

Discussion

We identified eight viral species in R. affinis lung tissue, including seven retroviruses such as HERV-K113, BEV-M7, and AMLV, and found that viral transcripts predominantly mapped to protein-coding regions (gag-pro-pol, gag-pol), suggesting potential viral replication activity.

Our observations indicate that the majority of viral RNA fragments are concentrated in the core protein-coding regions of the viral genome, specifically the gag-pro-pol and gag-pol regions. These genomic regions encode essential viral particle components such as capsid protein, membrane fusion protein, and integrase, suggesting that viral replication and assembly processes in the R. affinis lung are actively occurring. Interestingly, the viruses identified in the wild R. affinis in our study have also been reported in other bat studies. The earliest report of bats harboring endogenous viruses was a study from Brazilian free-tailed bats (Tadarida brasiliensis), which revealed that these bats are susceptible to endogenous retroviruses originating from various mammalian species [54]. Subsequent studies identified several endogenous retroviruses in bats that originated from other species, including BEV-M7 [55] and murine leukemia virus [56]. Yuan et al. [57] collected fecal samples from three insectivorous bat species (Hipposideros cineraceus, Hipposideros armiger and Taphozous melanopogon) and two frugivorous bat species (E. spelaea and Rousettus leschenaultii) in Yunnan Province, China, for metagenomic sequencing and RT-PCR experiments. They identified contigs of common viruses found in these bat species, and also discovered new viral contigs, including HERV-K113, AMLV, BEV-M7, simian leukemia virus and feline leukemia virus. However, as this study is based on fecal samples, it was not possible to determine the specific localization of these viruses within bats’ organs and tissues. Our scRNA-seq-based approach complements these findings by enabling tissue- and cell-type-specific localization of viral sequences and transcriptional profiling.

Li et al. previously conducted metagenomic sequencing on total DNA extracted from bat tissues and identified viral sequences belonging to 17 distinct virus families [39]. Although the Kraken2 results, including cross-species comparisons with E. spelaea and R. sinicus, support the robustness of our viral detection method, only a limited number of virus families overlapped with those reported by Li et al.’s study. Li et al. employed metagenomic sequencing of total DNA, an approach that allows for the detection of low-abundance or non-transcribed viral sequences. In contrast, our methodology relies on polyA-enriched single-cell RNA-seq, which specifically captures polyadenylated and actively transcribed viral RNAs. Many DNA viruses or latent viral species may lack polyadenylation or be expressed at levels below the detection threshold. Furthermore, the relatively shallow sequencing depth characteristic of single-cell datasets contributes to reduced sensitivity. These methodological differences likely explain the observed discrepancy.

We observed that virus-infected cells were present in nearly all lung cell types with the exception of ciliated cells. Comparative transcriptomic analysis revealed that, relative to uninfected cells, virus-infected cells exhibited activation in pathways associated with protein synthesis, tissue repair, and immune responses, as evidenced by elevated expression levels of corresponding genes. These findings suggest that the lung of wild R. affinis maintains a dynamic physiological equilibrium that facilitates adaptation to antiviral immunity.

Beyond cell-intrinsic responses, we investigated how viral infection affects intercellular signaling within the lung microenvironment. CCI analysis demonstrated substantial reorganization of communication networks in infected cells. Specifically, infected fibroblasts and proliferating T cells exhibited increased ligand-receptor interactions, particularly in pathways associated with extracellular matrix remodeling, epithelial regeneration, and immune regulation. These observations suggest that bats may modulate local signaling circuits during infection to facilitate effective immune responses while mitigating immunopathological damage.

We subsequently concentrated on the antiviral immune system of the R. affinis lung. Through gene module analysis, we identified two immune-related gene modules (Module 3 and Module 8). Module 3 showed elevated expression levels of genes associated with immune cell activation and immune responses, including CD14, CD74, and MRC1. Meanwhile, Module 8 exhibited increased expression of genes implicated in suppressing viral replication and enhancing antiviral immunity, such as SAMHD1, SLC11A1, TYROBP, and IL18. Previous research has confirmed that SAMHD1 plays a critical role in inhibiting viral replication and mitigating viral infection in COVID-19 patients [49]. Both gene modules were predominantly localized to lung macrophages.

Given the unique macrophage response signature in R. affinis, we further explored its evolutionary distinctiveness via cross-species single-cell comparisons. We conducted an in-depth cross-species single-cell transcriptomic comparative analysis involving R. affinis, human (Homo sapiens), mouse (Mus musculus), and Cynomolgus monkey (Macaca fascicularis) to elucidate the unique immune functions and gene expression patterns in the R. affinis lung. Our findings indicated that, compared to the other three species, R. affinis pulmonary macrophages exhibited significant enrichment in pathways related to vacuolar acidification, and negative regulation of response to external stimulus. Additionally, R. affinis demonstrated higher expression levels of coding genes for immune-suppressive transcription factors (IRF2, PTPRE and GPR65), and antiviral-related (IRF9, DDX5, IL6ST, ITGA4, and CSF1). These observations suggest that the R. affinis lung possesses a distinctive immune system, which may enable it to balance immune responses during viral infections while preventing excessive immune-mediated damage, thereby maintaining lung tissue homeostasis.

Although this study focuses on R. affinis, it is important to acknowledge that immune responses and mechanisms of viral tolerance can vary significantly across different bat species. Comparative immunological studies have identified both conserved and divergent features among bat lineages. For example, fruit bats (Pteropus alecto) show constitutive expression of type I IFNs, whereas microbats such as Myotis lucifugus rely more on inducible antiviral responses [58, 59]. Furthermore, differences in NK cell receptor repertoire, inflammasome regulation, and DNA sensing pathways have been documented across bat families, indicating that antiviral defense strategies may be shaped by distinct evolutionary and ecological pressures [5, 60, 61]. Therefore, while R. affinis serves as a valuable model for investigating host responses to endogenous and latent viral infections, generalization of these findings to the broader Chiroptera order should be approached with caution. Future studies incorporating a wider range of bat species and more comprehensive viral detection methodologies will be essential for advancing a holistic understanding of antiviral immunity and virus-host coevolution in bats.

Nonetheless, it is crucial to acknowledge that our current study has certain limitations. Firstly, we identified 2,534 infected cells using Viral-Track, only 359 of these overlapped with those identified by Cell Ranger. The partial overlap with Cell Ranger identified cells may reflect underestimation due to high viral RNA abundance lowering host genome alignment efficiency. While our analysis provides important insights into viral infection and immune responses in the R. affinis lung, we acknowledge that the scRNA-seq data used in this study was derived from a single wild-caught individual. This may limit the generalizability of our conclusions, as inter-individual variability could not be assessed. Nevertheless, given the challenges in obtaining high-quality single-cell data from wild bats—particularly species that are protected under conservation laws and subject to strict sampling regulations—this dataset remains a valuable resource for exploratory analysis. Future studies incorporating multiple individuals across different geographic locations and environmental conditions will be essential to validate and extend our findings.

By integrating virus detection, cell-type specific analysis, and cross-species comparisons, our study highlights a potentially unique immune adaptation in R. affinis: the ability to permit viral persistence while preserving tissue homeostasis. This dual strategy of mounting effective antiviral defense without inducing significant immunopathology may underlie bats’ role as asymptomatic viral reservoirs.
