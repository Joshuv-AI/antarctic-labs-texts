Manganese recycling from zinc anode slime via SO2 reductive leaching in an integrated copper–zinc hydrometallurgical plant
Authors: Shenghong Cheng, Shizhe Chen, Guanghua Chen, Sanping Liu, Kaixi Jiang, Bao Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractManganese is essential for zinc electrowinning but its recycling is challenging in oxide ore SX-EW circuits where P204 rejects Mn. This study proposes a novel manganese recycling strategy tailored for SX-EW streams involving the selective SO2 reductive leaching of zinc anode slime followed by impurity removal. By controlling leaching pH at 5.0 with lime, Mn recovery > 92% while Fe, Ni, Co co-dissolution is suppressed to < 1%, 3%, 12% respectively. Residual impurities in the spent electrolyte are further removed by neutralization and DMDTC chelation. This integrated process effectively recovers manganese while simultaneously eliminating harmful impurities (Cu, Fe, Co, Ni), offering a sustainable solution for Mn management in complex oxide ore hydrometallurgy. The purified electrolyte enabled production of Grade #1 zinc with Pb < 0.005%.Keywords: Zn electrowinning, Impurity removal, Mn recycling, Anode slime, SO2 reduction, DMDTCSubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

In zinc electrowinning, the quality of the electrolyte is paramount. Manganese ions Mn2+ are typically maintained at 3–15 g/L to oxidize and form a protective manganese dioxide MnO2 layer on Pb-Ag anodes, which extends anode life and prevents lead contamination in the cathode zinc1–4. However, the electrolyte is also susceptible to the accumulation of harmful impurities. Impurities such as copper, cobalt, nickel, and antimony can drastically reduce current efficiency and surface quality7–10. Cobalt is particularly detrimental; it lowers the hydrogen evolution overpotential and causes “burning plates,” forming holes in the cathode zinc, especially in the presence of germanium8. Therefore, a critical challenge in zinc hydrometallurgy is maintaining beneficial manganese levels while deeply removing harmful impurities.

In conventional zinc hydrometallurgical plants, the spent electrolyte containing 3–15 g/L Mn²⁺ is typically returned to the neutral leaching stage5,6. During this step, manganese dioxide (MnO₂) present in the zinc anode slime acts as an oxidant for ferrous ions, while Mn²⁺ itself is re‑dissolved and recycled back to the electrowinning circuit, thereby forming a closed manganese loop11,12. This conventional cycle effectively maintains the beneficial Mn²⁺ concentration required for forming a protective MnO₂ film on Pb‑Ag anodes, which extends anode life and prevents lead contamination of the cathode zinc13.

However, this conventional manganese recirculation is completely disrupted in a copper‑zinc oxide ore SX‑EW plant (Fig. 1). In such a plant, the zinc solvent extraction stage uses P204 (D2EHPA) as the extractant. P204 exhibits a very strong selectivity for Zn²⁺ over Mn²⁺ and almost completely rejects manganese from the loaded organic phase. Consequently, Mn²⁺ cannot be recycled back through the extraction circuit together with zinc. Moreover, returning any manganese‑bearing stream upstream to the leaching or copper extraction stages is highly undesirable, because even trace amounts of manganese can cause degradation of the copper extractant Lix984 (a mixture of 5‑nonylsalicylaldoxime and 2‑hydroxy‑5‑nonylacetophenone oxime)14,15. Such degradation leads to loss of extractant activity, increased reagent consumption, and potential emulsification problems. Therefore, a dedicated manganese management strategy is required for this type of SX‑EW plant, which is fundamentally different from that used in conventional zinc hydrometallurgy.

A closed‑loop manganese recycling route is developed for the first time for a Cu‑Zn SX‑EW plant, where manganese is recovered from zinc anode slime via SO₂ reductive leaching and returned directly to the electrowinning circuit.

Materials

XRD analysis of anode slime sample.

Figure 2 shows the XRD analysis of Soremi zinc anode slime, which is mainly composed of SrSO4 and MnO2. Among them, SrSO4 occupies the majority of the diffraction peaks, while the diffraction peak of MnO2 is not obvious, indicating that MnO2 is mainly amorphous.

Reagents

Sodium dimethyldithiocarbamate (DMDTC, AR) was purchased from Macklin. Sodium nitrite (NaNO₂, AR) and calcium oxide (CaO, AR) were obtained from Aladdin. Sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄, AR) was supplied by Orica. Industrial-grade lime with a purity of > 95% was self-produced on-site. All aqueous solutions were prepared using deionized water.

Experimental methods

During reductive leaching, SO2 gas produced by the acid plant (approximately 8 vol% in air) was used as the reducing agent. The leaching was conducted in a 5 L tank with a liquid-solid ratio of 4 L/2kg. The gas flow rate was maintained between 4 and 6 L/min. The reaction was carried out at room temperature for 4 h. Although higher temperatures (e.g., 80 °C) are often recommended for kinetics, room temperature was selected for two reasons: (1) to minimize energy consumption, and (2) because SO₂ solubility is higher at lower temperatures, which ensures sufficient reduction efficiency. After reductive leaching, as shown in Fig. 3, the diffraction peak of MnO2 completely disappears, and the leaching residue is mainly composed of SrSO4, which can be considered for comprehensive utilization.

XRD pattern of the zinc anode slime leaching residue.

Analytical methods

Phase compositions of the solid samples were analyzed using a Bruker D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer (XRD). The elemental compositions of solid materials (e.g., anode slime and leaching residues) were determined by X-ray fluorescence spectrometry (XRF, Thermo Scientific). For the liquid samples (e.g., electrolytes and leaching solutions), the elemental concentrations were measured using Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES, Thermo Fisher iCAP 7000).

All experimental results and data presented in this study represent the averages of at least three independent replicate experiments. Data points with excessive variance were excluded to ensure that the coefficient of variation (CV) was strictly controlled below 10%. Furthermore, the cathode zinc plates produced during electrowinning were evaluated through multiple sampling and dissolution assays, with the measurement errors strictly adhering to the aforementioned statistical standards, thereby ensuring the high reliability and reproducibility of the reported data.

Results and discussion

Diagnostic analysis of impurity distribution in Zn electrowinning

Anode slime carries away some impurities, mainly Cu, Fe, Ni, Co, and Sb. Comparative analysis of anode slime from different periods shows that Fe, Ni, and Co contents correlate positively with manganese content. Based on the monthly production of 10 tons of anode slime and 400 m3 of spent electrolyte, the distribution of manganese and various impurities was calculated and listed in Table 2. As shown in Table 2, approximately half of the iron (50.20%) is distributed into the anode slime, while the remaining half (49.80%) stays in the spent electrolyte. This distribution behavior is directly governed by the electrochemical and chemical reactions occurring during zinc electrowinning. At the anode, a portion of the Fe²⁺ in the electrolyte is oxidized to Fe³⁺, which subsequently undergoes hydrolysis and co-precipitation, thereby settling into the anode slime. Concurrently, the highly acidic environment of the electrolyte (pH ≈ 1) prevents the complete hydrolysis of iron, allowing the remaining fraction to stay stably dissolved in the aqueous phase. It is estimated that the proportion of Cu, Fe, Ni, and Co carried away by the anode slime is relatively large, reaching 98.81%, 50.20%, 73.53%, and 72.46% of the total discharge mass, respectively (Table 2).

If these impurities leach out and re-enter the electrolyte during the reductive leaching of anode slime, the burden on the purification system will increase significantly. Therefore, measures must be taken to suppress the leaching of impurities while recovering manganese.

Characterization of leaching residue

The phase transformation of the anode slime during the reductive leaching process was analyzed. As previously shown in the materials section, the raw anode slime is composed of SrSO4 and amorphous MnO2. After SO2 reductive leaching, the XRD pattern of the leaching residue is presented in Fig. 3.

The characteristic diffraction peaks of MnO2 disappeared completely after the reaction. The residue is predominantly composed of SrSO4. This indicates that SO2 acted as an effective reducing agent, converting insoluble MnO2 into soluble MnSO4, thereby achieving high manganese leaching efficiency. The separation of manganese from the strontium-rich residue allows for the potential further utilization of the strontium residue.

Impurity behavior in anode slime reductive leaching to recover Mn

Based on the strong oxidizing property of MnO₂ and the strong reducing property of SO₂, SO₂ reductive leaching was adopted. This method offers a simple process, fast reaction, and high leaching efficiency. The reaction equation for SO2 reduction of MnO2 is as follows:

MnO2 + SO2 = Mn2+ + SO42−

It can be seen that the reaction does not produce acidity or alkalinity, and the reaction product is soluble manganese sulfate. According to the E-pH diagram, the thermodynamics of this redox reaction are closely related to the pH value of the reaction system. As the pH value increases, the dominant region for SO2 reduction to form Mn2+ is slightly reduced due to the presence of intermediate species such as Mn2O3 and Mn3O4. However, at the same time, the dominant region of Fe2+ shrinks even faster with the increase of pH value, making the reductive leaching of Fe2O3 extremely difficult. The reductive leaching of Ni and Co is relatively easy, but they may form trivalent oxide co-precipitation with Fe. In addition, Ni2O3 and Co2O3 have extremely low solubility, making their reduction difficult with increasing pH. Therefore, in order to suppress the leaching of impurities such as Fe, Ni, Co while leaching manganese, the pH value of the reaction should be controlled as high as possible. The E-pH diagrams in Fig. 4 are calculated by HSC Chemistry 6.0, with ion concentrations as close as possible to the content of each component during the leaching process of anode slime.

Eh-pH diagrams for the systems at 25 °C: (a) Mn-S-H2O ([Mn] = 0.4 M), (b) Fe-S-H2O ([Fe] = 0.01 M), (c ) Co-S-H2O ([Co] = 0.0001 M), and (d) Ni-S-H2O ([Ni] = 0.00004 M).

The selective separation of manganese from impurities at pH 5.0 is governed by the distinct hydrolysis characteristics of the metal ions. During SO2 reductive leaching, Mn exists predominantly as stable Mn2+, which does not hydrolyze significantly until pH > 8.0. In contrast, Fe3+ (formed by oxidation or present in oxides) has a much lower solubility product (Ksp) and begins to precipitate as hydroxide at pH values as low as 2 ~ 3. By buffering the system at pH 5.0 with lime, the leaching reaction (MnO2 + SO2 → Mn2+ + SO42−) proceeds, while potential contaminants like ferric iron are sequestered in the residue as Fe(OH)3 or goethite. Although Ni and Co do not hydrolyze as readily as Fe, their leaching is physically hindered by the in-situ formation of iron precipitates and gypsum (CaSO4) layers on the particle surfaces, which passivates the dissolution of trace Ni/Co oxides.

According to the Eh-pH diagrams (Fig. 4), at a controlled pH of 5.0 and a potential range of − 0.2 to 0.2 V, manganese predominantly and stably exists in the aqueous phase as Mn²⁺ (Fig. 4a). Conversely, under the identical thermodynamic conditions, iron falls into the solid-phase stability region, existing primarily as solid Fe(OH)₃ (Fig. 4b). Similarly, both cobalt and nickel transition into their respective solid-phase regions (Fig. 4c and d). This thermodynamic differentiation demonstrates that maintaining the leaching system at pH 5.0 facilitates the continuous dissolution of Mn²⁺ while effectively immobilizing Fe, Co, and Ni within the solid residue.

Based on the above reasons, by adding lime produced on site, the pH of the slurry was first increased to about 5.0, and then SO2 gas was introduced for reaction. As shown in the test results of control pH 5.0 throughout the experiment by lime addition in Table 3, the leaching rates of Fe, Ni, and Co were 0.76%, 3.3%, and 12.2%, respectively. The redox reaction between SO2 and MnO2 does not produce acid, but when the redox reaction is completed, the pH of the slurry, if not controlled, will still decrease to 2 ~ 3 due to partial ionization of sulfite formed by the dissolution of SO2. The leaching rate of the above impurities will increase under pH 2 ~ 3 conditions, as shown in the test results of control pH 5.0 initially by lime addition in Table 3, but it is still lower than the reaction without any lime addition. Therefore, if the pH value decreases during the leaching reaction, lime should be added in a timely manner, and it is recommended to monitor the pH value throughout the production process. The leaching of manganese from the anode slime is relatively complete with leaching rate of more than 90% at any pH control, and a significant whitening can be observed in the leached slurry.

In the subsequent purification, the concentrations of Ni and Co were reduced to less than 1 mg/L at 80 ℃ with twice the dosage of DMDTC and twice the dosage of sodium nitrite.

Further laboratory experiments were conducted to investigate the kinetics of the leaching process. As shown in Table 5, the reaction rate is significantly dependent on the SO2 flow rate. Increasing the flow rate from 20 to 350 mL/min shortened the leaching time from 4.75 h to 0.5 h while maintaining a high manganese leaching efficiency (> 90%). However, the SO2 utilization efficiency decreased from 98% to 40% due to gas escape at high flow rates. This suggests that in industrial applications, a moderate gas flow rate with gas-liquid dispersion devices (e.g., Pachuca tanks) is optimal to balance reaction speed and reagent cost.

Impurity removal in spent electrolyte treatment

The conventional zinc hydrometallurgical plant generally goes through four stages of purification: (1) neutralization and precipitation of Fe (while adsorbing and removing As, Sb, Ge) into the leaching residue; (2) zinc powder substitution to remove Cu and Cd and obtain Cu-Cd enrichment residue; (3) removal of Ni and Co with the aid of organic; (4) removal of Cl in the form of CuCl. The Spent electrolyte in the conventional zinc hydrometallurgical plant is returned for leaching, which can then be purified.

The accumulated impurities in the SX-EW zinc hydrometallurgical plant are mainly Fe, Ni, Co, Sb, and a small amount of Cu, while the concentrations of F and Cl are relatively low. In conventional zinc hydrometallurgical plant, the total amount of F and Cl in zinc electrolyte is often around 500 mg/L, while the F and Cl in SX-EW electrolyte are measured to be 6.83 mg/L and 2.83 mg/L, respectively; Therefore, it is unnecessary to remove F and Cl very often from the SX-EW electrolyte. In addition, compared with Cu and Cd with concentrations of tens of mg/L or higher in the conventional zinc leaching solution, the concentration of Cu and Cd in the SX-EW electrolyte is very low, without add-on value to produce a Cu-Cd residue, so there is no need to use zinc powder for their recovery.

Based on the above analysis, it is proposed to use lime to neutralize the SX-EW Spent electrolyte to around pH 5.0. Impurities such as Sb will be adsorbed on the surface of Fe(OH)3 colloids and removed; Subsequently, the combination use of sodium dimethyl dithiocarbamate, C3H6NS2Na·2H2O, abbreviated for DMDTC) and sodium nitrite can effectively remove various impurities such as Cu, Ni, Co, etc. under near neutral conditions. This purification scheme only requires two stages, which is convenient for simplifying the process and reducing costs.

The high efficiency of DMDTC in deep purification is attributed to its strong chelating ability with transition metals. The dimethyldithiocarbamate anion (R2N-CS2−) acts as a bidentate ligand, donating electron pairs from its two sulfur atoms to the metal cation. This results in the formation of stable, hydrophobic four-membered ring complexes (e.g., (CH3)2N-CS2-M-S2C-N(CH3)2). According to the Hard and Soft Acids and Bases (HSAB) theory, the sulfur donor atoms (soft bases) have a high affinity for soft and borderline acids like Cu2+, Ni2+, and Co2+, forming precipitates with stability constants significantly higher than their corresponding hydroxides. This allows for the quantitative removal of these impurities even at low concentrations where simple neutralization is ineffective.

When impurity concentrations are very low, the removal efficiency of DMDTC decreases, and a dosage of 5× the theoretical amount is no longer sufficient. It is recommended to use 10 times the dosage, considering that the impurity concentrations of Fe, Ni, Co, and Cu in the electrolyte after neutralization are generally below 1 mg/L, and the overall dosage of DMDTC will not exceed 50 mg/L, which will not have a significant impact on production costs and organic removal.

Due to the low solvent extraction rate using P204 without saponification in SX-EW zinc plant processing oxide ore, a large amount of zinc in the leaching solution will be lost as it is discharged into the tailings pond. If the Spent electrolyte is directly returned for leaching, there will be at least 15% loss of zinc and complete loss of Mn. By applying the proposed purification strategy, the Spent electrolyte is completely returned to zinc electrowinning, and this loss can be avoided. In addition, the anode slime leaching solution can be processed in combination with the Spent electrolyte for purification before they are returned to electrowinning together. In this way, there is no need to deliberately control impurities during the leaching process of anode slime.

To validate the feasibility of the closed-loop recycling process, electrowinning tests were conducted using the electrolyte supplemented with the manganese recycling stream. Table 7 presents the quality of cathode zinc produced at different manganese concentrations ranging from 0 to 6 g/L. The concentrations of Pb, Cd, Fe, and Cu in the cathode zinc remained stable throughout the electrowinning experiments. The formation of a manganese-based anodic film inhibited lead dissolution, while the purification of the electrolyte prevented the accumulation of impurities. In most cases, the cathode zinc met the Grade #1 Zinc standards (Zn > 99.99%, Pb < 0.005%). At the maximum manganese concentration of 6 g/L, the zinc purity and lead content were 99.9892% and 0.0057%, respectively. Although these values at the boundary condition slightly exceed the Grade #1 limits, they remain highly consistent with the target specifications. This minor deviation may be attributed to experimental error or fluctuations in the anodic film stability at higher manganese levels. The results demonstrate that the proposed process effectively maintains cathode quality for high-purity zinc production.

Conclusion

The main components of zinc electrowinning anode slime are manganese dioxide (MnO2), strontium sulfate (SrSO4), as well as a small amount of lead sulfate (PbSO4). MnO2 can be leached by SO2 reduction and returned to zinc electrowinning in the form of manganese sulfate (MnSO4) solution, achieving the recycling of manganese. Although the content of impurities in the anode slime is small, they still carry away a large proportion of impurities compared to Spent electrolyte. Lime needs to be added to adjust the pH to around 5 to suppress the leaching of impurities such as Fe, Co, Ni, etc. The leaching rates are 0.7%, 12.2%, and 3.3%, respectively. At this time, the Mn leaching rate can still reach 92%. The spent electrolyte is neutralized and reacted with DMDTC to remove impurities, including Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, and Sb, before it is returned to zinc electrowinning.

Despite the demonstrated efficacy of this integrated process, certain technical limitations remain to be addressed for industrial scale-up. The kinetics of the SO2 reductive leaching are highly dependent on gas-liquid mass transfer efficiency; high gas flow rates can lead to a significant decrease in SO2 utilization due to gas escape, necessitating optimized dispersion equipment to balance reaction speed and exhaust management. Furthermore, while DMDTC exhibits excellent deep chelation capabilities for impurities at near-neutral pH, its application introduces supplementary reagent costs compared to conventional precipitation methods. Future research should prioritize continuous pilot-scale operations to evaluate the long-term stability of the organic extractants and optimize the economic viability of the DMDTC dosing regime.
