Heterogeneous trajectories of executive function in clinical Alzheimer's disease: Results from the 3C study
Authors: Hélène Amieva, Maris Dussartre, Viviane Philipps, Anaïs Rouanet, Carole Dufouil, Hélène Jacqmin‐Gadda, Jean‐François Dartigues, Karine Pérès, Cécilia Samieri, Gwenaëlle Catheline, Catherine Helmer, Cécile Proust‐Lima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDAlzheimer's disease (AD) shows considerable heterogeneity in cognitive decline. Executive function (EF), a core cognitive domain, may contribute to this heterogeneity and modulate AD progression.METHODSWe analyzed the trajectories of four EF‐related tests in 649 incident cases of AD from the Three‐City cohort, using multivariate latent class mixed modeling. We linked latent EF patterns with lifestyle at study entry, brain magnetic resonance imaging, clinical markers, and prognosis.RESULTSThe three identified EF patterns, Steep EF Decline, Low EF, and High EF, showed differences in lifestyle‐related factors at baseline, white matter hyperintensity burden, hippocampal volume, and clinical progression. Steep EF Decline predicted worse outcomes and higher mortality. High EF indicated slower decline and better prognosis.DISCUSSIONEF trajectories diverge substantially before AD diagnosis, with implications for prognosis. Preserved EF appears protective, while early or steep EF decline correlates with worse evolution. Lifestyle and vascular factors contribute significantly, highlighting potential intervention targets for cognitive resilience in AD.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, cognitive decline, executive function, heterogeneity, longitudinal data, prospective studies, subphenotypes

BACKGROUND

The complexity of interactions among the numerous biological, social, and environmental factors contributing to Alzheimer's disease (AD) results in a significant variability in neuroanatomical patterns, clinical phenotype, as well as in the rate of symptom progression (e.g., Dong et al., 1 Komarova and Thalhauser, 2 Libon et al., 3 Tand Duara and Barker 4 ). Importantly, this variability also reflects substantial syndromic heterogeneity, 5 with distinct clinical phenotypes characterized by predominant impairments beyond memory, including visuospatial or language variants, as well as a dysexecutive phenotype in which executive functions (EFs) are primarily impaired and represent the core deficit. 6

Examining the pattern of cognitive deficits provides valuable prognostic information. In mild cognitive impairment (MCI), 7 which involves different subtypes including amnestic MCI (predominant memory loss), and non‐amnestic MCI (impairments in other domains), 8 the risk of progression to dementia is higher in “amnestic” than “non‐amnestic” MCI, 9 , 10 while individuals with multi‐dimensional cognitive impairment show poorer daily functioning than those presenting only memory impairment. 11

Therefore, beyond memory, the degree to which the other functions decline or remain resilient provides valuable information. Among the non‐memory cognitive functions, EF deserve particular attention, as it is essential to multiple aspects of social and affective functioning. EF consists of a constellation of higher order cognitive processes including planning, initiation, inhibition, reasoning, and self‐monitoring. 12 EF controls and coordinates other cognitive abilities and behaviors, playing a key role in adaptive and compensatory strategies. Cognitive functions must be viewed as part of a set of interconnected functions, evolving throughout the lifespan and adjusting to the progressive changes occurring in the aging brain. This view has been conceptualized in various theories, such as the scaffolding theory of aging and cognition, which states that aging involves dynamic brain reorganization 13 whereby stable functions like language or semantics compensate for more vulnerable ones. Similarly, some studies have shown that the age‐related decline in episodic memory is partially due to the deterioration in speed and EF skills, 14 , 15 including flexibility, inhibitory control, working memory, and long‐term memory access. 16 , 17 In particular, EF plays a crucial role in developing new cognitive strategies or selecting already existing alternative ones, enabling individuals to compensate for functional decline and mitigate its consequences. Thus, one of the various sources of heterogeneity in the way cognitive symptoms progress and the rate at which they progress may be intertwined with how EF deteriorates.

This study examines heterogeneity in EF progression in AD in a population‐based cohort with repeated cognitive assessments in a large community‐dwelling sample, some of whom developed AD. Among the incident cases, we aimed to (1) identify EF deterioration patterns at the approach of AD clinical diagnosis, (2) assess associations with brain volumes, and lifestyle, and (3) study specific clinical progression and mortality. We hypothesized that distinct EF trajectories would be differentially associated with neurodegenerative changes and clinical outcomes, with steeper EF decline reflecting greater brain atrophy and faster clinical deterioration. The study used a multivariate latent class mixed modeling approach 18 combining mixed models for multivariate longitudinal data with a latent class approach to identify groups with distinct trajectories.

METHODS

Study population

The 3C population‐based study investigates cerebral aging in 9294 community dwellers aged ≥ 65 living in three French cities (Bordeaux, Dijon, Montpellier). 19 The study was approved by the Kremlin–Bicêtre University Hospital committee (Paris). All participants signed informed consent.

A clinical evaluation was performed by a psychologist at baseline and at 2, 4, 7, 10, 12 years, plus 14 and 17 years in Bordeaux. A subsample of 3442 participants underwent 1.5T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at baseline and 4 years, and 3T MRI at 10 years in Bordeaux with both T1‐ and T2‐weighted sequences (see details in Supplementary Section 1 in supporting information).

Our study focused on the incident cases of AD diagnosed during the follow‐up as having probable, possible, or mixed dementia. The age at clinical AD diagnosis (hereafter referred to as “diagnosis”) was defined as the midpoint between the age at the last negative assessment and the age at the first positive diagnosis. To model clinical progression, all data from baseline up to 5 years after diagnosis were analyzed.

The study sample included the participants from Bordeaux and Dijon diagnosed with incident AD (N = 768), with at least one measure of each EF test in the time window under study, and no missing data on education and apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 status, leading to a sample of 649 incident cases (see the flowchart in Figure S1 in supporting information) for a total of 3145 visits with EF assessment.

Clinical diagnosis of AD

At each visit, a standardized battery of neuropsychological tests was administered. Participants suspected of dementia based on cognitive performance were examined by a neurologist. Diagnoses were established according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition, and reviewed by an independent expert committee. Dementia cases were further classified as probable or possible AD based on the National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke and the Alzheimer's Disease and Related Disorders Association criteria. The clinical diagnosis, established by the neurologist, was then confirmed by an expert committee who reached consensus on diagnosis and etiology. MRI‐derived regional atrophy or biomarkers were not considered for diagnostic classification.

Explanatory variables

Analyses considered sex, age, APOE ε4 status (at least one ε4 allele versus none), time in study at diagnosis, city sample (Bordeaux, Dijon), education (primary school, short secondary school, higher), engagement in cognitively stimulating activities at study entry (highest tertile of a cognitive stimulating activity score 23 versus lowest two tertiles), physical inactivity at study entry (< 1 hour of sport or recreational walking per week 23 ), and Lifestyle for Brain Health (LIBRA) score at study entry reflecting the additive effect of lifestyle‐related risk factors of dementia (diet, physical activity, cognitively stimulating activities, alcohol consumption, smoking, heart disease history, diabetes, cholesterol, obesity, hypertension, renal dysfunction, depression). 24

Brain markers

EF profiles were contrasted according to markers of brain structure and vascular load. Gray matter, white matter, total intra‐cranial volume (TIV), parenchymal fraction, and hippocampal volume were measured using Statistical Parametric Mapping and FreeSurfer software, and vascular lesions (white matter hyperintensity volume standardized by the total white matter volume [WMH load] either in total or in deep and periventricular areas obtained by automated procedure) were evaluated at the last exam prior to diagnosis. See Supplementary Section 1 for details on MRI analysis.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis consisted of: (1) estimating mean EF trajectory profiles prior to and around AD diagnosis; (2) assessing associations between latent EF profiles, explanatory variables, and brain markers prior to diagnosis; and (3) describing trajectories of clinical progression markers around AD diagnosis by EF profile. All analyses used years centered on diagnosis as the timescale.

EF trajectories

Distinct EF trajectories were identified using a multivariate latent class mixed model. 18 This approach assumes that a parsimonious number of latent classes captures the heterogeneity observed in the trajectories. The repeated EF measures were analyzed according to time to AD diagnosis using a mixed model with class‐specific parameters. The shape of the trajectory was modeled using natural cubic splines on time with internal knots at the tertiles of observed times (5.1 and 1.1 years before diagnosis) and boundary knots at the 5% and 95% of observed times (12.6 years before, 3.4 years after) to limit boundary influence. The trajectories were assumed independent across tests given the latent classes. Test‐specific individual random effects accounted for the serial correlation within test‐specific repeated measures. Two strategies were considered (random intercept only vs. additional time random effects), yielding consistent results; we report here the more parsimonious model with random intercepts. Mixed models were adjusted for age at diagnosis, education, city, and their interactions with the time functions to avoid confounding EF profiles with known modulators of cognitive trajectories. When needed, departure from normality was handled using parameterized transformations (quadratic splines). The model was estimated using the mpjlcmm function in the lcmm R package 29 with 100 random starts for 1 to 6 classes to ensure convergence to the global maximum. The optimal number of latent classes was guided by statistical criteria (Bayesian information criterion [BIC] indicating the goodness of fit; the entropy and posterior classification, reflecting the latent class separation and discriminative ability, and the integrated classification likelihood [ICL], a trade‐off between goodness of fit and discrimination), class size, and clinical relevance.

Secondary assessment of characteristics associated with EF profiles

The EF profiles were compared according to individual characteristics and brain markers. First, potential predictors of the latent classes among the explanatory variables were determined using univariate multinomial logistic models. Non‐linear associations of continuous covariates were assessed using natural cubic splines (internal knots at the covariate distribution tertiles). Covariates at study entry were adjusted for time in study at diagnosis. Second, differences in brain markers at the approach of diagnosis according to the latent profiles were evaluated in regression models with the latent class as the primary predictor: the last measurement of each brain marker prior to diagnosis was regressed on the latent classes adjusting for time to diagnosis, magnet strength, and TIV for hippocampal, total gray, and white matter volumes.

Secondary assessment of clinical progression across EF profiles

The clinical marker of progression was modeled over time centered on diagnosis according to the latent class structure using a mixed model. As in (2.7.1), the shape of the trajectory was approximated using the same natural cubic spline basis considering a time window from cohort entry up to 5 years after diagnosis, the model was adjusted for education and city sample, and departure from normality was handled using non‐linear transformations. A proportional hazards model was estimated to assess mortality risk after diagnosis with adjustment for education, sex, age at diagnosis (proportionality assumption across classes was explored and satisfied).

When using latent classes in secondary analyses, the classification error in assigning participants to a class may lead to incorrect conclusions. 30 In steps (2.7.2) and (2.7.3), we accounted for this uncertainty using a two‐stage estimation procedure 18 implemented in the lcmm R package. 29

EF profiles

The statistical criteria (BIC, ICL, and entropy) reported for the 1‐ to 6‐class models in Figure S2 in supporting information (top panel) suggested an optimal 4‐class model. However, the 4‐class model included a small class comprising 6.8% of the sample (see Figure S2 bottom panel for a Sankey plot). We therefore focused on the 3‐class model, and reported the 4‐class model as a sensitivity analysis in supporting information (Section 4, Figures S3 to S7 in supporting information).

The three predicted EF profiles spanning over 15 years before and 5 years after AD diagnosis are displayed in Figure 1, stratified by education, for a participant from the Bordeaux sample with AD diagnosis at 85 years. The three classes exhibited different shapes of EF decline toward diagnosis. Class 1 (24.7% of participants), labeled Steep EF Decline, was characterized by the steepest mean decline around AD diagnosis regardless of the psychometric test. Class 2 (38.1% of participants), labeled Low EF, was characterized by the lowest levels across all psychometric tests throughout the follow‐up and showed almost no acceleration of decline around diagnosis. Class 3 (37.3% of participants), labeled High EF, was characterized by high and stable trajectories across all tests, with a slight decline around diagnosis.

Predictors of EF profiles

The classes did not exhibit differences according to city sample, education, APOE status, type, or age at diagnosis (Figure 2). Females were more likely to belong to the Low EF class than the Steep EF Decline class (odds ratio [OR] = 1.42/0.76 = 1.87 [1.08–3.25]) and to a lesser extent to the High EF class (OR = 1.42 [0.88−2.30]). Incident AD cases diagnosed later were slightly more likely to be in the Steep EF Decline class and Low EF class than in the High EF class. After adjustment for the time of diagnosis, participants with low LIBRA score were less likely to be in the Low EF class than the High EF class (OR = 0.54 [0.34−0.85] and OR = 0.72 [0.55−0.95] for a LIBRA score at quantiles 20% and 40% compared to quantile 50%), and those with a higher cognitive activity were less likely to be in the Low EF class than the High EF class (e.g., OR = 0.56 [0.40−0.79] and OR = 0.38 [0.21−0.67] for cognitive activity at quantiles 60% and 80% compared to quantile 50%). Participants without physical activity were more likely to belong to the Low EF class than the Steep EF Decline class (OR = 2.23 [1.20–4.17]) and High EF class to a lesser extent (OR = 1.50 [0.78−2.90]). No difference was found in lifestyle between the Steep EF Decline class and High EF class.

Brain structure and vascular lesions across EF profiles

Differences in brain markers across classes at the MRI exam closest to AD diagnosis were investigated in 215 participants for regional volumes, and 210 participants for WMH loads (Figure 3). Compared to the full sample, those with MRI data were younger, had longer follow‐up, were more often APOE ε4 carriers, and had higher education (Table 1). In this subsample, the Low EF class had smaller TIV (mean difference [MD] with Steep EF Decline class of –49.4 95% confidence interval [CI = –93.1, –4.0] cm3, p value = 0.034). No difference was found in hippocampal, total gray, and white matter volumes (adjusted for TIV), and parenchymal fraction (overall p value of differences across classes: p = 0.538, p = 0.568, p = 0.561, p = 0.157, respectively). High EF class had fewer WMHs (overall difference p = 0.0007). This was driven by differences in the percentage in the periventricular areas with MD = –1.316 (95% CI = [–2.086, –0.602]) points between High EF and Low EF classes.

Clinical progression of EF profiles

Predicted trajectories of disability, depression, global cognition, and memory differed across profiles (Figure 4 displays the trajectory of a participant with a primary school level). Overall, whatever the clinical marker considered, High EF class (green line) showed the most favorable progression. Low EF class (blue) and Steep EF Decline class (gray) presented an earlier functional decline compared to the High EF class. The three profiles also differed in depression: the Low EF class showed the highest level throughout follow‐up; Steep EF Decline class showed the lowest level 10 to 15 years before AD diagnosis, followed by a sharp increase reaching similar levels as Low EF class; while High EF class showed stable, consistently low levels. After adjustment for education, city sample, and age at diagnosis, MMSE was similar up to diagnosis in Steep EF Decline class and High EF class while it was lower in the Low EF class. After diagnosis, the Steep EF Decline class showed a rapid MMSE decline. The same pattern was found for FCSRT immediate and cued recalls. For the free recall, adjusted for education, sample and age at diagnosis, the Steep EF Decline class and the High EF class presented similar stable trajectories up to 6 years before diagnosis, followed by a progressive decline for High EF class and a steeper decline for Steep EF Decline class as observed for the other markers. In contrast, the Low EF class had the lowest MMSE before diagnosis, with slow and steady decline.

DISCUSSION

This study shows heterogeneity in EF trajectories among individuals diagnosed with AD. We identified three profiles of EF progression spanning the period preceding AD diagnosis and extending up to 5 years post‐diagnosis. These profiles differed in pattern and rate of decline and were associated with specific cognitive, mood, functional, lifestyle, and brain characteristics. Our findings underscore EF as a dynamic and central element in the natural history of AD, with implications for prognosis and intervention.

The Steep EF Decline trajectory, marked by initially preserved EF followed by rapid deterioration around diagnosis, showed the worst prognosis, with sharp declines in cognition and functional abilities and higher mortality. The acceleration in decline may reflect a tipping point in neuropathological burden at which compensatory mechanisms become insufficient.

In contrast, the Low EF trajectory displayed persistently low EF, with little acceleration around diagnosis, moderate decline in other cognitive functions, and similar mortality to the High EF group. However, this profile was accompanied by higher depressive symptoms and WMH burden, especially in periventricular areas, suggesting cerebrovascular pathology. This pattern supports the hypothesis that in some individuals, EF impairment may be driven more by vascular contributions rather than AD‐specific neurodegeneration alone.

The High EF profile showed the most favorable trajectory, characterized by preserved EF, slow functional and cognitive decline, low vascular brain damage, low depression, and healthier lifestyle. This group aligns with the concept of multifactorial brain resilience and cognitive reserve, which buffer neuropathological effects and delay clinical symptoms. 31

EF, as a higher order cognitive domain responsible for organizing and integrating other cognitive processes, appears to modulate the rate and pattern of AD clinical progression. While memory decline remains the hallmark of AD, our study emphasizes that EF trajectories diverge and influence the expression and functional consequences. This perspective aligns with theories, 13 which posit that the aging brain recruits alternative networks or cognitive strategies to compensate for deficits. Individuals with preserved EF may be better able to engage these compensatory processes, delaying functional loss. The results are consistent with findings highlighting EF as a key component of cognitive reserve 32 and with studies suggesting that executive dysfunction in MCI is associated with a more aggressive disease course. 11 , 33 Preserved EF in the face of incipient pathology may thus be a marker of resilience, while early and diffuse EF decline reflects vulnerability to worst outcomes.

Interestingly, lifestyle‐related factors were predictive of EF trajectories several years before AD diagnosis. Participants with healthier lifestyle behaviors were more likely to belong to the High EF group. Conversely, physical inactivity and higher lifestyle‐related risk were associated with the Low EF profile. These findings are consistent with studies showing that lifestyle factors play an important role in brain resilience 34 and suggest that early interventions targeting lifestyle may have long‐term benefits for cognitive resilience in preserving EF.

The higher WMH burden in the Low EF group supports the vascular hypothesis of cognitive impairment. WMHs are common in small vessel disease and associated with EF impairments. 35 Together, these findings point to a contribution of vascular mechanisms to EF decline, potentially interacting with neurodegenerative processes, as previously reported. 36 Accordingly, the observed heterogeneity in EF trajectories may reflect a stronger contribution of vascular factors than of AD‐specific neurodegeneration, calling for a nuanced interpretation of EF decline beyond a purely AD‐centered framework.

Another notable finding was the distinct depressive trajectories. The Low EF group exhibited consistently higher depressive symptoms, while the Steep EF Decline group showed a marked increase shortly before diagnosis. These patterns support the hypothesis of a relationship between executive dysfunction and mood disturbances in older adults 37 , 38 and AD. 39 Executive dysfunction may reflect alterations in frontostriatal–limbic circuits, pathways implicated in geriatric depression. 40 Chronic EF impairment may reduce coping capacity, promoting depression, while depression itself can also lead to EF deficits. Thus, treating depressive symptoms in at‐risk individuals may help preserve EF and slow cognitive decline.

Key limitations include MRI data being available only for a subset of participants, possibly introducing selection bias and limiting generalizability, and the absence of molecular biomarkers, which restricts insight into underlying neuropathology. Our sample included 15% of mixed dementia cases, which could potentially influence EF trajectories. Nevertheless, sensitivity analyses after their exclusion yielded similar results, indicating that the observed EF trajectories are not primarily driven by mixed dementia cases. Additionally, as an exploratory analysis, the results cannot support causal conclusions despite controlling for confounders.

Our study has strengths and clinical implications. It highlights the value of longitudinal EF assessment in AD, rather than focusing solely on memory, as it may provide prognostic information and help stratify patients for tailored interventions. Our study relies on a multidimensional view of AD, integrating neuropsychological, affective, lifestyle, and MRI data. Finally, we used a state‐of‐the‐art methodology; the multivariate latent class mixed modeling approach allowed us to jointly model the progression of multiple EF‐related tests, accounting for inter‐individual variability and latent group structures.
