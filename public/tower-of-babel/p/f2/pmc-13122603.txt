Temporal order–dependent and –independent cortical representation of gaze sequences
Authors: Radha Nila Meghanathan, Stefan Pollmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrain activation patterns in the ventral occipitotemporal cortex (VOTC) correspond not only to faces or scenes but also to the eye gaze sequences made while viewing these stimuli. But does the VOTC represent category-specific fixation sequences or rather category-specific spatial fixation patterns? To investigate this, we acquired eye movement and functional magnetic resonance imaging data from participants while they tracked dot sequences corresponding to fixations recorded during face or house viewing in the absence of the images themselves. To test for gaze sequences representation, the presented dots were either in the correct order or shuffled. Multivariate pattern analyses showed that activity in the parietal cortex was dependent on order information in the gaze sequences, while the VOTC and lateral occipital regions represented spatial fixation patterns but were not dependent on order. Our findings about cortical gaze sequence representation align with the existing understanding of visual processing, where the parietal cortex is involved in visuomotor control and the VOTC in object categorization. We argue that representation of category-specific fixation patterns in VOTC may support visual object categorization.

Introduction

When we look at a face, we carry out eye movements in patterns distinct from the eye movement patterns for other visual objects (Yarbus 1967; Walker-Smith et al. 1977; Henderson et al. 2005; Arizpe et al. 2012). Cortical brain areas involved in eye movement planning and control belong mostly to the dorsal stream, particularly the frontal and supplementary eye fields (FEF, SEF) and parietal eye fields in the posterior parietal cortex (PPC; Anderson et al. 1994; Colby et al. 1996; Snyder et al. 2000; Goldberg et al. 2002; Wurtz 2008). In a recent study, however, distinct neural activities of face- and house-viewing–related gaze tracks were observed in the ventral occipitotemporal cortex (VOTC; Wang et al. 2019), particularly in the fusiform face area (FFA) and the parahippocampal place area (PPA). This result was unexpected because these areas are not typically associated with eye movements but rather with object (including face) and scene perception (Mishkin et al. 1983; Kravitz et al. 2013). For instance, the FFA is known to represent face (Kanwisher et al. 1997), and the PPA represents house and scene images (Epstein and Kanwisher 1998).

Notably, in the study by Wang et al. (2019), neural activation patterns elicited by face- and house-related fixation sequences could be discriminated in the FFA and PPA in the absence of image input. In this paradigm, participants followed a sequence of dots on a uniform background by making eye movements, where the dot sequence replayed a gaze sequence previously recorded during viewing of face or house pictures. Here, we aimed to further investigate the nature of gaze sequence representation in the VOTC and compare it to its representation in dorsal cortical areas known to support eye movement planning.

Gaze sequence representation in the VOTC could have 2 possible purposes. For one, it might be that VOTC areas like the FFA and PPA initiate category-specific fixation sequences that, then, are forwarded to the parietal and frontal eye fields. This, however, would be a surprising finding requiring a new view of the distribution of labor between the dorsal and ventral streams, since the VOTC is not known to be involved in movement control.

Alternatively, the VOTC may receive information about spatial fixation patterns from more anterior cortical areas in order to support object categorization. In the case of face viewing, which elicits a quite typical T-shaped pattern of fixations in the space between the eyes and the mouth (Yarbus 1967; Arizpe et al. 2012; Mehoudar et al. 2014), this pattern could be used as a cue that a face is being looked at by the VOTC.

Initiating an eye movement sequence would pose different demands on the VOTC than receiving eye movement locations as a cue for perception. If the VOTC initiates categorical eye movement sequences, it needs to represent not only a category-specific spatial fixation pattern but also a temporal pattern in which these fixations develop, i.e., where to look first, second, etc. In contrast, if the occipitotemporal cortex receives information about fixations for object categorization, a spatial fixation pattern—like the T-shaped face viewing pattern—might suffice.

We investigated where in the brain category-specific gaze sequences are represented as a temporal sequence of fixation locations or, rather, as a spatial pattern of fixation locations. Beyond the occipitotemporal cortex, true sequence representation would be expected in parietal and frontal cortices, in line with the view that neural computations in the dorsal stream support real-time processing of sensory input in the service of ongoing action planning whereas the ventral stream supports object perception and recognition (Goodale and Milner 1992; Gallivan and Goodale 2018; Rauschecker 2018). Under this dual stream model, the ventral stream may lack the ability to process eye movements as a real-time series of events but may be able to use spatial fixation patterns as primes for determining which objects we are looking at. This is the hypothesis that we tested in this study.

We adapted the dot-tracking task used previously by Wang et al. (2019), where participants had to fixate on sequentially presented dots on a uniform gray background. The dots indicated fixation locations obtained from a different sample of participants while looking at pictures of faces and houses in a separate visual memory task. Wang et al. (2019) were able to classify gaze and house-related gaze sequences in the absence of face or house images in the FFA and PPA, as well as in the FEFs and in the PPC. In the present study, classification analysis was used to distinguish between functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) activation patterns elicited by face- or house-related fixation sequences. In order to test the importance of temporal sequence information for the classification, the dots were either correctly ordered or their sequence was shuffled. The shuffled condition would preserve the category-specific spatial locations while scrambling the exact temporal sequence. Given our above hypothesis, we predict that the neural representation of dot following depends on correct order information in the dorsal stream but not in the ventral stream.

Methods

Participants

Twenty-two students (8 males) of a mean age of 23.94 years (SD = 3.58) with normal or corrected-to-normal vision participated in the experiment with informed consent. The study was approved (approval code 53/20) by the Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty of Otto von Guericke University, Magdeburg. Participant exclusion criteria were strabismus and known neurological or psychiatric disorders. Participants were given 4 credit points or 30 Euros for participation. Data from 1 participant who did not finish the experiment were excluded. Data from 2 other participants were corrupted in 1 of the runs and were excluded. Data from another participant with poor performance (70%) in the task were removed. Data from 1 participant whose data were corrupted during preprocessing with fMRIprep were also removed.

Stimuli

Each trial contained a sequence of 5 black dots. These dots represented the first 5 fixations of gaze paths of a different set of participants recorded while they viewed face, house, and leaf images in a separate experimental session conducted earlier. From those trials containing no blinks, 480 trials were randomly selected as stimuli for the current experiment. Randomly, half of these sequences were used as such (ordered) and the other half were shuffled. This set of ordered and shuffled dot sequences was used for each participant, with trial order randomized.

Each dot in a sequence was presented for the duration of the corresponding fixation plus an additional 250 ms to account for saccade latency. Therefore, if a fixation lasted 280 ms, the dot corresponding to this fixation was presented for 530 ms (280 ms + 250 ms) to give the participant time to saccade to the dot after onset. While this made the dot durations longer, it preserved order dependencies between the dots, thus preserving the gaze path. In some of the trials, a white dot was presented in the center of any of the 5 black dots of the sequence to ensure that participants followed the gaze path. In the “ordered” condition, the first 5 fixations of a recorded sequence were selected. In the “shuffled” condition, the first 5 fixations of recorded sequence were selected and the sequence was shuffled. Overall, there were 6 conditions: ordered face, ordered house, ordered leaf, shuffled face, shuffled house, and shuffled leaf. Leaf stimuli were included for a distinct categorization analysis that will be presented in a separate manuscript. Thus, only the face and house stimuli are analyzed and discussed in this paper.

Stimuli were projected onto a 1280 × 1024 screen (25.6° × 20.5°) with an eye-camera distance of about 100 cm.

Procedure

Participants performed the main experimental session (Fig. 1A) followed by a localizer session.

A) Timeline and trial sequence of task. B) Overlay of input dots corresponding to face (red) and house (orange) fixations on an average image generated from all the face images (selected from KDEF database (Lundqvist et al. 1998)) and house images (selected from DalHouses database (Filliter et al. 2016)), which were presented to participants for recording eye movements in a separate session. Taking our learnings from experiment 1 of previous work from our lab (Wang et al. 2019), where larger dispersion in the house category led to higher activation, we attempted to minimize the dispersion of eye movements between the different categories by making the images all roughly the same size. In spite of this, feature differences between the categories produced dispersion differences. While eye movements in faces appear to stay largely central, house fixations are dispersed along both axes.

fMRI session

Participants were asked to perform a dot-tracking task in the fMRI scanner, while their eye was tracked at 250 Hz. Each trial started with a red fixation dot presented in the center of the display for a duration between 3.08 and 4.05 s. After a 0.2-s pause, the dot sequence (black dots) was presented. Each sequence was of a different time interval, varying from 2.25 to 3.22 s. To ensure that participants fixated on the red dot and followed the black dots of the sequence with precision, the participant was asked to press a key whenever a small, white target dot was presented in the center of any of these dots in 10% of the trials. The white target dot was presented for 0.1 s at a random interval when it occurred in the middle of the red fixation dot. The white target dot appeared in any of the black dots for the duration of the dot (less than 400 ms). After each trial, a brief blank screen of 0.5 s was presented. The next trial continued in a similar fashion. Each trial lasted 7 s.

Trials were grouped into 8 runs with 60 trials in each run, made up of 10 fixation sequences belonging to each of the 6 conditions. Conditions were presented in pseudorandom order with the restriction that maximally 2 subsequent trials were of the same category (face, house, leaf). Each run lasted about 8 min. Before the start of every second run (runs 1, 3, 5, and 7), the participant’s eyes were calibrated for accurate tracking. At the end of each run, the participant was given a mandatory break of 2 s to rest their eyes, after which the next run was initiated when they were ready. This break lasted approximately 2 min. Including the time for eye calibration and breaks, the experiment lasted around 70 min. After the experiment, participants also performed a localizer task for a separate analysis that is not presented here. The entire experimental session, including the delivery of instructions, practice of the task, fMRI setup, the experiment, and localizer session, lasted about 2 h.

Eye tracking

The right eye of each participant was tracked with the Eyelink 1000 (SR Research Ltd, Mississauga, Ontario, Canada) at 250 Hz. A 9-point calibration with the points spanning 75% of the screen was used before the start of each block. In case of a validation error larger than 1°, calibration was repeated.

fMRI data acquisition

A 3T Philips Achieva dStream MR-scanner equipped with a 32-channel head coil was used for MR-data acquisition. T1-weighted images were recorded before the experiment (192 sagittal slices, 256 × 256 1 mm isotropic voxels, TR = 2,300 ms, TE = 4.65 ms, flip angle [FA] = 8°). For the functional imaging during the experiment, an echo-planar imaging sequence was used (35 transversal slices, 3 mm isotropic voxels, matrix size = 80 voxels, TR = 2,000 ms, TE = 30 ms, FA = 90°).

Eye movement data preprocessing and analysis

Fixations and saccades were detected by the Eyelink software using a saccadic threshold of 30°/s velocity and 8,000°/s2 acceleration. Blinks were removed, and fixations or saccades lying within 100 ms of blink onset or offset were removed. Fixations that started before the start of trial or ended after the end of the trial were also removed.

We observed that participants’ eyes were often only partially open resulting in missing pupil detection by the eye tracker. This appeared to be because of fatigue (as reported by participants), suboptimal eyetracker camera position relative to the eye since the lens was on top of the screen in the scanner or obstructing eyelashes. Three participants with significant data loss (~90%) were removed from all eye movement analyses.

We analyzed dot-tracking performance, that is, how closely participants followed the dot sequences presented to them. For each participant, an average lag value was calculated for each run by trial-wise cross-correlating the eye sample data and a time series of the dot sequences. After shifting the sample data by this lag, the time series of the dot sequences were Spearman correlated with the eye sample data for each trial for each participant. The correlation coefficient was used as a metric of dot-tracking performance.

fMRI analysis

All analyses were performed for the house and face dots only. The leaf dots were not analyzed in this study.

Univariate analysis

Univariate analysis of fMRI data from the experiment was carried out using FEAT (FMRI Expert Analysis Tool) Version 6.00, part of FSL (FMRIB’s Software Library, www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl). Data preprocessing included motion correction using Motion Correction FMRIB’s Linear Image Registration Tool (MCFLIRT) (Jenkinson et al. 2002), slice-timing correction using Fourier-space time-series phase-shifting (Smith 2002), nonbrain removal using Brain Extraction Tool (BET) (Smith 2002), spatial smoothing using a Gaussian kernel of Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) 5 mm, grand-mean intensity normalization of the entire 4D dataset by a single multiplicative factor, and highpass temporal filtering (Gaussian-weighted least-squares straight line fitting, with sigma = 50.0 s).

Registration to high-resolution structural and/or standard space images was carried out using FMRIB's Linear Image Registration Tool (FLIRT) (Jenkinson and Smith 2001) (full search, 6 dof). Registration from high-resolution structural space to standard space was then further refined using FMRIB's Non-linear Image Registration Tool (FNIRT) nonlinear registration (full search, 12 dof, 100 mm warp resolution).

Before modeling data from the experiment, trials where a white dot was presented for participant response were removed. All 8 runs were modeled as event-related designs with the 6 experiment conditions (ordered face, ordered house, ordered leaf, shuffled face, shuffled house, shuffled leaf) as regressors. These regressors were defined over the duration of the dot sequence in each trial. The time series was convolved with a canonical double gamma Hemodynamic Response Function (HRF) before modeling. The 6 motion parameters were added as regressors in these General Linear Models (GLMs) too.

A whole-brain univariate analysis was performed in FSL to find significant face–house contrasts for ordered and shuffled sequences. Significant clusters were obtained by thresholding Z-statistics above 3.1 and using a corrected cluster significance threshold of P = 0.05.

Multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA)

The data were processed using an fMRIprep (v 21.0.1) pipeline. Anatomical data (T1w) preprocessing included intensity nonuniformity correction using “N4BiasFieldCorrection” from Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs) library, skull-stripping using the “antsBrainExtraction.sh” workflow and volume-based spatial normalization through nonlinear registration to ICBM 152 Nonlinear Asymmetrical template version 2009 (MNI152NLin6Asym) using “antsRegistration.” Functional data preprocessing for each participant involved obtaining a skull-stripped reference volume using fMRIPrep, obtaining head motion correction parameters (6 rotation and 6 translation parameters) using MCFLIRT (FSL 6.0.5) and slice-timing correction using 3dTshift from AFNI, followed by resampling into standard space (MNI152NLin6Asym) at resolution 2 mm. Motion outliers were identified as frames that exceeded a threshold of 0.5 FD (framewise displacement) and 1.5 standardized DVARS (derivative of root mean square (RMS) variance over voxels). All motion confounds including head estimates were output to a confound file.

For this analysis as well, the trials where a white dot was presented for participant response were removed. General Linear Modeling used the Least Squares-Separate method (LSS) (Mumford et al. 2012), where each trial is modeled separately with a GLM with all other trials being considered nuisance regressors. For each trial, data from the onset of the dot sequence lasting the duration of each sequence was convolved with a Glover HRF and modeled in a GLM using nilearn. Each trial from each experimental condition and the 6 standard motion confound variables obtained from fMRIprep were included as regressors in the model.

To perform a multivariate pattern analysis on the whole brain, we used the parcellation scheme by Schaefer et al. (2018) to divide the cortex into 100 neurobiologically meaningful parcels based on local and global functional connectivity patterns from task-based and resting-state fMRI. Each parcel belongs to a functional brain network such as the dorsal attention network, default-mode network, etc. (Yeo et al. 2011; Schaefer et al. 2018; Kong et al. 2021). To obtain beta values in each parcel, beta values (parameter estimates) obtained from the GLM were masked by parcel coordinates.

Decoders were built separately for each parcel for classification of beta values into the corresponding experimental condition. The 4 conditions resulted in 2 category comparisons (classifications)—ordered face vs. ordered house and shuffled face vs. shuffled house. For each pairwise classification in each parcel, the beta values obtained earlier were standardized and fed to a decoder with a Support Vector Machine (SVM) estimator (C = 1). The cross-validation scheme was an 8-fold run-wise split, which involved splitting the data in half run-wise for test and training sets. This way, any correlation between trials within a run was preserved. ROC AUC (Receiver Operator Characteristics Area Under the Curve) values were obtained as classifier scores.

To test for statistical significance of the decoder accuracies, we followed the procedure described by Stelzer et al. (2013). For each classifier of each participant, we set up a chance distribution of AUC values by permuting class labels a hundred times. From this chance distribution, we randomly selected an AUC value for each participant and averaged them to obtain a group-level chance score. We did this step 10,000 times to obtain a group-level chance distribution. For each classifier, if the score obtained from the original, unpermuted class labels lay above the 99th percentile of the group-level chance distribution, it was considered significant. To correct for multiple tests conducted on the 100 parcels, we used false discovery rate (FDR) correction.

Bayes conjunction analysis

The above analyses only show in which parcels brain activation can be classified above chance level. However, in the case of true order representation, we would expect both above-chance classification for ordered sequences and chance-level classification for shuffled gaze sequences.

Therefore, to identify parcels with order representation, we selected a parcel P, if it satisfied the condition OG > OC and SG < = SC, where OG is the group average of classification scores for ordered sequences, SG is the group average of classification scores for shuffled sequences, OC is the chance-level classification score for ordered sequences, and SC is the chance-level classification score for shuffled sequences.

For these selected parcels, for each participant, we normalized the MVPA score values by subtracting the chance-level score obtained from the permutation test using Equations (1) and (2).

where OP is the score for participant P for classification of ordered sequences and SP is the score for participant P for classification of shuffled sequences.

For these selected parcels, we performed Bayes tests on OPnorm and SPnorm using the BayesFactor R package. We tested for evidence for above-chance (BF10 > 3) face–house discrimination in ordered sequences and evidence for the null (BF01 > 3) for shuffled sequences. Therefore, a parcel was deemed order-dependent if BF10 > 3 for Onorm and BF01 > 3 for Snorm.

In contrast, truly order-independent parcels should lead to comparable classification accuracies for shuffled and ordered sequences. For testing order independence, we performed a conjunction, that is, we found parcels with above-chance performance for both ordered and shuffled sequences. In these parcels, we estimated the Bayes Factor for the null hypothesis that face–house discrimination was not different between ordered and shuffled sequences, that is, BF01 > 3 for Onorm ≠ Snorm.

Representational similarity analysis

To investigate how features of the stimulus (dot sequence) and the elicited eye movements contributed to distinct neural representations in order-dependent and -independent brain regions, we performed representational similarity analyses (RSAs).

For this analysis, 4 representative parcels (Fig. 3A) were selected—2 ventral occipital (left lateral occipital region and a region adjacent to the right fusiform gyrus) and 2 parietal (left and right SPL). Corresponding between-trial neural representational distance matrices (RDMs) were obtained by calculating the correlation distance (1-correlation) of beta series between trials for each parcel. Neural RDMs were compared in separate RSAs with parameter RDMs of the dot sequences and the elicited eye movements.

To obtain parameter RDMs for the dot sequences, we characterized the dot sequence for each trial in terms of low-level parameters of dot distance (in pixels) and dot angle (represented as the sine and cosine of the angle). We then generated between-trial RDMs by calculating the Euclidean distance between the corresponding parameters for the sequence. To perform the RSA, we compared neural RDMs with each of the dot sequence RDMs (dot distance and dot angle). To do this, for each participant, we extracted the top half of the dot RDMs and neural RDMs, separately, as vectors, normalized them, and computed Spearman correlations between them. To test for significance of these dot–neural correlations at the group level, we performed a 2-sided Fisher’s 1-sample permutation test on the z-transformed participant correlation values. Here, the z-scores were permuted in sign 5,000 times to test if they were significantly above 0. The p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

Additionally, we performed an RSA, similar to the one described above, but using saccade amplitude and saccade angle RDMs computed from the recorded eye movements for 14 participants instead of dot parameters.

Results

Behavioral results

Dot task performance

Participant performance in the central dot detection task was high (mean = 97.5%, SD = 3.7%) with little variation across runs (mean = 3.5%), indicating that the task was being performed throughout the duration of the experiment. Dot detection was high irrespective of stimulus category with 97.4% (SD = 5.5%) in face trials and 96.7% (SD = 7.3%) in house trials. A t-test comparing accuracy values for face and house categories did not reveal any significant differences [t(33) = 0.5, P = 0.6].

A comparison of ordered and shuffled face and house trials also did not show any performance differences in a t-test [t(33) = −0.87, P = 0.4] with 96.6% (SD = 7.4%) for ordered trials and 97.5% (SD = 5.3%) for shuffled trials.

Dot-tracking performance

For each participant, we computed a metric of task performance, that is, how well the participant followed the dot sequence as a correlation between the recorded eye sample data and the presented dots. Dot–data correlation [r(13)] was on average 0.57 (SD = 0.086) for 14 participants. This tracking performance was comparable to that obtained in our earlier study (Wang et al. 2019). Participants followed the dots more accurately (P = 0.001) when sequences were presented in the correct order [ordered sequences, r(13) = 0.59] than when they were shuffled [r(13) = 0.57]. Eleven of the 14 participants showed this trend; the remaining 3 showed comparable correlation values for ordered and shuffled sequences.

Dot sequences vs. elicited eye movements

There were 5 dots presented in each sequence. There was no difference in dot duration between faces and houses for ordered [t(157.3) = −0.3, P = 0.76] or shuffled [t(157.6) = 1, P = 0.3] sequences (Table 1). However, for both sequence types, faces had lower dot distances than houses [ordered: t(148.8) = −9.8, P < 0.001, shuffled: t(135.6) = −7.8, P < 0.001].

The eye movements made in response to the dot sequences (see Fig. 1B) showed similar patterns (Table 1). Fixation durations were not significantly different between faces and houses for ordered [t(23.5) = 2, P = 0.05] or shuffled sequences [(t(21.5) = 1.7, P = 0.09]. However, an Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) showed that, across sequence orders (both ordered and shuffled), faces had longer fixation durations than houses [F(1, 13) = 23.9, P < 0.001]. Saccade amplitudes were larger for houses than faces, both in ordered [t(21.5) = −6.2, P < 0.001] and shuffled [t(24.8) = −4.7, P < 0.001] sequences. It is noteworthy that, on average, fixation durations were longer than dot durations, while real saccade amplitudes were smaller than those expected from the distance between successive dots.

Imaging results

Order sensitivity in face–house classification

Given that we normalized the size of the face and house images and both categories had the same number of dots to be followed, we did not expect to see differences in univariate activation strength. This was confirmed in that we found no univariate differences between the face and house gaze paths in ordered or shuffled sequences. Therefore, we can rule out that classification of activation patterns relied on univariate activation strength differences.

Using the Schaefer et al. (2018) parcellation scheme, we performed whole-brain multivariate pattern analysis on the beta values obtained for each trial, separately for ordered and shuffled sequences.

First, we analyzed order-dependent representation of gaze sequences by looking for reliable category classification (BF10 > = 3) of ordered sequences plus reliable evidence for chance-level category classification (BF01 > = 3) of shuffled sequences. This pattern was observed bilaterally along the banks of the descending segments of the intraparietal sulcus, extending anteriorly into the medial banks of the horizontal segments and the posterior part of the superior parietal lobule (Fig. 2, Table 2). In the left hemisphere, order dependence was also observed in the cuneus.

Next, in order to isolate order-independent representation of fixation sequences, we checked which parcels showed significant above-chance probabilities of face vs. house classification both for ordered and shuffled sequences (indicated by a BF10 > = 3 each) and comparable classification performance for these 2 contrasts (indicated by a BF01 > = 3). This pattern was observed in the right cuneus, extending laterally into the parietooccipital transition area, bordering the order-dependent parcel. When we lowered the threshold of equal classification probabilities to BF01 > = 2, we additionally found parcels in the left lateral occipital cortex, reaching from the occipital–parietal border down to the border with the VOTC. Importantly, the lower threshold was also reached by parcels in the right VOTC (Fig. 2, Table 2). Since the evidence for order-independence is not strong in the VOTC, we compared classification probabilities for ordered vs. shuffled sequences to test for order sensitivity. While activation patterns for both ordered (mean = 59.9%, SD = 6) and shuffled sequences (mean = 57.6%, SD = 6.6) yielded above-chance face-house classification, we found no difference in classification probabilities [t(31.6) = 1.1, P = 0.29], yielding no evidence for order representation in the VOTC.

Although the left and right cuneus showed order-dependent and -independent patterns, respectively, classification probabilities were not significantly different between them for ordered [t(31.97) = −0.32, P = 0.7] or shuffled sequences [t(31.3) = 1.8, P = 0.08], indicating gradually rather than fundamentally different gaze sequence representations in left and right cuneus.

Characteristics of order-dependent vs. order-independent areas

To learn more about the nature of eye movement representation in order-dependent and independent areas, we investigated the manner in which features of the dot sequences (and resultant saccades) relate to neural representations in the order-dependent and order-independent regions. To this aim, we compared dot parameters (dot distance and angle) in the dot sequences with neural data in selected order-dependent and order-independent parcels (Fig. 3A) in an RSA (Fig. 3C). We found neural representations to be aligned with both dot distance and dot angle variability in order-independent parcels for both ordered and shuffled sequences (Fig. 3C). In order-dependent parcels, dot distance variability correlated with neural variability for both sequence types (ordered and shuffled), but dot angle showed similarity to neural activity only for the shuffled sequences.

A similar RSA performed on the parameters of eye movements elicited by the dot sequences showed a partly overlapping pattern. For the order-independent, ventral parcels, saccade amplitudes were similar to neural data for both ordered and shuffled sequences, mimicking the effect with the dots. Saccade angle aligned with neural data in all except for ordered sequences in the right hemispheric parcel. For the order-dependent parietal parcels, only saccade amplitude showed similarity to activity in the right hemispheric parcel for shuffled sequences.

The overall lower number of significant associations between saccade parameters and neural activation patterns was likely due to the eye movement RSA being performed on only 14 participants, owing to poor scanner eye data quality.

Overall, correlations between neural activity and eye movement parameters were higher for order-independent regions [t(26.5) = −2.7, P = 0.01 for dots and t(25.6) = −3.2, P = 0.004 for eye movement data] than order-dependent regions. To test if eye movement parameters resulted in order-dependent or -independent neural representations, we compared correlation values for ordered sequences with those for shuffled sequences in t-tests, separately in order-dependent and independent regions. We did not find order-based differences between correlations in the order-dependent [t(28.7) = −0.18, P = 0.86 for dots and t(23.1) = −0.17, P = 0.86 for eye movement data] or -independent regions [t(30.6) = −0.25, P = 0.8 for dots and t(28.7) = −0.83, P = 0.4 for eye movement data].

Therefore, basic eye movement parameters did not differentiate between ordered or shuffled sequences in order-dependent and order-independent brain areas, implying that these regions may primarily differ in more complex relationships between eye movements rather than features of individual eye movements.

Discussion

We investigated the neural representation of category-specific gaze sequences. Replicating a previous study (Wang et al. 2019), we could show that face- and house-related fixation sequences could be classified based on neural activation patterns not only in the parietal but also in the VOTC. Dorsal and ventral representation, however, differed in that in the PPC, classification was only successful if participants followed face and house-related fixation sequences presented in the correct temporal order, whereas classification accuracy was at chance level for shuffled fixation sequences. In contrast, activation patterns in left lateral occipital and right VOTC could be classified with comparable performance both for ordered and shuffled fixation sequences.

Given that the dorsal pathway is well known to support “real-time” processes of visuomotor control—in other words, the decision where to look next—it makes sense that the representation of where to look first, second, etc., plays a bigger role in parietal cortex than in the VOTC. In contrast, the VOTC, as part of the ventral pathway with its focus on perception, categorization and memory (Baldassi et al. 2013; Bracci and Op De Beeck 2016; Bao et al. 2020; Bracci and Op De Beeck 2023), may be less dependent on the temporal aspects of a gaze sequence, while it may be better suited to use the spatial configuration of fixation locations as a prime for category membership.

There was a clear transition from order-dependent to order-independent representation at the junction of the intraparietal sulcus and the lateral part of the parieto-occipital sulcus. Order-dependent representation followed the banks of the descending segment of the intraparietal sulcus and, more anteriorly, the medial banks of the horizontal segment and the posterior part of the superior parietal lobule. Below the junction (in the occipital cortex), order-independent representation was observed, with the exception of the cuneus in the left hemisphere, which showed order-dependent representation. Further analyses showed, however, that the difference in order dependence of sequence representation between the left and right cuneus was rather of a gradual than a fundamental nature.

One could argue that the nature of the task used in this study requires substantial top–down control of eye movements, which could potentially contribute to the observed parietal activation. However, we found that participants followed dots more accurately when the dots were in the correct order than in the shuffled order, indicating that following shuffled sequences was possibly more difficult and placed greater demands on top–down control than ordered sequences. This suggests that the observed parietal activity cannot be explained solely by top–down control and is more consistent with representing order information in eye movements.

The parietal areas demonstrating order-dependent fixation representations are commonly activated by studies of visuospatial attention changes (for reviews, see Corbetta and Shulman 2002; Wager et al. 2004). More specifically, they have been shown to elicit saccade-related activation (eg Sheremata and Silver 2015). Thus, our finding fits well with the role of these areas in guiding spatial attention and eye movements. However, the new finding is that these areas represent the temporal sequence of fixations in category-specific gaze sequences. As argued in the Introduction, this would be a prerequisite of planning a gaze sequence to a stimulus.

One could ask whether the representations in the parietal cortex would still be present if participants did not make eye movements, that is, if the dot sequences were presented with an instruction to fixate centrally. In a central fixation condition in an earlier study, we found that microsaccades were structurally similar to saccades made while freely viewing faces and houses (Wang et al. 2024). Thus, even under instructions to maintain central fixation, eye movement patterns were still generated. Therefore, our finding of order representation in the parietal cortex is inextricably linked to eye movements.

We found gaze sequence representation in the VOTC, replicating findings from an earlier study (Wang et al. 2019). Here, the representation was order independent, in contrast to the dorsal representation. In line with our reasoning, this could mean that the VOTC is not involved in generating a gaze sequence in response to stimulation but rather receives information about the spatial layout of an ongoing fixation sequence. This, in turn, might be used as a prime to support object or scene recognition, particularly when no sufficient visual input is available for recognition, as in our dot-following task. This would be in line with the well-known function of this cortex in object and scene recognition. Of course, we have no direct evidence for top–down feedback to the VOTC, so there is a need for additional research on potential gaze sequence priming of visual recognition processes.

One caveat may be that the classification of face versus house dot sequences was not based on the fixation sequences elicited by dot following but by stimulation changes associated with these fixation changes. Although participants viewed the dot sequences on a uniform gray background, saccades between dot locations would have co-occurred with subtle changes in visual stimulation (e.g. due to the border of the screen, etc., coming into view). However, this is unlikely because the representation of order information in eye movements in the parietal cortex but not in the ventral cortex cannot be explained by a stimulus confound. In addition, we did not find order-dependent or -independent processing in early retinotopic regions in the occipital cortex, in line with the lack of univariate differences in activation strength.

One should note that the dots corresponding to fixations belonging to the 2 categories differed spatially, as one would expect from the distribution of salient features of the 2 categories. For example, despite keeping the 2 stimulus categories of the same size, house gaze paths were more dispersed from participants looking at variably placed windows, doors, chimneys, etc., than the typically more central face fixations (covering the predictable triangular region spanning the eyes, nose, and mouth, see Fig. 1B). Since a lateral–medial eccentricity gradient is an organizing principle in the ventral temporal cortex (Levy et al. 2001; Malach et al. 2002), eccentricity differences could have contributed to the distinction between the 2 eye movement categories, just like between face and house images. However, eccentricity differences between the categories would have given rise to univariate regional differences (Levy et al. 2001), which we did not find, indicating that the successful classification of category-specific neural activity in the VOTC arose from distinct activation patterns. This is in line with the findings from Wang et al. (2019), where differences in univariate activation strength, but not activation pattern differences between the 2 categories, disappeared after the dispersion of fixation locations was made similar between categories. Further corroborating the eye movement origin of our category-specific differences, we found that saccade amplitudes and angles correlated with neural activity in the VOTC.

Further analyses tried to characterize the eye movement parameters that contributed to gaze sequence representation. Both saccade amplitude and direction are known to be represented in the parietal cortex (Vandenberghe et al. 2001; Schluppeck et al. 2005; Leoné et al. 2014), so we investigated if these parameters contributed to the distinction between order-dependent and -independent representation. While we found both parameters to be significantly correlated to brain activity patterns in some areas, these correlations did not differ based on order information in both the order-dependent and order-independent areas. This may hint at more complex representations of fixation sequences rather than basic saccade features.

Notably, these neural representations of gaze sequences were not elicited by pictures of faces or houses. Participants made eye movements by tracking dots as if viewing faces or houses but without the actual face or house images present. Fixation sequences during face and house viewing were recorded in a separate group of participants, making it very unlikely that participants guessed the nature of the dot sequences. In line with this, participants reported that they were not aware of what the dot sequences corresponded to.

Our findings suggest an anterior origin of gaze sequence representations. In a previous study from our lab (Wang et al. 2019), we found gaze sequence representation also in the FEFs. In our study, the parcellation scheme did not have the resolution to encapsulate the FEF with precision. However, even in FEFs identified individually in participants, we were not able to find distinct category representations. Similar to experiment 3 of Wang et al. (2019), eliminating large saccade amplitude differences between the categories by making gaze paths or stimulus sizes (in the present study) of similar scale may be one reason for not finding distinct category-specific neural patterns in the FEF.

Conclusion

We found eye movement sequence representation in both the VOTC and the PPC but serving different purposes, in line with the traditional functional specializations of the dorsal and ventral streams. Order information in eye movement sequences was a vital part of the representation in PPC, in line with the attentional and visuomotor role of these representations. In contrast, the representation of eye movement sequences in the VOTC was tied to spatial information, potentially supporting object perception.
