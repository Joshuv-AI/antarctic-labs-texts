Associations of Objective and Subjective Physical Function With Quality of Life and Well‐Being: A Cross‐Sectional Study From Ardakan Cohort Study on Aging (ACSA)
Authors: Mohammad Bidkhori, Vahid Rashedi, Mina Zarrin, Seyedeh Farhaneh Talebian, Fatemeh Ghavidel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAlthough physical functions are associated with quality of life and well‐being, research on objective and subjective measures in older adults is limited. This study examines the relationship between objective and subjective physical functions with health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) and well‐being in a sample of Iranian older adults.MethodsA cross‐sectional analysis was conducted on data from 3,480 participants in the Ardakan Cohort Study on Aging (ACSA). Physical function was assessed by objective (Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), and Timed Up and Go (TUG)) and subjective methods (Activities of Daily Living (ADL), Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL), and Physical Activity (PA). HRQoL was measured using the Short Form Health Survey (SF‐12), which provided Physical and Mental Component Summary scores (PCS and MCS). Psychological well‐being was evaluated using the World Health Organization‐Five Well‐Being Index (WHO‐5).ResultsAfter adjusting for confounders, the higher SPPB score (β = 1.30; 95% CI: 1.16 to 1.43), high TUG score (β = −3.21; 95% CI: −4.02 to −2.40), limitations in ADL (β = −4.97; 95% CI: −5.05 to −3.21) and IADL (β = −6.27; 95% CI: −7.16 to −5.37), and low levels of PA were associated with the PCS. Only ADL (β = −1.25; 95% CI: −2.18 to −0.31) and low levels of PA showed significant associations with MCS. Logistic regression further indicated that all physical function measures; SPPB score (OR = 0.92; 95% CI: 0.89–0.96), TUG (OR = 1.37; 95% CI: 1.10–1.71), ADL (OR = 1.69; 95% CI: 1.31–2.18), IADL (OR = 1.84; 95% CI: 1.43–2.36), and PA level, were significantly associated with psychological well‐being.ConclusionThe results emphasize the important role of both objective assessments and subjective evaluations of physical function in understanding and promoting health in older adults.

Introduction

Aging is an inevitable biological process, and the global population is undergoing a rapid demographic shift toward older age groups [1]. Furthermore, projections indicate that the older adults population in Iran, similar to other developing nations, is expected to experience the fastest growth among all age groups, highlighting the inevitability of population aging in the country [2]. Therefore, focusing on this demographic group is crucial for enhancing their quality of life (QoL) and overall Well‐being.

The QoL and Well‐being Index serves as a key indicator of the overall welfare and life satisfaction of the population. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), QoL refers to “the perception of one's life situation in the context of cultural conditions, value system, and about one's goals, norms, and interests” [3]. Similarly, based on the WHO definition, “Well‐being encompasses quality of life and the ability of people and societies to contribute to the world with a sense of meaning and purpose” [4].

Many factors related to QoL and well‐being, such as cognitive function [5], depression [6], and social factors [7]. Among these, physical health and functional ability are considered one of the most important determinants [8]. Physical function is a comprehensive and multifaceted concept that encompasses an individual's ability to perform physical activities necessary for daily living, as defined by Garber et al. [9]. Studies have indicated that, among the physical factors related to QoL and well‐being, abilities in activities of daily living (ADL), instrumental activities of daily living (IADL), standing balance, walking speed, and gait speed play a particularly important role [10, 11, 12]. As individuals experience declining functional abilities, increased dependence on others, and a greater need for hospital services, their QoL is likely to deteriorate, leading to further challenges in their overall well‐being and daily living [11].

While many studies have explored quality of life and well‐being in this demographic, they often focus on a single dimension of physical activity, overlooking the broader spectrum of physical function and its multifaceted relationship with quality of life and well‐being. Based on this consideration, the present study aimed to examine the association between objective and subjective physical functions with HRQoL and well‐being.

Methods

Study Population

A cross‐sectional analysis was performed using data from the recruitment phase of the Ardakan Cohort Study on Aging (ACSA). The ACSA is a subset of the IRanian Longitudinal Study on Aging (IRLSA), with more detailed information available in previous publications [13]. In summary, ACSA began in 2020 and included a cohort of community‐dwelling individuals aged 50 years and above in Ardakan, Iran. Individuals who were blind, deaf, nonverbal, or diagnosed by a physician with dementia or Alzheimer's disease were excluded from participation. A stratified multi‐stage random sampling procedure was employed to ensure a representative sample of the general population. A team of trained staff collected data and all participants were examined and interviewed on the site. For the present analysis, we considered participants aged ≥ 60 years who had complete outcome data.

Measures

Health‐Related Quality of Life

The Health‐Related Quality of Life Short Form (HRQoL SF‐12) tool was utilized in this study [14]. Weighted subscales were applied to score the Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS). The MCS includes subscales for vitality, social functioning, role‐emotional, and mental health, while the PCS comprises general health, physical functioning, role‐physical, and bodily pain. The scores for each subscale range from 0 to 100. A higher score on the questionnaire indicates HRQoL. This tools which had been previously translated and validated in Iran (Cronbach's α for PCS‐12 and MCS‐12 was 0.73 and 0.72) [15].

Well‐Being

Well‐being was assessed by the World Health Organization‐Five (WHO‐5) Well‐Being Index, which was validated among Iranians by Khosravi (Cronbach's α was 0.94) [16]. The questionnaire consists of 5 questions, each with six items. Each item is scored on a scale as follows: Never (0 points), Sometimes (1 point), A little less than half the time (2 points), A little more than half the time (3 points), Most of the time (4 points), All the time (5 points). The total scores of the questionnaire range from 0 to 25, and individuals are categorized into two groups based on their scores: those with a poor well‐being index (score of 13 or less) and those with a good well‐being index (score greater than 13).

Physical Function

A variety of subjective and objective methods were employed to assess physical function, including the following:

The Activities of Daily Living (ADL) questionnaire evaluates the independence of older adults in performing essential tasks, including eating, dressing, bathing, mobility within the home, toileting, and managing continence. Each ADL item is scored using three options: dependent (0 points), needs help (0 points), and independent (1 point). The total ADL score ranges from 0 to 6, with scores classified as follows: a score of 0 to 5 indicates dependency or the need for assistance, while a score of 6 indicates independence [17].

The Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) assess dependency on skills necessary for independent living, such as using the telephone, shopping, preparing meals, housekeeping, using transportation, taking medications, and managing finances. The total IADL score ranges from 0 to 8, with the following classifications: a score of 0 to 7 indicates dependency or the need for assistance, while a score of 8 signifies independence [18].

The Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly (PASE) was used to assess the level of Physical Activity (PA), consisting of 10 items that evaluate activities across three categories: leisure time, household time, and work time (Cronbach's α was 0.94) [19, 20]. The PASE score ranges from 0 to 400 or higher. Based on the PASE score, individuals were classified into four quartiles according to their physical activity level, ranging from the lowest to the highest.

The Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) was used to evaluate functional mobility in older adults, focusing on balance, lower body strength, and overall physical capacity. It consists of three assessments: gait speed, chair stand, and balance. For gait speed, participants were timed while walking a three‐meter distance at a normal pace, with the faster of two attempts being recorded. The chair stand test measured lower body strength by timing how quickly participants could complete five sit‐to‐stand repetitions with arms crossed over their chest. Balance was assessed by having participants maintain three different stances—side‐by‐side, semi‐tandem, and tandem—each for 10 s. Scores for each test range from 0 to 4. The overall SPPB score, which ranges from 0 (indicating the poorest performance) to 12 (indicating the best performance), classifies individuals into three categories: 0–6 points (poor performance), 7–9 points (moderate performance), and 10–12 points (good performance) [21].

The Timed Up and Go (TUG) test is a commonly used assessment to evaluate balance and functional mobility. In this test, participants are instructed to rise from a seated position, walk a distance of 3 meters (10 feet), turn around, return to the starting point, and then sit down again. Those who complete the test in more than 13.5 s are considered to have a low score, indicating poorer mobility and balance [22].

Covariates

Economic status was assessed by asking participants, “How would you generally evaluate your economic status?” Household living situations were categorized based on whether individuals lived alone or with others. Opioid use was determined by the question, “Are you currently using opium?”

The history of falls was assessed through self‐report, with participants being asked, “Have you experienced any falls in the past year?” Similarly, vertigo was evaluated by asking, “Have you experienced vertigo in the past few months?”.

Height and weight were measured according to a standard protocol, and the Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight (in kilograms) by the square of height (in meters).

Underlying diseases were identified based on clinical diagnoses provided by physicians, including multiple sclerosis, epilepsy, stroke, Parkinson's disease, osteoarthritis, and arthritis. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure (SBP) of ≥ 140 mmHg and/or a diastolic blood pressure (DBP) of ≥ 90 mmHg, a documented medical history of hypertension, or current use of antihypertensive medications [23]. Diabetes was diagnosed based on one of the following criteria: current pharmacological treatment for diabetes, a fasting blood sugar (FBS) level ≥ 126 mg/dL, or an HbA1c ≥ 6.5% [24].

Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) was assessed using different tools depending on the participants' literacy levels. Literate individuals were evaluated using the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), with scores < 25 indicating MCI. The Abbreviated Mental Test Score (AMTS) was used for illiterate participants, with scores < 7 suggesting MCI. The Persian versions of both the MMSE and AMTS have been previously translated and validated for Iranian populations (Cronbach's α for MMSE and AMTS was 0.78 and 0.90) [25, 26].

The Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression (CES‐D) scale was employed to assess depression. This 10‐item self‐report questionnaire evaluates depressive feelings and behaviors experienced by participants over the past week [27]. Scores between 0 and 10 are considered normal and CES‐D scores ≥ 10 are classified as depressive symptoms.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations, whereas categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages). Linear regression was employed to investigate the relationship between physical function and HRQoL (as a quantitative variable). Logistic regression was used to examine the association between physical function and well‐being (a qualitative variable). All analyses were conducted using three models: Model 1, the crude model; Model 2, adjusted for age and sex; and Model 3, adjusted for age, sex, occupational status, economic status, household status, vertigo, MCI, depression, history of falls, underlying diseases, PA, and BMI based on prior literature and their potential influence on outcomes. In all analyses, p‐values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Data analyses were conducted using STATA Version 15.

Results

Descriptive

Health‐Related Quality of Life

Table 3 presents the associations between physical function variables and PCS across three models. In all models, physical function measures were significantly associated with PCS. Specifically, in Model 3, after adjusting for confounders, each 1‐unit increase in the SPPB score was associated with a 1.30‐point increase in PCS (β = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.16 to 1.43). Moderate and poor performance on the SPPB were associated with reductions in PCS of −3.05 (95% CI: −3.73 to −2.36) and −8.34 (95% CI: −9.30 to −7.37), respectively. High scores on the TUG test were significantly negatively associated with PCS, with β values decreasing by β = −3.21 (95% CI: −4.02 to −2.40). Individuals who were dependent or needed little help with ADLs and IADLs had significantly lower PCS scores (β = −4.13, 95% CI: −5.05 to −3.21 and β = −6.27, 95% CI: −7.16 to −5.37, respectively).

Table 3 also presents the associations between physical function variables and MCS. In Model 1, all physical function variables were significantly associated with MCS. After adjusting for age and sex in Model 2, these associations remained significant (p < 0.05). However, in Model 3, after adjusting for confounders, only ADL and PA remained significantly associated with MCS. So, individuals who were dependent or needed little help with ADLs had significantly lower MCS scores (β = −1.25, 95% CI: ‐2.18 to ‐0.31). Additionally, compared to the highest quantile of PA (reference group), individuals in Quantile 1 and 2 had a reduction in MCS (β = −1.19, 95% CI: −2.09 to −0.29 and β = −1.001, 95% CI: −1.86 to −0.13, respectively).

Specifically, in Model 3, each one‐unit increase in the SPPB score was associated with a 10% decrease in the odds of poor well‐being (OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.89 to 0.96). Compared to individuals with good performance, those with moderate and poor SPPB performance had significantly higher odds of poor well‐being (OR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.28 to 1.87, and OR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.23 to 2.09, respectively). Discussion

This study examined the relationships between physical function, HRQoL, and overall well‐being in a sample of Iranian older adults. The findings revealed that all physical function variables were significantly associated with the PCS of HRQoL. Interestingly, the MCS of HRQoL showed significant associations only with ADL and PA. When examining well‐being, nearly all physical function variables demonstrated significant relationships, except for the balance test.

The majority of previous studies have not distinguished between the physical and mental dimensions of HRQoL [28, 29, 30, 31, 32]. However, most studies have highlighted the association between both objective measures, such as the SPPB test [30, 31], and TUG [33, 34], as well as subjective measures, including ADL [10, 32, 35, 36], IADL [10, 35], and PA [37] with HRQoL. In contrast, this study highlights the distinct roles of the physical and mental components of HRQoL, with the PCS and MCS assessed as separate dependent variables. This distinction enables a clearer understanding of how physical and mental health are independently related to both objective and subjective measures, providing a more precise framework for addressing HRQoL in diverse populations.

The results demonstrated that both objective and subjective measures of physical function were significantly associated with the PCS of HRQoL. The PCS reflects an individual's perception of PCS, comprising general health, physical functioning, role‐physical, and bodily pain, which makes it related to physical function [14]. Given this, it is unsurprising that physical function, whether objectively measured or subjectively reported, plays a significant role in shaping an individual's PCS score. Regarding the objective variables, moderate and poor performance in the SPPB and TUG showed the strongest associations with the PCS, respectively. As these tests measure an individual's physical capabilities in performing essential movements, it is expected that poorer performance on these tests would be strongly associated with lower perceptions of physical health and functioning, as reflected in the PCS [38]. Additionally, these variables are also important predictors of common aging‐related conditions, such as sarcopenia, which can significantly impact the quality of life in older adults [39, 40, 41].

Based on the results, only ADL and PA were significantly associated with MCS after adjusting for cofounders. ADL, which reflects an individual's ability to perform basic self‐care tasks [17]. When individuals experience difficulty with ADLs, it can lead to a loss of independence, potentially contributing to feelings of frustration or helplessness, which can still affect mental health even after accounting for mood disorders like depression and anxiety [42, 43]. A study also demonstrated that ADLs can impact quality of life both directly and indirectly [44]. Similarly, PA is known to have wide‐ranging benefits for mental health, beyond its effects on mood disorders [45]. PA has been shown to enhance emotional well‐being by improving sleep, reducing stress, and promoting cognitive and social engagement, all of which can contribute to better mental health outcomes [46, 47, 48].

The present study found a significant association between all physical function measurements and well‐being, except the balance test. Consistent with previous studies, the present study showed an association between the SPPB [49, 50], ADL [51], IADL [51], and PA [52] with well‐being. Studies have shown that improved physical performance, regardless of the physical dimension, can reflect neurobiological mechanisms, psychological factors, and behavioral changes. These include the release of endorphins, a sense of accomplishment, and healthier lifestyle choices, all of which contribute to better overall well‐being [53, 54, 55, 56]. The findings indicate that physical function serves as a significant determinant of well‐being, emphasizing the importance of maintaining and enhancing physical performance across various dimensions. Notably, the absence of a significant association with the balance test suggests that specific components of physical function may exert differential impacts on well‐being. Furthermore, the relationship between physical function and well‐being may be bidirectional, wherein improved well‐being could foster increased engagement in physical activities, thereby reinforcing this positive interplay [57].

Limitations and Strengths

This study has several noteworthy limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its cross‐sectional design limits the ability to establish temporal or causal relationships, making it impossible to determine whether the observed associations reflect cause‐and‐effect or are influenced by unmeasured confounding factors. This design also restricts insight into the underlying mechanisms driving the relationships between variables. Second, the reliance on self‐reported measures for subjective assessments introduces the potential for reporting bias, which may arise from memory inaccuracies, social desirability, or individual interpretation. Third, the dichotomization of key variables, such as ADL and IADL, may oversimplify the continuum of physical function, potentially obscuring nuanced variations and limiting the depth of analysis. Lastly, while the study population is diverse, its focus on a specific Iranian cohort may limit the generalizability of findings to broader or more heterogeneous populations.

Despite these limitations, the study has several notable strengths. First, the large sample size (3,480 participants) enhances the statistical power and reliability of the findings, allowing for more precise estimates and reducing the likelihood of random error. Second, the study adopts a holistic approach by simultaneously evaluating both objective and subjective measures of physical function, providing a more comprehensive understanding of their associations with HRQoL and well‐being. Third, the detailed examination of HRQoL through its distinct components (PCS and MCS) enables a nuanced exploration of the differential impact of physical function on physical and mental health domains. Finally, integrating multiple validated tools, including the SPPB and WHO‐5, underscores the methodological rigor and robustness of the study.

Conclusion

This study emphasizes the crucial role of both objective and subjective measures of physical function in shaping HRQoL and overall well‐being among older adults in Iran. Objective assessments, such as the SPPB and TUG, were closely linked to the PCS, whereas subjective measures, including ADL performance and physical activity levels, were more strongly associated with the MCS. These findings highlight how perceived physical abilities and actual functional capacity together influence mental and physical health.

The results suggest that interventions should take a dual approach: enhancing physical capacities) such as mobility, balance, and strength (through structured exercise programs, while also supporting subjective aspects, such as engagement in daily activities and maintaining independence. Tailored exercise routines, combined with strategies that encourage an active lifestyle and functional autonomy, have the potential to improve both physical and mental dimensions of HRQoL. Moreover, the links observed between ADL, physical activity, and mental health underscore the importance of public health initiatives and community programs that promote functional independence and active aging. Implementing culturally sensitive, multidimensional interventions can help optimize resilience, quality of life, and overall well‐being among older adults.
