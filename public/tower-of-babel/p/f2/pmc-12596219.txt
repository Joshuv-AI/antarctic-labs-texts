Distinct theta oscillation coherence patterns during visual selective attention in children with and without attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder
Authors: Jialiang Guo, Yuanjun Kong, Bingkun Li, Xiangsheng Luo, Chen Dang, Jipeng Huang, Xuye Yuan, Li Sun, Yan Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Children with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) have problems with visual selective attention. The present study explored the phase coherence connectivity of theta oscillations in 76 typically developing (TD) children and 85 medication-naive children with ADHD (7–13 years) while they performed a visual search task. We identified distinct coherence modulation patterns in the theta frequency band in the TD and ADHD groups, with weaker coherence between the frontal and target-contralateral posterior areas than between the frontal and target-ipsilateral posterior areas. TD children demonstrated significant coherence modulation between frontal and right posterior (FRP) areas, whereas children with ADHD showed predominant modulation between frontal and left posterior (FLP) areas. Trials with greater FLP modulation were associated with faster responses in the ADHD group and more stable responses in the TD group. FLP modulation increased with age in TD children. These findings indicate that the theta coherence modulation between the frontal and posterior areas may reflect the efficient suppression of bottom-up sensory processing to prioritize top-down control. Compared with TD children, children with ADHD develop distinct specialized brain networks for visual attention. The FLP modulation in children with ADHD may signify compensatory maturation of neural networks involved in visual attention processes.

Introduction

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurobehavioural disorder characterized by symptoms such as inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity that affects more than 5 % of children and adolescents (Willcutt, 2012). One of the most prominent features of ADHD is problems with visual selective attention (Huang-Pollock et al., 2005, Mason et al., 2003). Previous research has indicated a weakening of posterior neural activities in response to target selection and distractor inhibition in individuals with ADHD (Cross-Villasana et al., 2015, Wang et al., 2016). Our previous study revealed significantly attenuated theta oscillations and increased alpha modulation in posterior regions in children with ADHD during stimulus-driven visual selective attention tasks (Guo et al., 2023). These findings indicate impairments in executive function (evidenced by theta oscillations in frontal regions) and compensatory mechanisms within the top-down attention network (reflected by alpha modulation in posterior regions) in children with ADHD. These findings underscore the pivotal role of neural oscillations in the frontal and posterior regions in visual selective attention.

Functional connectivity across multiple brain areas reflects the cooperation of distributed neural activities to form an organized neural network to enable complex cognitive function and various behaviours (Bullmore and Sporns, 2009, Shephard et al., 2019). In electroencephalography (EEG) studies, attenuated amplitude correlations between frontal theta and posterior alpha oscillations have been found in children with ADHD during visual target recognition (Mazaheri et al., 2010, Mazaheri et al., 2014). Notably, reduced amplitude-based connectivity between frontal theta and lateralized posterior alpha modulations occurs in children with ADHD when targets appear in the lateral visual field (Guo et al., 2023). The diminished connections in children with ADHD are thought to reflect deficits in top-down attentional control in the gating of visual information, suggesting that impaired functional connectivity between the frontal and posterior areas may play a crucial role in the attentional difficulties commonly observed in children with ADHD.

In addition to amplitude correlations, large-scale neuronal interactions in the phase coherence of synchronized signals across distinct brain regions have been observed. The phase coherence of theta oscillations is thought to represent long-distance cortical communication associated with large-scale cognitive integration (Caruso et al., 2023, Palva and Palva, 2011, Rosjat et al., 2018, Schnitzler and Gross, 2005). Resting-state EEG studies have revealed widespread increases in theta coherence in frontal and posterior regions in children with ADHD (Ahmadi et al., 2021, Barry et al., 2002, Barry et al., 2005, Robbie et al., 2016), which are thought to reflect atypical cortical differentiation and specialization of brain networks.

Task-state EEG studies have shown that theta coherence between the frontal and posterior areas is significantly increased during tasks requiring more cognitive control resources (Kamzanova et al., 2020, Lorist et al., 2009, Sauseng et al., 2005). Furthermore, theta coherence is involved in various cognitive processes, including negative feedback processing (Demiral et al., 2017, Nurislamova et al., 2019), proactive task switching (López et al., 2019), temporal-spatial integration (Dezfouli et al., 2021), speech-image association (Luo et al., 2010, Ramos-Escobar et al., 2021), and novel instruction implementation (Formica et al., 2022). Notably, increased theta coherence in the frontoparietal network has been observed during the processing of visual spatial attention information (Daitch et al., 2013, Park et al., 2019), highlighting the crucial role of this coherence mechanism in the integration of spatial information.

Studies on ADHD have revealed distinct oscillation coherence patterns during visual target recognition in children with ADHD (Hernández-Andrade et al., 2022, Lin et al., 2024). Specifically, increased connectivity within the theta coherence network during cognitive processes related to visual vigilance and target preparation has been observed in children with ADHD (Shephard et al., 2019). This increased connectivity is thought to reflect excessive bottom-up processing of sensory information, which may inundate circuitry responsible for top-down attentional control (Shephard et al., 2019). These findings suggest that abnormal inter-regional coherence in theta oscillations may be associated with cognitive dysfunction in children with ADHD. However, to our knowledge, no published studies have examined theta coherence in children with ADHD during visual selective attention tasks.

Previous studies have demonstrated that visual spatial attention is closely associated with neural oscillations in distinct frontal and bilateral posterior regions (Guo et al., 2019, Guo et al., 2023, Mazaheri et al., 2010, Mazaheri et al., 2014). Owing to the volume conduction properties of the brain, neighbouring electrodes typically display comparable oscillation patterns and connectivity profiles. Thus, in the present study, we utilized a novel methodology that combines cluster-based permutation tests with hierarchical clustering, aiming to identify regional connections between the frontal and bilateral posterior areas instead of performing network analysis centred on individual electrodes.

In the present study, we aimed to investigate the attention-orienting effects of EEG coherence between frontal and posterior areas in typically developing (TD) children and children with ADHD through a classic visual search task. We aimed to explore (1) whether EEG coherence was modulated by attention-orienting conditions in children, (2) whether there was a significant difference in EEG coherence modulation between TD children and children with ADHD, (3) the association between the modulation of theta coherence and behavioural responses, and (4) the relationship between the modulation of theta coherence and children's age.

Methods and materials

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Sixth Hospital/Institute of Mental Health of Peking University and met the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Before the experiment, we obtained verbal consent from all the participants and signed informed consent forms from their parents.

Participants

We recruited 197 children aged 7–13 years for the experiment, including 89 TD children and 108 children with ADHD. Participants with poor behavioural performance (accuracy of less than 60 % or reaction time [RT] 2.5 standard deviations longer than the average value) or excessive artefacts (less than 50 % trials remained) in EEG signals (TD children: n = 13; children with ADHD: n = 23) were excluded. Therefore, data from 76 TD children (57 boys, 19 girls) and 85 children with ADHD (68 boys, 17 girls) were included in subsequent analyses. Children with ADHD were clinically diagnosed by professional psychiatrists using a diagnostic interview based on DSM-IV criteria. The severity of ADHD symptoms was scored using the parent ADHD Rating Scale-IV. Children with ADHD were diagnosed with the inattentive subtype (n = 61) or the combined subtype (n = 24). None of the children had a history of ADHD medication use before the experiment. TD children were screened for ADHD symptoms using the ADHD Rating Scale, which was completed by their parents. The intelligence quotient (IQ) of the participants was measured with the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC). All the participants met the following criteria: (1) were right-handed; (2) had normal or corrected-to-normal vision; (3) had normal IQ scores (above 80); (4) had no history of neurological illness or serious mental diseases; and (5) had no current diagnosis of schizophrenia, major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder or autism spectrum disorder. There was no significant difference in age or sex ratio between the two groups (ps >.447). The children in the ADHD group had lower IQ scores and higher ADHD symptom scores than did the children in the TD group (ps <.001). Attention task

The task used in this study was a classic visual search paradigm (shown in Fig. 1 A). In each trial, a fixation cross was first presented in the centre of the screen for 900–1100 ms, followed by a circular search array for 200 ms, and finally, a fixation cross was presented in the centre until the key response was provided. The search array was a ring consisting of a filled circle (target) and 11 diamonds. The solid circle appeared in one of four positions (2, 4, 8 or 10 o’clock) with equal probability (25 % for each position). The participants were asked to keep their gaze focused on the centre of the screen and determine whether the filled circle appeared in the top (2 or 10 o’clock) or bottom (4 or 8 o’clock) half of the screen. They responded by pressing two buttons orthogonal to the screen. Pressing the correct button within 1500 ms was marked as a correct response. Only trials with correct responses were included in the EEG analyses. The experiment consisted of 8 blocks, each containing 30 trials.Fig. 1Experimental paradigm and behavioural results. EEG recording and processing

EEG data were recorded from 128 electrodes (HydroCel Geodesic Sensor Net, Electrical Geodesics, Inc., Eugene, OR), with electrode Cz used as an online reference. The signals were recorded at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz. The impedance of all the electrodes was less than 50 kΩ.

EEG data were preprocessed using the EEGLAB toolbox in the MATLAB environment (Delorme and Makeig, 2004). We excluded data from the 32 outermost electrodes from the analyses because they were vulnerable to body motion artefacts (Supplementary Materials Figure S1). This method was also used in previous studies (Debnath et al., 2019). EEG data were downsampled to 250 Hz and bandpass filtered at 1–30 Hz using a zero-phase finite impulse response (FIR) filter. We used the spherical method to interpolate the signals of bad electrodes (<10 % for each participant) and then re-referenced all recordings to the average of the recordings by the bilateral mastoid electrodes (E57 and E100). EEG data from −800–1000 ms around the stimulus onset were extracted for segmentation. Eye blinks and horizontal movements were detected using a step function on the basis of the Fp1/2 average signal (threshold: 75 μV, sliding window: 200 ms) and the F9/10 difference signal (threshold: 50 μV, sliding window: 100 ms) between −50 and 250 ms. Trials with excessive eye blinks or horizontal movements were removed. After the EEG segments containing apparent artefacts were manually removed, independent component analysis (ICA) was performed to remove components with electrooculographic origins (Delorme and Makeig, 2004, Jung et al., 2000). This rigorous data processing pipeline, combined with our strict participant selection and standardized stimulus presentation, provides for the essential control of visual and ophthalmic function in participants, which is critical in visual attention research (Y.X. Wang et al., 2025). Following this, to remove extreme artefacts, trials with EEG amplitudes exceeding 100 μV were automatically excluded from the analysis.

Phase-based connectivity index

We used custom scripts and the FieldTrip toolbox to perform time—frequency analysis (Oostenveld et al., 2011). The time—frequency plural representation was produced using a continuous Morlet wavelet transform ranging from 2 to 12 Hz in 21 linearly spaced steps in the period of −300–500 ms (resolution: 10 ms). The number of wavelet cycles was linearly spaced from 2 to 7 cycles. For each time—frequency interval, the phase-based connectivity between each pair of electrodes was represented by the weighted phase lag index (WPLI). The WPLI is a measure of the phase coherence of two signals, weighted by the magnitude of the imaginary part of the cross-spectrum (Vinck et al., 2011). This method reduces the sensitivity of the index to noise sources, common references and volume conduction. The formula is as follows:WPLI=EImXij/EImXij=EImXijsgnImXij/EImXijwhere Xij represents the cross-spectrum density of signals i and j, the operator Im denotes the imaginary part of a complex number, the operator E denotes the expected value, and the operator sgn refers to the sign function. The WPLI value ranges between zero and one, with one indicating the strongest coupling and zero indicating no coupling.

We used the modulation index (MI) to analyse the attention-orienting effect of the WPLI. The MI was computed by subtracting the WPLI calculated for the right-target trials from that calculated for the left-trial trials for each pair of electrodes. The subtraction was normalized by dividing by the average of the two WPLI values. The formula is as follows:MI=(WPLILeft target−WPLIRight target)/[(WPLILeft target+WPLIRight target)/2]

Statistical analysis of network connections

To investigate the orienting effects of EEG coherence, we used cluster-based permutation tests (1000 iterations) to examine the neighbouring connections for which the MI value deviated significantly from zero (Maris and Oostenveld, 2007). The method controls for multiple comparisons by identifying significant neighbouring clusters of edges rather than independent edges. For the time—frequency analysis, we first calculated the average MI value specifically for the connections between the frontal and bilateral (left and right) posterior areas and then combined two adjacent time—frequency bins in which significant connections were found into a cluster. The electrodes of the frontal and bilateral posterior areas are shown in Supplementary Materials Figure S1.

For analysis within specific time—frequency intervals, two edges were defined as neighbouring if they shared a common endpoint node and their other endpoints were adjacent nodes. A t test was employed to assess the presence of experimental effects for each edge, with a significance level of 0.05. Neighbouring edges that show significant effects were grouped into a cluster, and the measured statistical values within each cluster were summed for analysis. To generate the null distribution, the condition labels were randomly permuted 1000 times. For each permutation, the maximum absolute value of the summed statistical values was recorded. The conditional effect was considered statistically significant if the sum of the measured statistical values fell into the 2.5 % extreme ends of the null distribution (two-tailed).

We used the Jaccard distance to measure the similarity between two nodes. For two nodes (labelled i and j), if the nodes were adjacent, we listed the sets of significantly connected nodes for each node (Ni for node i, Nj for node j). If the two nodes were not adjacent, the Jaccard distance was defined as one. The Jaccard distance is calculated as one minus the ratio of the intersection size to the union size of the two sets. The formula is shown below.Di,j=1−Ni∩Nj/Ni∪Nj,Nodeiandjare adjacent1,Nodeiandjare non−adjacentwhere |Ni∩Nj| represents the element number of the intersection of Ni and Nj, and |Ni∪Nj| represents the element number in the union of Ni and Nj.

We employed a hierarchical clustering method to identify node clusters in the network on the basis of the pairwise node distance. To minimize the impact of spurious connections, we limited the clustering analysis to nodes with at least three connections. A node was grouped into a cluster if its distance to any node within the cluster was less than a threshold of 0.5 within the frontal and posterior areas.

Trial-level correlation between phase-based connectivity and behaviour

We used a jackknife correlation (JC) method to estimate single-trial correlations between the MI and RT. The JC has been proven to be equivalent to an ordinary correlation (Richter et al., 2015). The JC method involves a leave-one-out resampling technique in which a parameter is calculated by leaving out each individual sample in a dataset and calculating the parameter over all remaining samples. The jackknife replications for each measure were defined as follows:Si=Sx1,x2,…,xi−1,xi+1,…,xnwhere S represents the operating of calculating the measure over samples x. This operation involves computing the measure n times, as each of the samples xi is systematically left out. This results in n jackknife replications of S. In terms of our experiment, we calculated the jackknife replications of the MI and behavioural indicators (mean RT (RTM) and standard deviation of the RT (RTSD)) for each participant and then calculated the Pearson correlation coefficients between the jackknife replications. The correlation coefficients were then converted to Fisher’s z values to obtain a normally distributed variable.

Statistical analysis

We used analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare EEG variables between groups. A simple effect analysis was performed if the ANOVA revealed an interaction effect. IQ was not controlled as a covariate because it is considered an intrinsic part of the ADHD phenotype (Dennis et al., 2009). We used Pearson correlation analysis to explore the relationships between two indicators.

Results

Behavioural data

We utilized the inverse efficiency score (IES) to assess the behavioural performance of the participants. The IES, computed by dividing the RTM by the accuracy, is a comprehensive metric that effectively mitigates the impact of the trade-off between the response speed and accuracy. Additionally, we employed the coefficient of variation of the reaction time (RTCV) to quantify the variability in behavioural performance. The RTCV, derived by dividing RTSD by RTM, provides a standardized measure that effectively accounts for differences in the magnitude of the response speed. Compared with the TD group, the ADHD group presented significantly greater IES values (F(1159) = 4.780, p = .030, ηp2 = .029) and RTCV values (F(1159) = 5.895, p = .016, ηp2 = .036). The results are shown in Fig. 1B.

Theta coherence between frontal and left posterior areas

For the connection between the frontal and left posterior (FLP) areas, the MI values in the ADHD group were significantly greater than zero in 136 time—frequency intervals ranging from 2 to 5.5 Hz (p < .001, Fig. 2 A). These intervals fell within the time-frequency range in which phase coherence was significantly increased, as shown in Supplementary Materials Figure S2. The MI values for all connections within these intervals are illustrated in Fig. 2 C. For significant connections in these intervals, 51 nodes in the frontal area were grouped into one cluster, 13 nodes in the left posterior area were grouped into another cluster, with 550 connections between the two clusters (FLP network, p < .001, shown in Fig. 3 A and B). We averaged the MI value in the theta band (2–5.5 Hz) in the FLP network for analyses. The cluster-based permutation test indicated that the theta FLP MI value was significantly lower than zero from 130 to 360 ms (p < .001, shown in Fig. 4 A). Theta coherence between frontal and right posterior areas

For the connection between the frontal and right posterior (FRP) areas, the MI values in the TD group were significantly lower than zero in 74 time–frequency intervals ranging from 2.5 to 4.5 Hz (p = .022, Fig. 2B). These intervals fell within the time-frequency range in which phase coherence was significantly increased, as shown in Supplementary Materials Figure S2. The MI values for all connections within these intervals are illustrated in Fig. 2D. For significant connections in these intervals, 43 nodes in the frontal area were grouped into one cluster, 13 nodes in the right posterior area were grouped into another cluster, with 400 connections between the two clusters (FRP network, p < .001, shown in Fig. 3 C and D). We averaged the MI value in the theta band (2.5–4.5 Hz) in the FRP network for analyses. The cluster-based permutation test revealed that the theta FRP MI was significantly lower than zero from 150 to 380 ms (p = .002, shown in Fig. 4 C). No significant MI results were detected for the ADHD group in the analyses (p = . 470).

In addition, we averaged the theta FRP MI value from 150 to 380 ms for analyses. The theta FRP MI was significantly lower than zero in the TD group (t(75) = -5.054, p < .001, d = .580) but not in the ADHD group (t(84) = -1.333, p = .186, d = . 145). Moreover, the theta FRP MI in the TD group was significantly lower than that in the ADHD group (F(1159) = 4.173, p = .043, ηp2 = .026). The results are shown in Fig. 4D.

Discussion

In the present study, we first identified distinct theta coherence modulation patterns between the frontal and posterior areas in TD children and children with ADHD during a visual selective attention task. For the TD group, the modulation was characterized mainly by significantly lower FRP coherence in the left-target condition than in the right-target condition (FRP MI < 0). In contrast, the modulation in the ADHD group was characterized primarily by greater FLP coherence in the left-target condition than in the right-target condition (FLP MI > 0). Trials that contributed to higher FLP MI in the theta band were associated with faster RTM values in children with ADHD and lower RTSD values in TD children. Furthermore, the theta FLP MI was positively correlated with age in the TD group.

The modulation patterns in both groups revealed weaker theta coherence between the frontal and target-contralateral posterior areas than between the frontal and target-ipsilateral posterior areas. This modulation highlights the connection between visual spatial attention control and the frontoparietal network (Petersen and Posner, 2012) and supports hemispheric differentiation in terms of function (Szczepanski et al., 2010, Szczepanski and Kastner, 2013). Previous studies have indicated that theta activity in the posterior area is closely related to bottom-up attentional capture and the processing of visual features (Harris et al., 2017). Research in macaques has revealed that theta coherence between the frontal and parietal regions is linked to the processing of task-relevant stimuli in automated attention control (Phillips et al., 2014). These findings highlight the role of theta coherence in the processing of bottom-up visual information.

The modulation in FRP regions in TD children is consistent with right hemispheric dominance in the ventral attention network (Bartolomeo and Malkinson, 2019, Corbetta et al., 2008), which is responsible for bottom-up attention and detecting unexpected stimuli. The right-lateralized ventral attention network receives forward connections from the visual cortex (Vossel et al., 2012) and is closely linked to the microstructural integrity of the frontoparietal white matter (Kocsis et al., 2019). Increased theta coherence is thought to reflect excessive sensory processing that inundates the circuitry responsible for top-down executive and attentional control (Heinrichs-Graham et al., 2014, Shephard et al., 2019). Thus, we speculate that the attenuated theta coherence between the frontal and target-contralateral posterior areas may reflect the weakening of bottom-up processing, thereby reducing the inundation for top-down processes. The modulation of theta coherence in FRP regions may reflect the functional differentiation of attention networks related to visual selective attention.

The coherence modulation in FRP regions is markedly diminished in children with ADHD. Prior functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies have demonstrated that children with ADHD exhibit decreased cortical thickness in the right temporoparietal junction (You et al., 2024). Additionally, reduced connectivity between the middle frontal gyrus and the inferior parietal lobule in the right hemisphere has been observed in children with ADHD, with the connection strength being negatively correlated with the severity of ADHD symptoms (Y.P. Wang et al., 2025). These findings suggest that the attentional and cognitive deficits in children with ADHD may stem from structural and functional alterations in specific brain regions, as well as disruptions in their connectivity. The reduced coherence modulation in FRP regions suggests an impairment in the integration of neural information, a process that is essential for visual attention and regulating executive functions.

Significant modulation of connections in FLP regions was also observed in children with ADHD. The modulation in the FLP regions facilitated faster behavioural responses in children with ADHD and more consistent responses in the TD group. This correlation aligns with the hypothesis that modulation between the frontal and posterior regions facilitates behavioural performance. Prior research has revealed that nicotine, a nicotinic acetylcholine receptor agonist, effectively improves the behavioural performance of patients with ADHD and increases activation in the frontal cortex and the left parietal cortex (Ettinger et al., 2017, Kasparbauer et al., 2019). Notably, the brain regions activated by nicotine considerably overlap with the areas implicated in FLP coherence modulation in children with ADHD. Given that nicotine is a stimulant that can modulate brain activity, the activation of these overlapping brain regions in children with ADHD might be a neural strategy for addressing the underlying cognitive processing deficits associated with this disorder. These regions may be activated more strongly in an attempt to achieve normal cognitive function. Prior fMRI studies have reported that children with advanced cognitive capabilities exhibit increased functional connectivity between frontal regions (such as the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and anterior cingulate cortex) and the posterior parietal cortex (Langeslag et al., 2013, Sherman et al., 2014). Furthermore, theta oscillations in the frontal region are closely related to the development of intelligence (Rico-Pico et al., 2021, Wang et al., 2022), and activation in the parietal region during visual attention tasks is associated with cognitive development (Fassbender et al., 2015). Thus, we speculate that the increased theta modulation in FLP regions in children with ADHD may reflect a compensatory mechanism related to cognitive processing. This mechanism likely contributes to improved behavioural control and enhanced overall behavioural performance. This mechanism may play a crucial role in the cognitive development of children with ADHD.

Furthermore, we observed a positive correlation between age and FLP modulation in the TD group. Previous studies have revealed that age-related alterations in brain networks are characterized by reductions in short-range functional connectivity and increases in long-range functional connectivity (Fair et al., 2009, Supekar et al., 2009). Macroscopic changes are thought to reflect progressive (e.g., myelination) and regressive neural events (e.g., synaptogenesis and synaptic pruning) in the process of brain maturation (Kharitonova et al., 2013, Khundrakpam et al., 2016). Cortical activation in the frontal and parietal regions is closely associated with the maturation process and white matter development (Klingberg, 2006, Tamnes et al., 2013, Wang et al., 2021). Therefore, the excessively elevated FLP modulation in children with ADHD may reflect the premature development of neural structures associated with progressive or/and regressive neural processes. This finding implies that the development of visual attention in children with ADHD might deviate from the typical developmental trajectory, which aligns with the developmental deviation hypothesis of ADHD (Giertuga et al., 2017). These findings provide new evidence to explain the abnormal development of children with ADHD.

Notably, during processes associated with goal-directed visual spatial attention, reduced posterior alpha power in the left hemisphere has been observed in children with ADHD (Guo et al., 2019), as well as increased theta amplitude-based connectivity between the FRP areas (Guo et al., 2020). This finding manifests as a deficit in the left attentional orienting system and compensation from the right attentional network. This pattern contrasts with the phase coherence features identified in the present study, which were observed during a stimulus-induced visual attention task. Consequently, we speculate that the two forms of attention may be differentially associated with changes in distinct dimensions (amplitude or phase) of brain oscillations and that these dimensional features may be associated with distinct patterns of hemispheric differentiation. The exploration of these potential correlations between brain oscillations and attentional network activities represents a compelling direction for future research, offering promising insights into the neural mechanisms underlying attentional processes in children with typical or atypical development trajectories.

Limitations

Although we identified distinct FRP modulation patterns in TD children, we observed no significant correlation between FRP modulation and behavioural responses or individual characteristics. This lack of correlation may stem from methodological limitations inherent in our experimental task. The high performance across trials in the relatively simple task likely attenuated variability in FRP modulation, thereby reducing the statistical power to detect meaningful associations. A ceiling effect, in which the neural responses failed to differentiate successful and suboptimal performance, may have occurred owing to the simplicity of the task. To address this limitation, future studies could systematically manipulate task difficulty by varying cognitive load or attention requirements. This approach would reveal whether FRP modulation dynamically adapts to task demands in cognitively challenging tasks. Furthermore, while our findings support a compensatory role for enhanced FLP modulation in ADHD, its precise functional role remains ambiguous. It is possible that the observed activity reflects adaptive compensation, inherent neurophysiological features such as attentional instability, or an interaction of both. Future research specifically designed to dissociate these possibilities is needed to clarify the nature of this modulation.

The estimation of EEG functional connectivity may be confounded by the inherent influence of volume conduction artefacts. Future studies should integrate multimodal neuroimaging, such as magnetic resonance imaging and diffusion tensor imaging, to clarify the structural foundations of the observed dynamics and enhance the interpretability of EEG-derived connectivity (Luo et al., 2022). Furthermore, dynamic functional connectivity analysis could uncover transient network states, providing a more comprehensive functional framework (You et al., 2022).

Conclusions

The present study reveals distinct patterns of theta coherence modulation between frontal and lateral posterior areas during visual selective attention in TD children and children with ADHD. The FRP modulation in TD children may reflect efficient suppression of bottom-up sensory processing to prioritize top-down control. In contrast, the increased FLP modulation in children with ADHD may serve as a compensatory mechanism to offset deficits in attentional control. Neural alterations in FLP regions may reflect deviations in typical developmental trajectories, such as premature or dysregulated connectivity changes. These findings underscore the role of theta coherence in balancing bottom-up and top-down attention processes, highlight functional and developmental differences in children with ADHD and TD children, and provide critical insights into the neural mechanisms underlying visual attention.
