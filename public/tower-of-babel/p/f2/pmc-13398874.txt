Metabolomic Analysis Reveals Structural Diversity of Pectic Polysaccharides From Prunus persica (L.) Batsch and Their Relationship With Purees Characteristics
Authors: Meng Liu, Xuan Liu, Shaojie Fu, Qianhui Yang, Christophe Blecker, Jinfeng Bi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo clarify how pectin heterogeneity relates to puree quality, this study investigated associations among pectin fractions, metabolite profiles, and physicochemical properties of peach purees from 20 cultivars with distinct viscosity, turbidity, and colour. Metabolomic analysis showed that sugars, organic acids, and phenolic compounds were closely associated with the yield of chelator‐soluble pectin (CSP), indicating coordinated variation between metabolite profiles and structurally integrated pectin fractions across cultivars. CSP yield was further related to resistance to deformation, as reflected by yield stress. In contrast, rheological behaviour after yielding was more strongly associated with water‐soluble pectin (WSP), whose yield and structural features were linked to viscosity, viscoelastic balance, and flow behaviour. Phenolic compounds such as taxifolin and phlorizin chalcone were associated with puree colour. Collectively, these results establish an applied evaluation framework linking metabolite variation, pectin fraction distribution, and cultivar‐dependent quality traits. By connecting micro‐biochemical compositions (metabolite profiles and pectin fractions) with macroscopic processing‐related properties, this framework provides insights into cell wall‐related characteristics relevant to peach puree quality and processing performance.

Introduction

Peaches ( Prunus persica (L.) Batch) belong to the Rosaceae family. China boasts a significant population of wild peach relatives and landraces that exhibit greater genetic diversity and integrity. To avoid physiological deterioration after harvest, pureeing is one of the processing methods to extend shelf life and reduce loss. As a vital component, the effect of pectin on the quality of purees has been widely investigated in carrot (Christiaens, Van Buggenhout, et al. 2012), blueberry (Chevalier et al. 2017), broccoli (Christiaens, Mbong, et al. 2012), cherry laurel (García‐Pérez et al. 2024), and black plum (Mohammadi‐Moghaddam et al. 2020). Pectin contains homogalacturonan (HG), rhamnogalacturonan I (RG‐I), rhamnogalacturonan II (RG‐II), and xylogalacturonan (XGA) regions (Ke et al. 2023; Zhu et al. 2023; Asimakopoulou et al. 2024). Pectin could be extracted sequentially from hot water, chelating agent, and sodium carbonate, namely water‐soluble pectin (WSP), chelator‐soluble pectin (CSP), and sodium carbonate‐soluble pectin (SSP). Unlike WSP, CSP, and SSP are attached to the cell wall via ionic and covalent bonds, suggesting the preservation of the structural integrity and hardness in the plant matrix (Kaczmarska et al. 2024).

To date, all evidence suggests that pectin was synthesized in the Golgi and transported to the wall in membrane vesicles (Mohnen 2008; Caffall and Mohnen 2009; Atmodjo et al. 2013). Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism produce UDP‐glucuronic acid, which serves as the shared precursor for arabinose (Ara), xylose (Xyl), galacturonic acid (GalA), and apiose residues present in the cell wall (Reboul et al. 2011). Moreover, Caffall and Mohnen (2009) showed pectin molecules were cross‐linked by phenolic compounds (more than 2%) and structural proteins (2%–10%) of wall dry weight and speculated that para‐coumaryl and feruloyl acids presented the opportunity for cross‐linking found in the wall of Arabidopsis. Thus, the cell wall structural integrity, cell adhesion, and signal transduction are supported by co‐cross‐linking among pectin, hemicellulose, cellulose, phenolics, and some structural proteins.

At the postharvest stage, the breakdown of cell wall polysaccharides provides glycosylation and power for other important cellular pathways and processes (Hu et al. 2024). The relationship between fruit weight (or size) and sugar content, like sucrose, fructose (Fru), glucose (Glc), and sorbitol, has obvious implications for fruit quality (Yu et al. 2018; Bie et al. 2023). For example, the ratio between sugars and acids, the overall organic acids concentration, the amount of citrate and shikimate, and fruit juiciness were related to fruit sweetness (Cirilli et al. 2016). Despite being a crucial polysaccharide in the cell wall, the molecular mechanisms underlying pectin biosynthesis in peaches have been infrequently investigated. The majority of existing research has reported the effects of pectin structure on the quality of purees (Wani et al. 2021; Zhou et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2024). Nevertheless, the co‐regulatory mechanisms among metabolites related to peach pectin biosynthesis remain poorly understood. A comprehensive elucidation of these intricate biological processes has yet to be documented. Therefore, it is of great significance to investigate pectin diversity among cultivars from the aspect of metabolome in peach to understand their role in tailoring the quality of puree.

Herein, this study aimed to investigate cultivar‐dependent variation in pectin fractions (WSP, CSP, and SSP), metabolite profiles, and physicochemical and rheological properties of peach purees across 20 cultivars. Using multivariate statistical analysis, associations among metabolites, pectin structural features, and processing‐related quality traits were explored. In addition, a pathway‐based, putative framework was proposed to contextualize the relationships between core metabolites and pectin biosynthesis, providing a basis for understanding cell wall‐related characteristics in peach fruit.

Results

Primary Structure of Pectin Fractions From 20 Varieties

In Table S1, the yield of CSP (Y c) took the highest proportion in alcohol‐insoluble residues (AIR) (32.89%). Molecular weight (M w) of WSP ranged from 143 to 538 kDa, while CSP was 353 kDa to 724 kDa, and SSP was 172 kDa to 662 kDa. Besides, WSP had the widest molecular weight distribution. The results remained generally consistent with other research (Ni et al. 2023). The neutral sugar indicated that WSP and CSP were HG‐type pectic polysaccharides due to the high ratio of GalA and degree of HG region (WSP: 64.74%, CSP: 69.70%, Table S1). On the contrary, the RG‐I region constituted 85% in SSP. The results aligned with previous research (Deng et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2023). The average level of GalA in CSP (71.47, mol%) was higher than that in WSP (66.41, mol%). However, HG ratios of CSP in 3# (31.67%), 9# (47.75%), 12# (28.52%), and 17# (30.01%) were less than 50%, indicating a shorter HG region. Interestingly, (Ara + Gal)/Rha (branching degree of G‐I, R1) of SSP (11.50%) was lower than WSP (14.86%) and CSP (13.64%), highlighting longer arabinan, galactan, or arabinogalactan side chains.

In Figure S1, the FT‐IR region between 4000 and 2700 cm−1 was dominated by stretching vibrations of OH and CH groups. WSP exhibited a stronger stretching vibration of esterified C=O at 1745 cm−1 compared with CSP, indicating a higher degree of esterification (DE). Based on the relative intensities of esterified and carboxylate groups, WSP was characterized as high‐methyl‐esterified pectin (DE = 68.43%), whereas CSP was classified as low‐methyl‐esterified pectin (DE = 30.94%). The difference in DE between WSP and CSP can be attributed to their distinct localization and binding states in the plant cell wall. The lower degree of esterification of CSP can be attributed to its chelating‐soluble nature. CSP is primarily extracted from pectin domains that are cross‐linked by Ca2+ through ‘egg‐box’ structures, which preferentially involve low‐methyl‐esterified homogalacturonan regions (Broxterman et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2020). In contrast, WSP represents more loosely associated pectin fractions that retain a higher level of methyl esterification. Differences in DE change the balance between ‐COOH and ‐COOCH3 groups, thereby regulating viscosity through competing hydrogen bonding, electrostatic repulsion, and hydrophobic aggregation effects, which leads to distinct rheological properties (Jiao et al. 2025). Previous studies have shown that differences in methyl esterification among pectin fractions, particularly WSP, and CSP, can influence intermolecular interactions and aggregation behaviour, thereby affecting particle organization and ultimately contributing to variations in texture and rheological properties of fruit purees (Pan et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2025), which would possess potential application prospects in special food for children, elderly, dysphagia patients, etc. Moreover, SSP was considered a de‐esterified polysaccharide. The results were consistent with previous research (Barbieri et al. 2019). In Figure S1D, CSP and SSP exhibited higher intensities in the 1500–1200 cm−1 region, with bands around ~1400 cm−1 (shifted to 1407 cm−1 in CSP and to 1413 cm−1 in SSP). This region is generally associated with the stretching vibration of carboxylate groups (COO−) in pectic polysaccharides (Szymańska‐Chargot et al. 2024). The observed band shifts and intensity differences likely reflect variations in pectin domains and their ionic environment among different fractions. Compared with CSP and WSP, SSP showed higher intensities around 1100 cm−1 and 1020 cm−1. These bands fall within the region generally assigned to polysaccharide skeleton and ring vibrations. The increased intensity in this region is consistent with the monosaccharide composition analysis (Table S1), which showed a higher proportion of neutral sugars in SSP.

Physicochemical and Rheological Properties of Peach Purees

In Table S2, the yield of purees (Y purees) exceeded 89% with the smallest relative standard deviation (RSD) value (1.79%). There was also little difference in turbidity among cultivars (average value: 1531 NTU). Most of the varieties were beyond 15 mV, except 12# (14.83 mV), followed by 20# (the lowest value, 13.73 mV). Particularly, 17# held the highest value (25.47 mV), indicating a more stable system. Apart from these, the average value of number‐weighted mean diameter (D 50), volume‐weighted mean diameter (D[4,3]), and surface‐weighted mean diameter (D[3,2]) was 32.92, 459.49, and 278.94 μm, respectively. There was a significant difference among varieties in D 50 and D[4,3] (RSD > 17%).

In Figure S2, peach purees exhibited shear‐thinning characteristics and behaved like a pseudoplastic fluid. This observation was also in line with the flow behaviour index (n HB, n HB < 1, Table S2). However, there was a significant difference in apparent viscosity (η) among cultivars (RSD > 30%, Table S2). Compared to 3#, 14#, and 8#, the downtrend of viscosity about 17#, 4#, and 16# was more obvious with increased shear rate, suggesting weaker anti‐shear ability. According to the Herschel‐Buckley model, RSD of yield stress (τ 0) was more than 50% (Table S2), and 15# had the highest value (8.55 Pa). High τ 0 in purees might be attributed to the ability to exhibit self‐ and intermolecular aggregation of macromolecules.

The dynamic rheological property was further investigated. The results implied peach purees with elastic behaviour (loss factor, tanδ, tanδ = 0.15). Relatively lower tanδ was observed in 3#, 14#, and 8# might contribute to a higher elastic component, potentially leading to reduced miscibility of the puree with the saliva. Conversely, relatively higher tanδ noted in 11#, 13#, and 18# might imply higher miscibility and ease of swallowing. The power‐law model showed the average value of k′ was six times greater than k″, indicating a higher frequency dependence of loss moduli (G") in the purees. k′ in 5# (429.80) and 14# (550.81) were extremely high, suggesting stronger interaction among macromolecules to resist the destruction of external force. Moreover, the frequency dependence of storage modulus (n′) was < 1, indicating weak physical gels. Among them, 18# had the highest value (0.12), contributing to superior elastic characteristics. In short, peach purees exhibited weak gel‐like behaviours, which were easily destroyed when the external force exceeded a certain limit.

Core Structural Metrics of 20 Peach Varieties

In Table S3, seven factors highlighted structural characteristics of three pectin fractions in common, including yield (F5, F7), GalA content (F1, F2, F3), and ratios of HG (F1, F2, F3) and RG‐I (F1, F2, F3). In terms of peach purees, five factors explain 82.80% of the total variance, including particle size (F1), rheological parameters (F2, F3, F4), and Y purees (F5). Correlation analysis (Figure 1A, Table S4) revealed a positive relationship between η and the linear chain of WSP (HG region and GalA content), while a negative relationship with the RG‐I region and branching. As for CSP, R1c had a higher contribution to the zeta‐potential of purees. Regarding SSP, Y s, polydispersity (P s), GalAs, and RG‐Is were related to η, consistency coefficient (K HB), and Y purees. Based on the results, the following indices have been identified as core metrics. For purees, it contained D 50, D[4,3], D[3,2], τ 0, η, tanδ, Y purees. For structural features, it included Y w, GalAw, HGw, RG‐Iw, Y c, P c, GalAc, HGc, RG‐Ic, R1c, GalAs, and RG‐Is.

PCA‐Based Grouping and Representative Structural Patterns

Based on the core structural parameters, PCA was applied to explore the classification of the 20 peach varieties, and the results are shown in Figure 1B,C. The first two principal components explained 58% of the total variance. Several varieties clustered together due to similar pectin composition or structural characteristics. For example, varieties 2#, 6#, 10#, 11#, 15#, 18#, and 20# were closely distributed in the score plot (Figure 1B) and were all located in the fourth quadrant. As indicated by the loading plot (Figure 1C), GalAc and HGc were the dominant variables in this quadrant, suggesting a relatively higher contribution of these parameters in the seven varieties compared with others. Similarly, varieties 12# and 17# were nearly overlapping in the second quadrant and were closely associated with RG‐Ic. Varieties located near the coordinate axes, such as 13#, 14#, and 16#, were interpreted based on the variables positioned closest to their locations. Overall, the PCA score plot indicates that the samples can be descriptively categorized into four groups according to their distribution across the four quadrants.

The first group (G1) mainly focused on the ‘smooth region’ of WSP, containing varieties 1#, 8#, and 19#. The second group (G2, 3#, 12#, 17#, and 9#) included RG‐Ic and R1c, which represented the side chain of CSP. The third group (G3), referred to as the ‘yield of pectin fractions’, consisted of varieties 14#, 13#, 5#, 4#, 7#, and 16#. The majority of varieties (11#, 2#, 6#, 15#, 18#, 10#, and 20#) were included in the fourth group (G4), representing the ‘smooth region’ of CSP (GalAc, HGc). These groups would be beneficial for metabolome profiling to explore the connection between the development of structural attributes of pectin and metabolites. Furthermore, association analyses between these four groups and individual categorical cultivar‐related variables were systematically performed (Table S5). Maturity group (early, mid, and late maturity) showed no significant association with group assignment (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.829), with a low effect size (Cramér's V = 0.287), indicating that maturity contributed minimally to the statistical classification. In contrast, flesh colour and pubescence exhibited moderate to relatively strong association with group assignment (Cramér's V = 0.486 and 0.526, respectively), although these associations did not reach statistical significance (Monte Carlo χ2 tests, p > 0.05). Collectively, these analyses demonstrate that the statistically defined groups do not correspond strictly to predefined biological categories such as maturity or a single phenotypic trait. Instead, the grouping reflects composite phenotypic characteristics across multiple cultivar‐related attributes.

Metabolome‐Scale Comparative Analysis of Four Groups

In Figure S3, a total of 1449 metabolites were detected in peach. The largest proportion was phenolic acids (264 metabolites, 18.22%), followed by flavonoids (241 metabolites, 16.63%) and lipids (197 metabolites, 13.6%). The results were consistent with the previous study (Wu et al. 2024). Figure 2A,B illustrate a clear separation among four groups with a strong appropriateness of the model (Q 2 > 0.90). Based on 333 differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs, Figure 2C) across four groups, 1#, 4#, and 11# exhibited elevated Z‐scores in phenolic acids and flavonoids that contribute to the formation of fruit colour. Conversely, 8# demonstrated a significantly lower Z‐score for sugars and organic acids. The study revealed that these metabolites could enhance the storage ability of fruit due to the conversion of sugars through carbohydrate metabolism (Jin et al. 2024). Additionally, organic acids and sugars are usually taste‐relevant metabolites that are closely connected to the internal quality of processed products presented to customers.

Metabolic Pathway Analysis of Core Differential Metabolites

As shown in Figure 4, high‐ and low‐expression information in four groups was correspondingly labelled. In carbohydrate metabolism, the consumption of myo‐inositol was observed in all groups and was accompanied by variable accumulation of neutral sugar‐related metabolites, including arabinose, galactose, and mannose. Among them, G4 exhibited the highest levels of these metabolites. In contrast, in G1, myo‐inositol appeared to be preferentially utilized for the synthesis of the pectin backbone rather than for the accumulation of neutral sugars. G2 and G4 exhibited metabolic features associated with CSP‐related pectic domains, showing relatively higher levels of neutral sugar‐related metabolites. These results suggest that distinct pectin structural domains may be differentially regulated during pectin biosynthesis.

Glucose could be converted to pyruvate through the glycolysis pathway and then entered into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle to produce intermediate metabolites such as citrate, malate, and quinate, etc. Itaconic acid was one of the organic acids, but with less research in plants. However, itaconic acid was mainly produced from microorganisms (Zhu et al. 2021) and has been found to have anti‐inflammatory and antibacterial effects in mammals (Wang et al. 2022). In this study, G2 and G4 exhibited a higher level of itaconic acid. Interestingly, G2 and G4 emphasized the backbone and side chain of CSP. The accumulation of these organic acid‐related metabolites in G2 and G4 may reflect differences in carbon flux associated with cell wall polysaccharide biosynthesis, particularly in CSP‐enriched fractions. In addition, glucose could also be converted to phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) through the glycolysis pathway and then converted to shikimate via phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan biosynthesis to produce phenolic compounds. It has been reported that phenolic compounds not only represent the major molecules involved in pigmentation in plants, but also may interact with pectin and hemicellulose (Siemińska‐Kuczer et al. 2022). In Figure 4, G1 exhibited higher levels of chromogenic metabolites such as taxifolin, while G1 and G2 showed a greater consumption of caffeic acid compared with the other groups.

Overall, organic acids, sugars, and phenolic‐related compounds exhibited distinct accumulation patterns among the groups, reflecting substantial metabolic differentiation. These metabolic differences provide a biochemical context for the observed heterogeneity in structural characteristics of pectin and associated physicochemical, rheological, and colour properties of peach purees.

Discussion

This study revealed cultivar‐dependent associations among metabolite profiles, pectin structural characteristics, and cell wall‐related quality traits in peach. To further interpret these associations within a biological context, the proposed putative framework (Figure 4) integrates metabolite variation with previously reported genomic and quantitative trait locus (QTL) information. In this framework, sugars, organic acids, and phenolic compounds are considered as associated metabolites. Previous genomic and QTL studies in peach have demonstrated that multiple metabolites, including sugars, organic acids, amino acids, and flavonoids, are associated with loci distributed mainly on chromosomes 4 and 5 (Li et al. 2019; Cao et al. 2022). For instance, sorbitol content has been mapped to chromosome 4 (11 352 337–13 028 258 bp; Prupe.4G191900) and is located in proximity to loci controlling fructose and glucose contents (Cao et al. 2019). In addition, QTLs related to glucose, fructose, and sorbitol contents have been reported to cluster on linkage groups (LGs) 4, 5, and 6. The co‐localization of sugar‐related loci with fruit colour traits further suggests an indirect genetic linkage between carbohydrate composition and visual characteristics. In particular, the FRU locus controlling fructose content and sweetness has been reported to co‐localize with the Y locus governing yellow/white flesh colour on LG1, providing a genetic basis for coordinated variation in sugar composition and flesh colour. With respect to organic acids, regions associated with acidity‐related traits have been reported on several chromosomes, including a key locus on the top of chromosome 5 (0.47–1.48 Mb) and a malic acid‐associated region on chromosome 2 (5 357 897–5 571 577 bp) (Cao et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2023). Total acidity and pH have also been shown to co‐localize on LG5. Similarly, QTLs associated with total phenolic and flavonoid contents have been identified on chromosome 2 (2.5–5.7 Mb) (Cao et al. 2022). Together, these findings suggest that sugars, organic acids, and phenolic compounds may be genetically interconnected through partially overlapping loci across several linkage groups. This genetic and metabolic interconnection may be associated with coordinated variation in cell wall‐related traits, providing a potential biological basis for differences in processing‐related quality characteristics among peach cultivars.

Within this context, D‐fructose‐6‐phosphate emerges as a central metabolic intermediate linking carbohydrate metabolism with pathways involved in sugar interconversion, organic acid metabolism, and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis. Based on KEGG pathway annotation, key enzymes involved in these pathways were summarized in Tables S7–S9 to outline possible biochemical connections between metabolite variation and pectin biosynthesis. Validation of these links will require future studies incorporating gene expression or enzyme activity analyses. Although metabolite abundance alone cannot prove enzymatic regulation, the proposed framework provides specific metabolic targets for future multi‐omics investigations. Integration of metabolomics with transcriptomic or enzymatic datasets may facilitate the identification of candidate genes and regulatory pathways involved in pectin biosynthesis in peach.

In the present study, eight core metabolites exhibited positive correlations with the yield of CSP (Figure 3B), suggesting that metabolic variation is associated with differences in the abundance of structurally integrated pectin fractions. CSP represents pectin fractions ionically associated with the cell wall matrix and is closely linked to tissue mechanical integrity during fruit softening (Chevalier et al. 2017; Hu et al. 2024). Consistent with observations in carrot and tomato purees (Moelants et al. 2013; Tibäck et al. 2014), peach purees in this study also behaved as weak gel‐like, yield stress systems, in which the material initially undergoes predominantly elastic deformation and subsequently transitions to plastic flow once the applied stress exceeds a yield threshold (Ahmed et al. 2003). Pectin properties are known to influence puree rheology through their effects on particle interactions and structural characteristics (Moelants et al. 2013). In the present study, CSP yield showed a positive association with yield stress (Figure 1A). This behaviour can be attributed to the specific structural role of CSP in the pectin network. CSP is enriched in GalA residues and typically exhibits a lower degree of esterification, which favours the formation of calcium‐mediated cross‐linking through the ‘egg‐box’ model. These calcium bridges generate a continuous ionically cross‐linked network that acts as a structural barrier against deformation. As a result, CSP‐rich systems require higher stress to disrupt this network, thereby enhancing resistance to deformation and delaying the transition from elastic deformation to plastic flow (Santiago et al. 2016; Buergy et al. 2021). Notably, both CSP yield and DE were negatively correlated with apparent viscosity (Figure 1A). Yield stress reflects the stress required to disrupt the pectin network and initiate flow, whereas viscosity primarily characterizes flow behaviour after structural yielding (Tibäck et al. 2014). In CSP‐rich systems, a higher proportion of methyl‐unesterified GalA residues promotes the formation of strong but relatively rigid calcium‐cross‐linked structures. While these structures effectively resist initial deformation, they do not necessarily sustain high viscosity once the network is disrupted, highlighting the distinct roles of CSP in governing pre‐yield versus post‐yield rheological behaviour.

In contrast, rheological behaviour after yielding was more strongly associated with the structural characteristics of WSP. WSP yield was positively associated with the flow behaviour index (n'), suggesting a more fluid‐like response following yielding. WSP constitutes the mobile pectin fraction and dominates intermolecular entanglement and dynamic rearrangement under shear. This interpretation is consistent with previous rheological analyses of fruit and vegetable purees, which emphasize the role of soluble pectins and particle‐matrix interactions in governing post‐yield flow behaviour (Huang et al. 2018; Fei et al. 2021; Audenhove et al. 2023; Sinkora et al. 2024). Higher HG content and GalA levels in WSP indicate longer, more linear chains with greater interaction capacity along their length, as these chains are accessible over a larger surface area. Enhanced chain continuity and intermolecular interactions may therefore contribute to higher apparent viscosity. Similar trends have been reported in tomato purees (Sinkora et al. 2024). In addition, pectins with higher uronic acid content may experience stronger electrostatic repulsion, leading to expanded molecular conformations and increased solution viscosity (Jia et al. 2026). By contrast, RG‐I regions do not support classical egg‐box formation, although arabinose‐rich side chains have been reported to facilitate pectin gelation in the presence of calcium (Jia et al. 2026). In the present study, increased RG‐I branching was negatively correlated with apparent viscosity (Figure 1A). A comparable effect has been observed for Sida cordata polysaccharides, where dense galactan side chains introduce multiple entanglement points that allow easier alignment and disentanglement under shear, resulting in pseudoplastic flow behaviour (Akash et al. 2025). Conversely, Buergy et al. (2021) reported that higher RG‐I branching increased cell adhesion in ‘Granny Smith’ apple, thereby increasing serum viscosity. These contrasting observations highlight that rheological behaviour in multiphase food systems is influenced not only by pectin structure in the serum phase but also by particle properties and characteristics of the dispersed phase. Although Figure 5 primarily provides a visual overview of the peach cultivars and corresponding purees, the observed differences in puree appearance and consistency are qualitatively consistent with the cultivar‐dependent variation in pectin fractions and puree properties discussed above. This phenotypic diversity further illustrates the contribution of pectin structural heterogeneity to macroscopic characteristics of peach purees.

For each cultivar, the fruit (left) and its corresponding puree (right) are shown. 1#–20# denote individual cultivars (see Table S1 for full names). The images illustrate the visual variability among samples. The observed phenotypic diversity among cultivars is consistent with the cultivar‐dependent variation in metabolite profiles, pectin structural characteristics, and puree properties identified in this study.

Collectively, these results support a fraction‐dependent interpretation of peach puree rheology. Metabolite‐associated variation in CSP abundance primarily governs resistance to deformation and the yield point, whereas WSP structural features dominate viscosity, viscoelastic balance, and plastic flow following yielding. This framework provides a physicochemical basis for linking metabolite profiles, pectin architecture, and the transition from elastic deformation to plastic flow in fruit‐based purees, thereby supporting the development of an applied framework for evaluating processing‐related quality traits in peach. Further studies integrating multi‐omics approaches, such as combining metabolomics with transcriptomics, as well as in vitro or in vivo validation of enzyme activities, will be necessary to verify the proposed metabolic‐enzymatic relationships.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Materials

A total of 20 peach cultivars at the commercially mature stage were collected in the same standardized planting base from June to September 2022. Harvested fruits were delivered to the Institute of Food Science and Technology laboratory. The fruits' weight was recorded before and after removal of the pit and stalk. Then, samples were rinsed and cut, cored into pieces, and then frozen with liquid nitrogen for the subsequent process. Detailed information on these cultivars was shown in Data S1, including harvest time, soluble solid content, hardness, distinct puree yield, viscosity, and turbidity. Specific accession names and numbers are also shown in Table S10. Images of peach fruits and corresponding purees from all cultivars are presented in Figure 5 to provide a visual overview of sample variability. All of the reagents were of analytical grade, most were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and some were from Sigma‐Aldrich (Shanghai) Trading Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of Pectin Fractions

AIR were extracted according to Trinette et al. (1997) with some modifications. Briefly, 20 g sample powder was dissolved into 600 mL ethanol (70%, v/v). The suspension was filtered and the residue was washed with 70% ethanol 3 times until the filtrate reacted negatively in the phenol‐sulfuric acid test. Subsequently, the residue was washed with acetone, dried at 40°C overnight, weighed, and stored in a desiccator.

Pectin fractions were obtained from sequential extraction according to Houben et al. (2011) and Broxterman et al. (2017) with some modifications. For WSP, AIR (2 g) was dispersed in 400 mL of boiled water and stirred for 10 min. The supernatant was extensively dialyzed against demineralized water. For CSP, the residue was re‐incubated in 200 mL 0.05 M cyclohexane trans‐1,2‐diamine‐tetraacetic acid (with 0.02 M potassium acetate pH = 6.5) for 6 h at 28°C. The supernatant was dialyzed against 0.1 M NaCl for 36 h followed by demineralized water for another 36 h. For SSP, the residue was suspended in 200 mL 0.05 M Na2CO3 (with 0.02 M NaBH4) and incubated at 4°C for 16 h, then shocked for 6 h at 28°C in a water bath oscillator. The supernatant was treated the same way as WSP. All supernatants were further weighed after lyophilization, namely the yield of WSP (Y w), the yield of CSP (Y c), and the yield of SSP (Y s).

Determination of Molecular Weight (M w) and Neutral Sugar

M w and polydispersity (P) of pectin fractions were determined using high‐performance size exclusion chromatography (HPSEC). Peach pectin fractions (10 mg) were completely dissolved in 0.1 mol/L NaCl aqueous solution, therewith were filtered through a 0.45 μm filter membrane, separately. Then, samples were injected (200 μL) into the TSK gel column (TSK Gel G4000PWxl, 300 × 7.8 mm). Elution was performed with NaCl solution (0.1 mol/L) at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min for 30 min and the column was kept at 35°C (Liu et al. 2023). High‐performance anion exchange chromatography (HPAEC) was achieved using a Dionex system (ICS‐3000, Dionex, California, USA) equipped with a CarboPacTM PA 20 column and a pulsed amperometric detector (Xian et al. 2024). The standards of monosaccharides included arabinose (Ara), galactose (Gal), rhamnose (Rha), xylose (Xyl), glucose (Glc), and GalA. R1 represents the branching degree of RG‐I equations are as follows (Xian et al. 2024): (1) HG=GalA−Rha (2) RG−I=2*Rha+Gal+Ara (3) R1=Gal+Ara/Rha

FTIR and Degree of Esterification (DE)

WSP, CSP, SSP powder (1 mg) was mixed with 50 mg KBr separately and firmly pelleted and then scanned by FTIR spectroscopy (TENSOR27 Germany) at infrared wavenumbers ranging from 4000 cm−1 to 400 cm−1 with the resolution of 4 cm−1. The spectra were converted into absorbance mode before baseline correction through OMNIC 3.2. After spectra preprocessing, the absorption area of the bands was situated around 1745 cm−1 and 1630–1600 cm−1. DE was calculated according to Equations (4) and (5) (Kyomugasho et al. 2015): (4) R=A1745/A1745+1629 (5) DE=124.7R+2013

Purees Preparation

The yield of purees (Y puree) was calculated according to the Equation (6). Frozen samples were thawed by running water at ambient temperature for 1 h. Then, thawing slices were pulped using a blender (SP503A, Supor, Zhejiang, China) with the mode ‘baby fruit puree’ for 2 min. Purees classified by cultivars were collected for physicochemical properties determination. (6) Ypuree=w2–w1/w1×100%

Y puree is the yield of peach puree. w1 is the weight of fresh fruit, g. w2 is the weight of fresh fruit after removal of pit and stalk, g.

Particle Size, Zeta‐Potential, and Turbidity of Peach Purees

The three determination methods were referred to Wellala et al. (2020). Particle size was measured by a laser particle size analyser (S3500, Microtrac, USA). Zeta‐potential was measured by Ζetasizer (Nano ZS, Malvern, UK) at 25°C ± 1°C. Purees were diluted via deionized water to ensure the Attn value ranged from 6 to 9. The turbidity of purees was determined by a turbidimeter (2100 N, HACH, USA). The fluid under the test was diluted properly.

Rheological Properties of Purees

Rheological properties of purees were carried out with a Physica MCR301 rheometer (Anton Paar, Graz, Austria) and parallel plates (50‐mm diameter) with a gap size of 1 mm. The samples were subjected to steady shearing at 25°C with the shear rates ranging from 0.1–300 s−1. The apparent viscosity concerning the shear rate was recorded as the viscosity curve. Yield stress was observed in all the cases and shear stress–shear rate data fitted the Herschel Bulkley model well which is represented as (Popov et al. 2023): (7) τ=τ0+KHBrnHB where τ is the shear stress (Pa), τ 0 is the yield stress, γ is the shear rate (s−1), K HB‐consistency coefficient (Pa s n ), and n HB‐flow behaviour index (dimensionless).

Dynamic frequency sweep was performed to determine the storage modulus (G') curves and loss modulus (G") versus frequency. The loss factor (tanδ) was calculated following the equation. A tanδ value less than 1 indicates a prevalence of elastic properties, while a tanδ > 1 suggests a dominance of viscous properties. (8) tanδ=G″/G'

The degrees of frequency dependence for G' and G" were determined by the power law parameters (Ramkumar et al. 1996) and are expressed as follows: (9) G'=k'×ωn' (10) G"=k"×ωn" where G' and G" are the storage and loss moduli, respectively; ω is the oscillation frequency (Hz); and k′ and k″ are constants, n′ and n″ are frequency dependence of storage modulus and loss modulus, separately.

Sample Extraction for Metabolome Analysis

Peach powder (50 mg) was incorporated into a 1.2 mL solution of 70% methanol to extract 100 mg of lyophilized powder at 4°C over an overnight period. The resulting solution underwent centrifugation at 12 000 rpm for 10 min in preparation for subsequent UPLC‐MS/MS analysis following filtration through a 0.22 μm pore size SCAA‐104 filter.

Metabolite Profiling and Quantification

Metabolite profiling and quantification were performed by Wuhan MetWare Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (www.metware.cn), adhering rigorously to their established standard operating procedures. The analysis utilized an ultra‐performance liquid chromatography‐electrospray ionization‐tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC‐ESI‐MS/MS) system, specifically the SHIMADZU Nexera X2 (www.shimadzu.com.cn/) for UPLC and the Applied Biosystems 4500 Q TRAP (www.appliedbiosystems.com.cn/) for mass spectrometry.

Analysis and Processing of Experimental Data

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments. The relative standard deviation (RSD) was calculated as the ratio of the total mean to the total standard deviation across the 20 peach varieties and expressed as a percentage.

Factor analysis (FA), Pearson correlation analysis, and contingency table analysis (chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, with effect sizes quantified by Cramér's V) were performed using SPSS 20.0 software (IBM Corporation, New York, USA). Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using SIMCA 14.1 software to explore multivariate patterns among pectin structural parameters and to visualize sample distribution in reduced dimensional space.

Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) of samples and metabolites was performed in R (version 1.0.1) using the ComplexHeatmap package. Normalized metabolite signal intensities (unit variance scaling) were visualized as heatmaps. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS‐DA) was performed in R (version 1.0.1) to evaluate metabolic differences among groups and to identify variables contributing to group discrimination. Model robustness was assessed by permutation testing. Differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were identified based on the combined criteria of variable importance in projection (VIP > 1), fold change (FC ≥ 2 or FC ≤ 0.5), and univariate statistical significance (p < 0.05).

Association analyses among core metabolites, as well as between metabolites and pectin structural parameters, were conducted using the online platform Chiplot (https://www.chiplot.online/). Identified metabolites were further mapped to KEGG compound and pathway databases to construct a putative framework linking core metabolites to pectin biosynthesis.
