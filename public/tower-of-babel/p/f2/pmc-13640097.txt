Stability of fungicide-variety management strategies for Septoria tritici blotch in wheat: insights from AMMI and GGE biplot analyses across multiple environments
Authors: Getachew G Mengesha, Habtamu Terefe, Tariku Simion, Dizgo Chencha, Meseret T Suure, Abu Jambo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWheat is a vital cereal crop globally, and over one-third of the world’s population consumes it as a staple food. However, Septoria tritici blotch (STB) (Zymoseptoria tritici), which is among the top ten ranked diseases in wheat, poses a significant threat to global wheat production, particularly in southern Ethiopia, where effective management strategies are scarce. The disease limits wheat productivity, necessitating exploration of alternatives due to limited fungicides and resistant varieties. The interaction between management strategies and environmental factors during the host’s growing period is crucial for judicious decision-making in STB management. Normal ANOVA assesses main effects but does not capture management by environment interactions (MEI), necessitating the use of AMMI and GGE models to identify reliable strategies. The study areas, despite variable conditions, remain hotspots for STB epidemics, underscoring urgent intervention needs. The objectives of this study were to evaluate the combined effects of wheat varieties and fungicide application on STB development, agronomic traits, and yield loss while examining MEI across multiple environments. Field experiments were conducted in southern Ethiopia (Burji, Bonke, and Chencha areas) across cropping years (2018–2019). The study employed a factorial arrangement with a randomized block design that included four wheat varieties (Shorima, Hidase, Ogolcho, and Kubsa), three fungicides (epoxiconazole + thiophanate-methyl (EPX + TPM), tebuconazole, and propiconazole) and unsprayed controls, and it was replicated three times. Disease severity, relative area under disease progress curve (rAUDPC), and agronomic traits were analyzed using normal ANOVA and biplot models. Key findings indicated that Shorima + EPX+TPM consistently showed the lowest STB severity (9.41%), rAUDPC (1.58%-days), and highest yield (6.45 t ha⁻¹). AMMI analyses for disease and yield responses across environments exhibited 62.05% and 76.83% of the total variation attributed to management, 27.97% and 2.79% to environmental effects, and 4.51% and 8.33% to MEI effects, highlighting the dominant role of management strategies. GGE biplots identified Shorima + EPX+TPM as the most consistent and responsive tactics across environments. EPX + TPM fungicide spray provided yield advantages of Shorima, Hidase, Ogolcho and Kubsa varieties by 149.76%, 175.65%, 255.74% and 263.28% over unsprayed plots of each variety, respectively. Economic analysis confirmed Shorima + EPX+TPM as the most profitable, with a net benefit of $2678.20 ha⁻¹ and a marginal rate of return of 27.53. In conclusion, integrating Shorima (or Hidase/Ogolcho) with EPX + TPM or Tebuconazole is optimal for STB management, yield enhancement, reducing yield losses, and achieving the highest economic returns in southern Ethiopia and elsewhere with similar agro-ecological conditions. However, the study’s limitations include geographic restrictions, a narrow focus on specific wheat varieties and fungicides, and reliance on uncharacterized natural infections, necessitating further extensive research across diverse conditions to provide concrete recommendations for the wider farming community. This work provides a template for sustainable STB management, emphasizing the need for context-specific, integrated approaches in wheat production systems.Keywords: AMMI biplot, Disease metrics, Economic returns, GGE biplot, Fungicides, Integrated approaches, Management by environment interactions, Wheat varieties, Yield advantages, Zymoseptoria tritici

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum spp.) is a major staple food among cereal crops grown all over the world. It covers well over 219 million hectares of land and yielding 808.44 million tons of grain yield [1]. In Ethiopia, it is produced under both irrigation and rain-fed conditions, covering around 1.86 million hectares of the cultivated area, and 5.81 million tons of grains were produced during 2022. The crop is also cultivated on > 0.14 million hectares of land and contributed around 0.41 million tons of grains in southern Ethiopia in 2022 [2]. The crop provides essential nutrients, mainly carbohydrates, followed by proteins, vitamins, and minerals [3], and plays a significant role in traditional house construction and as a commercial and animal feed source in the country [4]. Despite its remarkable significance, wheat productivity is highly constrained by several factors, such as biotic and abiotic stresses, climate change, a shortage of improved agricultural technologies, and ineffective mitigation practices against stresses around the world [5, 6]. Hence, the average national wheat productivity (3.11 t ha–1) and that of southern Ethiopia (2.87 t ha–1) [2], under farmers’ conditions, are considerably lower than the world average productivity of 3.69 t ha–1 [1].

Of the biotic stresses, diseases caused by various fungal pathogens significantly limit wheat production and productivity globally. Septoria tritici blotch (STB), caused by Zymoseptoria tritici (Teleomorph: Mycosphaerella graminicola), is ranked as one of the top ten significant and devastating diseases affecting wheat production worldwide [7], including in Ethiopia [8–10]. As noted by Mekonnen et al. [11], evidence suggests that there are differential interactions between various wheat genotypes and different isolates of Z. tritici, which confirm the existence of physiological specialization for the pathogen that is responsible for breaking resistance genes in released wheat varieties and fungicide resistance developments. It is more frequent and severe when wheat is cultivated under cool and humid environmental conditions [12]. The pathogen has wind-borne spores (like ascospores), splash-dispersed spores (like pycnidiospores), a polycyclic nature, and a few alternative hosts [13]. It survives in crop residues, seeds, volunteers, and other grass weeds as asexual (mycelium and pycnidiospores) or sexual (perithecia or ascospores) stages [14, 15]. The infection drives notable yield losses by influencing the photosynthetic green leaf area [16], although the magnitude of yield losses varies among varieties grown, locations, and the status of its infection [17]. The disease is reported to cause up to 54% yield loss in Ethiopia [18]. However, a yield loss of up to 82% has been reported in the susceptible wheat variety in the country [19]. Such circumstances call for efficient management strategies focusing on the pathosystems, where host-pathogen-environment interactions occur.

The following management options have been reported to manage STB: using cultivar mixtures [20, 21]; destroying residues and alternative hosts, maintaining population density and deep plowing or following crop rotation [22]; proper fertilization and use of disease-free seeds [23]; using resistant and taller varieties than dwarf susceptible ones [24]; and applying fungicides [18]. However, the penalties for plant diseases may not be significantly lessened by the sole application of management options. For instance, cultural measures for disease management may have limitations in their effectiveness in reducing disease, especially due to environmental changes and modifications in agronomic practices. Similarly, the use of resistant varieties is generally considered a reliable, effective and economical strategy for disease management. However, repeated reliance on this approach can lead to the breakdown of resistance genes. This breakdown may occur due to several factors, including the introduction of new exotic races, the evolution of new local races, environmental changes, or a combination of these elements [25]. In this regard, certain wheat varieties, including Kubsa, Galema, Hidase, Ogolcho, Danda’a, and other old varieties that are still widely cultivated in the country, have lost their resistance levels and have become susceptible to foliar diseases, such as rusts and STB, affecting wheat in Ethiopia [26]. Fungicidal action is a widely utilized tactic, depending on region, economics, and resistance status of wheat to STB, in the majority of wheat-producing countries globally due to its effectiveness in managing STB. Yet, long-term use may lead to resistance in the Z. tritici population [27] and contribute to the accumulation of toxic residues in goods and the environment, which can harm humans, animals, and non-target organisms [28].

Effects of fungicides on STB in wheat have been acknowledged by previous researchers worldwide [7, 18, 28]. However, there are only a limited number of registered fungicides for STB, and no completely resistant wheat variety to STB has been developed in Ethiopia. Experts at agricultural offices in the study areas informed us that the dominant fungicide available for many years for wheat diseases, like STB, management was Tilt 250 EC (Propiconazole 250 g/L), which is incorporated in this study. The experts and producers indicated that STB disease does not respond to this fungicide and that it failed to protect their fields from the disease's effects (personal communication). This suggests the need to conduct evidence-based empirical research on fungicide resistance to Z. tritici since in the country, as there is reported evidence of the existence of virulence variability in Ethiopian Z. tritici [29, 30]. The reasons mentioned, along with the need for improved alternative management options by growers in the study areas, highlight the necessity of conducting research using registered fungicides that are effective against other wheat diseases. This research focused on the active ingredients formulated in these products, which have proven successful in other countries.

Torriani et al. [27] and Kildea et al. [24] strongly suggested the operation of integrated management tactics for reducing STB pressure and combating fungicide resistance in Z. tritici populations. Thus, an integrated approach that combines multiple tactics, such as utilizing host resistance along with methods like cultural practices and fungicidal treatments, represents the most cost-effective strategy for managing STB [27, 31]. Earlier reports also indicated that a combination of host resistance, cultural practices, and fungicide application, as well as host resistance and fungicide application, are among the widely used and efficient approaches for disease management tactics in various pathosystems [25, 32]. Tadesse and Kesho [31] and Kristoffersen et al. [33] also demonstrated that combinations of cultural practices, variety, and fungicide spray resulted in a substantial reduction in STB intensity and a boost in wheat yield performance. To this end, the use of integrated measures is recommended as an eco-friendly and cost-effective alternative to lower disease intensity, reduce resistance development, and be less hazardous to health-related issues [34]. While there is no specific duration for reports on the durability of fungicide resistance when using integrated approaches for disease management, existing studies suggest that this method enhances the longevity of resistant varieties and decreases the chance of the pathogen developing fungicide resistance across various pathosystems [25, 32, 34].

Although wheat production in the study areas faces significant threats from STB, studies that integrate multiple management strategies remain uncommon in southern Ethiopia. Efforts by Tadesse et al. [18] and Tadesse and Kesho [31] primarily focused on fungicide frequencies and varietal resistance in central Ethiopia, highlighting a knowledge gap regarding the effectiveness of various registered fungicides in conjunction with new, widely grown wheat varieties for STB control. Evaluating the performance of existing wheat varieties alongside new fungicides could lead to more sustainable, cost-effective solutions for farmers while also enhancing safety for people and the environment. On the other hand, weather, soil qualities, tillage practices, and farmers’ crop choices all have a substantial impact on the effectiveness of crop management strategies [35, 36]. Moreover, evidence shows that climate change scenarios significantly impact wheat production and STB development and severities [37, 38]. These elements lead to variations in disease outbreaks and wheat yield performance, which are referred to as management strategy × environment interactions (MEI). MEI is a recently utilized approach by agronomists and protectionists beyond breeders for integrated field management practices and is of significant use in accurately identifying effective strategies, selecting superior strategies across environments and providing recommendations. Complexity of host-pathogen-environment interactions, combined with a wide range of hosts, infections, and settings, makes outcome predictions difficult [39]. A detailed understanding of these relationships is essential for choosing effective disease control techniques and improving crop productivity. It also helps to identify management practices that are consistent across different conditions or respond especially to distinct contexts. Strategies with limited environmental interaction are considered broadly applicable, whereas those with high interactions are designed for particular or mega-environment applications [40, 41]. In this context, current investigations on management strategy by environmental interactions impact farmers’ decision-making by forcing them to choose a disease management strategy that is consistent and stable with the current challenging environmental factors and influence them to change the cropping system to adapt to the existing environmental conditions. Further, it encourages farmers to upgrade existing disease management strategies to be environmentally safe, economically feasible, and socially acceptable while maintaining wheat crop production and productivity. Apart from this, combined analyses of variance (ANOVA) can quantify the MEI and describe main effects. However, it does not adequately clarify the interaction between management tactics and environmental factors in multi-location trials. This limitation hinders the ability to distinguish between specific and broad operational tactics. To address this, the current study employed additive main effects and multiplicative interaction (AMMI) and genotype plus genotype-by-environment interaction (GGE), set for management plus MEI in this study, in biplot models to identify consistent and effective management strategies for specific environments while acknowledging limitations such as the randomness of management strategies and environments, the significance of understanding interaction causes, the realism of “which-won-where” patterns, and the potential for the analysis to detect crossover interactions. AMMI biplots facilitate a visual comparison of management, environmental effects, and MEI, while GGE biplots utilize polygon views to highlight effective strategies across both specific and mega environments in multi-location trials [42]. Additionally, the AMMI model incorporates variance analyses and Interaction Principal Components Axis (IPCA) to account for both additive main effects and multiplicative interactions [43]. These models offer complementary insights from other stability models: GGE provides a more understandable graphical representation for choosing stable and effective management techniques, whereas AMMI excels at isolating interaction effects.

In Ethiopia, the application of MEI and statistical tools such as AMMI and GGE biplots has not been explored concerning integrated disease management for STB in wheat production, as there is no literature available online. While previous trials have utilized these tools for other crops and diseases to identify resistant genotypes [17, 43, 44], research on STB management in wheat remains undeveloped. No thorough studies have assessed combined management approaches against STB across various locations, seasons and years in the country. Consequently, testing wheat varieties under diverse environmental conditions with effective management strategies for STB is crucial for generating relevant data. Such research would help identify stable and effective tactics for STB management that can be applied consistently across southern Ethiopia as well as elsewhere in similar environments within the country and beyond. Additionally, the outcomes could offer significant insights for researchers focusing on integrated disease management strategies and agronomic performance across different locations and cropping seasons. Therefore, the objectives of this study were to evaluate the combined effects of variety and fungicide application on STB development, key agronomic traits, and yield loss, and to investigate the presence and influence of MEI on disease progression and yield performance in wheat.

Materials and methods

Experimental sites

Field experiments were conducted in Burji, Bonke, and Chencha, southern Ethiopia, in 2018 and 2019. The three test locations and cropping years were treated as separate environments, constituting a total of six distinct environments for the MEI analyses. Geographically, experimental sites are positioned at 05°30’30.9” N latitude and 037°54’01.7” E longitude (Burji), 06°06’26.6” N latitude and 037°19’17.2” E longitude (Bonke), and 06°16’10.5” N latitude and 037°35’28.7” E longitude (Chencha). Similarly, experimental sites in Burji, Bonke, and Chencha are located at altitudes of 2302, 2527, and 2543 m above sea level, respectively. The experiment was conducted from March to August in Burji and from July to December in the Bonke and Chencha districts during the two cropping seasons. The areas are characterized by a bimodal rainfall pattern where the short rainy season occurs from March to June and the main rainy season extends from July up to November. The mean values of weather variables, which were obtained from the Ethiopian Meteorological Agency, for the areas over the study periods are presented in Fig. 1. Study locations are characterized by diverse agroecological factors, topographical features, cultivated crops, cropping systems, land use, water shade management, and vegetation cover. The previous cropping history of the experimental fields included faba bean, field pea (at Burji), Irish potato, faba bean (at Bonke), field pea and faba bean (at Chencha) during the 2018 and 2019 cropping years, respectively. The soil at the study site in Burji is neutral with high organic matter levels of 6.82%, whereas the soil in Bonke and Chencha is characterized by acidic nature with low organic content (0.25‒1.05%) and sandy-loam texture. The soil at the study sites generally exhibits low levels of organic carbon, available sulfur, and carbon-to-nitrogen ratios. However, the total nitrogen, available phosphorus, and exchangeable levels of sodium, potassium, magnesium and calcium in the soil are moderate [45].

Experimental materials

Four wheat varieties (Shorima, Hidase, Ogolcho, and Kubsa), all of which are Triticum aestivum varieties, and three fungicides (Natura 250 EW, Rex® Duo and Tilt 250 EC) were used. These varieties are currently under production in mid to highland areas of Ethiopia. These varieties were chosen for a number of reasons, including their production potentials, exhibiting different levels of reaction to STB, widespread use by the growers year after year and common occurrence across the region compared to other varieties. Varieties Shorima, Hidase, Ogolcho, and Kubsa are reported as resistant, moderately resistant, susceptible, and highly susceptible to STB, respectively [46]. Seeds were obtained from Kulumsa Agricultural Research Center, Ethiopia. The fungicides were chosen from the market based on their active substance efficiency against STB in other parts of the world, systemic nature absorbed by the leaves and roots, and their ability to inhibit fungal cell division, which is required for polycyclic diseases, cost, widespread availability in local markets and long-term use without resistance threats. The fungicides were obtained from a licensed agrochemical dealer in Ethiopia. Tilt 250 EW (Propiconazole 250 g/L), Rex® Duo (Epoxiconazole 187 g/L + Thiophanate-methyl 310 g/L, EPX + TPM) and Natura 250 EW (Tebuconazole 250 g/L) were registered in 1995, 2010, and 2013, respectively, against fungal foliar diseases in cereals. Propiconazole and Tebuconazole are members of the triazole class, whereas EPX + TPM is a combination of the azole (epoxiconazole) and benzimidazole (thiophanate-methyl) classes [47]. All fungicides were applied at a rate of 500 mL product ha–1. The corresponding spray volume ranged from 100 to 400 L ha–1 for Tilt 250 EW, 200 to 400 L ha–1 for Rex® Duo, and 200 to 300 L ha–1 for Natura 250 EW. All products are recommended for 2–3 applications at an interval of 10–15 days, depending on disease incidence, severity, and weather conditions.

Experimental design and field management

There were 16 treatment combinations, including the unsprayed control. The experiment was a factorial arrangement in a randomized complete block design with three replications. The treatments were randomly assigned to experimental plots within a block, and depending on the nature of the soil (light or heavy), a well-prepared seedbed that had been plowed three or four times was used across the three locations over the two seasons. The study was conducted on farmer’s fields. Due to the shortage of broader fields for the study under farmer conditions, the investigators were forced to minimize unit plot size to fit the available field sizes. Accordingly, the unit plot was 1.6 m × 2 m (3.20 m2) in size and had eight rows, six of which were harvestable. Plots were separated from one another by 0.20 m. The aforementioned plot size was highly susceptible to “edge effects” and may not accurately reflect epidemic dynamics, significantly influencing the parameters determined and the broader explanation of the results obtained as a result of edge effects and the minimum number of harvestable rows. Each plot and replication were separated by 1.5 and 2.0 m, respectively. Sowing was performed with a recommended seed rate of 100 kg ha–1. In Burji, plots were manually sown in rows on April 06 and 13 of the short rainy seasons of 2018 and 2019, respectively. During the major rainy seasons of 2018 and 2019, plots were sown in rows on 12 and 10 August at Bonke and on 15 and 13 August at Chencha, respectively.

Soil nutrient management was maintained with P2O5 and nitrogen (N) fertilizers at rates of 46 and 41 kg ha–1, in that order, in all plots uniformly across locations over cropping seasons. The full rate of P2O5 and one-third of the N-fertilizer were applied at the time of sowing. Two-thirds of the remaining N-fertilizer was used 30–35 days after sowing (DAS), which corresponded to the Feeks’ growth stages (FGS) of 5 to 6, where the leaf sheath stood up straight and the first part of the stem was visible, depending on the crop’s growth stage in the three locations. Fungicide spray began at 45 DAS (FGS of 8, last leaf visible, but still rolled up, near the beginning to swell) and 49 DAS (FGS of 9, ligule of last leaf visible) (at Burji), 46 and 47 DAS (FGS of 8, last leaf visible, but still rolled up, near the beginning to swell) (at Bonke) and 49 DAS (FGS of 9, ligule of last leaf visible) and 46 (FGS of 8, last leaf visible, but still rolled up, near the beginning to swell) (at Chencha) DAS in 2018 and 2019, respectively, when the first symptoms of STB appeared on Hidase, Kubsa, and Ogolcho varieties in all locations during both cropping seasons. All associated growth stages were sourced from Large [48] FGS descriptions. A manual backpack sprayer, which was calibrated to provide 500–700 L of water ha–1, was used to carry out the spraying. Due to the rapid rates of disease progression and the early occurrence of the disease, three successive sprays were carried out at 10-day intervals at each location across seasons. The gap in optimizing fungicide spray scheduling beyond a 10-day interval arises from the polycyclic nature of the pathogen and its effectiveness under varying environmental conditions. For each variety, unsprayed plots were left as controls to enable the maximum spread and development of the STB epidemics. Regular supervision, earthing up, and weeding of the fields were carried out properly and uniformly according to the recommendations to ensure a successful crop. Fungicide spraying was stopped when the crop reached the FGS of 10.5.4 (flowering over, kernel watery ripe) at Bonke and Chencha and 11.1 (milky ripe) at Burji during the study periods.

Monitoring of Septoria tritici blotch

Monitoring of STB was started at 44 and 48 (FGS of 8 and 9, Burji), 45 and 46 (FGS of 8, Bonke) and 48 and 45 (FGS of 9 and 8) (Chencha) DAS in 2018 and 2019, in that order. Each assessment was carried out before each fungicide spray in all locations over the two seasons. A total of six (Burji) and seven (Bonke and Chencha) consecutive assessments per season were made for the disease. Disease evaluation was carried out on 15 selected and pre-tagged plants from six central rows at intervals of 10 days, starting from disease onset to physiological maturity of the crop. Septoria tritici blotch severity was rated using a scale of 0, 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 relative lesion size from the base to the top of the spike [49], where 0 = no disease symptom appeared, 5 = the midpoint of the lesion, which is the distance from the base to the top of the spike, and 9 = it is reserved for infection up to the spike. The severity scores were converted into a percentage severity index (PSI, Eq. 1) for data analysis [50].

The area under disease progress curve (AUDPC), which represents the progression and total amount of disease on a whole plant or a specific portion of a plant during epidemic periods, was assessed for each plot from the disease severity data following Campbell and Madden [32]:

The epidemic duration varied across locations in the two seasons. The epidemic durations lasted 50 days in Burji and 60 days in Bonke and Chencha. As a result, the AUDPC values were standardized into relative AUDPC (rAUDPC) by dividing their values by the total epidemic duration (tn‒t1) for the respective locations [32, 51].

Assessment of agronomic traits and relative yield loss

In this study, plant height (PH), ear length (EL), number of effective tillers (NET), number of kernels per ear (NKE), thousand kernel weight (TKW), grain yield (GY), and biomass yield (BMY) were considered for each evaluated treatment. Plant height (cm) and EL (cm) were assessed at 90% of the physiological maturity of wheat plants from the central rows of randomly chosen ten plants and ears, respectively. Plant height was measured from the base of the stem at the ground to the tip of the plant, while EL was measured from the base of the ear near the stalk end at the top to the last spikelet found. The number of effective tillers was recorded as the total number of effective or productive tillers present in each plot, and it was assessed from three places within the plot for each treatment using 0.5 m × 0.5 m = 0.25 m² quadrats. The NKE was recorded by counting the total number of kernels per ear from ten randomly selected ears of sample plants per plot. Average values for PH, EL, NET, and NKE were used for data analysis.

Harvesting for GY and BMS was carried out on 134, 160, and 170 DAS in 2018 and 140, 150, and 164 DAS in 2019 at Burji, Chencha, and Bonke in that order of appearance. The six middle rows were examined to assess GY and BMS, intentionally excluding the border rows to eliminate their potential influence among treatments. Grain yield was recorded on a plot basis for each treatment and then transformed to yield in terms of t ha–1. The harvested grain yield was adjusted to 12% based on the storable seed moisture content of wheat [52]; accordingly, BMS was adjusted based on GY obtained since it is the summation of all aboveground parts of the plant. Moreover, TKW was assessed from sampled grains, and its moisture content was adjusted to 12% for data analysis. On the other hand, the percentage yield reduction of the less protected plots relative to the highly protected plots was used to determine the relative yield loss of wheat due to STB disease.

Data analyses

Bartlett’s Chi-square test [55] was performed to assess the homogeneity of error variations across locations, cropping years, and weather conditions. Due to the variation in error variances, separate analyses were conducted for each location, whereas combined analyses were performed for cropping years with homogeneous variances [56]. Accordingly, individual and combined ANOVA for epidemiological (severity and rAUDPC) and yield parameters across locations over the cropping seasons were performed following PROC GLM with the MIXED model of SAS version 9.3 [57], where management and locations were fixed, while seasons and all interactions, such as replications, blocks, and errors, were random. The terms are i = 1, 2, 3 … 16; j = 1, 2, 3; k = 1, 2; l = 1, 2, 3; and m = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.

Fisher’s protected least significant difference (LSD) test was used to determine mean separation at a 5% level of significance if there were significant differences between the treatments [56]. Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to explore associations between epidemiological and agronomic parameters. A linear regression analysis of yield data versus rAUDPC was done to predict yield loss in wheat production due to STB pressure in the cropping seasons.

Combined ANOVA for the study parameters unveiled that there were significant interaction effects of location, cropping year, variety, and fungicide. Moreover, Bartlett’s test [55] was used for the heterogeneity of variance assumption to detect the significance of MEI, as it helps to determine whether the treatment effects on the study parameters differ across diverse conditions. Note that management strategy used as a factor in MEI referred to the combined effects of variety and fungicide that influence the STB development and yield traits across test environments. The MEI effect was observed in Bartlett’s test, which led to analyses of the MEI using AMMI stability and biplot models of AMMI and GGE.

The AMMI stability value (ASV) was calculated using the method proposed by Zobel et al. [59]. These scholars suggested that ASV analysis incorporating two IPCAs could represent the most effective prognostic model. Thus, in this study, we utilized the first two IPCAs for the ASV analysis.

Where, SSIPCA1/SSIPCA2 denotes the weighted value allotted to the IPCA1 score due to its high contributions in the MEI; SSIPCA1 and SSIPCA2 are the sums of squares for IPCA1 and IPCA2, respectively; and IPCA1 and IPCA2 are the first and second IPCA scores for each management strategy.

Following Smith et al. [60] procedure, AMMI and GGE biplot models were used to examine the interplay effects of disease and yield data. The two analytical tools were employed to describe the main effects (management tactics and environments) and their interaction effects for rAUDPC and yield traits across environments. The AMMI and GGE biplot analyses were constructed with GenStat statistical software version 18 [61].

The AMMI model was utilized to investigate the components of variance that influence interactions between management tactics and the environment, as well as the consistency of management tactics across experiments [61–63]. It is a hybrid model that encompasses both ANOVA (for the additive main component) and IPCA (for the multiplicative interactive component) to examine two-way (management × environment) data structures. To provide a clearer insight into particular MEI combinations and the general pattern of efficiency, a biplot of management strategies and environments was created. In the model, the influence of each management tactic and environment on the MEI is valued by using the biplot graphic demonstration [59]. The model formula for the AMMI model is denoted as:

Where, Yijr is the parameter (disease score or yield) of management tactic i in environment j for replication r; µ is the grand mean; gi is the deviation of tactic i from the grand mean; ej is the environment main effect as a deviation from µ; λk is the specific value for the IPCA k; αik and γjk are the management and environment IPCA scores for axis k; br(ej) is the effect of the block r within the environment j; r is the number of blocks; ρij is the residual containing all multiplicative terms not included in the model; n is the number of IPCAs that were retained in the model; and εij is the error under independent and identically distributed assumptions.

The GGE biplot analyses allow for a visual exploration of relationships between test management tactics, environments, and MEIs for the study parameters. In the model, the management levels and a number of environments were outlined into mega-environments, and superior management tactics against study variables for specific as well as mega-environments were identified. For the representation of the management and environment coordinates, a symmetrical scaling model was utilized to generate the “which-won-where” biplot for rAUDPC and yield performance for the MEI. The lines that link the test environment to the GGE biplot origin are termed environment vectors and the relationship between two environments is represented by the cosine of the angle between their vectors [40, 41]. In the model, IPCA 1 represented the variation because of the main effects, while IPCA 2 represented the variation in the study parameters due to the MEI [64]. The GGE biplot determines the consistency of management tactics based on the closeness of the test materials to the biplot origin, which is thought to be broadly consistent, while management strategies far from the biplot origin are regarded as specifically well for a particular environment [65].

Where, Ῡij is the efficiency of management tactic i in environment j; µ is the grand mean of all MEI; βj is the main effect of environment j; λ1 and λ2 are singular values of the first and second IPCA associated with the matrix of the effects of management tactics added to effects of MEI; γ1i and γ2i are eigenvectors of the first and second IPCA associated with the effect of the management tactic i; δ1j and δ2j are eigenvectors of the first and second IPCA associated with the effect of the environment j; and εij is the residual of the model associated with the management tactic i in the environment j.

Economic feasibility of fungicide application

Economic analysis was determined for the costs and returns of the fungicidal treatments. It was done to estimate the monetary advantage and attributes such as gross revenue, net benefit (NB), and marginal rate of return (MRR). Based on the combined data from the two cropping seasons for each location, an economic feasibility analysis of integrated STB management was performed using the procedures of CIMMYT [66]. Current costs of labor, market price of wheat, and fungicides were considered for partial budget analysis. The difference between gross revenue and total variable input costs, which were included as variable costs, was used to calculate NB. The ratio of the change between NB and total input variable costs was used to determine MRR. The unit price of grain yield per kilogram of wheat was $0.46, $0.48 and $0.50 in 2018 and $0.48, $0.51 and $0.54 in 2019 at Burji (mean = $0.47), Bonke (mean = $0.50), and Chencha (mean = $0.52), respectively. In 2018 and 2019, the exchange rate was $1 = ETB 27.73 and 31.45, in that order of presentation. To account for any variation between the trial and expected yields from the same treatment, a 10% downward adjustment was made to the actual yield, as suggested by CIMMYT [66].

Results

Analysis of variance

The combined ANOVA indicated varying levels of significance (P < 0.0001 to < 0.05) differences in mean squares for disease severity, rAUDPC, and agronomic traits among varieties, fungicides, and their interactions across locations and cropping seasons. However, there was no significant (P < 0.05) variation in the mean square for the grain yield trait regarding the interaction of varieties and fungicides across locations and cropping seasons. Moreover, combined ANOVA revealed significant differences in mean square results for both rAUDPC and yield due to the interaction effects of location × year × variety × fungicide (Table 1). The presence of such interactions invites further MEI, thereby enabling stability analyses to recognize the nature of MEI and the stability of the performance management strategies in relation to STB suppression and yield potential across environments.

AMMI based ANOVA for study parameters

The AMMI analyses of variance regarding rAUDPC and yield performance of the tested varieties are presented in Table 6. The results showed that management strategy, environment, and MEI significantly (P < 0.001) affected rAUDPC and yield performance. Regarding rAUDPC, the main effects of management strategy and environment accounted for 62.05% and 27.97%, in that order, while MEI accounted for only 4.51% of the total variation in management strategy × environment data (Table 6). The AMMI model also revealed that IPCA 1 and IPCA 2 were significant for the MEI sum of squares. The AMMI-based stability parameter (ASV, IPCA 1, and IPCA 2) was computed based on the first two IPCAs to generate a well-balanced measure between them for STB severity and yield (Table 7). The lowest IPCA 1 and IPCA 2 values that are near zero elucidate the high performance of the management strategies for STB at best in the biplot. Generally, a low ASV of these parameters indicates desirable management strategies with low disease pressure and high mean yield and stability across test environments. Accordingly, management strategies that have low ASV, such as any varieties combined with either EPX + TPM or tebuconazole for rAUDPC and yield potential, were considered stable (Table 7). The remaining management strategies were identified as unstable with relatively lower to higher STB severity and yield potential even if they had lower ASV values, implying the inconsistency of some of the management strategies for suppressing as well as allowing for high STB development and potential yields across environments. For disease and yields, unsprayed control plots of the varieties Hidase, Ogolcho, and Kubsa, along with the Hidase variety combined with propiconazole fungicide, exhibited higher ASV (Table 7), suggesting these varieties have unstable and inconsistent responses to STB development and yield potential across environments.

Regarding rAUDPC, AMMI stability analysis revealed negative values for both IPCA 1 (environmental influence) and IPCA 2 (management strategies response for STB), indicating that the strategies are not efficient across environments with lower or higher STB development. However, AMMI stability analysis cannot be the sole method for identifying effective and appropriate strategies in wheat production; hence, it is crucial to identify strategies coupled with high yield using AMMI and GGE biplot analyses and to observe consistent results across environments.

The lowest NB of $‒188.63 ha–1 at Burji and 83.94 ha–1 at Bonke was computed from unsprayed plots of Kubsa and Ogolcho, respectively. Likewise, integrating each variety with EPX + TPM fungicide, which exhibited the highest NB on most of the test varieties, also had the highest MRR, except for the variety Hidase at Burji and Chencha and Ogolcho at Burji. The lowest MRR of ‒0.38 was computed from the plots treated with Propiconazole on the varieties Ogolcho (Burji) and Shorima (Chencha). When an average over locations and cropping years was considered, cultivation of the variety Shorima with STB fungicidal management displayed the highest NB ($1557.62 ha⁻¹) and MRR (12.61) compared to the other test varieties.

Discussion

Globally, over one-third of the world’s population consumes wheat as a staple food [1]. However, its productivity is constrained by many factors, among which STB is recognized as a devastating disease among biotic factors that entails serious concern, as it is reported to induce high yield loss in wheat and may lead to an epidemic under favorable conditions [7, 13]. It is one of the economically significant and prevalent fungal diseases affecting wheat in Ethiopia [9] and the study areas as well [67]. Earlier studies have indicated that integrated management is a top priority for the disease because it is the most sustainable, feasible, and eco-friendly [28], boosts agronomic performance [31] and reduces fungicide resistance [27, 68]. However, management measures as well as plant disease development are significantly influenced by the pathosystem components across locations and growing seasons [25, 32]. Hence, realizing MEI is critical for generating relevant information and suggesting consistent and better tactics in particular or across locations and subsequent judicious operations in STB management. Such information regarding the evaluation of integrated measures against STB effects and the agronomic performance of wheat under the MEI notion was lacking in the study areas and elsewhere in Ethiopia.

In this study, a combined ANOVA revealed significant variations in disease metrics and agronomic characters among varieties, fungicides, and their interactions across locations and cropping seasons, suggesting that the effects of disease and agronomic performance within the evaluated treatments varied across locations and cropping seasons. But yield trait mean squares showed no significant variation for the interaction of varieties and fungicides, indicating consistent treatment effects on yield across locations and cropping seasons. ANOVA results for the treatments, locations, and cropping years demonstrated varied mean square values or effect sizes. Treatments regarding disease metrics and agronomic traits had high effect sizes, indicating consistency, while lower values revealed variable treatment effects on STB development, influenced by environmental conditions. Large effect sizes signify practical relevance, whereas small effect sizes suggest limited applicability of findings, comparable analogies have been stated by Gomez and Gomez [56].

Likewise, combined ANOVA demonstrated significant differences in mean square results for both rAUDPC and yield, attributed to the interaction effects of location, year, variety, and fungicide. These suggest that there was substantial variation among treatments across environments, and the comparison of treatments for selecting efficient tactics for STB and enhancing yield performance across locations was significantly influenced by the interaction of variety, location, cropping years, and management tactics. The results also indicated that the environment had various impacts on disease progress and yield performance of the varieties under fungicidal management despite the fact that the varieties had variable responses in the study locations over the years. The phenomenon put forward a call for employing additional variety, fungicide and environment interactions across locations and cropping years, thereby using AMMI and GGE biplot analyses to discern the nature of management tactics and environmental interaction and the consistency of the management for STB and yield performance across environments. Therefore, analyzing the diverse interactions among management strategies, environments and different facets of STB development, agronomic traits and yield is essential for designing efficient management strategies to manage STB progression and its effects on wheat productivity.

Findings indicated that all test environments experienced high STB pressure during both cropping seasons, irrespective of treatment effects. Such could be due to year-round production of wheat, favorable weather conditions, availability of volunteers or alternate hosts around the fields, and/or combinations of such elements of epidemics. Continuous wheat cultivation, along with factors such as the presence of alternate hosts, volunteer plants from prior crops, or infected crop debris in the soil, can significantly contribute to the primary sources of inoculum and the potential for epidemic development in the subsequent cropping season [14, 15]. Results of temporal analysis showed that a relatively higher STB pressure was observed in 2018 than in 2019 (at Burji and Chencha) and in 2019 than in 2018 (at Bonke), regardless of treatments imposed. Disease pressure variability across experimental areas over the years may be attributed to humid temperatures, frequent rainfall, and high relative humidity (Fig. 1). Early planting is believed to extend the time window for host-pathogen interaction and the initiation and development of STB during the growing periods. Moreover, early sowing might align the susceptible growth stages of wheat with the peak periods of the disease. For example, this includes the phase when the first node of the stem is visible and 90% of heading is complete, which corresponds to a growth stage score (FGS) of 5 to 10. Suffert et al. [14] reported that the highest STB infection rate and disease epidemics are associated with the growth stages of the crop and humid temperatures with frequent rainy weather conditions. Ideal situations for spore germination, hyphal penetration, mesophyll colonization, and pycnidia formation in neighboring stomatal cavities during infection include leaf moisture > 10 mm for 6–48 h and high relative humidity [69], as well as a temperature of 15–21 °C for infection, but successful infection can occur at 4.44 °C [14]. A range of these weather-related features was also recorded across the study locations during the growing periods of 2018 and 2019 (Fig. 1).

In the infection process, splash-dispersed pycnidiospores are the key source of secondary inoculum and STB dissemination from lower to upper leaves [14]. Zymoseptoria tritici is a hemibiotrophic pathogen exhibiting a symptomless biotrophic-like phase lasting approximately 10 days, during which it is difficult to take action, succeeded by a necrotrophic phase spanning 11 to 21 days. The fungus enters wheat leaves via stomata roughly 3 days post-spore germination [38]. Structures similar to appressoria, which are not essential for penetration, develop at the area of stomatal entry. Upon entering the substomatal cavities, the fungus proliferates within the intercellular space, closely interacting with mesophyll cells, while not creating specialized feeding structures. In the asymptomatic phase, fungal biomass is minimal and rises exponentially in the initial stages of the necrotrophic phase. In the necrotrophic stage, the fungus forms black pycnidia in sub-stomatal spaces, which generate pycnodiospores that are expelled by the discharge of mucilage (cirrus) through the stomata. Such a phenomenon depends on the suitability of the prevailing environmental conditions [70, 71]. In this study, it was observed that typical symptoms of STB were noted on the leaves of the varieties Hidase, Kubsa and Ogolcho across locations in both cropping years; varietal genetic susceptibility to STB could be the main reason. Singh et al. [72] and Osman et al. [73] also reported a similar result for characteristic symptoms of STB in wheat fields around the world. Symptoms of STB first appear as spot-like structures on the lower leaves and progress vertically along the wheat height in unsprayed plots of each variety. However, the integrated use of varieties and fungicide spray for STB management considerably prevented further disease progression compared with unsprayed plots in all varieties across locations and cropping years.

Findings showed that STB development varied significantly among tested varieties and fungicides. The EPX + TPM-sprayed plots exhibited lower disease pressure than Tebuconazole- and Propiconazole-sprayed plots, regardless of the varieties grown. Variability could likely be related to the product formulation, the proportion/amount of constituent materials, the better active substances, and the persistence of fungicides, which would create a more efficient control of STB than other products. Gudero et al. [74] posited that variations in the class of fungicide, the type of active ingredient, and their respective concentrations result in substantial differences in the effectiveness of fungicides for managing fungal diseases, regardless of the variety tested, application rates, and spray frequency. However, recurrent use of EPX + TPM might lead to resistance development in the Z. tritici population, and special attention should be paid as epoxiconazole is an at-risk triazole, suggesting STB resistance genotype development via a breeding program, though its durability is not guaranteed for long-term use. Moreover, climate change currently plays significant roles in many parts of pathosystems, and this might lead to fluctuations in disease onset, development of new biotypes/strains, and fungicide decay, and, as a result, it may influence fungicide efficacy in the future. A significant decline in disease scores may be ascribed to the suppressive effects of fungicides, along with the inherent genetic behaviour of the test varieties, which is known to reduce pathogen infectivity, inoculum spread, lesion formation and egress of secondary infections within or across fields. A similar phenomenon has been reported by Gan et al. [75], although the efficacy of foliar fungicides is influenced by host resistance, disease pressure, the success of foliar coverage and weather conditions.

Different levels of STB development were also observed in the test varieties with or without fungicide application. Application of any of the three fungicides on the variety Shorima, followed by Hidase and Ogolcho, minimized STB pressure compared to the variety Kubsa, which received the same fungicide applications. Such disparities in responses of the test wheat varieties to STB disease could be associated with the genetic background of the evaluated varieties. In other words, the test varieties may contain a gene or genes that confer resistance or susceptibility to STB, which would either reduce or increase disease epidemics. Corroboratively, a study to assess multiple fungal disease resistance in wheat genotypes across various environmental conditions revealed that wheat genotypes exhibited variable reactions to multiple diseases, including STB, due to the inherent genetic constituents of the test genotypes in the United States [72, 73].

In this study, it was found that using a combination of variety and fungicide declined disease parameters more than employing each tactic separately to manage STB. Previous research highlights that employing individual strategies in isolation has inherent limitations. These methods alone may be insufficient to achieve an adequate reduction in disease levels, even when taking into account the known traits of disease-causing pathogens and prevailing weather conditions [25, 34]. Hence, integrated disease management represents a significant option, as it is relatively safe for the environment, offers reduced health risks, minimizes resistance development, and provides cost-effectiveness from epidemiological perspectives [32]. Current investigations showed that the STB severity and rAUDPC were highly reduced when all wheat varieties were maintained with the fungicide EPX + TPM, followed by Tebuconazole, across the three locations during the two cropping years. Loughman and Thomas [76] and Gomes et al. [77] showed that fungicide sprays on wheat varieties reduced STB development under field conditions in Australia and Portugal, respectively. Likewise, previous scholars noted that fungicide sprays at the suggested rate, interval, and frequency on wheat varieties with different levels of reaction to STB highly lowered STB pressure relative to unsprayed plots in various parts of the world [18, 78, 79].

Findings also showed that combined implementation of variety and fungicide sprays comparatively enhanced agronomic responses compared to unsprayed control plots of each variety across locations and cropping seasons. This indicates that fungicide use could offer crop development while suppressing STB-associated adverse effects. The foliar sprays of EPX + TPM, followed by Tebuconazole, enhanced the growth, yield, and yield-related traits of the test varieties compared to the plots that were not sprayed. This could be due to low disease pressure in sprayed plots having no or minimal effects on the rate of assimilate accumulation, nutrient and water transport to the top of the plant and kernels, and overall yield productivity because the last three leaves on top play important roles in yield accumulation during crop development. In other words, the low GY and BMY gain/largest yield penalty could be due to the existence of favorable microclimates that support STB development and the failure of some tactics to suppress the disease. These phenomena would result in a notable decrease in the photosynthetic leaf area or photosynthetic efficiency, thereby significantly reducing the GY and BMY performance of the crop. Moreover, variability in agronomic trait performance could be due to environmental adaptability, varietal genetic potential, effectiveness of fungicide active substances across environments and treatment combinations.

Parallel to the above arguments, Steinberg [16] and Forrer and Zadoks [79] reported that the leaf area index, along with photosynthetic leaf area and photosynthetic efficiency, was low in wheat leaves highly infected with STB and no or inefficient measures were applied to the disease. This led to an overall reduction in growth and yield-related attributes of the crop. Similarly, Suffert et al. [14] noted that increased STB intensity due to no or inefficient management measures led to a reduction in green leaf area and the development of lesions, which ultimately curtailed the photosynthetic efficiency in infected leaves and reduced the yield obtained. Lynch et al. [80] and Marroni et al. [79] also demonstrated that the intensity of STB and the yields obtained varied among different varieties and fungicides across various locations and cropping seasons.

Research reports indicated that combined ANOVA is inadequate for multi-location trials to determine treatment effects against study parameters. Yan and Falk [39] stated that ANOVA does not exhibit the contributions of each of management strategy, environment, and their interaction effects, nor which tactic is stable for yield boosting under disease pressure across locations. Thus, AMMI and GGE biplot analyses are effective methods for identifying strategies that improve disease management and yield traits in multi-location field studies. In this study, tested management strategies had relatively better effects on overall mean STB reduction and yield enhancement in one environment than in others. Favorable weather, tested variety genetic backgrounds, fungicide sprays, and other factors impacting STB development and yield performance in various settings may account for this phenomenon.

Madden et al. [25] emphasize that the effectiveness of management tactics aimed at disease reduction and enhancing desirable traits in crops is significantly influenced by various factors. These include current weather conditions, the host’s genetic resistance to diseases, ecological adaptability, types of management tactics employed, and soil fertility status, among other elements. Prior reports have also suggested that MEI studies can capture and quantify those scenarios in which there exist of worthy acts of management strategy, environment, and their interactions on disease progress and yield traits [36, 39]. In essence, effective disease management entails a holistic approach that recognizes the interplay among the components of the pathosystem, exploiting an integration of various strategies to reduce disease intensity while enhancing yield performance.

Results obtained from AMMI-based ANOVA exhibited variance due to management strategies, environments and their interactions that were significant for the study parameters. This indicates the performances of management strategies and the environments were different. Additionally, significant variation in environments and MEI reflected the multiplicity of the environments and the response of the management measures these environments. This suggests that management options scored differently across diverse environments, and farmers can decrease STB pressure and increase productivity by choosing management tactics that are consistent across a range of weather patterns and soil types. In this study, the effect of management tactics was higher than that of the environment and the MEI on the aspects of the analyzed study parameters. On average, the degree of the management sum of squares was more than 4.51- and 10.81-fold higher than that of the environment and MEI, respectively. The large sum of squares for management indicates that the tactics varied significantly, contributing to most variations observed in disease and yield responses across different environments. Thus, such conditions suggested that STB management part contributed a greater portion of the variation, while the environment and MEI contributed to a lesser extent to the total variation among treatments. Overall, the large sum of squares for management strategies regarding the study parameters indicated that they have a substantial influence on controlling STB disease and improving the yield performance of wheat in growing areas of southern Ethiopia.

Earlier reports also suggested that management strategies play a vital role in disease development and agronomic performance across environments under MEI studies [35, 36]. Additionally, the AMMI-based ANOVA for the interaction effects was further divided into two significant IPCAs for the study parameters. The subsequent IPCAs were non-significant and got mostly noisy and thus do not aid in calling valuable observations. Such situations imply that they do not assist in predicting valuable observations in MEI for study parameters. Yan et al. [42] and Yan and Tinker [65] also reported that the worth of grasping most of the MEI sum of squares in the first two IPCAs is to acquire accurate information.

AMMI biplot analyses for disease and yield traits are performed and visualized to identify variation among test environments, evaluate consistent and effective strategies, and assess the environments that distinguish different management measures. In the biplot, some management strategies and environments established lower rAUDPC and higher yields compared to those located on the biplot’s left side of the Y-axis. This revealed that particular management strategies were more effective at reducing STB pressure and improving yields in specific environmental settings, emphasizing the importance of MEI in shaping disease onset and response. Yan and Tinker [65] supported that the AMMI model can be employed to evaluate management strategy by environment data and is mainly useful in appraising the MEI on the study parameters. The authors also indicated that the model offers a comprehensive method for evaluating management strategy efficiency across various environmental conditions, aiding in the selection of consistent and effective strategies for multi-location trials.

Likewise, Gollob [81] stated that if the main effects depict an IPCA value near zero, it specifies the least interaction effect or stability across environments, while a higher IPCA value hints at instability and the utilization of specific environments. In this study, some management strategies demonstrated lower rAUDPC and higher yield performance, with the highest IPCA 1 scores. Conversely, some other management strategies exhibited IPCA 1 values near zero, indicating greater consistency and comparatively less interplay with the test environment. On the other hand, the environments exhibited the biggest angles, indicating a low relationship between mean disease development and yield performance in various cropping years at the locations. Similarly, a cluster of cropping years and management levels within the same location in distinct quadrats suggests that the effectiveness of certain management strategies in reducing disease varies across cropping years and management levels at that location.

Such disparities in management strategies for disease development and yield response across various environments could be attributed to the nature of the pathosystem components and other influencing factors during the growing periods. Campbell and Madden [32] and Beres et al. [35] emphasized that the interaction of the pathosystem components within a specific area significantly affects disease management efficacy. This is influenced by factors such as the emergence of aggressive strains or new biotypes of the targeted pathogen, the involvement of additional disease-causing pathogens, the innate behavior of the host, environmental adaptability, suitable weather conditions for infection, and the sensitivity of fungicides to environmental conditions and the cultivated cultivar.

Current findings indicate effective management measures that consistently reduce disease and enhance crop yield performance across various environments. These measures are critical for crop protection strategies focused on mitigating disease pressure and increasing yield potential, especially under varying climatic conditions [35, 39]. A stable management strategy refers to one that exhibits consistent efficiency irrespective of variations in the prevailing environment [35, 36]. Moreover, the environments, all displaying positive IPCA 1 scores indicated that the management strategies experienced minimal MEI. However, the highest interactions among strategies were noted in some environments; comparable analogies have been made by Yan and Tinker [65] and Crossa et al. [82] in other parts of the world.

Similar to the AMMI biplot, the GGE biplot is a tool for summarizing data that enables an assessment of environments because of the GGE view’s representativeness and discriminative power, which gives it an edge over the AMMI biplot analyses. The GGE biplot offers advantages over the AMMI biplot by enhancing the precision of visual observations, which aids in differentiating ideal environments for management strategy studies focused on pests, yield traits, and resistance breeding [39, 72]. Management strategies and environments were examined together via the which-won-where model of the GGE-biplot. It provides a polygon view to visualize the summarized data by separating the biplot into various sectors. The presence of distinct testing environments in various sectors suggested that those sectors have distinct winning management strategies for the study parameters [41, 83]. In this study, the polygon view was partitioned into eight sectors for assessing disease scores and six sectors for yield traits. Nonetheless, every testing environment was categorized within one of the disease score sectors and two sectors for yield traits. This categorization could be attributable to variations in longitudinal and latitudinal differences [84]. Yan and Tinker [65] and Engida et al. [85] stated that environments within the same sector share similar features and reveal the same winning management strategies, whereas environments located in various sectors take hold of different winning strategies. Similarly, the vertex management strategies in each sector signify the most effective strategy in environments where the indicators are situated within the respective sector [84, 85].

Identifying testing environments as distinct mega-environments is a fundamental approach to achieving consistent management strategy efficiency in STB development and yield performance within that specific sector. Beres et al. [35] and Yan and Falk [39] suggested that representativeness is the key issue in ascertaining how a test environment ought to be used in management strategy testing under a suitable pathosystem, presuming acceptable discriminating ability. In this study, the GGE biplot analysis indicated that all environments examined were both discriminating and representative concerning the management of STB disease. This conclusion was supported by the presence of longer vectors and narrower angles relative to the average environmental coordination axis. Such characteristics suggest that the test environments shared similar traits and were responsive to STB, identifying them as hotspots and optimal settings for STB development. Comparable analogies concerning plant diseases and yield potentials across diverse test environments have been reported by Beres et al. [35] and Yan and Falk [39].

On the other hand, environments 5 and 6 are characterized as discriminating and representative, featuring longer vectors and shorter angles between the average environment axis and the environment vector. These properties make them effective for selecting optimal strategies against high yields, as they provide more information regarding the variances among test strategies. Environments 1, 2, 3, and 4 took a relatively greater angle between the environment vector and the abscissa of the mean environment axis, which provides little information about variations in yield among the efficiencies of test strategies and thus could not be considered test environments. Prior reports indicated that environments characterized by longer vectors and lower angles are effective in distinguishing and representing test management strategies, thereby providing substantial information about their variance. Conversely, environments with shorter vectors and greater angles fail to discriminate and represent these strategies, offering minimal to no information regarding variations among them [65, 84].

The GGE biplot demonstrated its effectiveness by accounting for 99.27% of the total sum of squares related to the rAUDPC and 95.24% regarding yield traits. This analysis was based on the main effects (IPCA 1) and MEI (IPCA 2) across various management strategies and environmental conditions, indicating a significant and intricate MEI in the disease and yield trial data. The vertex management strategies significantly influenced disease pressure and yield traits. They either reduced disease pressure while improving yield traits or increased disease development, negatively impacting yield performance. These strategies were notably located far from the biplot origin, indicating a strong contribution from the prevailing MEI. The rest of the management strategies were retained within the polygon view for both study parameters, indicating a non-crossover type of MEI, and the strategies were not affected by the change in environmental conditions and other influencing factors. However, variation in the management strategy efficiency within environments implied a significant influence of environments during the growing periods and the presence of a mega-environment [65, 85].

GGE biplot analyses identified the most effective strategies for lowering STB pressure and enhancing wheat yield as follows: Ogolcho (T11) and Shorima (T15) with EPX + TPM; Hidase (T2) with Tebuconazole; and Shorima (T16) with Propiconazole. However, varietal environmental adaptability and economic return were the criteria for a judicious decision in selecting the best one of whichever strategies won in the study areas. Thus, the GGE biplot served as a tool to pinpoint effective strategies in various environments and identify the optimal strategies for specific and mega-environments. It allowed for the proposal of tailored strategies for individual environments, facilitated the evaluation of disease scores, yield traits, and management consistency, and opened avenues for economic feasibility studies, especially when multiple strategies proved successful simultaneously in ascertaining the best approach for specific areas. Previous reports highlight the GGE biplot polygon view as the best method for identifying winning strategies by predicting interaction patterns between test management strategies and study environments. Current findings highlight the importance of selecting suitable test environments in crop protection programs, stressing the necessity of choosing discriminating environments to efficiently distinguish between management strategies.

However, the current study has its own limitations that should be acknowledged when interpreting and extrapolating the findings. First, experiments were conducted only in three highland sites in southern Ethiopia over two cropping seasons, which restricts the geographical and temporal scope of inference and may not fully capture the interannual and regional variability in STB epidemics. Second, the work focused on a limited set of wheat varieties and fungicide programs, so conclusions on integrated variety–fungicide tactics strictly apply to these specific genotypes, active ingredients, and spray schedules and may not extend to other cultivars or fungicide groups. Third, disease development was based on natural infection, and the Z. tritici population was not characterized in terms of pathotype diversity or fungicide sensitivity, which constrains the ability to generalize to other pathogen populations or anticipate future shifts in virulence and resistance.

Field investigation indicated that high STB intensity resulted in significant yield losses. Hence, yield losses might be ascribed to the severe infection of STB due to favorable conditions at the full crop growth stage, which may have interfered with the photosynthetic activities of the crop. Forrer and Zadoks [86] and Marroni et al. [81] reported that the highest risk to wheat from STB occurs when conducive environmental variables favor Z. tritici spore dispersal and spread during and just after flag leaf emergence. Yield losses have been linked to the entire leaf area infected, especially the top two or three leaves (flag, second and third leaves), which produce necrotic lesions and chlorotic flakes during the growing periods.

Thus, study findings indicate devising and applying STB control measures based on efficient fungicides and responsive varieties can improve yield margins in wheat. Thus, it is possible to recognize that using a combination of resistant to moderately resistant varieties, even with susceptible varieties and fungicide sprays, enhances agronomic performance while minimizing STB pressure and yield losses. Tadesse et al. [18] corroborated that combined use of wheat varieties and fungicides with appropriate spray frequency significantly reduced STB intensity, increased agronomic performance of the wheat, and decreased yield loss caused by STB. If not, STB has been reported to cause noteworthy yield losses of up to 82% [19], 30–50% [68], or around 54% [18] in wheat, depending on varietal susceptibility, disease onset, location, and favorable environmental conditions. The current study could not thoroughly define the consequences of medium levels of wheat rust pressure and other factors, which may also be held responsible for yield losses in addition to STB.

Correlation matrix analyses revealed diverse associations among and between epidemiological and agronomic parameters in the study locations over cropping years. Accordingly, high positive associations were observed between disease parameters, suggesting that epidemiological variables were associated, with unsprayed control plots developing diseases more rapidly than sprayed ones across various resistant varieties and fungicide treatments. Agrios [34] and Guant [87] also argued that there is a positive and significant link between and among epidemiological indicators and progressive epidemic development during disease outbreak periods. Supporting current findings, Tadesse et al. [18] reported that STB disease parameters were highly significant and strongly linked to one another. Additionally, agronomic parameters established positive and strong associations between and among themselves. Other research reports also indicated that agronomic parameters had positive and highly significant associations with one another in the correlation matrix studies [18, 44].

However, the correlation analyses identified that disease scores had negative and highly significant correlations with agronomic parameters in the three locations over cropping years, which attested to the economic significance of STB in causing yield loss in wheat varieties. Tadesse et al. [17] observed a significant negative association between epidemiological factors and agronomic parameters. This association is attributed to the detrimental effects of causal pathogens on the physiological activities of the host, which in turn lead to a decrease in the crop’s agronomic performance [79, 86]. The negative associations between epidemiological and agronomic parameters could suggest that epidemiological parameters are key elements in predicting yield losses.

Regression analyses found that when rAUDPC was taken into account as a predictor variable and yield as a responder variable in the model, significant losses in grain yield predictions were detected in every unit of disease progress. The regression graph exhibited a decline in yield and swept towards zero asymptotes as the rAUDPC tended to increase. This indicates the inverse relationship between disease and the yield performance of wheat. As the level of rAUDPC increased, the yield gains decreased, signifying high losses in the tested wheat. Guant [87] acknowledged the effects of plant diseases, which had significant relationships with growth and yield deceleration, as well as yield losses of cultivated crops at all stages of disease development.

Variations in economic returns in this study are linked to factors including disease pressure, treatment performance, locations, cropping years, weather conditions, and the costs of production and marketing of grain yields. Previous economic feasibility report also suggested that commodity selection based on market demand, total input costs of production, crop production time, product quality and quantity, and the market prices of these products at the time of sale significantly influence economic returns [66]. Integrated approach via variety and fungicide spraying to manage STB led to a significant reduction in its pressure, resulting in improved productivity and notable variations economic returns compared to unsprayed control plots. For instance, the use of the Shorima variety, followed by Hidase and Ogolcho, combined with EPX + TPM fungicide and Tebuconazole, resulted in the highest economic returns, representing the most profitable management measure. Other studies evaluated the profitability of integrating agrochemicals with different management strategies in host-pathogen dynamics [31, 54, 88], highlighting factors such as treatment effectiveness, disease status, weather conditions, production quality and quantity, and associated production and marketing costs. Overall, the current investigation indicated the affordability of the strategy followed for STB compared with the existing approach, but sustainability issues (like cost-effectiveness under fungicide price inflation and fungicide resistance due to unwise uses), environmental issues (like pollution and affecting non-target organisms), and consumer safety (health-related issues) require special attention.

Conclusions

Septoria tritici blotch significantly affects wheat variety responses due to high disease pressure, resulting in reduced agronomic performance, economic returns, and yield losses. Varietal performance varied based on integrated management strategies, with fungicides EPX + TPM and Tebuconazole used on varieties Shorima, Hidase, and Ogolcho proving most effective in reducing STB development and enhancing traits. AMMI and GGE analyses show these treatments yield consistent results against STB, particularly with EPX + TPM on Hidase, Shorima, and Ogolcho across various environments. Economic analyses indicated that using these varieties with fungicides applied three times at 10-day intervals following disease onset leads to better financial outcomes compared to unsprayed plots. Recommendations emphasize this combination for effective STB management to improve wheat productivity. Chencha and Burji are identified as optimal locations for assessing variety performance under STB stress. The study offers insights for policies guiding wheat disease management and producer advisory services focused on forecasting disease epidemics tied to host-pathogen-environment interactions amid changing conditions. Limitations include the focus on three highland sites in southern Ethiopia over two cropping seasons, which restricts broader applicability. The limited variety and fungicide programs, such as types, rates and spray frequency,also affect generalization. Consequently, the study underscores the need for extensive investigations across diverse locations and years to formulate concrete recommendations. Hence, integrated management strategies for long-term STB resistance involve diverse, resistant wheat varieties, varied fungicide types, rates and spray frequency, and practices like adhering to recommended planting dates, managing residues, and crop rotation with legumes. The aim is to develop a climate-smart approach against STB while ensuring economic feasibility and environmental safety through the use of effective fungicides and diverse varieties.
