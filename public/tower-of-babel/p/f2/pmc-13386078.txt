Factors affecting first-time expectant mothers’ communication about contraception and contraceptive use with their sexual partners: a qualitative study in urban Accra, Ghana
Authors: Monica Kekrebesi, John Kuumuori Ganle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo explore the factors that affect communication about contraception and modern contraceptive use among first-time expectant mothers attending antenatal clinics in urban Accra, Ghana.DesignA phenomenological qualitative study was conducted. In-depth interviews were conducted using a semistructured guide until data saturation was achieved. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Thematic analysis was performed, with data presented as narratives supported by quotes.SettingThe study was conducted at one polyclinic in the city of Accra, Ghana.ParticipantsParticipants were 25 first-time expectant mothers who were attending antenatal care clinics. These were purposively sampled.Primary and secondary outcomesThe main outcome of interest was understanding the factors that affect first-time expectant mothers’ communication about contraception and contraceptive use with their sexual partners. Secondary outcome was awareness/understanding about contraception and modern contraceptive methods.ResultsFirst-time expectant mothers reported varying levels of understanding about contraception and modern contraceptive methods. While majority reported hearing about contraception and modern contraceptives, only a few knew specific modern contraceptive methods. Information about modern contraceptives came from friends, media and midwives/nurses. As regards communication with their sexual partners about modern contraceptives, majority reported not being able to initiate any conversations. A few however reported open discussions. Key barriers to communication included women’s inability to initiate discussions on contraceptive use due to traditional gender role expectations, fear of stigmatisation and religious objections.ConclusionThis study has highlighted that communication about contraception and modern contraceptive use among first-time expectant mothers is hindered by a complex interplay of barriers—gender role expectations, stigma and religious beliefs. These barriers hamper open discussions and informed decision-making about modern contraceptive use. To address these barriers, we call for targeted interventions to improve awareness and knowledge about contraception and modern contraceptive methods, engage both men and women in family planning education and programming, empower women to address gender inequalities and promote open dialogue and mutual decision-making among couples.Keywords: Africa South of the Sahara, Public health, Pregnant Women, PUBLIC HEALTH, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH, SEXUAL MEDICINE

Introduction

Contraception, particularly modern contraception, is crucial for preventing unintended pregnancies and maternal mortality. Up to one-third of maternal deaths can be prevented through contraceptive use.1 Postponing childbirth can lead to women being free to participate in the labour market and other economic benefits.2 However, there is a significant gap in contraceptive uptake globally. Out of 1.9 billion women globally, 1.1 billion need family planning, with 842 million using modern methods and 80 million using traditional methods.3

Around 47 million women in sub-Saharan African countries have an unmet need for modern contraception.4 Unmet need is particularly prevalent in low socio-economic groups, rural communities and those coping with conflicts and disasters.1 In Ghana, the prevalence of modern contraceptive use is 36% for married women and 62.9% for sexually active unmarried women.5 Modern methods are more commonly used than traditional ones, with injectables and implants being the most widely used.6

Several factors including service-related barriers, accessibility and individual characteristics affect the availability and usage of modern contraception.7 Many women in union decide against using modern contraception for partner-related and personal reasons.8 The low prevalence of contraceptive use in sub-Saharan Africa has particularly been attributed to partner refusal.9 Men’s resistance to contraceptive use is linked to fears about marital infidelity, lack of options for male contraception and perceived adverse consequences of female contraceptive use.10 11

Increasingly, spousal communication is being emphasised in family planning programmes and research as one of the strategies to improve modern contraceptive uptake. In some analysts’ view, it is the first step in a rational fertility decision-making process.12 Several studies indicate a positive association between partner communication and contraceptive use.13 While some studies have characterised men’s role in modern contraceptive use as ‘absent or problematic’,14 other studies describe how partner communication is limited or non-existent, often reflected in high rates of discordant reporting of modern contraceptive use by couples or in findings that suggest that couples might not be using contraception because each falsely believes their spouse does not approve of its use.8

The determinants of spousal communication are varied. In sub-Saharan Africa, gender roles and norms are particularly salient, significantly shaping spousal communication and subsequent family planning decision-making.11 Although modern contraceptive methods and services are frequently geared toward women, men are often the primary decision-makers about family size and their partner’s use of contraceptive methods in many African settings.11 In addition to directly influencing contraceptive use decisions, male attitudes toward family planning can strongly affect contraceptive use indirectly. In the absence of explicit communication, women often incorrectly perceive that their partners are opposed to family planning and therefore engage in covert use or do not use contraception at all.10 Furthermore, spousal disagreement can be a deterrent because women might fear initiating a difficult conversation about family planning.15 16

While it has been suggested that effective communication between spouses regarding contraception allows for shared decision-making, increases mutual understanding and promotes modern contraceptive use, within Ghana, very limited research has examined the extent to which first-time expectant mothers communicate with their sexual partners and the factors that affect spousal communication on contraceptive use among first-time expectant women.16 We focus on first-time pregnant women because first-time pregnancies are a critical juncture for preventing future unintended pregnancies. Therefore, understanding how first-time expectant mothers and their sexual partners approach contraception can inform strategies to reduce unintended pregnancies. This study therefore explored the factors that affect first-time expectant mothers’ communication about contraception and contraceptive use with their sexual partners.

Methods

Study design

A phenomenological qualitative study was conducted. This design enabled the researchers to explore couples’ communication about contraception and contraceptive use and the different factors affecting couples’ communication about contraception and contraceptive use at a single time point.

Study area

The study was conducted at the Dansoman Polyclinic located in the Ablekuma West Municipality in the city of Accra, Ghana. The Ablekuma West municipality has a population of 156 713.17 The Dansoman Polyclinic is the main primary healthcare facility in the Ablekuma West Municipality. The polyclinic offers an array of healthcare services, including outpatient care, maternal and child health services, laboratory services and pharmacy provisions. Notable departments in the polyclinic include a well-organised family planning unit, a child health clinic and an antenatal clinic. The facility caters to approximately 242 192 clients annually, with a family planning acceptor rate of 15.8% in 2020, 22.8% in 2021 and 26.6% in 2022. The facility however does not have a skilled delivery suite or postnatal clinic. In 2022, the facility recorded a total of 388 first-time antenatal care registrants.17 This relatively high number of first-time mothers influenced the choice of the facility for this study.

Study participants

Participants were married and cohabiting women aged 15–49 who were expecting their first child. Our specific selection criteria considered first-time expectant mothers who were attending the Dansoman Polyclinic for antenatal care and who were willing to participate in the study. However, we did not select women who were not mentally capable of giving informed consent.

Recruitment of participants

A total of 25 participants were recruited into the study. This number of participants was dictated by data saturation: we stopped further recruitment and interviewing when recurrent information on major topics and themes was obtained from subsequent interviews.

A purposive recruitment strategy with maximum variation was used. This approach was especially appropriate for this study since it allowed for the deliberate selection of participants with qualities required to answer the study’s primary question. This was done using maximum variation technique. For example, we recruited participants based on whether they were officially married or cohabiting. We excluded single, separated or widowed women as our focus was on spousal communication. Similarly, we deliberately selected women from different age groups. This ensured that experiences from different age groups were captured. We also deliberately recruited women with different educational backgrounds, employment status and religious affiliation. This also ensured that the perspectives of women with different experiences were captured in our study.

In terms of recruitment process, eligible participants were initially identified through collaboration with the head of the antenatal care (ANC) clinic. Participants were then approached individually by the researchers. The researchers introduced the study to each participant and asked each participant if they were happy to participate in the study. Interviews were then subsequently conducted among those who indicated interest in participating.

Data collection methods and tools

In-depth interviews were conducted to collect data from the participants. Interviews lasted between 40 and 60 minutes. The interviews were conducted in languages that participants were fluent and comfortable speaking (ie, English, Ga, Twi, Ewe, Hausa and Ga-Adangbe). The interviews were conducted by the first author who is fluent in English, Ga and Twi. However, three research assistants were used in contexts where other local dialects were used (ie, Ewe, Hausa and Ga-Adangbe). These research assistants were trained to understand the questions and how to ask them in order to elicit the desired responses from participants. The interview sessions were tape-recorded with permission from participants and field notes were taken alongside.

A semi-structured interview guide with open-ended questions and probes was used to facilitate interviewing (see online supplemental table 1). The questions in the interview guide were open-ended, allowing participants to express themselves and provide unrestricted information. The interview guide was pretested on five first-time expectant women at the Dansoman Polyclinic to address any ambiguities in the questions. The five women who were involved in the pretest were not part of the actual study. Following the pre-test, the initial interview guide was updated with additional probes.

Data management

All tape recordings were transcribed verbatim. The first author transcribed all interviews that were done in English, Ga and Twi. For interviews that were conducted in Ewe, Hausa and Ga-Adangbe, the three research assistants first transcribed the interviews in the original language and then translated them into English. To check the quality of transcription, the second author randomly selected three interviews (one transcribed by the first author and the other two transcribed by two research assistants) and transcribed them. These were compared with the transcriptions done by the first author and the research assistants. No major discrepancies were noted. The second author also checked the quality of the translation from local dialects to English by performing back-to-back translation on two interviews. These were compared with the original transcripts, and no major differences were noted.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was used to analyse the data. The analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s18 six-step framework: familiarisation with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes and producing the final report. To begin, transcripts were carefully read several times to develop familiarity with the data and to identify similarities. A coding framework was then developed using both inductive and deductive coding methods. Initial open coding was inductive so that codes reflected participants’ language and experiences rather than a preset scheme. The first author led the initial coding. After refining an inductive codebook through coder discussion, light deductive structuring was applied to organise the data driven codes in relation to the study objective. Words, ideas and thoughts were grouped into codes based on the study’s constructs and objective. Each transcript was coded separately, and initial codes were then categorised and named. Similar codes were combined to form themes and subthemes. To enhance inter-coder reliability, a second coder (ie, second author) independently coded a subset of the transcripts. Any discrepancies were discussed and reconciled to refine the coding framework and minimise subjective bias. Furthermore, the agreed codes were grouped into sub-themes, which were then refined and categorised into overarching themes. Constant comparison was applied to ensure consistency and validity across themes. Finally, themes were presented as narratives, and relevant quotes were used to support identified themes. We validated the coded and themed data through member checking, where a summary of initial key results was shared with selected participants to ensure their perspectives were accurately represented. To further ensure data quality and analytical rigour, the research team held meetings regularly to reflect on how individual biases could affect the data. This process of continuous reflexivity ensured that personal biases were minimised. Transparency in data analysis was also maintained by fully documenting the coding procedures, decision criteria and any revisions made during the analysis process. In reporting results, the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research (COREQ) were followed.19 The NVivo V.14 software was used to help code, theme and analyse the data.

Patient involvement

Five first-time expectant women gave feedback on the interview guide to ensure its reliability and address any ambiguities in the questions. Additionally, approval was sought from various institutions, including the principal medical officer in charge of the Dansoman Polyclinic and the Director of the Ablekuma West Municipal Health Directorate.

Themes and subthemes

Awareness about contraception and modern contraceptive use

Majority of the first-time expectant mothers interviewed reported hearing about contraception and modern contraceptive methods:

At ANC clinic, the nurses told us about contraception…they said it can help us prevent pregnancy if we are not ready to be pregnant. So, yes, I know about contraception (Participant #6).

I have heard that contraceptives can help women avoid pregnancy…yes, that is the only thing that I have heard (Participant #13).

Another woman shared:

I have heard that there are tablets or pills that you can take and will not get pregnant. But I don’t know how it works…I have never seen it, and I have never taken it (Participant #5).

Awareness about contraception and contraceptive methods was however not uniform. Participants with higher levels of education (secondary and tertiary) appeared more knowledgeable compared with those with basic education. Similarly, older women (ie, 30–39) appeared more knowledgeable than their younger counterparts.

While many of the women reported hearing about contraception, only a few knew specific modern contraceptive methods and how they work:

Honestly, I don’t know any method. I have heard that there is contraception, but I don’t really know the specific methods and how they work (Participant #3).

Another woman said:

I know about the injections and pills. That’s all. I don’t know any others. Even these two [referring injection and pills], I don’t know how they work or when to take them. I have never used them. Maybe I have to ask the midwife (participant #9).

One woman also said:

I have heard that there are pills and something that you can insert into your arm. But I don’t really know the details. I don’t understand how they work (participant #7).

Again, we observed that knowledge of specific modern contraceptive methods varied across the different participant groups. Older participants (30–39) with tertiary education and Christians appeared to know more specific modern contraceptive methods.

When participants were probed about contraceptive use, only seven reported ever using a modern contraceptive method (see table 1). These participants were mostly those with tertiary education, Christians and employed. The most common method was the pill, which was sourced mostly from pharmacy shops.

I have ever used the pill. That’s the only one I have used…I bought it from a nearby pharmacy (participant #1).

Before this pregnancy, I used the pill. Yes, I used it a couple of times. But now no, because of the pregnancy. Maybe, after the pregnancy (participant #20).

Access to information on contraception modern contraceptive methods

As regards access to information on contraception and modern contraceptive methods, most participants reported hearing about contraception and modern contraceptive methods from friends, media especially social media and midwives/nurses.

I heard about it [referring to contraception] from my friend. She has been using it so she told me that if I don’t want to be pregnant, then I can use something like the pill or I can go to the clinic and ask for the injection. Later on, I also read something about it [referring to contraception] on social media…I mean Facebook (Participant #6).

Another woman said:

I first got to know about it through my friend. She sent me a WhatsApp message with some information about contraceptives (Participant #15).

Another woman shared:

For me it was when I went for antenatal that I heard the nurse and midwife talk about it. They were talking to women who came for weighing [referring to postnatal care]. I was just listening to what they were saying but we were not part (Participant #12).

However, the sources of information appeared to vary according to participant type. For instance, younger participants as well as those with secondary or tertiary education frequently cited the media including social media as their main source of information.

I’ll say mostly I get the information from the media…I read a lot from social media, and sometimes I google when I need specific information (Participant #7).

Participants who identified themselves as Muslims and those who were unemployed frequently cited friends as their main source while older women cited healthcare workers as their main source of information.

For me, it is the health workers. This is my third pregnancy, and so any time I get pregnant, and I go for ANC and even postnatal, the nurses and midwives always discuss contraception and the different methods we can use. That is how I have come to know these things (Participant #18).

When probed whether they feel the information they had received about modern contraceptives was adequate, almost all of them said no:

As I have said, I really don’t know much about contraceptives. What I know so far is very limited. So, yes, I would want to know more. Who knows, maybe it could help me in the future, or I could help a friend or a sister to avoid getting pregnant (Participant #21).

As for the education, I need it …in fact most women need it because what I know is not adequate, so we need more education about these things (Participant #23).

Communication about contraception and contraceptive use

A major objective of this study was to understand whether first-time expectant mothers communicate with their sexual partners about contraception and contraceptive use and the factors that affect this communication. Most of the women reported no to limited communication between them and their partners regarding the use of modern contraceptives:

We do not discuss these matters…I have only talked about it with friends but not my husband (Participant #14).

Another woman shared a similar sentiment:

I have never talked to my husband about it, and he has also not said anything. I feel if anything he should talk to me about it because he is the head of the family. So, I expect that if anything he should start the conversation (Participant #25).

A few participants (n=6) however reported discussing contraception and contraceptive use with their partners.

For me yes, we have discussed it. It wasn’t difficult. Maybe because my husband is a health worker and me too, I understand these things, we talked about it. Even now we talk about other things like sex during pregnancy. So, for me, it has not been a problem. I started it, and my husband didn’t have a problem with it (Participant #1).

Another participant also said:

We have discussed contraception and contraceptive use many times…it started when we were even dating, so it has not been a problem. When I had to change from the injection to the implant, we discussed it, and my partner was fine with the change (Participant #20).

Almost all the women who reported no difficulty discussing contraception and contraceptive use with their partners were employed and also had at least secondary education.

For those who reported no discussion with their partners, we asked them why they had not discussed contraception and contraceptive use with their partners. Many of them said they had not done so precisely because they found it difficult initiating that conversation:

I don’t know how to start this conversation. Maybe my partner will think that I don’t want to bear children for him that is why I am talking about contraception. So, I just keep quiet. If he starts the conversation, then I can share my thoughts, but for me to start, no, it is difficult (Participant #5).

Another woman said:

Actually, there were times that I wanted to discuss with my husband that maybe I should do family planning. But I was afraid of what he will think. So, I just kept quiet and went and took the injection. But even that I realised at some point that he was getting worried and suspicious. But I wish we could talk openly about it. I hope that after I give birth, we’ll be able to talk about it because I believe it is very important to discuss these issues (Participants #3).

Factors affecting communication about contraceptives

As regards the factors that affect communication about contraception and contraceptive use, three main factors were identified, namely traditional gender role expectations, fear of stigmatisation and religious objections.

Traditional gender role expectations

Several participants reported that they have not engaged in communication with their partners regarding contraception and contraceptive use because they expected their partner to initiate the conversation. This is because traditional gender norms construct males as the family head and lead decision-makers. Therefore, most women said they expected their male partners to start the conversation so that they (women) could contribute:

My husband is the head of the family. He is the leader when it comes to decision-making. Moreover, I live in his house and the children we will have are his. So, I think he should be the one to start this conversation. If I start, he will not be happy. Even if his family members hear about it, they will say I’m controlling him or something. I don’t want that. He’s the family head, so if anything, he must lead the discussion (Participants #9).

Another woman shared similar sentiment:

For me, how do I start conversation about family planning. My man should be the one to start. He is the head so if anything, he makes the final decisions. If he thinks contraceptive use is good for us, I’ll support it and use it. But if he doesn’t say anything, then I think he might not like or want me to use it so I will also be quiet about it (Participants #12).

According to several narratives, gender norms and societal expectations required men as household heads to lead conversations on such important matters as how many children to have and when to have them. This also included whether contraception should be considered to help with family planning as illustrated in the narrative below:

In our society, men are the heads…they lead us, and so I expect my husband to discuss with me whether we should use contraception to delay pregnancy. But as I have said, he doesn’t do that…and I know most men don’t do it. But if I start, it can bring problem because he might say I want to make decisions for him or that I want to cheat. So, that is my main problem (Participant #11).

Indeed, several women noted that traditionally, men are the heads of households and decision-makers. Therefore, they expected their male partners to decide on contraceptive use, the number of children they should have and spacing.

Fear of stigmatisation

Apart from traditional gender role expectations, many women also reported that they have not discussed contraception and contraceptive use with their partner for fear of being stigmatised as promiscuous.

I think the main reason I don’t want to start that conversation [referring to discussion on contraceptive use] is how my partner will see me. You know, I’m worried that he will think I want to sleep with other men and so contraceptives will help me not to get pregnant. This is why I can’t start the conversation. I really wish we could discuss these issues especially now that we’re going to be family. But I really worry about how he’ll perceive me (Participant #11).

Another woman said:

You want me to initiate…hmmm…I don’t know how my man will see me. He may think I’m a bad person…like I want to cheat on him with another man so that I will not get pregnant. This is the main reason I have not initiated this conversation (Participant #3).

One participant also shared:

The issue is stigma. Even if my husband does not say anything bad, if his family members hear that I’m using contraception, they will not be happy. They will say all manner of bad things like I’m not faithful or I don’t love my husband that’s why I’m using contraception. So, many of us…my friends …we all have the same fear. We don’t want to be stigmatised (Participant #13).

According to this account, society sometimes stigmatises women who use contraception. This stigma arises from misguided beliefs that contraceptive use aids women to cheat and not get pregnant:

Beyond my man [referring to her husband], other members of my community will even think that I want to cheat on my husband …because contraception helps to prevent pregnancy. You know, people think that if you’re married and you are using this thing [referring to contraceptives], then you are not a good woman…like you’re sleeping with other men and because you don’t want to get pregnant and be caught, then you’re using contraceptives to hide the cheating (Participant #9)

Religious objections

Many participants also noted how religious objections to contraceptive use were an important factor that influences their lack of communication. Among both Christian and Muslim participants, it was generally reported that their religion does not support contraceptive use or that contraceptive use is accepted but viewed in a negative way by religious leaders. According to these accounts, children are often viewed as God’s/Allah’s gift, and therefore no human efforts should be made to interfere with this divine gift:

I have not spoken with my partner about contraceptives because we are Christians, and the Bible says children are a gift from God. God also commanded us to reproduce and multiply. So, why would we want to disobey God? I see contraceptives as disobeying God’s commandment. This is why we have not had any conversations on contraception since we got married (Participant #5).

One participant reported that contraceptive use was sinful, and therefore she doesn’t want to commit sin by initiating such discussions with her partner:

Islam does not support these things. Allah has commanded us to produce and fill the earth. So, it is wrong or sin to stop Allah’s command by using contraception to prevent pregnancy (Participant #6).

Some of the Christian participants also reported that while the Bible did not mention contraception and contraceptive usage, their church leaders and members often do not look favourably on women who use contraceptives:

I will say that the Bible has not said we should not use contraception. However, our church is not very receptive to this idea because some of the church leaders say it is sin and others too say it can encourage promiscuity. So, yes although my church does not openly prohibit contraceptive use, it often does not encourage it and those who use it and make it known are not viewed positively (Participant #16).

Discussion

This study investigated spousal communication about contraception and modern contraceptive use among first-time expectant mothers in urban Ghana. The study revealed that first-time expectant mothers attending ANC at the Dansoman Polyclinic lacked adequate understanding about modern contraceptives. Many participants showed limited understanding of available modern contraceptive methods, highlighting a gap in education and awareness in this demographic group. These findings are consistent with findings from other researchers who examined the barriers and facilitators to implementing a patient-centred model of contraceptive provision in community health centres in the USA.20 Similar findings have also been reported in South Africa21 and the USA.22 But our findings further showed that awareness and knowledge about contraception and modern contraceptive methods varied among participants. For instance, younger participants and those with higher education appeared to be more knowledgeable than older and less educated participants. In previous studies, lack of education and misconceptions about contraception have been identified as significant barriers to communication about and use of modern contraception.23 On the contrary, higher educational level and exposure to media have been found to improve knowledge about contraception and facilitate better communication between couples regarding contraceptive use.24 This may explain why more women with higher levels of education in this study appeared more knowledgeable about contraception and were also more likely to report having less difficulty discussing contraception and contraceptive use with their partners. This highlights a need for continuous formal and informal education for girls in and out of school.

The study also showed that friends, media and healthcare workers are the main sources of information about contraception and modern contraceptive methods. This finding is consistent with a study carried out in Uganda, which indicated that interpersonal networks play a significant role in shaping contraceptive knowledge and attitudes and that women often rely on information from their social networks, including friends, family and community members, which may perpetuate misinformation and myths about contraceptives.25 Similar research in Nepal identified interpersonal communication as a key determinant of contraceptive use, with family members and peers influencing individuals’ contraceptive decisions.26 The reliance on family and friends as primary sources of contraceptive information highlights the importance of interpersonal networks in shaping knowledge about contraception and modern contraceptive methods. However, this reliance may perpetuate misinformation and myths about contraceptives as reported in previous studies.27 28 This underscores a need for accurate and accessible channels of information dissemination. In this regard, healthcare professionals and policymakers in Ghana must prioritise using both traditional (eg, community health outreach programmes and radio discussions) and new media (eg, social media) channels to enhance awareness and knowledge about contraception and contraceptive methods among first-time mothers.

Communication is crucial for informed decision-making regarding contraception and contraceptive use. However, many participants in this study expressed a lack of communication regarding contraception and contraceptive use. This lack of communication is consistent with existing literature, which suggests that many couples in Africa27 28 and Asia29 encounter grave difficulty engaging in communication on issues related to family planning and contraceptive use. The lack of communication reported in this study is related to three main intersectional factors including traditional gender role expectations that place males as family heads and decision-makers. The study’s findings particularly highlighted the impact of gender dynamics and power structures in sexual relationships on communication about contraception. Previous research conducted in sub-Saharan Africa has shown that men are frequently perceived as the primary decision-makers in family matters, including reproductive health choices, and this power dynamic often discourages women from initiating discussions about contraception, particularly if they believe such decisions are the prerogative of their male partners.11 16 Gender dynamics and power structures within relationships have a significant impact on communication about contraceptive use because, in many societies, traditional gender roles give men more decision-making power within relationships, including women’s reproductive health and contraceptive use.11 Unequal power dynamics can limit open and honest communication between couples, particularly among women.30 Indeed, findings from our study appear to confirm that first-time expectant mothers support the idea of male superiority in various aspects of life, including contraceptive use. While this could be detrimental to women’s well-being, it is nevertheless very consistent with previous research highlighting the influence of gender dynamics on contraceptive decision-making within relationships, such as spousal communication and patriarchal norms.30 31 This highlights the persistent challenges posed by gender inequality in reproductive health contexts. Promoting open communication and addressing cultural norms, misconceptions and structural barriers is therefore crucial for couples’ communication about contraception and contraceptive use.31,33 Addressing these issues requires promoting gender-sensitive approaches to reproductive health education and services, empowering women through education and economic opportunities, engaging men as allies in promoting gender equality, implementing community-level interventions to challenge traditional gender norms and ensuring that healthcare systems provide comprehensive and gender-sensitive reproductive health services. Overall, addressing gender dynamics within relationships is essential for promoting equitable communication about contraceptive use and advancing gender equality in reproductive health. Interventions aimed at promoting couple communication about contraception must therefore address underlying gender norms, promoting mutual respect and understanding, and providing couples with the necessary skills and knowledge to engage in constructive conversations about contraception, their contraception needs as well as method choices.

Another factor that negatively affected communication about contraception and contraceptive use is the social stigma associated with contraceptive use, leading to reluctance to discuss or seek contraception openly. This finding is consistent with previous research which highlights social stigma as a barrier to communication about contraception and contraceptive use.34 35 Studies across various cultural contexts have shown that fear of negative social judgement from a spouse, the community or family members can discourage individuals from accessing contraceptive services and discussing family planning openly.36 For instance, studies in Pakistan found that fear of social stigma and negative attitudes from healthcare providers were significant barriers to contraceptive use among women.37 38 Another recent study in Ethiopia found that fear of judgement from the community or family members discouraged couples from discussing family planning openly and that the fear of social stigma associated with contraceptive use prevented some couples from discussing and adopting contraception.39 The social stigma surrounding contraception and contraceptive use in our study appears to come from deep-rooted cultural and religious beliefs that equate contraceptive use with promiscuity or moral wrongdoing. This stigma can lead to social exclusion, gossip and discrimination, creating significant barriers to contraceptive access and communication.31,33 Addressing social norms and promoting supportive, non-judgmental environments could therefore be essential for increasing understanding about contraception and promoting modern contraceptive uptake among couples. This is particularly critical in our study context where some of the participants were of the view that when people get to know about a woman who uses contraceptives, she is often singled out and discussed negatively in social gatherings.

Religious beliefs were another factor that affected couples’ communication about contraception and contraception use. Generally, participants were of the view that either their religion prohibited contraceptive use or that those who accepted it had a negative attitude from their leaders. This finding is consistent with previous findings from Ghana, which revealed that some religious communities still perceive contraception as sinful, affecting couples’ willingness to discuss this openly.40 In Ethiopia, a study by Berhane et al41 also revealed that couples who shared the same religious beliefs and had received religious endorsements for contraception were more likely to openly communicate about their contraceptive choices. That religious beliefs affected communication about contraception and contraceptive use in our study is not surprising. Many religious beliefs often emphasise life’s sanctity or the divine nature of conception and childbirth, leading some to view contraception as sin against God’s divine command.42 However, as shown in this study, interpretations of religious texts and teachings do vary, with some religious communities endorsing contraceptive use under certain circumstances. Indeed, previous studies in Ghana42 and Ethiopia41 have found that couples with shared religious beliefs and endorsements for contraception by their religious leaders are more likely to not only openly discuss contraception but also to use contraceptives. Our findings therefore highlight a need for addressing religious barriers when it comes to couples’ communication about contraception and contraceptive use. This requires a nuanced approach that respects beliefs while providing accurate information about contraceptive methods and their compatibility with religious teachings. Engaging religious leaders and community members in discussions could also promote informed decision-making and provide religiously sensitive family planning services.

The findings of this study should however be interpreted with certain limitations in mind. First, only first-time expectant mothers attending the antenatal clinic at the Dansoman Polyclinic were studied. This means that those who did not attend ANC and who may experience limited or no spousal communication regarding contraceptive use would not have been captured in our study. The results of the study may not, therefore, be applicable to all women of reproductive age. Second, there might have been sampling bias, since participants were recruited only at the Dansoman Polyclinic. This means that participants who did not attend the antenatal care clinic would have been missed. This potentially limits the applicability of our findings to other populations beyond the sample. Finally, although measures were implemented to ensure data quality including back-to-back translation, some meaning may have been lost in transcribing and translating interviews that were not conducted in English. These limitations notwithstanding, the findings in this paper provide valuable insights into the challenges of discussing contraceptive use among first-time expectant mothers in an urban suburb in Ghana.

Conclusions

This study has shown that effective communication about contraception and modern contraceptive use between partners requires addressing various barriers, including knowledge gaps, gender inequalities, inequalities in decision-making autonomy, stigma and religious beliefs. To enhance communication about contraception and contraceptive use, interventions should be comprehensive, encompassing education, targeted communication engagement and initiatives to challenge gender norms and stigmas. These interventions are crucial for promoting women’s access to sexual and reproductive health and rights information and services.
