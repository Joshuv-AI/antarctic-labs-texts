Exploring the predominant factors associated with shaping the cultivable swine gut microbiota of Wuzhishan piglets under in vitro cultivation conditions
Authors: Yinfeng Chen, Xin Zhang, Xiaozhe Wang, Zhenlong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecent advances in swine gut culturomics have substantially expanded our understanding of the cultivable repertoire and revealed the compositional complexity of the porcine gut microbiota. Nevertheless, how multiple environmental and cultivation-related factors interact to shape cultured communities under controlled nutrient conditions remains poorly understood. This study evaluated the relative and interactive contributions of intestinal segment, oxygen availability, cultivation mode, and carbon-substrate identity within a standardized nutrient-rich basal medium framework. Using inocula from the ileum and colon of Wuzhishan piglets, a roughage-tolerant Chinese indigenous breed, microbial communities were cultured on modified yeast extract casitone fatty acid (YCFA) media in which the original carbon substrate was replaced with ten alternative carbohydrates representing simple and complex substrates.ResultsCulture-dependent (CD) sequencing showed that intestinal segment was the dominant factor shaping cultured communities (P < 0 0.001), followed by oxygen availability (P < 0 0.001) and donor identity (P < 0 0.001), whereas, when evaluated within a nutrient-rich basal medium, carbon-substrate identity did not function as an independent or strongly additive driver of community differentiation (P > 0 0.05). A total of 1,308 isolates belonging to 67 genera were obtained through an empirical streaking strategy, including several rare taxa such as Culturomica that were below sequencing detection thresholds, revealing a disparity between CD sequencing profiles and actual strain isolation. Integration of community composition with short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) profiles further indicated that metabolic accumulation represents an additional ecological dimension influencing cultured community differentiation.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that under multi-factor cultivation frameworks, ecological filters do not operate in a freely additive manner. Instead, intestinal origin, oxygen exposure, and metabolic context collectively constrain culturability, while carbon-substrate variation alone does not independently drive community clustering within nutrient-rich conditions. Together, this study provides a refined conceptual framework for interpreting culture-dependent outcomes and for designing multi-factor cultivation strategies in future swine gut culturomics research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04955-3.

Introduction

The swine gastrointestinal tract hosts a diverse and metabolically active microbial community that contributes fundamentally to nutrition utilization [1, 2], intestinal barrier integrity [3, 4], and host immune regulation [5]. Over the past decade, research on the porcine gut microbiome has progressed from descriptive surveys to mechanistic investigations that elucidate causal relationships between gut microorganisms, their metabolites, and host performance traits [6–9]. This shift has made isolate-oriented culture-based approaches increasingly indispensable. Although culture independent (CI) sequencing provides broad taxonomic and functional resolution, the verification of microbial functions ultimately depends on cultivating and characterizing representative strains.

Culturomics, which integrates diversified cultivation conditions with MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry and 16S rRNA gene sequencing [10], has markedly expanded the known repertoire of human gut bacteria and uncovered substantial functional diversity [11]. In contrast, before the widespread adoption of high-throughput sequencing, studies of the porcine gut microbiota primarily relied on selective or enrichment-based cultivation, focusing on probiotic isolation and phylogenetic characterization. Early work by Ziemer et al. [12], Levine et al. [13], and Li et al. [14] employed modified YCFA, multi-medium systems, and anaerobic enrichment to isolate diverse anaerobes and butyrate-producing bacteria from porcine feces and mucosa. A major advancement occurred in 2020, when Wylensek et al. [15] used 26 media formulations to isolate 110 bacterial species spanning 40 families and nine phyla, creating PiBAC (Pig intestinal bacterial collection), the first comprehensive culture collection of porcine gut isolates. Subsequent studies further expanded cultured diversity through genome-informed profiling, high-throughput isolation pipelines, and broader medium repertoires [16–19], collectively offering an increasingly detailed view of the swine culturable landscape.

The mammalian gastrointestinal tract exhibits steep oxygen gradients along its length, whereby the proximal intestine is relatively oxygenated and the distal colonic lumen is essentially anoxic, a pattern that fundamentally shapes microbial biogeography in vivo [20]. Nutrient composition is a central determinant of bacterial growth under in vitro culture conditions. Earlier work showed that gut bacteria exhibit distinct nutritional preferences toward complex and compositionally undefined media [21]. In vivo, dietary carbohydrates supply both the primary energy source for pigs and the dominant substrates for hindgut fermentation [22]. Carbohydrates, particularly dietary fibers, are now recognized as key drivers of microbial community structure and metabolic output [23]. Fermentation of dietary fibers by the gut microbiota generates short chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as acetate, propionate, and butyrate, which can be absorbed by the host as energy sources [24, 25] and exert broad effects on host physiology [26, 27]. However, the relative and interactive contribution of carbon source to community assembly in culturomic systems remains insufficiently understood and is often confounded by variables such as intestinal segment, oxygen availability, and cultivation mode. In particular, it remains unclear whether carbon-source identity functions as an independent and additive driver of cultured community differentiation when evaluated within multi-factor cultivation frameworks.

To address this question, we used Wuzhishan pigs, a Chinese indigenous breed known for its heat tolerance, disease resistance, and high roughage tolerance, as microbial donors [28]. We hypothesized that carbon-source composition would influence bacterial culturability and that the gut microbiota of Wuzhishan pigs, shaped by adaptation to roughage-based feeding, might exhibit responsiveness to different carbon substrates in vitro. We further evaluated its contribution within a multi-factor cultivation framework that included intestinal segment, oxygen availability, and cultivation mode. To test this hypothesis, we employed a modified YCFA medium in which the original carbon source was replaced with ten alternative carbohydrates and systematically evaluated the combined effects of intestinal segment, oxygen availability, and cultivation mode on cultured community composition. Empirical streaking was used to obtain isolates, and by integrating liquid enrichment, plate cultivation, and metabolic profiling, we examined how multiple interacting factors influenced bacterial recovery and SCFA production.

Overall, this study aimed to comparatively evaluate how predefined ecological variables shape the composition and recoverability of cultured porcine gut communities under a standardized cultivation framework. By integrating culture-dependent sequencing, strain isolation, and metabolic analyses, we delineate how multi-factor cultivation filters shape culturability, assess whether nutrient-driven selection operates additively within nutrient-rich conditions, and provide methodological guidance for rational multi-variable culturomic design.

Materials and methods

Samples

An overview of the experimental design and sampling framework is provided in Fig. 1A. Weaned Wuzhishan piglets were purchased at 35 days of age from Xianliang Farm in Qionghai, Hainan Province, raised to 65 days of age, and three piglets (5.15 ± 0.48 kg) from a different litter were selected for the study. All piglets were fed a commercial corn-soybean meal diet without antibiotics. The nutritional composition of the diet was provided in Table S1. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of China Agricultural University under the permit number AW70210202-1-2. The piglets were euthanized via intravenous injection of sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg). Fresh contents from the ileum and colon were immediately transferred into pre-anaerobic Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS) containing 25% glycerol, at a weight-to-volume ratio of 1:10 in MGC AnaeroPack (Mitsubishi Corporation, Japan) and then transported to the laboratory at 4 °C. The mixture was vortexed and filtered through gauze under anaerobic conditions for further culture. All anaerobic conditions were cultured in an anaerobic incubator (Electrotek, the United Kindom) to prepare stool suspension.

Sample culture, fermentation and colony isolation

The modified YCFA growth medium was prepared based on previously described methods [29, 30] and contained the following components: haemin 10 mg/L, L-cysteine hydrochloride 1 g/L, tryptone 10 g/L, yeast extract 2.5 g/L, NaHCO3 4.0 g/L, K2HPO4 0.45 g/L, MgSO4·7H2O 0.09 g/L, CaCl2 0.09 g/L, KH2PO4 0.45 g/L, NaCl 0.9 g/L, biotin 10 µg/L, cobalamin 10 µg/L, p-aminobenzoic acid 30 µg/L, folic acid 50 µg/L, pyridoxal 150 µg/L, and resazurin 1 mg/L. Xylose (Xo), xylan (Xa), rhamnose (Rh), raffinose (Ra), mannose (Ma), inulin (In), arabinose (Ar), cellobiose (Ce), galactose (Ga), maltose (Mt) were the 10 substrates that were supplemented (at 5 g/L) individually into the medium. An additional 15 g/L of agar was added to the liquid medium to prepare solid plates. The medium was adjusted to pH 6.5 before autoclave sterilization. After cooling to 50 °C, filter-sterilized thiamine and riboflavin were added to the medium to achieve a final concentration of 50 µg/L each. The liquid medium was aliquoted into 15 mL centrifuge tubes with 0.22 μm filter caps. For anaerobic culturing, all these liquid medium and plates were pre-incubated under anaerobic conditions over night before use.

For culture fermentation, as previously described [30], ileal and colonic fecal suspensions were inoculated at a 1% (v/v) into liquid media supplemented with 10 different carbon substrates and incubated at 37 °C for 72 h under either aerobic or anaerobic conditions. All culture samples, including 3 pigs ×2 fragments (ileum and colon) ×10 media ×2 conditions (aerobic and anaerobic conditions), were centrifuged to collect the bacterial cells for 16 S rRNA sequencing, while the culture supernatant was retained for subsequent metabolite analysis. For plate culture, ileal and colonic fecal suspensions were serially diluted and directly plated onto agar plates containing 10 different carbon substrates and incubated at 37 °C for 72 h under either aerobic or anaerobic conditions. The same number of plates as used in fermentation were processed, with colonies scraped off from each agar plate using 1 mL of 25% glycerol-PBS transport medium, collected in tubes, and stored at -80 °C until DNA extraction. Together, liquid fermentation and plate cultivation constituted the two cultivation modes in the full factorial design (3 pigs × 2 intestinal segments × 10 carbon substrates × 2 oxygen conditions × 2 cultivation modes).

For colony picking, ileal and colonic fecal suspensions from one pig were cultured on aerobic and anaerobic plates with 10 different carbon-substrate media, respectively. Colonies with distinct morphologies (based on color, shape, and size) were picked, with an average of 10–25 colonies selected per plate. After three rounds of streak purification, the colonies were subjected to full-length 16 S rRNA gene Sanger sequencing.

16S rRNA amplicon sequencing and data analysis

Samples collected from three pigs across two intestinal segments (n = 6; culture-independent, CI) and colony mixtures collected from agar plates and tubes (n = 240; culture-dependent, CD) were used for 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. DNA was extracted using the CTAB with reagents sourced from Nobleryder, China. Sequencing was performed at Wekemo Tech Group Co., Ltd (Shenzhen China). The 16S rDNA V3-V4 gene region was amplified using 341F (5’-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3’). Amplified libraries were sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq PE250 platform (Illumina, USA) by Wekemo (Shenzhen, China). The analysis was conducted by following the tutorial of Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology2 (QIIME2) platform (https://docs.qiime2.org/2019.1/). Briefly, raw sequences were assembled in QIIME2, followed by quality control and denoising with Deblur [31] to generate the feature table of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). For each sample, ASVs less than 10 reads were removed [32]. ASVs were classified against the Greengenes reference database (13 − 8 version) with 99% similarity [33]. Any contaminating mitochondrial and chloroplast sequences were filtered using the QIIME2 feature-table plugin. The Deblur-processed feature table was rarefied to a uniform sequencing depth based on the minimum number of reads retained per sample after denoising. After rarefaction of reads across all samples, alpha and beta diversity analyses were performed. Observed features and Shannon indices were used to represent alpha diversity. Beta diversity was represented by weighted Unifrac distances, visualized by Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) using R v3.4.3. Analysis of similarity (ANOSIM) was used to evaluate the significance of beta diversity differences between groups. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was performed to evaluate the effects of these factors, including intestinal segment, carbon substrate, and oxygen availability, on the cultivable gut microbiota of pigs [19]. PERMANOVA was conducted using the adonis function in the “vegan” package [34] of R. Pairwise PERMANOVA comparisons among individual carbon substrates were performed within the aligned cultivation framework, and P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. The phylogeny pipeline in QIIME2 was used to construct a rooted phylogenetic tree visualized with Evolview. Redundancy analysis (RDA) was performed to assess associations between microbial community structure and SCFAs, treated as continuous variables. The analysis was based on a linear response model and conducted with the R package “vegan”, with the significance of explanatory variables evaluated by permutation tests. Ordination plots were visualized using “ggplot2” [35].

Colony identification, and phylogenetic tree construction

Purified colonies were subjected to PCR amplification of 16S rRNA genes using the universal primers 27F(5’-AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3’) and 1492R(5’-TACGGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3’) to obtain amplification products. The resulting PCR products were sequenced by Sanger sequencing for taxonomic identification. Sequences were aligned and annotated using BLAST (version 2.15.0+) against the NCBI RefSeq Targeted Loci Project 16 S rRNA reference database. Taxonomic annotation was extracted based on the top-hit alignment with a minimum identity cutoff of 98.7% [29]. In addition to this taxonomic identification, representative sequences from high-throughput amplicon sequencing were used to construct a local BLAST database for downstream comparison. Isolate sequences obtained by streak isolation were aligned to this database using BLAST+ version 2.15.0, with results output in tabular format (outfmt 6) and restricted to the top hit (max_target_seqs 1); all other parameters were left as default.

Sequences were analyzed in MEGA 6.0 to construct phylogenetic trees and was visualized using iTOL(Version 7).

Gas chromatography in short-chain fatty acid quantification

Six types of SCFAs, including acetic acid (C2), propionic acid (C3), butyric acid (C4), isobutyric acid (C4), valeric acid (C5), and isovaleric acid (C5), in culture supernatants were quantified using gas chromatography (GC2030; SHIMADZU, Japan) as previous described [36]. Culture supernatants were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min, and 1 ml of the supernatant was mixed with 200 µl of internal standard (25% metaphosphoric acid containing 0.6464% crotonic acid [89.06 g/mol]). After filtration through a 0.22 μm filter membrane, 1.0 µl of the mixture was injected into the GC2030. SCFAs were separated on a capillary column (HP-FFAP, 30 m × 0.25 mm ID × 0.25 μm film thickness, Agilent Technologies, USA) and detected using a flame ionization detector. Nitrogen served as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 45 mL/min. Injection was performed in split mode at 20:1, with both the injector and detector temperatures set to 250 °C. The column temperature program started at 90 °C, increased to 240 °C at a rate of 15 °C/min, and was held for 10 min (total runtime: 20 min). SCFA concentrations were quantified using response factors calculated relative to crotonic acid, based on pure standard injections. The concentration of a specific SCFA (mM) was calculated using the formula as previously described [36]. Quantification was performed using an internal standard method with crotonic acid, yielding a linear response with an R² value of 0.998. The limit of detection (LOD) was < 0.03 mmol/L and the limit of quantification (LOQ) was < 0.1 mmol/L. Instrument sensitivity was S(C/g) = 0.016, meeting the performance criterion (≥ 0.01), with a minimum detectable quantity (MDQ) of 2.37 × 10–12 gC/s. Method reproducibility was assessed based on relative standard deviations, with intra-day RSDs < 5% (n = 6) and inter-day RSDs < 10% across three independent days. The significance of differences in short-chain fatty acids was assessed using the Friedman test, a non-parametric method for analyzing paired data.

Statistical analyses

Before significance testing, data distribution was assessed for normality using GraphPad Prism 9.5. Normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. An Ordinary one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used for significance tests if conforming to a normal distribution, otherwise, a Kruskal-Wallis test followed Dunn’s multiple comparisons was used. A three-way nonparametric ANOVA was conducted using the aligned rank transform (ART) to evaluate both main effects and interactions in SCFA. GraphPad Prism 9.5 was used for significance testing and visualization mentioned above. All data were presented as the means ± SEMs. P < 0.05 was taken to indicate statistical significance. Multivariate community analyses, including β-diversity ordination, PERMANOVA, and RDA were conducted in R, as described in the corresponding methodological sections. R packages “dplyr” [37], “ggpubr” [38], “ggplot2”, “vegan”, and “reshape2” [39] were used for data processing and visualization.

Results

Summary of sequencing data and microbial diversity

A total of 12,675,612 high-quality reads, with an average of 52,378 reads per sample, were obtained from six inoculum samples (three from ileum content and three from colon content) and 236 culture-dependent samples, after excluding four CD samples (cultured using colon inoculum under aerobic conditions on media with Mt, Ar, and Xo as carbon substrates, and under anaerobic conditions on medium with In as a carbon substrate) from the original 240 CD samples due to low sequencing depth. These sequences were resolved into ASVs with single-nucleotide resolution, resulting in 1,562 ASVs classified into 12 bacterial phyla, 77 families, and 172 genera.

Consistent with previous findings [19], the CI method detected more ASVs. In the ten carbon-substrate based media, CI identified 1,038 ASVs, while CD detected 984 ASVs, with 460 ASVs common to both (Fig. 1B). Segment-specific comparisons revealed distinct patterns. In the ileum, 542 ASVs were detected in the cultured samples but only 345 in the inocula, indicating that CD in ileum recovered more ASVs than CI. In contrast, in the colon, 870 ASVs were identified in the inocula compared with 651 in the cultured samples, showing that CI in colon exceeded CD (Fig. 1B). When comparing intestinal segments, the colon harbored more ASVs than the ileum under both methods (CI: 870 vs. 345; CD: 651 vs. 542), indicating higher richness in the colon. These abundance-based differences were further supported by α-diversity indices. Both the Shannon index and Observed Features were significantly higher in the inocula (CI) than in the cultured samples (CD) for both segments (P < 0.05; Fig. S1A, B). For the inocula, the Shannon index of the colon was significantly higher than that of the ileum (P < 0.01; Fig. S1A, B). In comparison, for the cultured samples, the Shannon index of the ileum was significantly higher than that of the colon (P < 0.01; Fig. S1A, B). These results suggest that colonic inocula preserved greater richness, whereas ileal cultures achieved greater evenness under culture-dependent conditions. To further assess whether this pattern was mirrored in taxonomic representation, we next show genus-level differences across segments in CI and CD conditions and evaluated recovery rates across multiple taxonomic levels.

As illustrated in Fig. 1C, genus-level differences were observed between the ileum and colon under both CI and CD conditions. In the CI profiles, several genera were not detected in ileal samples, including Bacteroides, Parabacteroides, f_24 − 7, Prevotella, as well as Christensenellaceae-related taxa, all of which were consistently detected in the colonic inocula. Conversely, Bifidobacterium, as well as some Clostridium taxa, were not detected in colonic CI samples. Importantly, several genera characteristic of the colonic inocula showed limited recovery under culture-dependent conditions. For example, f_24 − 7 and Prevotella were recovered with markedly reduced genus-level diversity in colonic cultures, while Treponema and Akkermansia were not recovered under any cultivation media. In contrast, a set of genera, including Lactobacillus, Streptococcus, Enterococcus, Staphylococcus, Paenibacillus, and Bacillus, were consistently recovered from both intestinal segments, indicating broad culturability across segments and cultivation conditions. Consistent with these observations, genus-level Venn analysis (Fig. S1C) showed that although the ileum and colon shared a substantial fraction of cultivable genera under CD conditions, each segment retained a distinct subset.

The taxa recovered and the recovery rates across different taxonomic levels in the ileum and colon under CD and CI conditions are presented in Table 1. At the phylum level, the ileum achieved a recovery rate of 83.33%, with CD recovering five out of six phyla, except for Verrucomicrobiota, and additionally detecting Spirochaetes, which was absent in CI. By contrast, the colon exhibited a lower recovery rate of only 50%, with six phyla- Actinomycetota, Bacteroidota, Bacillota (formerly Firmicutes), Pseudomonadota, Fusobacteriota, and Spirochaetota being recovered, whereas several non-dominant phyla, including TM7, Verrucomicrobiota, Elusimicrobiota, Deferribacterota, Mycoplasmatota, and Cyanobacteria, were not successfully cultivated. Consistently, the ileum showed higher recovery rates than the colon across all taxonomic levels except for the genus level, with rates exceeding 75%. At the genus level, however, the colon exhibited a higher recovery rate, which was consistent with the ASV-based richness pattern.

Across donors, donor P1 exhibited nearly twofold higher number of ASVs in both the inoculum and cultured samples compared to donors P2 and P3 (Fig. S1D). When ASVs with relative abundances below 0.1% were excluded, recovery rates in the ileum and colon varied by more than twofold among the three individuals (ileum: 45.45%-100%; colon: 30.28%-73.33%) (Table S2). Even with a stricter threshold of 1%, substantial inter-individual variation remained in the colon (ileum: 90.91%-100%; colon: 53.33%-91.67%) (Table S2), indicating a substantial inter-individual variation in cultivable ASV counts.

β-diversity analysis based on weighted UniFrac distances revealed a significant separation between colonic and ileal cultures (P < 0.001) (Fig. S1E). While the ileal inoculum and cultured samples were not significantly different (P = 0.067), a significant separation was observed between the colonic inoculum and cultured samples (P < 0.001). This pattern likely reflects a stronger selective filtering effect of the applied cultivation conditions on colonic microbiota. In terms of community composition, substantial shifts in dominant taxa were observed in both the ileum and colon after cultivation (Fig. S1F). Culturing increased the relative abundance of Enterococcus while reducing the proportion of Lactobacillus in both colon and ileum. The limitations of the culture medium resulted in a decrease in the abundance of dominant taxa such as Unspecified_Clostridiaceae (18%) and Unspecified_Clostridiales (20%) in the ileal inoculum, with their proportions decreasing to below 1% in the cultured samples. Similarly, Unspecified_Lachnospiraceae, which accounted for 8% of the colonic inoculum, had an average abundance of only 0.1% in the cultured samples. Furthermore, more than 50% of the low-abundance taxa in colonic inoculum were not recovered and were classified as “other” (Table S3).

Factors affecting culturability of swine gut microbiota

Next, PERMANOVA analysis (Table S4) revealed that intestinal segment was the strongest determinant of the cultured community structure (F = 333.37; R² = 0.53; P < 0.001), followed by oxygen availability (F = 23.00; R² = 0.04; P < 0.001) and inter-individual variation (F = 15.20; R² = 0.05; P < 0.001), whereas medium type exerted only a marginal effect (F = 1.45; R² = 0.02; P = 0.117). Additional PERMANOVA analyses designed to isolate the contribution of carbon source under aligned cultivation conditions indicated that the overall effect of carbohydrate type remained statistically marginal (PERMANOVA, P = 0.062; global comparison across carbon-source groups, P = 0.961; chain-length grouping, P = 0.111; monosaccharides versus microbiota-accessible polysaccharides, P = 0.802). Pairwise PERMANOVA comparisons among individual carbon substrates, conducted within the aligned cultivation framework, likewise did not reveal statistically significant differences after multiple-testing correction (Table S5). This ranking highlighted that intestinal segment and oxygen exposure overwhelmingly shaped the recovery of the culturable repertoire. Consistently, α-diversity of cultured samples (Fig. 2A) showed that richness (Observed Features) was significantly higher under anaerobic than aerobic incubation for both ileal samples (P < 0.001) and colonic samples (P < 0.05). For evenness, only the Shannon index of colonic cultured samples increased under anaerobic conditions (P < 0.0001). Under aerobic conditions, ileal cultured samples exhibited higher Observed Features (P < 0.05) and Shannon diversity (P < 0.0001) than colonic cultured samples, whereas no segmental differences in α-diversity were observed under anaerobic conditions. These findings indicate that intestinal segment and oxygen sensitivity shaped the selective enrichment of taxa under culture-dependent conditions.

The β-diversity analysis, based on weighted UniFrac distances, revealed statistically significant differences in all pairwise comparisons among the four groups (Co_A, Co_N, Ile_A, Ile_N) (Fig. 2B) (P < 0.001), confirming that both intestinal segment and oxygen availability shaped community composition. At the genus level (Fig. 2C), colonic cultured samples were dominated by unclassified Enterobacteriaceae, which accounted for approximately 86.6% under aerobic conditions and 69.0% under anaerobic conditions, whereas this lineage remained a minor component in ileal samples at about 14.3% and 9.4%, respectively. In contrast, Enterococcus consistently prevailed in ileal cultures, ranging from 27.6% under aerobic to 32.3% under anaerobic conditions, but remained scarce in the colon at about 3.2% in both treatments. In colonic cultures, Lactobacillus increased from 0.8% under aerobic to 8.0% under anaerobic conditions, and Megasphaera rose from 0.2% to 6.2% with both genera persisted at low levels in the ileum. Conversely, the ileum shifted from a Bacillus-rich profile under aerobic conditions (27.8%) toward marked enrichment of Clostridium (37.0%) and unclassified Peptostreptococcaceae (5.9%) under anaerobic conditions, taxa that remained nearly absent in the colon. These results demonstrate that oxygen availability imposed distinct restructuring in different intestinal segments, favoring Lactobacillus and Megasphaera in the colon while promoting Clostridium and unclassified Peptostreptococcaceae in the ileum, with Enterococcus maintaining stable dominance in ileal cultures.

Based on comparisons between cultured communities and inoculum communities, we further evaluated the cultivable genus-level variations across different individuals, oxygen conditions, and intestinal segments, with a focus on the cultured products. As illustrated, the result indicated the selective enrichment achieved using media supplemented with various carbon substrates under various individual-, oxygen-, and gut segment-specific conditions, as well as the amplification of taxa undetectable via culture-independent methods (Fig. 3A and B). Certain genera displayed condition-associated distributions across culture environments differing in medium type and oxygen availability. Notably, Bacillus reached up to 80% on aerobic plates from initially undetectable or low-abundance states but achieved at most 1% in liquid media, indicating preferential recovery under aerobic solid conditions (Fig. 3A and B). As another example, Clostridium exhibited complementary dynamics. Clostridium was broadly retained or enriched in liquid media, whereas on plates it persisted or expanded only under anaerobic conditions from ileal inocula (Fig. 3A). 4

Some genera were only detected under solid- or liquid-based culture conditions when provided with specific carbon substrates. Peptostreptococcaceae_Clostridium from donor P1 was enriched across six anaerobic plates but was absent from all anaerobic liquids (Fig. 3A and B). Conversely, Turicibacter from P3 ileal inocula did not grow on aerobic plates but persisted in seven aerobic liquids, whereas Lactobacillus from the same donor was not recovered in anaerobic liquids yet was enriched or maintained on eight anaerobic plates (Fig. 3A and B, Fig. S2). These observations are likely associated with limitations arising from metabolite accumulation and syntrophic interactions in liquid cultures.

Actinobacillus from the colon of donor P1 was enriched on all ten plates but in liquid media showed enrichment only on Ar, Ga, and Xo, while being merely maintained on Ma and Rh (Fig. 3A). Aerococcus from the ileum of donors P1 and P3 was enriched on all ten plates, yet in liquids it grew only under aerobic conditions across nine substrates, excluding Ga (Fig. 3A). Bacteroides showed a preference for Xa plates and in liquids was enriched in Rh, Xa, and Xo under anaerobic conditions and in Ar under aerobic conditions (Fig. 3A). These results illustrate substrate-specific responses that were further modulated by oxygen availability and the intestinal segment of origin.

Some genera that were present at low abundance or were only sporadically detectable in the inocula showed increased representation under selective culture conditions, reaching readily detectable abundances. Bifidobacterium, initially detectable only in P2 ileal inocula at 0.04%, expanded to 12% on anaerobic Xa plates and became detectable in multiple liquid and plate media, while Facklamia from P1 ileum and Megasphaera from P1 colon similarly expanded across both liquid and solid systems. Cellulosimicrobium, undetectable or at trace levels in inocula, persisted only on aerobic In and Rh plates, reaching 2% (Fig. 3A and B, Fig. S2). These examples highlight that genera with marginal or sporadic detection in the inocula could be selectively recovered and enriched under specific carbon-substrate and incubation conditions.

Several genera that were initially undetectable in the inocula became detectable under culture-dependent conditions. Acidaminococcus, Alkaliphilus, Fusobacterium, Lysinibacillus, Paenibacillus, Solibacillus, and Tepidibacter expanded on specific carbon-substrate plates, with some also reaching up to 10% in liquid media, such as Lysinibacillus in aerobic Rh and Oscillospira in anaerobic Ma (Fig. 3b, Fig. S2). Similarly, Veillonella, Aggregatibacter, and Klebsiella, absent from P1 colonic inocula, were recovered on multiple anaerobic plates, with Veillonella reaching 24% on In and Aggregatibacter 10% on Rh, while Klebsiella expanded broadly across both plates and liquids (Fig. S2). In contrast, other taxa present at trace levels, including SMB53 and Ruminococcus, were resuscitated only to low abundances under restricted conditions (Fig. 3b, Fig. S2). Together, these results demonstrate that culture-dependent approaches substantially extend the detectable range by enabling both the recovery of previously undetectable taxa and the amplification of rare lineages to measurable levels.

Metabolic preferences and selective enrichment of representative ASVs from dominant genera across specific carbon substrates

Although carbon source as a global factor did not exert a significant effect on culturability, several genera and ASVs displayed clear substrate-specific preferences. At the ASV level, distinct substrate-specific preferences were observed (Fig. 4), with representative ASVs annotated to particular genera showing clear enrichment patterns. Certain carbon sources consistently drove targeted enrichment, such as Ce for Lactobacillus, Rh for Lysinibacillus, Caloramator, and Escherichia, and In for Ruminococcus, Streptococcus, and Tepidibacter. Other substrates exhibited narrower selectivity, with Ra favoring Alkaliphilus and Facklamia, Xo promoting Actinobacteria-associated ASVs such as Collinsella and Olsenella, and Xa supporting a broader range of genera including Bifidobacterium, Blautia, Weissella, Desulfovibrio, and Klebsiella. Ar was associated with Parabacteroides, Ga with Anaerovorax, and Mt with Dorea. In contrast, Ma did not yield consistent enrichment of any representative ASVs. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that different carbon substrates imposed fine-grained selectivity, enabling differential amplification of specific lineages.

Isolation and identification of cultivable strains

After identifying the factors shaping culturability, we evaluated the concordance between plate-derived sequencing features and isolates obtained by colony picking. From donor P1 (ileum and colon; ten carbon-substrate plates; aerobic and anaerobic), 1,308 colonies were purified and identified by Sanger sequencing, spanning 4 phyla, 67 genera, and 150 species (Table 2). Local BLAST against representative amplicon features revealed high overall concordance, with 1,069 of 1,308 isolates showing 100% identity. Of the perfectly matched isolates, 512 originated from the ileum and corresponded to 52 ASVs annotated to 20 genera across three phyla, while 557 originated from the colon and corresponded to 58 ASVs annotated to 26 genera across four phyla.

Associations between SCFA production and microbial community structure

Three-factor analysis indicated that oxygen and intestinal segment were the primary determinants of SCFA production, with all acids except isovalerate showing significant effects (Table S6). Additional influences were observed for specific acids, such as carbon-substrate effects on acetate and extensive multi-factor interactions influencing isobutyrate. Therefore, pairwise comparisons were performed within each carbon-substrate treatment to specifically evaluate the effects of intestinal segment and oxygen status (Fig. 7A). Acetate levels were higher in Ile_N and Co_N than in Co_A, and Ile_N also exceeded Ile_A, indicating that anaerobic conditions, particularly in the ileum, promoted acetate accumulation. Butyrate was elevated in both Co_N and Ile_N relative to Co_A, reflecting a general enhancement of butyrate production under anaerobic conditions. In addition, isobutyrate decreased in Ile_N compared with both Co_A and Co_N. Similarly, propionate was lower in Ile_N than in Co_A, Ile_A, and Co_N. By comparison, no significant differences were detected for isovalerate and valerate across groups (Table S6).

To further explore the microbial responses underlying differences in SCFA metabolism, redundancy analysis (RDA) was performed at the genus level. RDA indicated that SCFA gradients explained a large proportion of community variation (RDA1: 56.68%, RDA2: 13.3%) (Fig. 7B). Among these factors, Megasphaera was positively associated with valerate, whereas Bacteroides and Blautia correlated with butyrate. In contrast, Alkaliphilus, Lachnospiraceae_Clostridium, Peptostreptococcaceae_Clostridium, and Bifidobacterium were more closely related to acetate. Streptococcus and Klebsiella were more closely related to propionate. These associations suggest that specific genera exhibited clear ecological selectivity under defined SCFA gradients. At the sample level (Fig. 7C), Co_N samples separated distinctly, driven by elevated butyrate, valerate, and isovalerate, whereas Ile_N samples were aligned more closely with acetate. The remaining groups clustered near the ordination origin, indicating comparatively weaker responses to SCFA variation under the tested cultivation conditions.

Overall, paired analyses across carbon-substrate treatments demonstrated that SCFA production varied significantly with intestinal segment and oxygen status, and these metabolic differences were accompanied by corresponding shifts in microbial community structure.

Discussion

Culturomic studies have considerably expanded the catalog of cultivable swine gut bacteria and revealed substantial taxonomic and functional diversity. However, most previous efforts have primarily emphasized genomic characterization and functional profiling, with far less attention given to isolate-oriented comparative evaluations [15–17]. As a result, relatively few studies have systematically examined how environmental and cultivation-related factors influence the recovery and composition of cultured microbial communities. By jointly evaluating intestinal segment, oxygen availability, cultivation mode, and carbon-source supplementation, our study shows that oxygen supply and intestinal origin have a much greater impact on the composition of cultivable communities than the type of carbohydrate provided. Although variation in carbon substrates has been reported to influence gut microbial community structure [40, 41], its effects were not readily separable from the dominant constraints imposed by oxygen exposure and intestinal origin under the tested conditions. Specifically, these effects are not freely combinatorial under the tested conditions, in that nutrient-driven selection does not act in a strongly additive manner. Instead, variation in carbon substrates often reinforces the dominance of highly competitive taxa rather than broadening overall community diversity. Collectively, these results clarify how multiple ecological factors interact to shape cultured communities and underscore the discrepancy between sequencing-based detection and actual strain recovery.

Among the factors examined, intestinal segment showed particularly clear differences in recovery patterns. In the colon, more than half of all ASVs were recovered across the carbon-substrate media and cultivation conditions tested, and the proportion of recovered taxa increased with taxonomic rank, consistent with patterns reported in swine fecal culturomics studies [19]. In contrast, ileal samples exhibited a marked decline in recovery from the order to the genus level, indicating that higher-level taxonomic structure remained broadly representative whereas finer-scale diversity was dominated by only a few genera. The ileum, characterized by higher bile salt concentrations [42] and faster digesta flow [43], likely presents a more selective environment that limits the recovery of condition-sensitive taxa. In contrast, the colon contains more complex substrates and has a longer retention time [43], conditions that better match the nutrient-rich cultivation system used here and therefore support broader recovery at the genus level. These segment-specific patterns underscore the importance of ecological niche specialization and the compatibility between in vivo environments and in vitro cultivation conditions. Consistent with these observations, PERMANOVA identified intestinal origin as the primary determinant of cultivable diversity.

Cultivation also reshaped community structure in ways that revealed both generalist taxa and those highly responsive to specific cultivation conditions. Enterococcus and Streptococcus consistently reached high relative abundance across donors, intestinal segments, and cultivation environments, showing broad enrichment patterns similar to earlier findings [19]. In contrast, several other genera displayed enrichment patterns that shifted according to the interplay between oxygen availability and cultivation mode. Bacillus provides a representative example, showing strong enrichment on aerobic plates but remaining at low abundance or sporadically detectable in liquid cultures. These patterns suggest that, beyond oxygen exposure alone, physicochemical features of the cultivation environment, including metabolite accumulation and potential microbial interactions, contribute to differential enrichment outcomes. Consistent with this interpretation, previous studies have shown that Bifidobacterium, Acidaminococcus, and Clostridium are oxygen-sensitive, while Bacillus is preferentially enriched under aerobic conditions [18, 19, 44].

Beyond compositional shifts, cultivation mode also translated into distinct metabolic outputs. Under anaerobic liquid cultivation, when intestinal segment was considered within a controlled substrate background anaerobic ileal and colonic liquid cultures displayed distinct SFCA profiles: with ileal cultures accumulating higher acetate concentrations and colonic cultures producing more butyrate. RDA showed that anaerobic ileal samples clustered along the acetate vector, with Bifidobacterium and Alkaliphilus, both primary carbohydrate fermenters producing acetate [45, 46], closely associated with acetate production. Megasphaera, a secondary fermenter that converts lactate to valerate, was also positioned in accordance with its known metabolic role. In contrast, taxa associated with the propionate and butyrate gradients differed from well-established fermenters reported previously. Bacteroides and Blautia are known to release acetate or propionate as major intermediates [47, 48], whereas Streptococcus primarily ferments simple carbohydrates to produce lactate and acetate [49], which can subsequently serve as substrates for secondary fermenters. Ordination analyses indicated that community structure seems to reflect adaptive responses to SCFA accumulation rather than direct metabolic output. In vitro fermentation systems lack the continuous SCFA absorption and pH buffering found in vivo, allowing acids to accumulate and selectively suppress acid sensitive taxa [50]. Consistent with this interpretation, cultivation condition was identified as a significant determinant of community structure, with plate and liquid cultures creating distinct spatial and metabolic contexts that may account for the differential appearance of certain taxa under identical carbon-substrate conditions [51]. In addition to intestinal segment, donor-specific variation exerted a notable influence on the efficacy of cultivation. Although the overall mean recovery rate in our study did not reach the levels previously reported in swine culturomics work (87% and 99%) [19], certain individual samples achieved comparably high recovery, underscoring the contribution of donor-dependent factors to culturability. Prior research has shown that host specific microbial community structure is a major determinant of fermentation profiles and microbial response patterns [52]. Similarly, Park et al. [53] reported that expanding the number of donors, rather than increasing colony-picking depth, more effectively enhanced the overall diversity of cultured isolates in human gut studies. Although donor number was not experimentally manipulated here, the pronounced differences in recovery among individuals indicate that intrinsic donor characteristics play a considerable role in determining the cultivable fraction of the swine gut microbiota.

Previous studies have extensively demonstrated that the gut microbiota of weaned piglets differs markedly in its capacity for carbohydrate metabolism across intestinal segments [54, 55]. Following weaning, these functional differences reflect the nutrient gradient and substrate availability along the gastrointestinal tract, giving rise to distinct metabolic specializations between intestinal regions. The ileum is dominated by bacteria that utilize mono- and disaccharides, whereas the colon harbors fermenters of complex polysaccharides [55, 56]. Continuous in vitro fermentation experiments further indicate that both monosaccharides [57] and complex polysaccharides [58] can substantially reshape microbial composition in the porcine hindgut, suggesting that substrate composition and complexity play important roles in microbial assembly. Based on this evidence, we originally hypothesized that, within a single basal medium, altering the type of carbohydrate supplied would act as a primary driver of community differentiation, enabling the selective enrichment of taxa with distinct substrate preferences. In other words, we expected that supplementing an otherwise identical medium with different carbohydrates would generate discrete clusters of culturable communities and preferentially recover strains exhibiting substrate-specific growth advantages. Accordingly, we replaced the original carbon source in the modified YCFA medium with ten physiologically relevant carbohydrates: five simple sugars (Ga, Ma, Ar, Xo, Mt) and five complex microbiota-accessible polysaccharides (In, Xa, Ra, Ce, Rh), to test whether carbohydrate identity functions as a major determinant of community clustering under controlled ecological conditions.

However, contrary to our expectations, β-diversity analyses showed that variation in carbon source did not significantly alter the composition of culturable communities. When the rich nutritional background was held constant, variable effects were not freely combinatorial; nutrient-driven selection did not act in a strongly additive manner when multiple environmental filters were simultaneously present. By conducting all comparisons within a single basal medium, our study offers a controlled-variable evaluation of carbohydrate effects in culturable gut communities, revealing that, when other cultivation parameters were aligned, the contribution of carbohydrate identity to community structure was comparatively limited. This outcome can be explained by the formulation of the modified YCFA medium, which contains nonspecific substrates, amino acids, vitamins, and minerals that collectively create a nutritionally rich environment supporting broad microbial growth. Such conditions reduce the selective pressure imposed by individual carbohydrates [59]. Therefore, under nutrient-rich cultivation conditions, carbohydrate identity did not function as an independent or strongly additive driver of community structure. Previous culturomics studies have shown that no single medium can capture the entire culturable microbiota [18] and that differences in basal formulation can markedly alter community structure and metabolic output [60]. Many gut bacteria also possess broad substrate utilization capacities, enabling growth on multiple carbon sources, which contributes to substantial overlap across treatments. Thus, in a system influenced by a nutrient-rich basal background provided by the modified YCFA medium and shaped by multiple interacting factors, including intestinal segment, oxygen availability, cultivation environment, and inter-individual variation, the effect of carbon source appeared subordinate to these stronger ecological filters. These combined effects explain the absence of significant clustering by carbohydrate type in β-diversity analyses.

Although the set of media employed here was limited, our culture-dependent approach nonetheless broadened the recovery of taxa beyond those detected by sequencing-based surveys, illustrating that empirical isolation can complement community-level sequencing by capturing rare or low-abundance taxa. A previous study [19] have classified taxa with culturability above 10% as easily culturable, between 1% and 10% as moderately culturable, and below 1% as difficult to culture, and used these thresholds to evaluate cultivation feasibility. If the aim is to isolate strains, our findings suggest that taxa with relative abundances below 0.1% can still be readily and repeatedly isolated through non-random colony picking, such as Feature 29. For example, Feature 75 (Bifidobacterium) formed exceptionally small colonies and were recovered despite being undetectable in culture dependent sequencing profiles Empirical colony picking has shown to markedly increases isolate diversity compared with random selection [61], and facilitated the recovery of previously undetectable taxa by preferentially selecting morphologically distinct or exceptionally small colonies.

Among isolates that did not show 100% identity to the culture-dependent 16 S features, most corresponded to extremely low-abundance taxa, or minimal sequencing representation. One notable example involved an isolate classified as Culturomica, which was repeatedly recovered despite being absent from both inoculum and cultured sequencing profiles. This discrepancy was attributable to differences in taxonomic assignment across databases and the exclusion of low abundance ASVs during sequence filtering [19, 32]. Such observations underscore that taxa undetectable by sequencing may still be isolated through non-random colony selection, underscoring the ability of empirical cultivation to reveal microbial diversity that remains hidden in sequencing-based surveys.

Several methodological considerations should be noted when interpreting these findings. The relatively small number of donor piglets, together with isolate recovery based on colony picking from a single donor, may limit statistical power and constrain the extent to which inter individual variability is captured. In addition, although culture dependent 16 S rRNA amplicon sequencing enables systematic comparisons across cultivation conditions, its limited taxonomic resolution restricts functional inference at the strain level. Finally, the relative contributions of viable DNA and relic DNA to the CD and CI datasets could not be explicitly distinguished in this study. Future studies incorporating expanded donor sampling, shotgun metagenomics, genome resolved approaches, and strategies informed by bacterial viability are expected to improve strain level interpretation and enhance biological generalizability.

Conclusion

This study provides an integrated assessment of how intestinal segment, oxygen availability, cultivation mode, and carbon-substrate supplementation influence the cultivable fraction of the swine gut microbiota. Intestinal origin and oxygen condition emerged as the strongest determinants of community composition, whereas carbohydrate type had only a limited impact when evaluated within a single nutrient-rich basal medium. Importantly, our results indicate that the effects of carbon substrates are not readily separable from other dominant cultivation constraints under the tested conditions, and that nutrient-driven selection does not act in a strongly additive manner, often reinforcing the dominance of highly competitive taxa rather than broadening overall community diversity. Together, these findings refine current understanding of the ecological and methodological factors that shape culturability in the swine gut and provide useful guidance for improving cultivation strategies in future culturomic research.
