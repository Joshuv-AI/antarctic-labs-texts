Evidence-to-Recommendation Framework for 2026 ACE Clinical Guideline on allergic rhinitis — diagnosis and management
Authors: Samuel Li Earn Goh, Jing Xi Yang, Shu Jun Kan, Expert Group for Allergic Rhinitis ACG, Valentina Ricci, Phyllis AL Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This Evidence-to-Recommendation (EtR) framework underpins the ACE Clinical Guideline (ACG) for the diagnosis and management of allergic rhinitis (AR), providing justifications for its recommendations. Allergic rhinitis is highly prevalent in Singapore, with most cases being moderate to severe. While AR is not associated with severe outcomes, poorly controlled or untreated AR can lead to complications, such as asthma, sinusitis or otitis media with effusion. The ACG covers the diagnosis and management of perennial AR, which is the predominant local form of AR and is largely attributed to house dust mites. The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation framework was followed to develop four evidence-based recommendations that cover diagnosis, role of allergy testing, and management through pharmacological interventions and allergen avoidance. This EtR framework summarises factors that have informed the direction and strength of the ACG recommendations, including balance of benefits and risks, certainty of evidence, patient preferences and values, resources and feasibility considerations, and acceptability. The ACG can be found at https://go.gov.sg/acg-ar.

INTRODUCTION

Allergic rhinitis (AR) was proposed in the 2023 Agency for Care Effectiveness (ACE) Clinical Guidelines (ACG) topic call. The prevalence of AR is estimated to be more than 30% in Singapore,[12] with most cases observed in the paediatric and young adult populations.[3] It is commonly perceived as a benign and self-limiting condition, leading to symptom normalisation, reliance on intermittent self-treatment and delays in seeking care.[4] Although AR is usually not associated with severe outcomes, symptoms can negatively impact quality of life (QoL), e.g. through sleep disruption.[1] Moreover, poorly controlled or untreated AR can lead to complications such as asthma, sinusitis or otitis media with effusion.[5] A recent local study also found that approximately 85% of AR cases are undiagnosed and 73% untreated, with most having moderate-to-severe disease.[1] These findings highlight a hidden disease burden among individuals with AR despite the availability of effective therapies. Furthermore, the last national guideline on AR was published in 2010 (now withdrawn), indicating a need to provide updated, national-level recommendations on the diagnosis and management of AR.

The healthcare burden for AR in Singapore has risen substantially, with AR-related polyclinic visits increasing from 1220 to 1643 per 100,000 residents between 2012 and 2022, costing SGD 13.9 million in 2022 (Ministry of Health National Healthcare Utilisation Data [unpublished]). Interviews with domain experts, an in-house primary-care survey (n = 92), and a national analysis of prescribing data revealed challenges that clinicians face in providing adequate allergen-avoidance guidance and ensuring treatment adherence, leading to suboptimal symptom control and unnecessary Ear, Nose and Throat (ENT) referrals. Of the surveyed clinicians, 65% identified poor patient adherence and incorrect intranasal technique as primary treatment challenges and highlighted concerns over unnecessary allergen testing.

METHODS

This guideline was developed based on the ACE methods and processes for ACG development and following a hybrid de novo/adaptation process.[6] A systematic search for international guidelines was conducted across databases, including Guideline Central, Guidelines International Network Library, ECRI Guidelines Trust, PubMed, Epistemonikos and Trip Medical Database, supplemented by manual retrieval from relevant speciality associations. English-language guidelines (n = 8) published within the previous 5 years were prioritised and assessed using the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II tool, specifically Domain 3 (Rigour of Development), applying a minimum 60% scaled domain score threshold. Overall, five high-quality guidelines were selected as the main reference guidelines. Recent systematic reviews (SRs) and meta-analyses (MAs) were identified from PubMed and Google Scholar to complement guideline evidence.

A multidisciplinary expert group (EG) comprising four ENT specialists, two rheumatologists, six family medicine clinicians, three paediatricians and two pharmacists was appointed to provide clinical expertise throughout guideline development. Draft recommendations were formulated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation Evidence-to-Recommendation (EtR) framework, incorporating five key domains: balance of benefits and harms, certainty of evidence, values and preferences, resources and feasibility, and acceptability and other considerations. The EG individually rated the appropriateness of the recommendation using the RAND/UCLA Appropriateness Method, followed by structured discussions to achieve consensus.

RECOMMENDATION 1

Diagnose AR based on the patient’s history, particularly their symptoms, combined with a physical examination. (Strong recommendation)

Summary

A strong recommendation for a clinical approach to diagnosing AR is proposed, centred on detailed history-taking and physical examination, without using any specific diagnostic test. This approach is consistent with guideline consensus and current practice, with no concerns regarding resources or feasibility.

Evidence-to-recommendation considerations

Balance of benefits and harms

A combined approach of patient history and physical examination improves diagnostic accuracy and, according to reference guidelines, is the recommended first-line evaluation strategy for AR. Using either history or examination alone is not recommended, as physical findings are nonspecific and can overlap with non-AR or viral rhinitis, lowering predictive value.[7]

With patient history, asking about relevant comorbidities and family history identifies at-risk patients and guides integrated disease management.[891011] Allergic rhinitis often coexists with other atopic and airway conditions, such as asthma, allergic conjunctivitis and atopic dermatitis. Furthermore, a family history of atopic conditions is a recognised risk factor for developing AR.[8910] Complementing patient history with a physical examination (e.g. inspection of nasal cavity, anterior rhinoscopy, eye exam) increases diagnostic confidence through observable findings (e.g. allergic shiners, turbinate hypertrophy, conjunctival erythema).

Certainty of evidence

As the balance of benefits and harms is based on using positions from high-quality guidelines instead of evidence, this is not applicable.

Values and preferences

No relevant literature was found, although it is plausible that patients would value diagnostic accuracy, which is in line with the benefits associated with the proposed recommendation.

Resources and feasibility

No significant concerns were identified regarding resources or feasibility because the proposed practice aligns with the standard clinical approach locally.

Acceptability and other considerations

No specific evidence was available on patient preferences for diagnostic approaches. However, an accurate diagnosis based on non-invasive methods (patient history and clinical examination) is generally preferred before moving to more invasive methods (e.g. allergy testing, nasal endoscopy).

Expert Group deliberation of the above factors

The EG agreed to a strong recommendation, acknowledging the position of reference guidelines and their applicability in current practice. The EG also noted clinician and patient preference to minimise invasive diagnostic procedures unless necessary, e.g. in complicated cases of AR.

RECOMMENDATION 2

Conduct allergy testing only when clinically warranted, for example, when the diagnosis is unclear, or when optimised pharmacotherapy provides inadequate symptom control. (Strong recommendation)

Summary

Allergy testing is not routinely required for AR diagnosis and management, but its value in detecting allergen-specific immunoglobulin E (IgE) is recognised. Based on reference guidelines, both skin prick test (SPT) and serum immunoglobulin E (sIgE) tests are widely accepted for confirming IgE-mediated sensitisation in suspected AR cases, with SPT recognised for its lower cost, immediate results and high sensitivity/specificity. However, in cases where SPT is not feasible, such as resource requirements or contraindications (e.g. patients with severe dermatoses or patients who are unable to stop antihistamines), sIgE testing is acknowledged as a safe and reliable alternative. This testing offers broader allergen panels and less variability in results, although at a higher cost and slower turnaround time compared with those of SPT.

Evidence-to-recommendation considerations

Balance of benefits and harms

Although clinical history and a physical examination are generally sufficient to diagnose AR, reference guidelines agree that allergy testing is useful in detecting allergen-specific IgE. Allergy testing is also essential when allergen immunotherapy (AIT) is being explored.[12] In terms of test types, SPT has been demonstrated as a safe method of allergy testing with sensitivity and specificity greater than 80%.[13141516] Moreover, sIgE testing is believed to be a safe and effective alternative to SPT and can also be used for a wider panel of allergens.[121718] However, sIgE testing also faces similar challenges as SPT, such as discomfort from blood tests, risk of inaccurate test results from false positives,[19] and increased cost.

Certainty of evidence

The certainty is high for both SPT and sIgE tests, supported by prospective studies, SRs and multiple guidelines.[810131415202122232425] Both tests demonstrate high diagnostic accuracy and a favourable safety profile, with a minimal risk of bias.

Values and preferences

Patients generally value the accuracy of allergy testing results, as it gives them clarity on the root cause of their symptoms.[26] This allows them to receive tailored management or targeted immunotherapy when appropriate. Minor harms associated with allergy testing, such as temporary discomfort, are generally acceptable given the perceived value of obtaining a definitive diagnosis.

Resources and feasibility

Allergy testing requires trained personnel and laboratory infrastructure; hence, this testing should only be recommended when the diagnosis is still unclear in primary care settings or where AIT is being explored as a treatment option.

Acceptability and other considerations

Allergy testing is generally acceptable to patients when explained clearly and recommended as part of a structured diagnostic or management process.

Expert Group deliberation of the above factors

The EG agreed to a strong recommendation, acknowledging the indications for allergy testing. They noted that testing for specific aeroallergens, especially in the local context, is sufficient. They agreed on SPT or sIgE as the main allergy tests, without expressing any preferences. However, they acknowledged that SPT is more commonly conducted in local settings due to its availability.

RECOMMENDATION 3

Prescribe intranasal corticosteroid (INCS), oral antihistamine (OAH) (second-generation or later), or combination INCS and intranasal antihistamine (INAH) to manage AR. (Strong recommendation)

Summary

A strong recommendation for pharmacotherapy is proposed, based on a favourable benefit–risk ratio across all three treatment options, moderate-to-high quality of evidence, alignment with patients’ values and preferences for outcomes, and no significant resource concerns. Although efficacy data are robust, treatment response and tolerability vary across patients depending on symptom profile and preference. Prescribing treatment options should therefore be individualised, balancing efficacy, onset of action, convenience and cost.

Evidence-to-recommendation considerations

Balance of benefits and harms

Overall, reference guidelines recognise second-generation OAH, INCS and fixed-dose INCS + INAH combination as effective first-line pharmacotherapy options for AR.[5112728] Among these, the INCS + INAH combination demonstrate the greatest efficacy and more rapid onset of action compared with INCS and OAH monotherapy.[528] However, their uptake may be limited by their bitter aftertaste and higher cost. Importantly, guidelines emphasise that patient preferences, tolerability and access should guide the choice of initial pharmacotherapy. For patients as young as 2 years old, INCS and OAH monotherapy options are indicated for AR. For INCS + INAH combination available locally, the indicated age is above 6 years. Benefits and harms for each treatment class are summarised below.

For OAH, SRs demonstrated their efficacy over placebo for both AR symptoms and QoL improvements.[293031323334353637] Outcomes were assessed using symptom scores such as the Total Nasal Symptom Score (TNSS), Peak Nasal Inspiratory Flow (PNIF) and Rhinoconjunctivitis Quality of Life Questionnaire (RQLQ). The effect size is modest overall, e.g. the SR conducted by Zhang et al. reported statistically significant but not clinically meaningful improvements in RQLQ scores, and the changes in PNIF were not significant.[32] There is low sedation risk (for second-generation and later OAH), with no major adverse effects and good tolerability across both paediatric and adult populations.[2938] Second-generation OAH is safer than first-generation OAH, with the latter causing sedation and cognitive impairment.[3940]

For INCS, SRs consistently demonstrated their efficacy in improving nasal symptoms and QoL compared with placebo, as measured by the TNSS and RQLQ, respectively, and improving ocular symptoms, as measured by the Total Ocular Symptom Score (TOSS).[284142] Moreover, INCS are superior to OAH for overall nasal symptom control, especially for nasal congestion and obstruction.[434445] However, the onset of action of INCS is slightly slower than that of OAH, with initial effects experienced within 3–60 hours and maximal effect after 2 weeks.[464748] Adverse effects are local (irritation, epistaxis and aftertaste) and generally mild.

For INCS + INAH, SRs have also demonstrated the efficacy of INCS + INAH in improving nasal symptoms as measured by the TNSS and in improving QoL as measured by the RQLQ.[284142] However, when compared with INCS monotherapy, INCS + INAH were not associated with significantly greater improvements in both symptom control and QoL.[2841] In terms of safety, compared with INCS monotherapy, the incidence of adverse effects increased with INCS + INAH combination, although absolute incidence was low.[495051525354] Dysgeusia (i.e. altered taste) is higher with INCS + INAH combination but still infrequent.

Certainty of evidence

For OAH, the certainty of evidence is moderate to high. Multiple SRs/MAs[293031323334353637] confirmed that OAH is superior to placebo for sneezing, itching and rhinorrhoea, but effect sizes are modest overall. For INCS, certainty is high. Multiple SRs and MAs[4142434445] consistently demonstrate the efficacy of INCS in AR symptom control and their superiority over OAH for certain AR symptoms (e.g. nasal congestion) with robust QoL improvements.

For INCS + INAH, certainty is moderate. Evidence summarised in SRs shows consistent improvements in TNSS, TOSS and RQLQ compared with those of placebo and INCS alone.[4155] However, there was heterogeneity detected in QoL outcomes (I2 up to 79%) and increased incidence of minor adverse events (e.g. dysgeusia), which reduces the overall certainty to moderate.

Values and preferences

One SR assessing patient values and preferences in AR reported that patients consistently prioritise nasal symptoms, particularly nasal congestion, over functional status or care-related experience.[56] Nasal symptoms (mainly nasal congestion), followed by breathing disorders, general and ocular symptoms, were also rated as the symptoms with the highest impact. For pharmacotherapy in AR, attributes related to the reduction and relief of symptoms were rated as more important for adult patients than the risk of side effects. Patients also showed a preference for more efficacious treatments over faster-acting treatments.

Resources and feasibility

Formulations of INCS are widely available, relatively inexpensive through subsidies and effective with brief technique training. Similarly, OAH are subsidised and readily available in primary care. The INCS + INAH combination is currently not subsidised and may not be available in some primary care settings (e.g. polyclinics). Furthermore, generic formulations are not yet available locally, and costs can be more than ten times higher than generic INCS monotherapy. No local cost-effectiveness analyses for INCS + INAH have been performed to date.

Expert Group deliberation of the above factors

The EG agreed on a strong recommendation and supported the inclusion of a summary to compare the three initial options in the ACG. They acknowledged the potential of the INCS + INAH combination as an efficacious treatment option with a rapid onset despite its bitter taste. They also recognised INCS as the preferred option in practice, considering their favourable efficacy and cost, but cited the lack of patient acceptance and adherence as the main barriers for treatment effectiveness to be realised.

RECOMMENDATION 4

Offer allergen-avoidance advice to all AR patients as an adjunct to pharmacological therapy, focusing on accessible and practical strategies. (Strong recommendation)

Summary

Although evidence has limitations and is of low quality, allergen avoidance remains a practical, low-risk strategy that complements pharmacotherapy in AR management. Therefore, a strong recommendation is proposed, with clinicians encouraged to prioritise the most accessible and practical allergen-avoidance strategy. For this EtR, strategies focused on house dust mite (HDM) avoidance were evaluated because of its high prevalence in Singapore. A local cohort study showed that HDM is the predominant allergen in Singapore, with more than 80% of allergic individuals sensitised to it, and environmental exposure in the local climate strongly drives this sensitisation.[57]

Evidence-to-recommendation considerations

Balance of benefits and harms

Reducing exposure to HDM through physical and chemical interventions is a potentially beneficial option in AR management.[19] Physical interventions (e.g. impermeable bedding, regular washing, vacuuming) could reduce HDM levels, but this effect did not reliably translate into symptom improvement for AR.[5859606162636465] Moreover, referenced trials were small, often methodologically weak and heterogeneous. Chemical interventions, such as acaricides in household cleaners, have been used to reduce HDM concentration, with studies evaluating the use of an acaricide spray showing improved mean symptom scores compared with controls, although not by a significant amount.[5966]

Certainty of evidence

Studies considered were of very low to low quality, as they were mostly small randomised controlled trials with methodological limitations, heterogeneous interventions and risk of bias.[5859606162636465666768]

Values and preferences

Patients with AR prioritise symptom reduction and improved QoL and would value strategies that alleviate their symptoms.[56] Based on clinical evidence of allergen avoidance, there may therefore be a disconnect, as these interventions may not lead to desirable reductions in AR symptoms.

Resources and feasibility

Physical measures (e.g. bedding encasements, HEPA filters, air purifiers) involve moderate-to-high upfront costs and regular ongoing usage, while chemical acaricides require regular application. The cost-effectiveness of these allergen-avoidance measures has not been evaluated. Regardless, some interventions are compatible with broader lifestyle modifications (e.g. improved ventilation, cleaning).

Acceptability and other considerations

Acceptability of allergen-avoidance strategies generally depends on the perceived benefit, cost and effort required to carry out the strategy. For HDM reduction, interventions such as regular washing of bedding in hot water, use of impermeable mattress and pillow covers, and routine cleaning are generally well accepted because they are low-risk and familiar household practices. However, compliance with more intensive or costly measures (e.g. HEPA air filters, acaricide sprays, specialised bedding) tends to be low, reflecting uncertainty about the financial burden and the associated clinical benefit.

Expert Group deliberation of the above factors

The EG agreed on a strong recommendation, acknowledging the limitations of evidence but recognised allergen avoidance as a practical measure to complement pharmacological treatment in managing AR symptoms. Although evidence to choose between specific strategies is lacking, the EG emphasised that the most practical, feasible and affordable measures should be prioritised.

IMPLEMENTATION

Systematic implementation of these evidence-based recommendations can substantially enhance the management of AR by standardising the approaches to diagnosis and encouraging prompt initiation and continuation of appropriate pharmacological and non-pharmacological management. These efforts can help improve patients’ QoL and clinical outcomes.

To empower clinicians towards optimising AR diagnosis and management, multi-faceted implementation strategies addressing capability, opportunity and motivation barriers should be considered:

Education and training: Clinical meetings and training programmes can focus on enhancing diagnostic confidence through systematic history-taking and physical examination techniques while reducing unnecessary allergy testing. CONCLUSION

This EtR framework summarises the evidence base and rationale, including expert deliberations, that underpin the ACG recommendations to guide clinicians in interpreting and applying them in the diagnosis and management of AR. The recommendations are applicable across all care settings but are especially relevant to primary and community care, where most AR cases present and can be managed holistically. By adopting these evidence-based recommendations, healthcare providers can alleviate the substantial burden of undiagnosed and undertreated AR in Singapore.

The transition from episodic symptom relief to comprehensive chronic management requires coordinated efforts to build healthcare provider confidence in clinical diagnosis and pharmacotherapy selection; enhance patient understanding of treatment options, such as allergen avoidance; leverage digital enablers; and establish systematic care pathways that support long-term care rather than reactive management. Collectively, these strategies can systematically address associated capability, opportunity and motivation barriers, fostering consistent, evidence-based care to improve outcomes for individuals with AR.
