Intolerance of uncertainty as a potential modifiable risk factor for eating disorder pathology in women athletes: a longitudinal investigation
Authors: Grace Koob, Carolyn Black Becker, Vivienne M. Hazzard, Kelsey A. Varzeas, Nicole Y. Wesley, Miriam Rowan, Tiffany M. Stewart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundResearch indicates that elevated intolerance of uncertainty (IU) is cross-sectionally associated with eating disorder (ED) pathology in women. Although longitudinal data is needed to better understand whether IU is a prospective risk factor for EDs in the general population, IU also influences the performance of athletes, a population at risk for EDs. Despite the relevance of both IU and ED within athletics, research assessing the association of IU and ED pathology in athletes has yet to be conducted. The current study longitudinally explored IU as a potential modifiable risk factor for ED pathology in a sample of women athletes.MethodsIU at baseline was hypothesized to predict downstream ED pathology. Participants (N = 393) were women athletes aged 18–39 years from athletic teams/activities across the United States who responded to surveys at baseline and at least one follow-up assessment (3-, 6-, and/or 12-months) as part of a large multi-measure longitudinal study of women athletes. IU and ED pathology were measured using the two-factor, 12-item Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale (IUS-12) and the nine-item Brief Eating Disorders in Athletes Questionnaire (BEDA-Q), respectively. Age-adjusted modified Poisson regression generalized estimating equations (GEEs) examined baseline IUS subscale scores as predictors of subsequent changes in prevalence of a probable ED.ResultsThe prevalence of a probable ED in this sample was 22.7% at baseline. ED outcomes were significantly predicted by baseline scores of the prospective anxiety subscale of the IUS-12 but not the inhibitory anxiety subscale. A one-standard-deviation higher baseline score on the prospective anxiety subscale was associated with an 18% increase in prevalence of a probable ED over the course of the longitudinal study (p = .01).ConclusionsBased on these findings, desire for predictability is a potentially modifiable target in preventing ED in women athletes.

Introduction

The ability to navigate uncertain situations and circumstances with mild to moderate (but bearable) anxiety can be beneficial in almost any context, as maintaining emotional stability in the face of ambiguity often leads to better decision making and action. In contrast, an inability to tolerate uncertainty can significantly hinder one’s quality of life because intense anxiety can fuel maladaptive avoidance and be debilitating. As a research construct, intolerance of uncertainty (IU) is a transdiagnostic, dispositional trait that represents a tendency to experience elevated anxiety during the anticipation and/or experience of uncertain events [1]. Some evidence suggests that IU loads onto two distinct factors: prospective IU, a cognitive fixation with knowing what will happen in the future (i.e. desire for predictability), and inhibitory IU, an inability to act during ambiguity (i.e. behavioral paralysis) Ref. [2]; see review by Ref. [3] for further support and criticisms regarding bifactor model of IU). Despite most often being associated with anxiety disorders [4], IU is currently understood to be a vulnerability for emotional symptoms across different types of psychopathology, having been empirically linked to obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD [5]), post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD [6]), and depression [7].

IUis also found to be heightened among individuals with an eating disorder (ED), though much of the research is limited to cross-sectional findings. For instance, in a sample of women meeting criteria for an ED, both patients with anorexia nervosa (AN) and bulimia nervosa (BN) indicated greater IU compared to controls [8]. A review by Brown et al., [9] further indicated that IU is most strongly associated with specific ED symptoms including drive for thinness, purging, restriction, and safety behaviors. The authors of this review suggest that IU could be a mechanism particularly underlying AN symptoms, as patients with this diagnosis more strongly endorse negative beliefs surrounding uncertainty and psychological rigidity relative to BN and other EDs [9, 10].

Looking at the limited longitudinal research on IU in EDs, Williams and Levinson [11] conducted a study of IU, OCD symptoms, and ED pathology among a clinical sample of 168 ED patients comprised of majority AN diagnoses (71.4%) in addition to patients with other specified feeding or ED (22.0%), BN (5.4%), and binge eating disorder (BED: 1.2%).Although IU was cross-sectionally correlated with ED symptom, Williams & Levinson found that IU was not a prospective predictor of ED symptoms. The authors noted that the limited variance in IU remaining after accounting for other study variables related to OCD and ED pathology might account for these null findings.

We contend that further longitudinal research investigating the association of IU with ED symptoms is warranted for several reasons. First, the existing longitudinal literature is sparse and it seems premature to conclude the null hypothesis. Second, although the research by Williams and Levinson represents foundational prospective research, their sample only included patients with a current ED. However, the association of IU and ED pathology should also be investigated in non-clinical populations that may be at an elevated risk for ED as data capturing pre- and sub-threshold ED stages may elucidate the extent to which IU is relevant at different points across the clinical course. Third, Williams and Levinson also noted that their results were surprising given the extant literature, and they called for additional longitudinal research to see if their findings replicated. Finally, Williams and Levinson used the 27-item version of the Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale (IUS-27). It is beyond the scope of this paper to fully elucidate the debate that has taken place regarding the best measure of IU; readers are referred to Roma and Hope [12] for discussion. However, to summarize, a short form of the IUS-27 was developed to address perceived problems with this measure (e.g., factor instability, high inter-item correlations [2]. Importantly, in an independent study that included a head-to-head comparison of the IUS-27 and the short form of the IUS (IUS-12), results indicated that the IUS-12 two-factor structure provided the best fit to the data [12]. Williams and Levinson also tested a total score of the IUS, not the separate factors. Thus, further research is needed to investigate if IU prospectively predicts ED pathology using the two-factor IUS-12.

The theoretical literature also supports further investigation of IU and ED pathology, Merwin et al., [13] proposed an anxiety-based model of ED that is highly relevant to IU, suggesting that psychological rigidity and desire for control are core traits contributing to the emergence and maintenance of ED symptoms, especially in patients with AN. For individuals with AN, desire for control often manifests as a drive for thinness or some other goal-directed manipulation of body weight, shape, or composition [14, 15]. Among the biggest factors impacting one’s ability to manipulate body weight/size/shape are hunger cues, which naturally fluctuate to keep the body in homeostasis (i.e. maintain balance between energy expenditure and intake) in patterns that are relatively unpredictable. In healthy individuals, hunger typically varies from day to day to accommodate the body’s demands, meaning that the exact amount of food one needs to function optimally will also vary [16]. Thus, there is a natural degree of uncertainty that accompanies everyday experiences of eating. However, for an individual with AN who has a desire for control aimed at extreme manipulations of their body, certainty surrounding their ability to control food intake is incredibly valuable, yet difficult to attain.

Overriding the physiological hunger cues keeping the body in homeostasis may help ease such uncertainty. Furthermore, during states of energy deficiency both hunger cues and affective arousal are suppressed [17, 18]. As such, AN individuals often employ extreme measures such as excessive dietary restriction and rigorous exercise, resulting in weight loss and subsequent dampening of hunger cues. Thus, Merwin et al., suggest that the rules dictating such disordered behaviors (e.g. the types and/or quantities of food that can be eaten, the amount of exercise that must be performed) are potent negative reinforcers. By suppressing both physiological and psychological sensations that hinder the pursuit of a body ideal, dreaded uncertainty is alleviated.

Another possible explanation for the connection between IU and ED pathology is that pathological eating and exercise behaviors can provide a sense of control when coping with uncertainty [9]. To illustrate, an individual may attain a sense of agency over their own actions and behaviors during periods of ambiguity by strictly adhering to self-imposed rules such as dietary restrictions and/or exercise rituals. Because control over external factors is often limited during uncertain situations, deliberately planning and performing disordered eating behaviors may temporarily satisfy a desire for control and ease unpleasant feelings caused by uncertainty. The reduction of aversive sensations and achievement of subjective control, albeit brief, simultaneously reinforce disordered behaviors as solutions for future experiences of uncertainty. Furthermore, reactions of excessive stress and anxiety that are disproportionately felt in response to uncertainty remain unchallenged when they are immediately suppressed by disordered eating safety behaviors [9].

It is well-established that women athletes are at least as at risk of developing an ED as the general population [19–21]. In many cases, they may even be at an even greater risk secondary to sport-related pressures to engage in disordered manipulations of weight, shape, and/or body composition [22]. From harmful narratives that leanness enhances performance (e.g. in distance running) to aesthetic-based judge evaluations and revealing uniforms (e.g. gymnastics, dance), women athletes must grapple with ED risks that are both specific and non-specific to sport. Indeed, subclinical disordered eating has been reported as high as 84% for collegiate-level women athletes and clinical ED rates have been as reported high as 49.2% [23]. Although less is known about EDs in men, the existing research suggests that men athletes are more likely to experience an ED that non-athlete men [24]. We nonetheless elected to focus exclusively on women athletes in the present study because prevalence data suggest that EDs are still more common among women athletes as compared to men athletes and because a number of additional risk factors appear to impact women athletes to a greater degree than men athletes (see Rowan et al., 2026 for review).

Given that female women athletes face similar if not greater risk of developing an ED compared to non-athletes, this group represents a high-risk community population. Identifying psychological constructs that are indicative of and/or protective against downstream ED pathology is a critical aspect of decreasing excessive ED burden across high-risk populations. As such, the present study, which is a secondary analysis of a larger study (see [25]), longitudinally explores the relationship between IU and ED pathology in a sample of women athletes, with the aim of identifying whether or not IU is a potentially modifiable risk factor for EDs. Given the previously noted limitations with Williams and Levinson [11] and theoretical rationale for expecting an association [13], we hypothesized that IU would longitudinally predict ED pathology in this high-risk community sample.

Methods

Participants

As a part of a larger, longitudinal study examining resilience in women athletes (see [25]), we recruited a baseline sample of 504 women athletes from across the United States using the following inclusion criteria: self-identified as women, endorsed participation in an athletic team/activity for at least 10 h per week, were willing/able to provide consent and complete study requirements, and were able to read English. Of the 504 eligible athletes who participated at baseline, 89 athletes who did not provide any data beyond baseline and 22 athletes who failed a fidelity check (i.e., implausible change in reported age across time) were excluded from analyses for the present study. These exclusions resulted in an analytic sample of 393 women athletes aged 18–39 years (Mage = 20.9 ± 3.1 years) who provided data at baseline and for at least one follow-up assessment (at 3-, 6-, and/or 12-month follow up). Of these 393, n = 354 (90.1%) completed the 3-month follow-up survey, n = 265 (67.4%) completed the 6-month follow-up survey, and n = 276 (70.2%) completed the 12-month follow-up survey.

Procedure

Flyers with a clickable link or scannable QR code directing users to the self-report assessments were used to recruit participants at various universities, athletic departments, and other organizations where researchers had existing contacts. Compensation for study participation was allocated following completion of measures at each time point. Participants received a link to a gift card worth $25 redeemable through Tango Card (Tango Card Inc., Minneapolis) at various retailers and were able to earn up to $100 total. For additional details regarding study procedures, see Stewart et al., [25].

Measures

IU was captured by the 12-item Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale (IUS-12), which has been proven to be suitable for both clinical and non-clinical groups [26]. Participants responded to prompts on a 5-point scale (e.g. 1- not characteristic of me at all, 5- entirely characteristic of me) [2]. The IUS-12 is divided into 2 factors: prospective anxiety (7 items, e.g. “I can’t stand being taken by surprise”) and inhibitory anxiety (5 items, e.g. “When it’s time to act, uncertainty paralyzes me”). Both subscales demonstrated good internal consistency in the present sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.88 for prospective anxiety and 0.89 for inhibitory anxiety at baseline). ED pathology was assessed at each assessment time point with Version 2 of the Brief Eating Disorders in Athletes Questionnaire (BEDA-Q), a 9-item measure to identify women athletes at risk for an ED [27]. Six items assessing ED-related cognitions and experiences were scored on a 6-point response scale ranging from “Always” to “Never.” Responses were scored as 3 (“Always”), 2 (“Usually”), 1 (“Often”) or 0 (“Sometimes,” “Rarely,” or “Never”); one item was reverse scored. Responses to these six items were summed to create a score ranging from 0 to 18. A separate dichotomous dieting variable was created based on responses to three weight loss questions. Participants received a score of 1 if they reported currently trying to lose weight or having attempted to lose weight three or more times, and a score of 0 otherwise. Following the scoring procedures developed by Martinsen et al., [27], the continuous score and the dichotomous dieting variable were combined using the following prediction equation: BEDA-Q score = −3.634 + (0.149 × continuous score) + (2.099 × dieting variable). The resulting overall score was used to classify participants as having a probable ED. Consistent with published scoring recommendations, participants with an overall score greater than 0.27 were classified as having a probable ED [27]. In the validation study, this version of the BEDA-Q demonstrated good discriminative accuracy for distinguishing athletes with and without an ED (AUC = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.78–0.93), with a cut-off score of 0.27 yielding a sensitivity of 82.1% (95% CI: 76.6%−87.6%) and specificity of 84.6% (95% CI: 79.4%−89.8%) [27]. In the present sample, the mean (standard deviation) overall score of athletes in the probable and non-probable ED groups at baseline were − 1.57 (1.31) and 0.76 (0.25), respectively.

Data analyses

Analyses were conducted in SPSS Version 30, with results considered statistically significant if p <.05. No influential observations or extreme values requiring exclusion were identified.

Missing data

Comparison of baseline characteristics across the 393 athletes in this study and the 111 athletes excluded from longitudinal analyses revealed that longitudinal-sample athletes were more likely to identify as non-Hispanic white than the athletes excluded from this analysis (76.1% versus 63.1%, p =.006) and exhibited significantly higher IUS-12 baseline scores on the prospective anxiety subscale (M = 20.1 ± 6.0 vs. M = 18.4 ± 6.4, p =.01) than the excluded athletes. Longitudinal-sample athletes did not differ significantly from baseline-only athletes on age, sexual orientation, parental education, sport competition level, perceived competitiveness of team, years training for sport, or probable ED. Considering the observed differences in race/ethnicity and prospective anxiety IUS-12 scores, though, inverse probability weighting (IPW) was used to address attrition bias [28]. IPW is recommended over imputation for handling missing data in longitudinal studies, where individuals who do not respond to surveys at various assessment time points have missing values across all variables [29]. Weights for IPW were derived as the inverse of the estimated probability that an athlete responded to at least one follow-up assessment based on age, race/ethnicity, sexual orientation, parental education, sport competition level, perceived competitiveness of team, and years training for sport. After weighting, there were no significant differences between the longitudinal and full baseline samples in any of these characteristics, IUS-12 subscale scores, or probable ED. All statistics reported in the present study except n’s, which represent observed counts, are weighted to address attrition bias. No data were missing at the item level.

Descriptive statistics

To describe the data from the longitudinal-sample athletes, means, standard deviations, and ranges were computed for continuous variables, and prevalences and counts were computed for categorical variables.

Generalized estimating equation (GEE) models

GEEs with a Poisson distribution, log link, and robust standard errors (i.e., modified Poisson GEEs) were used to examine the associations of interest for each of the study aims. The following three pairs of data points were included in modified Poisson GEEs: baseline to 3-month follow up (N = 354 data points available), baseline to 6-month follow up (N = 265 data points available), and baseline to 12-month follow up (N = 276 data points available). Participants could contribute data to more than one follow-up interval if outcome data were available at multiple follow-up assessments (e.g., 3-, 6-, and/or 12-month follow-up). Thus, some participants contributed multiple observations to the analytic dataset. GEEs account for the non-independence of repeated observations within individuals by modeling within-subject correlation, allowing all available data to be included while producing valid population-averaged estimates. Separate models were conducted for each IUS-12 subscale at baseline predicting probable ED status at 3-, 6-, and 12-months, adjusting for probable ED at baseline, age, and length of follow-up period. IUS-12 subscale scores were standardized for GEE models to facilitate comparison of results across subscales. Analyses incorporated IPWs to minimize attrition-related bias.

Discussion

IU has been linked to ED pathology previously [30]; however, this relationship has only been established cross-sectionally. As a result, conclusions regarding causality, which require rigorous longitudinal methodologies that satisfy the condition of temporal precedence, cannot be discerned from the existing literature as there is a dearth of longitudinal work on these constructs. Furthermore, limitations the existing longitudinal research on IU in EDs warrant reconsideration. For instance, previous longitudinal work by Williams and Levinson [11] did not find convincing evidence that IU predicted ED symptoms, yet they noted that this was an unexpected finding and needed replication and further investigation. Moreover, the clinical nature of their sample may have precluded detection of meaningful interactions between IU and ED factors that occur in pre-clinical phases of ED development. In addition, Williams and Levinson did not use what is increasingly viewed as the best measure of IU nor did they investigate the two factors that have emerged with this measure [12]. The potential role of IU in ED across time therefore has been insufficiently studied in a range of relevant samples.

The present study expands our current understanding of the relationship between IU and EDs in two ways. First, by including longitudinal evaluations of both constructs across multiple time points in a non-clinical sample, we explored the degree to which IU may serve as a potentially modifiable risk factor for the development of EDs. In other words, results from this study can provide clarification as to whether IU is merely a correlate of ED pathology or a possible risk factor. Second by exploring these associations in women athletes, we began to elucidate a new potential pathway for decreasing ED pathology in a high-risk population from the community. Third, we investigated both prospective and inhibitory IU using the IUS-12, which has not previously been done. Results of this study indicate that IU doespredict downstream ED pathology in women athletes. In particular, we found that elevated prospective IU at baseline predicted an increased prevalence of a probable ED later on, whereas inhibitory IU was not. Consideration of the differences between these types of uncertainty offer insight into why these results make sense.

To start, EDs are highly comorbid with anxiety pathology, and many patients express elevated levels of worry, obsessive tendencies, perfectionism, and harm avoidance [30]. Patients with EDs also tend to endorse a strong aversion to experiences of uncertainty and report that their ED behaviors (e.g. dietary restriction, excessive exercise) serve as a means of satisfying their desire for control, at least temporarily [31]. During times of uncertainty when control is often limited, the perception of control over one’s own eating and exercise behaviors may be especially valuable. In addition to affording one with a sense of control, systematically engaging in ED behaviors might also provide a source of predictability (the value of which is measured by the prospective IU subscale). Our findings seem to support this connection between aversion to uncertainty and a tendency to desire predictability with later engagement in ED behaviors, as we found that baseline prospective IU predicts an increase in later ED pathology.

In contrast to the findings with prospective IU, the longitudinal association between inhibitory IU and ED pathology was non-significant. There are several possible explanations for this. To start, whereas prospective IU represents a cognitive fixation with knowing what will happen in the future, inhibitory IU represents behavioral paralysis when certainty is absent. As noted above, individuals who desire predictability (high prospective IU) may employ ED behaviors to help alleviate distress when anticipating uncertain events, especially when they lack overt control over the situation. In contrast, a patterned inability to act during uncertainty (high inhibitory IU) is unlikely to directly predispose an individual to actively engage in any behavior, related to an ED or otherwise. Our findings seem to align with this theory, as inhibitory IU did not predict ED symptomatology, but prospective IU did. The behavioral profile indicating a probable ED by the BEDA-Q further shows stronger theoretical alignment with prospective IU than with inhibitory IU. The BEDA-Q probes ED status with items assessing cognitive fixations with body shape, weight, and size, as well as active efforts to lose weight and avoid disappointing others [27]. These items match with the tone of prospective IUS-12 items, which gauge cognitive fixations on uncertainty (e.g. “It frustrates me not having all the information I need,” “I always want to know what the future has in store for me”) and active efforts to prevent distress from uncertainty (e.g. “I should be able to organize everything in advance”) [2]. Likewise for the absence of longitudinal association between inhibitory IU and probable ED status, the behavioral profile indicated by the BEDA-Q does include items that align with themes of behavioral paralysis as relevant factors in ED pathology. We therefore suggest that a strong desire for predictability may be a plausible mechanism underlying the development of ED behaviors, whereas paralysis during uncertainty may be less relevant. However, it will be important for future researchers to investigate whether these findings replicate with other more comprehensive measures of ED pathology.

Although additional research is needed to better discern the independent implications of each IU subfactor in relation to ED pathology in different populations, existing evidence supports our finding that the IU subfactors may have different contributions to the presence of psychopathology. For instance, McEvoy and Mahoney [32] found that desire for predictability (prospective IU) has been linked specifically to OCD and generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) symptoms, whereas uncertainty paralysis (inhibitory IU) appears to associate more strongly with the profiles of depression and social anxiety. Unique associations between IU subfactors and specific symptoms within diagnostic categories have also been reported. For instance, inhibitory IU has been linked to specific OCD symptoms, including procrastination [33], contamination obsessions, and unacceptable thoughts [34]. In contrast, prospective IU has also been associated with the severity of compulsion symptoms [35] in OCD. Research also supports a relationship between prospective IU anxiety and avoidance behaviors [36], prolonged grief [37], and positive symptoms of schizophrenia [38].

Another question relates to the clinical significance of the present findings. Determining whether findings of statistical significance represent clinical significance is an important consideration for prevention and intervention research. Thus, we referred to the existing IU intervention literature to contextualize our finding that baseline prospective IU predicted an increased prevalence of ED pathology. Recent meta-analyses [39, 40] report large therapeutic effects yielded from IU interventions and show that IU improvements are associated with advantageous changes in symptoms of psychopathology (e.g. worry, depression) across IU interventions. However, specific conclusions as to the exact amount of IU change needed to yield therapeutic effects have not yet been specified in the literature and represent another endeavor for further research. Because anxiety disorders are better studied in relation to IU and intervention effectiveness than other forms of psychopathology, even less is known about the clinical implications of IU on ED outcomes. We are only aware of one study intervention specifically targeting IU in ED conducted by Sternheim and Harrison [41], which is still in the acceptability and feasibility phase. Nonetheless, the existing evidence that IU-based interventions show transdiagnostic effectiveness are promising [42].

Our participant sample of participants were recruited from a moderate-to-high-risk population: women athletes. In comparison to the general population, athletes (and especially women athletes) may be at a greater risk for the development of an ED [19, 22]. Inherently, sport participation provides unique sociocultural/environmental pressures for women athletes, particularly around body ideals [43]. These pressures may include inaccurate common narratives (e.g., equating a leaner physique with performance enhancement, [19]; revealing uniforms/attire, [44]); criticisms/comments from coaches [45] and media [46] on body shape, size, or weight; and competitive body comparison between teammates [47]. Saturation of these pressures may result in women athletes attempting to gain predictability via weight control behaviors (e.g., excessive exercising, disordered eating) to align their body with the “ideal body” found in their sport, thus increasing the potential for the development of an ED [43]. In the present sample, the prevalence of a probable ED at baseline was 22.7%, which is elevated relative to previous findings which typically range from 4 to 14% in non-athlete samples [22]. Therefore, future directions should include more longitudinal evaluations assessing the relationship between IU and ED pathology among other non-athlete groups facing an elevated ED risk, as well as across the general population.

It is important to acknowledge that the nature of our all-athlete sample may have influenced the results of the IUS-12 scores as well. The essence of sports includes uncertainty, as outcomes are largely based upon factors out of athletes’ control including environmental conditions (i.e., weather), coaching and officiating decisions, opponent performance, injuries, and behaviors of spectators. These inconsistent factors influence athletes’ perceptions of uncertain results, creating an opportunity to engage in actions that lead to perceived control over their performance, including ED behaviors [48, 49]. For instance, excessive overtraining and dietary restriction in preparation for a sporting event may provide an athlete with a sense of control over the outcome of their own performance, in order to cope with the uncertain nature of other factors. Conversely, the unpredictability of sports requires athletes to be able to navigate uncertain conditions requiring adaptability, flexibility, and the ability to change perspectives quickly, which can enhance performance in competitive situations [50, 51] by regulating their stress and anxiety [52].

It is possible that our athlete sample had lower levels of uncertainty paralysis (inhibitory IU) than might be expected of the general population due to the consistent nature of unpredictability in sport requiring athletes to frequently act despite uncertain circumstances. We report mean IUS-12 score on the inhibitory subscale to be 11.5 (SD = 4.5). Compared to other studies using the IUS-12 in comparable community samples of non-athlete women, the inhibitory IU scores reported by our sample of women athletes indeed appear to be somewhat lower. For instance, the sample of adolescent girls (N = 201, Mage = 13.67 years, SD = 1.27) surveyed by Cuesta-Zamora et al., [53] reported an average score of 12.51 (SD = 4.67), and the sample of 365 female nursing students included in analyses by Şentürk and Bakır [54] reported a mean score of 14.75 (SD = 5.12). The athletes in our sample have been training for over a decade on average (M = 11.9, SD = 5.3) and therefore may have had enough time in sports to develop the skills related to inhibitory IU that are necessary to maintain sport participation for such prolonged periods oftime.

This study has a number of limitations. The age of participants may have limited our power to detect the onset and/or progression of ED pathology. The average age of onset for an ED is reported to be 18 years [55]. However, our participants reported an average age of 21 years (SD = 3.1) and ranged from 18 to 39 years old. Because most participants were beyond the average age of onset for an ED, the measurement period may not have captured significant increases in ED pathology, thus limiting our ability to assess the predictive power of IU. Additionally, the timeline between the initial baseline and final follow-up assessments was limited to 12 months. This may not have been an adequate duration to capture the development of an ED. Beginning assessments of IU and ED pathology among individuals before they are most likely to experience the onset of an ED, as well as following up with participant across longer periods of time, may help to better discern the extent to which the temporal precedence of IU predicts later a ED.

Another limitation is that that the BEDA-Q is a screening instrument designed to identify probable ED status rather than specific ED diagnoses or symptom presentations. Consequently, we were unable to examine whether associations with IU differed across ED subtypes or symptom domains. Future research should investigate these associations using measures that capture specific ED symptoms and diagnoses. Additionally, due to substantial heterogeneity in sport type and few athletes competing at the high school level, our sample is not well suited to examine moderation by sport type or competition level, but such factors are important to consider and should be examined in future studies with adequate representation across groups. We also want to mention that findings from our all-women sample may not generalize to samples of men or gender diverse athletes, as sex and gender differences in mental health across athlete and non-athlete populations have been reported [56, 57]. In addition to the aforementioned future studies of women athletes, we also propose the study of men and gender diverse populations as worthwhile research directions.

Finally, our secondary analysis does not include participant ED history in the analyses because the initial study did not collect this measure in order to minimize participant burden given the extensive survey battery in the original study design [25]. The absence of ED history limits our discussion of IU as a risk factor for ED pathology as we are unable to specify whether probable ED status in the sample represents the emergence of new ED symptoms or relapse of a pre-existing disorder. We provide evidence that prospective IU does predict ED symptoms later on. However, because we cannot distinguish between new, continuing, and recurring ED symptoms, we are not able to conclude that IU is a specific risk factor for initial ED onset.

The present study poses implications for the prevention and treatment of EDs in women athletes, which is important especially because this population may already be at a greater risk for an ED. Our results may better inform the development of intervention efforts aimed at reducing EDs in women athletes. The prevalence of a probable ED in the present women athlete sample was indeed elevated relative to previous findings from non-athletes, supporting the need for prevention and treatment specially directed towards this population. Elevated prospective IU was longitudinally associated with an increased prevalence of an ED. Thus, desire for predictability may be a promising factor for future intervention research regarding modifiable psychological constructs worth targeting to prevent and treat EDs in women athletes and other moderate-to-high-risk groups.
