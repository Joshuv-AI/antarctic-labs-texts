Methotrexate Toxicity Management in Polymyalgia Rheumatica: A Case Report
Authors: Devi M Kotharu, Sai D Vutukuru, Prafullkumar Patel, Athmananda Nanjundappa, Sumanth K Bandaru
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMethotrexate (MTX) is an anti-folate drug with anti-inflammatory and anti-proliferative properties. Low-dose methotrexate (10–25 mg/week) is widely recognized as a safe and effective treatment for various autoimmune disorders. While toxicity from low dose methotrexate is uncommon it can lead to severe complications like pancytopenia. We present a case of a 68-year-old male with a history of steroid-resistant Polymyalgia Rheumatica on methotrexate (20 mg weekly) and prednisone. He presented to the emergency room with oral mucosal lesions and reduced oral intake. Upon evaluation, he was found to have pancytopenia and transaminitis, raising concern for methotrexate toxicity. Treatment included folinic acid rescue therapy and filgrastim. This case highlights the importance of promptly identifying signs of methotrexate toxicity and regularly monitoring patients for adverse effects by healthcare providers.Keywords: Methotrexate toxicity, Oral ulcers, Methotrexate, Pancytopenia

Introduction

Methotrexate, a folic acid antagonist, is widely prescribed due to its dual anti-proliferative and anti-inflammatory properties, making it increasingly utilized in clinical settings. Despite its therapeutic benefits, the drug necessitates regular monitoring due to its diverse range of adverse effects. These can include bone marrow suppression, pulmonary toxicity, liver, or kidney impairment, gastrointestinal disturbances, mucocutaneous issues, and neurotoxicity. These adverse reactions can develop rapidly and may lead to severe complications such as pancytopenia, sepsis, and renal failure, presenting significant challenges in clinical management. 1 Methotrexate is widely used in treatment of various diseases. FDA approved indications include Rheumatoid arthritis, Psoriasis, Non-Hodkins Lymphoma, Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, Mycosis fungoides. Non-FDA approved indications include Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis, Granulomatosis with polyangiitis, Microscopic polyangiitis, Polyarteritis nodosa, Behcet’s syndrome and various other disorders.

Case presentation

A 68 year old male with past medical history of steroid resistant Polymyalgia rheumatica (currently on methotrexate 20 mg once weekly and prednisone 2 mg daily), presented to the emergency department with nausea, poor appetite, decreased oral intake and oral mucosal lesions since 7 days.

The patient presented with a one-week history of painful oral ulcers and reduced oral intake, along with a chronic cough that had persisted for several weeks. On presentation patient was afebrile, tachycardic (120 bpm), respiratory rate (16/min), blood pressure (134/82 mmHg), saturating 96% on room air. Physical examination was significant for multiple oral mucosal ulcers. Cardiac, respiratory, neurologic and abdominal examinations were unremarkable. Initial labs were significant for leukopenia (WBC -2.8 k/μL, Reference range (4.0–10.8 k/μL)), thrombocytopenia (70,000 k/μL, Reference range (140–400 k/μL)), Absolute neutrophil count (ANC-1400 cells/μL, Reference range (2500–7000 cells/μL)), mild transaminitis (AST-38, ALT-54), his clinical presentation of oral mucosal ulcers with bicytopenia and chronic use of methotrexate raised suspicion for Methotrexate toxicity (Patient has been on methotrexate 20 mg weekly for 3 years). Methotrexate levels were undetectable on presentation. Methotrexate was discontinued and he was started on oral leucovorin 10 mg every 6 h. Following the day of his admission, his WBC count, ANC and platelet count dropped to 2.2 k/μL, 670 cells/μL and 56,000 k/μL respectively. Hematology was consulted and patient was given a dose of Filgrastim and was kept on neutropenic precautions. His WBC and ANC improved to 5.13 k/μL and 3900 cells/μL. Patient’s oral lesions gradually improved during his course of hospitalization, his WBC and platelet counts also improved. The patient was discharged in stable condition with a prescription for leucovorin 10 mg four times daily. He was advised to follow up with rheumatology, hematology-oncology on outpatient basis.

Discussion

Methotrexate (MTX), a synthetic analogue of folate, blocks the enzyme dihydrofolate reductase. It interferes with the production of reduced folate (tetrahydrofolate), which is crucial for several biochemical pathways such as de novo synthesis of purines and pyrimidines. In treating rheumatic diseases, methotrexate inhibits the proliferation of lymphocytes and other cells responsible for inflammation. In cancer treatment, methotrexate deprives malignant cells of the purine and pyrimidine precursors needed for DNA and RNA synthesis. This inhibition prevents the cancerous cells from proliferating. 2 Low-dose methotrexate primarily exerts its effects through anti-inflammatory mechanisms. On the other hand, high-dose methotrexate acts as a cytotoxic agent, demonstrating anti-proliferative properties. These two forms of methotrexate differ significantly in their toxicity profiles and the types of adverse effects they induce. Essentially, in clinical practice, low-dose and high-dose methotrexate are regarded as distinct therapeutic treatments.3

Low-dose methotrexate (5–25 mg/week) is extensively used in various systemic rheumatic diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, dermatomyositis, and systemic lupus erythematosus.4,5

High-dose methotrexate (500 mg/m2 or higher) is used in the treatment of various cancers affecting both adults and children, such as acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), osteosarcoma, and lymphomas. 6 On the other hand, observational studies involving patients receiving low-dose methotrexate (LD-MTX) have reported an elevated incidence of several cancer types, including melanoma, lung cancer, and non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma.4

Patients were categorized based on their methotrexate exposure and symptoms. Accidental overdose is the leading cause of acute MTX toxicity. A significant number of chronic users have mistakenly taken MTX instead of folic acid tablets. Additionally, patients who inadvertently consume daily doses of methotrexate instead of weekly doses also raise their risk of methotrexate toxicity (In our case, the patient was taking Methotrexate weekly as prescribed). Chronic MTX toxicity comprised prolonged MTX overdoses, concurrent use of other myelosuppressive medications, and low folate levels, despite receiving weekly folate supplements.7

In acute toxicity, the most frequently reported symptoms were nausea and vomiting. In chronic cases, the primary manifestations include mucosal ulcers, skin lesions, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, gastrointestinal bleeding, and hepatotoxicity.

Methotrexate toxicity affects several body systems. It can lead to hepatotoxicity, characterized by elevated aminotransferases, particularly in individuals with a history of conditions such as diabetes, obesity, NASH, hepatitis B or C infections, and alcohol use. Hematologic toxicity presents with symptoms like anemia, thrombocytopenia, and leukopenia. Mucocutaneous manifestations include oral mucosal ulcers, skin burning, photosensitivity reactions, acral erythema, erythema multiforme, urticaria, and vasculitis, affecting both skin and mucous membranes. Pulmonary toxicity results in interstitial pneumonitis. Nephrotoxicity can result in acute kidney injury with elevated levels of blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine due to methotrexate crystallizing within renal tubules, leading to impaired kidney function.11

Key predisposing factors associated with the development of chronic MTX toxicity include advanced age, renal failure, hypoalbuminemia, and concurrent use of medications that interact with MTX. These medications include salicylates and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, which reduce renal excretion and tubular secretion of MTX. Additionally, drugs like trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole and sulfasalazine increase MTX’s cytotoxic effects by concurrently inhibiting dihydrofolate reductase. Leflunomide and azathioprine contribute to myelosuppression when used together with MTX.7

Therefore, before initiating treatment, it is important to assess liver and kidney function and screen for alcohol use. Pretreatment evaluations should include baseline complete blood count, liver and kidney function tests, chest X-ray, and pregnancy testing for women of childbearing age. Throughout treatment, monitoring should include complete blood counts, creatinine levels, and liver function tests every 2–4 weeks during the initial 3 months, then every 8–12 weeks for the next 3–6 months, and subsequently every 12 weeks.8

Because methotrexate (MTX) concentrations in the blood decrease rapidly within 24 h after administration in cases of low dose toxicity, measuring these levels is not considered reliable. Treatment decisions are primarily guided by clinical evaluation rather than MTX blood levels. However, in cases of acute MTX poisoning, assessing serum MTX concentrations is crucial for determining folinic acid therapy. The dosage of folinic acid should be adjusted based on these levels, with serum MTX concentrations monitored every 24 h until they decrease below 0.2 μmol/L. Routine monitoring of MTX levels is unnecessary in cases of low-dose poisoning involving weekly MTX doses ranging from 5 to 25 mg.7,12,13

Folic acid (5 mg daily) given prophylactically decreases the risk of adverse effects of methotrexate. Leucovorin, given weekly at doses ranging from 2.5 mg to 5 mg, is particularly effective in preventing adverse effects such as stomatitis, myelosuppression, gastric toxicity, and neurotoxic effects, especially in cases where higher doses of folic acid have not been effective.12 It works by counteracting methotrexate’s inhibition of dihydrofolate reductase, thereby alleviating toxicity.8 Leucovorin (folinic acid) rescue and folate supplementation are standard approaches for managing methotrexate toxicity because it reduces MTX toxicity and restores intracellular folate levels.9

Glucarpidase is a newly approved drug by the FDA that enzymatically cleaves methotrexate. It is administered in cases where there is delayed elimination of methotrexate, acute kidney injury, and when plasma methotrexate concentrations are greater than 1 μmol/L.8

Our patient, who has steroid-resistant polymyalgia rheumatica (defined as poor response to initial glucocorticoid treatment of 15 mg/day prednisolone or experiencing a flare when tapered to a maintenance dose of 5 mg/day within the first 6 months), was started on methotrexate. Given his history of methotrexate use for three years, methotrexate toxicity was suspected. Moreover, in the absence of established guidelines for treating glucocorticoid-resistant polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR), other treatment options may be explored. This includes considering immunosuppressive medications like methotrexate (MTX), as well as biological agents such as anti-tumor necrosis factor-α (anti-TNFα) drugs and the anti-interleukin-6 (anti-IL-6) receptor antibody tocilizumab (TCZ) as potential adjunct therapies.10

Conclusion

This case presentation emphasizes the importance of closely monitoring patients taking methotrexate. Administering methotrexate at the correct dosage and conducting thorough pretreatment evaluations are essential for minimizing side effects. Highlighting the significance of patient education, frequent clinical assessments, and regular laboratory monitoring to promptly detect and diagnose methotrexate overdose in its initial stages.
