GLUT1 Bioactive Triterpenoids from Commiphora mukul Suppresses Glycolysis and Induces Apoptosis in Breast Cancer Cells
Authors: Ahmad Zeb, Santiago Jr. Bolivar Avila, Adeel Saleem, Shaikh Mizanoor Rahman, Kifayat Ullah, Hayat Ur Rahman, Sobia Ahsan Halim, Shab Niaz Khan, Numan Sikandar, Anwar Ullah, Ahmed Al-Harrasi, Najeeb Ur Rehman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cancer cells rely heavily on glycolysis for energy production and survival. Therefore, targeting glycolysis represents a promising therapeutic approach to cancer treatment. This study investigates the anticancer potential of myrrhanone B (MN) and myrrhanol B (ML), two bioactive triterpenoids isolated from the ethyl acetate extract of Commiphora mukul (CMEE), with a particular focus on their effects on cancer metabolism and apoptosis. The cytotoxic effects of CMEE, MN, and ML were evaluated in MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cell lines by using MTT assays. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to examine interactions with glucose transporter-1 (GLUT1). Glucose uptake and lactate production were assessed calorimetrically to determine their impact on glycolysis. Apoptosis was analyzed using flow cytometry with annexin V/PI staining, while reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and caspase-3 activity were measured to explore oxidative stress and apoptotic pathways. CMEE, MN, and ML significantly reduced cell viability in a dose-dependent manner, with IC50 values at 48 h of 30 μg/mL for CMEE, 18 μM for MN, and 23 μM for ML in MDA-MB-231 cells and 40 μg/mL for CMEE, 22 μM for MN, and 27 μM for ML in MCF-7 cells. In vitro, glucose uptake and lactate levels were significantly reduced in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells following treatment with IC50 concentrations of MN, ML, and CMEE, suggesting a strong antiglycolytic effect. In silico docking and simulation predict good complementarity of MN and ML with GLUT1 protein and high binding affinity for the GLUT1 receptor, which may be a probable drug target for these compounds. Furthermore, apoptosis assays revealed a significant increase in early and late apoptotic cell populations following treatment with IC50 doses of CMEE, MN, and ML, which correlate with the elevated intracellular ROS levels and enhanced caspase-3 activity. CMEE and its bioactive compounds, MN and ML, exert potent anticancer effects by glycolysis suppression, elevating ROS levels, and promoting apoptosis in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 breast cancer cells.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the frequently occurring cancer among women globally and is the primary cause of cancer-related death in women, with metabolic reprogramming playing a critical role in its progression. Cancer cells exhibit altered energy metabolism, as evidenced by a predilection for glycolysis for oxygen-independent energy synthesis. This adaptation of energy metabolism is known as the Warburg effect (aerobic glycolysis), where cancer cells counter the inherent inefficiency of glycolysis, relative to oxidative phosphorylation in ATP generation, by augmenting glucose uptake. This, in turn, results in an elevated rate of glycolysis, contributing to enhanced lactate production. Moreover, aerobic glycolysis also causes cancer cells, to produce efficient energy and macromolecules that will support their rapid growth and survival. Furthermore, this heightened glycolysis contributes to the generation of the cancer microenvironment that facilitates cancer cells to escape immune surveillance and promotes drug resistance. Given these implications, targeting glycolysis has, therefore, emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for breast cancer treatment.

Previous studies have suggested that glycolytic inhibition can trigger apoptosis, a programmed cell death process that is often dysregulated in cancer cells. Apoptosis is primarily mediated through intrinsic and extrinsic pathways, with caspase-3 playing a pivotal role in executing cellular death. By disrupting glucose metabolism, cancer cells experience metabolic stress, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and activation of apoptotic signaling cascades. One key factor linking metabolic stress to apoptosis is reactive oxygen species (ROS), which are natural byproducts of cellular metabolism. While physiological levels of ROS regulate cell signaling and homeostasis, excessive ROS accumulation induces oxidative damage to proteins, lipids, and DNA, ultimately triggering apoptotic pathways. Inhibition of glycolysis can exacerbate ROS generation by impairing antioxidant defense mechanisms and increasing mitochondrial stress, thereby promoting apoptosis. , Thus, targeting both glycolysis and apoptosis could offer a dual therapeutic advantage in breast cancer treatment.

There is growing interest in the effects of natural bioactive compounds derived from a variety of medicinal plants on human health, particularly in cancer therapy. Commiphora mukul (C. mukul), a medicinal plant native to India, Sri Lanka, and southern Arabia, produces a gum resin known as guggul, which has been traditionally used for its therapeutic benefits in treating inflammation, obesity, atherosclerosis, bacterial infections, and coagulation disorders. , Guggul is rich in bioactive compounds, including sesquiterpenes, triterpenes, diterpenoids, oligosaccharides, and sterols, all of which contribute to its diverse medicinal properties. Among its key bioactive compounds, myrrhanone B (MN) and myrrhanol B (ML) have shown potent cytotoxic effects against the breast cancer cell line. However, their precise mechanism of action, particularly in the context of metabolic reprogramming and apoptosis induction, remains to be fully elucidated. This study aims to elucidate the antiglycolytic and proapoptotic effects of MN and ML in breast cancer cells. Our findings may contribute to the development of novel metabolic-targeted cancer therapies derived from natural compounds.

Materials and Methods

Plant Collection, Identification, and Isolation

The gum resin of C. mukul (2.0 kg) was collected from the local market of Nizwa, Oman, and identified by the plant taxonomist at the Natural and Medical Sciences Research Center (NMSRC), University of Nizwa, Oman. A voucher specimen with a CMS-05/2018 label was deposited in the herbarium of the NMSRC. After dryness, 1.5 kg of resin was ground, minced, and extracted with analytical methanol (2.5 L) at normal temperature for 20 days (3 times). A dark yellowish crude extract (840 g) was obtained by the removal of the solvent under reduced pressure. The dried methanol extract was fractionated, based on increasing polarity of organic solvents, into subfractions including aqueous (90 g), n-butanol (155 g), ethyl acetate (210 g), dichloromethane (120 g), and n-hexane (266 g). The n-hexane fraction (220 g) was loaded to silica gel column chromatography (70–230 mesh; Merck), using the ethyl acetate–n-hexane (10:90, 20:80, and 30:70) system as an eluent to obtain MN (240 mg) and ML (550 mg) (Figure ). ,,

Structures of myrrhanone B (MN) and myrrhanol B (ML).

Cell Culture

MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells (ATCC strain, Manassas, USA) were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) and enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and a mixture of penicillin–streptomycin. Nonmalignant MCF-10A cells (ATCC strain, Manassas, USA) were cultured in DMEM/F-12 medium. All cells were grown at 37 °C in a humidified environment containing 5% CO2. The cells were harvested by rinsing three times in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then incubating with trypsin for 3 min at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After adding 0.5 mL of FBS to neutralize trypsin, the cells were centrifuged, reconstituted in a culture medium (2.0 mL), and counted using a cytometer (Neubauer counting chamber, Marienfeld, Germany).

Cell Proliferation Assay

2 × 104 cells/well were grown in a 96-well plate for 24 h, followed by treatment with various concentrations of CMEE, MN, and ML for up to 24 and 48 h. Cell proliferation was determined using an MTT assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance of the colorimetric assay was read at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Byonoy Absorbance 96, Germany). The IC50 value was calculated as the concentration of the compound required to inhibit 50% proliferation of the treated cells.

Apoptosis Assay

The CMEE, MN, and ML were checked for apoptosis induction in MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells using a FITC-annexin V apoptosis detection kit (abcam; ab14085) on a flow cytometer as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 5 × 104 cells/well were seeded in a 24-well tissue culture plate and treated with IC50 of CMEE, MN, and ML for 24 h. After treatment, cells were harvested by trypsinization, washed twice with cold PBS, and resuspended in 500 μL of 1× annexin V binding buffer. Annexin V-FITC (5 μL) and propidium iodide (PI, 5 μL) were added to each sample and incubated for 15 min in the dark at room temperature. The samples were then analyzed by using a flow cytometer. Data were processed to determine the percentage of normal (annexin V–/PI−), early apoptotic (annexin V+/PI−), late apoptotic (annexin V+/PI+), and necrotic (annexin V–/PI+) cells.

Caspase-3 Activity Assay

The level of caspase-3 activation was measured using a human caspase-3 (active) ELISA kit (Invitrogen Corporation, Camarillo, CA) as per the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 2 × 104 cells were plated in culture dishes, incubated for 24 h, and then exposed to compound treatments for the next 24 h. Cells were lysed in an extraction buffer with protease inhibitors and 5 mM PMSF. Lysates (100 μL) were incubated in microplate wells for 2 h, washed, and incubated with a caspase-3 detection antibody for 1 h. After washing, an HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit antibody was added for 30 min, followed by chromogen solution for 15–20 min. The reaction was stopped, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Active caspase-3 (ng/mg total protein) was quantified and expressed as the fold change relative to the control.

Intracellular ROS Analysis

MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well and allowed to attach overnight at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Cells were then treated with IC50 concentrations of CMEE, MN, and ML, with untreated cells serving as controls. After 24 h of incubation, the media was removed, and cells were washed with 1× PBS. The ROS detection reagent was prepared according to the ROS assay kit (Sigma-Aldrich; MAK144) instructions, and 100 μL of the reagent was added to each well. The plate was incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark, followed by fluorescence measurement at Ex/Em = 540/570 nm. The results were expressed in fold changes relative to corresponding controls.

Glucose Uptake Assay

A total of 2 × 104 cells/well were grown in a 96-well plate for 24 h, and then, the cells were treated with CMEE, MN, and ML at IC50 concentrations and were carried out for the next 24 h in glucose-containing medium. Apigenin (100 μM) treatment was used as a positive control. , After experimental treatments, the cells were starved in glucose-free culture medium for 2 h, and the level of glucose uptake was measured using a colorimetric assay kit (Sigma-Aldrich, MAK084), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The absorbance of the colorimetric assay was determined by using a microplate reader at 412 nm. A 2-DG6P standard calibration curve was used to calculate the concentration of unknown samples.

Lactate Assay

Cells at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well were placed in a 96-well plate. After incubating the cells for 24 h, treatment was administered for another 24 h with CMEE, MN, and ML at their respective IC5 0 concentrations. Cells were also treated with 130 μM sodium oxamate (positive control). , The level of lactate was measured using a colorimetric assay kit (Sigma-Aldrich, MAK064), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The absorbance was measured at 570 nm by using a microplate reader. A lactate standard curve was used to calculate the unknown concentration.

Statistical Analysis

In the current study, Graph (8.1) was utilized to perform statistical analysis. Variations among the different groups were determined using one-way ANOVA. The predetermined thresholds for statistical significance were *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Molecular Docking

The protein (GLUT1) used in this study was downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/) with PDB code 5EQG. The structure was evaluated by Molecular Operating Environment (MOE version 2024.06). Subsequently, the protein was prepared using MOE structure preparation and Protonate 3D wizards, which added hydrogen atoms on each residue and calculated partial charges according to the Amber14:EHT force field. The native ligand (2∼{S})-3-(4-fluorophenyl)-2-[2-(3-hydroxyphenyl)­ethanoylamino]-{N}-[(1∼{S})-1-phenylethyl]­propanamide, PDB ID 5RE (Figure S1), was extracted from the protein and used to validate the docking methodology (the Triangle Matcher docking method and London dG scoring function) by redocking experiment. This validation was based on the calculation of root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) between the redocked and the native conformations of the ligand, yielding a value of 1.78 Å (Figure S2). The same validated method was applied to dock ML and MN in GLUT1. The structures of compounds were drawn on ChemDraw and converted into a three-dimensional form by minimizing the molecules with a 0.1 RMS kcal/mol/Å gradient with AM1-BCC charges. The compounds were docked at the defined native ligand binding site with 50 conformations after refinement with the GBVI/WSA dG method. The docked orientations of compounds were quantitatively analyzed by the PLIF (protein ligand interaction fingerprints) descriptor in MOE.

Molecular Dynamics (MD) Simulation

The methodology used in this work was based on the approach described by Barraza et al., which consisted of performing molecular dynamics simulations of protein (PDB 5EQG) and their highest affinity ligand using Gromacs 2023.1 software. Input files for these simulations were constructed with the CHARMM-GUI platform utilizing the CHARMM36m force field for proteins and ions and the TIP3P model for water. Energy minimization was conducted with 5000 steps using the steep descent method to achieve stable conformations. Canonical (NVT) and isobaric–isothermal (NPT) ensembles were carried out, maintaining a constant temperature of 300.15 K for 100 ps in the NVT ensemble, followed by 100 ps at 300.15 K and 1 atm in the NPT ensemble. Subsequently, a 30,000 ps molecular dynamics simulation was executed. Initially, the system was minimized and equilibrated with restraints on the ligand, its heavy atoms, and the protein backbone atoms, leading to the production runs. The Verlet cutoff scheme was implemented, with a grid box and an nstlist of 10, and rcoulomb and rvdw set at 1.0 nm. Energy and log files were saved every 10.0 ps throughout the simulation, which comprised 15 million steps over 30,000 ps (30 ns). Based on the averages of these results, longer molecular dynamics simulations of 100 ns are recommended for future studies. The RMSD, RMSF, and radius of gyration (Rg) were calculated using the g-rmsd, g-rmsf, and g-Rg tools, respectively. Snapshots were generated at 5000 ps intervals using the g-trjconv command. RMSD and RMSF plots were created with the QtGrace v 0.2.6 program.

Results

Isolation of Compounds

Myrrhanone B (MN) was obtained as a colorless oil from the hexane n-hexane fraction of C. mukul, having the molecular formula C30H48O4 as determined by the molecular ion peak at m/z 495.3469 [M + Na]+ through HR-ESI-MS (calcd. for C30H48NaO4, 495.3450). The 1H and 13C NMR spectra of MN revealed signals due to three trisubstituted olefins at δH 5.12, 5.14, and 6.81, seven tertiary methyls along with a quaternary carbon bearing carboxyl carbon (δC 172.1), an oxygenated functionality (δC 74.1), and a carbonyl carbon (δC 217.0) at C-3. Based on these, the structure was identified as myrrhanone B. , Similarly, myrrhanol B (ML) was also reported as a colorless oil with the molecular formula C30H50O4, determined by the molecular ion peak observed at m/z 497.3704 [M + Na]+ via HR-ESI-MS analysis (calcd. for C30H50NaO4, 497.3607). The 1H NMR spectra of ML showed signals of methane bearing an oxygen functionality at δH 3.39, seven tertiary methyls, a carboxyl group at δC 171.8, and three trisubstituted olefins at δH 5.10, 5.13, and 6.78 together with a quaternary carbon bearing an oxygen functionality at δC 74.6 (C-8). The NMR and mass spectrometry data completely resemble with the published literature. , The functional groups in both compounds were confirmed by 2D NMR techniques including HMBC, HSQC, and COSY (see NMR data and spectra in the Supporting Information, Figures S3 and S4).

Effects of CMEE MN and ML on Cell Proliferation

Treatment with CMEE (up to 150 μg/mL) significantly reduced the proliferation of MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells in a time- and dose-dependent manner over 24 and 48 h (Figure ). Similarly, both ML and MN exhibited strong antiproliferative effects across all tested concentrations in both breast cancer cell lines (Figure ). The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values for MDA-MB-231 cells at 48 h were determined to be 30 μg/mL for CMEE, 18 μM for MN, and 23 μM for ML. Meanwhile, for MCF-7 cells, a slight increase in the IC50 values for CMEE (40 μg/mL), MN (22 μM), and ML (27 μM), at 48 h was observed. In contrast, the nontumorigenic MCF-10A cells remained largely unaffected by treatment at up to 100 μg/mL of CMEE, with only a slight reduction in proliferation observed at the highest concentration (150 μg/mL). Notably, ML and MN did not significantly alter the proliferation of MCF-10A cells at any tested concentration (Figure S5), highlighting their selective cytotoxicity toward cancerous cells.

Effects of CMEE, MN, and ML on Apoptosis

The proapoptotic effects of CMEE, MN, and ML were assessed in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells at their respective IC50 concentrations. The results revealed a substantial increase in apoptotic cell populations compared to that of the control group. In MDA-MB-231 cells, CMEE induced apoptosis in 45.6% of cells, followed by MN (29.3%) and ML (21.0%) (Figure A). A similar trend was observed in MCF-7 cells, where 31.5% of cells underwent apoptosis following CMEE treatment, while MN and ML induced apoptosis in 23.1 and 15.8% of cells, respectively (Figure B). These findings highlight the strong proapoptotic activity of CMEE, MN, and ML, with CMEE demonstrating the highest potency. The significant increase in apoptotic cell populations suggests that these natural compounds effectively disrupt cancer cell survival, reinforcing their potential as promising anticancer agents.

Effects of CMEE, MN, and ML on Caspase-3 Activation

To determine whether apoptosis induced by CMEE and its bioactive compounds, MN and ML, occurs through the caspase-dependent pathway, we evaluated caspase-3 activation in breast cancer cells. Treatment with CMEE, MN, and ML at their respective IC50 concentrations led to a significant increase in caspase-3 activity in both MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells compared to control cells (Figure ). The positive control, staurosporine, also exhibited a substantial increase in caspase-3 activity, validating the assay’s reliability. This heightened enzymatic activity strongly indicates that apoptosis is mediated through the caspase-3-dependent pathway, a crucial mechanism in programmed cell death.

Effects of CMEE, MN, and ML on the ROS Level

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) play a crucial role in cellular homeostasis, and excessive ROS accumulation can induce oxidative stress, leading to apoptosis and impaired cell proliferation. To assess whether CMEE, MN, and ML mediate their cytotoxic effects through oxidative stress, we measured the intracellular ROS levels in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells following treatment. Treatment with IC50 concentrations of CMEE, MN, and ML resulted in a significant increase in ROS levels in both MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells compared to control cells (Figure ). The positive control, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 100 μM), significantly increased ROS levels (p < 0.001), validating the assay’s sensitivity.

Effects of CMEE, MN, and ML on Glucose Uptake and Lactate Production

Cancer cells rely on elevated glucose uptake and lactate production to sustain their rapid proliferation and survival. Targeting glycolysis by inhibiting glucose uptake and lactate production can disrupt metabolic homeostasis, impairing cancer cell growth and death. To explore the antiglycolytic potential of CMEE and its bioactive compounds, MN and ML, we assessed their impact on glucose uptake and lactate production in breast cancer cells. The results revealed a significant reduction in glucose uptake in both MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells following treatment with CMEE, MN, and ML at their respective IC50 concentrations (Figure A,B). This inhibition was comparable to that observed with glycolysis inhibitor apigenin (100 μM), serving as a positive control. Similarly, lactate production was markedly reduced in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells treated with CMEE, MN, and ML (Figure C,D), further supporting their role in disrupting glycolytic metabolism. This effect mirrored the response to sodium oxamate (130 μM), which is a known lactate dehydrogenase inhibitor.

Molecular Docking Results

Molecular docking analysis was used to explore the potential binding interactions of MN and ML with the GLUT1 receptor as a probable drug target. Both the molecules showed excellent complementarity with the binding site of the selected target (Figure ) with highly negative docking scores and high binding interactions. The predicted binding energies (Table ) suggest favorable interactions between these triterpenoids and GLUT1. A detailed in silico interaction analysis indicated that MN may form multiple stabilizing hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts with the transporter. The ring carbonyl moiety of MN hydrogen-bonds with the side chains of Gln283 and Asn288 while the ring hydroxyl group interacts with the Glu380 side chain through a hydrogen bond. Additionally, Trp388 mediates hydrophobic interactions with the alkyl chain of the molecule. Because of these excellent interactions, the molecule is nicely fitted in the binding cavity of the receptor (Figure ). Similarly, the ring hydroxyl groups of ML showed hydrogen bonding with the side chains of Gln283, Asn288, and Glu380 (Figure ). The binding mode and docking score of compounds were compared with that of a cocrystallized ligand (5RE), which exhibited a docking score of −9.04 kcal/mol, slightly less than the docking scores of ML and MN. Moreover, 5RE mediates hydrogen bonds with the side chains of Gln282 and Trp388 via its carbonyl moiety, whereas Ile164 provides hydrophobic interaction to the 5RE. The docking scores of ML (−9.66 kcal/mol) and MN (−9.41 kcal/mol) reflect excellent binding potential of molecules with GLUT1, which can be a probable drug target for these molecules. While these computational findings are promising, further studies involving kinetic analyses and biochemical assays using purified GLUT1 protein are required to validate these interactions.

Molecular Dynamics Studies

To explore the temporal stability of the complexes formed between the natural products MN, ML, and GLUT1 protein, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of 100 ns were conducted. Initially, root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) analysis was performed to evaluate structural fluctuation over time, where higher RMSD values typically indicate reduced complex stability. , During the first 10 ns, no significant differences were observed between the GLUT1 complexes with MN, ML, and the cocrystallized (native) ligand (Figure A). Between 10.6 and 11.82 ns, the GLUT1-ML complex exhibited higher RMSD values (1.33–1.69 nm) compared to the GLUT1-MN (0.89–1.00 nm) and GLUT1-native (0.83–0.95 nm) complexes. From 12.9 ns onward, the native ligand complex displayed higher RMSD values (1.20–2.25 nm), while MN and ML complexes remained relatively stable. However, starting at 58.17 ns, the GLUT1-ML complex showed pronounced structural deviations, reaching RMSD values of 2.08–2.98 nm, compared to the GLUT1-MN (1.23–1.91 nm) and GLUT1-native ligand complex (1.33–2.28 nm). These findings suggest that the GLUT1-MN complex may exhibit a more stable binding mode throughout the simulation, while the GLUT1-ML complex shows greater instability.

(A) RMSD, (B) RMSF, and (C) Rg plots of the GLUT1 complexed with MN, ML, and the native ligand, plotted during 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations.

RMSF (root-mean-square fluctuation) analysis was then used to assess the flexibility of the residues within each complex over time. Higher RMSF values indicate increased local flexibility. The GLUT1-MN complex showed the highest RMSF values (0.50–0.68 nm) compared to the other two complexes (Figure B). This may suggest that MN binding induces flexibility in certain regions of GLUT1, possibly through interactions involving its aliphatic chain, enabling better accommodation within the binding cavity. Finally, the radius of gyration (Rg) was analyzed to assess the compactness of the protein–ligand complexes. The GLUT1-MN complex exhibited higher Rg values (2.26–2.30 nm), suggesting reduced compactness compared to the GLUT1-ML (2.24–2.28 nm) and GLUT1-native ligand (2.10–2.24 nm) complexes (Figure C). This reduced compactness could reflect increased flexibility or partial opening of hydrophobic pockets, a phenomenon often associated with inhibition through solvation effects.

Discussion

Commiphora mukul has recently been the focus of attention for its chemopreventive and chemotherapeutic potential for cancer. This resinous plant contains various bioactive compounds that have been proven to be effective in cancer therapy in different varieties of cancer by targeting different pathways such as modulation of NF-κB, MAPK, and β-catenin signaling, − angiogenesis, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) pathways. Among its bioactive constituents, the dammarane-type triterpenes myrrhanone B (MN) and myrrhanol B (ML) have exhibited antidiabetic, antioxidant, and antiproliferative activities. Given the reliance of malignant cells on glycolysis for energy production and survival, metabolic reprogramming represents a promising therapeutic target. In this context, the present work explored the potential effects of CMEE and its bioactive compounds, MN and ML, on glycolytic metabolism and apoptosis in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 breast cancer cell lines.

Uncontrolled proliferation is a defining characteristic of cancer cells, enabling their rapid and sustained growth. In this study, a preliminary MTT assay showed that ML and MN did not show any significant inhibitory effects on the MCF-10A cells. Previous studies have reported that normal mammary epithelial cells (HMEC), normal prostate epithelial cell line (PrEC), and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PMBC) are more resistant to growth inhibition by bioactive constituents from C. mukul. However, the proliferation of MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells was significantly reduced with all tested treatments of CMEE and its bioactive triterpenes, MN and ML, in a concentration- and time-dependent manner. The IC5 0 values at 48 h further confirmed the potent cytotoxic effects of these compounds, suggesting their potential as antiproliferative agents. These findings align with previous reports indicating that C. mukul and its metabolites (MN and ML) exhibit substantial cytotoxicity against MDA-MB-231 cells. Additionally, independent studies have demonstrated that C. mukul extract significantly inhibits the proliferation of both MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells in a dose-dependent manner. This observed decrease in the cell proliferation of cancer cells might be caused by cell death induced by apoptosis. Further analysis through FITC-annexin V/PI staining confirmed that the reduction in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cell viability upon treatment with CMEE, MN, and ML was due to apoptosis rather than necrosis, as evidenced by flow cytometric analysis. Interestingly, the apoptosis analysis showed that CMEE increased the apoptotic response more than did ML and MN, which could be attributed to the synergistic effects of bioactive compounds present in the extract. Meanwhile, ML and MN also showed increased early-stage apoptosis in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells.

Biological evidence suggests that glucose and other factors related to glucose may contribute to breast cancer development. Furthermore, a higher rate of glucose uptake/consumption is needed by rapidly proliferating cells to support de novo biosynthesis of nucleotides, lipids, amino acids, and NADPH through various metabolic pathways. Since glucose is vital to the proliferation of cancer cells, this study explored the impact of CMEE and its active components, MN and ML, on glucose metabolism in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells. As increased glucose uptake and lactate production are the key characteristic activities of glucose metabolism in cancer cells, we therefore measured the effects of CMEE, MN, and ML on both of these important glycolytic functions. Our findings demonstrated that treatment with CMEE, MN, and ML at their respective IC50 concentrations significantly inhibited glucose uptake in both MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells. The glucose uptake assay revealed a decrease in glucose consumption. Our findings from the molecular docking and dynamics simulations showed strong interactions and stable binding modes of ML and MN with GLUT1, suggesting that these treatments impair glucose transport mechanisms, potentially through modulation of GLUT1 function. Furthermore, the inhibition of glucose uptake was accompanied by a marked decrease in lactate production following treatments of CMEE, MN, and ML, indicating a disruption in glycolytic flux. The reduction in lactate production further supports the hypothesis that CMEE, MN, and ML suppress aerobic glycolysis, which is a critical feature of cancer cell metabolism. These results collectively suggest that targeting glucose metabolism could be the mechanism underlying the anticancer effects of CMEE and its bioactive components.

One of the key initiators of the apoptotic pathway is intracellular ROS. Previous research established that inhibition of glycolysis leads to ROS-mediated apoptosis. , It is well-established that increased intracellular ROS-induced apoptosis involves the activation of the proapoptotic proteins’ caspase-3. , Our findings from ROS analysis demonstrated that CMEE, MN, and ML significantly increased intracellular ROS levels in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells. These results from the ROS assay, in conjunction with apoptosis assay findings, suggest that CMEE and its bioactive compounds initiate apoptosis in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells via caspase-3 activation. This was further confirmed by an ELISA-based caspase-3 activity assay, which showed increased caspase-3 activation following treatment with CMEE, MN, and ML. The ability of these phytomolecules to activate the apoptotic pathway highlights their potential as candidates for cancer therapy.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that myrrhanol B (ML) and myrrhanone B (MN), isolated from C. mukul, exhibit strong anticancer potential against MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 breast cancer cells. Functional assays showed significant reductions in glucose uptake and lactate production, indicating glycolysis inhibition. The mechanism for reduction in glucose uptake, predicted by molecular docking and molecular dynamics studies, indicates high binding affinity of compounds for GLUT1. Furthermore, MN and ML induced apoptosis, increased caspase-3 activity, and elevated ROS levels, highlighting oxidative stress as a key mechanism of cytotoxicity. These findings suggest that MN and ML disrupt cancer metabolism and promote apoptosis, making them promising candidates for breast cancer therapy.
