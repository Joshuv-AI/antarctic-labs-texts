Splenic sequestration crisis in children with sickle cell disease in the Eastern region of Saudi Arabia
Authors: Abdalla Mohamed Zayed, Sulaiman Almohaimeed, Turki Alotaibi, Hossam Aldosari, Tahani Alotaibi, Basheer Ahmed, Khalid Abdullah, Yasser Awadallah, Shangrila Joy Ancheta, Zechariah Jebakumar Arulantham, Taha Khattab, Anfal Alrwili, Asalah Alhazmi, Eman Al Ghamdi, Shadin Alamrah

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute splenic sequestration crisis (ASSC) is a potentially life-threatening complication of sickle cell disease (SCD). Previous studies have shown that patients who carry the African sickle gene haplotypes have more severe SCD than those with the Asian haplotype. In Saudi Arabia (SA), people living in the eastern region are known to carry the Asian haplotype, whereas patients in the southwestern (SW) region are more likely to carry the African haplotype. The aim of this study was to determine the prevalence and clinical features of ASSC among children with SCD who originated from both regions but were living in the same environment in the eastern region of that country.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 340 children with SCD who visited our hospital from 2010 to 2020. The collected clinical data of patients with ASSC were compared between two groups of patients: eastern and SW children.ResultsOver the study period, out of 340 children with SCD, 50 patients had ASSC, with a male/female ratio of 2.1:1. There were 38 children from the SW region and 12 from the eastern region. The overall prevalence of ASSC was 14.7%, with no significant difference between the two groups (p = .56).The SW group was diagnosed with SCD at a younger age than their eastern peers [median (IQR)]: 8.5 (6–11) vs. 30.5 (24–36) months; p < .001.The median (IQR) steady state hemoglobin (HB) level in the SW group was significantly lower than that in the eastern group [8 (7.5–9) vs. 9 (8–10) gm/dL]; p = .001].During the first instance of episode of ASSC, there was a significant difference between the SW group and the eastern group in the following parameters [median (IQR)]: age [25 (12–48) v. 72 (39–134) months, p = .001], HB [5.1 (4.4–5.9) v. 6.1 (5.9–6.4) gm/dL, p = .01], splenic size on admission [5.5 (3–8) v. 8 (6-9.5) cm, p = .01], and splenic size upon discharge [3 (2–5) v. 5 (3.5–5.5) cm below the costal margin, p = .02]. Background

Sickle cell disease is one of the most common and severe hereditary blood disorders worldwide [1]. ASSC is a potentially life-threatening complication of SCD, and it usually occurs in young patients under 5 years of age [2]. Patients with a major episode may present with an acute decreases in HB levels greater than 2 g/dL below the steady state, accompanied by hypovolemic shock and splenomegaly [3]. Minor attacks also occur frequently and are characterized by a moderate increase in the splenic size as well as a decrease in the HB levels of 2–3 g/dL from the steady state. The episodes tend to recur at a high rate that may reach 50–78% after the first incident [4]. Historically, ASSC was the second leading cause of death among young children with SCD, with a mortality rate ranging from 12 to 44% in the 1980s. However, over the past forty years, mortality rates have decreased dramatically to less than 1% [3]. Nevertheless, ASSC remains an important cause of morbidity and mortality in individuals with SCD in developing countries [5].

Saudi Arabia is one of the countries with the highest incidence of SCD worldwide [6]. In this country, the prevalence of SCD is highest in the eastern region, followed by the SW region, with two major clinical and hematological phenotypes. SCD patients originating from the eastern region are known to have a mild form of the disease because they carry mainly the Asian HBB haplotype. In the SW region, SCD patients predominantly carry the African haplotype (Benin), which leads to more severe disease [7]. Given that SCD phenotypes vary widely between different regions of Saudi Arabia, the prediction of disease severity will aid in the risk stratification of patients and the development of appropriate plans for management and counseling.

Information about the variability in SCD phenotypes in SA has been based mainly on independent studies conducted in each region. Few studies have examined ASSC in SCD patients, particularly in Saudi Arabia, where the prevalence of this complication may be underestimated [6, 8, 9]. The aim of this study was to determine the prevalence and clinical characteristics of ASSC among children living in the eastern regions of SA. Therefore, this retrospective cross-sectional study examined a cohort of patients with SCD and compared ASSC characteristics between two groups of children from eastern and SW Saudi Arabia living in the same eastern region of SA.

Methods

This retrospective cohort examined children who were diagnosed with SCD (HBSS/HBSβ0 thalassemia phenotypes) and followed up at King Fahad Military Medical Complex (KFMMC), Dhahran, SA. The hospital provides medical care for military personnel and their dependents living in the eastern region of SA. The patients are either indigenous or originated from the SW area. The cohort included 340 patients with SCD: 247 (73%) from the SW region and 93 (27%) from the eastern region. A total of 50 children (38 from the SW region and 12 from the eastern region) had at least one ASSC. The reason for this discrepancy in number is that many eastern patients prefer to receive medical care in other hospitals near their residences in the eastern region. The inclusion period was from January 2010–December 2020. The medical records of the patients were reviewed, and the data were collected via case report forms. Notably, some of our patients had episodes of ASSC or other SCD complications that were occasionally treated in other hospitals. They received medical reports from these hospitals, including details of their medical status and management. The information from these reports was documented in their files upon arrival at our hospital.

The original province of the patients was obtained from the family name and confirmed by their ID copy attached to their file. Saudi society is mainly tribal, where the family name indicates the person’s ancestral origin. There is a lack of admixture between the tribal groups because of their conservative attitudes toward intermarriage. This resulted in a high prevalence of consanguineous marriages in this country [10].

Both groups of children were dependents of military personnel who were living in the same environment in the Saudi eastern region. They shared geographic environments, including altitudes, deserts, storms and climates (temperature, air quality, humidity, rainfall, and wind speed), exposure to infection, and medical care.

The hemoglobin (HB) genotype was determined via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) electrophoresis using Tosoh’s HPLC system (Tosoh Bioscience), and the patient’s HB and hematological indices were determined with an automated Coulter counter.

ASSC is defined as acute enlargement of the spleen with a decrease in the HB level of at least 20 g/l from the baseline level and a normal or increased basal reticulocyte count [11]. This complication is associated with an increase of at least 2 cm in palpable spleen size, accompanied by an increase in the reticulocyte count [12]. Vaso-occlusive and hemolytic crises occur independently or in association with ASSC. Hemolytic crisis was defined as an acute exacerbation of hemolysis with a significant drop in hemoglobin levels, resulting in severe anemia, jaundice, and elevated levels of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and bilirubin. During the study period, the KFMMC adopted a strategy to manage ASS following the Evidence-Based Management of Sickle Cell Disease Expert Panel Report [13].

In our study, the following information was collected from medical records:

Demographic and baseline data: date of birth (age), sex, consanguinity, province of origin, glucose-6-phosphate-dehydrogenase (G6PD) status, splenic size (if palpable before the first episode of ASSC), HB level, reticulocytes, and initial HB electrophoresis.

In this study, the spleen was considered enlarged if it was documented as palpable and measured in centimeters below the left costal margin on the midclavicular line. The steady-state splenic size was the average of three measurements taken during three routine clinic visits. The steady-state hematological data, including hemoglobin (HB), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), and reticulocyte percentage (Retic%), were calculated from the average of three complete blood counts (CBCs) during routine clinic follow-up visits.

Importantly, some children received hydroxyurea, but unfortunately, there were no data about the timing, compliance, and maximum tolerated dose.

Statistical analysis

In this study, patients with SCD were divided into two groups based on the presence or absence of ASSC. Patients with ASSC were further categorized into 2 subgroups on the basis of the province of origin: SW and eastern. The quantitative data are presented as the means (± SDs), medians (IQRs), and graphs were constructed using IBM SPSS version 28. For categorical data, comparisons between groups were performed via the chi-square test; however, if one or more cell frequencies in the contingency table were less than 5, Fisher’s exact test was used. For normally distributed continuous data, we used Student’s t test for comparisons; for nonnormally distributed continuous data, the Wilcoxon Mann‒Whitney U test was used for comparisons. The purposeful selection process was carried out beginning with univariate analysis to identify factors that were correlated with ASS. This was based on the Wald test from logistic regression, and the cutoff point of the p value was 0.25 [14]. Multivariate logistic regression analysis, expressed as the adjusted odds ratio (OR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI), was then conducted using all significant parameters identified in the univariate analysis. In all the statistical tests, the P value was considered statistically significant if it was < 0.05.

Results

The overall prevalence of ASS was 14.7%, with 15.4% in the SW group and 12.9% in the eastern group; the difference was not statistically significant (p =.56). The patients with ASSC received supportive care during the episodes, including small aliquots of PRBCs, followed by offering a temporizing blood transfusion program. Eighteen (36%) patients received a chronic blood transfusion, which started after the first episode of ASSC. Some patients from the SW had occasional episodes of sequestration while on the program, which could explain the difference in the number of transfusions; however, the difference was not statistically significant. The program was irregular for some children from the SW, as their families had to move between east and SW of the country from time to time (for vacations). After the age of five, 10 (20%) children had not experienced any new ASSC, possibly because of splenic atrophy, and 15 patients (30%) had no ASSC but maintained a persistently enlarged spleen. A third group (25 children, 50%) continued to have recurrent sequestration until surgical splenectomy was performed.

Three patients with ASSC from the SW group presented with hypovolemic shock, one of whom developed acute chest syndrome and stroke within 24 h. One patient from the eastern group presented with fainting and hypotension as the ASSC commenced. No one died due to ASSC at KFMMC during this study.

The most common symptoms accompanying ASSC were fever (23 patients; 46%) and pallor (22 patients; 44%). Five (10%) patients presented with other symptoms, including abdominal pain, cough, and diarrhea.

The neonatal sickle cell screening program was not implemented in our hospital during the study period. SCD was diagnosed when the patient presented with the first symptom or when the parents were concerned because of a positive family history of the disease. Table 5 describes the characteristics of the 1 st episode of ASSC among the children in both groups. There were significant differences between the two groups in the following parameters: age at both SCD diagnosis and at onset of the 1 st episode of ASSC, HB in the steady state and during the 1 st episode of ASSC, splenic size on admission for the 1 st episode and upon discharge after stabilization of the patient, and the number of ASSC relapses. The results also revealed a greater HBS and reticulocyte percentage in the SW group, whereas HBF, steady-state splenic size and the interval between the recurrent episodes were greater in the Eastern group. Discussion

Few studies have examined ASSC in the eastern, western and SW regions of SA, where SCD is most prevalent [8, 9, 15, 16]. The aim of this study was to determine the prevalence, clinical features and hematological features of ASSC in two groups of children with SCD originating from the eastern and SW regions of SA.

The analysis revealed that ASSC was common and associated with prior splenomegaly and hemolytic crises among these children. There was no significant difference in prevalence between the two groups; however, compared with their peers from eastern SA, children from the SW region were diagnosed with SCD at a younger age, had a lower steady-state HB and experienced more severe ASSC episodes.

The overall prevalence of ASSC in the current study was 14.7% in SCD patients, consistent with the rates observed in previous reports from different countries (1–35%) [4, 8, 11, 16, 17] and in a recent study in SA [18]. In accordance with other studies, the prevalence was greater among SCD children with the HBS/β-thalassemia genotype than among those with homozygous SS disease [4, 19, 20]. The reason for this difference is unknown; however, some authors have suggested that HBS/β-thalassemia patients may have maintained some of the splenic function necessary for sequestration to occur [4].

ASSC associations

The risk factors for ASSC in children with SCD have not been widely studied. Khaled et al. [4] reported that baseline splenomegaly, age less than 2 years, chronic pallor at SCD diagnosis, and reticulocytes > 300,000/mm3 were predisposing factors. In the present study, univariate analysis revealed that male sex, the HBS/β-thalassemia genotype, lower steady-state HB, more VOC, and hemolytic crises and a history of splenomegaly were associated with the occurrence of ASSC. However, in the multivariate analysis, only hemolytic crisis and prior splenomegaly were significant predictors of this complication. The prevalence of splenomegaly in SCD varies based on genetic and environmental factors [21]. In areas where the prevalence of splenomegaly is high (> 50%), it is considered a risk factor for the occurrence and recurrence of ASSC [4, 17].

Early use of HU may help preserve splenic function for a longer period and delay the onset of ASS [2]. HU was used by some of our patients, but the analysis revealed no association with the occurrence of ASSC, and there was no significant difference in the frequency of its use between SW children and eastern children who had ASSC. Unfortunately, there were no reliable data regarding the starting date, duration and maximum tolerated dose (MTD).

ASSC among children from eastern SA

Saudis from the eastern region and Indians with SCD carry the Asian (Arab Indian) haplotype of the disease [6]. This may explain the similarity in the prevalence rates of ASSC between children in our study and Indian patients [5, 19, 22]. On the other hand, the rate of occurrence of ASSC in our children from eastern SA was higher than that previously reported in two studies in the same region, one conducted in children younger than four years [10] and the other in adults [23]. The difference in patient age could explain the discrepancy in prevalence.

Studies have shown that the clinical features of SCD are relatively mild in places where the Asian haplotype is predominant [7]. This could explain the later diagnosis of SCD and later onset of ASSC in eastern patients in the current analysis. The exact reason for this phenotypic variation is not fully understood, but it can be partly attributed [24] to higher levels of HBF associated with the Asian haplotype of the sickle gene [25]. HBF was greater in children from eastern SA than in children from southwestern SA, although the difference was not statistically significant. However, the sample size of children from eastern SA was small. Interestingly, the splenic enlargement characteristic of these episodes was more pronounced in eastern patients. Unlike patients from SW Saudi Arabia with SCD, who usually undergo early auto-splenectomy, patients from the eastern region experience a unique late persistence of splenomegaly, possibly preserving some splenic function [25].

The sequestration attacks were relatively mild among children from eastern SA, consistent with previous studies on similar populations in the eastern region (Table 6) [9, 15]. Additionally, ASSC is considered an important SCD complication in Indians, but the details of its prevalence and natural history are lacking [5].

ASSC among children from southwestern SA

The prevalence of ASSC among children in the SW region with SCD who were living in the eastern region during the study period was 15.5%, consistent with earlier studies reporting a prevalence of 7–31% in children living in the western and SW regions [8, 16, 26]. The variability in prevalence among children from southwestern SA living in different regions of the country could be attributed to possible variations in genetic mutations and/or environmental factors. The eastern region is approximately 1500 km from the country west.

Previous research has shown that SCD manifestations are usually severe among patients with the African haplotype of the sickle gene, mainly in Africa and west/southwest of SA [6]. Our study revealed that the 1 st splenic sequestration was severe among children from southwestern SA. These sequestrations occurred at a young age with a low steady-state HB, which decreased to lower levels during the episodes and, on three occasions, were accompanied by circulatory collapse. Additionally, the rate of crisis recurrence was high. The early diagnosis of SCD in children from southwestern SA could have contributed to the early onset of ASSC among this group. Table 7 shows that features of the 1 st episode of ASSC in our children from southwestern SA are comparable to the available data in previous studies on similar populations but in their original provinces (western and SW regions) [8]. They are also similar to episodes that occur in SCD patients of African origin in other countries, including Jamaica [5], Tunisia [4] and France [11].

Although G6PD deficiency is relatively common, its impact on patients with SCD is still controversial. While some researchers have reported that it increases the frequency and severity of acute anemic episodes, including ASS [27], others have reported no clinical effects [28]. In the present study, there was no significant difference in ASSC occurrence between the eastern and SW groups. However, the number of G6PD-deficient patients was small.

ASSC recurrence

In accordance with other studies [4, 11], the ASSC relapse rate among our patients was high (78%). In contrast, in older studies, the recurrence rate was only 49–50% [8, 29]. This difference was believed to be due to increased rates of survival after the first ASSC and increased numbers of diagnosed patients [11]. The risk factors for ASSC recurrence have not yet been established. In our analysis, the only factor significantly associated with recurrence was steady-state HB. Brousse et al. [11] reported a lower risk when the first episode occurred after 2 years of age. In another study, a baseline splenic size ≥ 3 cm and the age of the 1 st episode of ASSC between 2 and 5 years were predictive [4].

Compared with genetically similar SCD patients in the northern hemisphere, patients with SCD have a much more severe clinical phenotype in most African countries. This is likely related to environmental factors, including climate, infections, and access to health care [30]. In the present study, SW patients (African haplotype) had more severe ASSC than children from eastern SA (Asian haplotype) living in the same environment. Accordingly, this difference could be related to genetic factors affecting the sickling process. This finding may support the theory of El Mouzan et al. [10] that SW children retain the severity of their African haplotype of the sickle gene despite living in an environment of mild SCD. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as although our patients share many environmental factors, other factors, such as the home environment, exercise, life conditions, family income, and many others, may have an impact.

Limitations of the study and future directions

There are several limitations to this study. First, the retrospective nature of the study made it difficult to collect all the important data, such as those concerning hydroxyurea use. Second, this was a single-center study with a small number of patients, particularly patients from the eastern region, thus limiting its generalizability. Third, there is a paucity of literature on ASSC, particularly Saudi studies. Some of our patients might have had episodes of ASSC that were occasionally treated in other hospitals with potential bias due to differences in the managing health care providers, laboratory equipment, etc. Further prospective multicenter studies in different regions of SA with more representative samples are needed to enhance our understanding of the natural history of ASSC in children with SCD. This will be helpful in developing appropriate care models.

Conclusions

ASSC is common in children with SCD living in the eastern region of SA, but the prevalence was not significantly different between SW children (African haplotype group) and their eastern peers (Asian haplotype group). The episodes appeared more severe in the SW group, despite the children living in the eastern region of Saudi Arabia.
