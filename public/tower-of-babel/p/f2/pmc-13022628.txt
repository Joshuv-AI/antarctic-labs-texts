Characterization of external defibrillator output and its impact on defibrillation protection of medical equipment
Authors: Dian Zhang, Jin Deng, Yong Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDefibrillators constitute a critical component of emergency care for ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia. Advances in defibrillation technology point to the potential need for reassessing current requirements for defibrillation protection and associated resuscitation guidelines.ObjectiveThis study aimed to establish reference information for future directions in defibrillator requirements, defibrillation protection requirements, and resuscitation guidelines based on the parameters of commercially available defibrillators.MethodsDefibrillator brands were identified from the registration records of the China National Medical Products Administration, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, and the European Database on Medical Devices. Key defibrillation parameters were obtained from publicly available manufacturer documentation.ResultsA total of 31 manufacturers and 115 external defibrillators were identified. All studied devices employ biphasic waveforms, with biphasic truncated exponential being the most common, appearing in products from 24 of 31 manufacturers (77.4%). Except for pulsed biphasic devices manufactured by Schiller, maximum output voltages are below 3 kV. Maximum energy limits vary across devices, with some models retaining an upper limit of 360 J.ConclusionIncorporating biphasic waveforms into defibrillation protection tests may be appropriate. Adjustments to the requirements and guidelines of defibrillation voltage or energy could be considered, depending on market trends and future studies on defibrillation efficacy.

Introduction

Electrical defibrillation is critical in managing sudden cardiac arrest caused by malignant arrhythmias, such as ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia.1, 2 A shock that induces sufficient myocardial depolarization can terminate these arrhythmias and restore sinus rhythm.1 The first direct-current defibrillator was developed by Naum Gurvich in 1939.3 A modern defibrillator analyzes the cardiac rhythm, charges to the set energy, and delivers a controlled transthoracic shock via chest electrodes. Because transthoracic impedance varies between patients, external defibrillators measure impedance through the pads using a short, low-energy pulse before the therapeutic shock. They then adjust voltage and pulse duration to achieve the intended energy delivery.

However, external defibrillator shocks may interfere with or damage other medical devices during concurrent operation, potentially compromising patient and operator safety. Defibrillation protection requirements were introduced to mitigate this risk. Throughout the 20th century, early defibrillators widely employed monophasic waveforms (e.g., damped sine waves), and protection standards were therefore based on the performance specifications of these devices. In the early 21st century, biphasic defibrillators gradually replaced monophasic devices.4 Additionally, biphasic waveforms have been adopted for testing protection of the active implantable medical devices against external defibrillator damage since 2014.5

These evolutions have raised concerns that existing standards and resuscitation guidelines may require revision. In the existing literature, systematic analysis of defibrillator output parameters remains relatively limited, with a comprehensive study published in 2013 including 18 defibrillators.6 Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the performance characteristics of currently available external defibrillators and compare them with the requirements for defibrillators and defibrillation protection, in order to examine whether the test waveform and test voltages in use continue to be appropriate.

Methods

The data collection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. Data extraction was performed by one researcher and independently verified by two additional researchers. Defibrillator brands were identified through systematic searches of the medical device registration databases of the China National Medical Products Administration,7 U.S. Food and Drug Administration,8 and European Database on Medical Devices,9 using the keywords “defibrillator” and “defibrillation”. As this study focused on external defibrillation, implantable cardioverter defibrillators were excluded. Subsequently, three brands (SUPERSTAR MED, Sinopharm Group Co., Ltd., and Creative Medical) were further excluded due to insufficient publicly available information. Finally, 31 manufacturers were included (see Table 1). Products were classified by intended use into automated external defibrillators (AEDs), defibrillator monitors, and wearable cardioverter defibrillators (WCDs). Technical parameters, including operating mode, waveform, defibrillation energy, peak voltage, peak current, and patient impedance, were extracted from product manuals, brochures, official websites, registration databases, and third-party distributors. Defibrillation waveform and energy, which are essential determinants of clinical defibrillation performance, were included in the analysis. In addition, because the peak voltage of the test waveform is a critical parameter for evaluating device safety in defibrillation‑protection testing, the maximum output voltage for each defibrillator was also collected. If documentation specified the peak voltage of a defibrillation pulse, it was taken as the maximum output voltage (Vmax). Otherwise, IEC (International Electrotechnical Committee) standard 60601-2-4 (Clause 201.7.9.3.101) requires manufacturers to provide time–voltage or time–current waveforms,10 from which the maximum voltage was derived. When only peak current was reported, voltage (V) was calculated using Ohm’s law, V=IR, where I represents peak current and R represents patient impedance. For biphasic waveforms, the peak currents of both phases were compared, and the higher value was used for analysis. In this study, Vmax consistently occurred in the first phase. When waveform diagrams lacked numerical values, peaks were estimated from the graphical scales. A detailed table is available in Supplementary Materials, presenting the brands, models, and collected parameters of all defibrillators in this study.

Results

Operating mode

This survey identified 70 AEDs. AEDs can operate in manual, semi‑automatic, or fully automatic modes, and different models may integrate these modes in various configurations based on their design. In manual mode, the energy level is selected by the operator, whereas semi‑automatic and fully automatic modes operate with factory default settings. The distinction between semi-automatic and fully automatic modes lies in whether the user is required to press a shock button to deliver the defibrillation shock. A total of 42 defibrillator monitors were identified. Among the models included in this survey, Philips HeartStart FR3, Mindray BeneHeart D30, and three Corpuls models (Corpuls1, Corpuls3, and Corpuls3T) support both manual defibrillation and AED modes. Meanwhile, the three WCDs—ZOLL LifeVest, Kestra Medical ASSURE WCD System, and Element Science Jewel—are fully automatic.

Defibrillation waveform

In this study, all devices with publicly available information use biphasic waveforms (see Table 1). EMTEL only specifies biphasic waveforms for its DefiMax defibrillator monitors, without detailing the waveshape. Among other manufacturers, biphasic truncated exponential (BTE) waveforms are the most common type (see Fig. 2(a)), appearing in products from 24 of 31 manufacturers. Nihon Kohden's ActiBiphasic is a modified BTE waveform with a power-controlled second phase (see Fig. 2(b)). ZOLL employs BTE waveforms in its LifeVest but uses rectilinear biphasic (RB) waveforms (see Fig. 2(c)) in its AEDs (AED Plus, ZOLL AED 3, AED Pro) and defibrillator monitors (X and R series). Resecond reports its RB waveform is equivalent to ZOLL's. Corpuls employs a “rectangular” waveform in its Corpuls3 series defibrillator monitors. ZOLL’s RB waveform is designed to maintain adequate current at high impedance to support effective defibrillation. In contrast, some other devices adopt current‑control techniques primarily to prevent excessively high currents at low impedance. For example, WEINMANN Emergency and CardiAid utilize current‑limit control in their defibrillator designs, which produces saw‑tooth waveform at higher current levels. Metrax GmbH (part of the Chinese Yuwell Group since 2017) applies current‑limit control in its HeartSave AED series (M250) and X‑series defibrillator monitors, and adopts BTE waveforms in its HeartSave Y series. Except for ZOLL and Metrax GmbH, manufacturers typically employ a single waveform type across their product lines. Pulsed biphasic (PB) waveforms are less common. Only Schiller’s FRED easy AED and DEFIGARD 5000 defibrillator monitors use a PB waveform, termed “Multipulse Biowave” (see Fig. 2(d)). Defibrillation energy

Typical first shock energies are 150 J for adults and 50 J for pediatrics (at 50 Ω). Fixed-energy devices include Philips HeartStart FRx/FR3 AEDs and ViVest PowerBeat X/M series. The ASSURE WCD System operates at a fixed 170 J. Most AEDs escalate energy across successive shocks: Nihon Kohden AEDs deliver 150–200–200 J (adult) and 50–70–70 J (pediatric); M&B Elec AED 7000 uses 150–150–200 J; and ZOLL AEDs deliver 120–150–200 J (adult) and 50–70–85 J (pediatric). IEC 60601-2-4 (Clause 201.12.4.1) limits selectable defibrillation energy to 360 J.10 This limit was historically established for monophasic waveforms. Although biphasic waveforms require less energy to achieve effective defibrillation,11, 12 manufacturers have adopted varying maximum energy settings. For instance, WEINMANN Emergency limits the output of defibrillator monitors to 200 J, ZOLL applies a uniform 200 J limit across both AEDs and monitors, and Nihon Kohden applies 270 J for monitors but restricts AEDs to 200 J. In contrast, manufacturers such as Physio-Control, Innomed, EMTEL, Mindray, and Comen Medical maintain the traditional 360 J maximum to accommodate extreme conditions (e.g., high-impedance patients).

Schiller's Multipulse Biowave, though biphasic, produces exceptionally high Vmax. The FRED easy AED and DEFIGARD 5000 defibrillator monitors reach 4550 V at 175 Ω, corresponding to 150 J. Furthermore, the DEFIGARD 5000′s maximum energy is 180 J. As Zelinka et al. reported, the Schiller FRED achieves a peak current of 37 A at 180 J and 130 Ω,13 corresponding to 4810 V. Thus, the DEFIGARD 5000’s Vmax at 175 Ω may approach 5 kV. In summary, excluding Schiller products, all surveyed defibrillators exhibit Vmax below 3 kV.

With the exception of Schiller devices, all other defibrillators surveyed show Vmax below 3 kV. Defibrillator monitors generally show higher Vmax due to greater maximum energy. The highest reported values include ZOLL X and R series (2730 V), followed by the Physio-Control LIFEPAK 35 (2600 V at 200 Ω) and the Nihon Kohden TEC-5600/8300 series (2415 V). Approximately half of all monitors report values that do not exceed 2.2 kV. Among AEDs, the ZOLL AED Pro shows the highest Vmax at 2125 V (125 Ω load). In comparison, 13 of the remaining 18 devices, including the Philips HeartStart and Physio‑Control LIFEPAK series, have Vmax values below 2 kV. Furthermore, the ASSURE WCD System and Jewel achieve Vmax of 1650 V (150 Ω) and 1520 V (200 Ω), respectively. Manufacturer specifications typically cover a patient impedance range of 25–175 Ω in 25-Ω increments, consistent with IEC 60601-2-4 (Clause 201.7.9.3.101) requirement.10 Some manufacturers extend this range to 200 Ω. Since Vmax generally occurs at high impedance and some documentation reports theoretical values without measurement uncertainty, actual Vmax may be marginally higher but remains well below the 5 kV defibrillation protection test voltage.

Discussion

Biphasic waveforms achieve comparable or superior defibrillation efficacy to monophasic waveforms at lower energy levels,11, 12, 14, 15 thereby reducing energy storage demands and enabling more compact device designs. In addition to BTE, RB, and PB waveforms, other waveforms, such as ascending-ramp biphasic waveforms16 and nanosecond electric shocks,17 have been proposed but remain experimental. The 2010 European Resuscitation Council (ERC) guidelines reported that monophasic defibrillators are no longer manufactured,4 and this edition represents the last ERC guideline to mention monophasic waveforms. Notably, Physio-Control’s LIFEPAK 12 was available in both monophasic and biphasic versions, with the monophasic version discontinued in 2013. Our findings indicate that BTE and RB waveforms are most prevalent among current devices. Their relative efficacy remains debated, with some studies suggesting comparable performance.18 RB waveforms maintain high current at high impedance, which may improve the chance of defibrillation. Consequently, their maximum output voltage is relatively higher.

Despite years of advancement in biphasic waveforms, current standards continue to use monophasic waveforms for defibrillation protection tests, reflecting a conservative worst-case approach. However, biphasic waveforms more accurately reflect contemporary clinical practice and may help reduce the insulation design burden on the device under test, particularly for wearable medical devices. During the transitional period, the residual use of monophasic waveforms in clinical practice is uncertain, while technological development indicates a progressive shift toward biphasic waveforms. Under these circumstances, the inclusion of biphasic waveforms in testing and the separate evaluation of monophasic and biphasic waveforms may be considered. This testing framework is also specified in ISO 14708-1 (Clause 20.2), which is designed for active implantable medical devices.5 Once sufficient evidence supports the complete phase-out of monophasic waveforms, tests can then evolve to include a representative set of biphasic waveforms.

The primary advantage of biphasic defibrillators over monophasic ones is the lower energy required to achieve effective defibrillation. Delivered energy depends on both the magnitude and the duration of current delivery. Different biphasic waveform designs can reduce the required energy through multiple mechanisms. These may include reducing the required current (BTE and RB) or using intermittent current delivery (PB). However, as noted in Section ‘Defibrillation Energy’, some manufacturers still specify an upper energy limit of 360 J. At present, guidance on biphasic defibrillation energy remains broad and is largely determined by manufacturer-specified dosing. The ERC guidelines 202119 recommend 150–360 J for the second and subsequent shocks, thereby implying an upper limit of 360 J for non-first shock. The ERC guidelines 202520 do not define an explicit upper limit but note potential harm with energies above 360 J. The 2020 American Heart Association guidelines,2 however, do not specify a maximum biphasic energy.

Experimental and clinical evidence suggests that higher biphasic energy levels (360 J) are not more harmful than lower levels (150 J) in swine models,21 and patients with ventricular fibrillation benefit from higher-energy biphasic defibrillation during repeated shocks.22 On this basis, the use of 360 J biphasic defibrillation is considered acceptable for non-first shock. However, the relationship between clinical benefits and higher energy of biphasic defibrillation remains uncertain. Although an escalating-energy strategy (200–300–360 J) has demonstrated superior performance compared with a fixed low energy defibrillation in both animal models (150 J) and humans (200 J),23, 24 there is no difference in 30-day survival rate in humans when comparing the escalating-energy (200–200/300–360 J) with fixed high energy (360 J) defibrillation.25 Overall, further clinical evidence is required to clarify the relationship between recommended biphasic defibrillation energy levels and clinical outcomes, thereby informing whether a reduction in the maximum permissible output energy of defibrillators is justified.

IEC 60601-2-4 (Clause 201.12.4.101) specifies that the output voltage of a defibrillator shall not exceed 5 kV when measured at a 175 Ω load.10 This limit is consistent with the 5 kV direct-current voltage defined in the defibrillation protection test circuits of IEC 60601-1 (Clause 8.5.5.1 Figure 9 and Figure 10).26 Particular standards for electrocardiographs (IEC 60601-2-25 Subclause 201.8.5.5.1) states that, when a medical device is positioned on the thorax, in the thorax, or at a predictable electrical midpoint between the defibrillator paddles, the test voltage could be reduced to 3 kV.27 Among the particular standards, only IEC 60601-2-2 (Clause 201.8.5.5) specifies a reduced test voltage of 2 kV for high-frequency surgical equipment and accessories.28 Except for Schiller products (4550 V), the highest value recorded is 2730 V for the ZOLL X and R series, which remains below 3 kV even when a ±5% tolerance is considered.

Higher defibrillation protection test voltages can translate into more demanding insulation and protection design, with implications for device size, complexity, and cost. Reducing the test voltage would allow a corresponding reduction in insulation requirements. For example, lowering the defibrillation voltage from 5 kV to 3 kV would permit the required air clearance, under inhomogeneous field conditions and at altitudes up to 2000 m, to be reduced from 4.0 mm to 2.0 mm according to IEC 60664-1 (Table F.2).29 This reduction would support more compact device designs. It is particularly relevant for space-constrained applications, such as increasingly adopted wearable cardioverter-defibrillators, portable monitors, and emergency devices.

Schiller’s PB waveforms reduce the energy required for defibrillation through pulsed delivery, while maintaining relatively high output voltage. This approach represents another evolution of biphasic waveform technology, which offers greater potential for energy reduction than BTE and RB waveforms. The diversity of biphasic waveform technologies has influenced both standards development and resuscitation guidelines. Notably, the 2021 ERC guidelines included recommendations for PB waveforms for the first time,19 reflecting their increasing clinical relevance. Schiller’s PB waveforms do not confer an advantage in output voltage. However, if future evidence demonstrates their superior defibrillation efficacy and safety, this could support consideration of lower recommended defibrillation energies. Defibrillation testing may therefore incorporate multiple biphasic waveforms, with waveform-specific voltage limits defined accordingly.

The analysis in the present study was based on manufacturer-specified parameters rather than direct measurements. Moreover, variations in the measurement and reporting methodologies adopted by different manufacturers introduce further uncertainty. Future work may build on this approach by incorporating independently measured data across a broader range of brands and models. Another limitation is the focus on currently marketed products, which excludes defibrillators that have been discontinued but may remain in clinical use. Monophasic waveforms have been discontinued for several years and are therefore presumed to be used infrequently. For example, the Physio-Control LIFEPAK 10 received its most recent recall notice in 2008, while the LIFEPAK 12 monophasic was discontinued in 2013. A related concern is that some earlier biphasic models exhibit relatively high maximum output voltages. For instance, the LIFEPAK 12 biphasic, with a Vmax exceeding 3250 V,13 was discontinued in 2016 and ceased to receive maintenance support in 2020. Other products with similar circumstances may exist, although systematic verification is difficult. However, given the ongoing evolution of defibrillator technology, a reduction in defibrillation protection test voltage appears both feasible and justified.

Conclusion

Across commercially available external defibrillators, all evaluated devices employ biphasic waveforms, most commonly BTE. Except for Schiller’s PB devices, maximum defibrillation voltages do not exceed 3 kV. These findings provide reference data to inform future requirements for defibrillation protection and device design considerations.
