Mobile instant messaging supported brief physical exercise intervention for smoking cessation: a community-based, cluster randomised controlled trial
Authors: Sheng Zhi Zhao, Meng Yao Li, Ya Jie Li, Tzu Tsun Luk, Derek Yee Tak Cheung, Agnes Yuen Kwan Lai, Henry Sau Chai Tong, Vienna Wai Yin Lai, Tai Hing Lam, Man Ping Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPhysical exercise has been used to assist smoking cessation, but supervised programmes are bounded by limited scalability and generalisability. Mobile instant messaging (MIM) offers a scalable platform for delivering exercise support with minimal supervision. We assessed the effectiveness of MIM-supported brief physical exercise intervention on smoking cessation.MethodsIn this two-arm, parallel, cluster randomised controlled trial, we recruited daily smokers aged 18 years or older from 70 community sites in Hong Kong from June to October 2022. Sites were randomised (1:1) to either the intervention group (n=492) or control group (n=539). Brief cessation advice, physical exercise instructions and MIM-based practice reminders were offered to the intervention group for 3 months. The primary outcome was biochemically validated 7-day point prevalence abstinence (PPA) at 6 months.ResultsOf the 1031 participants (80.9% male, mean age 44.2 years), 59.8% were followed up at 6 months. Biochemically validated abstinence rates at 6 months were non-significantly higher in the intervention than the control group (10.4% vs 9.1%; risk ratio (RR) 1.14, 95% CI 0.79 to 1.66, p=0.48). Self-reported 7-day PPA was 21.9% and 19.5%, respectively (RR 1.13, 95% CI 0.89 to 1.43, p=0.32). Weekly practice of handgrip and elastic band exercises in the intervention group declined significantly over 6 months (from 10.3 min to 2.5 min and from 9.4 min to 0.8 min; p<0.001). The proportion of participants with moderate to high physical activity levels was quite similar between groups at 6 months (41.1% vs 39.9%; RR 1.05, 95% CI 0.82 to 1.35, p=0.70).ConclusionMIM-supported brief physical exercise intervention did not significantly increase smoking abstinence or physical activity compared with brief cessation advice alone.Trial registration numberNCT05430451.Keywords: Exercise, Smoking cessation, Tobacco control

Introduction

Physical exercise has emerged as a promising adjunctive strategy for smoking cessation (SC)1 2 and is recommended by the current US Clinical Practice Guideline for Treating Tobacco Use and Dependence.3 In persons who smoke, being physically active was associated with intention to quit,4 quit attempt,5 SC6 and relapse prevention.7 8 Many smokers perceived exercise as a useful quitting strategy,9 which effectively reduced cravings, alleviated withdrawal symptoms and improved mood.2 However, a 2019 Cochrane review of 21 randomised controlled trials (RCT) (n=6607) found no evidence that exercise as a standalone or adjunctive SC treatment improves long-term abstinence (6 months or longer) compared with standard support alone (risk ratio (RR) 1.08, 95% CI 0.96 to 1.22).1 Only one RCT showed a significant effect at 6 months in adults with high anxiety sensitivity,10 and another RCT at 12 months in women.11 A 2021 meta-analysis showed that aerobic exercise can increase short-term but not long-term abstinence.12 Another meta-analysis in 2017 on different types of exercise suggested yoga may enhance cessation,13 but it was based on a single RCT of 55 participants.14 The inconsistent trial findings can be attributed to underpowered studies, methodological variations (eg, exercise type and intensity), selective populations (eg, women, youth) and low exercise adherence.

Supervised, group-based or equipment-dependent exercise regimens, such as pedometer-monitored walking, often require frequent or intensive sessions, leading to low attendance and adherence,1 which will be especially challenging for community-dwelling smokers with waning motivation to quit.15 The 2019 Cochrane review on exercise interventions suggested that high-quality trials were typically small scale (sample sizes of 20–330) and focused on motivated, physically inactive smokers.1 Isometric exercise offers a practical alternative for less motivated populations, providing cessation benefits comparable to aerobic exercise while being easier to adopt and sustain.16 The ‘zero-time’ exercise intervention, which requires minimal time or resources, has been shown to significantly improve physical activity,17 reduce insomnia severity,18 enhance mental well-being,19 benefit cardiovascular health20 and promote family health behaviours.21 22 Our pilot RCT exploring brief isometric exercises (eg, handgrip) for SC found that 36% of participants used them during cravings, and higher adherence predicted self-reported abstinence at 6 months (53% vs 23%; OR 3.70, 95% CI 1.15 to 11.92),23 despite overall abstinence rates were not significantly increased. These findings suggest that such simple, low-burden interventions, if effective, would be more practicable for broader application.

Mobile instant messaging (MIM) apps (eg, WeChat, WhatsApp) offer a scalable and cost-effective platform to provide personalised intervention and may overcome barriers associated with traditional exercise interventions.24 25 Integrating mobile health (mHealth) support in exercise interventions enhanced remote supervision, facilitated real-time feedback and improved adherence in participants with cardiovascular disease26 and general populations.27 Higher participant retention rate (88% at 2-month follow-up)28 and increased self-reported exercise time postintervention (mean difference: 48.01 min, p<0.001)29 were observed when personalised message support was provided. Therefore, we tested the effectiveness of a brief physical exercise intervention integrated with real-time chat-based reminders and cessation advice for SC among proactively recruited smokers.

Methods

Study design

This was a pragmatic, community-based, two-arm, cluster RCT nested within the annual ‘Quit to Win’ (QTW) smoke-free community campaign organised by the Hong Kong Council on Smoking and Health.30–32 The trial protocol was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05430451). Written informed consent was collected. The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials flow diagram is presented in figure 1.

The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) flow diagram.

Recruitment and participants

From June to October 2022, recruitment was conducted at 70 community sites across 18 districts of Hong Kong, including shopping malls, smoking hotspots and housing estates. Trained SC ambassadors, consisting mainly of university students, proactively approached smokers, screened for eligibility and obtained written informed consent. All ambassadors attended a half-day training workshop that provided an overview of the study, training in the delivery of baseline interventions, and assessed their knowledge, attitudes and practices prior to participant recruitment. Online supplemental appendix 1 shows the details of the training. A member of the research team supervised recruitment at each site and provided support as needed.

Eligible participants were Hong Kong residents aged 18 years or older who had smoked cigarettes, heated tobacco products or e-cigarettes daily for the past 3 months, verified by an exhaled carbon monoxide concentration exceeding 4 parts per million (ppm) or a saliva cotinine level of 30 ng/mL or higher. Other criteria included intended to quit or reduce smoking, able to read and communicate in Chinese and own a smartphone with instant messaging apps. Individuals with physical or cognitive communication barriers or those participating in other SC programmes or services were excluded.

Randomisation and masking

We randomised 70 community sites (1:1) into the intervention or control group, using random permuted blocks of 2, 4 and 6 to achieve a balanced number of clusters in each group. The cluster RCT design aimed to prevent treatment contamination among participants at the same community site (as smokers often gathered around ashtrays). Intracluster correlation coefficients (ICCs) were considered in the following sample size calculation to account for potential clustering effects. The random allocation sequence was computer generated by an investigator (SZZ) who managed group assignment but was not involved in recruitment. Due to the nature of intervention, masking of the research team and participants was not feasible. Outcome assessors and data analysts were blinded to group allocation.

Interventions

After obtaining written informed consent and baseline assessments, all participants received a health warnings leaflet, a referral card for SC services and a 12-page self-help booklet, with a face-to-face brief SC advice guided by the Ask, Warn, Advice, Refer and Do it again (AWARD) model, as previously described.25 30–32 In addition, those assigned to the intervention group received an integrated intervention comprising brief physical exercise and MIM-based cessation support delivered via WhatsApp over a 3-month period. A handgrip and an elastic band, along with an introductory leaflet, were provided for free with brief instructions following the cessation advice.

Participants in the intervention group were advised to do a set of simple and brief physical exercises, including zero-time exercises, handgrip exercises and resistance exercises. Zero-time exercises are characterised by their ease, enjoyment and effectiveness (3Es), requiring no extra time, money or equipment, and can be performed by anyone, anywhere at any time.17 These exercises include brief periods of hand pulling and pushing (10 s each), seated leg raising (30 repetitions in 10 s) and a 10 s strength exercise. Handgrip exercises involve rapid gripping and releasing to engage the forearm muscles, thereby inducing transient fatigue and soreness that can help distract from smoking urges and keeping the hands occupied to deter cigarette use. Participants were taught to perform 30 grips and releases per hand within a 10 s interval, with progressive increases in frequency and intensity encouraged. Resistance exercises with elastic bands were used to enhance muscle strength, flexibility and balance. Prior studies in older adults33 and women34 have shown that these exercises effectively target various muscle groups, including the arms, chest, back and legs, and can be performed in different positions (eg, standing, seated or lying), serving as a complementary extension to handgrip exercise. Participants performed horizontal and vertical arm extensions with the elastic band, completing 30 repetitions in 10 s, with recommendations to gradually increase the exercise frequency as feasible. Exercise instructions are summarised in online supplemental appendix 2.

To facilitate engagement with the brief physical exercises and support craving management, we delivered a series of reminder messages based on the behaviour change techniques (BCTs)35 with a tapering schedule (two times per week in the first month, reducing to once a week by the third month). These messages provided: (1) demonstration, detailed information and reminder of the use of brief physical exercise; (2) benefits and ways of using moderate/vigorous exercise for SC; (3) encouragement of using brief physical exercise for cravings and withdrawal symptoms management; (4) other symptom management strategies, including the 5Ds (Delay, Distract, Drink water, Deep breathing and Discuss); (5) introduction and assistance on using SC services; and (6) reminders for telephone follow-ups at 1, 2 and 3 months, with an additional reminder at 26 weeks for the 6-month assessment. In addition to the scheduled messages, participants’ responses were followed up in real time during office hours by SC counsellors (eg, research team members), who had at least 1 year of experience in SC research. These counsellors provided individualised, theory-driven behavioural support to encourage the use of brief physical exercise as adjuvant activities to aid SC. To ensure intervention fidelity, counsellors met weekly to review case loads, and all instant messaging dialogues were recorded and monitored. A detailed message plan is provided in online supplemental appendix 3.

Participants in the control group received general cessation advice and reminders for follow-ups at 1, 2, 3 and 6 months via SMS in addition to the first brief SC advice.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was 7-day smoking abstinence at 6 months post-treatment initiation (ie, 3 months after the end of treatment), verified by exhaled carbon monoxide (<4 ppm) and saliva cotinine level of 30 ng/mL or higher.36 The main secondary outcome was validated 7-day abstinence at 3 months post-baseline. Participants received a cash incentive of HK$500 (equivalent to US$64) for each successful validation at 3 and 6 months. Other secondary outcomes included self-reported 7-day point prevalence abstinence (PPA), smoking reduction by at least 50% of baseline cigarette consumption, quit attempts (defined as abstinence for ≥24 hours) and physical exercise level assessed at 3 and 6 months. Physical exercise level was measured by the International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short Form and categorised into low (≤600 metabolic equivalent of task, MET-min/week), moderate (600–3000 MET-min/week) or high (≥3000 MET-min/week). For intervention engagement, participants reported the frequency and duration of practising the simple physical exercises. Online supplemental appendix 4 shows detailed trial procedures and assessments.

Sample size

We estimated the sample size based on findings from our previous QTW trials, which reported a 6-month validated smoking abstinence rate of 5.0% by intention to treat (ITT) in the control group.30 37 A meta-analysis showed that mHealth intervention yielded a relative risk of 1.83 for 6-month validated abstinence,38 and a Cochrane review reported a small effect size (RR 1.08) for exercise interventions combined with SC support. We assumed a combined effect size of 1.98 (1.83×1.08) for the integrated intervention incorporating mHealth support with simple physical exercise. To achieve 80% power and a 5% significance level, 525 participants per group were required. The ICCs were incorporated into the sample size calculations to account for potential clustering effects. Previous trials showed an ICC for 6-month biochemically validated abstinence of <0.001,39 suggesting a negligible design effect. Thus, the total sample size needed is 1050.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata/MP (V.16.0). Primary analyses were performed by ITT, and participants with missing outcome data were considered to have no change in smoking behaviour from baseline. The primary and secondary outcomes were compared between groups using Poisson regression models with robust variance estimator to directly estimate RRs.40 We analysed the quitting outcomes with both unadjusted and adjusted models, which accounted for key predictors of cessation outcomes, including previous quit attempts, intention to quit and nicotine dependence.41 Two sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the findings. (1) First, a complete-case analysis was conducted by excluding participants with missing quitting outcomes. (2) Second, missing abstinence outcomes were imputed using multiple imputations by chained equations, with study group included as a predictor. Estimates were then pooled across 50 imputed datasets using Rubin’s rules. Intervention adherence was analysed using self-reported practising of the brief physical exercises. Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the intervention effect by sex, age, nicotine dependence, past quit attempt, readiness to quit within 30 days, readiness to quit within 60 days and physical activity level at baseline. Multiplicative interactions were assessed by introducing the corresponding interaction terms into the Poisson regression model with robust error variance. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

SC outcomes

Table 2 shows that by ITT analysis, biochemically validated abstinence rate at 6 months in the intervention group was slightly but non-significantly higher than that in the control group (10.4% vs 9.1%; RR 1.14, 95% CI 0.79 to 1.66, p=0.48). The RR of primary outcome was 0.98 (95% CI 0.68 to 1.43, p=0.93) after adjusting baseline characteristics. Similar rates were found at 3 months (9.3% vs 9.3%; RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.69 to 1.48). The self-reported 7-day PPA (21.9% vs 19.5%; RR 1.13, 95% CI 0.89 to 1.43), smoking reduction by ≥50% (14.2% vs 15.6%; RR 0.91, 95% CI 0.68 to 1.22), accumulated quit attempts (64.2% vs 62.3%; RR 1.03, 95% CI 0.94 to 1.13) and the use of SC services (13.6% vs 13.2%; RR 1.03, 95% CI 0.76 to 1.41) showed consistent results by 6 months. Online supplemental appendix 5 presents consistent findings across complete-case analysis and multiple imputation models. The RR for 6-month validated abstinence in the multiple imputation model was higher than in the complete-case model (2.34 vs 1.07), but with a wide 95% CI, and the estimates remained statistically non-significant.

Discussion

This study is the largest physical exercise-based SC trial that evaluated the effectiveness of an MIM-supported brief physical exercise in promoting smoking abstinence among proactively recruited community smokers. Contrary to our hypothesis, the intervention did not significantly improve biochemically validated abstinence rates at 3 or 6 months compared with the control group receiving brief cessation advice alone. Secondary outcomes, including self-reported 7-day PPA, smoking reduction, quit attempts and use of cessation services, also showed no significant differences between groups. These findings align with the results of the meta-analysis12 and the Cochrane review,1 which found limited evidence for effectiveness of aerobic exercise and overall exercise interventions to improve long-term abstinence, and extended the implications to MIM-supported brief physical exercise.

The absence of a significant intervention effect may be attributed to several factors. First, the brief physical exercise may not be of sufficient dosage to significantly reduce tobacco cravings and withdrawal symptoms. Compared with high-intensity aerobic exercise, the brief physical exercise represents a low level of physical activity which may require more intensive practice to overcome craving. Consistent results were reported in RCTs of cognitive–behavioural smoking treatment plus exercise on US female inactive smokers, that participants receiving vigorous-intensity exercise (12 sessions, 24.6%)11 showed higher smoking abstinence rates than those receiving moderate-intensity exercise (eight sessions, 11.9%)42 at 3-month follow-up. Our participants reported mean scores of 5.9 (SD 1.8) and 5.6 (SD 2.3) on the perceived effectiveness of brief physical exercise on reducing cravings at 3 and 6-month follow-ups, respectively. Higher practice intensity and frequency may improve the efficacy but currently remain unclear. Second, the adherence to the brief physical exercise attenuated across the intervention period (overall 18.4% reported adherence by 6 months) and might dilute the potential effectiveness. The decline of exercise adherence suggests that declining motivation and engagement remain important barriers,13 43 even for low-burden interventions. The MIM-based reminders, grounded in BCTs, were intended to enhance adherence through strengthened behavioural support, but the tapering schedule (from two times a week to once a week) might have been insufficient to maintain engagement over time. The low adherence might also be due to over half of the enrolled smokers having no past year quit attempt and motivation to quit. Designing a scalable and sustainable physical exercise intervention using online platforms is challenging, incorporating more intensive and personalised digital health strategies, such as online scheduled in-person exercise coaching or virtual exercise sessions facilitated by artificial intelligence with dynamic feedback, may be worthwhile to be tested.44 Physical exercise intervention engagement may be improved by using gamified elements, including points, badges, progress levels or integrated with incentives.45 More immersive and interactive approaches such as virtual reality (VR) are increasingly being applied to promote physical activity and enhance social connection.46 Future research could explore the integration of more engaging technologies, including VR-based platforms, to further strengthen user engagement and intervention effectiveness.

Our intervention did not increase physical activity levels, with the proportion of participants achieving moderate to high physical activity levels remaining similar between groups at 6 months. Although the brief exercises were designed to be easily integrated into daily routines, the brief nature of the exercises, while feasible, might not have been sufficient to produce measurable changes in overall physical activity.47 The lack of personalised feedback or progressive exercise goals may further limit participants’ ability to build the brief physical exercise into their routines. And thus, the low impact of the brief exercise intervention on physical activity levels likely contributed to the null effect of the intervention in increasing cessation outcomes. In future studies, ecological momentary assessments could be integrated to provide a close monitoring of the pattern of the craving and withdrawal symptoms and facilitate the personalised guidance or reminder of the exercise practice (eg, ecological momentary intervention) to improve the adherence and experience in achieving cessation.

The pragmatic design, targeting a diverse, proactively recruited population, may attenuate the intervention’s impact given a large variation of smokers. Although this approach enhances real-world applicability, engaging the participants in adherence to brief, low-burden exercises was shown to be challenging. The higher intervention effects in certain subgroups (non-significant), such as females (RR=1.39), those with motivation to quit (within 30 or 60 days) (within 30 days: RR=1.19; within 60 days: RR=1.18) and those with moderate baseline activity (RR=1.31), align with the literature that specific populations were more susceptive to exercise-based interventions.26 42 Emphasising the link between exercise and SC benefits, including reduced cravings and improved mood, may boost intrinsic motivation, particularly when combined with motivational interviewing and personalised goal setting.9 Establishing achievable, individualised exercise targets and using self-monitoring tools, such as fitness apps, could promote adherence.48 Future studies integrating behavioural reinforcement strategies, social support elements and personalised exercise plans tailoring to participants’ fitness levels and preferences, thereby improving motivation and sustained engagement, are warranted.

This study had several limitations. First, our intervention messages were designed to promote brief physical exercises and support craving management, information on pharmacotherapy treatments was not included. SC services in Hong Kong provided free pharmacotherapy treatment, but referral was briefly delivered within the baseline AWARD model to avoid synergy or antagonism intervention effect. However, incorporating pharmacological treatment within MIM-based and exercise-based behavioural support may further reduce withdrawal symptoms and improve overall effectiveness, which could be tested in the future. Second, 40.2% of the participants were lost to follow-up at 6 months. The wide ranges of the 95% CI of estimates in the multiple imputation model suggested instability due to high attrition. Although ITT was adopted and the retention rate was comparable between the two groups (62.0% vs 57.9%), the findings should be interpreted with caution. Exploring methods to improve the retention rates is needed in mHealth studies to obtain more stable and precise estimates of long-term cessation outcomes. Third, all measures related to physical exercise were self-reported, which might be subject to recall bias. The perceived intensity varied in different types of exercise, and thus the measurement may not reflect objective physical activity level. Wearable devices (ie, pedometer, heart rate monitor) and biological assessments (ie, body mass index, blood oxygen) could be added to provide objective physical activity data. Fourth, adherence to the prescribed exercise sessions within the intervention group was not regularly assessed. The absence of detailed adherence data limits our ability to directly evaluate the dose–response relationship between exercise participation and cessation outcomes. Detailed assessments of engagement in brief physical exercises, such as frequency, duration and intensity during craving episodes, could enhance understanding of intervention adherence and effectiveness and should be investigated in future studies. Finally, a significant proportion of participants (66.6%) already engaged in moderate to high levels of physical activity at baseline may lead to lower motivation of the participant to adopt brief physical exercise and limit the generalisability of the findings to less active populations. The effectiveness of the intervention might be different in regions or populations with lower baseline activity levels, and this warrants further exploration in diverse settings.49

Conclusions

An MIM-supported brief physical exercise intervention did not significantly increase smoking abstinence or physical activity compared with brief cessation advice alone.
