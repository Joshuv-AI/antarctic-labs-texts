Poultry fecal Escherichia coli contaminate soil, water, and vegetables in Gazipur, Bangladesh: Potential implications for the environment and public health
Authors: Md Aminul Islam, Md Robiul Karim, S H M Faruk Siddiki, Md Sodrul Islam, Sheikh Arafatur Rahman, Mohammad Shah Alam, Mousumi Saha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobally poultry production is increasing to fulfill the protein requirements for human consumption. Poultry waste from farms might contaminate adjacent environments and agricultural products grown in lands that used poultry manure as a soil organic fertilizer. In order to determine the spread of poultry waste bacteria to adjacent water bodies, soils, and agricultural products, Escherichia coli (E. coli) were isolated from poultry fecal wastes as well as from nearby water bodies, soils, and agricultural products grown in lands that used poultry wastes. E. coli of poultry manure and poultry environment (soils, water, and vegetables) were isolated and confirmed by culturing and amplification of malK gene of E. coli by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Contamination of the nearby environment by poultry waste E. coli was also determined by VT1 (stx1) gene of E. coli. Subsequently, gene sequencing of the positive isolates of E. coli and phylogenetic trees was developed to find out evolutionary dynamics of the poultry environment E. coli. The overall prevalence of E. coli in the poultry environment was 61.02%. E. coli were detected in the manure of all broiler, layer, and Sonali types of poultry farms. E. coli also detected in both chicks and adult layer farms during all the seasons by malK gene. The VT1 (stx1) gene of E. coli was not detected in any of the samples. Contamination by poultry fecal E. coli to adjacent water bodies, soils, and vegetables grown on soils that used poultry waste as manure were also found. Phylogenetic tree denotes there were genetic similarities of poultry manure E. coli of Gazipur, Bangladesh to global isolates of E. coli from livestock, their environment, and humans. These findings will be crucial to undertake measures to limit spread of poultry manure E. coli to the surrounding environment as well as controlling zoonoses.Keywords: Environment friendly poultry production, Food safety, Microbial contamination, Waste management, Zoonotic risk assessment

Introduction

Globally commercial poultry farming and production has been growing rapidly since the early 1990s by using manufactured feeds, improved genetics, and good management to address global protein demands (FAO, 2023; Alam et al., 2022; Islam et al., 2020a). Based on this poultry industry, a number of industries are developed both in the inputs sector and outputs sector along with a number of services providing organizations (Raha, 2013). Thus, the poultry industry has a huge impact on the global economy (Khaleda, 2013; Haque et al., 2020). From the last decade much emphasis has been given to produce maximum economic benefit from the poultry industry without considering the negative impact of poultry farms on the environment and food safety (Mahmud et al., 2011).

The poultry farms can disseminate antibiotics, vaccines, different types of microorganisms etc. to the environment and degrade nearby environmental quality (Soupir et al., 2006; Delgado et al., 2011). The pathogenic bacteria from poultry farms can disseminate to the surrounding environment and contaminate them and may cause infectious diseases in animals and humans remotely (USEPA, 2013; CDC, 2010; Castiglioni Tessari et al., 2012). One of the crucial sources of the dissemination of bacteria from poultry farms through poultry feces, manure, waste water etc. (Mandal et al., 2022).

Poultry farming practices produce lots of manure and wastes. The management and disposal of these manure using scientific methods is essential to reduce the environmental footprint of poultry production. But it is common practice in developing countries to use poultry manure and wastes in soils and ponds to cultivate agricultural commodities and fishes, respectively (Sarker et al., 2009; Atidégla et al., 2016; Adeyemi et al., 2022). By this way, poultry waste bacteria can easily disseminate and contaminate soils, water, plants, vegetable products, and their environment. As a result, poultry manure bacteria can enter the food and water cycle and contaminate food and drinking water for humans and animal consumption (Dale et al., 2010; Craun et al., 2010).

In Bangladesh, poultry manure is disposed of haphazardly without following any guidelines (Sarker et al., 2009). Among many uses poultry manure was commonly used for biogas production and as land manure in the form of compost for crop production (Kyakuwaire et al., 2019; Atidégla et al., 2016; Sarker et al., 2009). By this way different pathogenic bacteria from poultry manure ultimately affect humans and animals and may produce various zoonotic infectious diseases to them (Hruby et al., 2018; Begum et al., 2023). Thus, the minimization of emission and transmission of hazardous materials including pathogenic bacteria from poultry manure to the surrounding environment is crucial to produce environmentally friendly poultry products.

Previously several studies were undertaken to measure poultry diseases in Gazipur, Bangladesh (Islam et al., 2020b; Hasan et al., 2020; Saleque et al., 2003; Islam et al., 2025). To the best of our knowledge, there was no study undertaken to detect E. coli in poultry manure in Bangladesh by molecular methods and their genetic similarities to global isolates from livestock farms, their environment, and humans (Rahman et al., 2022; Begum et al., 2023). By considering the fact, present research work has been undertaken to find out E. coli in poultry manure and their adjacent environment (soil, water, and vegetables) and to measure genetic relatedness of poultry E. coli in Gazipur, Bangladesh to global isolates of E. coli from animal farming systems and human isolates. The first objective of the present study was the determination of prevalence of E. coli in poultry fecal wastes and their environment (soils, water, and vegetables) by PCR. The second objective was to compare the prevalence of E. coli in poultry environments in finding out the significant difference of E. coli in poultry fecal wastes and their environment. The third objective is to measure molecular genetic evolutionary relationships (genetic diversity) of the poultry fecal E. coli to the global isolates of E. coli from livestock, livestock environment, and humans to determine potential hazards of poultry fecal E. coli to animals, environment, and humans in Gazipur, Bangladesh.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study area was Gazipur Sadar and Sreepur Upazilla of Gazipur district (Fig. 1). The Gazipur district is considered as one of the crucial poultry production areas of Bangladesh. The samples were collected from 17 (seventeen) poultry farms comprising broiler, layer, and sonali farms and their environment (soils, water, and vegetables). The number of birds ranging in those farms were 600-3500. Among the farms, some of the farms actively reared birds but others kept emptying to enter new batches.

Study area.

Sample collection and preservation

Poultry wastes/manure samples (5–10 grams), soils & water (sufficient quantity) from adjacent to poultry farms, and vegetables grown in soils that used poultry manure as soil fertilizer were collected in a plastic tube as described previously (Islam et al., 2024) from April, 2022 to October, 2023 . A total of 59 different types of samples were collected from different types of farms using different types of waste management systems throughout the year. After collection, samples were immediately shifted to the laboratory using an ice box and kept in a refrigerator until analysis. Those samples were cultured in different bacteriological media, and were immediately cultured.

Collection of the data about farm management, manure disposal system, and seasons

A questionnaire was developed to collect data about poultry farm management (age, production type, number of birds, feeds etc.), waste disposal systems and management (storage system, length of storage before using as a soil conditioner etc.) during different seasons.

Culturing samples in different types of growth media

Among 59 samples, a total of 21 samples were cultured in nutrient broth and subsequently sub-cultured in MacConkey’s agar to isolate E. coli from the samples. The growth characteristics of E. coli in MacConkey’s agar media are shown in Fig. 2.

Pink colour colonies in MacConkey’s agar are E. coli.

Extraction of bacterial DNA from waste samples

Poultry manure bacterial DNA was extracted from each sample as described by Dungan et al. (2012). QIAamp® Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit was used to extract DNA directly from 38 manure samples. The manufacturer’s guidelines were used for the extraction of E. coli DNA from poultry manure samples. Boiling method was used to extract DNA from 21 pure cultured E. coli from different samples as described previously (Pui et al., 2011). In brief, a loopful of colony from MacConkey’s agar was suspended in 500 µL distilled water and vortexed vigorously. The cell suspension was boiled for 10 min, cooled at -20°C for 10 min, centrifuged again at 15,000 × g for 3 min. The supernatant was used as the DNA template solution for the PCR amplification.

PCR amplification of the E. coli DNA

Gene amplification of the E. coli DNA was performed using specific primers (Table 1) for detecting E. coli in poultry manure, adjacent environments (soils, water), and vegetable samples as described previously (Pass et al., 2000). The PCR cycling conditions consisted of denaturation initially at 95°C for 5 min., followed by 30 cycles for 1 min at 94°C, 45 sec 55°C and 1 min at 72°C with a final extension for 7 min at 72°C for malK gene of E. coli. The PCR cycling conditions for VT1 (stx1) gene was initial denaturation at 94°C for 5 min., followed by 30 cycles for 2 min at 94°C, 1 min at 55°C and 1 min at 72°C with a final extension for 7 min at 72°C. The E. coli positive samples by malK gene amplification using PCR are shown in Fig. 3.

A single factor analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post-hoc analysis was conducted to determine significant difference of E. coli in poultry manure and their environment for three or more sets of data. The F tests followed by t tests were conducted to determine significant differences between two sets of data as described previously (Islam et al., 2025a). Statistical test results were considered significant at p ≤ 0.05.

Gene sequencing

The partial malK gene sequencing of the positive E. coli samples was sequenced from a commercial company (macrogen) by Sanger dideoxy sequencing. The sequencing was performed for both reverse and forward order. The sequences were analyzed using ClustalW and BioEdit software. Sequence identification was performed using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) in GenBank. Two sequences from poultry fecal E. coli were submitted to the NCBI, GenBank and get accession numbers in GenBank (Table 2).

Evolutionary relationships of E. coli to global isolates of E. coli from animal, their environment, and humans

The phylogenetic trees using two positive isolates of E. coli were developed using the software MEGA X (Kumar et al., 2018) to find out the evolutionary characteristics of poultry fecal E. coli to the global animal and human E. coli. Phylogenetic tree (evolutionary history) was constructed using the neighbor-joining method as described previously (Saitou and Nei, 1987) to detect genetic similarities of poultry waste E. coli to the global isolates. The optimal trees are developed and shown (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). The evolutionary history of E. coli from animals and their environment are shown in Fig. 5. The evolutionary history of E. coli from our isolates to the global E. coli isolates from humans (zoonotic E. coli) are shown in Fig. 6. The percentage of replicate trees in which the associated taxa clustered together in the bootstrap test (1000 replicates) are shown next to the branches (Felsenstein, 1985). The tree is drawn to scale, with branch lengths in the same units as those of the evolutionary distances used to infer the phylogenetic tree. The evolutionary distances were computed using the Maximum Composite Likelihood method (Tamura et al., 2004) and are in the units of the number of base substitutions per site. This analysis involved 21 nucleotide sequences for both trees. Codon positions included were 1st+2nd+3rd+Noncoding. All ambiguous positions were removed for each sequence pair (pairwise deletion option). There were a total of 591 positions in the final dataset for animal origin E. coli (Fig. 5) and there were a total of 575 positions in the final dataset for human origin E. coli (Fig. 6). PV176514 and PQ187094 are GenBank accession numbers of samples from our present study (Table 2).

All 21 samples showed growth characteristics in Nutrient broth media. The culture positive samples on Nutrient broth media is 100%. Out of 21 cultured samples, 19 samples showed growth characteristics on MacConkey’s agar media and 2 samples did not grow in MacConkey’s agar media (Fig. 2). Out of 57 analyzed samples (38 direct DNA extracted from fecal samples and 19 positive cultured samples), 36 samples were positive for E. coli by malK gene (Fig. 3). The overall prevalence of E. coli in the poultry environment was 61.02% (36 positive samples out of 59 total samples) by malK gene. None of the samples were positive for VT1 (stx1) virulence genes of E. coli.

The percent prevalence of E. coli in various samples were shown in Table 3 and Fig. 4. The highest prevalence of E. coli (83.34%) was observed in the feces of Sonali types of birds and lowest prevalence of E. coli (48.15%) was observed in the broiler birds among all types of birds (Fig. 4). A greater contamination of water (80.00%) was found by poultry fecal E. coli than soils (33.34%). A cent percent contamination of all types of vegetables were observed by poultry fecal E. coli (Fig. 4) at present. A significantly different prevalence of E. coli among various samples were not found (Table 3).

Two isolates from our present study are closely related to the cloacal swab of broiler from Cuba, feces of Canada goose etc. More close evolutionary relationships of our isolates were found with isolates from chicken in India, farm environment E. coli from Switzerland etc. (Fig. 5). Furthermore, our two isolates were related to the human isolates of E. coli from Mali, China, and India etc. The isolates from our present study were also related to the isolates from neonatal infected diarrhea and adult human feces from Bangladesh (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Livestock production is increasing globally to meet the demand of protein requirements for humans. Increased production of livestock has negative consequences on human health, environment, food safety etc. (Naide et al., 2018; Islam et al., 2018). Now a day’s environmentally friendly livestock production is a challenge and this area of science needs more attention to limit spread of zoonotic diseases from animals to humans and to protect the environment (Islam et al., 2022). The present research was undertaken in finding out the presence of E. coli in the poultry fecal wastes and their potential public health implications in contaminating the surrounding natural environment, water resources, agricultural products etc.

At present, the overall prevalence of E. coli in poultry feces and their environment is 61.02% (36 positive E. coli out of 59 samples) which could be considered high and a potential threat to the poultry farms and their surrounding environment. The E. coli were observed in the fecal wastes/floor feces of all broiler, layer, and Sonali farms in all age groups (chicks, adult) and in all types (small and bigger) of farms in Gazipur sadar and Sreepur Upazillas of Gazipur district (Fig. 1. and Table 3). Previously, Nauanova et al. (2020) and Himathongkham et al. (2000) also detected E. coli in poultry manure and wastes. Previously, Mandal et al. (2022) also observed 76% overall prevalence of E. coli in poultry environments (e.g., feces, farm sewage, water etc.) which is similar to the present findings. The E. coli is a potential zoonotic bacterium having enormous negative effects on animals, humans, and the environment. Thus measures should be undertaken to eliminate E. coli from poultry farms and their environments in Gazipur, Bangladesh.

Significant differences of E. coli among feces of broiler, layer, and Sonali types of birds were not observed (Table 3) at our study. These findings indicate all types of poultry farms in Gazipur can spread E. coli and are hazardous to the surrounding environment. The prevalence of E. coli in layer and broiler feces were 50% and 48.15%, respectively (Table 3). Previously another study from Nigeria reported 45% E. coli in animal feces (Moses et al., 2016) which supports our present study. But the highest prevalence of E. coli (83.34%) was observed in the feces of Sonali type of birds among all types of birds (Fig. 4) and similar findings also reported previously from Bangladesh (Mandal et al., 2022). Sonali type of birds is a newly developed crossbred meat type of bird in Bangladesh and has gained acceptance and popularity over broiler birds because their meats are closely related to the local chicken in Bangladesh considering appearance and taste (Prank et al., 2024). One of the reasons for the higher prevalence of E. coli in the wastes of the Sonali birds might be due to the differences in management of Sonali types of birds than broiler and layer birds. Broiler and layer birds are rearing from the past decades in Bangladesh and farmers are well adapted to the management of broiler and layer. This present study elucidates farmers should be well trained about the management of Sonali birds to protect environmental health (Rimi et al., 2019; Prank et al., 2024).

At present, 33.34% soil was contaminated by the poultry fecal waste E. coli (Table 3) in Gazipur, Bangladesh which could be considered a potential hazard for soil health. Previously, Hruby et al. (2018) applied poultry manure to cornfields and collected soil samples after manure application to assess the effects of time and application rates on frequency of detection and concentrations of E. coli in soil samples. They concluded that E. coli can survive in soil despite the dry conditions. They also detected E. coli in spring soil samples from manured plots nearly a full year after poultry manure application, suggesting that these organisms can persist in the soil environment long after application of poultry manure which supports present findings. Previously, significant contamination of soil by poultry waste bacteria were also reported (Ajayi et al., 2003; Hutchison et al., 2004) which is similar to the present findings. This finding indicates that methods should be developed to inactivate poultry waste E. coli in the soils (Griffiths, 2007; Ayilara et al., 2020) for environmental protection.

At present, the soil of a closed farm for one year at Sreepur, Gazipur (Table 3) did not show any growth of bacteria which is different from a previous finding (Hruby et al., 2018). The probable reason for this different finding might be due to soil pH (alkalinity or acidity), texture, oxygen, temperature etc. because soil physical and biochemical characteristics affect survivability of microbes in the soil as reported here (Franz et al., 2005; Xing et al., 2019). This finding indicates that E. coli can’t survive in farm soil for one year. Thus, if a farm can be closed for one year that farm soil can’t contain any E. coli. This might be due to effects of environmental stresses on soil E. coli because E. coli is a Gram-negative bacterium which is more susceptible to environmental stresses (Williams et al., 2005; Berry and Miller 2005; Islam et al., 2025b).

At our present study, it was found that 80.00% water bodies adjacent to the poultry farms were contaminated by the poultry fecal E. coli (Table 3) which could be considered a potential hazard for water quality. Previously, significant spread of livestock farm bacteria to the nearby water resources were also reported (Ajayi et al., 2003; Ercumen et al., 2017; Begum et al., 2023; Adeyemi et al., 2022) which supports our present findings. The pond water of an adjacent farm at Sreepur, Gazipur (Table 3) that closed for twenty days did not show any growth of E. coli. This indicates that E. coli can’t survive in a water body adjacent to a poultry farm that is closed for twenty days. The negative growth of E. coli from pond water might be due to dilution of bacteria to the large pond water.

Poultry fecal waste E. coli contaminates all types of vegetables at our present study (Table 3) which could be considered as a potential human health hazard. Previously, spread of animal and poultry environment E. coli to agricultural products have also been reported (Moses et al., 2016; Begum et al., 2023) which supports our present findings. This finding implicates a severe public health consequence of poultry farming practices in Gazipur, Bangladesh. Thus, immediate measures should be developed to minimize spread of poultry waste E. coli to the nearby agricultural products to safeguard human health. Treated poultry waste should be used for agricultural production (Bolan et al., 2010) to minimize the spread of poultry fecal E. coli to the agricultural products and vegetables.

At present, molecular evolutionary dynamics through phylogenetic tree denotes there were genetic similarities of poultry waste E. coli in Gazipur, Bangladesh (PQ187094 and PV176514) (Table 2) to E. coli at farm environment in Switzerland, raw chicken in India, cattle feces in United Kingdom, wastewater influent sample in the United Kingdom etc. (Fig. 5). The isolates were also related to the isolates from pork in Cambodia and raw chicken meat in India (Fig. 5). The isolates were also genetically related to the isolates of E. coli from Cucumber in South Korea (Fig. 5). Previously spread of poultry manure bacteria to the vegetables and foods have also been reported (Atidégla et al., 2016; Ercumen et al., 2017) which supports our present findings.

Two isolates of E. coli from our present study (PQ187094 and PV176514) (Table 2) showed evolutionary relationships to human isolates of E. coli from diarrhoeic feces in India, infected urine in China etc. Our isolates were also evolutionary related to the E. coli of human neonatal and adult infected feces in Bangladesh (Fig. 6). Previously zoonotic spread of bacteria from poultry farming systems have also been reported (Grzinic et al., 2023; Amir et al., 2019; Salaheen et al., 2015; Norizuki et al., 2017) which is similar to our present study. The genetic relatedness of E. coli in our study suggests there might be zoonotic transmission of E. coli from poultry farming systems to the humans in Gazipur, Bangladesh. These findings also denote that neonatal babies are more vulnerable to E. coli infections from poultry farming systems in Gazipur, Bangladesh (Fig. 6). Previously zoonotic spread of infections from poultry farms to vulnerable communities were also reported (Nga et al., 2019; Ayim-Akonor et al., 2020).

One of the limitations of the present study is the analysis of a lower number of samples because of the lack of skilled working manpower, a common picture in the developing countries of the world. But the authors believe the findings are crucial for environmental and public health friendly poultry production and appropriate actions should be taken by policymakers of Bangladesh (Khaleda, 2013) to adopt our present findings in the field of poultry sector to safeguard animal, environment, and human health.

Conclusions

The present study elucidates prevalence of E. coli in poultry wastes and their surrounding environment by molecular genetic identification methods. The prevalence of E. coli in poultry manure and their associated environment were 61.02% which could be considered high. E. coli were found in all age groups (chicks, layer) and all production types (broiler, layer, Sonali) during all the seasons in the year. The present study also elucidates that poultry fecal E. coli contaminate nearby soil (33.34%), water bodies (80%), and vegetables (100%). Molecular evolutionary dynamics through phylogenetic tree denotes, there were genetic similarities of poultry manure E. coli of Gazipur, Bangladesh to global isolates of E. coli from cattle feces, chicken feces, goose feces, farm environment, agricultural products, humans etc. The present study suggests that for sustainable poultry farming, improvement of biosecurity to the poultry farms and scientific treatment of poultry wastes (effective waste management) is urgently needed in Gazipur, Bangladesh to limit the spread of poultry manure E. coli to the surrounding environment (soil, water, and vegetables) to protect the animal, environmental, and human health.
