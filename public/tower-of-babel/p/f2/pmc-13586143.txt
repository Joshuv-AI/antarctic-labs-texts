Mass spectrometry imaging reveals different allocation of the starting steps of benzalkonium biotransformation in wastewater biofilms
Authors: Min Qiu, Yrsa Larsson, James McKenzie, Vaidotas Kisielius, Raquel Gonzalez de Vega, David Clases, Daniel Heffernan, Kai Bester, Nicole Strittmatter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn urban water engineering, biofilm reactors are often used to remove organic micropollutants from water both in drinking water production and wastewater treatment. However, the functioning of multispecies biofilms is largely unknown. We used mass spectrometry imaging (MSI) to reveal spatially localized reactions of the pollutant benzalkonium chloride to test whether the degradation reactions are evenly distributed over the biomass or localized in reactive centers. Here we show, that several reactions can be localized. It can thus be concluded that the few microorganisms that can metabolize these pollutants are not evenly (homogeneously) distributed over the biofilm. We also show that the different biotransformations have different reaction centers, especially i) the oxidation of the terminal carbon atom by ω-oxidation, followed by both α- and β-oxidation, and ii) the second hydroxylation to form bis-hydroxy derivatives are located differently. Thus, these two different reactions are conducted by different microbial complexes or microcolonies. These differed further in their lipid patterns, which were measured in the same MSI analysis. These findings could have vast impact on designing biofilms for wastewater engineering as they provide a new analytical tool that can tell which domains in a multispecies biofilm need to be promoted for dedicated tasks.Subject terms: Environmental biotechnology, Environmental chemistry

Introduction

Biofilm reactors and processes are quite common in environmental engineering in urban water systems1,2. Slow sand filters or generally porous medium biofilm reactors are mostly used in drinking water purification, while in wastewater engineering, classically trickling filters, rotating drums and more recently both moving bed biofilm reactors (MBBRs) and membrane aerated biofilm reactors (MABRs) are common. These are used for various purposes such as nitrification, denitrification, iron removal and increasingly micropollutant removal3,4. Although they often outperform conventional activated sludge reactors (CAS), their biochemical functions and microbial architecture remain poorly understood.

Micropollutants have been detected at concentrations from nanograms to tens of micrograms per liter in effluent wastewater samples globally5–7. They are only partially removed in CAS wastewater treatment plants (CAS-WWTPs) and are subsequently released into the environment8–10. Thus, micropollutants are a cause for global concern due to adverse environmental effects, and putting potential drinking water resources at risk8.

Unlike CAS, biofilm reactors, especially moving bed biofilm reactors (MBBRs), as well as slow multilayer filtration units, have proven to have high biodegradation abilities even for compounds that are classified as non-degradable or recalcitrant11–14. Biofilms are microbial assemblies that usually grow at the solid/aqueous boundary layer15,16. In environmental and environmental technology contexts, these biofilms usually comprise several thousand species with cells in close proximity and embedded into an extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) that creates a protective layer against environmental stresses15,16. Biofilms form multi-compartmentalized ecosystems with gradients in oxygen, nutrients, pH, and signaling molecules, which are driven antagonistically by microbial metabolic activity and diffusion processes15–17. As the respective microbial cells are in close contact with each other, it has been hypothesized that complex reactions are performed by the cells in cooperation18,19. Previous studies on multispecies wastewater biofilms estimated that only 0.1% of the biofilm biomass contributes to the actual degradation processes of micropollutants20. However, it remains unclear whether the cells contributing to degradation are uniformly dispersed in the biofilm or localized within microcolonies, thereby performing localized metabolism. It has also been resolved that different substrate conditions triggered different biodegradation pathways, leaving the open question whether the composition of pathways is an effect of a changed microbial community composition, different activity of enzymes21 or structural elements such as internal gradients15. While most of our current knowledge stems from studies analyzing bulk water or bulk biomass in biofilm reactors, it is increasingly clear that spatial components must be taken into account when studying complex samples such as biofilms. However, biofilms are challenging to analyze using mass spectrometry imaging (MSI), due to their high water content and morphological heterogeneity. Burzio et al.22 recently imaged the distribution of micropollutants in aerobic granular sludge biofilms, using secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) with a low-resolution mass analyzer and found local differences among micropollutant distribution patterns. However, only three of the eight investigated compounds were detected, while signal attributed to drug metabolites could only be detected in the case of ketoconazole, with occurrence maxima (approximately 50 µm size22) overlapping with those of the parent compounds.

To our knowledge, no investigation of the spatial heterogeneity of different biotransformation reactions has been performed to date. Thus, the objective of this study was to use ambient MSI to determine whether localized micropollutant metabolism occurs or whether metabolism is distributed homogeneously across the biofilms. Ambient, soft-ionization-based MSI can visualize hundreds of chemical constituents simultaneously and in a label-free manner, including micropollutants and their metabolites. In this study, we used Desorption ElectroSpray Ionization (DESI) mass spectrometry imaging23 to perform spatially resolved metabolomics on native biofilm specimens and HPLC-MS/MS (liquid chromatography coupled to high resolution mass spectrometry) to analyze the same compounds in bulk reactor supernatants. We focused especially on the metabolism of benzalkonium compounds (BACs), as their metabolism is known, and these compounds sorb well to negatively charged surfaces, i.e., biomass24. Their nomination reflects the length of the alkyl chain, BAC12 contains a dodecyl chain. BACs are one of the most predominantly used disinfectants, and exhibit relatively high concentrations (1–10 µg/L in the dissolved phase) in raw wastewater25,26. On top of that, they are relatively toxic to aquatic organisms (EC50 for aquatic invertebrates as low as 5.9 µg/L for BAC12)27.

Results

Distribution of micropollutants in different surface areas of the biofilms

The experimental workflow we deployed is shown in Fig. 1a. Prior to the experiments described here, all biofilms were established on hyperbolic paraboloid carriers (Z-carriers®) for three months by initially inoculating with heterogeneous bacterial communities from a CAS-WWTP in a flow-through reactor. For the presented study, we moved a defined number of the carriers from the flow-through reactor and incubated them in batched mini reactors in artificial wastewater containing five different classes of micropollutants (i.e., two representatives each of benzalkonium chlorides, macrolides, tetracyclines, sulfonamides, and fluoroquinolones, structures are given in Supplementary Fig. 1). Due to the comparatively low sensitivity of MSI as compared to LC-MS/MS analysis, the 1 mg/L concentration of these compounds were exceeding those found in municipal wastewater by a factor 100–1000 to enable visualization of low abundance metabolites. However, concentrations deployed here are still well below the effective concentrations, which are usually in the 100–1000 mg/L range (product information), while minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) are between 0.24 and 13.75 mg/L for suspensions of pure bacterial cultures that are not adapted to these compounds, while the value increases once the organisms get adapted to it28. Compounds from the same micropollutant class had similar removal kinetics (as determined in the supernatant, see Fig. 1b for BACs and Supplementary Fig. 2 for all other compounds). The benzalkonium compounds (BACs) were removed with the fastest kinetics, while the macrolides, sulfonamides and fluoroquinolones showed little change in concentration in bulk water, exceeding the measurement uncertainties. After 7 h of incubation, the removal of the parent BACs from the aqueous phase exceeded 90% (Fig. 1b). After 24 h, the formation of BAC metabolites was observed (Fig. 1c, d).

Due to the structure of the Z-carriers, the biofilms are naturally divided into small squares of 3×3 mm size by the grid pattern on the carrier surface (compare Fig. 1a), delivering multiple biological replicates on each carrier. These biofilms were carefully lifted off using tweezers and fixed to a microscope glass slide as described in the experimental section. Thus, the background signals from micropollutants sorbed to the plastic carrier are excluded. Ambient DESI-MSI was then used to visualize compounds on the surfaces of individual dried biofilm specimens in positive ion mode. As the biofilms generally did not show signs of physical damage following MSI acquisition (see Supplementary Fig. 3), the same specimens were re-analyzed in negative ion mode.

While most micropollutants in this study were detected on the biofilm carriers at all time points, BACs and macrolides were observed with highest sensitivity (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 4). Additionally, biotransformation products were identified for BACs and macrolides. Several structurally different metabolites were observed in the case of BACs (Table 1). Only a single metabolite signal each was observed by MSI for roxithromycin and erythromycin (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Discussion 1), while the HPLC-MS/MS determinations revealed several isomers of these macrolide metabolites, complicating the determination of exact biodegradation products. Thus, the following discussion will be largely focused on BACs (especially BAC12) and their biotransformation products. The analogous data for BAC14, a structurally closely related chemical homolog, are available in the Supplementary Material and follow the same trends. No interfering signal was observed in the control biofilm specimen for any of the compounds or their suspect metabolites.

In the brightfield microscopy images (Fig. 2a), the majority of the biofilms are visible as areas of varying degrees of light and dark color. While these areas of differential light transmission could be due to different cellular densities, they do not seem correlated with biofilm thickness, which is found to be largely constant over each 3 × 3 mm biofilm specimen (Supplementary Table 1). Thickness of the dried biofilms was determined on two specimens (54 h and 246 h, n = 4 each) using cross-sectional microscopy and ranged from 25.6 to 65.1 µm for the dried biofilms. No increase in thickness was observed from 54 to 246 h. Previous studies on the same carriers reported an average thickness of 111.6 ± 11.3 μm (wet biofilms), which fits the growth restriction of 200 µm (wet biofilm) imposed by the grid wall height29. Lighter and darker spots can similarly be detected on these cross-sections. Ionization efficiency in DESI-MSI can, in theory, be influenced by the roughness and uneven surface height in the sample. However, the detected average differences in height of dry biofilms are negligible compared to the sprayer tip-to-sample surface distance of 1.5 mm. Similarly, in a study investigating the effect of tissue thicknesses, no effect on DESI performance was found30.

Lighter and darker areas observed on the biofilm specimen could not be explained by inorganic deposits. Using laser-ablation inductively coupled plasma time-of-flight mass spectrometry imaging (LA-ICP-TOFMS), metal ion distributions were generally found to be homogenous (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Supplementary Fig. 6 shows four biofilm replicates from the same carrier and illustrates the high heterogeneity of biofilms even within a single condition. Thus, direct comparison of localization patterns can only be performed on a numerical (not visual) level. Spatial patterns are discussed in greater detail for a biofilm at an intermediate time point of 198 h; however, for comparisons between different time points and replicates we use a spatial colocalization index (SCI values).

To further assess whether darker and lighter biofilm areas might be due to increased local bacterial biomass, we deployed so-called taxon-specific markers (TSMs), small molecular biomarkers (up to 1000 Da), to identify certain groups of bacteria using metabolomics workflows and derived from unique spectral features found among a set of 233 bacterial species31. Using these, we can detect the presence of specific bacterial taxa, ranging from species to class level. Forty-seven out of the overall 359 markers can be found in these wastewater biofilms (Supplementary Fig. 7), showing especially the presence of bacteria such as Flavobacteria (class) and Bacteroidaceae (family), gut microbes which were previously detected in wastewater by 16S rRNA sequence analysis32,33. Bacterial TSMs were detected in several hotspots distributed throughout the biofilm, with no correlation to the morphological structures visible in the brightfield microscopic image (Fig. 3).

Parent compounds

In general, the parent BACs, i.e., BAC12 (and BAC14), are distributed over the whole biofilm area, albeit with local differences in abundance (Figs. 2, 4). The 198 h biofilm specimen in Fig. 4 exhibits a somewhat lower BAC intensity region, ranging from the center of the biofilm to the bottom right biofilm region. While BAC12 (Fig. 4b) appears to be predominantly detected in those biofilm areas that appear dark in the brightfield microscopic image, this cannot be generalized, as BAC12 is detected in both lighter areas (black arrows) and absent from dark areas (white arrows).

Metabolites of micropollutants in biofilms

The spatial distribution of the metabolites of BACs is shown in Fig. 2b-f and Fig. 4. Only such compounds for which increasing signals were found in the bulk supernatant and which could be structurally linked to the spiked parents were considered metabolites. Compared to HPLC-MS/MS24, a lower number of metabolites was detected in biofilms using MSI. This can have several reasons: i) decreased sensitivity due to the in situ ionization and direct infusion nature of MS imaging analysis, ii) metabolism usually renders these metabolites more hydrophilic, resulting in a higher fraction of them partitioning into the bulk water phase, where they are less accessible to DESI-MSI.

Four metabolites for BAC12 were detected by MSI, with the analogous metabolites detected for BAC14 (Table 1). Their distribution over the biofilm surface at 198 h is shown in Fig. 4, together with their respective metabolic pathways.

While DESI-MSI measurements at the MS1 level alone yield low confidence in metabolite identification, the combined evidence from the study design and complementary LC-MS/MS analyses substantially increases annotation confidence. High-resolution mass defects, fragmentation patterns in matched supernatants, and the absence of interfering signals in unspiked controls support a defined set of structural candidates. In situ MS/MS using DESI further supports the structural relationship between BAC12 and BAC14 and two of their downstream metabolites (Supplementary Fig. 10). Therefore, considering all orthogonal information, metabolite annotations in this study are assigned Confidence Level 3 (“tentative candidates”), consistent with the presence of clear structural relationships but remaining positional uncertainty34. Aldehydes and carboxylic acid metabolites showing no isomers in the LC-MS analysis would correspond to level 2b, while dosed compounds with available standards correspond to level 1.

Larsson et al. identified α-, ß-, and ω-oxidation pathways for BACs as well as hydroxylation reactions24,35 by means of HPLC-HRMS/MS. Using DESI-MSI, we could detect especially those metabolites from the beginning of the BAC biotransformation cascade in samples from all time points (Fig. 2). A scheme of the pathways and enzymes involved in the production of these metabolites is shown in Fig. 4g. As conventional MSI cannot distinguish between positional isomers, these metabolites are discussed in combination with data on isomers obtained using HPLC-MS/MS, where these isomers are separated by retention times. According to HPLC-MS/MS analysis, BAC metabolism in these biofilms is generally dominated by one isomer of each metabolite (except those from mono-hydroxylation). As ionization behavior between DESI and ESI is similar36, we expect the same most abundant metabolites to dominate the MS images. Exemplary for BAC12, the first metabolite is hydroxylated BAC12, M320. Three isomers of M320 have been resolved by HPLC-MS/MS (Supplementary Fig. 11b) from the aqueous phase of the reactor. Following35 all hydroxy groups are located on the alkyl chain. While one isomer is oxidized on the terminal carbon and thus results from ω-oxidation, the other isomers must be oxidized on carbon atoms other than the terminal one. Terminal carbon atoms are typically oxidized by non-HEM di-iron-based enzymes, while the hydroxylation of carbon atoms along the chain would usually be conducted by cytochrome P450 monooxygenasises27–30. Further terminal oxidation products M318 (aldehyde) and M334 (terminal carboxylic acid) were detected24 by MSI as well as HPLC-MS/MS. Additionally, M336 (di-hydroxy BAC12), resulting from the addition of two hydroxy groups to BAC12, was detected with five isomers in LC-MS/MS. For all of these di-hydroxy BAC12 isomers, at least one of the hydroxy groups must be located on non-terminal carbon atoms of the alkyl chain, as a maximum of one hydroxy group can be localized on the terminal carbon and cytochrom P450 monooxygenases must be involved in their formation.

The colocalization between the metabolites and their BAC12 precursor, as expressed by the spatial colocalization index (SCI), are high, ranging from 0.50–0.82 (with SCI = 1.00 meaning identical distribution, Fig. 4c–f), but clearly diverges from the distribution of the parent BAC12. The analogous metabolites are observed for BAC14 (Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 15); i.e., M348 (mono hydroxylation of BAC14) is the start of the ω-oxidation cascade, followed by M346 for the BAC14 aldehyde, M362 for the BAC14 carboxylic acid and M364 for the double hydroxylated metabolite of BAC14. The same number of isomers is detected for these metabolites by HPLC-HRMS/MS for both BAC12 and BAC14. However, the non-terminally modified isomers appear to be increased for BAC14 in comparison to BAC12.

MS images of BAC12 and its metabolite distributions are shown in Fig. 4a–f. Regions of metabolite abundance occur macroscopically in the same biofilm regions as those observed to be high in parent micropollutant abundance. However, to ensure that the different spatial distribution is an expression of metabolization and not of sorption, the spatial distributions of the different BAC12 metabolites (M) were related to their respective precursor (P) compounds on a pixel-by-pixel basis, by forming metabolite-to-precursor (M/P) ratios (Fig. 5). These ratios further offer the advantage that matrix effects are vastly compensated due to the high chemical similarity of the respective compounds. If metabolism occurs at a uniform rate throughout the entire biofilm, the M/P ratios are expected to be uniform. However, the M/P images shown in Fig. 5 reveal heterogeneity for all the identified metabolites, suggesting that the production of metabolites is localized to specific microbial niches. Such different niches could consist of different microcolonies of specific organisms or mixtures thereof. Such microcolonies could also trigger gradients of co-substrates, signaling or oxygen16. Notably, SCI values of the M/P ratios to the BAC12 precursor are considerably lower than those of the metabolite single ion images to the single ion image of BAC12 (Fig. 5).

A particularly striking difference is visible between the production sites of metabolites M320 (Mono-hydroxy BAC12 via ω-oxidation) and M318 (BAC12 aldehyde via a dehydrogenase) from their respective precursors. Both metabolites are detected in most pixels of the biofilm, yet their production varies significantly: The mono-hydroxylated M320 metabolite occurs largely in the same areas where the parent compound is detected with the highest abundance, on the left and top border of the biofilm specimen shown in Fig. 5a. An entirely different production pattern, however, can be observed for M318 (BAC12 aldehyde), the downstream metabolite of M320. M318 production hotspots are observed in distinctly different locations than those of M320, with more pronounced aldehyde production in the central biofilm regions. This is further supported by the very low SCI values between these two M/P images comparing the 2nd and the 3rd column, which are generally in the negative range (SCI range from -1 to 1, SCI = 1 meaning perfectly identical while SCI = -1 means perfect opposite patterns). This suggests that different microbial species or niches could be involved in these two different enzymatic transformations. The same trends are visible on other biofilm replicates from the same (198 h) and different incubation time points (366 h). However, due to highly heterogeneous distribution patterns and differing measurement sensitivities, a direct comparison of SCI values beyond trends remains difficult. Due to the lower signal intensity of the metabolites further into the metabolic pathway, M334 (carboxy BAC12) and M336 (di-hydroxy BAC12), not all pixels contain signals for these metabolites. Ratios and SCI values, however, can only be calculated in such pixels where both (parent and metabolite) signals are present, restricting their use in this case (see Fig. 4e, f). We also observed the same trends shown in Fig. 5 for BAC14 metabolites (see Supplementary Fig. 16). SCI values for the time-course experiment shown in Fig. 2 are given in Supplementary Fig. 17. The same reactions showed the same SCI trend. After 149 h, the differences in SCIs among different reactions become larger.

To investigate localized BAC12 metabolism further, we chose two biotransformation reactions that originate from the same parent, i.e., M318 from M320 (oxidation of an alcohol to an aldehyde) and M336 from M320 (second hydroxylation, predominantly along the alkyl chain). An overlay of both metabolite production localizations in red and green, respectively, is shown in Fig. 6a, while Fig. 6b shows biofilm regions of interest (ROIs) chosen for spectral comparison due to the high production of one metabolite over another. Multivariate analysis of these regions using unsupervised Principal Component Analysis (Fig. 6c) shows a trend of separation for these two regions based on their metabolic composition. This is further demonstrated in the volcano plot shown in Fig. 6d, showing a variety of metabolic constituents significantly changed in these areas. Figure 6e shows the signal abundance of some of these features that were found significantly altered in the volcano plot, such as m/z 330.21 and m/z 633.50 (tentatively assigned as CerPE 32:1;O2, i.e., N-(octadecanoyl)-tetradecasphing-4- enine-1-phosphoethanolamine)), which are significantly increased in those areas that are producing the second hydroxylation reaction, while m/z 546.32 (tentatively assigned to PE(20:0), i.e. (2-aminoethoxy)[(2 R)-2,3-bis(icosanoyloxy)propoxy]phosphinic acid)) is significantly increased in those areas where the terminal aldehyde is produced. However, it remains to be determined whether these are microbial lipids and from which microbes these lipids originate.

Discussion

The present study is a mechanistic investigation of spatially resolved micropollutant metabolism in biofilms. It was necessary to work with concentrations considerably higher than real-world levels to detect low-abundance metabolites by MSI. While MSI has unique capabilities to use localization data to infer function through colocalization, it is generally significantly less sensitive than HPLC-MS/MS due to the lack of separation and pre-concentration.

The results presented in this study support the finding that in operationally defined technical multispecies biofilms, different metabolic reactions (di-hydroxylation vs. ω-oxidation as well as hydroxylation vs. aldehyde formation) on BACs are located in different loci and are not uniformly distributed over the biomass. We found that metabolites from different metabolic pathways, especially those associated with dehydrogenase and mono-hydroxylation activities, do not co-localize. This means that localizable single cells or microcolonies are responsible for performing selected reactions/groups of reactions, rather than these being uniformly distributed cells. Understanding the ecology of the respective microcolonies in the biofilm will enable us to understand the performance of the biofilms to a level that was hitherto not possible. Thus, the micro-inhomogeneity of biofilms is not an obstacle but a prerequisite for highly functional (effective) biofilm reactors in environmental technology.

When using MSI in a scenario as described, two possibilities can be responsible for the observed results: (i) localized sorption or (ii) localized metabolization (or a combination of both). Option (i) requires sorption sites in the biofilm with a considerably higher specificity to the metabolites than to the parents, which we deem improbable due to the high structural similarity between parent and metabolites in this case. To account for sorption, we have demonstrated localized metabolite formation by presenting metabolite/parent ratios to localize metabolism. However, this approach requires a) consistent detection of signals belonging to both parent and metabolite and b) metabolites with the same strongly sorbing functional group as the parent (in this case, metabolism maintaining the tert. ammonium group and a long alkyl chain).

Using this approach, we were able to provide a new perspective on the metabolic spatio-temporal heterogeneity of micropollutant degradation in complex biofilms, which begins to fill gaps in current knowledge of localized biotransformation in biofilms and offers opportunities for further exploration. We expect that the knowledge about metabolically more and less active zones that were discovered in this study, as well as metabolism for different reactions located in mesoscopic metabolic niches, will be useful for controlling and engineering biofilm growth so that these biofilms can be better utilized for the removal of micropollutants in wastewater.

We noticed that in this experimental MBBR setup, there are considerable differences between biofilms even within an individual time point and carrier (Fig. 5). Thus, it can be concluded that the microstructure of the biofilms is unique from square to square and bigger samples or averaging over a larger number of squares might be recommended in the future. We further experienced that MS1 and even MS2 imaging approaches are not able to resolve or separate isomers of micropollutant metabolites. Future work exploring the incorporation of ion mobility separations into imaging experiments is needed to distinguish micropollutant metabolite isomers in MSI experiments, which will be imperative to distinguish between different enzymatic pathways involved in the biotransformation reactions37. Other possibilities include linking some new technologies to MSI, such as electron-induced dissociation38 and ion-ion reactions39.

We demonstrated that the localization of metabolite production comes with inherent changes in the local metabolic composition of the biofilm, as demonstrated by lipid composition. We also showed that TSMs can be used on environmental biofilm specimens to detect bacteria, showing that species of these bacteria are largely distributed in hotspots over the entire biofilm area. While TSMs and thus specific bacterial species can be found in some areas where high metabolite production was observed, no TSM explains the localized production patterns entirely. As a result, the presented approach does not allow, at this stage, to associate specific biotransformation products with individual bacterial species. This might be possible in the future by expanding the database for TSM generation, structurally identifying and separating unique metabolite isomers through orthogonal dimensions such as ion mobility, or a rather coarse analysis of dissected biotransformation hotspots, followed by sequencing analysis.

Using this MSI-based approach as a foundation for further methodological developments, we believe it will be possible to gain a deeper insight into biofilm processes and thus achieve a better design of biofilm reactors, steering the processes in these reactors more towards a “fit to purpose” outcome in the long term. If, hypothetically, a wanted process is allocated more on the rims of the carrier structures than in the middle, carrier design could be changed towards more rims. Knowing which group of microorganisms is actually performing the task, even when collaborating with other microorganisms, can be used to promote these organisms in a targeted way. For such analysis, a future combination of this approach would be to guide laser capture microdissection by MSI and conduct untargeted sequencing-based analyses to identify microbial species that are locally increased in areas of high metabolite production. Further development of the TSM approach31 to cover a wider array of environmentally-relevant organisms might also improve the colocalization of bacterial species with biotransformation hotspots, especially if metabolite isomers can be visualized independently

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Two representative compounds, each for five commonly detected classes of micropollutants, were chosen for this study: benzalkonium compounds (BACs) with dodecyl- and tetradecyl groups, i.e., BAC12 and BAC14 (quaternary ammonium compounds), erythromycin and roxithromycin (macrolides), sulfadiazine and sulfamethoxazole (sulfonamides), ciprofloxacin and ofloxacin (fluoroquinolones), as well as tetracycline and chlortetracycline (tetracyclines). Their structure and relevant physicochemical properties are shown in Supplementary Fig.1. Tetracycline (TC), Chlortetracycline (CTC), Sulfadiazine (SDZ), Sulfamethoxazole (SMX), Ciprofloxacin (CIP), Ofloxacin (OFL), Erythromycin (ERY), Roxithromycin (ROX), Benzalkonium chloride 12 (BAC12), Benzalkonium chloride 14 (BAC14), Poly N-(hydroxypropyl)-methacrylate, polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) (MW 360 kDa), (Hydroxypropyl)-methylcellulose (HPMC) (viscosity 40–60 cP, 2% in H2O (20 °C) and methanol were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). The stock solutions were prepared at a concentration of 100 mg L-1 in HPLC-MS grade methanol and stored at –20 °C until usage.

Biofilm generation

The biofilms were grown on polypropylene carriers (AnoxKaldnes™ Z200, with a maximum (wet) biofilm thickness of 200 µm and an area of 1280 mm2/carrier40. These were conditioned for three months in two laboratory moving bed biofilm reactors (MBBRs) at 22 °C by initially inoculating with heterogeneous bacterial communities originating from a CAS-WWTP for two days. Each of the 1 L MBBRs held 100 carriers and was supplied continuously with synthetic wastewater (100 µL min-1), i.e., 3 g L-1 glucose, 0.3 g L-1 ammonium bicarbonate ≥99.5%, 0.03 g L-1 yeast extract and 0.015 g L-1 microcrystalline cellulose from Sigma-Aldrich (Darmstadt, Germany). The reactors were aerated to achieve oxygen saturation in the bulk water. Specimens of the carriers were removed from the respective conditioning reactors and incubated in 200 mL Erlenmeyer flasks, along with elevated concentrations of the tested micropollutants.

Micropollutant incubation

As mechanistic experiments in the laboratory were intended, we worked with concentrations exceeding wastewater concentrations by two orders of magnitude26,41. This enabled the production of sufficient metabolites to detect them over the entire biofilm surface, which is a prerequisite for a pixel-by-pixel assessment of metabolite-to-parent compound ratios to evaluate the extent of localized biotransformation. For the incubations, 0.6 mL of the methanolic stock solution was spiked into 60 mL of water, resulting in a final micropollutant concentration of 1 mg L-1 and a methanol content of ~1% v/v. Although a low methanol concentration can alter kinetics, it is not expected to interfere with the MSI experiment. 40 carriers with pre-grown biofilms were added to the flask at the beginning of the experiment (t = 0 h). The flask was then placed on a shaker (140 rpm) and covered in aluminum foil to avoid photodegradation. Subsequently, biofilm carriers and 1 mL bulk water were sampled (i.e., removed from the incubation) at 0, 4, 7, 24, 30, 54, 78, 149, 198, 246, and 366 h. At each time point, two carriers were removed from the incubation (Erlenmeyer flask): One carrier was placed in a Falcon tube and frozen directly at −80 °C, while the second carrier was shock-frozen by immersion into liquid nitrogen, placed into a 20 mL screw cap vial, covered with aluminum foil to prevent dust contamination, and lyophilized for 24 h at –103 °C using a ScanVac CoolSafe 110-4 freeze dryer (Labogene, Allerød, Denmark). Samples were stored at −20 °C until analysis. All substrate samples were stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Control biofilm specimens were produced at t = −0.01 h, i.e., before micropollutants were added, and processed (frozen, lyophilized) as described under sample preparation for MSI. Controls with inactivated biofilm were not included in this study, as inactivated biofilms would detach from the carrier and thus be unsuitable for a longitudinal experiment under constant movement. Controls without biofilms were not included, as biofilms were removed from the carriers prior to imaging, and we do not expect metabolism to occur on the native carriers. While we expect some sorption to the carrier, we expect this to only affect the data shown in Fig. 1b. Time-matched control specimens were not included as the addition of micropollutants is likely to change microbial composition (as observed in Supplementary Fig. 8), complicating comparisons to incubations obtained from a different flask.

LC-HRMS measurement of bulk supernatant

Micropollutants and metabolites were determined in the bulk water by HPLC-HRMS utilizing a SCIEX ExionLC 2.0 + UPLC system (AB SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA) coupled to an API 6600 quadrupole/time-of-flight (Q-TOF) mass analyzer (AB SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA). An Acquity UPLC BEH Shield RP18 1.7 μm (2.1×30 mm) (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) at 30 °C was used. The HPLC eluents were of A: water with 0.1% formic acid (Chromasolv, Honeywell) and B: methanol with 0.1% formic acid (Chromasolv, Honeywell). Flow was 0.3 mL min-1 while the gradient was: 0% B 0–1 min, 0%- > 80% B 1- > 9 min, 80%- > 100% B 9- > 11 min, 100% B 11-16. The HRMS operating parameters were as follows: electrospray ionization in positive mode (ESI + ). TOF mass range: 101–1200 Da; accumulation time: 250 ms; ion spray voltage: 5300 V; ion source temperature: 500 °C; collision energy 20–50 eV24.

Sample preparation for MSI

The Z200 carrier used in this study has a structure that forces the biofilms to grow in little squares of 3 × 3 mm. Only the lyophilized specimens were used in this study. Removing water from the biofilms prior to frozen storage reduces artifact formation during subsequent freezing and thawing. Falcon tubes used for storage were opened only after samples were brought to room temperature, to prevent condensation on the cold carriers. Each carrier contained about 160 individual biofilm specimens. Individual biofilm squares were subsequently carefully transferred onto microscope glass slides (Superfrost microscope glass slide) using tweezers, where they were fixed using a hydrogel. The hydrogel matrix was composed of 7.5% HPMC and 2.5% PVP42. It was applied evenly and in a thin layer on the slide using a brush, and biofilms were instantly placed on the hydrogel film using tweezers. Samples were left to air dry until the hydrogel solidified completely. Samples were vacuum-packed and kept at −80 °C until MSI analysis. Prior to analysis, biofilms were optically imaged using an Olympus CX31 microscope (at 4x magnification) with a camera attachment compatible with a regular iPhone.

DESI-MSI

Slides with biofilms were analyzed using a Q-Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, USA) equipped with an automated, home-built 2D-DESI ion source as described elsewhere43. Samples were scanned in positive and negative polarity using a mass range of m/z 150–1200 and a mass resolution of 70,000 (at m/z 200). Ion source parameters were set: spray voltage: ±4.5 kV, capillary temperature: 320 °C, S-lens RF level: 100, injection time: 250 ms. A home-built Swagelok DESI sprayer23 was used and operated with a mixture of 95% methanol and 5% water delivered with a volumetric flow rate of 1–1.5 µL min-1 and nebulized with N2 at a backpressure of 7 bar. The distance of the sprayer to the sample surface was 1.5 mm at a spray angle of 75°, the distance of the sprayer to the MS inlet was 5 mm, and the distance of the MS inlet to the sample surface was 1 mm at a collection angle of 10°. The solvent was delivered using a Harvard Apparatus Pump 11 Elite. The step size (in x- and y-direction) for the time course experiment was set to 75 µm (276 µm s-1 scan speed). To enable a more detailed assessment of the compound distribution and to test whether the metabolism is distributed evenly, individual biofilm specimens were analyzed at a 50 µm step size (163 µm s−1 scan speed). As the DESI imaging process generally did not damage the biofilm specimen (Supplementary Fig. 3), positive and negative ion modes were analyzed on the same biofilms sequentially by repeating the same measurement in the opposite polarity without changing imaging coordinates. While the measurements in positive ion mode were used to assess micropollutant distribution and metabolism, measurements in negative ion mode were used to assess taxon-specific markers. Using the 20–80 method to determine feature resolution in the absence of a ground truth specimen44, we determined a resolution in the range of 60 to 110 µm (Supplementary Fig. 22).

Image data analysis

The resulting raw files were converted to.mzML files using msConvert of the ProteoWizard 3.0 toolkit45, and subsequently compiled to.imzML format using imzML converter v1.346. MSiReader47 was used to generate the ion images and a 10 ppm tolerance was used to export the pixel-by-pixel intensities for each m/z value. All peaks in the centroided data were rounded to an m/z resolution of 0.001, and the frequency of each peak was determined across all pixels of the image. This vector was first smoothed with a moving sum filter equivalent to 10 ppm and then by a moving Gaussian filter of 15 ppm. From this, any local maxima present greater than 300 pixels (approx. 1.8%) were regarded as peaks and extracted from the data with a ± 50 ppm tolerance. A total of 3128 peaks were identified. All images shown are raw intensity, as TIC normalization was not possible due to the high BAC intensity governing the TIC (Supplementary Fig. 23). The spatial colocalization indexes (SCI) are based on the Pearson correlation coefficient calculated on the image on a pixel-by-pixel basis for non-zero pixels only and were used to quantify the colocalization of single ion images and M/P ratios. For multivariate analysis, spectra were normalized according to total intensity over all variables (n = 3019) and multiplied by 1000, and then log10 transformed with an added offset of 1; principal components and univariate analyses were run using this data. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to identify features that differed significantly between the red and green hotspots. The log2 fold change was determined based on the mean intensity over the selected pixels. Variables with infinite fold changes were set to 0, as were those with a 25th percentile of zero. Drug-associated peaks were removed from the data by first computing the Pearson correlation between each known metabolite and all other peaks. A total of 43 drug-related ions were correlated to 66 other features. Thus, a total of 66 + 43 = 109 features were removed from the total 3128 across the 150 <m/z < 1200 range. Additional Methods concerning Fig. 6a can be found in the Supplementary Material.

MSI peak identification

Candidate pollutant metabolites were collected from our previous studies24 and the metabolism prediction tool in LipostarMS48. The compiled list of putative metabolites was used to screen for suspects in the biofilm MSI data. Exact masses were used for all identifications, and the mass tolerance was set as ±5 ppm. The candidate metabolites detected in biofilms were further characterized by MS/MS in the supernatant collected from the reactor for molecular identification and on biofilm MS/MS using DESI, where possible. To verify the structures of metabolites in biofilm, fragmentations of BACs and relative metabolites were conducted directly in biofilms. LIPID MAPS was used to tentatively identify lipid species using exact mass49.
