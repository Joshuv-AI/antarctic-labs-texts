Effects of Mecobalamin on the Prevention of Complex Regional Pain Syndrome Type 1 Following Foot Ankle Surgery: A Randomized, Double-Blinded, Placebo-Controlled Trial
Authors: Marut Arunakul, Chakkris Leesakul, Pheemaphol Samornpitakul, Prapasri Kulalert, Adinun Apivatgaroon, Preeyaphan Arunakul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Mecobalamin has been proposed to improve the outcomes of Foot and Ankle patients with complex regional pain syndrome type 1 (CRPS-1). The primary outcome was to evaluate the effects of mecobalamin on the event rate of CRPS-1 after foot ankle surgery. The secondary outcomes were to compare the visual analog scale (VAS) and functional outcomes of both groups.Methods:The study recruited 440 patients. Operative foot and ankle patients were randomized into control (220 patients) and Mecobalamin groups (220 patients). Patients were assessed for CRPS-1 using the Budapest criteria for a follow-up period of 12 months. Preoperative and postoperative foot and ankle ability measure, Short Form 36, and VAS were compared.Results:Analysis included 432 patients. CRPS-1 occurred in 4 patients (1.8%) in the mecobalamin group compared with 20 patients (9.3%) in the placebo group. After adjustment using multivariable regression, mecobalamin was associated with a significantly lower risk of CRPS-1, with the risk difference of −8.1% (95% confidence interval: −12.4 to −3.8; p < 0.001) compared with placebo. Postoperative pain and functional outcomes also favored mecobalamin. The reduction in VAS score from preoperation to postoperation was significantly greater in the mecobalamin group, with an adjusted mean difference of −0.49 (95% confidence interval: −0.8 to −0.2; p = 0.001) at the 1-year follow-up.Conclusion:Prophylactic mecobalamin significantly lowered the occurrence of CRPS-1 in foot ankle surgery.Level of Evidence:Level I. Introduction

The Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS) is characterized by a pain that is manifested excessively when compared with the actual degree of tissue injury. The syndrome is diagnosed using the Budapest Criteria; 2022-revised version of the original criteria developed by the International Association for the Study of Pain, a widely accepted standardized framework for diagnosing CRPS1. CRPS can be classified into 2 types. Type 1 (complex regional pain syndrome type 1 [CRPS-1]) has no clear nerve damage, whereas type 2 (CRPS-2) arises after nerve injury1. Patients often present with allodynia that is accompanied by soft-tissue swelling and trophic changes of the skin after trauma, surgery, or cast immobilization2. Following wrist and hand, the foot and ankle region is the second most common site of CRPS3, of which its postoperative incidence is approximately 4.36 to 13.6%4-8, possibly due to the relatively thin soft tissue coverage, as well as the densely packed peripheral nerves in the foot and ankle region. Hence, patients are predisposed to the development of CRPS-1. There is still a lack of consensus on the clear pathophysiological “gold standard” for CRPS diagnosis and treatment; however, multidisciplinary pain management techniques emphasize on the functional restoration through the resetting of altered central processing and normalizing of the distal environment2,9-11. Although early detection and treatment of CRPS-I are associated with improved outcomes, preventing the development of CRPS-I is ultimately more effective12. Limited studies, however, have explored the prevention of CRPS-1 in the realm of foot ankle surgery, both of which used vitamin C as prophylactic treatment after injuries and surgeries around the foot and ankle5,13.

Mecobalamin is an activated form of vitamin B12, which is extensively prescribed in neuropathies and malnutrition. It helps expedite nerve regeneration, facilitate neurotransmission, and prevent neuronal toxicities14,15. Several studies have investigated the potential of mecobalamin to alleviate neuropathic pain such as diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN), where it has shown promising outcomes in terms of pain reduction and alleviation of numbness14,16,17. In fact, a recently conducted randomized controlled trial to examine the effects of mecobalamin on patients with CRPS-1 of the foot and ankle also revealed a statistically significant improvement in various outcome measures18. This clinical trial aimed to evaluate the effects of mecobalamin on the event rate of CRPS type I after foot ankle surgery. The secondary outcomes were to compare the visual analog scale (VAS) and functional outcomes of the mecobalamin and placebo groups.

Materials and Methods

This single-centered, prospective, double-blinded, randomized, controlled trial was conducted between June 2022 and May 2025. The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Thammasat University (Medicine; MTU-EC-OT-6-188/64) and registered in Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20220606006). Inclusion criteria consisted of patients aged at least 18 years who underwent foot and/or ankle surgery performed by a single Foot Ankle Orthopaedist between June 2022 and October 2023.

Patients with comorbidities associated with neuropathies, such as diabetes mellitus with target organ involvement, documented history of vitamin B adverse reactions, patients whose pain could not be evaluated, patients with impaired cognitive function, and patients with history of CRPS, anxiety and/or emotional and mental disorders were excluded from the study. Pregnant individuals, patients taking additional supplements containing vitamin B, and patients who underwent minor operations, including ganglion cyst and hardware removals, were also excluded.

A total of 440 eligible patients were screened and enrolled by an independent physician who was not involved in the outcome assessment. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before inclusion in the study. After enrollment, participants were randomly assigned to receive either study medication or an identical placebo. The medications were prepared and administered by an independent physician to maintain allocation concealment.

Both participants and the treating physicians were blinded to treatment allocation. The placebo was identical in appearance to the study medication. The physician responsible for diagnosing CRPS and the investigators collecting the outcome scores were also blinded to the treatment allocation and were not involved in the medication administration process.

After all participants provided informed consent, demographic data were recorded, and the participants were preoperatively allocated into 2 groups using a stratified, block of 4 randomization. Stratification was conducted according to the operative procedure patients underwent. A total of 150 tablets with identical appearance, consisting of Mecobalamin (500 mcg) and starch placebo, produced by the Applied Thai traditional Medical Institute of Thammasat University Hospital, were sealed into opaque envelopes by the research assistant. The participants were instructed to consume 1 tablet, 3 times daily, after meals, for a duration of 50 days after surgery. At weeks 1, 2, 6, and 12, the research assistant counted the number of leftover tablets at each follow-up visit to ensure good drug compliance. Operation-specific postoperative protocol was advised to each patient, and the participants were provided with an identical home medication regimen, which consisted of cephalexin (500 mg) 1 tablet per oral 4 times a day (24 tablets), paracetamol (500 mg) 1 tablet per oral as needed for pain every 6 hours (20 tablets), naproxen (250 mg) 1 tablet per oral 2 times a day (10 tablets), omeprazole (20 mg) 1 tablet per oral 2 times a day (10 tablets), and tramadol (50) 1 tablet per oral as needed for severe pain every 8 hours (20 tablets).

Patients were assessed at postoperative weeks 1, 2, and 6 and months 3, 6, 9, and 12 for the event rate of CRPS Type I (CRPS-1) using the Budapest Criteria (Appendix 1) by the Foot Ankle Orthopedist. CRPS was diagnosed if patient experienced pain disproportionate to the inciting event and presented with at least 3 symptoms and 2 signs (evidence) of sensory/vasomotor/sudomotor/motor changes; (1) Sensory—hyperesthesia or allodynia, (2) Vasomotor—color or temperature asymmetry, (3) Sudomotor—edema or sweating, and (4) Motor—decreased range of motion or weakness of the affected limb, and no other diagnosis better explains the symptoms. Evidence of vasomotor changes was assessed by comparing skin color changes in the affected side with the nonaffected side. Temperatures were measured using an infrared thermometer; a difference of ≥1°C was considered significant.

Preoperative foot and ankle ability measure, Short Form 36 (SF-36), and VAS were compared with the postoperative measurements at 1 year.

Statistical Analysis

All data were analyzed using Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Categorical variables are presented as percentages, whereas continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD. The independent t test was used to compare normally distributed continuous variables, while the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied for non-normally distributed continuous variables. The χ2 test or Fisher exact test was used to compare categorical variables, as appropriate. Multivariable regression models, including mean difference regression, were used to adjust for imbalances in preoperative baseline characteristics (foot and ankle ability measure-activities of daily living [FAAM-ADL] and Short Form 36-mental component summary [SF-36 MCS] scores). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Sample size was calculated based on CRPS-1 event rate of our pilot study of 200 patients who underwent foot and/or ankle surgery following the same inclusion and exclusion criteria of this study. The event rate of CRPS-1 in patients with and without mecobalamin were 2% and 10%, respectively. Using the formula of two-sample comparison of proportions test, alpha 0.05, power of 90, and 10% dropout rate, the sample required at least 202 patients in each group.

Results

A total of 440 participants enrolled in this study, of which 8 patients were lost to follow-up from the trial, 3 from the mecobalamin group, and 5 from the placebo group. A total of 432 patients were analyzed. Of these, 60.4% (261 individuals) were female, and 39.6% (171 individuals) were male. The mean age was 51.1 ± 16 years. Two hundred and seventeen patients were from the mecobalamin group, and 215 were from the placebo group, as shown in the CONsolidated Standards Of Reporting Trials flowchart (Fig. 1). The total follow-up time was 12 months. All follow-ups were conducted as face-to-face, office visits. No significant differences were discovered between the 2 groups regarding demographics, as well as the operative type and data (Table I).

Discussion

This trial revealed that prophylactic mecobalamin significantly lowered the occurrence of CRPS-1 in foot ankle surgery. The mecobalamin group showed significantly improved functional outcomes and reduced pain scores postoperatively, potentially indicating that mecobalamin may generally improve functional outcomes of postoperative foot and ankle patients. Nevertheless, the increased functional outcomes in mecobalamin may also be explained by the significant reduction of CRPS-1 occurrence in the particular group. As a result, the whole mecobalamin group seemingly had increased functional outcomes.

The findings are consistent with a past study on DPN, which discovered that a daily intake of mecobalamin reduced the rate of development of DPN manifestations19. Several other randomized controlled trials and a meta-analysis also exhibited mecobalamin’s potential to treat certain peripheral neuropathies, including DPN and postherpetic neuralgia20-22. Finally, a recently conducted trial on the effects of mecobalamin on the functional outcomes of CRPS-1 supported its capabilities in alleviating neuropathic pain and promoting nerve regeneration18,23,24.

The Budapest Criteria were used in the current study to diagnose CRPS-1. Although usage of skin temperatures in its diagnosis has been mentioned, as included in the American Medical Association criteria for CRPS diagnosis, inconsistency still exists regarding the variations in skin temperatures during the initial and late phases of CRPS. In fact, a study discovered that 29.7% of the skin temperature changes that occurred in CRPS-1 patients did not fulfill the American Medical Association criteria, and most patients had decreases, rather than increased skin temperatures during the initial phase25.

Although the downregulation of certain cytokines, such as IL-1B and IL-4, may begin after approximately 28 days26 and the neuropathic symptoms that accompany CRPS often occur within the first month of nerve injury2, an animal study on allodynia revealed that inflammatory markers may remain active in the dorsal root ganglion after nerve injuries for approximately 42 days, thus resulting in chronic neuropathic pain27. As a result, prophylactic mecobalamin against CRPS-1 was prescribed for 50 days in our study to extensively cover this period.

Although the use of vitamin C to prevent CRPS-1 is popular among hand and wrist surgeons28-30, and several studies proposed its utilization in the foot and ankle region5,13, the mechanism by which vitamin C prevents CRPS-1 is inconclusive31. Suggested mechanisms include the formation of free radical scavengers and a decrease in lipid peroxidation, resulting in a protection of the capillary endothelium5,13. However, a recent randomized-controlled trial, along with a meta-analysis, has discovered prophylactic vitamin C to demonstrate no clinical benefits in the prevention of CRPS-132,33, thereby indicating that its efficacy in the prevention of CRPS-1 remains debatable31. Hence, the selection of prophylactic vitamin B12 over vitamin C in this particular clinical trial. In addition, mecobalamin was selected in the trial rather than inactive vitamin B12 (cyanocobalamin) due to its enhanced bioavailability and tissue retention, increased liver storage, and decreased urinary excretion34.

Past studies discovered that significantly impaired functional ability and reduced health-related quality of life accompany patients with CRPS6,35. In addition, patients with CRPS often exhibit resistant responses to conservative and interventional therapies36,37, resulting in excessive healthcare resource utilization and expenditures38. As a result, effective treatment or prevention of CRPS are essential to control these excessive healthcare expenditures39, thus reinforcing the significance of our study.

There were limitations to our trial. First, the study was single-centered. As a result, the sample size was relatively small. Second, diagnosis of CRPS was determined by a single reviewer. Although standardized diagnostic criteria were applied, several components of the criteria such as vasomotor and sudomotor changes are inherently subjective, which may introduce observer bias and limit interobserver reliability. The use of multiple independent evaluators or a formal assessment of interobserver agreement would strengthen diagnostic validity in future studies. The observed effect sizes were relatively small. Although statistically significant differences were detected, the modest magnitude of the treatment effect may limit the clinical relevance of the findings and raises the possibility that the study was underpowered to detect larger or more clinically meaningful differences. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these findings and better define the clinical impact of the intervention. Third, significant differences in preoperative baseline characteristics, specifically FAAM-ADL and SF-36 MCS scores, were observed between the groups. To address this imbalance, adjusted analyses were performed using multivariable regression models, including mean difference regression.

Regardless of the limitations, this study discovered that prophylactic mecobalamin significantly lowered the event rate of CRPS-1 after foot ankle surgery and improved pain and functional outcomes of postoperative Foot and Ankle patients.
