Directed Evolution in Escherichia coli for Novel Ligand‐Binding Regulators: Evolving a Progesterone‐Responsive Transcription Factor to Bind Cortisol
Authors: Alia Clark‐ElSayed, Kaila E Nayvelt, Satoshi Ishida, Simon d'Oelsnitz, Andrew D Ellington
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAllosteric transcription factors (aTFs) regulate gene expression in response to small molecules. Engineering aTFs with new ligand specificities expands their utility in environmental monitoring and diagnostics. Here, we present protocols for improving the responsiveness of an aTF, SRTF1, to its non‐native ligand, cortisol, based on the SELIS (Seamless Enrichment of Ligand‐Inducible Sensors) methodology. First, an aTF library is generated by randomizing the ligand binding pocket. These libraries are then subjected to a selection process to remove protein variants that have lost the ability to repress transcription. The libraries are then plated onto solid media containing the target ligand. The most fluorescent colonies, signifying the most responsive aTFs, are cultured and assayed for the fold change in fluorescence upon induction with the target ligand. Colonies with the highest fold change are cultured, and the plasmid DNA is purified and sequenced. The gene variants are then subcloned into new expression vectors and assayed in a dose‐response assay with the target and non‐target ligands to have complete characterization of the sensor. Through one round of these protocols, we improved the response of SRTF1 to cortisol by seven‐fold; however, the protocols outlined here are applicable to virtually any protein:effector pair. © 2025 The Author(s) Current Protocols published by Wiley Periodicals LLC. INTRODUCTION

Transcription factors are an important class of gene expression regulators that include activators and repressors. In particular, there are numerous allosteric transcription factors (aTFs) that regulate metabolism and signaling by responding to small molecules (Libis et al., 2016). aTFs belong to diverse protein families, most notably the TetR and LysR families (Cuthbertson & Nodwell, 2013; Maddocks & Oyston, 2008), and generally have two domains: a DNA‐binding domain and a ligand‐binding domain. Binding of a small molecule to the ligand‐binding domain causes conformational changes in the protein that can either increase or decrease the protein's affinity for DNA, resulting in subsequent changes in gene expression (Fig. 1).

Schematic of repressor activity (A) Binding of the repressor to the operator sequence prevents RNA polymerase access and inhibits transcription. (B) In the presence of a ligand, binding to the repressor causes its dissociation from the operator, allowing for transcription.

A variety of aTFs have been used as biosensors in a range of applications, including water contamination detection (Agarwal et al., 2025), biomarker detection (Cao et al., 2018), and metabolic engineering (Tang & Cirino, 2011). Here, we focus on engineering an aTF, SRTF1, from a steroid‐metabolizing bacterium, Pimelobacter simplex (Grazon et al., 2020). SRTF1 significantly responds to progesterone, and we attempted to change its activity to respond to a different effector molecule, cortisol. While the choice of both aTF and effector is slightly arbitrary, depending on user needs, initial assays showed that SRTF1 displayed a small amount of cross‐reactivity to cortisol, and therefore was likely a good starting point for evolving a cortisol‐responsive sensor.

Previous efforts have led to the development of a directed evolution workflow for aTFs that we term Seamless Enrichment of Ligand‐Inducible Sensors, or SELIS (d'Oelsnitz, Kim, et al., 2022; d'Oelsnitz, Nguyen, et al., 2022; d'Oelsnitz, Diaz, et al., 2024). This workflow relies on a combination of selection and screening to identify novel aTF variants. Escherichia coli cells containing an aTF library are initially subjected to a growth‐based selection to remove transcription factor variants that are unable to repress transcription. The library is then plated onto agar plates containing the target molecule, and the most fluorescent colonies (those containing the most responsive aTF variants) are picked and further assayed in the presence and absence of ligand to determine the fold change in induction. Colonies with the highest fold change are sequenced and subcloned into new expression vectors for a more complete dose‐response characterization. This process can then be repeated for multiple rounds until a desired EC50 is achieved.

In this article, we provide methods for identifying aTF variants that respond to a specific small molecule target. In Basic Protocol 1, we outline the design and amplification of a DNA library. This can be used in conjunction with Support Protocol 1 and Support Protocol 2 for modeling the protein:effector complex and designing the mutagenesis primers, respectively. In Basic Protocol 2, we outline the library construction through electrotransformation of reporter E. coli and library assessment using sequencing. In Basic Protocol 3, we describe the selective enrichment of aTF variants that can bind to the DNA operator and the screening for identifying improved target‐specific sensors. In Basic Protocol 4, we outline the workflow for genotyping variants, subcloning the variants into new backbones for validation, and sensor characterization using dose‐response assays. STRATEGIC PLANNING

The user must first choose what transcription factor they wish to alter. A valuable resource for identifying aTFs and their associated operator sequences is the GroovDB database, which includes a repository of ligand‐inducible transcription factors found in the literature (d'Oelsnitz, Love, et al., 2022). There also exist many uncharacterized regulatory factors for which DNA‐binding sequences are not known, and a helpful bioinformatic tool for identifying promoters for these is Snowprint (d'Oelsnitz, Stofel, et al., 2024). For cases where there is no known aTF to respond to the target ligand, resources such as Ligify and TFBMiner can predict which aTFs may be responsive to the defined ligand (Hanko et al., 2023; d'Oelsnitz, Love, et al., 2024). This protocol assumes that the user is starting from an expression vector (pREG) containing the aTF and a reporter plasmid (pSELIS) that contains an aTF‐responsive promoter, and that a dose‐response assay has been performed to determine the approximate EC50 and dynamic range of the wild‐type sensor with the target ligand.

Basic Protocol 1 involves mutagenizing the aTF sequence to create a library of aTFs and then assembling the DNA library into pReg. There are many methods for diversifying libraries; however, for the scope of this protocol, we will focus on site saturation mutagenesis (SSM) and, in particular, on combinatorial randomization of multiple residues in the ligand‐binding pocket. Given that screening is typically a limiting factor in engineering sensors, the use of SSM best allows for the functional orientation of new ligands in aTF variants (however, also see Critical Parameters: Library design). The modeling of the protein:effector complex is covered in Support Protocol 1; however, the field of protein modeling moves quickly and there may be other methods available (see Critical Parameters: Protein:effector complex modeling). The design of mutagenic primers is covered in Support Protocol 2.

Basic Protocol 2 sets up the library for enrichment. The aTF library in pReg is further transformed into E. coli DH10B that contain pSELIS. Transformation reactions are plated to determine the transformation efficiency, and overnight cultures are started in selective media. The following day, the glycerol stocks of the library are saved.

Basic Protocol 3 involves selection and screening. Cells are subjected to a negative selection based on antibiotic resistance to remove transcription factor variants that have lost their ability to repress transcription. The pSELIS plasmid contains an inverter circuit that selects for aTF variants that inactivate expression of the λ cI repressor, thereby allowing for the expression of zeocin resistance. Following the negative selection, cells are plated onto LB agar plates containing the target ligand, and induction of the aTF is measured via a GFP reporter. It should be noted that aTFs that are activators, rather than repressors, can potentially be engineered, but this might require a change in the configuration of the circuitry (also see Critical Parameters: Other types of transcription factors). Fluorescent colonies are picked, cultured, and re‐assayed in the presence and absence of the target ligand to validate induction and quantitatively determine the level of induction.

In Basic Protocol 4, the hits identified in Basic Protocol 3 are sequenced and further analyzed, ultimately yielding a complete dose‐response curve for the target ligand and a measure of specificity relative to potential cross‐reactive ligands.

These protocols are presented in the context of optimizing SRTF1, an aTF known to be responsive to progesterone, to respond to cortisol. By following the experimental procedure, induction was improved by 6‐ to 9‐fold at 100 µM cortisol.

LIBRARY DESIGN

aTF library

This protocol aims to design the aTF library, introduce mutations into select positions into the aTF gene, and assemble the library into plasmid DNA (Fig. 3). Based on a predicted effector‐bound protein structure (see Support Protocol 1 and Critical Parameters: Protein:effector complex modeling), we were able to identify residues adjacent to the effector binding pocket and targeted these for site saturation mutagenesis (SSM). 15 residues in the SRTF1 binding pocket were selected for mutagenesis, and these were divided into 5 groups of 3 residues each for mutagenesis, since a typical transformation can only produce libraries that completely cover roughly 3 to 5 positions (see Critical Parameters: Library design). In general, linear stretches of the protein that contained multiple contact points were chosen for mutagenesis. To simplify the mutagenesis procedure, we chose to only mutagenize 3 positions at a time, creating 5 libraries. Primers were designed within each linear region to introduce randomized codons using an NNS degenerate codon scheme. Flanking the degenerate codons were BsaI cut sites, which allow the amplicons to be used in subsequent Golden Gate assembly cloning (for primer design details, see Support Protocol 2). As an alternative, Gibson assembly can also be used. While the 5 libraries were mixed prior to selection and screening, there are advantages and disadvantages to this approach (see Critical Parameters: Library design).

Graphical representation of Basic Protocol 1. Part 1 involves selecting the residues that will be randomized in the ligand‐binding pocket. Part 2 involves designing the mutagenesis primers, which contain a region with 15‐25 bp annealing to the wild‐type gene, the randomized codon(s), a 4 bp ligation site, a BsaI recognition site, and a 4 bp extension sequence to optimize cut efficiency. Part 3 outlines the PCR amplification of the aTF with primers (green) to linearize pREG, randomize the selected residues and attach BsaI recognition sites. Part 4 includes a DpnI digestion of the PCR template DNA, followed by gel purification of the amplified DNA. In Part 5, the purified DNA is Golden Gate assembled into a plasmid.

LIBRARY CONSTRUCTION

The regulatory circuits for selection and screening are contained within two plasmids, pREG and pSELIS. In this protocol, the libraries generated in Basic Protocol 1 are transformed into E. coli DH10B cells containing pSELIS (Fig. 4) to carry out selections and screens (Basic Protocol 3).

Graphical representation of Basic Protocol 2. This protocol is separated into two parts: (1) competent cell preparation and (2) transformations. In part one, a colony of DH10B containing pSELIS is cultured overnight and then subcultured into fresh superior broth and grown to mid‐log phase before being chilled, washed with ice‐cold glycerol, and concentrated to generate electrocompetent cells. In part two, the electrocompetent cells are transformed with the pREG library from Basic Protocol 1. Library size is determined, and sequencing QC is performed before saving glycerol stocks of the library.

Regulator and reporter plasmid

The plasmid pREG uses a constitutive, medium‐strength promoter to express an E. coli codon‐optimized gene encoding the aTF under investigation (Fig. 5A) (d'Oelsnitz, Nguyen, et al., 2022). However, the choice of promoter strength is highly dependent on the aTF itself. Overexpression of the aTF causes a low background signal, but poor responsiveness to ligand, while underexpression leads to a high background signal and poor fold‐induction with ligand (Wang et al., 2015). We recommend choosing promoter and ribosome‐binding site (RBS) strengths driving aTF expression at a level where the background signal is ∼3‐fold above a non‐fluorescent, cells‐only control. To tune the transcription level of the aTF, we use an extended Anderson promoter series that covers a ∼1000‐fold range of expression (see Promoters/Catalog/Anderson at https://parts.igem.org/Promoters/Catalog/Anderson). To tune the translational level of the aTF, we use a bicistronic RBS design containing a translated leader peptide that provides a greater degree of context‐independent translation control (Jansen et al., 2023). A ribozyme is also located upstream from the constitutive promoter to buffer expression of the downstream aTF against gene‐specific perturbations due to RNA folding or availability (Lou et al., 2012). The pSELIS plasmid contains an aTF‐responsive promoter upstream of both the λ cI repressor (for negative selection via zeocin resistance encoded by Sh ble) and sfGFP (for screening) (Fig. 5B). There is a bidirectional terminator upstream and an additional terminator downstream of λ cI to prevent readthrough. The two plasmids have compatible replication origins. For our purposes, aTF‐responsive promoters can be readily designed by placing the operator sequence (Grazon et al., 2020) downstream of a synthetic promoter (Fig. 5C). Users could also adapt their own native, regulated promoters to this system by replacing the synthetic promoters; however, it is very important that the TF‐regulated promoter is very strong, with similar strength to the J23119 Anderson promoter, to ensure that responsive TF variants produce enough fluorescence for easy visualization during the colony screening step (see Basic Protocol 3).

Genetic constructs used in this study. (A) pREG expresses the aTF from a medium‐strength promoter. (B) pSELIS contains an aTF‐responsive promoter upstream of both the λ cI repressor (for negative selection) and sfGFP (for screening). (C) PaTF construct used in this study. The SRTF1 operator sequence is placed downstream of a synthetic promoter.

In the negative selection in the absence of the target ligand, the aTF would bind to the operator upstream of λ cI, thereby repressing the expression of λ cI and allowing for the expression of Sh ble. In contrast, if the aTF has lost the ability to bind to the operator, the cells would not be resistant to zeocin. In the screen, in the presence of the target ligand and absence of zeocin, the repressor should be induced, and GFP would be expressed.

Transformation

Due to the need for highly diverse libraries, electrocompetent E. coli cells are used rather than chemically competent cells. DH10B cells containing pSELIS are freshly streaked out to isolate from a glycerol stock. A single colony from this plate is inoculated into a small culture, which is subsequently used to inoculate a larger culture the following day. This culture is grown to mid‐log phase and made electrocompetent using standard washing with 10% glycerol. Afterward, the newly prepared cells are electroporated with the plasmid DNA generated in Basic Protocol 1.

Library size determination and genotyping

Following transformation, it is essential to assess the efficiency of the transformation to determine the number of library transformants and, thus, library coverage. This is achieved by diluting the recovered transformation reaction and then plating it onto selective plates, incubating overnight, and subsequently calculating the number of transformants. The recovered transformation is also cultured in parallel in selective liquid medium overnight. The following day, glycerol stocks are prepared for downstream use in selection and screening (Basic Protocol 3). For quality control, a small number of transformed plasmids are individually assessed; four colonies are cultured from the LB agar plates used to determine the transformation efficiency, and the plasmid DNA is purified and sequenced. Long‐read sequencing validates that the desired positions in each library have been mutagenized, and that there are no attendant mutations in other regions, e.g., that promoters have not been inactivated due to unexpected toxicities.

SELECTION AND SCREENING

This protocol outlines a combined selection and screening workflow to identify aTFs that can respond to new effector molecules (Fig. 6).

Graphical representation of Basic Protocol 3. This protocol is separated into two parts: (1) selection and (2) screening. In part one, the aTF libraries built in Basic Protocol 2 are inoculated into selection cultures with zeocin. After incubation, the cultures are plated onto LB agar plates with the target ligand at different concentrations. In part 2, fluorescent colonies are inoculated into cultures in a deep‐well plate and assayed for response to the target ligand. Colonies exhibiting a range of ligand‐dependent increases in fluorescence are cultured for use in Basic Protocol 4.

Selection

Initially, a selection is carried out to remove sensors that do not repress transcription in the absence of the target ligand. This is because the most likely transcriptionally “active” variants in the library are those that have accumulated mutations that lead to their inactivation as repressors. The aTF‐regulated promoter has been placed upstream of the λ cI repressor, and the λ cI‐regulated promoter is in turn placed upstream of the zeocin resistance gene (Sh ble). This is a so‐called “inverter” circuit that inverts the normal logic of repressor function: repression of repression is, in fact, activation. In the absence of an effector that the aTF responds to, the aTF remains bound to its operator, and cells are ultimately resistant to zeocin. Sh ble was chosen as the resistance protein due to its non‐catalytic mechanism of action, which allows for a more linear application of selection stringency than other antibiotics (Gatignol et al., 1988).

For the negative selection, the different aTF libraries were grown in five different concentrations of zeocin. This was done to identify the concentration that gave a desired level of stringency (see Critical Parameters: Stringency of selections and screens).

Screening

Following selection in liquid media, each of the cultures was plated onto LB agar plates containing one of the following four concentrations of the target ligand, cortisol: 200 µM, 100 µM, 50 µM, and 0 µM. These concentrations were chosen as they are approximately 2/3, 1/3, 1/6, and 0 of the measured EC50 , and this range again allowed a desired level of stringency to be set (see Critical Parameters: Stringency of selections and screens). Following overnight incubation, the most highly fluorescent colonies were picked (by eye) for further characterization. The control plate without the target ligand is used to evaluate how many clones (or “cheaters”) bypassed the growth selection; colonies are only picked if <1 % of colonies are fluorescent on the plate without ligand. Typically, colonies that are moderately bright, but not the brightest, represent the most promising clones, as highly fluorescent colonies are often clones that have cheated the growth selection. After growth overnight, individual cultures were sub‐cultured into two wells in a microtiter plate, one with the target effector and one with the solvent only as a control. After shaking incubation for 4 hr, the microtiter plates were spun down, and the cell pellets were resuspended in buffer. Both fluorescence and absorbance were determined with a plate reader, and the fold change in induction of GFP production was calculated.

Interpretation of results

When selecting and screening SRTF1 for responsivity to cortisol, we ultimately isolated three variants (Variants 1, 3, and 11) that showed substantially improved responsivity relative to the wild‐type parent, all with a ∼32‐fold change in fluorescence when induced with 200 µM cortisol. Two of the variants came from the 12.5 µg/ml zeocin selection and one came from the 50 µg/ml zeocin selection, validating the need to have a range of stringencies during the selection prior to moving into screening (also see Critical Parameters: Stringency of selections and screens). For Basic Protocol 4, the colonies indicated with asterisks in Figure 7 were cultured for use in Basic Protocol 4, allowing us to assess a range of isolates.

SEQUENCING AND VALIDATION OF HITS

The variants derived from the selection and screen are further characterized by sequencing and in terms of their ability to respond to effectors via dose‐response assays (Fig. 9). The initial hits can then be further optimized through additional rounds of selection and screening, or rational combinations of identified mutations.

Graphical representation of Basic Protocol 4. In part one, the cultures that were started of variants from Basic Protocol 3 are genotyped. This is done by pelleting the cultures, extracting, and purifying the plasmid DNA and sequencing the DNA using long‐read sequencing to identify aTF variants. In part two, the unique variants identified in part 1 are PCR amplified and subcloned into new pREG vectors. These plasmid isolates are subsequently used in dose‐response assays to fully characterize the sensor's response to the target ligand and assess cross‐reactivities to other ligands.

Sequencing

After the microplate assay in Basic Protocol 3, 15 single colonies were inoculated into culture medium for genotyping. Purified plasmid DNA from these cultures contains both pREG and pSELIS DNA, and the sequences of the variants were confirmed by long‐read sequencing. Sequencing of both plasmids also helps to ensure there are no mutations in the reporter plasmid that arose during selection and screening. For example, one colony sequenced from the 50 µg/ml selection (Variant 13) and both colonies sequenced from the 100 µg/ml selection (Variants 14 and 15) had deletions in the λ cI coding sequence. Additionally, two colonies (Variants 7 and 10) from the 25 µg/ml selection and one colony from the 50 µg/ml selection (Variant 12) had a mutation in Sh ble. This likely indicates that at this stringency, the most probable route to zeocin resistance did not involve the aTF. Table 10 shows the genotypes of the 15 variants, the selection culture that they originated from, the cortisol concentration plate that they were picked from, and the calculated fold change in induction based on the microplate screen. As can be seen, the lowest zeocin concentration tested (12.5 µg/ml) gave the best hits, consistent with higher zeocin concentrations having been too stringent. The ultimate utility and breadth of the selection and screen can also be seen by the fact that Variants 6 and 7 (identified at 25 µg/ml zeocin) both reproduced the wild‐type aTF, but with different codons.

Validation

The individual aTF genes for the best three variants (Variants 1, 3, and 11) were PCR‐amplified and subcloned into the pREG backbone (this allows construction of the pREG plasmid so that it is separate from pSELIS). The newly cloned pREG plasmid is then transformed into cells containing pSELIS. The resultant cells were assayed in biological triplicate for their dose‐responses to cortisol; cross‐reactivities with structurally similar compounds (progesterone, cholesterol, and estrone) were also assessed. Other possible validation approaches are discussed in Critical Parameters: Validation of variants.

Interpretation of results

The three most improved hits initially identified in Basic Protocol 3 (Variants 1, 3, and 11) were further validated by examining their full dose‐response curves (Fig. 10). The Hill equation was used to fit the sigmoidal dose‐response data, which is expected for many transcription factors that are allosteric or otherwise cooperative. The dose‐response curves, half‐maximal effective concentration (EC50), the cooperativity (referred to as Hill Slope), and the maximum and background signals were calculated using the [Agonist] vs response (four parameters) variable slope fitting tool in GraphPad Prism 10.0 software. This tool uses least squares regression analysis to fit the data to the Hill equation:

where a = maximum signal, b = Hill coefficient, c = EC50, d = background signal, x = ligand concentration, and y = output signal. The maximum and background signals are plateaus in the units of the Y axis, which are referred to as top and bottom in Prism, respectively. The Hill slope describes the steepness of the sigmoidal curve, providing insight into the cooperativity of the system. EC50 refers to the concentration of ligand required to increase the measured response to half of its maximal value. Additionally, due to the asymmetry of the EC50 value, the confidence intervals for the EC50 values were calculated using a likelihood ratio asymmetric method (Kemmer & Keller, 2010; Venzon & Moolgavkar, 1988; Watts, 1994). The dynamic range was calculated by subtracting the maximum signal from the background. Due to the solubility limits of the hormones, complete dose‐response curves could not be obtained for multiple variant:effector pairs; therefore, these were not fitted to the Hill equation.

Further functional improvements

Two of the variants (Variants 1 and 3) came from the library that randomized positions 153, 154, and 155; both variants had no substitution at L154 and a L155R substitution. Variant 1 also had a G153A mutation, while Variant 3 had a G153S mutation. Variant 3 also had an A85E mutation that was outside of the SSM region; this is a common occurrence because of the mutagenic nature of PCR and is often helpful for identifying additional positions that can contribute to function. Variant 11 came from the library that randomized positions 127, 130, and 131, and had a single F127W mutation.

There are several options for further improving the cortisol‐responsive aTF (also see Critical Parameters: Further improvements in activity). One option is to synthetically combine the mutations from Variants 1 and 3 with the mutation identified in Variant 11. Additional designed combinations could be considered based on the sequences retrieved from less active variants, e.g., Variant 5 came from the library that randomized positions 93, 94, and 96 but only showed a ∼16‐fold activation by cortisol. A second option would be to return to Basic Protocol 1 and build additional libraries on top of Variants 1, 3, and/or 11, and then carry out another iteration of SELIS via Basic Protocols 2, 3, and 4 to identify further improved variants. The path taken is likely to be idiosyncratic for each transcription factor and/or effector attempted and will be highly dependent on the results of the selection and screen (also see Critical Parameters: Further improvements in activity).

PREDICTING PROTEIN:EFFECTOR STRUCTURE

If possible, work with an aTF whose structure with a bound effector is known. However, in many instances, the structure of the bound aTF with an effector bound will not be known. Modern computational modeling procedures do allow the in silico docking of effectors with some degree of accuracy. While we provide a rough protocol for how to do this for SRTF1 with our target effector, cortisol, keep in mind that there may be other or newer models that will work better for your aTF and effector of interest.

In the absence of a known structure, we suggest using AlphaFold (Jumper et al., 2021) to create a good structural model, and docking a given effector using DiffDock (Corso et al., 2023). In fact, the AlphaFold structure of SRTF1 (UniProt ID: A0A0A1DUF2) was available from the AlphaFold database (Varadi et al., 2024). The 3D hydrocortisone SDF file from the PubChem database (PubChem CID: 5754) was added to this structure using DiffDock. This state‐of‐the‐art deep learning model uses a diffusion generative model to predict ligand poses based on learned geometric and chemical interactions between proteins and ligands. DiffDock produced a series of predicted poses for the chemical structure, and we combined these poses with the AlphaFold‐predicted structure into a single PDB file that allowed us to visualize options for the effector‐binding pocket. Since all 10 of the predicted poses placed the ligand in the same position, we were confident that we had identified the effector‐binding pocket and used the top‐ranked structure for library design. However, in general, docking methods can be unreliable, and it is always good to rationally examine whether they seem to make sense, prior to library design (also see Support Protocol 2).

DESIGN OF MUTAGENESIS PRIMERS

As previously stated, and as further elaborated in Critical Parameters: Library design, there are a variety of mutagenesis designs and methods that can be utilized, but we prefer small, site‐saturated mutagenesis libraries that give complete coverage of a given set of mutagenized positions. In general, to change the effector‐binding properties of a transcription factor, we focus on the residues in the effector‐binding domain that directly contact the ligand. Other choices (DNA‐binding residues, residues thought to be involved in allostery) could also be chosen, depending on the functionalities desired by the user.

To identify the residues in or near the effector‐binding domain, we visualized the relevant domain in PyMol. In deciding how libraries would be designed, we took into consideration: (1) those residues that were in close proximity to the ligand, and (2) those residues that were grouped such that a single primer might coordinately introduce multiple mutations. For our selection with SRTF1, 15 amino acids near the effector were selected for mutagenesis (Fig. 11).

Protein‐ligand complex of SRTF1 and cortisol that was generated using AlphaFold and DiffDock. Residues that were randomized in this study are labeled and designated with a color corresponding to the oligonucleotide pool that was used to randomize that position. Oligonucleotide pool 1 is shown in blue, pool 2 is shown in red, pool 3 is shown in purple, pool 4 is shown in yellow, and pool 5 is shown in teal. Cortisol is shown in green and non‐randomized residues are shown in grey.

For the targeted sets of residues in each library, primers were designed to have NNS at each targeted amino acid position, where N = A/T/C/G and S = G/C. This scheme encodes all 20 standard amino acids and one stop codon, encompassing 32 (4 × 4 × 2) codon combinations. The strategy we focus on for this protocol utilizes one PCR to linearize the gene, introduce degenerate codons, and attach cut sites for subsequent Golden Gate assembly cloning. An important parameter for this protocol is that the user has checked for internal cut sites and removed them if present when using Golden Gate assembly. This is achieved by using two primers, where the forward primer contains the BsaI cut site in the forward direction, and the reverse primer contains the BsaI cut site in the reverse direction. Each primer has 15 to 25 base pairs of homologous sequence for annealing to the gene. The NNS codons are generally introduced in either the forward or reverse strand, to coordinately mutagenize multiple, adjacent residues. However, both primers could contain mutagenized residues, these would then be at a distance from one another along the length of the gene (Fig. 12). COMMENTARY

Critical Parameters

Parts optimization

Compared to other biological components, transcription factors are relatively complex, as they interact with both ligands (effectors) and DNA, and undergo allosteric conformational changes. When performing dose‐response experiments with the original wild‐type transcription factor, it is important to consider the dynamic range of induction, and how the selection and screen might be optimized by adjusting the operator sequence, by increasing the strength of the promoter that expresses the transcription factor, and/or by using more or less effector (or antibiotic). In the construct used in this study, the transcription factor is expressed from a medium‐strength promoter, allowing for further modifications both up and down (which, in this instance, were unnecessary). Similarly, the affinity of the transcription factor for the operator can be modified by introducing substitutions that might be predicted to decrease or increase interactions; the former might be predicted by disrupting phylogenetically‐conserved variations, while the latter might be predicted by including more phylogenetically‐conserved variations.

Other types of transcription factors

The SELIS method is theoretically applicable to any ligand‐inducible repressor, as well as activators. To reconfigure the method for effector‐dependent activators, rather than repressors, it might be more useful to drop the λ cI repressor from the circuit and select for antibiotic function in the presence of the effector, then further screen to filter out variants that were somehow constitutively active, rather than effector‐dependent.

Similarly, in those instances where binding of the effector leads to repression, rather than induction (a corepressor, e.g., TrpR with tryptophan), λ cI should be eliminated, and the aTF‐responsive promoter should again be moved directly in front of the antibiotic resistance gene. This will allow for the selection against variants that are not bound in the absence of ligand. Then, during the subsequent screen, the aTF‐responsive promoter should control the expression of λ cI, which would in turn control the expression of GFP (i.e., the inverter would be present in the screen, rather than the selection).

Protein:effector complex modeling

Many tools exist for generating protein‐ligand complexes. For this study, we used a protein structure generated by AlphaFold and then used DiffDock to model the ligand position. Current alternatives to using an AlphaFold predicted structure are to use a crystal structure (if one exists), a homology model based on the known structure of a homologous protein, or other deep learning‐based tools such as ESMFold (Lin et al., 2023). For modeling ligand interactions, DiffDock uses diffusion to predict the ligand binding poses, whereas traditional tools like AutoDock Vina use physics‐based scoring and sampling (Eberhardt et al., 2021). Newer machine learning‐based models allow for the direct modeling of proteins in complex with both small molecules and DNA (Abramson et al., 2024; Discovery et al., 2024; Krishna et al., 2024). It is currently possible to use a variety of these tools to identify the residue involved in ligand recognition to guide mutagenesis. In the future, there will likely be even more sophisticated methods that can be used individually or in combination.

Library design

As is the case with most protein engineering methods that rely at some level on selection and/or screening, the design of the library is one of the most important parameters. Allosteric transcription factors are especially difficult to design libraries for, as their functionality rests on binding an effector, undergoing a programmed conformational change, and altering their binding to DNA. Multiple domains are involved in function, and the functional connectivities between those domains are not often known. Because of this, we advocate for using site‐saturation mutagenesis in or near the effector‐binding pocket, especially in those cases where a researcher is attempting to change the effector specificity of the aTF (Nishikawa et al., 2024; Taylor et al., 2016). This approach likely preserves the DNA‐binding domain and stands a good chance of productively interacting with those amino acids involved in an allosteric conformational change. Conversely, to change the operator specificity, residues in direct contact with DNA could be changed (Ellefson et al., 2018). However, researchers have seen that changing residues outside of the ligand‐ and DNA‐binding domains can also change ligand affinities (Taylor et al., 2016). Because of this, additional bioinformatic analyses could be performed to identify additional positions to randomize.

Other mutagenesis methods could be utilized, such as PCR mutagenesis (Rasila et al., 2009) or the synthesis of longer segments of DNA with a defined level of mutation (so‐called doped sequence pools). These approaches potentially allow multiple regions of the protein to co‐evolve, thus retaining, restoring, or improving effector binding, allosteric communication, and DNA binding, all at once. aTF engineering studies have shown variants outside of the effector‐binding pocket that have conferred alternate ligand specificity. Additionally, researchers have shown that allosteric hotspots are distributed across the length of the protein (Liu et al., 2024).

Irrespective of the mutagenesis method used, the library size dictates in large measure what can be achieved. For SSM, the number of positions mutagenized will determine the library size, although this will be further modulated depending on whether degenerate or nondegenerate saturation is performed. For degenerate optimization, the conventional degenerate codon is NNN (where N = a mixture of A, C, G, and T, and NNN contains all four bases at each position of the codon). Approaches have been taken to reduce the redundancy using limited redundancy, such as NNK or NNS (K = T/G, S = G/C). This strategy reduces the number of codons from 64 to 32 to encode all 20 amino acids plus one stop codon (Ferreira Amaral et al., 2017). Recently, codon‐defined strategies have been described, such as the “22c‐trick”, which reduces the number of codons to just 22 per saturated position (Kille et al., 2013).

Given the constraints of bacterial transformation, it is best to attempt smaller library sizes. For simplicity, we chose to select and screen five libraries that each had 8000 protein variants (203) with a coverage of at least 10 fold (thus needing ≥8 × 104 transformants; actual numbers of transformants are in Table 25). While selections for each library can be carried out, we pooled the 5 different libraries prior to selection and screening.

There are advantages and disadvantages to the choice of the number of residues to mutagenize, and whether or not to pool the variants. By choosing fewer residues/constructed libraries, coverage is greater, but more libraries must be screened. In addition, with smaller libraries, potential synergies or dissonances between individual residues will be less likely to be identified (although see Basic Protocol 4 for insights into how to best combine initially identified substitutions). Similarly, we pooled libraries for selection to most quickly identify those variants from any given library that would predominate; this was the quickest way to find “winners.” However, if only a few sets of substitutions from only a few libraries predominate, information on other positions with lesser contributions, or potentially synergistic contributions, will be lost. Therefore, if resources allow, the most comprehensive screen would be one where the libraries were not pooled and individual libraries were instead more deeply screened. To best cover the functional sequence space and find the best variants, we suggest that selections and screens be followed with rational combinations of mutations (see Basic Protocol 4 and Critical Parameters: Further improvements in activity).

Stringency of selections and screens

Zeocin is a member of the bleomycin/phleomycin family of antibiotics and is used in both bacterial and mammalian cells. Zeocin intercalates into DNA and causes double‐stranded breaks that result in cell death. A zeocin resistance protein, Sh ble, has been identified, which binds stoichiometrically to zeocin, inhibiting its DNA cleavage activity. This resistance mechanism is non‐catalytic and therefore enables a more linear response to the antibiotic, and thus the opportunity to increase selection pressure more gradually.

The recommended Zeocin concentration for selection in E. coli is 25 to 50 µg/ml in low‐salt medium. Therefore, for this selection, we initially used 5 different concentrations of zeocin: 0 µg/ml, 12.5 µg/ml, 25 µg/ml, 50 µg/ml, and 100 µg/ml. These different stringencies allow the survival of different numbers of variants to subsequently screen and thereby provide a range of possible functional variants that can both repress and be induced. There is essentially an unstated tradeoff between the stringency of repression and the likelihood of obtaining induction: the more strongly bound the repressor is, the harder it will be to find mutations that will enable the effector to allow its ready removal. The selections from the 5 concentrations of zeocin were plated separately onto LB agar plates with cortisol (see Fig. 13 for schematic), and moving forward, we selected colonies from each of the 12.5, 25, 50, and 100 µg/ml zeocin selections. In the end, we observed that our best hits for cortisol activation originated from the 12.5 µg/ml and 50 µg/ml selections. Still, we recommend that researchers perform a similar selection titration experiment when working with otherwise uncharacterized aTF libraries.

Graphical representation of the selection and plating procedure described in Basic Protocol 3. The 5 SSM libraries are pooled and then inoculated into five different cultures, each containing a different concentration of zeocin. After growing them for 6 hr, each of the selection cultures is plated onto four LB agar plates with varying amounts of the target ligand.

It is important to note that Zeocin should be used in a low‐salt (<5 g/L) medium, as the ionic strength of the environment can impact electrostatic interactions between Zeocin and DNA. Therefore, we used a low‐salt medium for our selection, which has ∼0.8 g/L salt.

In addition to varying the amount of antibiotic, it is possible to make changes to the plasmid architecture to alter the stringency of the selection. For example, the strength of the promoter expressing the aTF is a crucial parameter for building a successful evolution circuit. The strength of this promoter can be increased to allow for a less stringent selection or decreased to allow for a more stringent selection. The strength of the aTF‐responsive promoter should always be very strong, which ensures facile screening of fluorescent colonies as well as a stringent growth‐based selection, with the need for higher repression restricting the degree to which a library member can compromise DNA‐binding.

For plate‐based screening following selection, we recommend using effector concentrations that are roughly 2/3, 1/3, 1/6, and 0 of the EC50. This allows users to generally identify variants that are activated, since after the selection, it is unknown to what extent activation may be present. It is typical to see a greater number of fluorescent colonies on the plates with a higher concentration of effector, as there could be variants that are responsive at, for example, 2/3 of the EC50 but not 1/3. The use of the lower concentrations allows users to potentially pick repressors with very good induction, and to carry them into later rounds of selection and screening. We picked 24 colonies from the highest concentration plate, 12 from the middle concentration plate, and 9 from the lowest concentration plate. We saw that the higher concentrations of cortisol ultimately yielded variants with better EC50 values, likely indicating that no better variants were available from at least the initial libraries we screened. The no (0) effector control plate should yield very few to no fluorescent colonies and serves to validate the procedure. We suggest proceeding with screening only when <1% of colonies on the control plate are fluorescent.

Validation of variants

While re‐cloning an aTF variant is an excellent way to validate performance (as in Basic Protocol 4), other methods may prove more time‐efficient, but that comes with other tradeoffs. The first option involves using the single‐colony culture to further examine dose‐response, prior to sequencing. This limits assessment to a single biological replicate and may result in unwanted examination of multiple equivalent protein variants with different underlying codons. The second shortcut involves taking the miniprepped plasmid pool (pREG and pSELIS) and transforming 1 µl of that into cells already containing pSELIS. However, if there are mutations in the pSELIS plasmid, these could be propagated (although analysis of the sequencing results should be sufficient to rule out colonies where this might be the case). We have generated results where this method correlates well with the subcloned variants, allowing for the dose‐response of biological triplicates with less cloning.

Further improvements in activity

The variants identified in this study can potentially be further improved, and the library strategy suggested is only one of several possible routes to improvement. When winners are identified from different source libraries, a rational strategy involves combining the substitutions to rational variants to test or combining the randomized positions to create new libraries with potentially greater synergies (rather than building additional libraries on top of a few defined variants, as suggested in Basic Protocol 4).

In addition, the SELIS protocol can be adapted to reduce cross‐reactivity (d'Oelsnitz, Diaz, et al., 2024). For example, the variants identified in this study are still cross‐reactive to progesterone; therefore, on the second iteration of this protocol, progesterone can be added to the zeocin selection to remove variants that are cross‐reactive.

Time Considerations

The library design (Basic Protocol 1) can be completed in 1 day, and the DNA generation (amplification, purification, and assembly) can be performed within 2 days after receiving the oligonucleotides. The library cloning (electrocompetent cell preparation and transformation) described in Basic Protocol 2 can be performed in 3 days, and the library sequencing can be performed in 2 days. When starting with an optimized aTF expression system and reporter construct, the library assay (including selection and screening) described in Basic Protocol 3 can be performed in 3 days. The variant genotyping described in Basic Protocol 4 can be performed in 2 days. When starting with primers for amplifying pREG, the subcloning and dose‐response assays, also described in Basic Protocol 4, can be performed within 6 days.
