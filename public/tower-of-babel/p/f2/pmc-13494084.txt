Complementary plant protein pairing does not further increase postexercise myofibrillar protein synthesis after a 20 g protein dose within a high-carbohydrate whole-food matrix in young adults: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Žan Zupančič, Takeshi M Barnes, Max T Deutz, Gena L Irwin, Joshua E Zimring, Calvin Chen, Andrew T Askow, Alexander V Ulanov, Jared W Willard, Luc JC van Loon, Nicholas A Burd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCombining plant proteins is a strategy to improve overall amino acid balance; however, the impact of this pairing on postexercise myofibrillar protein synthesis (MPS) remains unclear.ObjectivesWe compared postexercise MPS after ingestion of beans and rice [complementary pairing of rice and black beans (COMP)] compared with a nutrient-matched mixture of isolated nutrients [isolated mixture of crystallized amino acids, maltodextrin, cellulose, pectin, and soy oil (ISO)].MethodsIn this randomized, crossover trial, 11 physically active adults (24 ± 4 y; 25.0 ± 3 kg/m2; 10 M, 1 F) were randomly assigned (1:1 allocation) to 1 of 2 treatment sequences (COMP-ISO or ISO-COMP). Each trial was separated by ∼1-wk recovery period to allow for muscle biopsy site healing. During primed-constant infusions of L-[ring-13C6]phenylalanine, participants consumed either COMP (548 kcal; 20 g protein, 114 g carbohydrate, 0.8 g fat) or ISO immediately after resistance exercise. Blood and muscle samples were collected at rest and during the 0 to 5 h recovery period to assess outcomes. The primary outcome was MPS. Data were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models.ResultsA total of 10 randomly assigned participants completed both arms of the study, whereas 1 participant completed only 1 arm. All participants were included in the final analysis. Postexercise aminoacidemia and net amino acid exposure (incremental AUC) decreased over the 0 to 5 h recovery period regardless of condition (all, P < 0.05). Phosphorylation of AKTSer473, 4E-BP1Thr37/46, rpS6Ser240/244, and p706kThr389 significantly increased at 5 h postexercise in both groups (all P < 0.05). MPS increased from rest but did not differ between COMP (0.057 ± 0.013 %/h) and ISO (0.052 ± 0.013 %/h; P = 0.260; d = 0.4). In an exploratory, cross-trial comparison, MEAT (ground pork) elicited a significantly greater postprandial MPS compared with ISO, COMP, or carbohydrate alone (all, P < 0.05).ConclusionsPost exercise, MPS does not differ after the ingestion of 20 g protein provided as a blend of beans and rice or as a nutrient-matched mix of crystalline amino acids, maltodextrin, soy oil, and fiber in healthy young adults.This study was registered at clinicaltrials.gov as NCT06781723 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06781723).Keywords: skeletal muscle, vegan, anabolism, resistance exercise, protein quality, leucine

Introduction

Protein intake and resistance exercise are 2 of the most potent anabolic stimuli for increasing muscle protein synthesis rates [1]. Previous work has demonstrated that consuming animal-based proteins, likely due to their higher protein quality [2], more effectively stimulates postexercise muscle protein synthesis rates than plant-derived proteins [3,4]. The lower anabolic properties of plant-based proteins may be attributed to their amino acid composition and reduced digestibility [5,6]. Additionally, plant proteins have been shown to exhibit greater splanchnic extraction, higher amino acid oxidation rates, and greater urea synthesis than animal-based proteins [7,8]. Collectively, these factors may limit plasma and muscle amino acid availability, thereby impairing postexercise muscle protein synthesis rates [9]. Although increasing total plant protein intake in isolated forms can enhance resting anabolic responses [10], it remains unclear how other vegan dietary approaches influence myofibrillar protein synthesis (MPS) during postexercise recovery.

The complementary protein pairing strategy involves combining plant foods with different limiting amino acids to improve protein quality [2]. For example, legumes are relatively low in methionine, whereas grains are lower in lysine and threonine. Pairing these foods provides a more balanced essential amino acid (EAA) profile [11]. Complementing grains (e.g., rice, sorghum, or millet) with legumes (e.g., lentils or chickpeas) has been shown to reduce whole-body protein oxidation rates (ranging from 14% to 27%) and improve protein metabolic availability [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. However, ingestion of a whole-food vegan meal based on complementary proteins (∼36 g protein) failed to stimulate resting mixed muscle protein synthesis rates in healthy older adults [16]. Furthermore, the impact of the plant food matrix, or their interplay of nutrients and nonnutritive components, on postexercise MPS remains largely unexplored [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. Beyond their amino acid composition, whole-plant foods contain antinutritional components [21,22] that, in conjunction with their complex fiber network, may reduce nutrient bioavailability and slow amino acid appearance [23], thereby impacting anabolic properties [3,24].

Although previous work suggests that ∼30 to 40 g of isolated plant protein may be required to match the anabolic response of whey [25,26], the purpose of this study was to assess the single-meal response of ingesting whole-food plant protein complements on stimulating postexercise MPS rates in young adults rather than an optimized, high-dose supplement. We hypothesized that consuming beans and rice (548 kcal; 20 g protein, 114 g carbohydrate, and 0.8 g fat) will lead to a more moderate postprandial aminoacidemia to enhance total net plasma amino acid exposure [0–5 h incremental AUC (iAUC)] and the subsequent stimulation of MPS rates when compared with ingesting the same nutrient as an isolated blend during postexercise recovery in young adults. To provide benchmarks to animal protein and an exercise stimulus, we also compared plasma and muscle intracellular amino acid concentrations and muscle anabolic responses between plant and ground meat (i.e., animal protein reference) and carbohydrate controls (i.e., nonprotein exercise reference).

Methods

Participants and ethical approval

Eleven healthy, physically active adults (males = 10 and females = 1; mean ± SD: age: 24.4 ± 3.8y; BMI: 24.8 ± 2.9 kg/m2) volunteered to participate in the study (see Supplemental Figure 1 for CONSORT diagram). Detailed participant characteristics are shown in Table 1. Baseline physical activity was assessed using the Godin–Shephard Leisure-Time Physical Activity Questionnaire [27] and the International Physical Activity Questionnaires (IPAQ) [28]. Only participants scoring >14 (Godin–Shephard) and >1500 (IPAQ) were considered for participation in the study. All participants were classified as healthy based on responses to a health and medical screening questionnaire and had maintained a stable body weight for ≥6 mo before enrollment. Ten participants completed both infusion trials, whereas 1 participant completed 1 infusion trial. In 2 trials, muscle samples were not collected due to participant discomfort with the procedure; blood samples were collected as planned. All trials were conducted in our clinical laboratory at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign between November 2024 and April 2025. The female participant performed the trials during the assumed follicular phase (i.e., the 3rd to the 10th day of their menstrual cycle). Before participation, all participants were informed of the experimental procedures, the purpose of the study, and the potential risks associated with involvement before signing an informed consent form approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign (IRB #240582). All procedures involving human subjects conformed to standards for the use of human participants in research as outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. This trial was preregistered at clinicaltrials.gov as NCT06781723.

Study design and preliminary testing

A randomized crossover design was used in this study. One week before the first experimental infusion, baseline testing was performed in the morning after an overnight fast. Anthropometric measures (height and body mass) were obtained before blood pressure assessment with an automated device (HEM-907XL; Omron Healthcare Inc.). Assessment of body composition was performed using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (QDR 4500A; Hologic). Resting metabolic rate (RMR) was assessed according to established procedures [29]. Lastly, maximal strength was assessed using 10-repetition maximum (10RM) testing for the leg press and leg extension as previously described [30].

After preliminary testing, participants were randomly assigned to 1 of 2 treatment sequences [complementary pairing of rice and black beans (COMP)–isolated mixture of crystallized amino acids (ISO) or ISO-COMP]. The treatment sequence was determined by a separate member of the research team using computer-generated random numbers, with block randomization applied to ensure balanced allocation between sequences. Personnel involved in participant enrollment and intervention assignment did not have access to the random allocation sequence.

Dietary and physical activity guidance

Baseline habitual dietary intake was assessed using 3 consecutive 24-h recalls (2 weekdays and 1 weekend day) collected before the first infusion trial via the Automated Self-Administered 24-h Dietary Assessment Tool, version 2022 (National Cancer Institute) [31]. Participants were adhering to omnivorous diets before enrollment into the study. On the evening before each infusion trial, participants consumed a standardized meal provided by the research team. The omnivorous meal provided ∼30% of their estimated daily energy needs, which were calculated by multiplying their RMR by an activity factor of 1.4. The macronutrient composition of the meal was 25% protein, 50% carbohydrate, and 25% fat. Participants were also instructed to avoid exercise for 72 h and alcohol for 48 h before each trial.

Infusion protocol

Participants arrived to the laboratory at ∼07:00 after an overnight fast (>10 h). An intravenous catheter was placed in an antecubital vein for baseline blood collection, and a primed-constant infusion of L-[ring-13C6]phenylalanine (prime: 2 μmol/kg; 0.05 μmol/kg/min) was initiated and maintained throughout the trial. A second catheter was inserted in the contralateral arm for repeated blood sampling and remained patent by a 0.9% saline drip. Repeated blood and muscle samples were collected before and after resistance exercise to measure plasma and muscle variables as shown in Figure 1. The acute resistance exercise bout consisted of leg press and leg extension exercise (4 × 10 repetitions at 90% 10RM for each exercise). Immediately after exercise, participants consumed their assigned beverage of ISO or COMP. For trial 2, the infusion was initiated 1 h before the first biopsy to ensure isotopic steady-state within the muscle intracellular free pool. Muscle biopsies were collected from the vastus lateralis under local anesthesia using a Bergström needle. Volume load was matched between crossover trials. In this crossover design, each participant served as their own control with a consistent intertrial interval (7.5 ± 0.9 d). To account for residual enrichment from the first trial, baseline muscle protein tracer enrichment is used for the calculation of MPS rates to correct for the elevated background enrichment present during the second trial.

Treatment conditions

The nutritional composition for each treatment is provided in Table 2. This includes total energy, macronutrient content, amino acid profiles, and the percentage of the daily requirement for EAAs met by each condition. Long-grain jasmine rice (Iberia Foods) and canned black beans (Bush’s) were purchased from a local grocery store to serve as the complementary plant protein source. Compositional analysis was performed on the rice and beans for protein, amino acid, fat, and carbohydrate content based on methods established by the Association of Official Analytical Chemists [32]. The meal consisted of 108 g of rice, which was cooked for 12 min in a rice cooker, and 143 g of canned black beans, drained and reheated in a microwave. The meal was served with ∼250 mL of water. The meal was formulated to provide equal amounts of protein from rice (10 g) and beans (10 g), creating a complementary pairing with a more balanced amino acid profile than compared with ingesting either plant protein alone (Table 2). For an 80-kg individual, this meal supplied ≥42% of the daily requirement for each EAA or ∼3 oz-equivalents of the protein food group (i.e., black beans) according to the USDA MyPlate guidelines [33].

Plasma analyses

Commercially available ELISA [Alpco Diagnostics; limits of quantification (LOQ): 3.0–200 μIU/mL] were used to determine plasma insulin concentrations.

Plasma glucose concentrations were quantified using the 2900 Stat Plus automated biochemistry analyzer (YSI Life Sciences; LOQ: 5–900 mg/dL). Plasma samples (5 μL) were deproteinized with 190 μL of ddH2O:acetonitrile:isopropyl alcohol (2:3:3, v/v/v), spiked with 5 μL DL-p-chlorophenylalanine (0.01 mg/mL), then centrifuged at 20,817 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The resulting 100 μL supernatant was transferred to a new tube, dried under vacuum, and resuspended in 100 μL of 0.1% formic acid for analysis. Plasma amino acid concentrations and L-[ring-13C6]phenylalanine enrichment were determined as previously described [24]. All samples were within the LOQ and processed using identical preparation procedures. To ensure analytical quality control, each batch included reference human plasma with certified amino acid concentrations (NIST1950), BioIVT serum samples as external quality controls, and standard calibration solutions for amino acid quantification. In addition, blank samples were analyzed across instruments and analytical methods to detect potential contamination or carryover within the system.

Muscle analyses

Myofibrillar protein–enriched fractions were prepared from ∼50 mg wet muscle using ice-cold RIPA buffer (10 μL/mg; Pierce RIPA ((Radio-Immunoprecipitation Assa) Buffer, ThermoFisher Scientific) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (cOmplete and PhosSTOP; Roche Applied Science). Proteins were isolated by differential centrifugation and analyzed for L-[ring-13C6]phenylalanine enrichment as described previously [34]. The leftover muscle homogenate was frozen at −80°C for Western blot analysis of target proteins, conducted as described previously [34]. The following primary antibodies were employed to detect target proteins: rabbit anti-α-tubulin (1:1000; Cell Signaling# 2144), rabbit phosphoribosomal protein S6 kinase β1 (p70S6K; Thr389) (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 9205), rabbit anti-p70S6K (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 9202), rabbit phosphoribosomal protein 4E-BP1 (Thr37/46)(1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 9459), rabbit anti-4E-BP1 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 9452), rabbit phosphoribosomal protein Akt (Ser473) (1:1000; Cell Signaling# 9271), rabbit antiphospho–eukaryotic elongation factor 2 (eEF2; Thr56) (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 2331, RRID:AB_10015204), rabbit anti-eEF2 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 2332), rabbit IgG anti-phosphoribosomal protein S6 (rpS6; Ser240/244) (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 5364), and rabbit IgG anti-rpS6 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 2217).

Calculations

Fractional synthesis rates (FSR; %/h) of myofibrillar proteins were calculated using the standard precursor–product method by dividing the change in myofibrillar protein–bound L-[ring-13C6]phenylalanine enrichment by the plasma precursor pool enrichment as described previously [34]. The net iAUC for plasma insulin, glucose, and amino acid concentrations was calculated over the 5-h postprandial period using the trapezoidal rule, with t = 0 min as the baseline. Peak concentration (Cmax) was determined for all plasma time curves. Resistance exercise volume load was calculated as the sum of sets × repetitions × load (kg) performed across all completed sets for each exercise.

Animal and carbohydrate conditions

We included data from a randomized control trial that included the postexercise ingestion of MEAT and carbohydrates (clinicaltrials.gov identifier: NCT05876299). Full details of the participant characteristics and nutritional composition of the treatment conditions have been described previously [24]. In brief, participants included 16 physically active adults (25 ± 5 y; 25.0 ± 2.3 kg/m2; 12M, 4F) who received primed-constant infusions of L-[ring-13C6]phenylalanine and performed an acute bout of resistance exercise. Following exercise, participants ingested MEAT (120 kcal, 20 g protein, and 4.4 g fat) or carbohydrate control drink (CHO) (266 kcal, 0 g protein, 0 g fat, and 73.3 g carbohydrate). The blood and muscle sampling protocol was identical to that shown in Figure 1.

To minimize interassay variability, plasma samples from the MEAT and CHO conditions were analyzed concurrently with the current study samples. Muscle tissue free amino acids were isolated from a separate piece of bio-banked [24] and wet muscle (∼30 mg) from the current study by homogenization on ice with a Teflon pestle in 500 μL of 0.6 M perchloric acid (PCA). Samples were centrifuged at 4500 × g for 5 min at 4°C, and the supernatant containing IC amino acids was collected. This process was repeated twice by adding 500 μL of 0.6 M PCA, followed by centrifugation and collection of the supernatant. The combined supernatants were purified using cation-exchange chromatography (Dowex 50W-X8-200 resin; Acros Organics) and dried under vacuum. Samples were resuspended in 0.1 M HCL and analyzed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) as previously described [35]. Muscle protein–bound enrichments used to calculate myofibrillar protein fractional synthetic rates for the MEAT and CHO conditions were derived from the previously conducted study [24].

Statistical analyses

Power analysis

The primary outcome was MPS after ingestion of COMP and ISO. Secondary outcomes included plasma variables, muscle tissue free amino acid concentrations, and markers of anabolic signaling. Exploratory tertiary comparisons were performed to include MEAT and CHO conditions. An a priori power analysis was conducted using G∗Power (version 3.1.9.2) to determine the sample size required to detect differences in postexercise MPS rates [36]. On the basis of the previous research examining the food matrix and protein sources [[17], [18], [19]], we estimated a large effect size f = 0.7. Using a repeated-measures design with 2 conditions (COMP and ISO) and 2 time points (basal and 0–5 h) with an alpha level of 0.05, power of 0.80, and assuming a conservative correlation of 0.5, the analysis indicated that 10 participants would be sufficient to detect a significant condition × time interaction. We ultimately recruited 11 participants to ensure sufficient power in the event of any data loss due to participant dropouts.

Statistical modeling

All data are presented as means ± SD and/or mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence interval (CIs). Differences in blood variables, MPS rates, muscle-free amino acid concentrations, and anabolic signaling were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with time and condition as fixed factors and participant as a random effect. To account for the crossover design, sequence and period were included as fixed factors in all models assessing primary outcomes to preserve the design features of the experimental framework. To protect against type I error inflation, we established a clear analytical hierarchy where MPS was defined as the primary endpoint. Plasma variables, muscle tissue free amino acid concentrations, and anabolic signaling readouts were designated as secondary mechanistic outcomes and tertiary comparisons involving MEAT and CHO conditions were performed for exploratory context only.

Postprandial plasma total amino acids (TAA), EAA, leucine, methionine, lysine, insulin, and glucose iAUC and Cmax were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with condition as a fixed factor and participant as a random effect. When a significant main effect or interaction was detected, Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons were performed separately for pairwise comparisons across conditions within each time point and for comparisons across time points within each condition to control the familywise error rate at each level of comparison. Although multiplicity corrections were applied to these individual outcomes, we did not perform formal corrections across distinct exploratory endpoints; these secondary analyses were intended to provide mechanistic insight rather than serve as confirmatory hypothesis testing. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. The normality assumption for the linear mixed-effects models was evaluated by inspecting the distribution of model residuals using Q–Q plots, skewness/kurtosis values, and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Missing data were accommodated within the mixed-effects modeling framework. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp.).

Results

Exercise variables

Resistance exercise volume load did not differ between trials for leg press in the COMP (4381 ± 1888 kg) and ISO (4503 ± 1960 kg; P = 0.998). Similarly, no difference in leg extension volume has been observed in the COMP (3384 ± 1171 kg) and ISO (3442 ± 1227 kg; P = 0.991).

Plasma glucose and insulin

Plasma glucose concentrations increased regardless of condition from 30 to 120 min (P < 0.05) of the postexercise period before returning to baseline (P = 0.68; Figure 3A). As such, postprandial glucose iAUC did not differ between conditions (P = 0.890; Figure 3B). Plasma insulin concentrations increased from 30 to 90 min in the ISO condition (all, P < 0.05) and from 30 to 120 min in the COMP condition (all, P < 0.05). Postexercise insulin concentrations were significantly higher in the ISO compared with the COMP condition (P < 0.05; Figure 3C). This resulted in a higher Cmax (113 ± 49 μIU/mL) in ISO compared with COMP condition (72 ± 33 μIU/mL, P = 0.015; Supplemental Table 1). Postprandial insulin iAUC did not differ significantly between ISO and COMP (P = 0.057), with a moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.61; Figure 3D). Plasma amino acid concentrations and enrichments

Plasma TAA, EAA, leucine, and methionine concentrations transiently increased at 30 min only in the ISO condition before declining throughout recovery from exercise (all, P < 0.05; Figure 4). The ingestion of COMP resulted in a postexercise decrease (all, P < 0.05) in plasma amino acid concentrations (i.e., TAA, EAA, leucine, and methionine; Figure 4). Plasma net amino acid exposure (iAUC) for TAA, EAA, leucine, and methionine demonstrated a decrease during postexercise recovery irrespective of condition (Figure 4). Cmax were significantly higher in the ISO condition for TAA (P = 0.010), EAA (P < 0.001), and leucine (P = 0.004) than in the COMP condition (Supplemental Table 1). Postprandial plasma phenylalanine concentrations did not differ from baseline at any time point (P > 0.05) and did not differ between conditions (P = 0.433; Figure 5A). Postabsorptive and postexercise plasma L-[ring-13C6]phenylalanine enrichments did not differ between conditions (P = 0.433; Figure 5B). MPS

Postabsorptive MPS rates did not differ between conditions (P = 0.995; Figure 6A). Postprandial MPS rates were elevated above baseline in both conditions for ISO (from 0.029 ± 0.013 to 0.052 ± 0.013 %/h; P < 0.001) and COMP conditions (from 0.030 ± 0.012 to 0.057 ± 0.013 %/h; P < 0.001). Postprandial MPS rates did not differ between conditions [MD (95% CI): 0.005 (−0.008, 0.018) %/h; P = 0.260]. Exploratory food comparison

To provide an exploratory physiological context for the observed responses, the COMP and ISO data were compared with previously collected benchmarks (MEAT and CHO) [24].

Participant comparisons

The relative protein intakes per kg body mass in the postexercise meal were similar between isonitrogenous conditions (ISO: 0.26 ± 0.04 g/kg, COMP: 0.26 ± 0.04 g/kg, and MEAT: 0.27 ± 0.03 g/kg; all P > 0.05). Furthermore, resistance exercise volume load did not differ between conditions for leg press (COMP: 4381 ± 1888 kg, ISO: 4503 ± 1960 kg, CHO: 5377 ± 1610 kg, MEAT: 4576 ± 1823 kg; P = 0.611) or leg extension (COMP: 3384 ± 1171 kg, ISO: 3442 ± 1227 kg, CHO: 3462 ± 846 kg, MEAT: 3051 ± 1156 kg; P = 0.828).

Plasma variables

Postprandial increases in plasma EAAs and leucine occurred more rapidly in the ISO condition compared with MEAT, CHO, and COMP conditions (all P < 0.05; Supplemental Figure 2). In contrast, plasma concentrations of EAA, leucine, methionine, and lysine were higher in the MEAT condition during the following postprandial period (60 to 180–240 min) than in the other 3 conditions (all P < 0.05). Consequently, MEAT ingestion resulted in a positive postprandial amino acid exposure (iAUC) that was significantly greater than all other conditions (all P < 0.001; Supplemental Figure 2). Plasma EAA and leucine concentrations were higher in ISO and COMP than in CHO during the early postprandial period (0–120 min; all P < 0.05), whereas methionine and lysine concentrations did not differ from CHO (P > 0.05; Supplemental Figure 2). Postprandial iAUC did not differ between ISO, COMP, and CHO (P > 0.05; Supplemental Figure 2). Cmax of plasma EAA and lysine were highest after MEAT ingestion than the other 3 conditions (all P < 0.05), whereas plasma leucine Cmax was higher in MEAT than COMP (P < 0.001; Supplemental Table 11). ISO ingestion resulted in higher plasma EAA and leucine Cmax compared with CHO (P = 0.039 and P = 0.018, respectively). Discussion

This study is the first to examine the effectiveness of complementary protein pairing to enhance the regulation of postexercise MPS rates in healthy young adults. Despite a more balanced amino acid profile than either rice or beans alone (Table 2), COMP failed to elevate postexercise plasma amino acid concentrations, suggesting impaired protein digestibility, reduced amino acid absorbability, and/or higher splanchnic amino acid sequestration. The ISO condition was used to overcome the limitations of whole-food plant foods and to investigate the influence of the plant food matrix. Indeed, ingestion of ISO led to a rapid but transient elevation in plasma amino acid concentrations. However, the myofibrillar protein synthetic response assessed over 5 h of postexercise recovery did not differ between conditions.

Whole-plant foods are generally recognized as lower-quality protein sources, partly due to less favorable amino acid composition compared with animal-based proteins [11,37]. Although COMP (and ISO) matched the TAA content of MEAT [24], the EAA content remained 31% to 47% lower, particularly histidine, lysine, methionine, and tryptophan (Figure 2). Notably, ISO and COMP provided leucine and other BCAAs in amounts that matched or exceeded those in the MEAT condition. Despite improved amino acid composition, COMP ingestion did not elicit a significant postprandial increase in plasma amino acid concentrations. In fact, most of the plasma amino acid concentrations declined, resulting in a reduced postprandial iAUC for all amino acids except lysine. In contrast, ISO induced a rapid yet transient increase in plasma amino acid concentrations, followed by a decline below baseline, resulting in a negative postprandial plasma amino acid iAUCs during the postexercise period.

These results contrast with previous studies examining plant protein isolates/concentrates [25,[38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44], [45]], meat substitutes [10,46], and whole foods in the postprandial and postexercise states [16]. This contrast is likely underpinned by 2 distinct factors associated with our study. First, the 20 g plant protein dose used here differs from other studies showing positive results, which generally used ≥30 g of plant protein [16,25,[38], [39], [40],[42], [43], [44]]. Second, the coingestion of 114 g of carbohydrates, which is ∼double used in previous work [47,48], likely attenuated plasma amino acid availability. This is evidenced by our observed high postprandial insulinemia (4.5 × higher than isonitrogenous animal whole food) [24]. Beyond this, the inherent structural characteristic of the whole-food plant matrix may have further reduced true ileal digestibility compared with isolated plant proteins [5,6,49]. Although other factors, such as increased splanchnic amino acid extraction rates with plant protein ingestion [7] or exercise-induced gut damage [50], remain plausible, these mechanisms were not directly assessed in the current study. Overall, the negative net plasma amino acid exposure (iAUC) is likely related to the specific combination of a lower protein dose and a high-carbohydrate whole-food matrix that limited the postprandial rise in plasma amino acids during the postexercise period. In other words, our results demonstrate that when complementary whole foods (i.e., beans and rice) are paired to reach a protein dose (∼20 g) typically associated with a robust stimulation of postexercise MPS rates, the obligatory coingestion of nonprotein nutrients (a high-carbohydrate load in this case) can significantly reduce postexercise plasma amino acid concentrations. Our findings underpin the notion that postexercise aminoacidemia in response to protein ingestion is not solely dependent on protein dose or quality but is further modulated by the caloric and carbohydrate density required to achieve a 20 g dose in a whole-food context.

Despite both conditions elevating postexercise MPS rates above rest, we also observed a pronounced suppression of postexercise amino acid availability after ISO ingestion. The decline of plasma EAA and leucine concentrations below baseline, resulting in negative postprandial iAUC values, suggests that neither COMP nor ISO delivered sufficient dietary amino acids to interact with the potent anabolic stimulus of resistance exercise. This blunted dietary amino acid availability likely constrained our ability to detect any subtle anabolic effects and contributed to null myofibrillar protein synthetic outcomes. The absence of differences in postexercise MPS between conditions indicates that the plant-based matrix did not prevent the stimulation of MPS rates. Additionally, the lack of additive effect in the ISO condition, despite the rapid increase in aminoacidemia, is likely related to the fact that the “spike” in elevated plasma amino acids was too short-lived to sustain elevated MPS rates throughout the 5-h recovery window.

The examination of the Akt-mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin) signaling events did not reveal any anabolic mechanistic differences between ISO and COMP. The increased phosphorylation of AktSer473, p70S6kThr389, rpS6Ser240/244, and 4EBP1Thr37/46 and the decreased phosphorylation of eEF2Thr56 are consistent with previous work examining postprandial and postexercise related changes in anabolic signaling [[51], [52], [53]]. Although leucine is a key regulator of mTOR signaling and muscle protein synthesis [[54], [55], [56]], the higher leucine Cmax and early postprandial concentrations after ISO compared with COMP did not translate into measurable differences in phosphorylation of signaling proteins. This may be attributed to the specific timing of the muscle biopsies or, alternatively, the strong anabolic influence of the resistance exercise bout. It is possible that the exercise stimulus effectively “masked” or overridden the subtle dose-dependent effects of leucine on the stimulation of postprandial MPS rates, as suggested in previous research [19,56,57].

To provide a broader physiological perspective, we included an exploratory comparison with prior data involving the ingestion of ground meat (MEAT) and carbohydrates alone [24]. We acknowledge that these conditions were not randomly assigned within the current experimental framework and differed in total energy and carbohydrate content. Although these cross-trial observations should be viewed as hypothesis generating as opposed to confirmatory, the results suggest that the high-carbohydrate whole-plant matrix may present unique challenges for dietary amino acid delivery to skeletal muscle tissue that are not present in leaner, animal-based whole foods [24]. Consistent with the attenuated postexercise aminoacidemia, muscle-free concentrations of EAAs, BCAAs, leucine, methionine, and lysine declined at 300 min with no differences between ISO and COMP (Figure 8). Although muscle protein synthesis is assumed to be primarily signaled by extracellular amino acids [58], neither condition replenished the intramuscular pool, which was likely depleted to support the elevated demand associated with increased stimulation of MPS rates. This pattern mirrored the decline in muscle-free amino acids under the nonprotein (CHO) condition (Figure 8), which also showed negative postprandial plasma amino acids iAUCs during postexercise recovery [24]. However, ingesting an equal amount of animal protein (MEAT) maintained the muscle tissue free amino acid pool during postexercise recovery. As opposed to COMP, ISO, and CHO, MEAT ingestion was also associated with a more positive postprandial plasma amino acid iAUC (Supplemental Figure 2). This highlights the critical role of elevated plasma amino acid availability in supporting the postexercise replenishment of the intramuscular amino acid pool [58]

To further evaluate the anabolic potential of ISO and COMP in the postexercise state, a CHO condition was included as a nonprotein exercise control. We demonstrated that postprandial MPS was not different between the ISO, COMP, or CHO conditions (Figure 6B). This result suggests that ISO and COMP ingestion did not further enhance the stimulation of MPS beyond the resistance exercise stimulus alone. In contrast, ingestion of MEAT significantly increased postexercise MPS rates above all other conditions. These findings support a role for high-quality animal protein for increasing plasma amino acid availability, maintaining the intramuscular amino acid pool, and robustly stimulating the postexercise myofibrillar protein synthetic response. This aligns with previous research demonstrating that stimulation of muscle protein synthesis rates is greater after a beef-based omnivore meal than after an isonitrogenous whole-food plant-based meal in the absence of an exercise stimulus in older adults [16].

In conclusion, complementary protein pairing improved the amino acid profile of the plant-based meal; however, the whole-food matrix and high-carbohydrate context likely compromised absorption kinetics, resulting in a blunted postexercise leucine Cmax compared with the isolated condition. Although blunted Cmax did not impair postexercise anabolic signaling or MPS rates, which were similarly stimulated in both conditions, these results must be interpreted within the specific context of the limited postprandial aminoacidemia and negative net amino acid exposure observed. Although exploratory cross-trial comparisons with previously collected data suggest that whole-animal protein foods may provide a different level of postexercise anabolic support, these observations are hypothesis generating and sit outside the randomized framework of the current study. It remains possible that the ∼20 g dose used here, when combined with a high-carbohydrate whole-food matrix, is anabolically insufficient to further augment the exercise response, functioning at a threshold where the interaction between ingested nutrients and the exercise stimulus remains constrained. Therefore, our findings should not be viewed as definitive evidence against the effectiveness of protein pairing at higher absolute intakes, other plant protein pairings, or isolated forms. Furthermore, these findings reflect acute postexercise responses in a predominantly male cohort of healthy young adults and may not directly translate to other populations. Our skewed sex ratio should not limit the translation of our findings, as prior evidence showed no sex differences in postexercise MPS rates after protein ingestion [59]. Although the acute stimulation of muscle protein synthesis rates is a direct marker of muscle protein remodeling, these results only represent a single meal or “snapshot” in time. Long-term longitudinal studies are ultimately required to determine how these acute metabolic signals integrate over time to modulate the long-term skeletal muscle phenotype.
