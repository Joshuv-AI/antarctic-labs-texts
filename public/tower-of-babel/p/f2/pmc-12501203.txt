Incentivizing teamwork to promote sustainability
Authors: Kathleen Segerson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceEnvironmental economists have long studied the role of firm-level policies (e.g., regulations, taxes, subsidies) in promoting improvements in environmental protection and natural resource management. More recently, drawing from work on collective action and cooperation, economists have also studied policies based on group-level performance. In both cases, the focus has been on promoting individualistic effort rather than teamwork. In contexts where teamwork can be an important contributor to addressing environmental challenges, it is important to understand the extent to which alternative policy approaches can incentivize this type of collaborative behavior. The results presented here highlight a potentially important role for policies based on group-level performance as a means of incentivizing teamwork, which cannot be achieved under policies based solely on an individual-level performance.

There are a variety of contexts in which “pitching in” to address environmental or resource management problems could benefit from individuals or firms working not just atomistically on their own but also collaboratively as a group. The collaboration could involve joint production, where a given outcome requires input or action by all members of the group (pure team production), as, for example, when a team works together to develop a less polluting product or production process. Alternatively, it could simply involve one member of the group helping others improve the other’s environmental performance, through, for example, the construction of beneficial structures to promote conservation or habitat protection or the sharing of information about environmental technologies or vulnerabilities.

There is an extensive literature on the extent to which we can expect individuals to “cooperate” to solve problems involving the provision of public goods, much of it in the context of environmental and natural resource management. The interest in this question dates back at least to Olson’s seminal book on collective action (1) but is often associated with the work of Elinor Ostrom as well (2). A key interest of Ostrom’s was in identifying conditions under which individuals would contribute to the management of a common property resource and in developing “design principles” that would foster this behavior and thereby solve the “prisoners’ dilemma” problem that arises when the benefits of an individual’s actions are shared jointly but the associated costs are not (2, 3).

Two features are notable about the contexts generally studied by Ostrom and others who built on her work. First, the concept of cooperation considered in this and related work typically takes the form of increased individual voluntary contributions to a public good (or restriction on use of a common property resource), or what is often referred to as increased individual “effort” in the related economics literature on team production (4, 5). The fully cooperative outcome is one in which individuals make contributions or effort decisions that maximize net benefits for the group as a whole rather than shirking or free-riding on the contributions or effort of others. Note that it is the outcome that is deemed to be cooperative, not the specific process used to reach that outcome, and individuals need not “work together” to achieve the outcome.* In particular, this notion of cooperation does not include the possibility that cooperation might instead take the form of one member of a group directly helping another member through, for example, directly offering assistance or sharing knowledge or information.† Such help could be a substitute for or a complement to the latter’s own effort. This type of cooperation through directly helping others improve their performance has received some (albeit limited) attention in the economics and management literature on the design of employee compensation schemes, where it has been recognized that managers might want to incentivize members of a work team to work cooperatively (rather than individually in isolation) to achieve a collective goal (e.g., development of a new product) (e.g., refs. 7–11). While help can be thought of as a form of cooperation among team members (e.g., ref. 9), to distinguish it from the notion of cooperation (i.e., increased effort or contribution) employed by Ostrom and others (2, 4), it is sometimes instead termed a form of collaboration or teamwork (12, 13).

The second feature of the typical problems studied by Ostrom (2) is the absence of impacts outside the group of potential contributors, often referred to as “third-party externalities”. For example, when a group of herders share a common grazing land, both the benefits and costs of use of the land are typically viewed as being borne by the herders themselves rather than by others. Likewise, when a group of fishers in a village seek to manage a local fishery, the short-term benefits of harvesting the resource as well as the long-term costs of any overexploitation are assumed to be borne primarily by the local fishers. In these contexts, under certain conditions, successful self-governance by the group itself can lead to socially desirable outcomes where all benefits and costs are considered (2, 14). In such cases, there is no need for an external party (such as a regulator) to intervene to ensure that impacts on others outside the group are considered as well. However, in many cases the activities of the members of a group can impact others outside the group. For example, runoff from fertilizer and pesticide use by farmers upstream can damage downstream ecosystems and those who benefit from those ecosystems. Likewise, deforestation by a group of landowners can contribute to climate change with the associated damages being borne largely by others. In these cases, it is unlikely that pure self-governance of the type studied by Ostrom (2, 14) will adequately account for these external impacts.

The goal of this paper is to explore ways to incentivize actions to improve sustainability when there is the possibility for individuals (or firms) to help one another or collaborate to meet common goals and when those actions can impact others who have not contributed to the associated environmental damages. Following a long tradition in economics that looks at the role of alternative policy instruments in addressing environmental and natural resource management (e.g., ref. 15), we consider whether (and if so, how) public policies can be designed to incentivize collaboration/help in this context. In particular, we ask the following two questions: 1) when might it be desirable from a social perspective to incentivize help (in addition to effort) to foster sustainability, and 2) how can help be incentivized? We consider two types of policy approaches, one based on individual performance and the second based on group (or collective) performance. This parallels the consideration of individual vs. team-based employee compensation schemes (e.g., individual vs. team bonuses) in the context of workplace incentives. We present a generalization of the model of collective approaches in Kotchen and Segerson (16) that incorporates help and use the model to answer these questions. A key result is that, although traditional individual policy approaches (such as emissions/discharge taxes) that have been the focus of most of the economic literature on environmental policy can incentivize an individual or firm’s contributions to improving their own environmental performance, they cannot incentivize improving overall environmental performance through helping others. In contrast, policies based on group performance can incentivize both types of actions.

The paper contributes to several areas of research. First, it contributes to the literature on collective action by broadening the notion of cooperation to explicitly include help as a form of cooperation. To date, the theoretical and experimental analysis of cooperation has viewed cooperation as primarily consisting of individual voluntary contributions or effort (as opposed to free-riding) to meet collective goals (e.g., ref. 4). Second, it contributes to the general literature on environmental policy design by incorporating help into a formal model of incentives in this context and showing that distinguishing between help and effort is potentially important in understanding the incentive effects of alternative policy approaches. A notable difference between effort and help relates to regulatory control—while it might be possible to regulate individual effort (if it is observable), help cannot typically be regulated (i.e., mandated) and must instead be incentivized. Third, it contributes to the literature on the use of group performance approaches to promote sustainability, by suggesting an additional potential rationale for the use of collective approaches that has not been recognized in previous literature on environmental policy design. Finally, it furthers previous efforts to bring insights from the literature on employee compensation schemes into discussions about solving social collective action problems, providing an additional bridge between two bodies of literature that have been largely disconnected to date (5, 7, 17).

Previous Related Work

Help as a Form of Cooperation/Collaboration.

As noted above, there are a number of contexts in which individuals could work as a team and provide help to one another to improve overall performance by the group. Help or collaboration can take a number of different forms. In its simplest form, help might simply take the form of some people or firms helping to pay for the costly activities undertaken by others. This is akin to one individual or firm making a payment to another in exchange for the latter increasing their effort to achieve a given outcome, such as increased production, i.e., paying someone else to do more (18). In this case, the help is purely monetary. However, help can also be “real” (rather than simply monetary). For example, in the context of agricultural nonpoint source pollution, one farmer might help another farmer construct structural best management practices (BMPs) to reduce the latter’s pollution loadings, where the help could go beyond assistance in paying the associated costs and include helping with the needed labor (akin to Amish barn raising); or a farmer who is experienced with erosion management and one who has expertise in precision farming might help each other develop and implement plans for reducing runoff from their fields. Alternatively, help could take the form of sharing information with one’s neighbors or other fishing vessels, as in the case of sharing information about bycatch hotspots (19). Help in the form of information sharing can also arise when a more skilled or experienced party helps another learn and improve their skills, so that over time the effort exerted by the less-skilled party will become more effective (e.g., refs. 20 and 21).

A limited literature exists within economics and management on the provision of help in the workplace.‡ This literature suggests that workers who earn individual performance pay have little incentive to help other employees and a strong incentive to free ride on others’ help (e.g., refs. 9 and 31). However, compensation schemes that depend at least partially on group performance—for example, through team bonuses—can incentivize both effort and help (7, 11). This has implications for the design of optimal employee compensation schemes. For example, Itoh (8, 32) studies compensation schemes in a context where workers can choose both effort and help. The focus is on whether it is optimal for a manager to choose a compensation scheme that induces help (and hence team production).§ A key consideration is the nature of the cost of helping others and in particular whether effort and help are perfect substitutes (as would be expected, for example, if they are measured solely in terms of expenditure or time).¶

As noted above, in some contexts help takes the form of information sharing.# For example, in teams where skill levels vary across members, more skilled or experienced workers can help less skilled members of the team, providing a form of mentorship (20). Moreover, commitments to provide help over a sufficiently long period of time can increase incentives for high-ability types to provide help over time to low-ability types to move the outcome from an equilibrium with low overall productivity or performance to a better equilibrium (21).

Group-Performance Approaches.

The economic literature on environmental policy design has traditionally focused on policy instruments that involve restrictions or rewards or penalties that are based on the activities or performance of individual firms or landowners (15). These include regulations of individual or firm activities, as well as price-based instruments such as taxes on emissions or discharges, subsidies for reductions in emissions or investments in environmentally friendly technologies, and cap-and-trade programs with allowances that are tradable. The key to these instruments is that the restrictions or rewards/penalties faced by each individual or firm depend only on its own environmental performance, reflecting its own choices, and not on the choices and hence performance of others.

However, it is also possible for policies to be based on group-level activity or performance where the restrictions or rewards/penalties faced by an individual or firm depend on the overall performance of an identified group to which it belongs. In this case, each individual or firm is affected not only by their own choices but by the choices of others within the group as well. For example, fishing harvest or bycatch quotas can be allocated to a group of vessels such as a cooperative (rather than to individual vessels), with penalties imposed if the group exceeds its collective quota (33, 34). Similarly, payments for provision of ecosystem services (through, for example, reduced deforestation) can be made to villages (rather than individual landowners) based on the aggregate performance of the entire village (35). In the context of agricultural water pollution, tax-based penalties or rewards can be based on ambient levels of water quality, which depend on the combined decisions of all farmers contributing to the loadings of a given waterbody (e.g., refs. 36 and 37). More generally, penalties for violations of ambient water quality standards, such as total maximum daily loads (TMDLs), or ambient air quality standards, such as the National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) established under the Clean Air Act, are a form of collective approach given that ambient air or water quality depends on the combined actions of all polluters in the relevant airshed or watershed (38). Another example is the use of threats of regulation or imposition of a tax on an entire industry based on industry-level performance (39).

Interest in the use of policies based on group performance has spurred a body of theoretical and experimental literature that seeks to understand the incentives these approaches create. This literature draws from literature within industrial organization on the design of compensation schemes for team production as well as on the literature on the provision of public goods (5). A key issue has been the potential tradeoff between incentives to free-ride and incentives to cooperate to improve group performance, and the impact of different policy designs (e.g., different collective reward or punishment schemes) on these incentives.

The economic literature on group performance mechanisms has its roots in the industrial organization literature on team production, drawing from early work by Alchian and Demsetz (40), Marschak and Radner (41), and Holmstrom (42). This literature focuses on the design of employee compensation schemes (e.g., group bonuses) when the efforts of multiple employees combine to produce a valuable output, but also includes profit sharing and employee ownership schemes (12, 43–45) as well as microfinance (46, 47). A related literature on private provision of public goods and collective action (e.g., refs. 48–50) looks at individual contributions to provide a good that benefits multiple parties. Both cases emphasize the need to structure mechanisms to address free-riding and induce cooperation, as well as the potential for coordination problems and multiple equilibria under some designs. However, the literature on team production and compensation typically considers only incentives to induce effort and, with the exception of studies references above, does not consider the possibility of inducing help.

Insights from the literature on team production have been applied in the area of environmental and natural resource management, beginning with the literature on ambient taxes and subsidies for agricultural nonpoint source pollution (36, 51, 52) but extending to a variety of other contexts as well (5, 38). These include collective payments for ecosystem services (35, 53–55), collective harvest and bycatch quotas in fisheries (e.g., refs. 33 and 56–58), industry-level threats by environmental regulators (39, 59–62), and artisanal mining (63–65). However, all of the above studies have focused on individual effort or contribution choices designed to impact the individual’s own environmental footprint or performance. They do not consider the possibility that individuals could work as a team and provide help to one another to improve overall performance by the group.

Particularly relevant for this paper is the small literature that links incentives to help (e.g., share information or expertise) with the structure of reward systems (e.g., group vs. individual). For example, Pearsall et al. (66) present results from a laboratory experiment showing that hybrid reward systems outperformed individual or group rewards, due to increased information sharing and reduced free-riding (“social loafing”). Several papers within environmental and resource management have argued that group-based approaches foster information sharing in fisheries (e.g., refs. 33 and 34). Sharing information can reduce search costs (67) but can also create free riding due to the public goods nature of information (68). Empirical evidence regarding the effectiveness of voluntary information-sharing mechanisms such as Sea State|| is mixed, and likely depends on the underlying incentives for cooperation in a given fishery (19). These studies do not, however, present a general framework for incorporating help into group performance mechanisms.

Results

Here, we present the results of an analysis of an economic model that can be used to examine conditions under which policymakers might want to encourage help and hence incentivize it (see Methods section for model details). We then examine whether alternative policy approaches can provide those incentives, focusing on a comparison of individual vs. group-based (collective) approaches.

When Might Policymakers Want to Encourage Help?

More specifically, when would it be economically efficient (in the sense of maximizing net benefits for society as a whole) to reduce pollution through a combination of effort and help (rather than simply through individuals undertaking effort to improve their own performance)? The analysis suggests the following conditions under which incentivizing help can improve societal well-being (see Methods section). These conditions depend on the nature of costs and group production. First, when the marginal benefits of help and effort are the same, incentivizing both help and effort is needed to maximize well-being if the marginal costs of the two differ. This could arise in a situation where effort and help are quite different. For example, in the context of bycatch reduction, effort might take the form of a change in one’s own fishing location or technique, whereas help might entail sharing information about bycatch hotspots with other vessels. Alternatively, it is possible that help is economically efficient because of the nature of production. For example, if the marginal costs of both help and effort are identical, it would still be efficient to incentivize both if increases in help and effort are not equally effective in improving environmental performance or resource management. In other words, if help and effort are not interchangeable (perfect substitutes) in terms of either costs or effectiveness, then incentivizing both (rather than just effort) can lead to higher overall societal well-being.

How Can Help Be Incentivized?

As noted above, unlike effort, help cannot typically be regulated (i.e., mandated) and must instead be incentivized. Absent feelings of altruism or warm-glow, policies that reward or punish a firm based only on their own performance (like a standard emissions tax) will not provide incentives for one party to help another when helping others is costly. In this case, the individual incurs a cost for helping others but receives no offsetting benefit (in the form of a bigger reward or a lower penalty when the other party’s performance improves because of the help they received). From a purely self-interested perspective, then, they will not have an incentive to help others. It has been previously shown that penalty or reward schemes based on group performance can induce efficient effort by members of the group and they thus provide an efficient policy alternative to traditional mechanisms such as Pigouvian taxes when individual performance is not observable (see refs. 5 and 16). The question here is whether these approaches can incentivize not only efficient effort but also efficient help.

A standard intuition from the environmental economics literature is that a single instrument cannot efficiently control multiple distorted decisions unless those decisions perfectly align (i.e., go hand-in-hand) (69). For example, two taxes are typically needed to induce efficiency when there are two pollutants that are not produced in fixed proportions, and two instruments (a tax on emissions and a subsidy on output) are generally needed to efficiently control pollution by a monopolist. However, we show here (see Methods) that a single instrument, such as a tax penalty based on group performance (rather than individual performance), can induce both efficient effort and efficient help by both parties, even when parties are not identical. Thus, a single instrument with a single (uniform) tax rate for both parties will induce efficiency for both parties for both decisions even when those parties differ in their cost and production functions and the two distortionary margins are not perfectly aligned. This provides an additional rationale for the use of a policy based on group (rather than individual) production or performance. Moreover, if the marginal damages from pollution are constant, an efficient outcome can be achieved by setting the tax rate equal to that constant marginal damage.

The above results are based on the assumption that one party does not wait to see how much help will be received before committing to their own effort level. In some contexts, though, this assumption might not be appropriate. For example, in the context of a fishery where help takes the form of information about bycatch hotspots and effort is the amount of harvesting in a potential hotspot area, the information (help) will often be received before the effort decision needs to be made. If help does not affect effort choices by the receiving party, then the collective penalty will provide efficient incentives for help even when the amount of help that will be received is known when effort decisions are made. However, if the party receiving the help has an incentive to alter their effort decision in response to the help received because help is either a substitute for or a complement to their own effort, then the incentive to provide help will not be fully efficient even under the group performance penalty. Moreover, depending on whether help and effort are complements or substitutes—that is, whether more help leads to more or less effort by the party receiving the help—the amount of help provided will be higher or lower than the efficient level. In either case, though, help incentives will be greater than under an individual approach.

Discussion/Significance

This paper takes a step toward understanding how group performance-based policies might be used to incentivize not only individual effort but individuals or firms helping others. Broadly speaking, this can be viewed as an explicit form of “cooperation” (collaboration) across parties that might help in meeting environmental and natural resource management goals. There are a variety of conditions under which regulators might want to incentivize both effort and help, as when the two are not perfect substitutes in either production or cost. These could arise in numerous contexts, including agricultural nonpoint pollution control, collective PES programs, and fisheries. In addition, although policies based on individual performance do not incentivize help, group performance based policies can. In particular, a single policy instrument, namely, a uniform tax that is proportional to group output or performance and paid by all members of the group, can lead to efficient choices of both effort and help by all parties, even when those parties are not identical (in their costs and/or production functions), provided effort and help decisions are made at the same time.

When one party commits to helping another before decisions about effort are made, a group performance-based tax will still incentivize some help (and hence more than a policy based on individual performance), but the resulting level of help will not generally be efficient if the effort of one party depends on the level of help provided by another. In this case, whether there is “too much” or “too little” help (relative to the economically efficient level) will depend on the nature of the strategic interaction between the two parties, i.e., whether providing more help to the other party leads to an increase or a decrease in the other party’s effort. Although making help commitments first provides an opportunity for commitment, it also provides an opportunity for welfare-reducing strategic behavior. This has implications for information sharing. For example, if help takes the form of sharing information about bycatch hotspots in a fishery, having one vessel share that information with another vessel might lead to more or less harvesting effort by the second vessel, depending on the information revealed about bycatch at a particular harvest location. Under a group performance mechanism, such as penalties based on fishery-wide bycatch levels, a vessel will have more incentive to share information than under an individual performance mechanism, but might still share more or less often than is efficient because of failure to consider how the impact of the information shared will affect effort and thus overall costs of other vessels. Nonetheless, incentivizing help provides an additional possible benefit of using a group performance-based approach to environmental and natural resource management in contexts where working together to improve environmental outcomes would be socially beneficial.

A key caveat to the analysis and results presented here is the underlying assumption that, if offered, help would be accepted. Evidence suggests that there are contexts in which offers of help might not be accepted, and individuals might be reluctant to offer or ask for help, even if from a rational perspective they would benefit from doing so (70). This has often been studied in a context where help takes the form of altruistic behavior and the reward to the giver comes in the form of esteem or social reputation and self-image (71, 72). Behavioral or psychological factors, such as identity, trust, control, or embarrassment (73) as well as cultural differences and expectations (74, 75) can play a role. This suggests that, in order for collective approaches to be effective in promoting teamwork to foster sustainability, they must address potential barriers to collaboration (working together) and foster an environment of trust and reciprocity where individuals recognize and accept the mutual benefits that teamwork can provide when appropriately incentivized.

Methods

A Basic Model of Group Production with Effort and Help.

Here we present the model used to generate the above results. We begin by presenting a basic 2-party model of group or team production that follows the structure in Kotchen and Segerson (16) but incorporates not only effort but also the possibility of help. We do this in a context where individuals care only about their own payoffs—so they do not help or exert effort for altruistic reasons—and make decisions that are rationally individual, given the decisions of others. Following Itoh (8) and Danilov et al. (11), we initially assume that effort and help are chosen simultaneously. (This assumption is relaxed below.) The key distinction is that effort increases own production or performance while help increases the production or performance of the other party. More specifically, let the production/performance of Party i be given by yi=yi(xi,hj) where xi is the effort level of Party i and hj is the help provided to Party i by Party j (j≠i). For concreteness, using the context of agricultural pollution, yi might represent the pollutant loadings from farm i into a given waterbody, where xi is the effort taken by farmer i to reduce those loadings (i.e., farmer i′s pollution abatement), and hj is the help provided by farmer j to reduce loadings from farm i. With this interpretation, ∂yi∂xi<0 and ∂yi∂hj<0. Group production or performance is then given by y=y(y1,y2). The relevant functional form depends on the specific context of interest. For example, in the agricultural nonpoint pollution context where y represents total loadings to the waterbody, the linear form might be most appropriate, with γi representing delivery coefficients (which could vary across the two farms, depending on, for example, location relative to the waterbody and the farm’s physical characteristics such as topography and soils). A threshold function is appropriate in a pollution context where pollution impacts only arise once aggregate contributions reach a given threshold. This could be the case if a receiving waterbody has an assimilative capacity that would allow low levels of total loadings to be readily dispersed without any damage. In contrast, a weakest-link function captures a situation where input from all contributors is critical to generating the output, i.e., in the absence of one contributor, no output will be generated. An example is smog, which requires the combined presence of pollutants and sunlight. Similarly, production of environmental services by a given ecosystem can be limited by either nitrogen or phosphorus, when the level of one or the other is too low.** A best-shot function captures situations where the overall group performance depends on the contribution of the best (or, depending on the context, the worst) performer/contributor.

Individual Performance-Based Policies.

To see the incentive effects created by different policy approaches, we begin by first considering the use of an individual performance policy, namely, a Pigouvian tax, under the assumption that individual performance is observable. Under this policy, each party pays a total tax that is proportional to their production of the externality-generating good, i.e., Ti=τi·yi, where Ti is the total tax payment by i and τi is i’s tax rate. Comparing the first-order conditions for Eqs. 1 and 4 (for i = 1, 2) shows that it is not possible to find tax rates τi and τj that will induce each party to provide an efficient amount of help to the other party.## The obvious reason is that, because i′s tax payments depend only on i′s performance and not on the performance of j, i has no incentive to help j improve j′s performance when providing help is costly.*** In other words, Pigouvian taxes can incentivize efficient effort but not efficient help.

Group Performance-Based Policies.

However, we show here that a policy based on group performance can induce both efficient effort and efficient help by both parties, even though parties are not identical. To illustrate this, we consider the simplest form of a group performance penalty (collective tax) where the total amount of tax paid by each party is proportional to group output, i.e., Ti=τ·y, where the tax rate τ is identical for both parties.††† Note that the use of the collective tax creates an interdependence between the two parties that does not exist in the absence of any policy or under a policy based on individual performance (16). As a result, modeling the equilibrium under this policy approach requires an assumption about strategic interaction between the parties (see Zhou and Segerson (58) for a related discussion). Here we model Nash behavior, assuming each party chooses their own effort and help levels to minimize their own costs, given the effort and help decisions of the other party.

Under the collective tax, the Nash equilibrium is defined by the following equations for i=1,2 and i≠j:[5]∂Ci∂xi+τ·∂y∂yi·∂yi∂xi=0,[6]∂Ci∂hi+τ·∂y∂yj·∂yj∂hi=0.

Comparing Eqs. 5 and 6 (for i=1,2) to the first-order conditions for Eq. 1 shows that setting τ=-W′(y∗) will induce both efficient effort and efficient help by both parties. Moreover, if W is linear in y, i.e., marginal damages are constant and equal to d, then τ∗=d, even when the impact of effort and help on individual performance differs across the two parties.

Committing to Help Up-Front.

The above results are based on the assumption that help and effort are chosen by both parties at the same time (the Nash assumption). However, in some contexts a party might know whether they will be receiving help before having to decide how much individual effort to undertake. For example, in a fisheries context, a vessel might know whether information about bycatch hotspots will be shared with it before it needs to decide on its harvesting effort. To consider the impact of up-front help commitments, we make two changes to the model above. First, we assume that help is unidirectional, i.e., Party 1 can help Party 2 but not vice versa. Second, we assume that effort decisions are made after the level of help is determined‡‡‡. Combined, these two assumptions allow for a clear sequence of choices by the two parties, where Party 1 first chooses its level of help and then both parties (simultaneously) decide their effort levels. (Similar conclusions hold when help is bilateral, where both parties first choose help levels and then both choose effort levels.) In addition, for simplicity, we also retain the assumption of constant marginal damages and y=y1+y2.

Letting x1∗(h) and x2∗(h) denote the efficient levels of x1 and x2 conditional on h, the efficient level of h (h∗) then solves:[7]MaxW(y1(x1∗(h)+y2(x2∗(h),h))-C1(x1∗(h),h)-C2(x2∗(h)).

We turn next to equilibrium choices of effort and help. Conditional on the level of help, the Nash equilibrium effort levels under the group performance tax are given by the solutions to (5a) for i=1,2. Comparing these to the first-order conditions for Eq. 1 shows that, conditional on h, effort levels for both parties will be efficient if τ=-W′(y∗)=d, as before. Party 1’s choice of h then solves:[8]MinC1(x1∗(h),h)+d·[y1(x1∗(h))+y2(x2∗(h),h)].

Using the Envelope Theorem gives the following first-order condition:[9]∂C1∂h+d·∂y2∂h+d·∂y2∂x2·∂x2∗∂h=0.

In contrast, again by the Envelope Theorem the efficient choice of h satisfies the following condition:[10]∂C1∂h+d·∂y2∂h=0.

The presence of the second (unbracketed) term in Eq. 8 implies that Party 1’s choice of help will not be efficient when h is determined before effort decisions are made and the choice of h affects the choice of 2’s effort, i.e., when ∂x2∗∂h≠0. The presence of this term stems from the fact that Party 1 does not consider 2’s costs when choosing the level of h. If Party 1 considered these costs, then the impact of h on these costs, i.e., -∂C2∂x2·∂x2∗∂h, would have exactly offset the second term in Eq. 8 and the level of help chosen by 1 would have been efficient. However, failure to consider these leads to an inefficient help decision here. Moreover, depending on the sign of ∂x2∗∂h, the chosen level of h can be higher or lower than the efficient level. In particular, given ∂y2∂x2<0, when ∂x2∗∂h>0, implying a form of complementarity between help and effort, the second term in (8) is negative§§§. This means it adds to the marginal (private) benefit of h, yielding a choice of h that is higher than the efficient level. Conversely, if ∂x2∗∂h<0, implying a type of substitutability between help and effort, then the second term adds to the marginal cost of h and results in a level of h that is below the efficient level (but still very likely positive). In other words, the collective tax will induce more help than a tax that is proportional to individual performance (which creates no incentive for help), but, if the level of help impacts the effort level of the other party, then the collective tax will not yield an efficient level of help. In particular, too much or too little help can occur (relative to the efficient level), depending on whether help and effort by the other party are complements or substitutes.
