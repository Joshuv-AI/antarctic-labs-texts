Association of gestational diabetes mellitus with intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy: A 2-sample Mendelian randomization analysis utilizing FinnGen consortium data
Authors: Chunyan Yang, Wenhui Song, Kaosheng Lu, Wei Dang, Yefang Zhao, Liqiao Zhang, Meng Li, Xiaoyuan Wu, Ying He, Xiaohua Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a common metabolic disorder during pregnancy and a significant risk factor for maternal and neonatal complications. Although management strategies have improved, early identification of high-risk cases remains challenging. This study aimed to clarify whether GDM exerts a causal effect on intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP), a hepatic disorder associated with adverse perinatal outcomes. We performed a 2-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis using summary data from the genome-wide association study of the FinnGen consortium. Genetic variants strongly associated with GDM at a predefined significance threshold (P < 5 × 10−6) were selected as instrumental variables. Linkage disequilibrium clumping was applied (r2 < 0.001, 10,000 kb window), and weak instruments with F < 10 were excluded. Multiple MR approaches, including inverse variance weighted, weighted median, MR-Egger, and Mendelian Randomization Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier, were employed to ensure robustness. Inverse variance weighted analysis demonstrated a significant causal association between genetically predicted GDM and ICP (odds ratio [OR] = 1.30, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.08–1.56, P = .005). The weighted median method yielded similar results (OR = 1.36, 95% CI 1.06–1.74, P = .014). Sensitivity analyses showed no evidence of heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy, and the MR-Steiger test confirmed the direction of causality from GDM to ICP. These findings provide genetic evidence supporting a causal effect of GDM on ICP risk and suggest that women with GDM may benefit from closer monitoring of liver function and bile acid levels during pregnancy.

1. Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is recognized as one of the most common metabolic disorders that complicate pregnancy, significantly affecting both maternal and fetal well-being on a global scale. Characterized by glucose intolerance first identified during pregnancy, GDM affects approximately 7% of pregnancies in developed nations, with prevalence rates increasing in tandem with advancing maternal age and the increased incidence of obesity.[1] The clinical consequences of GDM are extensive, including increased risks of preeclampsia, macrosomia, neonatal hypoglycemia, and long-term outcomes such as type 2 diabetes for both mother and child. Despite progress in screening and management strategies, the burden associated with GDM continues to escalate, highlighting the urgent need for improved preventive, diagnostic, and therapeutic approaches.

Meanwhile, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP) is identified as the most common hepatobiliary complication exclusive to pregnancy, with its incidence varying significantly depending on geographic region, ethnicity and environmental influences, but generally estimated at 0.5% to 2% of pregnancies.[2,3] Clinically, ICP is characterized by pruritus and biochemical indicators of cholestasis, particularly elevated maternal serum bile acids. Although the maternal prognosis is typically favorable, ICP poses serious risks to the fetus, including preterm delivery, meconium-stained amniotic fluid, and stillbirth.[2,4] The etiopathogenesis of ICP is multifactorial, involving genetic, hormonal, and environmental factors, and is incompletely understood.[5] Notably, GDM and ICP share several common risk factors, including advanced maternal age, metabolic irregularities, and certain ethnic backgrounds. Both conditions are independently associated with unfavorable perinatal outcomes.[2,4,6,7] Prior observational research has indicated potential epidemiological and clinical correlations between GDM and ICP, implying that women diagnosed with GDM may face a heightened risk of developing ICP within the same gestational period. However, these investigations have been constrained by confounding factors and an inability to establish definitive causality due to their observational frameworks.[6,8] As a result, the mechanistic relationship between GDM and ICP remains unclear, with the directionality and strength of any causal link yet to be conclusively defined.

There is a significant gap in the existing literature on the causal connection between GDM and ICP. Although associations have been identified, the possibility of confounding by shared risk factors, reverse causation, and unmeasured variables diminishes the clarity of findings derived from conventional epidemiological methods. In addition, the lack of robust causal evidence has hindered the establishment of targeted screening and intervention strategies for women at dual risk, highlighting a considerable unmet need in perinatal healthcare. Addressing this knowledge gap is imperative, as elucidating the nature of the relationship between GDM and ICP could improve clinical risk stratification and inform surveillance or preventive measures, potentially alleviating adverse perinatal outcomes.

To address these limitations, Mendelian randomization (MR) has emerged as a powerful epidemiological technique to infer causality by using genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs).[8,9] By leveraging the random assortment of alleles at the point of conception, MR analysis can mitigate bias from confounding and reverse causation that are often present in traditional observational studies. Incorporation of summary statistics from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) within a 2-sample MR framework further enhances statistical power and facilitates the evaluation of causal effects in large independent cohorts.[10,11] Recent advancements in this methodology have enabled more accurate estimations of the causal relationships between complex traits and diseases across diverse populations.

In this study, we conducted a 2-sample MR analysis using summary statistics from the FinnGen biobank, a comprehensive genomic resource, to systematically explore the potential causal effect of GDM on the risk of ICP. This approach allows for stringent control of confounding factors and strengthens the inference regarding causality. By clarifying the etiological relationship between these 2 significant pregnancy complications, our research aims to provide novel insights that could inform clinical risk assessment, guide early screening strategies, and ultimately improve maternal and fetal health outcomes.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and data sources

A 2-sample MR analysis was conducted to investigate the causal relationship between GDM and ICP. This approach leverages genetic variants as IVs to minimize bias from confounding and reverse causation. Summary-level data for both exposure (GDM) and outcome (ICP) were obtained from the FinnGen biobank, a large-scale GWAS dataset encompassing participants of Finnish ancestry. Exposure and outcome GWAS were derived from non-overlapping case–control analyses within FinnGen. All GWAS summary statistics were restricted to participants of European (Finnish) ancestry to minimize population stratification bias.

Both GDM and ICP are pregnancy-specific diagnoses in FinnGen and are defined using national health registry diagnosis codes. FinnGen is a population-based biobank; therefore, the GWAS dataset is not restricted to a dedicated pregnancy cohort. However, cases of GDM and ICP predominantly occur among women with a pregnancy history. In the present 2-sample MR analysis, genetic liability to GDM was treated as the exposure and genetic liability to ICP as the outcome.

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2) with the “TwoSampleMR” and “MR-PRESSO” packages. Ethical approval and informed consent were previously obtained by the original GWAS investigators; therefore, no additional ethical clearance was required for this secondary analysis.

2.2. Selection of instrumental variables

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) significantly associated with GDM were extracted as potential IVs at a genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5 × 10−6). To ensure variant independence, linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping was performed with a threshold of r2 < 0.001 and a window size of 10,000 kb. SNPs with minor allele frequency < 0.01 were excluded. Palindromic SNPs with ambiguous strand alignment were removed to prevent allele mismatch. The strength of each instrument was assessed using the F-statistic, and SNPs with F < 10 were discarded to minimize weak instrument bias.

2.3. Instrumental variable validation

The validity of the selected SNPs as instruments for MR was evaluated according to the 3 core MR assumptions: Relevance: confirmed by the genome-wide significant associations and F-statistics (F > 10); Independence: Independence was supported by the use of genetic variants randomly allocated at conception and by sensitivity analyses assessing potential pleiotropy; Exclusion restriction: assessed using MR-Egger intercept tests, funnel plot symmetry, and Cochran Q-based heterogeneity tests to ensure no horizontal pleiotropy.

These diagnostic analyses collectively confirmed the reliability and validity of the IVs used in this study.

2.4. Statistical analysis

The primary causal estimate was derived using the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method under a random-effects model. Complementary MR methods, including MR-Egger regression, weighted median, weighted mode, and simple mode, were applied to assess the robustness of findings. The Mendelian Randomization Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier global test was used to detect horizontal pleiotropy. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran Q test, while the MR-Egger intercept test was applied to evaluate directional pleiotropy. Detailed heterogeneity statistics are provided in Supplementary Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R563.

To examine the influence of individual SNPs, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted, excluding each SNP in turn and recalculating the causal estimate. The causal direction between GDM and ICP was evaluated using the MR Steiger directionality test.

3. Results

3.1. Instrumental variables

A total of 17 independent SNPs associated with GDM were selected as IVs after applying LD pruning and quality control filters. All SNPs had F-statistics > 10, indicating strong instrument strength. No palindromic or ambiguous variants remained after harmonization between exposure and outcome datasets.

3.2. Primary MR analysis

The IVW analysis demonstrated a statistically significant association between genetically predicted GDM and increased risk of ICP (odds ratio [OR] = 1.30, 95% CI 1.08–1.56, P = .005). Consistent estimates were observed across complementary MR methods: weighted median (OR = 1.36, 95% CI 1.06–1.74, P = .014), weighted mode (OR = 1.40, 95% CI 0.97–2.02, P = .088), simple mode (OR = 1.39, 95% CI 0.93–2.10, P = .131), and MR-Egger regression (OR = 1.45, 95% CI 0.89–2.33, P = .153). These results indicate a positive association between genetic liability to GDM and ICP risk, as summarized in Table 2 and visualized in the forest plot (Fig. 2).

3.3. Sensitivity analyses

Scatter plot analysis demonstrated consistent slopes across all MR methods, supporting a positive causal association between GDM and ICP (Fig. 3). Leave-one-out analysis showed that no single SNP disproportionately influenced the overall causal estimate, indicating the robustness of the results (Fig. 4).

Heterogeneity testing using Cochran Q statistic revealed no significant heterogeneity for either IVW (Q = 13.69, P = .622) or MR-Egger regression (Q = 13.46, P = .567), suggesting consistency among SNP-specific estimates. The funnel plot appeared symmetrical, providing no evidence of directional pleiotropy (Fig. 5). Moreover, the MR-Egger intercept test yielded a P-value > 0.05, suggesting no evidence of directional pleiotropy.

3.4. Directionality and robustness

The MR Steiger directionality test supported that the causal direction flows from GDM to ICP rather than the reverse, with an R2exposure > R2outcome and a P-value indicating inferred direction. Results of the MR Steiger directionality test are presented in Supplementary Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R563.

The overall causal estimates of all MR approaches are summarized in Fig. 6, illustrating the direction and magnitude of GDM effect on ICP. Collectively, these findings provide genetic evidence consistent with a potential causal association between GDM and increased ICP risk.

4. Discussion

4.1. Summary of main findings

The global prevalence of GDM is on the rise, primarily attributed to increasing maternal age and obesity rates. Given the significant health risks associated with GDM, it is crucial to investigate its relationships with other pregnancy complications, such as ICP, which can further complicate outcomes for both the mother and fetus.

4.2. Potential biological mechanisms

This study is designed to clarify the causal link between GDM and ICP by employing a 2-sample MR approach utilizing data from the FinnGen consortium. By utilizing genetic predispositions to GDM as instrumental variables, we aim to furnish convincing evidence concerning the influence of GDM on the onset of ICP. The significant relationship identified between genetic predisposition to GDM and the heightened risk of ICP, as evidenced by the IVW analysis, prompts important mechanistic considerations. A plausible explanation lies in the metabolic and endocrine changes associated with GDM, particularly hyperinsulinemia and dyslipidemia, which may aggravate hepatic bile acid imbalances. Prior research has emphasized the modulatory function of bile acids in glucose and lipid metabolism, with dysregulated bile acid signaling, especially through farnesoid X receptor and Takeda G-protein-coupled receptor 5, being implicated in both insulin resistance and cholestatic liver diseases.[6] Furthermore, GDM is linked to chronic low-grade inflammation and altered chemokine profiles, potentially further hindering hepatocellular bile acid transport and contributing to the pathogenesis of ICP.[12] Notably, this observation is consistent with research indicating that women experiencing early-onset ICP have a greater incidence of GDM, highlighting the bidirectional metabolic interaction between these conditions.[7] However, while previous observational studies have established epidemiological correlations, the current genetic evidence substantiates a causal association and points toward shared metabolic and inflammatory pathways.

4.3. Robustness and sensitivity analyses

The consistency of findings across various MR methods, including the weighted median approach, further bolsters the robustness of the identified causal relationship. The weighted median method is particularly advantageous in scenarios involving heterogeneous or potentially invalid IVs, as it can yield consistent estimates even when up to 50% of the instruments are invalid.[11] This methodological strength is especially pertinent considering the polygenic and multifactorial characteristics of both GDM and ICP, where pleiotropic effects may be significant. The agreement between the estimates derived from IVW and the weighted median method suggests that the causal effect is not influenced by a small group of outlier variants, thereby alleviating concerns regarding horizontal pleiotropy. Moreover, the capacity of the weighted median approach to produce reliable estimates in the context of complex trait genetics has been substantiated by prior research. Recent literature on MR methodologies highlights its significance in reinforcing causal inferences related to metabolic disorders.[10] In this context, the findings presented here broaden the application of robust MR techniques within the dual domains of endocrinology and hepatology, thereby enhancing methodological rigor in unraveling intricate relationships among diseases.

Although secondary MR analyses employing alternative techniques produced effect estimates consistent with those from primary analyses, the absence of statistical significance necessitates careful interpretation. This inconsistency may stem from variations in statistical power or sensitivity to outlier genetic instruments. For example, MR-Egger regression aims to identify and correct for directional pleiotropy but is inherently less powerful and more prone to imprecision, particularly when the number of IVs is limited.[13] Prior research has indicated that a lack of statistical significance in MR-Egger or simple mode-based estimators does not automatically discredit the observed association, provided that results from primary and median-based methodologies are consistent.[10] Additionally, the directionality observed aligns with earlier cohort studies, which indicate a heightened risk of ICP among patients with GDM, even if statistical robustness is not consistently maintained following multivariable adjustments.[8] Collectively, these insights underscore the critical role of methodological triangulation in MR investigations and highlight the necessity for larger, well-powered GWAS to substantiate nuanced causal effects related to complex pregnancy complications.

The absence of significant heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy, as evidenced by sensitivity analyses, bolsters the credibility of the causal relationship between genetic predisposition to GDM and the risk of ICP. Sensitivity evaluations, including Cochran Q test for heterogeneity and the MR-Egger intercept test for pleiotropy, did not indicate substantial violations of IV assumptions, thus confirming the reliability of the MR framework in this analysis.[9] This methodological strength is further corroborated by leave-one-out analyses, which demonstrated that no SNP disproportionately affected the overall effect estimate, suggesting a polygenic rather than an artifact-driven association due to specific loci.[11] This observation notably contrasts with some earlier observational studies where residual confounding and measurement errors remained unaddressed as potential sources of bias. Consequently, the absence of observable pleiotropy or heterogeneity in the present MR analyses yields a high level of confidence in the inferred causal relationship, distinguishing this study from previous investigations that lacked methodological rigor.

4.4. Polygenic nature of the association

Finally, the finding that no single SNP demonstrated a predominant effect on the observed association, as evidenced by leave-one-out analysis, emphasizes the polygenic nature of the connection between GDM and ICP. This finding aligns with contemporary perspectives that both GDM and ICP are genetically intricate traits influenced by multiple loci with minor effects. Moreover, it resonates with recent advances in polygenic risk modeling, which highlight the need to aggregate numerous genetic signals to capture the heritable aspects of metabolic and hepatic disorders.[14]

4.5. Progesterone and exogenous progestogens

Progesterone metabolites may increase cholestatic susceptibility, and exogenous progesterone has been associated with earlier onset or exacerbation of cholestasis in susceptible individuals.[15] Therefore, women receiving progestogens who develop pruritus or biochemical abnormalities may warrant a lower threshold for bile acid testing and closer follow-up.

4.6. Dietary patterns and modifiable risk factors

High glycemic load diets and low dietary fiber intake are associated with an increased risk of GDM, whereas higher fiber intake appears protective.[16] Such dietary patterns may also influence bile acid metabolism and systemic inflammation, providing a plausible link to cholestatic susceptibility during pregnancy.

Although the present MR study does not directly evaluate dietary exposures, the observed causal relationship between genetic liability to GDM and ICP supports the hypothesis that shared metabolic pathways, potentially shaped by diet, may contribute to both conditions.

4.7. Clinical implications

The present MR analysis provides genetic evidence supporting a potential causal role of GDM in increasing the risk of ICP. From a clinical perspective, these findings underscore the importance of increased surveillance of cholestatic symptoms and biochemical abnormalities among women diagnosed with GDM.

In practice, clinicians may consider actively asking for information about pruritus in patients with GDM, particularly during the second and third trimesters. Liver function tests and serum bile acid measurements may be warranted when pruritus occurs or liver enzymes are elevated. Closer monitoring may be especially justified in patients with markedly elevated bile acid levels, given the established association between severe bile acid elevation and adverse perinatal outcomes.[4] In contrast, women diagnosed with ICP may also benefit from evaluating glucose metabolism, as metabolic dysregulation may coexist.

Although MR does not define specific screening intervals, the inferred causal direction from GDM to ICP supports a risk-stratified clinical approach aimed at early identification and prevention of adverse maternal and fetal outcomes.

4.8. Limitations

The primary limitations of this investigation arise from its dependence on observational data sourced from the FinnGen consortium, which may introduce biases attributable to population stratification and the genetic diversity observed among various ethnic groups. Although the cohort size is considerable, the extent to which the findings can be generalized to different populations remains uncertain, given that the genetic underpinnings of GDM and ICP may exhibit considerable variability across distinct groups. Furthermore, the absence of clinical validation through experimental methodologies constrains the ability to extrapolate the results, as the biological mechanisms that underpin the identified associations necessitate additional investigation.

In summary, this research strengthens support for a potential causal association between GDM and ICP as demonstrated through MR analysis, positioning GDM as a notable risk factor for ICP. This innovative perspective highlights the necessity for proactive screening and intervention strategies aimed at managing GDM to reduce the potential risks associated with ICP. Future studies should prioritize validation of these results in diverse populations while integrating clinical methodologies that account for genetic predispositions, thus enhance individualized maternal care and improve maternal–fetal health outcomes. 5. Conclusion and clinical implications

In conclusion, this MR study provides genetic evidence supporting an increased risk of ICP associated with GDM. Early identification and close monitoring of women with GDM, including assessment of serum bile acids and liver function, may contribute to reducing adverse perinatal outcomes. These findings highlight the importance of integrating metabolic and hepatobiliary risk assessments in prenatal care and may inform the design of preventive strategies aimed at improving maternal and fetal health.
