Identifying the Healthcare Burden of Diabetic Ketoacidosis: Economic Analysis and Mitigation Strategies for Adult Patients in the United Arab Emirates
Authors: Fayez AlShamsi, Ketan K. Dhatariya, Meira Sulaiman Aldhanhani, Naser Salem Alshkeili, Mohammed Hareb M. Alsaadi, Rashid Hamad Almansoori, Adnan Agha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDiabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is a potentially fatal diabetes complication marked by hyperglycemia, ketonemia, and acidosis that remains a major global healthcare burden despite advances in diabetes management. The aim of this study was to quantify the healthcare costs of DKA admissions at a tertiary hospital in the United Arab Emirates (UAE), and evaluate cost-effective preventative strategies.MethodsA retrospective electronic record analysis of all DKA admissions from 2019 to 2023 at Tawam Hospital, Al Ain, was conducted after ethical approval from Tawam Human Research Ethics Committee, approval number MF2058-2023-916. Clinical characteristics, precipitating factors, length of stay, insurance status, and total inpatient costs were analyzed for all consecutive adult (≥ 16 years) DKA admissions over this period. The cost of DKA-related admission was compared to the price of insulin therapy in underinsured patients who are unable to afford insulin.ResultsThere were 134 patients admitted with DKA (157 admissions) over 5 years (one DKA admission every 11.6 days). The mean hospitalization cost per DKA admission was 12,274 ± 10,213 USD (45,461 ± 37,826 AED), with an average length of stay of 10.2 ± 11.7 days (1203 USD [4,457 AED] per day). Insulin unaffordability, a preventable factor, led to 13.4% (21 cases) of admissions, incurring a total cost of 257,765 USD (954,686 AED). Underinsured patients demonstrated significantly higher ICU utilization (76.8% vs 52.5%, p < 0.01) and mean costs (14,269 USD [52,847 AED] vs 11,133 USD [41,235 AED], p = 0.07) compared to adequately insured patients. In comparison, the average cost of insulin is 38 USD (139.5 AED) per patient/month, indicating that the cost of one preventable DKA admission equals 27 years of insulin therapy. The overall mortality rate of DKA admissions was 4.5%.ConclusionDKA imposes a substantial economic and clinical burden in the UAE, with mean hospitalization costs of 12,274 USD per admission. While insulin unaffordability accounts for 13.4% of admissions (costing 257,765 over 5 years), infections represent the largest precipitating factor at 49.0%. Comprehensive DKA prevention requires a multifaceted approach: infection management education, ensuring insulin access for underinsured patients, and structured diabetes education. Our analysis suggests that addressing insulin affordability could be economically favorable, as preventing one DKA admission would offset 27 years of insulin costs. However, optimal resource allocation requires addressing all major precipitating factors to achieve meaningful reductions in DKA burden.Graphical abstract available for this article.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13300-025-01812-w.

Introduction

Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is a life-threatening acute complication of diabetes mellitus characterized by the triad of hyperglycemia and/or history of diabetes, ketonemia, and metabolic acidosis [1]. Despite advances in diabetes management, DKA continues to impose a rising clinical and economic burden on healthcare systems worldwide [2]. In the United States (USA), annual DKA-related hospitalizations increased from 140,000 in 2008 (costing over US $2.4 billion [8.8 billion AED]) to 220,340 admissions (costs reaching US $6.6 billion [24.2 billion AED]) in 2017 [2, 3]. This represents a stark contrast from the early 2000s, when the frequency of DKA admission declined, versus the past decade, which has seen a steadily rising trend, reflecting a complex interplay of clinical, socioeconomic, and health system factors [4]. In the UK, DKA admissions have increased in certain populations Though treatment costs remain significantly lower than in the USA, averaging 2,064 GBP (10,120 AED) per adult and 1,387 GBP (6,800 AED) per adolescent case [5, 6]. These figures highlight the global burden of DKA and the urgent need for effective prevention strategies.

In the Middle East, while DKA has traditionally been associated with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), approximately 30% of DKA cases now occur in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), reflecting the increasing prevalence of T2DM in the region [7]. Patients with T1DM can also experience higher rates of DKA than Western cohorts, often due to suboptimal treatment adherence and barriers to healthcare access [8]. In Saudi Arabia, over half of DKA cases are triggered by treatment nonadherence, followed by infections, with one-third of patients readmitted with DKA within a year, highlighting missed opportunities for intervention [9]. The United Arab Emirates (UAE) faces distinct challenges in DKA management due to high diabetes prevalence, population diversity, and disparities in insurance coverage that affect access to care [10]. Research in the UAE indicates that DKA remains a significant and recurrent complication among patients with diabetes, with substantial clinical and economic implications, with the direct cost of a DKA admission estimated at 4410 USD (16,335 AED) in 2011 [11]. Another study from the Middle East demonstrated that one-third of DKA admissions in a tertiary hospital occurred in patients within 1 year of prior DKA events, indicating possible gaps in outpatient management and patient education [12]. Research on precipitating factors of DKA identified infections and insulin nonadherence as the most frequent triggers [13]. In a 2022 UAE study of 220 adult DKA admissions, 171 (77.9%) involved patients with T1DM, while 49 (22.3%) had T2DM, and 9 (18.4%) had euglycemic DKA attributed to sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor usage [14]. These findings collectively underscore the urgent need for structured prevention strategies to mitigate the clinical and financial burden of DKA in the UAE.

Current evidence supports the role of technology and structured interventions in DKA prevention. A recent study by Mohler et al. demonstrated that a comprehensive program incorporating diabetes technology (continuous glucose monitoring [CGM], hybrid closed-loop systems) reduced DKA hospitalizations by 74% and decreased costs by 71%, saving 31,000 USD per patient annually [15]. Similarly, research from New Zealand showed that free access to CGM devices reduced DKA-related emergency department visits by 65% in patients with T1DM [16]. In pediatric populations, timely ketone monitoring and standardized protocols have been shown to reduce DKA admissions by 30–50% [17, 18]. These interventions are particularly relevant for regions with multitiered insurance systems, where disparities in technology access may exacerbate DKA risk among underinsured populations [19].

The economic burden of DKA extends beyond direct hospitalization costs, encompassing lost productivity, long-term complications, and reduced quality of life. In the USA, indirect costs of diabetes-related hospitalizations, including DKA, account for an additional 30–40% beyond direct medical expenses [20]. Moreover, each DKA admission is associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment, cardiovascular complications, and premature mortality, particularly in younger patients [21]. Young adults aged 18–30 years face unique challenges, with DKA rates in this group doubling over the past decade, often related to insurance transitions, psychosocial factors, and cost-related insulin rationing [22, 23]. In the UAE context, where expatriate workers comprise a significant portion of the population and often have employment-linked insurance, job loss or transition can result in catastrophic interruptions in diabetes care [24].

Previous research has highlighted the critical role of insulin affordability in DKA prevention. Herkert et al. found that 25.5% of patients with T1DM in the USA reported cost-related insulin underuse, with these individuals experiencing significantly higher rates of poor glycemic control and acute complications [21]. Similarly, research from low- and middle-income countries demonstrates that even modest co-payments for insulin can lead to treatment interruption and increased DKA risk [25]. The World Health Organization has identified insulin access as a global health priority, noting that in many countries, out-of-pocket insulin costs exceed 5% of household income—a threshold associated with catastrophic health expenditure [26]. These findings suggest that targeted interventions addressing insulin affordability could yield substantial clinical and economic benefits.

The UAE presents a distinctive setting to examine the relationship between healthcare system structure and DKA admissions. Unlike the universal coverage model of the UK’s National Health Service or the mixed public–private system of the USA, the UAE operates a multitiered insurance framework. In Abu Dhabi, citizens receive comprehensive government-funded coverage (Thiqa), while the expatriate population receives insurance of varying levels—from enhanced to basic—often linked to employment status and income [27]. This stratification may create disparities in diabetes care access, where underinsured or uninsured residents may face financial barriers to essential therapies like insulin, increasing the risk of preventable DKA [25]. Analyzing DKA within this context is key to providing insights for developing equitable, cost-effective prevention strategies in other nations with similarly heterogeneous coverage. Therefore, this study aimed to quantify the direct healthcare costs of DKA admissions at a major UAE tertiary hospital, characterize precipitating factors with emphasis on preventable causes, evaluate the economic implications of improving insulin access for underinsured patients, and identify factors contributing to DKA recurrence to inform targeted prevention strategies.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a retrospective electronic chart review of all DKA admissions at Tawam Hospital in Al Ain, UAE, from January 2019 through December 2023. Tawam Hospital is a 468-bed tertiary care referral center serving the Al Ain region and neighboring areas. This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from Tawam Human Research Ethics Committee, approval number MF2058-2023-916 with a waiver of informed consent due to the retrospective design and use of de-identified data. The UAE healthcare system operates a multitiered insurance framework where UAE nationals receive comprehensive coverage (Thiqa), while expatriates have employment-linked insurance ranging from enhanced to basic coverage. This stratification creates varying levels of medication access, particularly for essential therapies like insulin.

Patient Selection and Diagnostic Criteria

Patients aged ≥ 16 years admitted with a primary diagnosis of DKA were identified through electronic medical records using the following International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes: E10.10 (Type 1 diabetes mellitus with ketoacidosis without coma), E11.10 (Type 2 diabetes mellitus with ketoacidosis without coma), and E13.10 (Other specified diabetes mellitus with ketoacidosis without coma) [28]. DKA diagnosis was confirmed on the basis of American Diabetes Association criteria: plasma glucose > 250 mg/dL (13.9 mmol/L) or known diabetes, arterial pH < 7.30 or serum bicarbonate < 18 mEq/L, and presence of serum or urine ketones [1]. Cases were further classified by severity: mild (pH 7.25–7.30), moderate (pH 7.00–7.24), or severe (pH < 7.00) [29]. Cases were considered as new-onset diabetes (n = 15, 9.6%) if DKA was the presenting manifestation leading to initial diabetes diagnosis, versus established diabetes with known diagnosis prior to admission.

Data Collection

We extracted comprehensive clinical and administrative data including demographics (age, sex, nationality, diabetes type, and duration), clinical parameters at presentation (glucose, pH, bicarbonate, anion gap, ketone levels), precipitating factors, complications during admission, length of stay (total and ICU), and discharge disposition. Insurance status was categorized as (1) comprehensive government coverage (Thiqa for UAE nationals), (2) enhanced private insurance (covering > 80% of medication costs), (3) basic private insurance (covering < 50% of medication costs), or (4) uninsured. For analysis, categories 3 and 4 were combined as “underinsured.”

Cost Analysis

Direct medical costs were obtained from the hospital’s financial database and included all charges from admission to discharge: emergency department services, laboratory and imaging studies, medications, nursing care, physician fees, ICU charges, and facility fees. Costs are presented in both US dollars and UAE dirhams (1 USD = 3.67 AED). Insulin costs for comparison were based on current retail prices at hospital pharmacies for a standard regimen of basal-bolus therapy (approximately 38 USD [139.5 AED] per month).

Precipitating Factors

Precipitating factors were determined through systematic chart review by two independent reviewers, with discrepancies resolved by a third reviewer. Categories included (1) infection (documented by positive cultures, imaging, or clinical diagnosis), (2) insulin omission due to cost/access issues (explicitly documented), (3) other non-compliance (missed doses without financial barriers), (4) new diagnosis of diabetes, (5) pump/device failure, (6) other identifiable causes, and (7) undetermined.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on distribution. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using Student’s t test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with ICU admission and prolonged length of stay (> 7 days). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Clinical Presentation and Severity

At presentation, mean plasma glucose was 468 ± 156 mg/dL (26.0 ± 8.7 mmol/L), pH 7.14 ± 0.13, bicarbonate 10.2 ± 4.8 mEq/L, and anion gap 24.3 ± 6.2 mEq/L. DKA severity distribution was mild in 31 (19.7%), moderate in 88 (56.1%), and severe in 38 (24.2%) admissions. ICU admission was required for 96 admissions (61.1%), with mean ICU stay of 3.8 ± 4.2 days. Overall mean length of stay was 10.2 ± 11.7 days. Seven patients (4.5% of admissions) died during hospitalization, all with severe DKA and multiple comorbidities.

Economic Burden

The mean total cost per DKA admission was 12,274 ± 10,213 USD (45,461 ± 37,826 AED), with a median of 9156 USD (33,902 AED). Daily hospitalization cost averaged 1203 USD (4457 AED). ICU-requiring admissions cost significantly more than ward-only care: 16,006 ± 10,847 USD (59,283 ± 40,171 AED) versus 6125 ± 3892 USD (22,685 ± 14,413 AED), p < 0.001. The cumulative 5-year cost for all DKA admissions was 1,927,018 USD (7,137,219 AED).

Infections were the most common precipitating factor (77 admissions, 49.0%), followed by insulin non-compliance unrelated to cost (21 admissions, 13.4%), insulin unaffordability (21 admissions, 13.4%), new diabetes diagnosis (15 admissions, 9.6%), pump failure (6 admissions, 3.8%), and other/undetermined causes (17 admissions, 10.8%). Insurance Status and Outcomes

Comprehensive insurance coverage (government or enhanced private) was present in 101 admissions (64.3%), while 56 admissions (35.7%) involved underinsured patients. Underinsured patients had significantly higher rates of ICU admission (76.8% vs 52.5%, p < 0.01), longer ICU stays (5.2 ± 5.1 vs 3.1 ± 3.4 days, p = 0.02), and showed a trend toward higher mean costs (14,269 USD [52,847 AED] vs 11,133 USD [41,235 AED], p = 0.07). Notably, 75% (15/20) of insulin unaffordability cases occurred in underinsured patients. Recurrence Patterns

Analysis of recurrence patterns revealed significant disparities based on precipitating factors and insurance status. During the 5-year study period, 23 of the 134 patients (17.2%) had multiple DKA admissions, accounting for 46 of the 157 total admissions. Among these 23 patients with recurrent DKA, 15 individuals (11.2% of all patients) had three or more episodes during the study period. These 15 high-utilizers with three or more admissions were predominantly young adults (mean age 32.4 ± 14.7 years) with T1DM (80%), limited insurance coverage (73.3%), and documented psychosocial challenges including depression (46.7%), substance use (20%), or unstable housing (26.7%).

Recurrence rates within 1 year varied dramatically by precipitating factor: among the 18 patients whose initial DKA was precipitated by insulin unaffordability, 9 (50%) were readmitted within 1 year, compared to 40% for other non-financial non-compliance and only 22% for infection-triggered DKA (p < 0.01). Insurance status was also strongly associated with recurrence: underinsured patients had a 43.8% readmission rate compared to 20.9% for adequately insured patients (p < 0.01).

Discussion

This comprehensive analysis of DKA admissions at a UAE tertiary care center reveals a substantial clinical and economic burden, with an average cost of 12,274 USD (45,461 AED) per admission and a mean length of stay of 10.2 days. The leading precipitating factors—infections (49.0%) and insulin non-compliance (26.8%)—accounted for approximately three-quarters of all admissions, mirroring regional patterns [9]. Most critically, 13.4% of admissions were directly attributable to insulin unaffordability, highlighting systemic failures in providing essential medication to patients with T1DM. This finding aligns with international data where cost-related insulin rationing affects nearly one-quarter of insulin users and is increasingly recognized as a major driver of acute complications [21]. With UAE having one of the highest rates of T2DM in the world, this raises significant concerns regarding the future cost of DKA admission in patients with T2DM.

The 21 admissions due to insulin unaffordability required ICU care in 71.4% of cases, contributing to costs averaging 12,274 USD per admission. Our overall ICU utilization rate of 61.1% exceeds international reports, likely reflecting delayed presentations among underinsured patients [30]. However, approximately 15% of moderate DKA admissions could potentially be managed on general wards with appropriate protocols [31].

The recurrence pattern in our cohort reveals important insights for targeted intervention. Patients with insulin unaffordability-triggered DKA had a 50% 1-year readmission rate, more than double that of infection-triggered cases (22%). This suggests that addressing the root cause of insulin access could have multiplicative benefits beyond preventing the index admission. The subset of high-utilizers (11.2% of patients accounting for 29.3% of admissions) represents a particularly important target population. These individuals, characterized by young age, T1DM, inadequate insurance, and psychosocial challenges, would benefit from intensive case management combining medication assistance, mental health support, and social services [32].

The striking economic paradox revealed by our analysis, that one DKA admission costs equivalent to 27 years of insulin therapy, underscores the fiscal irrationality of restricted insulin access. The 257,765 USD (954,686 AED) spent on preventable DKA admissions over 5 years could have provided insulin for 565 patient-years, but it excludes indirect costs such as lost productivity, long-term complications from recurrent DKA, and the psychological burden on patients and families [33]. Research literature suggests that ensuring medication access improves adherence to other aspects of diabetes care, potentially yielding additional benefits [34].

International evidence supports addressing insulin affordability as a key DKA prevention strategy. Colorado’s 2020 insulin copayment cap law, limiting out-of-pocket costs to US $100 per month, was associated with a 30% reduction in DKA admissions among affected populations within the first year [35]. Similarly, Australia’s National Diabetes Services Scheme, providing subsidized insulin and supplies, maintains DKA admission rates at approximately one-third of US levels despite similar diabetes prevalence [36]. Our underinsured patients showed comparable patterns, with higher ICU utilization (76.8% vs 52.5%) and costs, suggesting delayed presentation due to cost concerns, limited preventive care access, and inability to afford monitoring supplies [37]. These findings collectively indicate that while insulin provision is critical, comprehensive interventions would likely yield greater population-level impact.

While insulin unaffordability accounted for 13.4% of admissions (costing US $257,765 over 5 years), infections represented the predominant precipitating factor at 49.0% (costing US $961,345). This distribution highlights the need for comprehensive prevention strategies rather than single-intervention approaches. Our cost analysis suggests that improving insulin access could be economically favorable, as preventing one DKA admission would offset 27 years of insulin costs. However, formal cost-effectiveness modeling incorporating population-level data, implementation costs, and indirect benefits is needed to definitively establish the economic case for universal insulin coverage.

The disparities observed between insured and underinsured patients—with underinsured patients showing higher ICU utilization (76.8% vs 52.5%) and readmission rates (43.8% vs 20.9%)—indicate systemic barriers extending beyond medication access. These findings suggest that optimal DKA prevention requires multilevel interventions: enhanced infection prevention education and sick-day management protocols (potentially preventing 49% of admissions), improved insulin access for underinsured patients (preventing 13.4%), and structured diabetes education addressing medication adherence (preventing another 13.4%).

Implications for Health Policy

Our findings support three key policy recommendations: (1) universal insulin access for patients with T1DM would be cost-effective, preventing one DKA admission per 27 patient-years of insulin provided; (2) intensive case management for high-utilizers could reduce readmissions by 40–60%; and (3) standardized DKA protocols could reduce ICU utilization by 15–20% without compromising outcomes [38–40].

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our results. First, the retrospective design relies on documentation quality, and insulin unaffordability may be underreported as a result of patient reluctance to disclose financial hardship or inadequate provider inquiry. Second, our single-center study in a government tertiary hospital may not fully represent the UAE’s diverse healthcare landscape, particularly private sector facilities that may serve different patient populations. Third, we captured only direct medical costs; inclusion of indirect costs would substantially increase the economic burden estimates. Fourth, the study period included the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have influenced both DKA incidence and healthcare utilization patterns, though our year-by-year analysis showed consistent trends. Finally, we could not assess the impact of newer medications such as SGLT2 inhibitors on DKA risk in our T2DM population because of incomplete medication records.

Conclusions

This study provides important evidence regarding the clinical and economic burden of DKA in the UAE healthcare system. Our analysis of 157 consecutive DKA admissions revealed a mean hospitalization cost of 12,274 USD (45,461 AED) per admission, with a complex array of precipitating factors requiring targeted interventions. While insulin unaffordability accounts for 13.4% of admissions, infections (49.0%) represent the largest preventable category. A comprehensive DKA prevention program should prioritize (1) patient education around infection prevention education and management of sick-day rules, (2) ensuring insulin access for underinsured patients, and (3) structured diabetes education addressing all forms of non-adherence.

Our findings have implications for healthcare systems with multitiered insurance structures. The UAE experience demonstrates how coverage disparities translate to differential clinical outcomes and healthcare utilization patterns. However, determining optimal resource allocation requires rigorous economic evaluation comparing the cost-effectiveness of various intervention strategies, from universal insulin coverage to enhanced diabetes education programs to technology-enabled monitoring systems.

Future research should employ formal cost-effectiveness analyses to evaluate different prevention strategies, including their implementation feasibility, population reach, and sustainability. Prospective studies should capture both direct and indirect costs, quality-adjusted life years, and the broader societal impact of preventable DKA. Additionally, implementation science approaches could identify optimal delivery mechanisms for interventions in diverse healthcare contexts.

In conclusion, while our analysis suggests economic favorability of improving insulin access, comprehensive DKA prevention must address all major precipitating factors, with particular emphasis on infection management and structured diabetes education. Formal cost-effectiveness modeling is needed to guide optimal resource allocation. The substantial burden of DKA—both preventable and non-preventable—underscores the need for evidence-based policies that balance clinical effectiveness, economic efficiency, and health equity in diabetes care delivery.
