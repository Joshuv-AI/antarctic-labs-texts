Switchable synthesis of aldehydes and alcohols by hydroformylation with ligand-modified Rh single-atom catalyst
Authors: Bowen Qiu, Shujuan Liu, Kang Zhao, Bin Wang, Weimin Ye, Dongcheng He, Hongyan Ni, Xiaoshun Huang, Xinyu Yang, Xinjiang Cui, Feng Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractControlling product selectivity in catalysis is fundamental for green synthesis, yet challenging, especially aiming for switchable outcomes between different products within a single catalytic system. This is exemplified in the industrially crucial hydroformylation reaction, where achieving controlled switching between aldehydes and alcohols remains difficult due to the inability of uniform active site to independently govern the hydrogenation and desorption steps of the aldehyde intermediate. Herein, we report CeO2-supported single-atom Rh catalyst, in which triphenylphosphine (PPh3) serves as molecular switch to precisely modulate the chemoselectivity between alcohols and aldehydes in alkene hydroformylation with broad substrate compatibility and excellent stability. The Rh/CeO2 exhibits up to 99% chemoselectivity and 93% yield toward alcohols, while the PPh3-Rh/CeO2 shifts the chemoselectivity to aldehydes with up to 99% with 91% yield. Characterizations and theoretical calculations reveal that the coordination of PPh3 modulates the electronic structure of Rh, increasing the electron density at Rh centers. This electronic modification alters the hydrogenation pathway from oxygen-then-carbon to carbon-then-oxygen, raising the Gibbs free energy barrier for the rate-determining step of aldehyde hydrogenation by 0.26 eV, thereby switching the chemoselectivity to aldehyde. This work establishes ligand-modified single-atom catalysts as a versatile platform for controlling reaction chemoselectivity.Subject terms: Heterogeneous catalysis, Catalyst synthesis, Catalytic mechanisms

Introduction

Selectivity control over products in catalytic reactions constitutes a fundamental scientific challenge for achieving atom-economical green synthesis and enhancing process efficiency1–3. To achieve this goal, developing catalytic systems capable of effectively steering reaction pathways is essential, which critically relies on the precise design of the active sites and their local environment. Current strategies for selectivity control mainly include pore structure engineering, support surface modification, and microenvironment regulation of active sites4,5. Pore engineering6, which is widely applied in zeolites7–9 and metal-organic frameworks10–13, relies on spatial confinement effects but often confers selectivity only toward specific molecular dimensions, thus limiting substrate generality. Surface modification via the introduction of acid-base sites14–17, amphiphilic structures18–20, or chiral groups21–23 offers a viable approach for selectivity tuning. However, this strategy is constrained by difficulties in achieving uniform functionalization, potential degradation of the catalyst’s structural integrity, and complex synthesis procedures. Microenvironment regulation through bimetallic design24–26 allows adjustment of the interaction between the active site and reactants. Yet, bimetallic systems often suffer from ambiguous synergistic mechanisms and insufficient thermal stability, leading to metal migration or aggregation under operating conditions. Besides, surface ligand modification strategies27–29, which tune the electronic structure and coordination environment of active centers, enable the manipulation of key energy barriers, offering a promising route for switchable product selectivity.

Hydroformylation is an efficient and atom-economic transformation of alkenes into high-value aldehydes and alcohols, which are pivotal in the manufacturing of detergents, plasticizers, and fine chemicals and boast an annual production capacity exceeding 12 million tons30–32. Nevertheless, the chemoselectivity tuning of alcohols and aldehydes in hydroformylation remains a persistent scientific hurdle. The available catalytic systems, such as RhCo/ZSM-533, MgRh/Mg34, and Rh@Zn-S-135, primarily favor aldehyde formation, whereas RhCo/Al2O336, Rh-Au/CeO237, and Rh-Ru/Shvo’s complex38 tend to produce alcohols. Although significant progress has been made in targeting a single product, achieving a controllable switch between alcohols and aldehydes remains a significant challenge. The core of this limitation lies in the inability to accurately regulate both the hydrogenation activity and desorption barrier of the aldehyde intermediate via a uniform set of active sites.

The coordination of ligands to Rh single-atom catalysts provides molecular-level control over the active site’s electronic and spatial structure, which allows for the directional tuning of energy barriers in aldehyde hydrogenation/desorption, thereby enabling precise switching between alcohols and aldehydes in hydroformylation. Herein, we demonstrate that the single Rh atom supported on CeO2 can be precisely engineered via the introduction of a molecular switch to modulate chemoselectivity between alcohols and aldehydes in alkene hydroformylation. When triphenylphosphine (PPh3) is employed as a molecular switch, the resulting catalytic system exhibits a broad substrate scope and excellent recyclability. Specifically, the unmodified Rh/CeO2 exhibits up to 99% chemoselectivity and 93% yield toward alcohols, while the PPh3-Rh/CeO2 shifts the chemoselectivity to aldehydes with up to 99% with 91% yield. Combined spectroscopic characterizations and theoretical calculations reveal that PPh3 coordination enriches the electron density at Rh active sites. This electronic modification alters the aldehyde hydrogenation route, shifting the pathway from oxygen-then-carbon to carbon-then-oxygen, and facilitates aldehyde desorption rather than aldehyde hydrogenation, thereby favoring the production of aldehyde.

Results and discussion

Catalytic performance investigation

Styrene was employed as a model substrate to investigate the controllable chemoselectivity towards aldehyde or alcohol in the hydroformylation reaction (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 1). The styrene conversion of 96% and 3-phenylpropanol chemoselectivity of 93% were attained when Rh/CeO2 was used as the catalyst, indicating that the catalyst facilitated further hydrogenation of the intermediate phenylpropanal.

a Reaction conditions: Styrene (2 mmol), CO/H2 = 3/3 MPa, in 2 mL of toluene at 170 °C for 12 h. Styrene conversion and product chemoselectivity were determined by GC analysis with decane as the internal standard. b Dependence of aldehyde yield on ligand steric (cone angle) and electronic (Tolman electronic parameter, TEP) parameters for a Rh catalyst.

To modulate the chemoselectivity between aldehyde and alcohol, the influence of ligands on the electronic distribution and spatial configuration of Rh sites was investigated (Fig. 1a). Initial screening revealed that functionalizing Rh/CeO2 with cyclohexyldiphenylphosphane (PPh2Cy-Rh/CeO2) reversed the chemoselectivity, increasing aldehyde selectivity to 27%, thereby confirming the potential of ligand modification in regulating product distribution. Moreover, a systematic evaluation of triphenylphosphine derivatives functionalized with different substituents (P(o-tol)3, P(o-MeOPh)3, P(4-FPh)3 and PPh3) indicated that PPh3-Rh/CeO2 achieved 93% aldehyde selectivity at 98% styrene conversion, significantly outperforming other triphenylphosphine analogs bearing either electron-donating or electron-withdrawing groups. In pursuit of higher phenylpropanal selectivity, benzyl-substituted phosphine ligand PPh2Bn and aliphatic phosphine ligand P(oct)3 were further examined. The correlation of catalytic performance with the steric and electronic properties of the ligands was studied (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Table 2). Under conditions of similar ligand cone angles, the aldehyde yield exhibited a volcano-type trend with increasing TEP value, indicating an optimal electronic effect range. In contrast, when the electronic properties (similar TEP values) were fixed, varying the ligand cone angle did not significantly affect the yield. These comparative results demonstrated that, although both factors cooperatively modulated the catalytic activity, the electronic effect served as the primary factor governing the activity of the aldehyde hydrogenation reaction. Consequently, the PPh3 ligand, with its moderate electron-donating ability (TEP ≈ 2068.9 cm−1), constructed the most suitable Rh active center, thereby achieving the highest aldehyde yield. These results clearly demonstrated that the Rh/CeO2 catalyst enabled chemoselective hydroformylation of styrene toward 3-phenylpropanol, whereas the PPh3-Rh/CeO2 system efficiently produced phenylpropanal, achieving precise and switchable synthesis of alcohol and aldehyde. Furthermore, our ligand-modification strategy enables complete chemoselectivity switching within a single catalyst, bypassing the need to alter its fundamental composition or metal identity (Supplementary Table 3). Furthermore, other reaction parameters were screened to obtain the optimal conditions, as shown in Supplementary Figs. 1–7.

In order to investigate the correlation between reaction performance and reaction time, a kinetic study of styrene hydroformylation was performed. As shown in Fig. 2a, c, the yields of 3-phenylpropanol and 3-phenylpropanal catalyzed by Rh/CeO2 and PPh3-Rh/CeO2 gradually increased with reaction time, reaching maximum values (89% and 91% yield, respectively) after 24 h. Hot filtration tests showed that the yields of the alcohols and aldehydes were 16% and 19%, respectively, after 1 h of the reaction. After catalyst removal, the filtrate continued to react for another 23 h, and the yields remained constant throughout this period, confirming the heterogeneous nature of the hydroformylation. ICP-AES analysis for fresh and spent catalysts (Supplementary Table 6) showed no detectable Rh and P leaching (below test limit), demonstrating the outstanding stability of the catalysts. This stability of PPh3-Rh/CeO2 was further supported by 31P NMR analysis (Supplementary Fig. 23) of the solution after reaction, where no free phosphorus species were observed, indicating the integrity of the Rh-P framework under reaction conditions. Subsequently, the recyclability of Rh/CeO2 and PPh3-Rh/CeO2 catalysts was evaluated (Fig. 2b, d). Both catalysts were successfully reused for 6 consecutive cycles without significant loss of catalytic activity.

Catalyst characterizations

TEM imaging (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Figs. 8, 9) confirmed the successful synthesis of CeO2 nanoparticles across all catalysts (CeO2, Rh/CeO2, and PPh3-Rh/CeO2), and no visible Rh nanoparticles were detected, consistent with the XRD patterns (Supplementary Fig. 10) which exhibited only the characteristic diffractions of the CeO2 phase (PDF#43-1002) with no detectable signals from Rh species. Meanwhile, the highly dispersed Rh was affirmed by EDS elemental mapping (Fig. 3b), which revealed a uniform spatial distribution of Rh species that fully overlapped with the CeO2 support. The local structure of Rh/CeO2 was characterized using aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-HAADF-STEM) (Fig. 3c). Clear lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.312 nm were identified, attributable to the (111) plane of CeO2. Since the atomic number of Rh was lower than that of Ce, the intensity of atomic columns containing Rh single atoms was lower than that of pure Ce columns when viewed along the zone axis. Intensity profile analysis (bottom-right inset of Fig. 3c) revealed that the low-intensity regions matched isolated Rh single atoms on the CeO2 surface39. Furthermore, the AC-HAADF-STEM images of PPh3-Rh/CeO2 were virtually identical to those of Rh/CeO2, indicating that the introduction of PPh3 caused no significant alteration to the atomic dispersion of Rh and the crystalline structure of the CeO2 support.

Structural characterization.

a TEM image of Rh/CeO2. b EDS elemental mapping of Rh/CeO2. c AC-HAADF-STEM image of Rh/CeO2. d CO-DRIFTS spectra. e FTIR spectra, and f 31P MAS NMR spectrum of the PPh3-Rh/CeO2.

The coordination state of Rh species was investigated by in situ CO diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (CO-DRIFTS) (Fig. 3d)40,41. No absorption peaks were detected for pure CeO2 within the observed spectral range. In contrast, both Rh/CeO2 and PPh3-Rh/CeO2 exhibited a pair of characteristic peaks at ∼2080 cm−1 and ∼2020 cm−1, assigned to the symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of Rh(CO)2 species, respectively, indicating that CO adsorption and activation occurred specifically at Rh sites rather than on the CeO2 support. Moreover, the absence of a bridged CO adsorption peak at ∼1910 cm−1 further confirmed the atomic dispersion of Rh in both catalysts. Notably, compared to Rh/CeO2, the Rh(CO)2 stretching vibrations in PPh3-Rh/CeO2 showed a red-shift. This phenomenon was explained by the donation of electrons from the P atom in PPh3 to the Rh center, which increased the electron density of Rh and enhanced its ability to back-donate electrons to the π* antibonding orbitals of CO molecules. This resulted in a weakened CO bond and a corresponding decrease in vibrational frequency, providing strong evidence for the coordination between PPh3 and Rh single atoms in PPh3-Rh/CeO2.

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy (Fig. 3e) of PPh3-Rh/CeO2 spectra exhibited distinctive vibrational peaks of PPh3 while retaining the characteristic absorption peaks of Rh/CeO2, indicating the successful incorporation of PPh3. Consistent with this conclusion, the PPh3 modification induced a series of physicochemical changes, including reduced specific surface area, altered hysteresis loop type, attenuated Lewis acidity and enhanced basicity, as determined by comparative analysis of CeO2, Rh/CeO2 and PPh3-Rh/CeO2 (Supplementary Figs. 11–14, Supplementary Table 2, see Supplementary Information for details). Meanwhile, the 31P MAS NMR spectra42,43 of PPh3-Rh/CeO2 (Fig. 3f) displayed characteristic peaks at approximately 40 ppm and −5 ppm, which were assigned to PPh3 coordinated to Rh sites and physically adsorbed PPh3, respectively.

XPS analysis (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. 15) revealed that the Rh 3d3/2 binding energy in PPh3-Rh/CeO2 decreased to 309.1 eV compared with that in Rh/CeO2 (309.3 eV), confirming electron transfer from phosphorus to rhodium and further demonstrating the coordination interaction between PPh3 and Rh44. Moreover, projected density of states (PDOS) analysis (Fig. 4b) of Rh atoms revealed that the introduction of PPh3 shifted the Rh electron density entirely below the Fermi level, providing direct electronic evidence for electron donation from PPh3 to Rh. These results were consistent with Bader charge analysis, which also indicated an electron-enriched character of the Rh sites in PPh3-Rh/CeO2 compared to Rh/CeO2 (Supplementary Fig. 16).

The Rh K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra (Fig. 4c)45 demonstrated that the valence state of Rh in both Rh/CeO2 and PPh3-Rh/CeO2 was between 0 and +3 compared with Rh foil and Rh2O3 references. The Rh absorption edge of PPh3-Rh/CeO2 shifted by approximately 0.9 eV toward lower energy than Rh/CeO2, indicating a reduced valence state and increased electron density of Rh atoms, which was attributed to the coordination between PPh3 and Rh sites. The extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra (Fig. 4d)46 showed that Rh/CeO2 exhibited a Rh-O coordination shell at approximately 1.5 Å, while PPh3-Rh/CeO2 displayed a characteristic peak at approximately 1.7 Å assigned to the Rh-P coordination shell. No signals corresponding to the Rh-Rh scattering path near 2.5 Å were observed in Rh/CeO2 and PPh3-Rh/CeO2, confirming the atomic dispersion of Rh in both catalysts. Wavelet transform (WT) analysis further verified the formation of the Rh-P coordination shell (Fig. 4e), showing that the maximum intensity shifted from k = 6.9 Å−1 for Rh-O coordination to k = 7.1 Å−1 for Rh-P coordination, primarily resulting from the difference in atomic numbers between O and P43. EXAFS fitting analysis of the first coordination shell (Supplementary Fig. 17, Supplementary Table 5) revealed that the Rh-O coordination numbers in Rh/CeO2 and PPh3-Rh/CeO2 were 2.4 and 2.3, respectively. Additionally, the Rh-P coordination shell with an average bond length of 2.26 Å was formed in PPh3-Rh/CeO2, with a corresponding coordination number of 1.2. The fitting evidenced that Rh single atoms were loading in CeO2 supports rather than embedded into the lattice (Supplementary Fig. 18), and Rh was coordinated with P species in PPh3-Rh/CeO2. Based on the EXAFS results, atomic models of Rh/CeO2 and PPh3-Rh/CeO2 were built.

Characterizations of the spent Rh/CeO2 and PPh3-Rh/CeO2 were compared with their fresh counterparts and showed that the catalyst particles exhibited no clear change (Supplementary Figs. 19–23, Supplementary Table 6) and the Rh active sites not only maintained their single-atom dispersion but also preserved their original valence state and coordination environment. Notably, the characteristic Rh-P coordination structure of PPh3-Rh/CeO2 was retained. Furthermore, no significant leaching of the active Rh metal was detected. These findings are collectively attributed to the excellent stability of both catalysts under the reaction conditions.

Mechanism study

To gain insight into the regulatory mechanism of PPh3 on alcohol selectivity in the styrene hydroformylation process over Rh/CeO2 and PPh3-Rh/CeO2, DFT calculations were performed to investigate the reaction pathways of phenylpropanal hydrogenation to phenylpropanol on these two catalysts, based on the experimental evidence that phenylpropanal served as the key intermediate for phenylpropanol synthesis. The hydrogenation of phenylpropanal proceeds through three elementary steps. First, phenylpropanal (RCHO, R = C6H5(CH2)2) is adsorbed on the metal site through its carbonyl group (C = O), adopting either an η-O or η-(C, O) coordination mode to form adsorbed RCHO. Second, H₂ is adsorbed on the surface and dissociates with the assistance of the metal site and adjacent surface oxygen species (Osurf), leading to the formation of metal hydride (M–H) and surface hydroxyl (OsurfH) species47,48. Third, the aldehyde group is hydrogenated to produce the corresponding alcohol (RCH2OH). This C = O hydrogenation may follow two possible pathways, namely the C-then-O pathway and the O-then-C pathway, depending on the hydrogenation sequence of the carbon and oxygen atoms in the carbonyl group. In the C-then-O pathway, M-H first transfers a nucleophilic hydride to the electrophilic carbonyl carbon of RCHO, generating an alkoxide intermediate (RCH2O). Subsequent proton transfer from OsurfH to the terminal oxygen then affords adsorbed RCH2OH. In contrast, in the O-then-C pathway, protonation of the carbonyl oxygen first produces a hydroxyl intermediate (RCHOH), which is subsequently hydrogenated at the carbon center to yield the final adsorbed product, RCH2OH.

In the presence of Rh/CeO2 (Fig. 5a, structural detail in Figs. S24–S28), RCHO was adsorbed in η-(C, O) mode and exergonic by 0.39 eV (IM-A0 → IM-A1). Subsequently, two molecular adsorbed structures and four dissociative adsorption states for H2 were explored. In the molecular state, H2 preferred to bind with the Rh atom and its Gibbs free energy for adsorption was −0.03 eV (IM-A2). Due to its coordinating to the Rh site via sigma bond, the H–H bond length elongated from 0.75 Å in the gaseous state to 0.87 Å in adsorbed state (IM-A2), which was highly activated. Therefore, the Gibbs free energy barrier for H2 dissociation was only 0.28 eV and led to the formation of Rh-H and Osurf-H (IM-A3). In IM-A3, the distance between the hydride and carbon in the CHO group was 2.59 Å, shorter than the distance (2.97 Å) between the proton on Osurf and oxygen in the CHO group. Considering the Bader charge of hydride (−0.08) and carbon in the CHO group (0.59), hydride should attack carbon terminal first to form RCH2O* and triggered the C-then-O pathway with a Gibbs energy barrier of 0.31 eV (endergonic by 0.27 eV) (IM-A3 → TS-A2’ → IM-A4’). However, RCH2O* hydrogenation occupied a much higher Gibbs free energy barrier (1.39 eV, IM-A4’ → TS-A3’ → IM-A5), and the Gibbs free energy barrier for RCH2O* dehydrogenation back to RCHO and H was only 0.04 eV (IM-A4’ → TS-A2’ → IM-A3), such larger difference (1.35 eV) indicated that the species RCH2O* was unstable and turned back to RCHO to find another reaction pathway, such as hydrogenation at oxygen terminal (O-then-C). For the O-then-C hydrogenation route of RCHO, the proton from OsurfH was transferred to the oxygen atom of RCHO* to generate RCHOH*, the Gibbs free energy barrier was 0.93 eV and the reaction Gibbs free energy was 0.16 eV (IM-A3 → TS-A2 → IM-A4). Then, Rh-H attacked the carbon of RCHOH*, forming RCH2OH* with a Gibbs free energy barrier of 0.96 eV (IM-A4 → TS-A3 → IM-A5). Compared the Gibbs free energy barrier for RCHOH dehydrogenation to RCHO (0.77 eV, IM-A4 → TS-A2 → IM-A3), the difference was 0.19 eV, which means that the intermediate RCHOH is stable and can be transformed into RCH2OH. Though the Gibbs free energy barrier for RCH2OH dehydrogenation was low (0.03 eV), it could release from the surface and thereby vacate the Rh-Osurf active site for the next reaction step; the Gibbs free energy for RCH2OH desorption was -0.41 eV (IM-A5 → IM-A0). In addition, the Rh-H could migrate from Rh to the surface oxygen site, and proton hopping between neighbor two oxygen sites over the CeO2 surface was examined (Supplementary Fig. 29). These surface sites just provided a reservoir for hydrogen, and the structures with two surface hydroxyl would move back to RhH+OsurfH (IM-A3) for the following hydrogenation reactions.

In the case of PPh3-Rh/CeO2 (Fig. 5b, structural detail in Figs. S24, S30–S32), RCHO adsorbs in two adsorption modes where the -HC = O have opposite orientation, and the repulsion between the phenyl ring in PPh3 and the CHO group led the adsorption Gibbs free energies to be −0.20 and −0.56 eV, respectively (IM-B0 → IM-B1 and IM-B0 → IM-B1’). Then, the H2 molecule was physically adsorbed on the surface without bonding to the Rh atom due to its positive Gibbs free energies of adsorption, 0.42 eV from IM-B1 to IM-B2 and 0.31 eV from IM-B1 to IM-B2’. In IM-B2, H2 was located at the region between surface oxygen and C terminal of RCHO, such adsorption state resulted in the formed Rh-H was close to carbonyl carbon after H2 dissociation, the related free energy barrier was 0.78 eV (IM-B2 → TS-B1 → IM-B3). Therefore, this structure initiated the C-then-O pathway, and RCHO* was converted into RCH2OH* via RCH2O* intermediate (IM-B3 → TS-B2 → IM-B4 → TS-B3 → IM-B5). The Gibbs free energy barriers for these two steps were 0.43 and 1.22 eV. As for IM-B2’, H2 was adsorbed at the region between the surface oxygen and oxygen terminal of RCHO. With the decomposition of H2, there still formed Rh-H and was close to carbonyl oxygen, the related Gibbs free energy barrier was 1.28 eV (IM-B2’ → TS-B1’ → IM-B3’). Next, this IM-B3’ structure started the O-then-C pathway, and RCHO* was converted into RCH2OH* via RCHOH* intermediate (IM-B3’ → TS-B2’ → IM-B4’ → TS-B3’ → IM-B5’). The Gibbs free energy barriers for these two steps were 0.38 and 1.63 eV. According to the overall potential energy surfaces in Fig. 5b, RCH2O and RCHOH hydrogenation were the rate-determining steps in C-then-O and O-then-C pathways, but the Gibbs free energy barrier for RCH2O hydrogenation was 0.41 eV lower than that for RCHOH hydrogenation, indicating that the C-then-O pathway was more favored on PPh3 decorated Rh-CeO2.

In order to elucidate the PPh3 effect on the selectivity of RCHO and RCH2OH over the Rh/CeO2 catalyst, the O-then-C pathway on Rh/CeO2 and the C-then-O pathway on PPh3-Rh/CeO2 were enclosed in Fig. 5c for detailed comparison. Due to the existence of PPh3, there were several differences. First, the adsorption Gibbs free energy for RCHO was reduced from -0.39 eV on Rh/CeO2 to -0.20 eV on PPh3-Rh/CeO2, which was caused by the repulsion between PPh3 and RCHO. The weakened interaction between the Rh site and RCHO could further facilitate the release of RCHO from the catalyst. Second, H2 molecules were chemisorbed on Rh sites with a low dissociation energy barrier of 0.28 eV on Rh/CeO2, whereas they were physically adsorbed on the catalyst surface with a high dissociation energy barrier of 0.78 eV. Furthermore, the stronger bonding interaction of Rh-H on Rh/CeO2 was confirmed by its greater IpCOHP value (-1.53 vs. -1.44 eV, Supplementary Fig. 33). The weaker adsorption energy and bond strength, higher dissociation barrier, suggested a lower possibility for H2 approaching the PPh3-Rh/CeO2 catalyst than Rh/CeO2. Last, the rate-determining step exhibited a significantly higher RCHO hydrogenation energy barrier (1.22 eV) on PPh3-Rh/CeO2 than on Rh/CeO2 (0.96 eV), such the 0.26 eV difference led to a reaction rate constant on Rh/CeO2 being 880 times larger than that on PPh3-Rh/CeO2, suggesting less favorable hydrogenation over PPh3-Rh/CeO2 kinetically. These differences resulted in the switch of the chemoselectivity, whereas the more favorable hydrogenation on Rh/CeO2 facilitated the selective production of alcohols.

The proposed mechanism was further validated by in-situ DRIFTS experiments. During hydrogenation over Rh/CeO2, a species characterized by a C-C stretching vibration at 1137 cm-1 was observed; its intensity initially rose and subsequently declined, confirming its role as an intermediate. In parallel, a peak at 1076 cm-1 showed a continuous increase in intensity over time, corresponding to the alcohol product. For comparison, Supplementary Table 7 reported the calculated frequencies of phenylpropanal hydrogenation. Direct comparison between these experimental frequencies and DFT‑calculated vibrational modes provides strong validation for the oxygen-then-carbon hydrogenation pathway on Rh/CeO2. The intermediate RCHOH featured a characteristic C-C stretching vibration at 1136 cm-1 (CH2-CH), whereas the RCH2OH product exhibited a corresponding mode at 1084 cm-1 (CH2-CH2). The excellent agreement provided strong support for the mechanism of oxygen-then-carbon hydrogenation pathway on Rh/CeO2.

Synthetic scope and generality

The substrate scope and limitation of the hydroformylation reaction over Rh/CeO2 and PPh3-Rh/CeO2 were evaluated under the optimized reaction conditions. Employing the same alkenes as starting materials, switchable chemoselectivity between alcohols and aldehydes was achieved up to >99%, with the branched products being predominantly obtained (Fig. 6, Supplementary Fig. 35). The catalytic system demonstrated broad applicability toward various aliphatic alkenes, affording the corresponding alcohols and aldehydes in yields of 83%-93% (1a, 2a) and 78%-86% (1b, 2b), respectively. Internal aliphatic alkenes were also efficiently converted, with yields up to 85-96% (3a, 4a) and 80-95% (3b, 4b). Aliphatic cyclic alkenes were also viable substrates, produced in 90%-94% (5a–8a) and 71%-92% yield (5b-8b), each exhibiting >80% chemoselectivity.

Furthermore, the reaction performance of styrene derivatives bearing electron-donating or electron-withdrawing functional groups at various positions on the phenyl ring was investigated, successfully delivering the corresponding alcohols (9a-16a) and aldehydes (9b-16b) in excellent yields (79%-90% and 75%-91%, respectively) with up to >99% chemoselectivity. Aryl alkene with conjugated-fused ring structure (1-vinylnaphthalene) was also a competent substrate, giving the alcohol in 91% yield (17a) and aldehyde in 92% yield (17b). In addition, the hydroformylation of 1-allylnaphthalene also proceeded effectively, affording alcohol in 92% yield (18a) and aldehyde in 90% yield (18b).

In conclusion, we have demonstrated that single Rh atom catalysts can be engineered to achieve switchable chemoselectivity between aldehydes and alcohols in hydroformylation. Modifying the Rh/CeO2 catalyst with PPh3 effectively reverses the product chemoselectivity from alcohols (up to 93%) to aldehydes (up to 91%). Combined spectroscopic and theoretical studies reveal that PPh3 coordination enriches the electron density of Rh sites, which alters the aldehyde hydrogenation pathway from oxygen-then-carbon on Rh/CeO2 to carbon-then-oxygen on PPh3-Rh/CeO2, which raises the Gibbs free energy barrier of the rate-determining hydrogen transfer step by 0.26 eV and concurrently facilitates aldehyde desorption. The combined effects suppress the hydrogenation of aldehydes on PPh3-Rh/CeO2, thereby shifting the product profile from alcohols to aldehydes. The catalyst system exhibits broad substrate compatibility and excellent stability, establishing ligand-modified single-atom catalysts as a versatile platform for precise chemoselectivity control.

Methods

Preparation of CeO2

The CeO2 carrier was prepared via a chemical precipitation method. Typically, Ce(NO3)2·6H2O (4.34 g) was dissolved in deionized water (100 mL) with stirring. The pH of the solution was adjusted to 8 by dropwise addition of aqueous ammonia (3 mL). The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 4 h. The resulting precipitate was collected by filtration, washed thoroughly with deionized water until the filtrate reached a neutral pH (~7), and dried at 100 °C for 12 h. Finally, the solid was calcined at 550 °C for 4 h with a heating rate of 5 °C/min, affording a yellow CeO2 powder.

Preparation of Rh/CeO2

The Rh/CeO2 catalyst was prepared via an impregnation method. Typically, CeO2 (1.00 g) was impregnated with an aqueous solution of RhCl3 (0.013 g in 10 mL deionized water). The mixture was stirred and evaporated to dryness at 80 °C. The resulting solid was then calcined at 550 °C for 4 h (heating rate: 5 °C/min) to obtain the final Rh/ CeO2 catalyst.

Preparation of PPh3-Rh/CeO2

A mixture of Rh/CeO2 (100 mg), PPh3, and toluene (6 mL) was charged into a 25 mL pressure tube and purged with N2 for 5 min. The mixture was heated at 120 °C for 12 h under static N2 atmosphere with stirring at 300 rpm. After cooling, the solvent was evaporated, followed by vacuum drying at 80 °C for 6 h. All other ligand-modified catalysts were synthesized using the same protocol.

Typical procedure for hydroformylation

A typical catalytic test was conducted in a 100 mL stainless-steel autoclave. A mixture of catalyst, styrene (0.208 g, 2.0 mmol), toluene (2.0 mL), and decane (internal standard) was added to the reactor equipped with a magnetic stir bar. The autoclave was sealed, purged three times with CO, and then pressurized to 6.0 MPa with syngas (CO/H2 = 1:1). The reaction was stirred at 170 °C for 12 h. After cooling to room temperature and slowly releasing the pressure, the catalyst was separated by centrifugation. The liquid products were analyzed by gas chromatography (Agilent 7890 A GC equipped with an HP-5 column and an FID detector) to determine conversion and chemoselectivity. For substrate scope investigation, the yields of products (1a-8a, 1b-8b) were established via GC calibration curves using authentic standards, while the aromatic products (9a-18a, 9b-18b) were quantified by isolation followed by subsequent quantitative 1H NMR analysis. In recycling tests, the spent catalyst was recovered by centrifugation, dried under vacuum at 100 °C for 6 h, and directly reused. A hot filtration test was performed by stopping the reaction after 6 h, and removing the catalyst by centrifugation. The product concentration in the supernatant was determined by GC. The catalyst-free supernatant was then transferred to a clean autoclave, repressurized with syngas, and heated again to assess leaching.

Computational methods

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed with a periodic slab model using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP)49, version 6.3.0. The projected augmented wave method (PAW) was used to describe the interaction of electron and ion50. The electron exchange and correlation energies were calculated within the generalized gradient approximation method (GGA) using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional51,52. To make sure that the energy difference is less than 10−4 eV and the force per atom is less than 0.03 eV/Å, Gaussian smearing (0.02 eV) was used. The Monkhorst-Pack mesh method was adopted for sampling at the Brillouin zone, and the k point was set as 3 x 3 x 1. The kinetic energy cutoff was set up to 400 eV and dispersion correction was considered by using the DFT-D3 method with Becke-Jonson damping53.

The adsorption energy (ΔGads) of the adsorbate (X) was obtained from the equation ΔGads = ΔGX/slab - ΔGslab - ΔGX, where ΔGX/slab was the total energy after adsorption, ΔGslab was the total energy of the clean surface, and ΔGX was the total energy of the free adsorbate (X) in a 20 × 15 × 15 cubic box; and therefore, the more negative ΔGads, the stronger the interactions between the adsorbates and surface; and the opposite number of ΔGads was regarded as the desorption energy ΔGdes. For reactions, the climbing-image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method was adopted to search the transition states (TS) and the vibrational frequency analysis was also processed to verify the authentic transition state with only one imaginary frequency54. The reaction Gibbs energy barrier (ΔGa) is defined as ΔGa = ΔGTS - ΔGIS, and the reaction energy (ΔGr) is defined as ΔGr = ΔGFS - ΔGIS, where ΔGIS, ΔGFS and ΔGTS were the total energies of the initial, final and transition states, respectively. The temperature was 443 K.

To study the hydroformylation of styrene on the surface of Rh/CeO2 and PPh3-Rh/CeO2, we chose the (111) surface of CeO2, and Rh atom was loaded by O atoms of CeO2. The Hubbard U parameters of 5.0 eV had been incorporated to address the electron localization effects within the 4 f orbitals of cerium in PPh3-Rh/CeO2 and Rh/CeO255. For the modeling of different surface models of Rh/CeO2 and PPh3-Rh/CeO2, a four-layer model with p (4 × 4) supercell with the two upper layers relaxed and another two lower layer fixed, and the thickness of the vacuum layer was 15 Å.

Physical characterization

Gas chromatography analyses were performed on an Agilent 7890 A GC equipped with an HP-5 capillary column and FID detector, while an Agilent 5977 A GC-MS system was used for identification.

For TEM observation, the catalyst samples were dispersed in ethanol via ultrasonication and deposited onto carbon-coated copper grids. Images were acquired using an FEI G2 F20 transmission electron microscope operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV, providing a line resolution of 0.14 nm and a point resolution of 0.20 nm.

Aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-HAADF-STEM) was performed using a JEOL NEOARM microscope. For imaging, the powder samples were dispersed in methanol and drop-cast onto a carbon-coated copper grid.

Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected on a STOE STADIP automated transmission diffractometer equipped with a curved Ge (111) monochromator (Cu Kα₁ radiation) and a 6° position-sensitive detector (PSD). Data were acquired in the 2θ range of 10–80°. Phase identification was performed using the WinXpow software (STOE) and the ICDD PDF database.

XPS were obtained using a VG ES-CALAB 210 instrument equipped with a dual Mg/Al anode X-ray source, a hemispherical capacitor analyzer, and a 5 keV Ar iron gun. The electron binding energy was referenced to the C 1 s peak at 284.8 eV. The background pressure in the chamber was less than 10−7 Pa. The peaks were fitted by Gaussian-Lorentzian curves after linear background subtraction. For quantitative analysis, the peak areas were divided by the element-specific Scofield factor and the transmission function of the analyzer.

The contents of Rh in the catalysts were measured by inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-AES), using an Iris Advantage Thermo Jarrel Ash device.

Nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms were measured at 77 K using an American Quantachrome iQ2 automated gas sorption analyzer. The pore-size distribution was calculated by the Barrett, Joyner, and Halenda (BJH) method from the desorption isotherm.

The in-situ FTIR of samples was analyzed by a Bruker VERTEX 70 FTIR spectrometer and used for the identification of IR absorbance in the mid-IR region (400-4000 cm-1). It was equipped with liquid-nitrogen-cooled MCT detector and low-volume gas cell (8.7 mL) with a 123 mm path length and KBr windows, the IR spectra were collected by 32 scans at a resolution of 4 cm-1. The sample was pretreated at 300 °C for 60 min under Ar (20 mL/min) and cooled to 140 °C for adsorption and reaction. The CO (10 mL/min) stream was induced into the IR cell for 30 min, then Ar (20 mL/min) purging.

The X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) experiments were performed on the BL11B beamline of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). The XAS spectra were energy-calibrated and normalized using the Athena software of the Demeter package. The Fourier transformation of the k3-weighted extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) oscillations, k3·χ(k), from the k space to the R space were performed over a range of 3.0-11.0 Å-1 to obtain the radial distribution function. EXAFS spectra were fitted by the Artemis software. For Wavelet Transform (WT) analysis, the χ(k) exported from Athena was imported into the Hama Fortran code. The parameters were listed as follows: R range, 1-4 Å, k range, 0-12 Å-1 for samples; k weight, 3; and Morlet function with κ = 10, σ = 1 was used as the mother wavelet to provide the overall distribution.
