A prospective randomized study on the efficacy of real-time dynamic navigation in deep horizontal mandibular third molar extractions
Authors: Xu Fangfang, Gong Yuxin, Sina Ahmadi, Wang Chunyan, Pouria Arian, Yu Ming, Tu Junbo, Na Sijia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the clinical efficacy of applying real-time dynamic navigation (RDN) in the extraction of deep horizontal mandibular impacted third molars, hypothesizing that RDN reduces surgical time and minimizes the risk of injury to adjacent anatomical structures.MethodsA prospective study was conducted on 160 patients aged between 18 and 37 years with deep horizontal impaction of the mandibular third molar. The participants were randomly assigned to either the experimental group (receiving RDN-assisted extractions) or the control group (undergoing traditional extraction methods). Preoperative planning utilized cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) and Mimics software for the accurate localization and segmentation of impacted teeth. Parametric data were analysed via an independent t test for intergroup comparisons, and significance was set to p < 0.05.ResultsIn the experimental group, an average of 11 ± 1 min was required for preoperative planning via RDN, which was not required in the control group. The setup of the navigation system took an average of 4 ± 1 min in the experimental group and 0 min in the control group. The experimental group demonstrated a significantly shorter average surgical time (22 ± 3 min) than did the control group (36 ± 3 min). The differences in the preoperative design time, surgical time, and complication rates between the two groups were statistically significant (p = 0.005). Additionally, the RDN group reported no complications related to adjacent tooth damage or nerve injury.ConclusionThe precision, safety, real-time guidance of RDN supports its use in complicated dental extractions, which would introduce a new era of oral and maxillofacial surgery.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Real-time dynamic navigation, Mandibular impacted third molar, Minimally invasive tooth extraction, Inferior alveolar nerve

Introduction

Impacted teeth occur due to an evolutionary discord between the amounts of dental tissue and bone tissue [1]. These teeth are obstructed by adjacent teeth, bone, or soft tissue, which leads to either partial eruption or a complete inability to erupt, with no possibility of future eruption. The lower mandibular third molar is the most commonly impacted tooth, and the incidence rate of impacted lower mandibular third molars is approximately 24% [2, 3]. Deep horizontally impacted third molars that may be possible sites of pathology should be extracted to prevent the displacement of adjacent teeth or the development of odontogenic cystic lesions.

Due to the proximity of deep horizontally impacted mandibular third molars to the roots of the second molars and the inferior alveolar nerve, surgical procedures are very likely to cause damage to these adjacent teeth and nerves [4, 5]. Preoperatively, surgeons rely mainly on panoramic radiographs or cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) to determine the position of the impacted tooth. However, these methods do not allow real-time evaluation of the relationship between high-speed dental handpieces and critical anatomical structures during surgical procedures. This poses a significant challenge for impacted teeth that are adjacent to or overlapping with the inferior alveolar nerve. To protect nerves and adjacent teeth, surgeons frequently find it necessary to enlarge the flap and extend the amount of bone that is removed. This approach not only prolongs the surgery time but also exacerbates postoperative reactions such as pain, swelling, and bleeding [6–8].

The expertise of surgeons and continuous advances in oral instruments complement each other in the process of extracting impacted mandibular lower third molars (wisdom teeth). Presently, various instruments are available for extracting impacted teeth, including pneumatic dental handpieces [9], ultrasonic osteotomes [10, 11], and lasers [12]. Minimally invasive and comfortable surgical techniques have facilitated the gradual incorporation of digital positioning guides [13, 14], virtual and simulation technologies [15–17], and intraoperative navigation into tooth extraction procedures [18–22]. Nevertheless, there are numerous obstacles. For example, pneumatic dental handpieces cannot precisely locate the position of the tooth that needs to be sectioned during surgery; instead, identifying the location of the affected tooth depends significantly on the surgeon’s skills. This makes it challenging for novice doctors to master this skill, leading to potential damage to the inferior alveolar nerve or even the abandonment of extraction. Ultrasonic osteotomes or lasers may decrease the occurrence of nerve injury during bone removal or tooth sectioning, but their low cutting efficiency prolongs the surgery time, resulting in increased patient discomfort during the operation and increased risk of postoperative infection. Digital positioning guides allow the quick and accurate determination of an impacted tooth’s location, which leads to reduced surgical duration and minimized surgical trauma. However, these methods have lengthy production periods, and their clinical application is restricted due to their impact on soft tissues. Therefore, these tools are used only to locate the tooth and do not provide real-time monitoring of the interaction between the dental handpiece and the nerves. Currently, virtual and simulation technologies are more commonly applied in clinical teaching to improve the precise repositioning of impacted teeth in three dimensions, although their practical clinical application still needs further optimization [19, 23]. This study sought to evaluate the clinical safety and efficacy of RDN in the extraction of deeply impacted horizontal mandibular third molars.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the College of Stomatology, Xi’an Jiaotong University (Approval No: 2023-XJKQIEC-033-002), and informed consent was obtained from all the patients.

Clinical data

This study included the records of 160 patients who were treated at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery at Xi’an Jiaotong University between January and December 2023. This study focused on 160 mandibular impacted third molars in deep-positioned horizontal impaction. The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: ①deep horizontal third molars that overlapped the inferior alveolar nerve, as confirmed by imaging; ②no adjacent second molars that were missing; and ③and no severe caries. The exclusion criteria for this study ①were as follows: severe chronic inflammation or granulation tissue; ②cysts, tumours, or fractures in the lower third molar area; ③ severe decay or apical inflammation in the adjacent second molar; ④ inability to comply with medical advice for timely follow-up or incomplete records, which may affect the evaluation of treatment effectiveness; and ⑤ preoperative allergies to amoxicillin and loxoprofen.

Randomized grouping methodology

In our study, the random allocation of patients to experimental (receiving RDN-assisted extractions) and control groups (undergoing traditional extraction methods) is crucial to ensure the scientific rigor and reliability of the results. Random allocation was performed via computer-generated random number tables. Patients were assigned random numbers according to their enrolment order and were allocated to the RDN or control group on the basis of these numbers. Throughout the randomization process, the personnel responsible for randomization received thorough training to ensure consistency and independence in the randomization procedure.

Preoperative preparation

Prior to surgery, CBCT scans were performed on all patients to assess the relationships between the impacted lower horizontal third molars and adjacent teeth, the number of tooth roots, and their associations with the mandibular nerve canal. The administration of antibiotics (amoxicillin, 250 mg) and painkillers (loxoprofen, 60 mg) was carried out as a preventive measure before surgery. In addition, antibiotics were continuously administered for 3 days after surgery (amoxicillin, 250 mg/tid, loxoprofen, 60 mg/bid). The study included 74 males and 86 females, with ages ranging from 18 to 37 years and a mean age of 24.12 years. The patients were randomly assigned to the experimental and control groups, each including 80 cases of mandibular impacted third molars in a deep-positioned horizontal impaction (See Table 1).

Navigation planning, design, and application methods

Data collection

A mandibular positioner was selected. A U-shaped tube filled with silicone rubber was placed as a reference coordinate system in the area where the impacted tooth was located, and the patient wore it while CBCT images were captured.

Extraction plan design

The collected CBCT data were imported into Mimics 21.0 software, and the impacted tooth, adjacent teeth, surrounding bone tissues, and important anatomical structures, such as nerves, were segmented and reconstructed. Three-dimensional measurement analysis was conducted with the measurement data used to determine the safe distance between the impacted tooth and important anatomical structures for subsequent navigation identification (Fig. 2).

Preoperative digital design: using Mimics 21.0 software for 3D reconstruction, followed by labeling the mandibular neural canal, and safe distance between important anatomical structures. (Green column : tooth dividing line )

Intraoperative application of dynamic navigation

(1) Installation of the navigation system: ① Assembly: The dental high-speed handpiece instrument was assembled with the navigation emitter, and the silicone rubber model was prepared (Fig. 3a). ② Calibration: The reference coordinate system of the high-speed dental handpiece instrument was established (Fig. 3b). ③ Matching: Dental resin was used to fix the reference template in the contralateral area of the mandible for matching (alignment between the dental high-speed handpiece instrument and CBCT) (Fig. 3c). ④ Validation of navigation matching accuracy: The fissure drill of the high-speed dental handpiece instrument was placed on the anatomical cusp of the mandibular tooth for navigation accuracy validation (Fig. 3d).

Preoperative dynamic navigation: (a) Assembly: The dental high-speed handpiece instrument was installed by connecting the navigation emitter. (b) Calibration: The reference coordinate system was set. (c) Matching: The reference template was attached to the contralateral mandible with dental resin. (d) Validation: The drill was placed on the mandibular tooth anatomical cusp to test navigation accuracy

(2) Extraction: ① Precise positioning of the impacted tooth was performed under RDN guidance (Fig. 4b). ② The bone covering the crown surface was removed by matching the high-speed handpiece (we used high-speed pneumatic handpieces and cooled the area with cold water throughout the entire process) with an instrument attached to the navigation system close to the tooth crown surface (Fig. 4c); ③ Tooth cutting was performed along the previously planned direction. During the cutting process, real-time monitoring of the relationship between the dental high-speed handpiece instrument and the mandibular nerve was needed to segment and remove the tooth (Fig. 4d, e). After the socket was flushed with physiological saline, the wound was intermittently sutured (Fig. 4f). In the control group, the traditional minimally invasive tooth extraction method was used for extracting the impacted tooth.

(a, b) Intraoral incision and flap to remove coronal soft tissue resistance. (c, d) The bone was removed to expose the affected tooth and remove the coronal bone resistance. (e) T-shaped tooth separation was used to remove resistance from adjacent teeth. (f) Minimally invasive extraction of the affected tooth was performed in pieces, and the wound was tightly sutured

Recorded indicators

The time indicators were as follows: ① Preoperative design time: time required to design the tooth extraction plan before surgery. ② Navigation installation time: time required from navigation calibration to completion of matching. ③ Surgical time: time required from flap incision to suture completion.

Complications: ① Adjacent tooth damage: neighbouring teeth are not loose, and the patient experiences discomfort or mild biting pain. ② Inferior alveolar and lingual nerve damage: sensation abnormalities in the tip of the tongue and lower lip after the effects of anaesthesia wore off.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed via SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The data are expressed as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Correlation analysis was conducted via Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation coefficient, depending on the data distribution. The statistical power of the study was determined to ensure an adequate sample size for detecting significant differences. All the experimental procedures were repeated at least three times to ensure the reproducibility and reliability of the results.

Results

Time indicators

Preoperative design time

Experimental Group: The average time spent on the preoperative design was 11 ± 1 min. This preparation may involve planning the surgical approach, possibly using advanced tools such as digital planning or simulation. Control Group: The preoperative design time was 0 min, suggesting that either no specific preoperative planning was required or the planning was performed during the surgery.

Installation time

Experimental Group: The installation time, likely related to setting up the equipment or technology that was used in surgery, was 4 ± 1 min. This additional setup time might be associated with advanced surgical aids such as navigation systems. Control Group: Again, the installation time was 0 min, indicating either the lack of specific equipment or a simpler setup.

Surgical time

Experimental Group: The average surgical time in this group was 22 ± 3 min. This shorter duration indicates a more efficient surgical procedure, likely due to advanced techniques and better preoperative planning. In the control group, the average surgical time was significantly longer, 36 ± 3 min. This extended duration could be due to the use of traditional methods, which might be less efficient than the techniques used in the experimental group. The difference in surgical time between the two groups was statistically significant (p = 0.005). This suggests that the reduced time in the experimental group is not due to random variation but is likely a result of the procedures or technologies employed in this group.

The significant difference in surgical time between the experimental and control groups suggests that the experimental group was treated with more efficient techniques or technologies, leading to faster surgeries. The presence of a preoperative design and installation time in the experimental group indicates the use of advanced planning tools or equipment, which are absent or not separately accounted for in the control group. This advanced planning and setup in the experimental group likely contribute to the reduced surgical time.

However, it is important to consider that while the approach used in the experimental group reduces surgical time, it does require additional preoperative preparation. The overall time savings and enhanced efficiency in the experimental group would need to be weighed against these additional preparatory steps to fully assess the benefits of the employed methods. Additionally, the impact of these techniques on other surgical outcomes, such as patient recovery, complication rates, and overall effectiveness of the surgery, should also be considered for a comprehensive evaluation.

Complications

All the patients in the study were successfully treated via the RDN within the clinical setting. This advanced technology provides real-time guidance during surgical procedures, greatly enhancing the precision and safety of the operations. In the experimental group, where RDN was employed, the outcomes were notably positive. There were no recorded complications, such as damage to adjacent teeth or nerve injuries. This is a significant finding, as it highlights the efficacy of RDN in minimizing risks that are commonly associated with dental surgeries, particularly in complex cases such as the extraction of impacted teeth or surgeries in anatomically challenging areas.

In contrast, the control group, which presumably underwent conventional surgical methods without the aid of RDN, reported some complications. Specifically, there were 3 cases of damage to adjacent teeth. This type of complication can occur due to a variety of factors, such as limited visibility, challenges in accessing the surgical site, or difficulties in manoeuvring surgical instruments in tight spaces. Additionally, 4 cases of lower lip discomfort were reported in the control group. This symptom is often indicative of trauma or irritation to the inferior alveolar nerve, which can occur during dental surgeries, particularly in the absence of real-time navigational guidance (See Table 2, chart 1).

Discussion

The extraction of lower third molars is one of the most common outpatient surgeries in oral and maxillofacial surgery, especially since the close relationship between impacted lower third molars and the inferior alveolar nerve makes extraction more challenging, time-consuming, and prone to complications [24, 25]. The difficulty of extraction increases with increasing depth of the impacted teeth, and correspondingly, the risk of nerve damage also increases [26, 27]. 90% of inferior alveolar nerve injuries are caused by the extraction of impacted lower third molars, and such injuries lead to severe nerve damage symptoms such as numbness in the tongue and lower lip [7, 28].

Therefore, protecting the inferior alveolar nerve before and during surgery is a primary concern. Navigation on the basis of radiological data allows direct visualization of the relationships between impacted teeth and adjacent critical anatomical structures. There have been reports on the use of intraoperative navigation to assist in the precise localization of impacted teeth. RDN is specifically used in cases where alveolar bone removal is needed during the extraction of mandibular third molars. During the bone removal process, it is essential to protect the inferior alveolar nerve and other important anatomical structures. The introduction of RDN into clinical practice reflects the continuous development of dental technology, which prioritizes procedures that are minimally invasive while optimizing patient safety. This evolution is a component of a broader trend in health care, namely, a trend towards digitization and precision therapy. RDN is a combination of imaging technology, computer-assisted design, and surgical expertise, and it embraces the concepts of modern surgical practice in a manner that complements rather than replaces the clinician’s talent. The application of RDN in the extraction of deep horizontal mandibular impacted third molars represents an important step forward in oral and maxillofacial surgery. The findings of this study illustrate the effectiveness of RDN in improving surgical precision, decreasing operative times, and limiting potential risks associated with standard extraction procedures. The consequences of these outcomes extend beyond the immediate surgical benefits, implying a new era in the approach for challenging dental extractions.

However, the main issues with current navigation systems are reliance on CT for data acquisition, which does not provide accurate dental arch data; complexity of the system, leading to time-consuming preoperative design and installation of navigation devices; and inability to achieve real-time and continuous positioning of teeth. ① Reliance on CT for data acquisition: Current navigation systems primarily depend on CT scans to acquire the necessary three-dimensional data for surgery. However, CT imaging is more suitable for hard tissues and less effective in capturing the fine details of teeth and soft tissues. As a result, navigation systems may face issues related to precision during the removal of mandibular third molars. ② Complexity of the system: Dynamic navigation systems often require intricate preoperative planning and the installation of navigation devices. This complexity can burden health care professionals and extend the overall duration of the surgical procedure. ③ Initial expense of equipment and training: The significant initial investment required to purchase equipment (dynamic navigation instruments are valued at RMB 1 million and disposable consumables for each patient are valued at RMB 480 per patient per session) and personnel training can limit the broader adoption of dynamic navigation systems. In resource-limited settings, high costs become a major barrier to the widespread use of such advanced technologies. The approach taken by the research, which concentrates on particular patient demographic data and surgical scenarios, might restrict the practical use of the findings. Moreover, depending on certain technologies and software, the learning curve associated with the adoption of new surgical instruments must be considered. The initial expense of equipment and training could hinder wider implementation, especially in areas with limited resources. These limitations restrict its application in maxillofacial surgery [29, 30]. Traditional assessment facial swelling is mainly done by visual observation and scale measurements, which are subject to large errors and do not allow for accurate retention of follow-up data [31]. Alessandro Antonelli et al. found that postoperative facial edema could be accurately assessed in three dimensions with the help of the Bellus3D Face App [32]. Therefore, we will use 3D facial scanning to assess facial swelling in the subsequent clinical follow-up.

This study used Mimics software for the preoperative segmentation and marking of impacted teeth and their relationships with critical anatomical structures. It also attempted to bind and match the navigation system with dental high-speed handpieces to achieve real-time tracking and understanding of the relationship between the dental high-speed handpiece and the inferior alveolar nerve. In dynamic navigation in the extraction of lower third molars impacted at a deep horizontal level, a surgeon needs to spend time on tooth segmentation and extraction design on the basis of CBCT data (11 ± 1 min) and navigation installation (4 ± 1 min). However, compared with that of the control group, the extraction time (experimental group: 22 ± 3 min; control group: 36 ± 3 min) was significantly different (P = 0.005). Therefore, the advantages of RDN in extracting lower third molars impacted deep and horizontally placed are as follows: ① preoperative design: CBCT, preoperative planning, and surgery, which can be conducted on the same day without intraoral impressions or dental lab assistance; ② minimally invasive: shorter surgery time and reduced postoperative complications; and ③ precision: accurate location of and segmentation of the teeth, increased operator flexibility, elimination of guide plate errors and associated costs, and ability to be used in limited space; ④ real-time visualization: real-time and visual observation of the relationship between the dental high-speed handpiece and the nerve prevents nerve damage; ⑤ improved intraoperative posture as a result of surgical plan matching and direct navigation display visualization; and ⑥ easy to use by well-trained beginners, reducing obstacles related to learning new skills. This study highlights the significant advantages of the use of RDN in the surgical removal of deeply impacted horizontal mandibular third molars. These findings confirm prior studies demonstrating that RDN improves surgical accuracy, decreases surgery duration, and reduces the risk of nerve damage and harm to adjacent teeth. RDN offers real-time, three-dimensional guidance for decreasing the risks associated with complex extractions, in contrast to traditional methods, which depend on the operator’s expertise and skill. This study revealed that the RDN group had faster surgery times and no nerve injury, which is consistent with the expected advantages of minimally invasive surgeries, which are known to promote quicker recovery and reduce postoperative complications. In summary, this study preliminarily explores the application of dynamic navigation in the extraction of lower third molars impacted at a deep horizontal level. Although the preoperative design and navigation setup require a certain amount of time, they do not increase the patient’s chairside visit duration.

Moreover, the preoperative design helps the surgeon visually and clearly understand the relationship between the lower third molars impacted at a deep horizontal level and surrounding critical anatomical structures (such as adjacent teeth and the inferior alveolar nerve). Appropriate measures can be planned before surgery, enabling better protection of these vital structures. Therefore, the use of dynamic navigation for the extraction of lower third molars impacted at a deep horizontal level can shorten the extraction time and minimize the risk of complications, making it a viable option for clinical use.

Future directions

The future of DNS in dentistry appears promising, with expected advances leading to broader implementation in modern practice. Further research should focus on increasing the evidence base for the safety and effectiveness of RDN in oral and maxillofacial surgery. Comparative studies involving multiple technological platforms and surgical procedures would provide valuable insights into the optimal use of RDN. Additionally, long-term follow-up studies are necessary to assess the durability of the observed benefits and to analyse patient-reported outcomes, including postoperative pain, oedema, and overall satisfaction. Investigating the cost-effectiveness of RDN in various health care systems can provide important information for policy and medical practice.

Conclusion

RDN requires a certain amount of time for design and installation before surgery, but it does not increase the patient’s chair time. The preoperative design helps the surgeon instinctively understand the relationship between the horizontally impacted third molar in the low-lying mandibular region and the surrounding crucial anatomical structures. It enhances the protection of vital structures during surgical procedures. Therefore, this study highlights the significant advantages associated with employing RDN to extract deep horizontal mandibular impacted third molars, which include a reduced surgical time and a minimized risk of injuring adjacent anatomical structures. The results support the wider implementation of RDN in clinical procedures, introducing a new era of accuracy and safety in oral and maxillofacial surgery.
