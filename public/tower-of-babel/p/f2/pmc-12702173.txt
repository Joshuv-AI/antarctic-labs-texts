Characteristics and mortality factors of severe malaria in children and teenager aged from 5 to 15 years in Thiès region, Senegal
Authors: Agbogbenkou Tevi Dela-Dem Lawson, Maimouna Sidibé, Mohamed Achraf Mahmoud, Bruno Hans Bidzanga Bahada, Sylvie Audrey Diop
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•First multicenter study on severe malaria in 5–15-year-olds in Senegal.•Strong adherence to World Health Organization guidance: artesunate followed by artemisinin-based combination therapy.•Identifies gaps in prehospital care needing system improvement.

Introduction

Malaria is a parasitic, febrile, and hemolytic disease caused by the presence and development of one or more hematozoa of the genus Plasmodium in the human body [1]. Plasmodium falciparum, predominantly found in tropical and subtropical regions—particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, parts of Latin America, and Asia—is responsible for the most severe form of the disease. Delayed diagnosis and inappropriate or late management of uncomplicated malaria can lead to progression toward severe, life-threatening, or disabling forms [2]. Sub-Saharan Africa bears the brunt of this disease: according to the World Health Organization (WHO), in 2021, the continent accounted for 95% of new cases and 96% of malaria-related deaths [3].

In 2023, malaria remained a major global health concern, with 263 million cases reported across 83 countries and 597,000 associated deaths. Africa accounted for 94% of global cases and 95% of malaria-related deaths. Among children aged 5 to 15 years, malaria was the fifth leading cause of death in Africa in 2019, responsible for 6.52% of all-cause mortality in this age group [4].

In Senegal, the implementation of the National Malaria Control Program (NMCP) has led to a more structured and strategic response, contributing to a significant reduction in malaria-related morbidity and mortality over the past two decades.

According to the 2024 PNLP report, over the past decade, proportional malaria morbidity declined from 4.86% in 2015 to 3.23% in 2024, corresponding to a 33.6% reduction. Similarly, proportional malaria mortality decreased from 3.52% in 2015 to 1.93% in 2024, representing a 45.3% reduction [5].

National parasite prevalence, estimated at approximately 3% in 2012, dropped to near-zero levels in 11 of the country’s 14 regions by 2021. The Thiès region is currently classified as a malaria pre-elimination zone, with an incidence of fewer than one case per 1000 inhabitants [6]. In 2024, the case fatality rate in the Thiès region was 4.5% across all age groups, and zero among children under 5 years of age. The available studies focus on the general population, children under 5 years of age, and pregnant women [5].

Despite its significant representation among malaria cases, the 5–15-year age group has received relatively little attention in research. In 2021, Saidou’s team in Niger reported that this group accounted for 51.41% of pediatric hospitalizations due to malaria [7]. Within this context, we conducted the present study to analyze the epidemiological, clinical, paraclinical, therapeutic, and prognostic characteristics of severe malaria in children and adolescents aged 5 to 15 years in the Thiès health region of Senegal.

Methodology

This retrospective, multicenter, descriptive study with analytical aims was conducted in the Thiès health region of Senegal. The region has a total population of 2,463,677 inhabitants, including 925,375 children under the age of 15. Its healthcare infrastructure comprises 297 health huts, 191 health posts, 10 health centers (six classified as level 1 and four as level 2), and four public hospitals—two designated as level 1 public health establishments and two as level 2.

The study was conducted in the pediatric departments of the four referral hospitals responsible for managing severe malaria cases in the Thiès region, from July 1, 2019, to June 30, 2024.

In accordance with the guidelines of the NMCP, rectal artesunate should be administered prior to hospital transfer in suspected severe cases under 6 years of age.

Children aged 5 to 15 years were included if they had a confirmed diagnosis of malaria defined by a positive rapid diagnostic test (RDT) pan-Plasmodium and/or thick blood smear, along with at least one severity criterion as established by the WHO in 2015.

Data were collected from patient medical records and hospitalization registers. Patient anonymity and confidentiality were strictly maintained. Authorization was obtained from hospital directors and department heads prior to the initiation of the study. We declare that this work presents no conflict of interest and complies with all ethical and professional standards.

Results

During the study period, 109 cases of severe malaria were identified out of a total of 10,792 hospitalizations, corresponding to an overall hospital frequency of 1.01%. Nearly half of the patients (52.29%) were managed at the Thiès Regional Hospital.

Severe malaria cases occurred predominantly at the end of the rainy season, between September and January, with peak frequencies observed in October (36.7%) and November (22.9%).

A pathological history was identified in nine patients (8.25%), with asthma being the most frequently reported comorbidity (three cases). No documentation of comorbidity management was found in the patient records.

In our study, the majority of children hospitalized for severe malaria were admitted following a consultation or emergency room visit (56.88%), while 41.28% were referred from another healthcare facility.

Among the 45 referred patients, prehospital treatment was documented in 12 cases (26.6%). Oral antimalarials were the most commonly administered (66.67%), followed by undocumented antibiotic therapy (25%). Notably, no cases of prereferral treatment using rectal artesunate—as recommended by the NMCP program—were reported in the medical records.

At the clinical and paraclinical level, the average time to treatment initiation was 4.46 ± 2.86 days. The main clinical symptoms prompting hospital admission were fever (78.9%), vomiting (43.12%), and headache (39.45%). Neurological signs observed at admission included altered consciousness (13.7%) and convulsions (11.9%).

Anemia was confirmed by blood count in 38.24% of cases, while thrombocytopenia was observed in 61.62%.

Blood group was documented in 71 patients. Group O was the most represented (39%, n = 28), followed by group A (36.6%, n = 26), group B (18.3%, n = 13), and group AB (5.6%, n = 4).

Radiographic examinations were performed in 10 patients (9.17%). Abnormal findings were noted in three cases: pulmonary condensation (n = 2) and cardiomegaly (n = 1).

Regarding therapeutic and prognostic aspects, injectable artesunate was administered as first-line treatment in all patients. A transition to artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) was initiated in 88 cases, representing 80.7% of the cohort.

Among the 109 patients, 55 (50.46%) received antibiotic therapy. However, no diagnostic or prognostic justification for initiating antibiotics was documented in the medical records.

Symptomatic treatments were also administered, including diazepam in 11 patients and phenobarbital in six. Blood transfusions were required in 59 cases, accounting for 54.12% of the sample.

The median duration of hospitalization was 7 days (Q1–Q3: 5–10 days). Clinical outcomes were favorable in 90% of cases. The observed case fatality rate was 4.5% (n = 5). Among the recovered patients, six (5.5%) presented with sequelae, each involving a distinct impairment: aphasia, sinus arrhythmia, epilepsy, psychiatric disorders, neurological deficits, and visual disturbances.

Discussion

The hospital frequency of severe malaria observed in our study (1.01%) is lower than those reported in several other studies, such as Keita’s in Mali (11.55%) and Saidou’s in Niger (21.32%) [1,7]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the specific characteristics of our study population, which resides in a region where malaria incidence remains relatively low. The Thiès health region benefits from its classification as a pre-elimination zone and from the sustained efforts of the NMCP [6].

During the study period, peaks in the frequency of severe malaria cases were recorded in October (36.7%) and November (22.9%), followed by September (16.5%). These findings are consistent with those of Camara et al. [8], who documented a similar seasonal trend in Senegal in 2003. This distribution reflects the well-established seasonal nature of malaria in the country, with a surge in cases typically occurring at the end of the rainy season, a period conducive to the proliferation of vector mosquitoes.

The correlation between malaria prevalence and the rainy season has been widely documented in sub-Saharan Africa. Keita’s study in Mali reported a peak in November (36.75%) and a marked decline in January and February (1.709%) [1]. Similarly, Saidou’s study in Niger identified peaks in October (38.28%), September (22.82%), and December (14.7%) [7]. These findings align with our results, which show a seasonal concentration of severe malaria cases between September and November, consistent with the post-rainy period favorable to vector proliferation.

In our cohort, the 10–15 year age group accounted for 54.13% of cases. This is comparable to Saidou’s findings in Niger (51.41%) and to data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2019, which reported that 36.5% of malaria cases involved children aged 12 years and above [7,9]. In contrast, studies by Mbono in Cameroon and Keita in Mali reported lower proportions of 25.3% and 26.5%, respectively [1,3].

In Senegal, seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC) targets children aged 3–120 months, stratified into three groups: 3–11 months, 12–59 months, and 60–120 months. SMC delivery consists of locally organized campaigns providing free mass distribution or administration of sulfadoxine–pyrimethamine plus amodiaquine (SP-AQ) through a door-to-door strategy led by the community health system. The program includes strong components of pharmacovigilance, health communication, advocacy, and social mobilization. To ensure full adherence, the NMCP has implemented directly observed therapy for the three-dose regimen by community health workers since 2019 [5].

In Senegal, where malaria transmission is intermediate and seasonal, premunition tends to develop later, typically after age 10, which may explain the higher incidence among preadolescents and adolescents.

A male predominance was observed in our study, representing 66.06% of cases. This trend has also been reported by several authors [1,3,7,10], suggesting potential behavioral or biological factors influencing exposure and susceptibility.

Direct hospital admission occurred in 56.88% of cases, while 41.28% were referred from other health facilities. These figures are similar to those reported by Gadiaga et al. [11] in 2024 at the Tambacounda health center, where 42.21% of cases were referred and 57.79% admitted directly. This distribution reflects the role of referral hospitals in pediatric care and the structure of the health pyramid, where initial management is often initiated at peripheral health posts.

Among the referred patients, only 12 (26.7%) had documented prehospital treatment. Oral antimalarials were the most frequently administered (66.67%), followed by undocumented antibiotic therapy. However, adherence to pretransfer treatment protocols, such as the use of rectal artesunate recommended by the NMCP (PNLP), was not systematically observed [6].

The time to consultation was recorded in 81 patients, with only 12.35% presenting within 48 hours of symptom onset, despite WHO recommendations to initiate antimalarial treatment within 24 to 48 hours. The average delay was 4.46 ± 2.86 days, comparable to the findings of Okoko et al. [12], who reported a mean delay of 3.9 days (range: 1–15 days).

Most patients in our study resided in rural areas (59.63%). Delayed care-seeking behavior contributes to the progression from uncomplicated to severe malaria. Although socioeconomic status was not assessed in our study, Keita et al. [1] in Mali highlighted the negative impact of parental socioeconomic vulnerability and late presentation on disease outcomes.

Clinically and paraclinically, the most common symptoms leading to admission were fever (78.9%), vomiting (43.12%), and headache (39.45%). These findings are consistent with those of Keita et al. [1] in Mali, fever (80.3%), vomiting (42.7%), and headache (36.8%), and comparable to the results of Gadiaga et al. [11] and other African series, including Saidou et al. in Niger, where fever was present in 90.36% of cases [7]. Neurological signs at admission included altered consciousness (13.7%) and convulsions (11.9%), figures close to those reported by Saidou, convulsions (14.99%) and coma (12.58%) [7].

Two patients in our cohort were diagnosed with HbSS sickle cell disease. Although this hemoglobinopathy is traditionally considered to confer partial protection against malaria infection, several studies, including that of McAuley et al. [13], have demonstrated that individuals with sickle cell disease are at increased risk of developing severe forms of malaria.

Malnutrition was also prevalent in our series. The weight-for-age Z-score was below –3 standard deviations (SD) in 12.4% of patients, a rate significantly higher than the regional average of 3%. Additionally, 30.5% of patients had a Z-score between –3 SD and –2 SD, nearly double the prevalence reported in the Thiès region according to the 2023 ANSD data [14].

On clinical examination, the most frequent signs were clinical anemia (74.31%), vomiting (49.54%), jaundice (39.45%), prostration (24.77%), convulsions (21.10%), and severe impairment of consciousness with a Glasgow Coma Score (GCS) <11 (15.59%). These signs were also reported by Ilunga-Ilunga et al. [15], who found anemia in 88.6% of cases, vomiting in 29.9%, prostration in 10.7%, and convulsions in 22.7% of cases.

Prostration was observed in 24.77% of cases in our series, making it a frequent but non-specific sign of severe malaria. It has also been reported at varying frequencies in several African studies: 33%, 41.3%, and 65.2% in the series by F.P. Mockenhaupt, B. Camara, and R. Idro, respectively [8,16,17]. Although classified among the severity criteria, prostration cannot be systematically included in the strict definition of cerebral malaria, as it does not always reflect direct cerebral involvement. It may indicate generalized weakness, a postictal state, or a response to profound anemia, and remains a somewhat subjective observation.

Neurological manifestations were mainly represented by severe impairment of consciousness (GCS ≤ 11) in 17.43% of cases. These results are broadly comparable to those reported by Rakotojoelimaria et al. [18]. Convulsions were observed in 21.10% of cases, a figure close to that reported by Maïga et al. (19.1%) [19], but markedly lower than the 52.5% reported by Camara et al. [8].

The threshold of GCS <11 is recognized as clinically relevant, as it is associated with a significantly increased risk of death, as demonstrated by Seidlein et al. in a multicenter randomized study [20].

Jaundice was observed in 39.5% of our patients. Notable variability has been reported across studies conducted in Senegal, including that of Imbert et al. [21], who found a prevalence of 9.9%.

The WHO [22] recommends promptly diagnosing malaria using parasitological tests in all patients suspected of having the disease before administering treatment. RDTs for malaria can significantly improve the quality of malaria case management, especially in remote areas with limited access to high-quality microscopic examination services. RDTs are relatively easy to perform and interpret, provide quick results, require minimal training, and allow malaria diagnosis at the community level. The PAN-RDT is a test capable of detecting multiple Plasmodium species, including P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. malariae, and P. ovale [23].

The RDT was performed on all patients and returned positive results.

Thick blood smears were performed in 46 patients, with only one negative result. The mean parasite density observed in our study was 46,465 parasites/µL. These values are relatively close to those reported by Camara et al. [8] in 2003, who found a mean parasite density of 35,824 parasites/µL. Although our average is higher, both studies reflect high parasitemia in children with severe malaria, indicating a substantial parasite burden associated with clinical severity.

In our series, blood group O was the most represented, found in 39.4% of patients. Several studies conducted in diverse populations have demonstrated a significant association between ABO blood group and severe forms of Plasmodium falciparum malaria. Blood group A has been correlated with a higher frequency of severe malaria in studies conducted in Zimbabwe (Fischer et al. [23]), Gabon (Lell et al. [24]), and elsewhere. Similarly, blood group AB has been implicated in increased susceptibility in studies conducted in Sri Lanka (Pathirana et al. [25]) and Mali (Rowe et al. [26]). In Rowe et al.’s [26] study, blood group O was associated with a significant reduction in rosetting. In a more recent study conducted in Ghana in 2024, Aninagyei et al. [27] reported that the risk of developing severe malarial anemia in children under 12 and patients aged ≥12 was 16-fold and 17.8-fold higher, respectively, in blood group O compared to blood group A.

A complete blood count was performed in 102 patients. Severe anemia was observed in 38.24% of cases, a rate comparable to that reported by Savadogo et al. [28] in 2014 in Burkina Faso, where it was present in 45.9% of children.

Thrombocytopenia was identified in 99 patients, representing 61.62% of the sample. This high proportion aligns with findings by Nsue and F. LY, who reported a frequency of 65.2% [9,29]. Furthermore, Imbert et al. [21], in a study conducted in Senegal, demonstrated that thrombocytopenia below 100,000 platelets/mm³ was an independent severity factor, increasing mortality when associated with other WHO-defined criteria.

Serum creatinine levels were assessed in 67 patients, with values >30 mg/L—WHO’s severity threshold—observed in 10.45% of cases. Samake et al. reported that 2.9% of cases presented with severe acute kidney injury (AKIN stage 3) due to malaria [30].

Renal involvement in severe malaria is common and initially functional, often linked to renal hypoperfusion. Without prompt management, it may progress to organic renal failure. This evolution is facilitated or exacerbated by massive hemolysis and hemoglobinuria, as reported by Lalya et al. [31].

In our series, only one patient presented with severe hypoglycemia (WHO criterion), with a favorable clinical outcome. This low rate may be explained by prompt management and the vigilance of healthcare personnel. Additionally, the use of injectable artesunate as the reference treatment for severe malaria, replacing quinine, has significantly reduced the occurrence of hypoglycemic episodes. Camara et al. [8] reported hypoglycemia in 6.9% of cases while in India.

Hyponatremia was found in 78% of cases in our series, making it a common electrolyte disturbance in children with severe malaria. This rate is higher than that reported by Camara et al. [8] in Senegal (53.8%) and by Dainguy et al. [32], who reported 65.6% in a recent series.

This variability in proportions may be explained by several factors, including the severity of cases at inclusion, the delay before consultation, associated digestive losses (vomiting, diarrhea), and rehydration practices prior to admission.

In our series, all patients (100%) received injectable artesunate, with a switch to ACT in 80.7% of cases, in accordance with WHO recommendations. In Niger, the initiation rate for injectable artesunate was 99.67%, with an ACT relay in 87.48% of cases [7]. This therapeutic strategy far exceeds those reported by Alihonou et al. [10] in Benin, where artesunate was used in 28.6% of cases compared to 52.4% for quinine, and by Thiongane et al. [33] in Dakar (2017), who still reported 29.5% quinine use.

In our study, 50.46% of patients received antibiotic therapy, of which 67.3% were third-generation cephalosporins (3GC). Comparable rates have been reported elsewhere: 44% in Thiongane et al. [33] and 36.5% in Alihonou et al. [10] (Cotonou, Benin). This frequent prescription reflects an empirical approach aimed at covering possible bacterial co-infections, whose clinical manifestations may mimic or accompany signs of severe malaria. This practice is supported by international recommendations; the WHO guidelines (3rd edition [34]) advise antibiotic therapy when bacterial co-infection is clinically suspected [34].

The median length of hospital stay in our study was 7 days (interquartile range, 5–10), consistent with Thiongane et al. [33], who also reported a mean of 7 days. A longer mean duration was observed by Camara et al. [8] (8.6 days; range 12 hours–66 days), likely reflecting differences in clinical presentation at admission and the occurrence of complications.

The mortality rate in our series was 4.50%, lower than that reported by Camara et al. [8] in Dakar (11.1%) and by Idro et al. [16] in Uganda (8.6–17.8%). This difference may be related to contextual factors such as initial disease severity, the quality and timeliness of therapeutic management, and disparities in access to pediatric intensive care.

Prognostic aspects: In multivariate analysis, mortality was significantly associated with respiratory distress (P = 0.0005) and with the presence of at least two severe clinical-biological criteria (odds ratio = 13.48; P = 0.0248). In the study by Mbono et al. [3] in Cameroon, only respiratory distress was significantly associated with death (risk ratio = 53.80; 95% confidence interval: 10.94–264.52; P <0.001).

Conclusion

Severe malaria in children aged 5–15 years remains a major public health concern in Senegal, with substantial mortality. In the Thiès region, neurological forms are the most common presentation in this age group. Mortality is significantly associated with respiratory distress and the concomitant presence of multiple severity criteria.

Limitations of our study

•Our study is retrospective, which entails exclusive reliance on data recorded in medical records and hospital registries. This approach limits the quality and accuracy of the information collected, as some parameters of interest were not systematically documented.•Furthermore, the low socioeconomic status of the patients restricted the performance of certain essential laboratory tests, particularly those related to the paraclinical criteria of severe malaria.
