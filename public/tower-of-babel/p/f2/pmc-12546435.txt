Kidney Function Recovery After Left Ventricular Assist Device Implantation: A Transient Hemodynamic Effect or an Artifact?

Kidney dysfunction is a central comorbidity in advanced heart failure, affecting 70% of patients and profoundly influencing both prognosis and therapeutic decision-making.1 The etiology of kidney dysfunction in this population spans both irreversible factors such as longstanding diabetes and atherosclerosis, and potentially reversible hemodynamic mechanisms, including low cardiac output and venous congestion. Patients with advanced heart failure experience symptoms that are refractory to guideline-directed medical therapy, with a median survival of only 12 months unless they receive life-saving interventions such as heart transplantation or left ventricular assist device (LVAD).2 When severe kidney dysfunction coexists with advanced heart failure, outcomes are even worse, often narrowing therapeutic options.

At the first glance, LVAD therapy may seem ideally suited for patients with combined cardiac and kidney disease; by improving forward flow and relieving venous congestion, it has the potential to reverse the hemodynamic component of kidney dysfunction, while avoiding the nephrotoxic effects of calcineurin inhibitors required after heart transplantation. However, postoperative acute kidney injury is frequent in LVAD recipients, and up to 13% may require dialysis.3 For LVAD recipients who require long-term dialysis, outcomes are poor; early mortality approaches 50%, and < 30% are discharged home, with most requiring transfer to long-term care facilities.4

Considering these data, current guidelines consider severe, irreversible kidney dysfunction a contraindication to LVAD implantation.5 However, the evidence base informing clinical practice is limited. In particular, the extent to which kidney function may recover post-LVAD is unclear, and predictors of sustained kidney function improvement are not well-defined. As a result, these high-stakes decisions are often made without objective criteria and based on center-specific practices and individual clinical judgment. Real-world factors, such as availability of outpatient dialysis facilities equipped to care for LVAD recipients, further influence these determinations. Consequently, patients who might benefit from LVAD support may be excluded because of presumed irreversibility of kidney dysfunction, whereas others with unrecognized kidney disease may undergo implantation only to experience poor postoperative outcomes.

In the current issue of Kidney International Reports, Walther et al.6 leverage both a single-center cohort and a large registry to characterize estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) trajectories over the first 90 days following LVAD implantation. They report a marked early increase in eGFR of almost 20 ml/min per 1.73 m2, peaking at a median of 11 days postimplantation, followed by a gradual decline. A potential interpretation is that early hemodynamic improvement drives an initial recovery in kidney function, which is not sustained because of other factors such as subclinical right ventricular failure or the lack of pulsatility from continuous-flow pumps. Nevertheless, this narrative is difficult to reconcile with the clinical picture of the early post-LVAD phase, where a substantial subset of patients experiences right ventricular failure requiring inotropic support and exhibits suboptimal hemodynamics. In a cohort with a high burden of diabetes, atherosclerosis, and chronic kidney disease often complicated by acute kidney injury, a rapid and complete normalization of kidney function seems unlikely.

An alternative explanation is that the observed eGFR trajectories partly reflect changes in determinants of serum creatinine independent of kidney function, particularly muscle wasting and nutritional status. In critically ill populations, previous research has demonstrated spurious increases in creatinine-based eGFR because of progressive sarcopenia, leading to substantial overestimation of true kidney function.7 In the LVAD setting, previous work has shown that muscle mass may decline early after implantation before recovering over time, potentially mirroring changes in creatinine.8,9 In addition, our group previously reported that the transient increase in eGFR post-LVAD was not detected when using cystatin C, a filtration marker less influenced by muscle mass and nutritional status.9

Some of the findings from the study by Walther et al.6 support the hypothesis that early post-LVAD improvements in creatinine-based eGFR may reflect changes in muscle mass and nutritional status rather than true kidney function recovery. Although worsening kidney function defined by creatinine is a strong predictor of adverse outcomes across heart failure populations,S1,S2 this study found only a weak association between eGFR changes and clinical outcomes in LVAD recipients. Notably, early improvements in eGFR did not predict lower risk of dialysis, and paradoxically, a higher early eGFR was associated with increased mortality. This counterintuitive finding is consistent with the hypothesis that, in this context, creatinine may serve more as a marker of muscle wasting than of kidney function.

Taken together, the study by Walther et al.6 provides an important foundation for future research investigating kidney-related outcomes of LVAD therapy. Given the limitations of creatinine, it remains uncertain whether post-LVAD trajectories in true kidney function are accurately captured by eGFR. The reversibility of kidney dysfunction in advanced heart failure may be more limited than previously believed, because persistent venous congestion superimposed on chronic risk factors such as diabetes and vascular disease may promote kidney fibrosis and limit post-LVAD kidney function recovery. The most pressing research need is to determine the extent to which creatinine reflects measured GFR in this unique population, and whether alternative filtration markers such as cystatin C offer greater accuracy. By building on the work of Walther et al.,6 future studies can establish the physiological impact of LVAD therapy on kidney function and move the field toward an evidence-based framework for patient selection and management. Until then, decisions regarding LVAD implantation in the context of kidney dysfunction will continue to rely on institutional practices and clinical judgment rather than validated metrics, leaving patients and clinicians to navigate life-altering choices on uncertain grounds.
