Physical Activity and Exercise for People with Parkinson's Disease: The Past, Present, and Future
Authors: Daniel M Corcos, Miranda J Munoz, Niyati Mehta, Kathleen E McKee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis invited perspective paper celebrating the 40th anniversary of Movement Disorders demonstrates that the topic of physical activity and exercise as a treatment for Parkinson's disease did not feature before 2002 in this journal. The topic of physical activity and exercise garnered increasing recognition over the next two decades with several important papers published in Movement Disorders. As of 2026, there are four well‐recognized treatments for Parkinson's disease: (1) exercise and physical activity; (2) general lifestyle modifications, including avoiding harmful environmental toxins; (3) medication; and (4) surgery and devices. The paper concludes by suggesting a variety of questions, the answers to which will improve clinicians' ability to help patients understand and implement the full Parkinson's exercise prescription. © 2026 The Author(s). There has been a tremendous evolution in the role of physical activity and exercise to improve health in people with Parkinson's disease (PD) since Movement Disorders was first published in 1986. In this perspective, we summarize the past contributions of Movement Disorders to advancing the role of physical activity and exercise for people with PD over the last 40 years, lay out the present status of physical activity and exercise as an established first‐line treatment for PD, discuss the challenges with facilitating exercise in people with PD, and look to the future to suggest the kinds of research studies we would like to see published in Movement Disorders going forward.

According to the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM), 1 “Physical activity is defined as any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that results in an increase in caloric requirements over resting expenditure. Exercise, in contrast, is a type of physical activity consisting of planned, structured, and repetitive bodily movement done to improve and/or maintain one or more components of physical fitness” (p. 1). Both are proactive interventions that improve health, attenuate the manifestations of disease, and, in many instances, also bring joy.

The Past: The Movement Disorders Journal (1986–2025)

We found no articles published in Movement Disorders between 1986 and 2000 that were related to exercise, physical activity, and physical therapy. In 2002, Deane and colleagues 2 authored a systematic review of “paramedical therapies” for PD (physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and speech and language therapy) and found no conclusive evidence of the effectiveness for any form of paramedical therapy that would warrant recommendation in routine clinical practice. A similar conclusion was drawn in a supplement published by Movement Disorders in 2002. 3 Both papers did encourage further research and acknowledged the potential benefits of these therapies.

The outlook brightened as we continued into the new millennium. Over the next 25 years, Movement Disorders published at least 100 articles related to physical activity and exercise for people with PD. These and other articles laid the foundation for current physical activity and exercise recommendations for people with PD. The eight most cited publications, all of which exceed 200 citations as determined by SCOPUS, show that Movement Disorders has had a significant impact in advancing the importance of exercise, physical activity, and physical therapy as treatments for PD. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Two are research articles that form the basis for recommending resistance training for people with PD (Fig. 1). 4 , 9 Six of the papers are review papers and/or meta‐analyses. One of these is “The Movement Disorder Society Evidence‐Based Medicine Review Update: Treatments for the Motor Symptoms of Parkinson's Disease,” 5 which was followed in 2018 with a comprehensive review. 11 It is in the 2018 review that exercise climbs aboard the list of possible disease‐modifying treatments for PD. This pattern of an increased rate of publishing articles related to exercise and physical activity is mirrored in other PD journals. 12 , 13

The Present (2026)

There have been two important developments over the last decade that make it easier for people with PD to increase their physical activity and exercise and be confident of the health‐ and disease‐related benefits. The first is that clear guidelines for exercise and physical activity have been published. 14 The second development is an increase in access to facilities and programs.

First, there is growing recognition that although medication and deep brain stimulation (DBS) are strikingly effective for the motor symptoms of PD, they are limited in value for nonmotor symptoms and do not affect disease progression. In contrast, there is increasing evidence for the benefits of physical activity and exercise in PD across motor and nonmotor symptoms, in addition to its potential to slow disease progression. Exercise, physical activity, and physical therapy have been shown to have symptomatic benefits 15 , 16 and improve physical function, 17 cognition, 18 and sleep. 19 In addition, behavioral therapies have been shown to be the most effective symptomatic treatment of both dysphagia and hypokinetic dysarthria in the short term (the degree of long‐term efficacy posttreatment is unclear). 20 Finally, there is emerging evidence that high‐intensity aerobic exercise may slow disease progression. 21 , 22 , 23 Currently, the first Phase 3 clinical trial on exercise in PD is underway (SPARX3) 24 ; results will be published in 2029 (Fig. 1). Through exclusive enrollment of de novo participants and the use of DaTscan (dopamine transporter scan) quantification as a key secondary outcome measure, SPARX3 is designed to test the hypothesis that high‐intensity aerobic exercise (80%–85% of peak heart rate [HR]) can slow disease progression as measured both by the Movement Disorder Society–sponsored revision of the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale Part III and a reduction in the decline of the striatal specific binding ratio. 24 This growing body of evidence for the beneficial effects of exercise has been used to develop an exercise prescription for PD, which includes recommendations for aerobic, resistance, flexibility, and neuromotor exercises (Fig. 1). 14

Second, over the past 5 years or so, there has been an increase in large PD‐specific centers that provide a wide variety of opportunities for people with PD to take part in physical activities. This increase is almost certainly linked to the growing body of research evidence in support of physical activity and exercise. Examples of such programs can be found in Minnesota (https://www.healthpartners.com/care/specialty‐centers/struthers‐parkinsons‐center/), Detroit (https://kirkgibsoncenter.org), Richmond (https://www.poweroverpd.org), Nashville (https://pdwellnessclub.com), and Chicago (https://movement‐revolution.com), to name just a few in the United States. Some of these centers are organized under the leadership of Movement Disorders specialists, while others are organized by physical therapists and exercise specialists. These centers all offer a wide variety of activities and are terrific for people with PD. There are also many exercise and activity classes designed specifically for people with PD taking place in health clubs, YMCAs, and small privately owned facilities. We fully recognize that although some other countries offer similar opportunities for people with PD, many do not. Much of the research thus far has focused on access to physical therapy in other countries, but we recognize that these barriers to access likely extend to exercise intervention implementation as well. 25 There is a growing recognition of the importance of social interaction in motivating exercise and other behavior changes. 26 There are more than 1000 PD‐specific community boxing classes in the United States, and people with PD respond very well to such classes. 27 Table tennis and dance are other activities that have a powerful affective component while also helping people with PD participate in exercise and physical activity. 28 , 29 Music therapy, which can incorporate physical activities, is also very beneficial. 30

The Future: Challenges and Opportunities

As a result of the research published in a variety of journals over the past 30+ years, and especially since the introduction of the idea that “Exercise is Medicine,” 31 we know that physical activity and exercise are clearly beneficial for general health, for addressing the symptoms of PD, and can possibly slow disease progression. In this section, we lay out some of the challenges and opportunities in delivering high‐quality exercise and physical activity guidance to people with PD.

Our Field Is Not Universally Aligned on the Importance of Exercise as Medicine

Despite the overwhelming evidence for the benefits of physical activity and exercise for PD, such a dramatic shift in orientation to treatment can take decades to permeate a field, which risks delaying deployment of this powerful treatment to people with PD. As recently as 2013, in response to a paper on exercise, Rascol 32 wrote, “The current pillars of the treatment for Parkinson disease (PD) include drugs, surgery, and devices.” He concluded his commentary by calling for more research, which has occurred in the intervening 12 years. And yet, there is still confusion about the message we should be sending to people with PD. In 2025, Boon and colleagues 33 wrote a letter suggesting that moderate‐intensity HR exercise may well be preferred over high‐intensity HR exercise, and highlighted the perceived danger of people with PD overexercising to their detriment. We have published a response in which we agreed on the importance of monitoring patients who overexercise but stressed the importance of high‐intensity aerobic exercise. 34 Boon and colleagues 35 concurred. One of the greatest challenges moving forward is to provide patients with a clear understanding of how strong the evidence is for the benefits of exercise and activity at the optimal dose. Exercise must be viewed as medicine and presented to patients as such. 31 We anticipate Movement Disorders and Movement Disorders Clinical Practice will take lead roles in articulating the value of exercise as medicine.

People With PD Are Often Not Informed About the Benefits of Exercise

Despite the substantial progress that has been made, many people with PD are still generally not aware of what lifestyle changes they should make and why. This is especially true for physical activity and exercise. One of the authors (D.M.C.) has Zoom sessions monthly with people with PD from around the world. It has become clear that most people with PD have not been informed by their physicians or even physical therapists of all the exercise and activity options available to them and the rationale for the importance of exercise and activity. 36 It has been well documented that there is a missed opportunity to refer patients to allied health services for help navigating their disease. 37 This is also true with respect to referring to exercise professionals. This needs to change and we are confident it will.

The ACSM and the Parkinson's Foundation have published joint guidelines on the exercise prescription for PD. These easy‐to‐read handouts articulate to patients and clinicians alike what people with PD should do to exercise (https://www.parkinson.org/about‐us/news/parkinsons‐foundation‐american‐college‐of‐sports‐medicine‐exercise‐recommendations). They are based on the published exercise prescription for PD. 1 , 14 There is no reason why every movement disorders clinic should not make these handouts available to their patients or at least direct them to appropriate web pages.

Quality of Exercise Programs for People With PD Is Variable

Although there has been a proliferation of exercise and activity programs for people with PD, the quality is variable. Within the United States, we have observed the nascent stages of a trend to certify coaches and trainers with a PD‐specific exercise certification so that people with PD can find exercise experts who understand PD and can provide safe and effective evidence‐based exercise programs. This certification will support quality training while minimizing the risk of common PD adverse events such as falls, pain/discomfort, and hypotension. 38 This move is being led by the Parkinson's Foundation in collaboration with the ACSM (https://www.parkinson.org/resources‐support/professionals/special‐programs/accreditation). We are confident that this trend will continue to ensure greater access to high‐quality exercise and activity programs specifically for people with PD.

Exercise Is Hard to Translate From Efficacy to Effectiveness

Efficacy refers to how well an intervention performs under ideal laboratory‐type conditions. Effectiveness refers to how well that same intervention performs in the real world. Well‐controlled efficacy studies have demonstrated that many people with PD will adhere closely to exercise protocols under experimental but highly supervised and supported conditions, and people with PD derive clear motor and nonmotor benefit from these exercise interventions. 4 , 18 , 21 If a novel drug showed such benefit, within a decade or so, we would see high use throughout a patient population. Although we see some uptake in exercise as medicine for PD, it falls far short of widespread utilization. This is, in part, because what worked in clinical trials often does not work in the real world. The scientific challenge is to implement behavioral interventions that facilitate translating exercise from efficacy to effectiveness.

Financial Incentives Are Not Aligned to Support Prescription of Exercise as Medicine

In the United States' fee‐for‐service healthcare system, revenue is driven by volume of services provided. Additionally, most medical services react to an injury or disease (e.g., hip replacement for a broken hip or physical therapy after a fall). In such a system, there is no financial incentive to prescribe and implement exercise. This is because exercise keeps people healthy and healthy people generate little revenue. One way to align financial incentives with keeping people healthy is to have the healthcare system share the financial risk with the insurance company for both quality and total cost of care for each patient. In such an arrangement, every visit for reactive care (emergency room, etc.) is now an expense rather than revenue to the health system. In this scenario, financial incentives are now aligned to support investment in interventions that keep people healthy—like exercise.

Two of the authors (K.E.M. and D.M.C.) are currently working on deploying this financial model of care for people with PD at Intermountain Health. 39 In this program, called Parkinson's Elevated, the healthcare system (Intermountain) will assume shared responsibility with an insurance company (Select Health) for quality and total cost of care for each person with PD. Because financial and professional incentives are now aligned to keep people healthy, and because exercise for PD is a powerful tool not just to keep people healthy but also to possibly slow down the disease, it is advantageous for exercise to be deployed in this clinic.

Notably, to overcome the efficacy to effectiveness barrier for exercise described in the previous section, we are currently running an effectiveness trial examining different approaches to providing patients the support they need to implement the SPARX3 exercise prescription (high‐intensity aerobic exercise) in clinical practice. We will then deploy this intervention as part of Parkinson's Elevated. Over time, we will also incorporate the other elements of the Parkinson's Exercise Prescription, 14 and we look with eagerness toward scalable solutions being developed.

Personalized Exercise Coaching Is Costly and Demand Exceeds Supply

Personalized coaching is one way to ensure people with PD can effectively incorporate aerobic, resistance, flexibility, and neuromotor training into their lives. Yet, people with PD struggle to find a coach experienced in the Parkinson's Exercise Prescription, and many cannot afford such a service. Several companies now exist that are developing artificial intelligence (AI) “coaches” to help people exercise and to supplement physical therapy. One such AI “coach” will be used to operationalize the exercise and wellness program of the American Parkinson's Disease Association (https://www.apdaparkinson.org/download‐exercise‐guide/). Four major advantages of such programs are: (1) the fact that people with PD can access exercise coaching in their own homes, which is particularly important in remote and rural areas and for people who find travel difficult; (2) the cost is lower than that of many other coaching programs (the APDA program, e.g., will be free); (3) it will be possible for the coach to speak different languages, which will minimize a major barrier to access 40 ; and (4) the AI coach will be able to coach groups of people, which can increase motivation to participate. We look forward to seeing future studies examining whether AI coaching can be used as a tool to successfully execute the Parkinson's Exercise prescription. Assuming the efficacy of AI coaching is similar to traditional modes of exercise coaching, factors such as ease of access and the fact there will be a free coach available are important to consider in adoption.

Wearable Technologies Present Opportunities but Also Challenges

Increasing numbers of companies enable people to track the signs and symptoms of their disease, as well as their health, using body‐worn microsensors and other sensors. 41 There are now companies that provide support for monitoring the data of patients using such technologies. 42 The epidemiological data collected from sensors provides strong support for the health‐related benefits of activity. This feature is pivotal in a recent model of care for people with PD. 43

Despite the wonderful opportunity presented by this technology, it can also be a distraction. It remains to be seen how best to use wearables and AI in people with PD, and it will not be the same for every person. Although there are many potential benefits to wearable technologies, the excitement over wearables and the ability to so easily collect so much data can lead to solutions without problems and costs without benefits. There are real challenges in taking the best care of our patients that can be solved with wearables, but we urge companies, scientists, and clinicians to slow down, work with people with PD, and ensure they are not deploying a solution without a problem.

The Future: Key Research Questions of Interest

As we look toward the next decade and beyond, we propose a series of questions—the answers to which could delay the onset of PD symptoms, improve symptom management over time, and improve quality of life.

Can Exercise Delay the Time at Which PD Manifests and Build Resilience When It Does?

Currently, several exercise studies are underway that target at‐risk populations for PD. 44 Yet, unanswered questions remain. Does the intervention need to be implemented at high HR intensity, or is increased physical activity alone sufficient to delay conversion from at‐risk disorders to PD?

What are the best approaches to building resilience, motor and cognitive reserve, as people age so they have a buffer to maintain high health span in the presence of disease and aging? 45 This question is especially critical for people with early‐onset PD who may live with the disease for many decades. Ultimately, encouraging our patients to invest in their health is a way to build their “health retirement account.” The healthier they are, the more resistant they are to the negative consequences of disease.

What Are the Acute Benefits of Exercise on Motor and Nonmotor Systems?

Preliminary evidence suggests acute benefits on both motor and nonmotor symptoms of PD. 46 , 47 Future questions include elucidating which motor and nonmotor symptoms improve and how long these benefits last. In addition, can these acute benefits help motivate individuals to exercise regularly?

Can We Improve and Generalize the Exercise Prescription?

Current research suggests that aerobic exercise three times per week for 30 minutes continuously at a HR of 80% to 85% of maximum HR may slow disease progression. 14 Can interval training—in which bursts of high‐intensity HR activity are interspersed with periods of lower‐intensity HR—produce the same effect? Can exercise sessions be broken into smaller units (30 continuous minutes versus three sets of 10 minutes during the day) and achieve the same benefit?

Considering the entire Parkinson's Exercise prescription, what is the best combination of aerobic, resistance, flexibility, and neuromotor (balance/mobility) training for an individual with PD? To what extent does this depend on the stage of the disease? Can the Parkinson's Exercise prescription be effectively personalized to each individual? Some people with PD experience excessive fatigue and musculoskeletal injuries with the recommended exercise prescription. How should the prescription be structured for these individuals? It is also important to keep adverse events in mind, but their prevalence is low. 16

It is also important to note that exercise prescriptions in PD should be dosed based on a measured maximal HR from a cardiopulmonary exercise test. 48 , 49 , 50 This is because there is an increased prevalence of chronotropic incompetence (CI) in PD, 51 , 52 and maximal HR estimations can overestimate HR in people with PD. 49 , 51 Through a retrospective analysis of SPARX2 clinical trial data, our laboratory has found that by using a measured maximal HR for exercise prescriptions instead of an estimated maximal HR, people with PD and CI benefitted equally from high‐intensity endurance exercise. 53

What Is the Best Way to Combine the Main Treatments for PD?

Figure 1 lays out the four key treatments for PD. One of the most difficult challenges for people with PD is to identify the best way to combine these treatments. Does the timing of taking medications alter the effectiveness of the exercise session? What is the optimal medication strategy to mitigate unintended consequences of activity such as running‐induced dystonia? 54 Finally, so many studies have excluded people with PD who have DBS that we can only extrapolate existing evidence to people with DBS. 55 More studies are needed to ensure adequate exercise recommendations in the context of DBS.

How Can We Support Behavior Change to Sustain Physical Activity and Exercise?

We have now accumulated 40 years of powerful evidence that physical activity and exercise have positive effects on people with PD. We are in our infancy when it comes to understanding the tools of behavior change. What has become clear is that willpower alone is ineffective at facilitating change (https://www.nytimes.com/2025/12/28/opinion/willpower‐doesnt‐work‐this‐does.html). Instead of relying on willpower, how can we create environments in which healthy behaviors emerge? For example, being able to exercise at home or in retirement facilities makes physical activity and exercise more accessible, and therefore easier to do. Group classes create accountability. Exergaming may also provide an environment that promotes exercise. 56

To create environments that facilitate exercise and physical activity, we must better understand the barriers and facilitators to exercise from the point of view of people with PD. Medical anthropology can help us do this. Medical anthropology is a subfield of anthropology that examines how social, cultural, biological, and linguistic factors influence human health, illness experiences, and healthcare systems. Medical anthropology uses ethnographic fieldwork to understand communities and implement health programs. There is already overwhelming evidence for the therapeutic benefits of physical activity and exercise. There is now an urgent need for innovative medical anthropology studies that find out why uptake is so low and what can be done to help people with PD increase activity and exercise levels.

Can We Improve Monitoring of Exercise and Health?

There are several questions to be answered in this space. First, how do we optimize digital monitoring to help individuals monitor their own health? Second, how can movement disorders specialists track the health‐related activities of their patients, as well as monitor the signs and symptoms of the disease? Third, what are the best biomarkers to monitor? Finally, how do both biological and digital biomarkers change with exercise? 42 , 57

Conclusions

As illustrated in Figure 1, it is now clear that there are four treatments for PD: (1) exercise and physical activity, (2) general lifestyle modifications including avoiding harmful environmental toxins, 58 (3) medication, and (4) surgery and devices. Patients need help and support to find the best balance of these treatments as they navigate this complicated disease that affects all 13 organ systems. 59 Physicians and healthcare professionals need to more actively prescribe exercise as a treatment for PD. Researchers need to answer some key questions to further clarify the exercise prescription. A significant amount of future research, however, should not focus on documenting the already well‐established benefits of exercise, but rather on examining ways to ensure this treatment is actually used by the people who stand to benefit the most. We look forward with optimism to the improvements in health care we foresee for people with PD.
