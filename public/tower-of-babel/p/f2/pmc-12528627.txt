Pan-cancer analysis of INHBA indicates its potential prognostic value and correlation with HNSC and LIHC
Authors: Shumin Li, Beibei Xu, Guanghui Wang, Luan Xu, Liya Ding, Xiaopei Wu, Baoying Zheng, Yuefeng Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundINHBA is a member of the TGF-β superfamily and is overexpressed in a variety of cancers and affects prognosis.However, its pan-cancer expression profile and specific roles in HNSC and LIHC remain poorly understood.MethodsWe used several databases such as TCGA to study the pan-cancer effects of INHBA, including the expression of INHBA and its correlation with clinical survival, immune checkpoints, tumor stemness scores, prognostic value, immunomodulators, genomic profiles, immune profiling, immunotherapy and functional enrichment; and further analyzed its relationship with HNSC and LIHC.ResultsFirst, we observed that the expression level of INHBA was elevated in most tumor tissues compared to non-tumor tissues, and that high expression of INHBA may have a higher prognostic value in some cancers. In addition, INHBA was significantly associated with immune checkpoints, immunomodulators, prognosis, immunomodulatory genes, tumor stemness score, cellular functional status, and immune infiltration in most tumors. Finally, further analysis of INHBA-associated gene enrichment, mutation sites and types, RNA modifications, and genomic heterogeneity showed that the main mutation types of INHBA were missense mutations and that INHBA played a very important role in HNSC and LIHC; and immunotherapy analysis also suggested that low expression of INHBA might result in better specific immunotherapy response.ConclusionThis study comprehensively reveals the important functions of INHBA in different types of cancer. It also has the potential to identify INHBA as a potential biomarker for predicting treatment response. This may help us better understand how INHBA works in cancer and provide valuable insights for developing personalized treatments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03505-z.Keywords: Immunotherapy, Pan-cancer, INHBA, HNSC, LIHC, Prognosis

Introduction

Cancer is one of the leading causes of death in humans. Because it is difficult to detect in the early stages, many patients have limited treatment options after diagnosis and poor prognosis [1]. According to statistics, by 2024, there will be 2,001,140 new cancer cases and 611,720 cancer deaths in the United States alone [2]. It is estimated that by 2050, the cancer prevalence will increase by 49% compared to 2015 [3]. Current cancer treatments include surgical resection, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and systemic therapy. With the rapid development of technologies such as high-throughput sequencing, we can discover new promising targets from public databases such as TCGA [4, 5].

INHBA is a member of the TGF-β family. Currently known TGF-β proteins are closely related to tissue morphology, cell and tissue differentiation, and many disease states [6, 7]. INHBA forms a homodimer called activin A, which plays a role in a variety of biological processes [8]. It has been reported that it can play a role in the hypothalamus-pituitary-gonadal axis [9]. It has been reported that INHBA affects the proliferation of lung cancer cells [10]. INHBA upregulation has been shown to affect the proliferation, migration, and invasion of colon cancer cells [11]. INHBA has also been reported to be associated with immune infiltration of cervical cancer [12]. It can be seen that INHBA is involved in the development of cancer. While INHBA has been implicated in multiple cancer types, its pan-cancer prognostic value and immune regulatory mechanisms remain underexplored. And no specific studies have yet investigated the role of INHBA in head and neck tumors or cancer.

In this study, we performed a pan-cancer analysis of INHBA using the TCGA/GTEx database and evaluated the expression and prognosis of INHBA in normal and tumor tissues in various tumors. In addition, to reveal its mechanism of action, we also performed immunomodulatory gene, immune checkpoint and tumor stemness score analysis, and performed KEGG and GO correlation analysis in HNSC and LIHC to explore the possible mechanism of action of INHBA.

Methods

INHBA expression analysis

The HPA database (Supplementary Table S1; https://www.proteinatlas.org) was used to study the INHBA mRNA expression level in normal human tissues, immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence in normal or tumor tissues. The TIMER2.0 website (Supplementary Table S1; http://timer.cistrome.org/) was used to obtain the expression level of INHBA gene in various cancer tissues. The expression data of INHBA in normal and tumor samples from the TCGA (Supplementary Table S1; http://cancergenome.nih.gov) and GTEx (Supplementary Table S1; http://commonfund.nih.gov/GTEx/) databases were obtained, the abbreviations of 33 types of tumors are shown in Table 1.

Pathological stage analysis

We used the Gene Expression Analysis Interactive Analysis Database (Supplementary Table S1; GEPIA2; http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) to analyze the correlation between INHBA expression and tumor stage. The pan-cancer dataset TCGA Pan-Cancer (PANCAN, N = 10,535, G = 60,499) was obtained from the UCSC database (Supplementary Table S1; https://xenabrowser.net/). The expression data of the INHBA gene were extracted from each sample, and samples with an expression level of 0 were excluded. Subsequently, log2(x 0.001) transformation was applied to each expression value. To ensure stability, cancer types with less than 3 samples of each type were removed, resulting in expression data for 37 cancer types. Differential expression analysis of genes in different clinical stages within each tumor was performed using R software (version 4.4.2). Pairwise significance analysis of differences was performed using unpaired Student’s t-test, while analysis of variance was used to test differences for multiple sample groups.

Prognostic analysis

We used GEPIA2 to obtain OS (overall survival) and DFS (disease-free survival) significance map data for all TCGA tumors and survival plots for INHBA. Cut-off high (50%) and critical low (50%) values were used as expression thresholds to distinguish high- and low-expression cohorts. Log-rank test was used for hypothesis testing. We obtained the pan-cancer dataset TCGA Pan-Cancer from the UCSC database. Expression data of the INHBA gene were extracted from each sample. Samples with zero expression levels and samples with a follow-up time of less than 30 days were excluded. Subsequently, we applied log2 transformation to each expression value and merged pseudo counts of 0.001. In addition, cancer types with less than 10 samples per type were removed from the analysis. This preprocessing step generated expression data for 39 cancer types, as well as the corresponding overall survival data of the samples.

Analysis of Immunomodulatory genes, immune checkpoints, and tumor stemness scores

The relationship between gene expression and five immune pathway markers was analyzed using the UCSC database, MuTect2 software, and R software (version 4.4.2). The expression data of 60 marker genes of the INHBA gene and two immune checkpoint pathway genes extracted from the Pan-Cancer dataset downloaded from the UCSC database were calculated for Pearson correlation. The INHBA gene extracted from the Pan-Cancer dataset downloaded from the UCSC database and DNAs calculated by the methylation profile of each tumor were used for tumor stemness scores.

Immune infiltration analysis

We extracted the expression data of the INHBA gene in each sample from the Pan-Cancer dataset downloaded from the UCSC database, extracted the gene expression profile of each tumor, mapped the expression profile to Gene Symbol, and further used the R package ESTIMATE (version 1.0.13). The stroma, immune, and ESTIMATE scores of each patient in each tumor were calculated based on gene expression. Finally, we obtained the immune infiltration score. The Pearson correlation between the gene and immune infiltration score in each tumor was calculated using the corr.test function of the R package psych (version 2.0.9) coefficient to determine the significant correlation of the immune infiltration score. The TIMER database was used to evaluate the degree of immune cell infiltration in 32 cancers.

INHBA-related gene analysis

STRING (Supplementary Table S1; https://cn.string-db.org/) was used to retrieve available experimentally identified INHBA-binding proteins (INHBA; homo sapiens); A combined score > 0.4 was considered statistically significant. The PPI network of the results was depicted using Cytoscape 3.10.3 software. The top 10 hub genes were determined using the cytoHubba plug-in based on the Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC) algorithms. We screened the top 100 INHBA-related target genes using the “Similar Gene Detection” module of GEPIA2. Then, we used the TIMER2.0 website to obtain the expression levels of the top 10 target genes in each tumor and displayed them in the form of a heat map. For gene set enrichment analysis, we first downloaded the c5.go.bp.v7.4.symbols.gmt subset from the Molecular Signatures Database (DOI: 10.1093/bioinformatics/btr260, GSEA | Login) to serve as the background. We mapped the genes to this background set and performed enrichment analysis using the R package clusterProfiler (version 3.14.3) to obtain the results of gene set enrichment. The minimum gene set size was set to 5 and the maximum to 5000, with P values less than 0.01 and a FDR of < 0.1 considered statistically significant.

Analysis of genetic alterations

The cBioPortal tool (Supplementary Table S1; http://www.cbioportal.org/) was used to analyze the mutational signatures of INHBA in different cancers. The cBioPortal was further used to analyze the types and frequencies of INHBA gene mutations in all tumors. We used the UCSC database to extract the expression data of 44 marker genes for the INHBA gene and three RNA modification genes (m1A (10), m5C (13), and m6A (21)) in each sample, and performed log2 (x 0.001) on each expression value. Pearson correlations were calculated between INHBA and five immune pathway markers.

Function and enrichment analysis

The correlation between INHBA and the functional status of 14 cancers was analyzed using single-cell sequence data from the CancerSEA website (Supplementary Table S1; http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.cn/CancerSEA/home.jsp). Gene co-expression analysis of INHBA was performed using the LinkedOmics website (Supplementary Table S1; https://www.linkedomics.org/login.php). The Pearson test was used to detect the correlation between INHBA and co-expressed genes.

Immunotherapy analysis

Kmplot (Supplementary Table S1; https://kmplot.com/analysis/) was used to analyze the association between immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy and INHBA. TISMO website (Supplementary Table S1; http://cis.hku.hk/TISIDB/index.php) was used to analyze the association between ICB therapy or cytokine therapy and INHBA.

Statistics analysis

All analyses were performed using R (v4.3.1). INHBA differential expression (tumor vs. normal) was assessed by Wilcoxon test. Survival (OS/PFI) was analyzed via Kaplan-Meier/log-rank tests with median INHBA expression stratification, and Cox regression calculated HRs (95% CIs). Spearman’s correlation (FDR-corrected) evaluated INHBA-immune relationships. PPI networks (STRING, score ≥ 0.7) and hub genes (cytoHubba/MCC in Cytoscape v3.9.1) were analyzed. GO/KEGG enrichment (clusterProfiler v3.14.3, adj. p < 0.05) and visualizations (ggplot2/pheatmap) were performed.

Results

INHBA is upregulated in multiple human cancers

To explore the potential role of INHBA in the occurrence and development of different cancers, we conducted differential analysis of gene expression levels, which laid the foundation for further investigation of its relationship with patient prognosis. We first evaluated the mRNA level of INHBA in normal human tissues and found that it is expressed in most tissues. It is mainly expressed in tissues such as gallbladder, endometrium, lung, placenta, lung, bone marrow, bladder, liver, salivary gland, myocardium, appendix, testis, ovary, cerebral cortex, tongue, smooth muscle, epididymis, prostate and kidney (Fig. 1A). We further evaluated the expression of INHBA in various cancers. The expression of INHBA between tumor tissues and normal tissues was studied from the TCGA and GTEx databases. INHBA was found We observed significantly elevated mRNA expression in 20 tumor types, including GBM, GBMLGG, LGG, BRCA, CESC, ESCA, STES, KIPAN, COAD, COADREAD, PRAD, STAD, HNSC, and KIRC, BLCA, THCA, READ, PAAD, LAML, and CHOL. We observed that INHBA was significantly downregulated in 8 tumor cells, including LUAD, KIRP, LIHC, SKCM, UCS, ALL, ACC, and KICH (Fig. 1B-C).

High expression of INHBA in some cancers May have lower prognostic value

Subsequently, we meticulously examined the relationship between INHBA gene expression and patient prognosis, aiming to determine whether INHBA could potentially serve as a prognostic biomarker. We investigated the correlation between INHBA expression and clinical pathological stages across various cancer types and discovered that in certain tumors, including BLCA, ACC, ESCA, KIRC, KIRP, LUAD, STAD, and THCA, INHBA expression exhibited a significant positive association with the pathological stage (Fig. 2A-H). Utilizing the UCSC database, we conducted a subsequent analysis of INHBA expression across different pathological stages, which revealed a significant positive correlation between INHBA expression and pathological stage in specific cancers, such as COAD, COADREAD, BRCA, ESCA, STES, KIRP, KIPAN, STAD, PRAD, KIRC, THCA, READ, and BLCA (Fig. 2I). These findings indicate that INHBA expression may be a critical factor in the progression of certain cancers, with its level being positively correlated with the pathological stage. This suggests its potential as a biomarker for cancer progression and offers a novel direction for targeted therapeutic research. In addition, comparison of the INHBA high-expression cohort and low-expression cohort showed that in LUSC and MESO tumor types, increased CLDN3 expression was associated with decreased overall survival (OS) (Fig. 3A), and decreased CLDN3 expression was associated with decreased overall survival (OS) in BLCA, CESC, COAD, and HNSC tumors. At the same time, in BLCA, increased CLDN3 expression was associated with decreased disease-free survival (DFS), and in KIRP, MESO, STAD, and ACC tumor types, decreased INHBA expression was associated with decreased disease-free survival (DFS) (Fig. 3A-N). Subsequently, we used the UCSC database and found that high expression was associated with poor prognosis in 11 tumor types (TARGET-LAML, TCGA-CESC, TCGA-KIRP, TCGA-KIPAN, TCGA-STAD, TCGA-HNSC, TCGA-BLCA, TCGA-MESO, TCGA-PAAD, TCGA-ACC, TARGET-ALL-R) (Fig. 3O).

Association between INHBA expression and Immunomodulatory genes

Moreover, in light of the pivotal role of the tumor immune microenvironment in the initiation and progression of cancer, we further investigated the correlation between INHBA gene expression and immune cell infiltration. We investigated the immunomodulatory effect of INHBA in tumors by analyzing the correlation between immunomodulatory genes, immune checkpoints, tumor stemness score, immune infiltration, and INHBA. First, we downloaded the standardized pan-cancer dataset from the UCSC (https://xenabrowser.net/) database: TCGA TARGET GTEx (PANCAN, N = 19131, G = 60499). We further extracted the expression data of the ENSG00000122641 (INHBA) gene and 150 marker genes of five immune pathways (chemokine (41), receptor (18), MHC (21), Immunoinhibitor (24), Immunostimulator (46)) in each sample, and calculated the Pearson correlation between INHBA and marker genes of five immune pathways. The results showed that the expression of INHBA was positively correlated with most immunomodulatory genes (Fig. 4A). Then, we analyzed the correlation between INHBA and certain immune checkpoints. We downloaded the standardized pan-cancer dataset from the UCSC database (https://xenabrowser.net/): TCGA TARGET GTEx (PANCAN, N = 19131, G = 60499), and further extracted the INHBA gene and 60 two-category immune checkpoint pathway genes (Inhibitory (24), Stimulatory (36)). We performed Pearson correlation analysis between INHBA and marker genes of five immune pathways and found that there was a significant correlation between INHBA and most immune checkpoints (Fig. 4B). In addition, we found that the CLDN3 gene was significantly correlated with the tumor stemness score in 14 tumors, of which there was a significant positive correlation in 4 tumors: KIPAN, KIRC, THYMh and THCA, and a significant negative correlation in 10 tumors: COAD, COADREAD, STES, STAD, HNSC, LUSC, TGCT, SKCM and BLCA (Fig. 4C).

Characterization of INHBA mutations in the TCGA Pan-Cancer cohort

Lastly, through the analysis of INHBA gene mutations, we gained further insights into the mutational profiles of this gene across diverse cancers. To investigate whether INHBA causes cancer through gene mutation, we analyzed the mutation sites and types, RNA modifications, and genomic heterogeneity of INHBA mutations in pan-cancer. There were 24 cancer types with INHBA alterations, with Skin Cutaneous Melanoma having the highest frequency of 6.0% (Fig. 7A). The results show the types, loci, and number of cases of INHBA gene mutations. The main form of gene mutation is missense mutation in INHBA (Fig. 7B). To better understand the role of INHBA in tumors, we analyzed genomic heterogeneity and INHBA gene expression: We observed TMB (tumor mutation burden) and INHBA gene expression data in 37 tumors, and observed significant correlations in 5 tumors, including significant positive correlations in 2 tumors, such as THYM and OV, and significant negative correlations in 3 tumors, such as KIRP, HNSC, and CHOL (Fig. 7C). To gain a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms behind the effects of INHBA on cancer, we further analyzed the expression of INHBA genes and 44 marker genes for three types of RNA modifications (m1A (10), m5C (13), and m6A (21)) in each sample (Fig. 7D). The above studies on the INHBA gene have enhanced our understanding of the molecular processes of cancer and proposed new strategies for treating this disease.

INHBA immunotherapy analysis

In order to assess the potential of INHBA as a therapeutic target for immunotherapy in tumor treatment, we examined the overall survival of patients stratified by high or low INHBA expression following treatment with anti-PD1, anti-PDL1, or anti-CTLA4 agents. The analysis revealed that patients with elevated INHBA expression exhibited significantly improved overall survival compared to those with low INHBA expression (Fig. 10A). Specifically, patients undergoing immunotherapy with Nivolumab alone (Fig. 10C), any anti-PD-L1 agent (Fig. 10E), Atezolizumab alone (Fig. 10F), or any anti-CTLA-4 agent, including Ipilimumab alone, demonstrated significantly better overall survival relative to those with low INHBA expression. Conversely, patients treated with anti-PD1 alone (Fig. 10B) or Pembrolizumab alone (Fig. 10D) and having high INHBA expression did not show enhanced overall survival compared to those with low INHBA expression. These findings suggest that high INHBA expression may correlate with enhanced responsiveness to certain immunotherapies, such as anti-PDL1 or anti-CTLA4. This insight holds significant implications for personalized medicine, as it implies that by assessing a patient’s INHBA expression level, clinicians can more precisely tailor immunotherapy regimens, thereby potentially improving survival rates and therapeutic efficacy.

Discussion

Cancer is the leading cause of human death, with some studies estimating that the global cancer burden will increase by 47% by 2040 compared to 2020, with about 28.4 million cases [13]. Due to the uncontrolled growth and metastasis of cancer cells, cancer still causes a high number of deaths despite the emergence of new treatments, and cancer remains a major public security problem worldwide. It is one of the most convenient, safe, effective and low-cost methods to mine new biomarkers to improve the accuracy of early diagnosis of cancer and improve the survival rate of cancer patients using public databases. Previous studies have confirmed that INHBA is highly expressed in a variety of cancers and may be a potential prognostic biomarker [14]. It has also been reported to play a tumor suppressor role in colon cancer and breast cancer [15, 16]. However, there have been no studies to conduct a dedicated pan-cancer analysis of INHBA and explore its potential mechanism of action in human cancers. Therefore, we first used TCGA and GTEx databases to analyze the genetic differences of INHBA to explore its potential mechanism of action in human cancer. The results showed that INHBA was highly expressed in a variety of cancer tissues, which is consistent with previous reported results [17, 18], while significant downregulation of INHBA was observed in tumor cells of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), melanoma (SKCM), uterine sarcoma (UCS), leukemia (ALL), adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), and renal chromophobe carcinoma (KICH). In order to further study the impact of this phenomenon, we analyzed the pathological stage and prognosis, and the results showed that in specific tumor types, the expression of INHBA was positively correlated with the clinicopathological stage, and patients with high expression of INHBA had a lower prognosis, indicating the potential of INHBA as a biomarker of cancer progression, providing a new research direction for targeted therapy.

The immune defense system is the most important part of the body’s role in fighting foreign invaders, and immune checkpoint inhibitors have been widely studied. However, not all tumors will have a good effect after the application of inhibitors such as anti-PD1 [19], so we need to find new immunotherapy targets. INHBA has been reported to exert anti-tumor immune effects in cervical cancer [20] and can induce a decrease in PD-L1 expression through IFN-γ [21]. To investigate whether INHBA has a potential role in immunotherapy, we analyzed the correlation between immunoregulatory genes, immune checkpoints, tumor stemness scores, immune infiltration and INHBA, and found that INHBA was correlated with immunoregulatory genes and immune checkpoints, studies have reported that INHBA exerts antitumor immune effects by inhibiting the IFN-γ signaling pathway [21]and INHBA also plays a role in antitumor immunity in cervical cancer [20]. Most importantly, patients with high expression of INHBA showed better survival when receiving immunotherapy. At the same time, we analyzed the role of INHBA in HNSC and LIHC cancers and found through KEGG and GO analysis that INHBA was mainly related to focal adhesion, extracellular matrix and growth factor binding.

INHBA is a member of the TGF-β family and encodes a subunit of activin and inhibin [20],Most TGF-β family proteins are related to tissue morphology, cell differentiation and disease status [22]. It has been reported that INHBA was involved in the development of various malignant tumors. Some studies have reported that INHBA is an independent prognostic predictor in gastric cancer [23, 24]. Seder et al. found that upregulation of INHBA can promote cell proliferation and affect the survival rate of lung adenocarcinoma [25]. There are also studies reporting that INHBA is a promising target for CRC immunotherapy [26]. Similar to pan-cancer studies on TGF-β family members [27], our findings highlight INHBA as a context-dependent regulator, but uniquely implicate its role in immune checkpoint modulation.

Chemotherapeutic agents primarily function by inhibiting rapid cell division, a hallmark of cancer cells. However, this mechanism indiscriminately targets other rapidly proliferating normal cells, such as those in hair follicles, bone marrow, and the gastrointestinal tract, thereby inducing the characteristic adverse effects associated with chemotherapy [28]. The nonspecific cytotoxicity of conventional chemotherapy drugs and the emergence of multidrug resistance highlight the necessity for novel, targeted therapies that capitalize on the molecular alterations in tumor cells. To explore the potential of INHBA as an immunotherapy target in oncology, we evaluated the overall survival of patients with varying INHBA expression levels (high or low) following treatment with anti-PD1, anti-PDL1, or anti-LACT4 agents. Our findings indicated that patients with high INHBA expression exhibited significantly reduced overall survival following anti-PD1 therapy compared to those with low INHBA expression. Collectively, patients with elevated INHBA expression demonstrated a more favorable prognosis. Consistent with emerging pan-cancer paradigms [29, 30], our data establish INHBA as both a prognostic biomarker and immunotherapy predictor, patients with elevated INHBA expression demonstrated a more favorable prognosis.

In summary, through a comprehensive bioinformatics approach, this study examined the expression patterns, diagnostic and prognostic potential, genetic mutations, protein methylation status, immunomodulatory effects, and associated signaling pathways of INHBA across multiple cancer types. The results revealed that INHBA is overexpressed and holds diagnostic value in various cancers. Moreover, INHBA may serve as a potential prognostic and immune-related biomarker for cancer patients. Although our investigation is limited to bioinformatics analyses without validation through in vivo or in vitro experiments, this study elucidates the multifaceted role of INHBA in tumorigenesis and provides a foundation for further research into the specific mechanisms underlying INHBA’s involvement in cancer progression and treatment Fig. 11.

Limitation

Despite the comprehensive bioinformatics analysis employed in this study to elucidate the expression profile, diagnostic and prognostic potential, and immune regulatory role of INHBA in pan-cancer, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our findings are solely based on bioinformatics analysis without experimental validation through in vivo or in vitro studies, which precludes direct confirmation of the biological functions of INHBA in tumorigenesis. Second, the data utilized were derived from public databases, potentially introducing biases or limitations in sample selection that may affect the generalizability of the results. Concurrently, the inconsistent mechanisms of INHBA in the prognosis of immunosuppressive agents remain to be elucidated, necessitating further in-depth mechanistic investigations. Additionally, the analysis of INHBA-related signaling pathways was primarily based on computational predictions, lacking experimental evidence to support their specific roles in various cancer types.
