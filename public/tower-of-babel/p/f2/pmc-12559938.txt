Intermittent MDMA Attenuates Ovariectomy-induced Bone Loss via a Gut Microbiota–Bone Axis
Authors: Xiayun Wan, Akifumi Eguchi, Rumi Murayama, Chisato Mori, Kenji Hashimoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective3,4-Methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA) is widely used recreationally but also modulates serotonergic signaling in the gut, potentially influencing the microbiota. Because low bone mineral density (BMD) is common in depression and other psychiatric disorders, we tested whether repeated, intermittent MDMA attenuates BMD loss in ovariectomized (OVX) mice.MethodsOVX mice received MDMA (10 mg/kg) three times per week for six weeks. BMD was measured, and untargeted metabolomics of plasma samples along with gut microbiota profiling of fecal samples were performed.ResultsCompared with vehicle, MDMA increased whole-body and femoral BMD and shifted circulating bone-remodeling markers toward an antiresorptive profile—lower receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) and higher osteoprotegerin. Gut microbiota profiling and untargeted metabolomics showed reduced Clostridia and enrichment of Bacilli, along with a marked decrease in plasma β-D-allose, a metabolite linked to Lactobacillus johnsonii.ConclusionThese findings suggest that intermittent MDMA may mitigate OVX-induced BMD loss in association with remodeling of a gut microbiota–bone axis. Future studies should define causal microbial and metabolic mediators and evaluate generalizability across additional bone-loss models.

INTRODUCTION

The entactogen 3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA; “ecstasy”) is widely used as a recreational drug [1,2]. Acute adverse effects include hyperthermia, dehydration, serotonin syndrome, cardiovascular complications, and neurotoxicity, which in severe cases can lead to seizures or death. Chronic use has been linked to long-term neurochemical alterations—particularly serotonin depletion—potentially contributing to cognitive impairment, mood disturbance, memory deficits, and heightened anxiety [3,4]. At the same time, observational studies of U.S. adults report that lifetime MDMA use is associated with lower risks of depression, psychological distress, and suicidal ideation, suggesting enhanced psychological resilience [5-7]; a complementary epidemiological analysis reached similar conclusions [8]. Experimental work further indicates that MDMA can modulate socio-emotional processing by facilitating early detection of salient social cues [9] and amplifying positive affective responses to feedback [10]. In a cohort of survivors from the Nova music festival attack in Re’im, Israel, MDMA users reported less psychological distress and milder post-traumatic stress disorder symptoms following the Hamas assault [11]. Together, these findings underscore a complex risk–benefit profile and motivate mechanistic investigation.

The serotonin system is central to MDMA’s pharmacology, and > 90% of the body’s serotonin resides in the gastrointestinal tract [12-15]. We recently showed that repeated MDMA dosing enhances resilience to chronic social defeat and restraint stress in rodents via a vagus nerve–mediated gut–brain axis [16,17]. In cuprizone-treated mice, repeated MDMA administration reduced brain demyelination through gut–brain signaling [18], and oral MDMA altered gut microbiota composition across intestinal regions, implicating the microbiome in its systemic effects [19]. Consistent with this, several studies indicate that gut microbes modulate MDMA-induced hyperthermia in rodents [20-22]. Collectively, these data suggest that repeated MDMA reshapes the gut microbiota with broad physiological consequences.

Ovariectomized (OVX) mice are a well-established model of postmenopausal bone loss and osteoporosis, recapitulating the decline in bone mineral density (BMD) observed after menopause [23-25]. Dysbiosis has been linked to impaired bone health through altered immune signaling, disrupted calcium and vitamin D absorption, and changes in microbial metabolites—especially short-chain fatty acids that regulate bone resorption and formation [26-28]. We previously demonstrated that modulation of the gut microbiome and its metabolites contributes to the bone-protective effects of the novel antidepressant arketamine in OVX and chronic-stress models [29,30].

Despite growing interest in MDMA’s systemic actions, its impact on bone health remains unexplored. Here, we tested whether repeated, intermittent MDMA administration mitigates OVX-induced BMD loss via alterations in a gut–microbiota–bone axis. We first assessed BMD and plasma levels of receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL) and osteoprotegerin (OPG)—key regulators of bone turnover—in MDMA- versus vehicle-treated OVX mice [31,32]. We then characterized MDMA-associated shifts in gut microbial communities and performed untargeted plasma metabolomics, integrating these datasets through network analysis to identify microbial–metabolite pathways potentially linked to bone outcomes.

METHODS

Animals

We obtained female C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old, 18−22 g) from Japan SLC Inc. (Hamamatsu). The mice were housed in translucent polycarbonate cages (21 × 30 × 22.5 cm) with 4 to 5 mice per cage. They were kept under controlled conditions with a 12-hour light/dark cycle (lights on from 7:00 AM to 7:00 PM), a stable room temperature of 23 ± 1°C, and a relative humidity of 55 ± 5%. The mice had free access to food and water.

All experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Chiba University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Approval number 5-331). Deep anesthesia was induced using inhaled isoflurane, and the mice were euthanized promptly by cervical dislocation, performed with proficient techniques. Efforts were made to minimize animal suffering throughout the study.

Ovariectomy, MDMA Treatment, and Collection of Samples

In the conducted experiments, 40 female mice were subjected to isoflurane-induced anesthesia and underwent either ovariectomy (OVX) or sham surgery on day 0, as previously described (Fig. 1A) [29,33]. After anesthesia, the hair on the dorsal region below the rib cage was shaved, and the surgical site was sterilized with betadine. A 1−2 cm incision was made through the skin and muscle layer below the rib on one side to access the ovary. The fallopian tube was ligated, and the ovary was excised. The muscle layer and skin were then sutured and disinfected, and the procedure was repeated on the opposite side. Mice in the sham-operated group underwent the same procedure, except that the ovaries were not removed, and no ligation was performed.

MDMA hydrochloride was synthesized by one of the authors (K.H.) as previously reported [18,19,34]. MDMA (10 mg/kg/day) or water (10 ml/kg/day) was administered via oral injection three times per week over six weeks (Fig. 1A), consistent with our prior OVX studies in which BMD was assessed 6 weeks after surgery [29,30]. On the afternoon of day 43, femur samples were harvested and preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C overnight for subsequent BMD assessments (Fig. 1A). Concurrently, plasma samples were collected and stored at −80°C for enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) analysis. On the morning of day 44, the femur samples were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline, sequentially immersed in 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% ethanol, and stored at 4°C until BMD measurement, as previously described [29,30].

Measurement of Bone Mineral Density

The BMD of the isolated femoral neck was measured and analyzed using the LaTheta LCT-200 scanner (Hitachi Aloka Medical) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, as previously described [29-33].

Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay for Inflammatory Bone Markers

Plasma levels of RANKL (catalog number: MTR00, R&D Systems) and OPG (catalog number: MOP00, R&D Systems) were measured in MDMA- and vehicle-treated OVX mice using ELISA kits and quantified according to the manufacturer’s instructions [31,32].

16S rRNA Sequencing Analysis

Total DNA was extracted from mouse fecal samples using the NucleoSpin DNA Fecal Kit (REF: 740472.50, MACHEREY-NAGEL) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The subsequent experimental workflow was carried out by BGI Japan. The V3-V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using universal bacterial primers. All PCR products were purified using Agencourt AMPure XP beads, dissolved in Elution Buffer, and labeled to complete library construction. The size and concentration of the libraries were evaluated using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and qualified libraries were sequenced on the DNB platform based on their insert size. The sequencing data have been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information Sequence Read Archive under the accession number PRJNA1163759.

Using the OTU profile table and taxonomic annotations, we assessed within-sample (α) diversity to estimate gut microbiota richness and evenness through observed species, Chao, abundance-based coverage estimator (ACE), and Shannon indices. β-diversity was analyzed using the weighted UniFrac distance metric and visualized with principal coordinate analysis to examine variations in microbial communities across samples. The Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size algorithm was used to identify biomarkers in different sample groups and to determine the effect size of each biomarker [35].

Non-targeted Metabolomics Profiling of Plasma Samples

Non-targeted metabolomics profiles derived from plasma samples were analyzed using ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry, as previously described [29,30,36]. Metabolomics data analysis was performed using the R statistical environment (version 4.4.1) and Mass Spectrometry-Data Independent Analysis software (version 5.3) [37]. Metabolites were identified in at least 50% of the analyzed samples, with a coefficient of variation of 30% in pooled quality control samples, and were annotated according to level 2 standards as proposed by Schymanski et al. [38].

Statistical Analysis

Differences between two groups were assessed using either a two-tailed Student’s ttest or the Mann-Whitney Utest. For comparisons among multiple groups, we used two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test and the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test followed by appropriate post-hoc analysis. Repeated measures data were analyzed using repeated measures ANOVA. Variations in β-diversity were evaluated using analysis of similarity (ANOSIM). Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was employed to analyze correlations between variables. These analyses were prespecified as hypothesis-generating and were therefore conducted without multiplicity adjustment; results are reported qualitatively and are not used to infer mechanistic causality. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Effects of MDMA on Reduced BMD in OVX Mice

All groups of mice showed significant body weight gain. From the second week to the sixth week post-surgery, the body weight of the OVX + vehicle group was significantly higher than that of the sham + vehicle and sham + MDMA groups (Fig. 1B). To evaluate the effects of MDMA on reduced BMD in OVX mice, we measured the BMD of the femoral neck in all groups using microCT. Compared to the sham + vehicle and sham + MDMA groups, the cortical BMD, cancellous BMD, and total BMD were significantly lower in the OVX + vehicle group, consistent with our previous findings [29,33]. Notably, repeated intermittent MDMA administration significantly mitigated the reduction in BMD observed in OVX mice (Fig. 1C). In contrast, there were no significant differences in cortical, cancellous, or total BMD between the sham + vehicle and sham + MDMA groups. Additionally, planar BMD did not show significant variation among the four experimental groups (Fig. 1C). These findings indicate a protective effect of MDMA against BMD reduction in OVX mice. Representative CT images of femurs from each group are presented in Figure 1D.

The RANK/RANKL/OPG Axis Is Critically Involved in the Beneficial Effects of MDMA on BMD Reduction in OVX Mice

Given the inhibitory effect of OPG on RANKL–RANK signaling, which is crucial for osteoclast differentiation, we measured OPG and RANKL levels in plasma samples from the OVX + vehicle and OVX + MDMA groups. A significant difference in OPG levels was observed between the two groups (p = 0.011) (Fig. 2A). Plasma levels of RANKL were significantly lower in OVX mice treated with MDMA compared to those treated with vehicle (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2A). The unpaired Student’s ttest of the OPG/RANKL ratio showed a significant increase in the OVX + MDMA group compared to the OVX + vehicle group (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2A). Additionally, Spearman correlation analysis revealed positive correlations between the OPG/RANKL ratio and all BMD measurements—cortical, cancellous, and total BMD (Fig. 2B). These findings suggest that the RANK/RANKL/OPG system may play a role in the beneficial effects of MDMA on reduced BMD in OVX mice.

Composition of Gut Microbiota

Examination of α-diversity indices across all 40 fecal samples revealed significant differences in bacterial diversity among the four groups (Kruskal-Wallis test with Benjamini, Krieger, and Yekutieli two-step linear step-up procedure; observed species: p = 0.0002, Chao: p = 0.0002, ACE: p = 0.0004, Shannon: p = 0.0085) (Fig. 2C). The sham + MDMA group and OVX + vehicle group showed a greater increase in the observed species, Chao index, and ACE index compared to the sham + vehicle group. Conversely, the observed species in the OVX + MDMA group were significantly lower than in the OVX + vehicle group (Fig. 2C).

Microbial community structure analysis using weighted UniFrac distances indicated distinct clustering of bacterial communities across the groups, with significant differences observed between sham + vehicle and sham + MDMA, sham + MDMA and OVX + MDMA, and OVX + vehicle and OVX + MDMA (Fig. 2D). In contrast, no significant clustering difference was observed between the sham + vehicle and OVX + vehicle groups (Fig. 2D). This pattern was confirmed by ANOSIM with weighted UniFrac distances, suggesting that MDMA treatment or the combination of OVX and MDMA accounted for substantial variation in bacterial community composition. These findings indicate that MDMA and OVX combined with MDMA significantly influence gut microbiota composition.

Clostridia was the most predominant class in the sham + vehicle group (relative abundance [RA]: 53.8%), sham + MDMA group (RA: 34.4%), and OVX + vehicle group (RA: 55.6%). However, a significant depletion of Clostridia was observed in the OVX + MDMA group, with an RA of 28.3% (Fig. 3A). In contrast, the most abundant class in the OVX + MDMA group was Erysipelotrichia, with an RA of 29.4%, which was markedly lower in the other three groups (Fig. 3A). The sham + MDMA group showed a higher RA of taxa belonging to the class Bacilli compared to the sham + vehicle group (sham + vehicle: 8.3%, sham + MDMA: 18.5%), and a similar pattern was observed when comparing the OVX + vehicle group to the OVX + MDMA group (OVX + vehicle: 3.3%, OVX + MDMA: 12.2%) (Fig. 3A). These findings suggest that MDMA plays a significant role in reshaping the bacterial community composition.

Microbiome shifts were evident when comparing the sham + vehicle group with the sham + MDMA group and the OVX + vehicle group, revealing distinct microbial profiles across groups. Elevated species in the sham + vehicle and OVX + vehicle groups included Acinetobacter johnsonii, Anaerostipes hadrus, Bifidobacterium catenulatum, Dialister hominis, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Gemmiger formicilis, Lachnospira eligens, Marvinbryantia formatexigens, Megamonas funiformis, Mitsuokella multacida, Phascolarctobacterium faecium, Phocaeicola vulgatus, Roseburia inulinivorans, Segatella copri, while Lactobacillus intestinalis was significantly depleted (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3B). We also noted that the elevation of 12 species, including A. hadrus, B. catenulatum, D. hominis, F. prausnitzii, G. formicilis, L. eligens, M. formatexigens, M. funiformis, M. multacida, P. faecium, P. vulgatus, and R. inulinivorans, observed in the OVX + vehicle group, was attenuated by MDMA treatment (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3B).

Overall, our findings suggest that MDMA treatment has the potential to normalize alterations in the gut microbiota of OVX mice.

Biomarkers of the Gut Microbiota, Functional Annotation of the 16S rRNA Gene, Host Bacteria and BMD Exhibit Stable Associations

LEfSe analysis identified taxonomic enrichment in each group. The gut microbiota of the sham + MDMA group was enriched in the phylum Actinomycetota, classes Actinobacteria and Bacilli, and genera such as Romboutsia, Limosilactobacillus, and Mammaliicoccus (Fig. 4A). In the OVX + MDMA group, enrichment was observed in the class Erysipelotrichia, whereas the OVX + vehicle group showed enrichment in the phylum Pseudomonadota and the class Gammaproteobacteria (Fig. 4A). Phylogenetic investigation of communities by reconstruction of unobserved states analysis of functional profiles indicated upregulation of metabolic pathways related to amino acids and secondary metabolites in the OVX + MDMA group. In contrast, the gut microbiota of the OVX + vehicle group was primarily enriched in pathways associated with carbohydrate, glycan, and nucleotide metabolism (Fig. 4B).

We hypothesized that distinct gut microbes across taxa contribute to host BMD indices. Spearman correlation analysis was performed between species with differing abundance in the OVX + vehicle and OVX + MDMA groups. Significant positive correlations were found between certain bacteria and BMD indices, including A. johnsonii, Akkermansia muciniphila, Alistipes inops, Bifidobacterium longum, S. copri, and Vibrio zhuhaiensis, which were significantly (p < 0.01, Q < 0.01) positively correlated with cortical, cancellous, and total BMD (Fig. 4C). Conversely, eight species—Fusobacterium periodonticum, Kineothrix alyssoides, L. intestinalis, Oribacterium asaccharolyticum, Pediococcus acidilactici, Prevotella melaninogenica, Rothia mucilaginosa, and Streptococcus oralis—showed significant (p < 0.05, Q < 0.05) negative correlations with cortical, cancellous, and total BMD (Fig. 4C).

These findings suggest that specific bacterial populations efficiently colonize the host, forming stable associations with BMD that may persist over evolutionary timescales.

Metabolomic Analysis of Plasma Samples, Dynamic Links between Gut Microbes, Plasma Metabolites, and BMD Indices

Six metabolites exhibited significant differences in the OVX + MDMA group compared to the OVX + vehicle group (Fig. 5A). Specifically, the concentration of L-propionylcarnitine was significantly elevated in the OVX + MDMA group (p = 0.015). Conversely, the concentrations of N-acetylneuraminate (p = 0.0007), β-D-allose (p = 0.001), N-acetylneuraminic acid (p = 0.002), maltotriose (p = 0.004), and deoxycholic acid (p = 0.035) were significantly reduced in the OVX + MDMA group compared to the OVX + vehicle group (Fig. 5B).

To explore the relationship between gut microbiota composition and metabolites in OVX mice, we performed Spearman’s correlation analysis of species and metabolites that differed in abundance between the OVX + vehicle and OVX + MDMA groups. The results, visualized in a heatmap, highlighted significant species-metabolite associations. We identified 20 significant correlations between differentially expressed species and metabolites. Notably, significant correlations (p < 0.05) were observed between microbes and metabolites that were concurrently enriched or depleted in the OVX + MDMA condition. For example, Lactobacillus_johnsonii showed a negative association with several metabolites enriched in the OVX + MDMA group, including β-D-allose, N-acetylneuraminate, and N-acetylneuraminic acid, supporting a mechanistic link between these bacterial species and metabolites (Fig. 5C). Additionally, positive associations were found between various microbes and metabolites enriched or depleted in the OVX + MDMA group (Fig. 5C).

To further elucidate the relationships among gut microbiota, plasma metabolites, and BMD indices, we constructed a correlation network incorporating BMD indices, microbiota species with RAs greater than 0.1% across all samples, and plasma metabolites that showed significant differences between the OVX + vehicle and OVX + MDMA groups (Fig. 5D). This network analysis revealed a substantial subnetwork linked to species enriched in the OVX + MDMA group, particularly A. johnsonii, as well as other species associated with plasma metabolites. Within this subnetwork, Lactobacillus johnsonii, Muribaculum gordoncarteri, Anaerobiospirillum succiniciproducens, S. copri, and A. johnsonii emerged as central nodes, showing positive or negative correlations with β-D-allose, maltotriose, N-acetylneuraminate, N-acetylneuraminic acid, and other species enriched or depleted in the OVX + MDMA group. Notably, A. johnsonii and S. copri exhibited positive correlations with cortical, cancellous, and total BMD (Fig. 5D).

Additionally, a high degree of connectivity was observed between the OVX + MDMA-enriched bacterium Rhizobium rhizoryzae and sixteen OVX + MDMA-depleted species, including Pseudomonas fulva, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Fictibacillus phosphorivorans, B. longum, Bacillus cereus, among others, indicating that host BMD is associated with microbiome diversity (Fig. 5D). The presence of these bacteria influenced the concentrations of certain plasma metabolites, with β-D-allose, maltotriose, N-acetylneuraminate, and N-acetylneuraminic acid levels negatively correlated with the abundance of Lactobacillus_johnsonii (Fig. 5D).

Overall, the taxa correlated with the plasma metabolites demonstrated significant modulation of the BMD indices, with diverse correlations observed within the taxa, suggesting that treatment in each sample group may influence these associations.

DISCUSSION

The major findings of this study are as follows: First, repeated intermittent MDMA attenuated OVX-induced reductions in cortical, cancellous, and total BMD, consistent with a protective effect under estrogen deficiency. Second, circulating RANKL decreased while OPG increased, and the OPG/RANKL ratio correlated with BMD, implicating this axis in the observed phenotype. Third, MDMA reshaped gut community structure—reducing the RA of Clostridia and increasing Erysipelotrichia and Bacilli. Fourth, untargeted metabolomics identified six plasma metabolites that differed between OVX + MDMA and OVX + vehicle, including elevated L-propionylcarnitine and reduced N-acetylneuraminate and β-D-allose. Finally, network analyses linked specific taxa to BMD indices—A. johnsonii and S. copri positively, L. johnsonii negatively—highlighting microbiota–bone relationships. Together, these data indicate that MDMA is associated with coordinated shifts in bone remodeling markers, gut microbiota, and circulating metabolites in OVX mice. While associative, the findings point to a gut microbiota–bone axis that warrants causal testing in future work.

In osteoporosis, reduced BMD is closely tied to dysregulation of the RANKL–OPG system, a key regulator of bone remodeling [39-41]. RANKL promotes osteoclast formation, activation, and survival, whereas OPG acts as a decoy receptor that prevents RANKL from binding RANK on osteoclasts, thereby limiting bone resorption. In this study, the OPG/RANKL ratio correlated positively with cortical and cancellous BMD. Moreover, MDMA lowered circulating RANKL and increased OPG in OVX mice. These findings suggest that MDMA may influence bone homeostasis via the RANKL–OPG axis, although mechanistic studies are needed to define causality and cellular targets.

The gut microbiota regulates BMD via a gut–bone axis linking intestinal communities to skeletal remodeling [42-44]. In our study, microbial diversity and community composition differed between OVX + vehicle and OVX + MDMA groups, indicating that MDMA can reshape the gut ecosystem in OVX mice. Notably, MDMA reduced the RA of Clostridia and increased Bacilli. Clostridia influence bone through immune, metabolic, and inflammatory pathways, and certain Clostridia taxa are enriched in patients with osteoporosis [45], making their reduction noteworthy. Conversely, Bacilli—particularly probiotic genera such as Lactobacillus—have been associated with preserved or improved BMD; for example, a randomized, placebo-controlled trial showed that Bacillus subtilis C-3102 increased BMD in healthy postmenopausal women via a microbiota–bone pathway [46]. Taken together, these observations suggest that MDMA may modulate gut community features relevant to bone health, although causal links and specific microbial mediators remain to be defined.

Untargeted metabolomics identified six plasma metabolites that differed between OVX + MDMA and OVX + vehicle. Among these, β-D-allose was decreased by MDMA and showed a negative association with L. johnsonii. Although this reduction coincided with improved BMD, we do not infer causality. Notably, prior studies report that β-D-allose can benefit bone—enhancing BMD, improving gut microbiota composition in aged mice, and ameliorating cognitive deficits while increasing bone strength [47,48]. The discrepancy may reflect context- and compartment-specific factors (e.g., OVX model vs. aging, plasma vs. luminal/tissue pools), altered production versus enhanced tissue utilization, or consortia-dependent microbial metabolism. Clarifying the role of β-D-allose in bone health—and its relationship to MDMA-induced microbiome shifts—will require targeted validation.

The present findings suggest that intermittent MDMA administration is associated with attenuation of OVX-induced BMD loss, consistent with modulation of a gut–microbiota–bone axis. Given MDMA’s status as a recreational controlled substance and its known acute and chronic risks, we do not recommend its use for osteoporosis. Instead, future work should (i) evaluate—epidemiologically—whether lifetime MDMA exposure is associated with osteoporosis risk (with appropriate control for confounding), and (ii) identify the specific microbial taxa, microbial functions (e.g., metabolic pathways), and microbe-derived metabolites that mediate bone effects. These components represent tractable targets for drug, probiotic, prebiotic, or postbiotic interventions. More broadly, we highlight serotonergic–microbiome signaling as the putative axis—rather than MDMA itself—pointing toward safer serotonergic or microbiome-directed strategies.

This study has several limitations. First, we did not implement pair-feeding, leaving open the possibility that differences in food intake contributed to outcomes; future causal studies should incorporate pair-feeding or caloric-restriction controls. Second, although microbiota differed between OVX + vehicle and OVX + MDMA groups, we did not test their direct effects on BMD. Approaches such as antibiotic depletion/reconstitution and fecal microbiota transplantation from OVX + MDMA donors into antibiotic-pretreated or germ-free OVX recipients would help establish causality. Third, while six plasma metabolites differed between groups, we did not examine their specific impact on BMD; targeted supplementation or inhibition studies are warranted. Fourth, our work was restricted to the OVX model; validation in additional bone-loss models (e.g., glucocorticoid-induced or age-related osteoporosis) is needed. Fifth, we did not benchmark against established anti-osteoporotic agents (e.g., anti-RANKL antibody, bisphosphonates), which would contextualize effect sizes and mechanisms. Finally, translation to humans remains uncertain. Ongoing clinical trials of MDMA for psychiatric indications in the U.S. [49] may offer opportunities to assess associations with BMD, but dedicated, well-controlled studies will be required.

In conclusion, the study suggests that intermittent MDMA administration attenuated OVX-induced bone loss in mice, accompanied by a favorable shift in the RANKL–OPG axis. MDMA also reshaped gut communities and altered plasma metabolites, with taxa–metabolite features correlating with BMD. These findings are associative and point to a gut microbiota–bone axis but do not establish causality. Given MDMA’s risks, the translational emphasis is on identifying microbial functions and metabolites as safer, tractable targets for future interventions.
