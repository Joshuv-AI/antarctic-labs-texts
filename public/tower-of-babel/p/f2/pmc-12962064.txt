scSCCNIA: similarity matrix based contrastive clustering with neighbor information aggregation for single-cell RNA sequencing data
Authors: Jing Wang, Junfeng Xia, Yansen Su, Chun-Hou Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology provides unprecedented opportunities for elucidating cell heterogeneity and gene expression. Identifying and discovering cell types through cell clustering is a crucial step in analyzing scRNA-seq data. However, the high-dimensionality nature and frequent dropout events of the data raise great challenges for cell clustering. Here, we propose a novel contrastive clustering framework called scSCCNIA (Similarity-matrix-based Contrastive Clustering with Neighbor Information Aggregation), for the accurate identification of cell clusters from scRNA-seq data. scSCCNIA adopts a Laplacian filter to conduct neighbor information aggregation, constructs different graph views by using special un-shared parameters Siamese encoders for data augmentation, and learns the latent low-dimensional embedding representations via similarity-matrix-based contrastive learning. Comparative analyses of multiple scRNA-seq datasets from different platforms and with varying cell numbers demonstrate that scSCCNIA outperforms existing methods in terms of cell clustering and marker gene identification. Furthermore, scSCCNIA reveals the heterogeneity and functional specificity of various cell types through Gene Ontology terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses. Overall, scSCCNIA is an effective algorithm for learning latent features from scRNA-seq data, enhancing cell type identification accuracy and facilitating downstream analyses of scRNA-seq data.Keywords: single cell RNA-seq data, neighbor information aggregation, similarity matrix, contrastive learning, clustering

Introduction

The rapid development of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology has offers unprecedented opportunities for biological research by measuring gene expression at the cellular level [1]. This technology plays a crucial role in revealing cellular heterogeneity [2–4], studying disease mechanisms [5–7], and exploring cellular development processes [8–11]. Mining the potential information in single-cell transcriptome data has become an important approach for cell type recognition [12], marker gene identification [13, 14], gene regulatory networks inference [15, 16] and analysis of cell–cell interactions [17–19]. Annotating cell types plays a crucial role in the analysis and mining of single-cell transcriptome data, and its results affect downstream analyses [20, 21]. Currently, although researchers have proposed many single-cell clustering algorithms based on deep learning and machine learning for cell type identification [22–25], there are still many issues that need to be addressed. For example, the high dimensionality and sparsity of single-cell RNA sequencing data lead to poor effects or instability in clustering performance [26]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop clustering algorithms for single-cell RNA data that can effectively learn the features of scRNA-seq data, thereby accurately identifying cell clusters and providing accurate basis for the downstream analysis of single-cell transcriptome data.

Recent years, computational methods based on machine learning and deep learning have been proposed to distinguish cell subpopulations by exploring structural relationships between cells [27]. For example, scDeepCluster [28] is an auto-encoder-based deep embedded clustering method, which simultaneously learns feature representation and clustering via explicit modelling of scRNA-seq data generation. scDSC [29] is a deep structural clustering method which integrate the structural information into deep clustering of single cells. Contrastive-sc [30] is an unsupervised deep learning method to cluster scRNA-seq data using contrastive representation learning. ScDCCA [31] acquires the pairwise proximity of cells with a dual contrastive learning module and learns intrinsic feature representations by a denoising ZINB model-based auto-encoder. ScNAME [32] incorporates a mask estimation task for gene pertinence mining and a neighborhood contrastive learning framework for cell intrinsic structure exploitation. ScMMN [33] is a multi-level multi-view network based on structural consistency contrastive learning for scRNA-seq data clustering. Seurat [34] constructs cell-to-cell similarity using shared nearest neighbors (SNNs) and divides the cell population through the Louvain community detection algorithm by maximizing the modularity.

Although these methods have achieved certain clustering effects in single-cell RNA sequencing data and promoted the development of single-cell transcriptomics, there are still challenges or issues that need to be addressed. For example, current clustering algorithms often fail to incorporate the intrinsic hierarchical structure among cells, leading to potential inaccuracies [17]. Additionally, single-cell clustering methods based on contrastive learning (such as Contrastive-sc) implements data augmentation through node perturbation, which easily lead to semantic changes and indiscriminate positive sample pairs [35]. Moreover, in the multi-view self-supervised contrastive learning of scNAME, the selection of positive and negative sample only relies on physical neighborhoods, ignoring functional similarity and resulting in structural learning bias.

To address these issues, we introduce a novel scRNA-seq contrastive clustering framework called scSCCNIA. This framework utilizes Neighbor Information Aggregation and Similarity-matrix-based Contrastive Learning to explore cell similarity. Initially, scSCCNIA employs a Laplacian filter to aggregate information from neighboring cells, thereby strengthening the connectivity data among adjacent cells. Subsequently, scSCCNIA leverages a pair of Siamese encoders with unique parameters to augment the dataset, preventing semantic drift and avoiding the creation of ambiguously similar positive sample pairs that might arise from improper data augmentation. Furthermore, scSCCNIA efficiently determines the similarity among samples through contrastive learning, utilizing a similarity matrix derived from the low-dimensional embeddings of two perspectives. Ultimately, cell clusters are identified by applying K-means clustering to the low-dimensional embeddings obtained from the contrastive learning component. For evaluation, scSCCNIA is compared against seven other methods across 17 real scRNA-seq datasets. The results of real data experiments and biological analyses indicate that scSCCNIA outperforms existing methods in terms of cell clustering, robustness and marker gene identification, revealing the heterogeneity and functional specificity of various cell types.

Materials and methods

Overview of scSCCNIA

The scSCCNIA algorithm comprises four main components: data augmentation, Siamese encoder, contrastive learning, downstream analysis (Fig. 1). First, scSCCNIA employs a widely-used Laplacian filter [36] to facilitate data augmentation through neighbor information aggregation. Notably, scSCCNIA constructs the initial similarity matrix by calculating Pearson correlation coefficient between cells, and adopts NE to denoise . Subsequently, scSCCNIA constructs different embedding views by adopting two Siamese encoders with un-shared parameters, thus avoiding the semantic drift caused by inappropriate augmentation. After encoding, scSCCNIA integrates the low-dimensional embedding features from both views through concatenation and subsequently computes the similarity matrix of the samples. Then, scSCCNIA selects positive and negative sample pairs from the similarity matrix, and then leverages contrastive learning to derive the embedding representations of cells. Ultimately, scSCCNIA performs cell clustering and leverages the resultant clusters to conduct comprehensive downstream analyses, encompassing the identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis.

(D) Downstream analysis: the low-dimensional representations enable cell clustering, visualization, marker gene identification, and functional enrichment (GO/KEGG).

Data preprocessing

We collect a total of 13 real scRNA-seq datasets from different platforms. Supplementary Table S1 summarizes the details of these data sets, which include various human and mouse tissues with sample sizes range from 56 to 3005. Each cell in these datasets is annotated with a category label, serving as the gold-standard reference. Inspired by scDHA [37] and Seurat, the gene expression matrix is preprocessed as follows: First, log2-transformation is applied when the expression range exceeded 100. Second, genes detected (expression value >0) in fewer than 5% of cells are filtered out. Subsequently, we perform library size normalization using total read counts after log transformation. Formally, for cell i with library size si, we compute size factors as si/median(s), where is the vector of all cells' library sizes. The top 3000 genes by normalized dispersion are selected as highly variable genes (HVGs) for downstream analysis. The above procedures are implemented with the SCANPY [38] package.

Cell clustering and downstream analysis

Cell clusters are acquired using K-means applied to the low-dimensional embedding representations generated by scSCCNIA. We evaluate the clustering performance of different methods using two standard metrics, including NMI and ARI. The definitions of these two metrics can be found in Supplementary Note S2. NMI produce scores from 0 to 1, and the score of ARI ranges from −1 to 1. Higher values represent better clustering performances. Based on the clustering results, downstream analysis including visualization, cell-type annotation, marker gene identification, and GO terms/KEGG pathway enrichment are subsequently performed.

Results and discussion

Benchmarking scSCCNIA with other seven methods

To assess the clustering performance of scSCCNIA, we apply it to 17 real scRNA-seq datasets [41–53]. These datasets are collected from different platforms and each cell in these datasets is assigned a category label, which is regarded as a ‘gold standard’ with reference values. With these gold standard as ground truth, we employed the adjusted Rand index (ARI) [54] and normalized mutual information (NMI) [55] to evaluate the accuracy of the algorithm in predicting cell clusters. In the evaluation, we compared the performance of scSCCNIA with seven proposed scRNA-seq data clustering approaches, including scDCCA, scNAME, scMMN, Contrastive-sc, scDSC, scDeepcluster, and Seurat. We ran each method according to the official technical documentation and used the default parameters for each method (Supplementary Note S1). The comparison results reveal that scSCCNIA achieves a significantly higher median ARI score than the other methods across the 17 datasets (Fig. 2A). Following that, scDCCA, scDSC, and scMMN have higher median ARI scores on these datasets. We conducted a one-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test comparing the results of scSCCNIA with these three methods. The statistical test results indicate that scSCCNIA performs significantly better in clustering on these 17 datasets compared to scDCCA (P = 6.535 × 10−3), scMMN (P = 3.23× 10−4), and scDSC (7.604× 10−5). Notably, Supplementary Table S2-S3 and Fig. 2B presented the NMI and ARI values for each method on each dataset. Specifically, scSCCNIA outperformed the other seven methods by achieving the highest NMI scores across eight datasets and securing the second-highest scores in six out of the remaining nine. Similarly, it excelled in ARI scoring, leading in 13 datasets and ranking second in two of the remaining four.

Furthermore, we conducted an in-depth analysis on Xin and Usoskin datasets (Fig. 2C and D). Specifically, we applied UMAP [56] to project the pre-processed gene expression data into the 2D space based on the predicted cell clusters obtained by each method and compared them to ground truth (Fig. 2C). As shown in Fig. 2C, each point denotes a cell, and different colors mean different cell clusters. The cell visualization of scDSC, scMMN, Seurat, Contrastive-sc, scNAME, and scDeepCluster mix different cell subtypes, leading to errors. Although scDCCA achieves high clustering accuracy(ARI = 0.8042), it still suffers from the same challenges as other methods, such as merging smaller cell clusters into a single whole. In contrast, the boundaries of various cell clusters predicted by scSCCNIA are clearer than those of the other methods, and cells clusters predicted by scSCCNIA are closer to ground truth, implying that scSCCNIA is superior to other competing methods. Additionally, we also plotted Sankey diagrams of Usoskin datasets based on predicted cell clusters of each method and the ground truth. As shown in Fig. 2D, Seurat tends to divide larger cell clusters into multiple clusters, while contrastive-sc, scDeepCluster, scDSC, scNAME, and scMMN are prone to merge distinct clusters into a single one. By comparison, scSCCNIA achieves the best clustering results, showing clearer cell clusters and higher consistency with the ground truth.

scSCCNIA augments gene expression for better clustering cells and downstream analysis

Subsequently, we illustrated the efficacy of the neighbor information aggregation-based data augmentation module in noise reduction for scRNA-seq data, emphasizing its ability to extract gene expression features. scSCCNIA was applied to the aforementioned 17 datasets, and ablation experiments results revealed that enhancing gene expression data significantly improved the accuracy of cell cluster identification (Fig. 3). Specifically, to assess the contribution of neighbor information aggregation-based data augmentation in the scSCCNIA algorithm, we eliminated the Laplacian filter to obtain the variant of scSCCNIA, namely, scSCCNIA_without_augmentation (scSCCNIA_wo_augment). The experimental results indicate that the clustering performance of scSCCNIA is superior to its variant (scSCCNIA_wo_augment) on 11 data sets except Camp1 and Bladder (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table S4). On the Camp1 and Bladder datasets, the reason why scSCCNIA_wo_augment achieves slightly better clustering performance than scSCCNIA may be as follows: The gene expression sparsity of the Camp1 dataset (777 cells) is only 5.2% (far lower than the average of 12%), and neighborhood aggregation instead smooths out key expression differences; while the anomaly on the Bladder dataset is due to model training fluctuations. Additionally, we selected five of these data sets to demonstrate that the augmented data facilitated other cell clustering task and downstream analysis as well (Fig. 3B). In the UMAP visualizations, the enhanced data displayed clearer cell cluster structures on the ‘Pollen,’ ‘Xin,’ ‘Segerstolpe,’ ‘Romanov,’ and ‘Zeisel’ datasets compared to the raw data, the visualization of the raw data was accompanied by a significant amount of noise, making it difficult to distinctly identify the cell clusters. Furthermore, the enhanced gene expression data facilitates the identification of marker genes in downstream analysis. For instance, we adopted COSG [57] to identify the marker genes of each cell cluster predicted by scSCCNIA and scSCCNIA_wo_augment on Xin dataset (Fig. 3C). As shown in Fig. 3C, in comparison, the cell clusters predicted by scSCCNIA exhibit more pronounced differential gene expression patterns than those predicted by scSCCNIA_wo_augment, and the identified marker genes are also more accurate than those identified by scSCCNIA_wo_augment.

scSCCNIA employs similarity matrix-based contrastive learning to better extract effective features from cells for clustering and downstream analysis

Furthermore, we verify the effectiveness of similarity-matrix-based contrastive learning in extracting data features, confirming that it is beneficial for cell clustering and downstream analysis. We applied scSCCNIA to the aforementioned 17 datasets, the experiments results indicated that similarity-matrix-based contrastive learning strategy significantly enhanced the performance of cell clustering (Fig. S1). Specifically, to evaluate the contribution of the similarity-matrix-based contrastive learning strategy in the scSCCNIA algorithm, we replaced the similarity-matrix-based contrastive module with an instance contrastive learning for two-view data, thereby obtaining another variant of scSCCNIA, known as scSCCNIA without similarity-matrix-based contrastive learning (referred to as scSCCNIA_wo_SCL). The experimental outcomes demonstrate that scSCCNIA exhibits better clustering performance compared to its variant scSCCNIA_wo_SCL across 12 datasets, as shown in Fig. S1A and Supplementary Table S4. Additionally, we selected five of these datasets to demonstrate that similarity-matrix-based contrastive learning can better facilitate cell clustering tasks and downstream analyses. For example, as shown in the UMAP visualizations of the ‘Pollen,’ ‘Camp1,’ ‘Xin,’ ‘Segerstolpe,’ and ‘Klein’ datasets (Fig. S1B), similarity-matrix-based contrastive learning can more clearly reveal the true structure of cell clusters compared to instance contrastive learning using two views. Moreover, the low-dimensional embedding representations learned through similarity-matrix-based contrastive learning can better facilitate the identification of marker genes in downstream analysis. For example, we also employed COSG to identify the marker genes of each cell cluster predicted by scSCCNIA and scSCCNIA_wo_SCL on the ‘Segerstolpe’ dataset (Fig. S1C). As shown in Fig. S1C, compared with scSCCNIA_wo_SCL, the cell clusters predicted by scSCCNIA show more distinct differential gene expression patterns, and the identified marker genes are also more accurate than those identified by scSCCNIA_wo_SCL.

scSCCNIA facilitates accurate cell annotation and marker gene identification

To explore whether the cell cluster labels predicted by scSCCNIA can facilitate the accurate annotation of cell types and identification of marker genes, we applied the algorithm to the Bladder dataset for marker gene identification and cell type annotation. Firstly, we utilized COSG [57] to identify the DEGs of each cell cluster predicted by scSCCNIA, as well as those of each actual cell type for the dataset. Figure 4A illustrates the top 20 DEGs of Bladder dataset identified by COSG. The upper part section pertains to the DEGs of the predicted cell clusters, while the lower part refers to the DEGs of the actual cell types. The figure demonstrates a significant alignment between the DEGs identified from the cell clusters forecasted by scSCCNIA algorithm and those from the genuine cell types. According to the consistency of the DEGs, we can annotate each cell cluster with its corresponding cell type. For example, in the Bladder data set, the predicted cell clusters 0-3 are annotated as bladder cells, leukocyte cells, bladder urothelial cells, and endothelial cells, respectively. Additionally, we use COSG to identify the marker genes of each cell cluster predicted by scSCCNIA. Figure 4B shows the expression heat map of the top three marker genes identified by COSG for each cluster. As shown, the marker genes exhibit distinct expression profiles unique to their respective clusters. To further verify the consistency of these marker genes with those of the actual cell types, we also utilized COSG to identify the marker genes of the genuine cell types. As can be seen from Fig. 4C, the identified marker genes of the actual cell types show significant alignment with those of the corresponding predicted cell clusters. In summary, the scSCCNIA algorithm not only achieves a high level of accuracy in predicting cell clusters but also effectively facilitates accurate cell type annotation and the detection of marker genes.

scSCCNIA reveals cellular heterogeneity and functional specificity

To further explore the heterogeneity of the cell clusters predicted by the scSCCNIA algorithm, we selected Bladder dataset to perform GO terms and KEGG enrichment analysis on the DEGs of each cell cluster and verified their biological functions. Figure 5A shows the KEGG enrichment results of the top 50 DEGs of each subpopulation. It can be seen that the DEGs of different cell types are significantly enriched in diverse pathways, suggesting that they differ not only in expression patterns but also in the biological pathways. And the biological enrichment pathways are also verifiable. For example, the top 50 DEGs of endothelial cells in the Bladder data set are significantly enriched in pathways such as ‘Coronavirus disease_COVID_19.’ This enrichment underscores the potential vital role of endothelial cells in this biological pathways, a finding that aligns with previous study [58]. And we also perform GO terms enrichment analysis on the top 10 DEGs of Bladder urothelial cell of this dataset. Figure 5B shows the GO terms enrichment results. As can be seen from the figure, the biological processes significantly enriched in Bladder urothelial cells include: cytoplasmic translation, regulation of peptidase activity, and regulation of T cell proliferation, which indicates that these specific biological functions may be particularly active or play an important role in the cell's physiological state. And the cellular component significantly enriched in Bladder urothelial cells include: collagen-containing extracellular matrix and extracellular matrix structural constituent, which indicates that these cells play a key role in maintaining the integrity and elasticity of the bladder wall. Additionally, the molecular function significantly enriched in Bladder urothelial cells include: structural constituent of ribosome, serine-type endopeptidase inhibitor activity, and peptidase inhibitor activity, which indicate that bladder urothelial cells play a significant role in the regulation of protein synthesis and degradation. And all these biological processes have been validated in reference [59].

scSCCNIA exhibits excellent performance with appropriate parameters

In this section, we explore the impact of different parameters on the scSCCNIA algorithm. Firstly, the number of the neighbor nodes k is an essential parameter in our scSCCNIA algorithm. To investigate its sensitivity, we conducted parameter analysis on 17 datasets with k at different values. Specifically, we selected the parameter range from 2 to 6 to for these data sets and ran scSCCNIA algorithm. Figure 6A shows the experimental results on 17 datasets. It can be seen that the scSCCNIA algorithm is not sensitive to the parameter k on nine datasets (Biase, Yan, Pollen, Darmanis, Bladder, Usoskin, Camp1, Klein, and Romanvov). On the remaining eight datasets, except for Kolod, the ARI metric decreases as the parameter k increases, possibly because the selection of more neighboring nodes introduces more redundant noise. In contrast, on the Kolod dataset, the ARI metric shows an upward trend as the parameter k increases. A possible reason for this phenomenon is that the effective aggregation of neighbor information enhances the performance of cell clustering. Figure 6B and Supplementary Table S5 (A) display the ARI metric for 17 datasets with varying values of parameter k. The figure indicates that the scSCCNIA algorithm yielded optimal average performance across all 17 datasets when k is equal to 2.

Clustering performance of scSCCNIA under different values of the parameter k and the number of highly variable genes.

Furthermore, we also conduct sensitivity analysis on other parameter that may influence our experimental results across these 17 datasets. For example, the number of highly variable genes has a certain impact on model performance. Figure 6C and Supplementary Table S5 (B) show the ARI metrics for 17 datasets under different values of highly variable genes (highly variable gene number = 1000, 2000, 3000, 4000, 5000). As illustrated in the figure, except for the Yan, Camp1, Romanov, and Young datasets, the scSCCNIA algorithm demonstrates stable and relatively high average ARI performance when the number of highly variable genes is set to 3000 or 4000, across the remaining 13 datasets. Figure 6D illustrates a boxplot that displays the ARI metric across 17 datasets with different settings of the highly variable gene parameter. Although the figure indicates that scSCCNIA demonstrated the best average performance across all 17 datasets when the number of highly variable genes was configured to 4000, this was mainly influenced by the Kolod dataset. After comprehensive consideration, we choose the number of highly variable genes to be 3000 for the scSCCNIA algorithm.

scSCCNIA algorithm manifests remarkable robustness

To verify the robustness of scSCCNIA, we first conduct down-sampling experiment on these 17 real scRNA-seq data sets with different sample sizes. Each data set is randomly down-sampled to obtain partial data sets containing 80%, 60%, and 40% cells, respectively. We input these down-sampled data sets into the scSCCNIA algorithm respectively, and the experimental results are illustrated in Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table S6. Figure 7A and B displays the ARI and NMI values for 17 datasets at different sampling rates. These figures show that the clustering performance of scSCCNIA on the down-sampled data has little difference from the complete data, indicating that our scSCCNIA is still stable and robust when dealing with the incomplete data. Additionally, we also assessed the performance of our scSCCNIA model in identifying rare cell types to further demonstrate the robustness of the model. Specifically, we performed down-sampling on the Kolod and Klein datasets. The Kolod dataset includes three types of mouse embryonic stem cells (with cell counts of 295, 159, and 250 respectively), while the Klein dataset comprises four types of mouse embryonic stem cells (with cell counts of 933, 303, 683, and 798 respectively). The down-sampling number is set to {10, 15, 20, 25, 30}, respectively. For each down-sampling number, we randomly select a cell type and randomly select certain number of cells from this cell type to generate a rare cell type, and keep the other remain types of cells unchanged. Then, we apply scSCCNIA on the two down-sampled dataset. Figure 7C and D illustrates the ARI and NMI scores for the two datasets that have undergone down-sampling. The figure clearly demonstrates that our scSCCNIA algorithm maintains superior clustering performance across datasets, including those with rare cell types.

Conclusion

scRNA-seq technology has been widely applied in various fields of biology and medicine [60–62], particularly in the characterization of tumor heterogeneity [63] and the identification of cellular immune microenvironments [64]. Accurately identifying cell types using single-cell RNA sequencing data is crucial for studying cellular heterogeneity and its biological functions. In this study, we introduced a contrastive learning approach for identifying cell types from scRNA-seq data. The contribution of scSCCNIA is three-fold: (i) aggregate neighborhood information using a Laplacian filter; (ii) constructs graph data augmentations with un-shared parameter Siamese encoders; and (iii) learns discriminative features by similarity-matrix based contrastive learning. Extensive benchmarking on 17 real scRNA-seq datasets demonstrates that scSCCNIA outperforms the current state-of-the-art methods. scSCCNIA method can: (i) properly segregate cells of different types from distinct platform, (ii) annotate cell types based on DEGs within cell clusters and identify marker genes for these cell types, and (iii) facilitate downstream GO and KEGG enrichment analysis. These analyses further reveal the heterogeneity and functional specificity of various cell types.

Future work could extend scSCCNIA to single-cell multi-omics analyses, such as chromatin accessibility and DNA methylation profiling. Additionally, further algorithmic improvements aiming at enhancing its scalability for processing large-scale datasets would significantly broaden its utility in the field of single-cell genomics.
