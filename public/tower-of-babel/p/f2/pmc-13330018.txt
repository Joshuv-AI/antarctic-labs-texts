Structural mining and engineering of metagenome-derived Cas12a orthologs expands the CRISPR genome editing and multiplex diagnostics toolkit
Authors: Dagang Tao, Bingrong Xu, Sheng Li, Hailong Liu, Yinghui Wei, Xi Cao, Suyu Shi, Yuan Wang, Rui Jiang, Yonghui Zhang, Changzhi Zhao, Jinxue Ruan, Liangliang Fu, Xingxu Huang, Xinyun Li, Shuhong Zhao, Shengsong Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
CRISPR-Cas12a is a compact, RNA-guided nuclease widely deployed in genome editing and molecular diagnostics, yet its broader utility is limited by suboptimal cis-cleavage efficiency and incompletely defined trans-cleavage behavior. To overcome these constraints, we developed an artificial intelligence-guided structural discovery pipeline powered by AlphaFold2, which identified 1,261 previously uncharacterized Cas12a orthologs. From this set, 21 structurally conserved but sequence-divergent candidates were selected for biochemical characterization. Using structure-informed engineering, we generated PcuCas12a MAX, a high-fidelity variant that achieves genome-editing efficiencies in human cells comparable to the benchmark AsCas12a Ultra while retaining robust activity in murine and porcine systems. In addition, four orthologs (LcoCas12a, FcaCas12a, EsoCas12a, and Mac2Cas12a), when paired with specifically engineered CRISPR RNAs, exhibited distinct single-stranded DNA trans-cleavage signatures. These properties enabled construction of a multiplex CRISPR sensor capable of simultaneously detecting multiple nucleic acid targets. Together, these findings expand the Cas12a endonuclease repertoire and enhance its utility in genome engineering and next-generation diagnostics.

Introduction

CRISPR-Cas12a, originally identified as a prokaryotic RNA-guided immune effector, has evolved into a streamlined, programmable nuclease that is reshaping genome editing and molecular diagnostics.1,2,3,4 Unlike Cas9, Cas12a is guided by a single 42- to 44-nucleotide (nt) CRISPR RNA (crRNA), recognizes 5′-TTTV protospacer adjacent motifs (PAMs), and promotes ATP-independent R-loop formation, generating staggered double-strand breaks with 4- to 5-nt 5′ overhangs.5,6 Beyond target-specific (cis) cleavage, Cas12a also exhibits robust, non-specific trans cleavage of single-stranded DNA upon activation, a feature that underpins ultrasensitive nucleic acid detection platforms.7,8 Structurally, Cas12a is a compact ∼1,200-aa monomer harboring a single RuvC domain responsible for both cis and collateral (trans) cleavage activities.9,10 This streamlined architecture enables a reduced genomic footprint, relaxed PAM requirements, and enhanced multiplexing capacity relative to SpCas9.11,12,13 Expanding both the diversity and functional understanding of Cas12a orthologs is therefore essential for advancing editing and diagnostic applications.

Conventional Cas12a discovery pipelines rely on sequence similarity (e.g., HMM-based mining) or CRISPR-locus co-localization (e.g., PILER-CR, MacSyFinder), approaches that have substantially expanded the catalog of known Cas12a proteins.14,15 However, sequence-based strategies often fail to detect distantly related orthologs that, despite extensive sequence divergence, preserve conserved structural and functional motifs. In contrast, structure-guided clustering using spatial similarity matrices has emerged as a powerful and widely adopted approach,16,17 enabling discovery of functional enzymes, such as highly efficient cytidine deaminases, that are typically missed by sequence-based searches.18,19 Despite these advances, the functional landscape remains incomplete: naturally occurring orthologs that combine high fidelity with strong genome-editing activity are rare, and engineered high-performance variants (e.g., AsCas12a Ultra, enAsCas12a-HF1) remain confined to a narrow phylogenetic space.20,21 In diagnostics, robust multiplexing with Cas12a is further constrained by the limited number of orthologs that support orthogonal crRNA scaffolds, distinct PAM specificities, and divergent trans-cleavage properties.22,23,24

To identify potent Cas12a variants, we established a structure-guided discovery framework integrating AlphaFold2-based25 structural prediction with high-throughput biochemical screening, enabling systematic mining of cryptic Cas12a orthologs from metagenomic datasets. Among 21 structurally conserved, low-homology candidates, we identified and engineered a standout variant, PcuCas12a MAX, which achieves high-fidelity genome editing across mammalian systems with activity matching or exceeding AsCas12a Ultra. In parallel, we characterized four additional orthologs (LcoCas12a, FcaCas12a, EsoCas12a, and Mac2Cas12a), each displaying distinct trans-cleavage kinetics and crRNA specificities. These nucleases were integrated into a single-tube, quadruplex CRISPR diagnostic platform capable of visually detecting up to four viral targets simultaneously. Collectively, this work substantially expands the Cas12a toolbox and establishes a versatile platform for genome editing and multiplexed molecular diagnostics.

Results

Global metagenomic mining identifies structurally conserved and sequence-divergent Cas12a orthologs

To expand the Cas12a repertoire, we developed an artificial intelligence (AI)-driven discovery pipeline integrating protein structure prediction, structural alignment, and cluster-based classification (Figure 1A).25 Beginning with 278,629 genome bins (>20 kb each) from the Global Microbial Gene Catalog (GMGC),26 we identified CRISPR arrays using MinCED27 and predicted open reading frames with Prodigal.28 PSI-BLAST29 searches yielded ∼216,429 candidate Cas proteins. Our structure-based discovery workflow centered on pairwise structural comparisons using TM-align, followed by Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic (UPGMA) clustering.31 This approach refined 1,261 preliminary hits to 127 non-redundant candidates that clustered with known Cas12a proteins (Figure 1B). From these, we selected 21 orthologs for functional characterization based on two criteria: TM score >0.6 and <60% amino acid identity to any Cas12a in the Non-redundant Protein Sequence Database (Figure 1B). A standardized naming scheme (source organism + nickname; e.g., PcuCas12a or Gs12-7) was applied for clarity, and the selected candidates were subsequently subjected to in-depth analysis, with their complete list and characterization provided in Table S2. Analysis of crRNA architecture revealed seven conserved scaffolds with variable loop sequences (AUU, GU, GUU, CUC, UAGU, UUC, and CUU), suggesting potential for orthogonal recognition (Figure 1C). Genomic context analysis showed that most loci also encode Cas1, Cas2, Cas4, and CRISPR arrays (Figure S1). This final panel of 21 structurally conserved yet sequence-divergent Cas12a orthologs was advanced for detailed biochemical and functional characterization.

Comparative structural analysis reveals distinct clustering driven by divergence in key functional domains

To quantify sequence divergence, we calculated pairwise protein identity between the novel orthologs and 53 known Cas12a proteins (representative comparisons in Figure S2). The 21 candidates exhibited low sequence identity (≤54%) but high structural similarity (≥62%) relative to known Cas12a nucleases (Figures S2A and S2B). All predicted structures retained canonical Cas12a domains (WED, REC1, REC2, PI, RuvC, and NUC), confirming structural integrity (Figure S2C). Global structural comparisons showed strong overall similarity, whereas domain-resolved analyses revealed non-uniform divergence across regions (Table S3). Structural variation, quantified by TM scores across canonical domains, was most pronounced in the PAM-interacting (PI) domain and the recognition lobe (REC1, REC2) (Figures 1D and S3).

Detailed analysis of the PI domain revealed divergence with likely functional consequences. Protein-PAM interface quantification using PISA showed that, although overall interface sizes (buried surface area) were comparable, interface residue composition and hydrogen-bonding patterns varied substantially among orthologs (Figures S4 and S5). These differences suggest altered interface geometry and chemistry, potentially underlying differential PAM tolerance. Consistently, contact maps between the WED/REC region and the PI domain revealed a conserved interaction core with variable peripheral contacts (Figure S6), indicating modified inter-domain coupling rather than wholesale interface remodeling. Together, these features provide a structural basis for differences in PAM sensing and trans-cleavage gating among Cas12a orthologs.

Across the 21 orthologs, the catalytic RuvC domain and NUC lobe were the most structurally conserved, consistent with their essential roles in cleavage (Figures 1D and S3). Sequence conservation mapping (ConSurf) showed higher conservation near nucleic-acid-interacting surfaces than in distal regions (Figures 1E, left, and S7). Within the RuvC pocket, 20 of 21 orthologs retained the canonical Asp-Glu-Asp (DED) motif,9 with Mme4Cas12a as the sole exception (Figure 1E, right). Structural inspection revealed that this conserved DED motif contacts nucleotides dT22, dT23, and dC24 of the non-target strand (NTS), underscoring its direct role in DNA cleavage (Figure 1F). Collectively, these analyses demonstrate strong conservation of the catalytic core amid diversification of recognition domains across Cas12a orthologs.

PcuCas12a MAX enables efficient genome editing in human cells

To evaluate cis-cleavage activity, we performed in vitro cleavage assays on a 750-bp dsDNA substrate containing a canonical 5′-TTTV PAM. All 21 orthologs, expressed and purified to high quality (Figure S8), were tested under identical buffer conditions and benchmarked against LbCas12a, AsCas12a, and the engineered AsCas12a Ultra. Among the novel enzymes, PcuCas12a exhibited the highest activity, cleaving >90% of the substrate and outperforming wild-type AsCas12a and LbCas12a, though remaining below AsCas12a Ultra (Figure 2A). Several others, including LcoCas12a, CcaCas12a, and Wwe2Cas12a, achieved intermediate efficiencies (>70%), comparable to wild-type AsCas12a and exceeding LbCas12a. Activity across diverse substrates (100-, 150-, and 200-bp amplicons and a 3.5-kb plasmid) confirmed the robust, context-independent performance of PcuCas12a, as well as the relative efficiencies of CcaCas12a and LsnCas12a (Figure S9). To further enhance PcuCas12a activity, we conducted structural overlays with AsCas12a and LbCas12a (Figures 2B and S10). Guided by this comparison, we introduced the homologous mutation E157R in PcuCas12a, corresponding to the activity-enhancing E174R in AsCas12a and E156R in LbCas12a.21,32 Structural modeling indicated that wild-type E157 forms limited distal contacts (∼3.1–3.2 Å), whereas the E157R substitution expands the DNA interface, establishing closer (∼2.1 Å) and additional contacts with the PAM-proximal nucleotide at position 4, potentially stabilizing R-loop nucleation (Figure 2C). We designated this variant PcuCas12a MAX and hypothesized enhanced activity. This was confirmed both in vitro, where PcuCas12a MAX showed increased cis cleavage relative to wild type (Figure 2D), and in human cells, where it achieved substantially higher editing efficiencies (Figures 2E and 2F). Based on this improved performance, PcuCas12a MAX was selected for detailed downstream characterization.

PcuCas12a MAX exhibits high genome-wide specificity

To benchmark PcuCas12a MAX against the established high-activity, high-fidelity AsCas12a Ultra, we co-transfected HEK293T cells with plasmids encoding each nuclease and their corresponding crRNAs. Editing efficiencies were assessed at 15 endogenous TTTV PAM-containing loci across four genes: DNMT1, FANCF, RUNX1, and VEGFA. Both nucleases achieved >60% editing at most sites, with comparable performance at both low-efficiency (e.g., DNMT1-1, DNMT1-3, FANCF-4) and high-efficiency (e.g., DNMT1-4, FANCF-5, RUNX1-6) targets (Figure 3A). Notably, PcuCas12a MAX showed significantly higher editing at VEGFA-1 and VEGFA-2, exceeding AsCas12a Ultra by 24% and 41%, respectively (Figure 3A). Genome-wide specificity was assessed using GUIDE-seq33 at four representative targets. Both nucleases showed strong on-target activity (>50%) at Matched-site-8, DNMT1-site-3, FANCF-target-8, and VEGFA-target-3. At Matched-site-8 and DNMT1-site-3, PcuCas12a MAX generated only four and one low-read off-target sites, respectively, whereas AsCas12a Ultra produced 15 and 13 off-target sites. Chromosomal distributions of double-strand breaks (DSBs) and quantitative off-target analyses are summarized in Table S4. Collectively, these results demonstrate that PcuCas12a MAX achieves enhanced genome-wide specificity while maintaining high on-target editing efficiency.

PcuCas12a MAX enables robust genome editing in murine and porcine models

To evaluate the in vivo performance of PcuCas12a MAX, we targeted the murine tyrosinase (Tyr) gene, leveraging its knockout-associated albino phenotype as a direct visual readout of editing efficiency.34 D2B6F1 zygotes, which harbor a functional Tyr allele from DBA/2 sires, were microinjected with PcuCas12a MAX mRNA and two Tyr-specific crRNAs (Figure 4A). This yielded high editing efficiencies of 87.36% and 69.43% at the two target sites in blastocysts (Figure 4B). In live-born F0 (founding) pups, editing rates increased further to 89.89% and 92.33% (Figure 4C). Following transfer of 16 embryos per target site, 10 (Tyr-site1) and 13 (Tyr-site2) pups were born. Phenotypic analysis revealed albinism in 90% (9/10) and 92.3% (12/13) of these offspring, respectively (Figures 4D, S12, and S13). Genotyping of F0 tail biopsies showed predominantly mosaic editing, with variable proportions of biallelic and monoallelic alleles (Figures S12 and S13). To assess cross-species editing capability, we targeted the porcine CD163 gene, which encodes a critical receptor for PRRSV.35 To accelerate generation of edited animals, male Duroc fetal fibroblasts were co-transfected with a PcuCas12a MAX expression plasmid and a CD163-specific crRNA, and the pooled, unsorted edited cells were used directly as nuclear donors for somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT) (Figure 4E). Transfer of 730 reconstructed embryos into three synchronized recipient sows yielded one pregnancy and four live-born piglets. Sanger sequencing of ear-biopsy DNA identified one piglet (Founder #4) carrying biallelic edits, whereas the remaining three were unedited. All piglets were healthy, developed normally, and displayed no overt abnormalities (Figure S14). Notably, Founder #4 harbored a precise 58-bp in-frame deletion in exon 7 of CD163 (Figures 4F and 4G). Together, these results demonstrate efficient generation of viable CD163-edited pigs and underscore the cross-species utility of PcuCas12a MAX as a genome-editing platform.

Development of a multiplex nucleic acid detection platform using Cas12a orthologs

We next developed a multiplex nucleic acid detection platform by exploiting the trans-cleavage activities of 21 distinct Cas12a orthologs. Using 10 ssDNA-FQ reporters with diverse base compositions, we observed that most orthologs, including PcuCas12a and Wwe2Cas12a, preferentially recognized the ROX-random-BHQ2 reporter, similar to LbCas12a (Figure 5A). Distinct cleavage patterns emerged: LcoCas12a, FcaCas12a, PcuCas12a, Wwe2Cas12a, and LbCas12a exhibited broad reporter tolerance, whereas others showed strong sequence selectivity. For example, LcoCas12a favored polyA and AATAA motifs, EsoCas12a preferred TA/AT repeats, and Mac2Cas12a selectively cleaved TTATT (Figure 5A). Using these optimized Cas12a-crRNA pairs, we constructed a multiplex detection system for simultaneous identification of three porcine viral targets: ASFV P72, SVA VP1, and CSFV E2. In a single-pot reaction, each ortholog was directed by its engineered crRNA while sharing a common reporter framework (Figure 6C). The system unambiguously discriminated all targets, establishing a highly specific CRISPR-Cas12a multiplex diagnostic platform.

Multiplex CRISPR-Cas12a detection enables high-sensitivity, simultaneous identification of three viruses

We subsequently assembled a triple-channel detection system using optimized ortholog/reporter combinations: LcoCas12a/scaffold-54/FAM-AAAAA-BHQ1, FcaCas12a/scaffold-20/HEX-TTTTT-BHQ1, and Mac2Cas12a/scaffold-42/ROX-TTATT-BHQ2 (Figure 7A). The system accurately discriminated single and mixed targets using plasmid templates for ASFV P72, SVA VP1, and CSFV E2, confirming multiplex capability (Figure 7B). To overcome the limited sensitivity of the unamplified assay (limit of detection [LOD] ∼108–109 copies/μL; Figures S15A–S15C), we integrated multiplex recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA)/RT-RPA. Following a simplified boiling-based extraction, all three targets were amplified within 30 min, and the products were detected via Cas12a trans cleavage using either a microplate reader or blue-light visualization (Figure 7C). Benchmarking against qPCR showed that the multiplex RPA-Cas12a assay achieved LODs as low as 2–4 copies/μL for each virus, matching qPCR sensitivity (Ct > 30 defined as negative) (Figures 7D and S16A–S16C). Collectively, these findings establish a robust CRISPR-Cas12a multiplex diagnostic platform. By integrating novel orthologs, engineered crRNAs, and orthogonal reporters, the system enables sensitive, specific, and simultaneous detection of multiple pathogens in a single reaction.

Discussion

CRISPR-Cas12a nucleases are central tools for genome editing and molecular diagnostics.3,39 Using a custom AlphaFold2-integrated discovery pipeline, we screened the GMGC database and identified 1,261 previously unreported Cas12a orthologs, expanding the known diversity of this family by an order of magnitude. Subsequent phylogenetic and structural filtering yielded 21 candidate enzymes that retained conserved protein folds yet displayed low sequence identity to known Cas12a variants. Systematic characterization of their cis- and trans-cleavage activities revealed distinct biochemical profiles, establishing a substantial functional resource for the field. Notably, we engineered PcuCas12a MAX, a hyperactive, high-fidelity editor that operates efficiently in human, porcine, and murine systems. By further exploiting orthogonal cleavage specificities among Cas12a orthologs (LcoCas12a, FcaCas12a, EsoCas12a, and Mac2Cas12a), we developed a multiplex detection platform enabling sensitive, simultaneous viral identification. Together, these findings highlight the extensive, underexplored functional diversity of natural Cas12a proteins and deliver a dual-purpose toolkit for genome engineering and diagnostics.

We prioritized PcuCas12a from the candidate pool because of its superior in vitro cis-cleavage activity, which translated into robust genome editing in HEK293T cells via both plasmid and RNP delivery. Guided by structural homology with enhanced AsCas12a variants, we introduced a point mutation that further increased activity, yielding the optimized PcuCas12a MAX. This variant matched AsCas12a Ultra in on-target efficiency while exhibiting reduced off-target editing, as demonstrated by GUIDE-seq. Its cross-species utility was confirmed in vivo through high-efficiency Tyr editing in mouse embryos and the successful generation of CD163 knockout pigs, underscoring its broad translational potential. Future studies should extend evaluation to additional species, including fish and plants. Systematic benchmarking against CRISPR-Cas9 in terms of efficiency and specificity will further inform optimal tool selection.

We also demonstrate that the biochemical diversity of Cas12a orthologs can be systematically leveraged to overcome a major bottleneck in multiplex CRISPR diagnostics. Functional profiling of 21 orthologs not only produced advanced editors such as PcuCas12a MAX but also uncovered a wide spectrum of trans-cleavage activities and substrate preferences. To address the limited scalability of multiplex detection,40,41 we selected four orthologs (LcoCas12a, FcaCas12a, EsoCas12a, and Mac2Cas12a) with strong trans-cleavage activity and distinct ssDNA motif preferences, likely arising from unique structural gating mechanisms. Although differences in ssDNA-binding clefts, particularly within the REC2 and WED domains, have been proposed to underlie motif selectivity,36 the precise structural determinants remain incompletely defined and merit further investigation.

To further validate multiplex capability, we paired each ortholog with engineered crRNAs and orthogonal fluorescent reporters, establishing a four-channel assay capable of single-copy detection of ASFV, CSFV, and SVA in complex swine-derived samples. We recognize that inhibitors in crude clinical matrices (e.g., blood or tissue homogenates) can impair amplification efficiency and assay kinetics, potentially reducing sensitivity or increasing background. Accordingly, our protocol incorporates brief heat treatment and dilution to mitigate inhibitor effects in most samples. While the CRISPR-Cas12a system itself is highly specific, non-specific amplification under RPA conditions could contribute to background signal. The use of spectrally distinct reporters and single-channel fluorescence readout minimizes crosstalk and supports reliable multiplex detection. To advance point-of-care deployment, future efforts should focus on two priorities: engineering a fully integrated one-pot reaction that combines multiplex RPA with CRISPR-Cas12a detection, and implementing the assay on a microfluidic platform for robust, field-deployable multiplex testing.

In summary, we establish that AI-augmented metagenomic mining unlocks extensive functional diversity within the Cas12a family. From this expanded repertoire, we derived both an enhanced editor, PcuCas12a MAX, for cross-species genome engineering, and a multiplex diagnostic platform built on orthologs with orthogonal cleavage specificities. This study presents a cohesive discovery-to-application pipeline in which computational identification of sequence-structure diversity directly yields practical biotechnological advances. The resulting Cas12a atlas provides a foundational resource for the next generation of precision genome editors and multi-analyte diagnostic systems.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatic pipeline for mining novel Cas12a orthologs

We established a computational pipeline to mine uncharacterized Cas12a orthologs from microbial genomes. First, 278,629 genome bins were retrieved from the GMGC,26 and those >20 kb were scanned for CRISPR arrays using MinCED (v0.4.2).27 Open reading frames in CRISPR-containing bins were predicted and translated with Prodigal (v2.6.3).28 Then, a custom Python script extracted the 10 proteins flanking each CRISPR array. These sequences were clustered at 90% identity with CD-HIT (v4.8.1) to create a non-redundant library. Subsequently, psi-BLAST (BLAST+2.13.0)29 was used to align the library against known Cas proteins and annotate Cas12a candidates. An additional script filtered candidates based on canonical CRISPR-Cas locus architecture. Final candidates were validated by protein BLAST against the NCBI NR42 database, and the highest-identity hit for each was recorded. A phylogenetically diverse set of candidates was selected for downstream analysis; their GMGC IDs and taxonomy are listed in Table S2.

Functional domain annotation of Cas12a proteins

To annotate functional domains, sequences were analyzed using the hh-suite (v3.3.0).43 hhblits44 was first used to search against the UniRef30 database; significant matches were then queried against the Pfam database via hhsearch.45,46 This approach identified REC1, RuvC, and NUC domains based on Pfam profiles.47 For REC2 and PI domains, which are not adequately covered in Pfam, we constructed custom profile hidden Markov models (HMMs). Known Cas12a sequences were curated, their REC2 and PI regions were extracted and aligned with HMMER (v3.1b2),48 and the resulting HMMs were used for domain assignment in candidate proteins.

Protein structure prediction and comparison

Three-dimensional structures of candidate proteins were predicted using AlphaFold2 in monomer mode, and model confidence was assessed by predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT). The mean pLDDT across all models was 85.35, indicating overall high confidence in the predicted folds (Table S1). For each protein, five models were generated, and the top-ranked (ranked_0.pdb) was selected. Structural alignments were performed against seven canonical Cas12a proteins (AsCas12a, LbCas12a, FnCas12a, MbCas12a, PiCas12a, YmeCas12a, CmeCas12a) and 53 CasPEDIA entries using TM-align (v20220412) and visualized in PyMOL (v3.0.0) (http://www.pymol.org/pymol). TM scores and RMSD values were calculated to quantify structural similarity. In addition, domain-level structural comparisons were performed using AlphaFold2-predicted monomer structures. Individual structural domains (WED-I&REC-I, REC-II, WED-II&PI&WED-III, RuvC, and NUC) were extracted according to domain annotations. Foldseek49 was used to compute domain-level TM scores. RMSD values were calculated in PyMOL.

To further evaluate protein-nucleic acid interactions, AlphaFold350 (local installation) was used to predict structures of 21 Cas12a orthologs and PcuCas12aMAX in complex with crRNA and target DNA. For complex prediction, the target strand sequence was TAACCACCACGATGAAAAACTAATGTCTGCTCTTAAATGGCCC, the NTS sequence was GGGCCATTTAAGAGCAGACATTAGTTTTTCATCGTGGTGGTTA, and the RNA sequence consisted of the crRNA scaffold followed by the spacer sequence UAGAGCAGACAUUAGUUUUUCAUC.

Protein-nucleic acid interface analysis

Protein-nucleic acid interfaces were analyzed using the PISA module implemented in the CCP4 suite.51 Structural models of the 21 Cas12a orthologs were obtained from AlphaFold3, while experimentally determined structures of canonical Cas12a enzymes were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (AsCas12a, LbCas12a, FnCas12a; PDB: 5B43, 5XUS, 8Y0C).

For interface comparisons, interactions between the protein and the PAM and PAM-upstream nucleotides were quantified. Because the available LbCas12a structure (PDB: 5XUS) contains only the PAM (TTTA) and five upstream nucleotides of the NTS, AlphaFold3-predicted models were truncated to the corresponding nucleotide region to ensure consistency across all structures.

Sequence conservation analysis and structural mapping

Sequence conservation was calculated using ConSurf52 based on multiple sequence alignments constructed from the 21 candidate Cas12a orthologs together with representative canonical Cas12a enzymes (AsCas12a, LbCas12a, and FnCas12a). Multiple sequence alignment was performed using MAFFT (v7.490)53 with the auto parameter. Conservation scores were projected onto representative Cas12a structures (PcuCas12a) to visualize the spatial distribution of conserved and variable residues.

Contact analysis between the WED-I&REC-I domain and the PI domain

To assess contacts between the WED-I&REC-I domain and the PI domain, pairwise distances were calculated for all residues in these regions based on AlphaFold3-predicted complex structures using PyMOL’s distance command. Distance matrices were subsequently converted into contact maps, which were visualized as heatmaps to compare domain interaction patterns across orthologs.

RuvC catalytic pocket analysis

Residues constituting the RuvC catalytic pocket were identified based on previously reported key residues in AsCas12a (D908, E993, and D1263).9 The multiple sequence alignment in the sequence conservation analysis was used to map the canonical DED catalytic motif onto each ortholog, allowing identification of any substitutions or deviations in the catalytic pocket.

PAM sequence inference and visualization

PAM sequences were inferred using the CRISPRTarget54 pipeline, in which spacer sequences were queried against viral genome databases to identify putative protospacers and their flanking sequences. Retrieved flanking regions were aligned to infer conserved PAM positions. Sequence logos were generated using WebLogo3,55 providing a visual representation of nucleotide conservation at each PAM position.

UPGMA-based hierarchical clustering

To perform UPGMA clustering, a structural similarity matrix was first generated from pairwise comparisons between GMGC-derived and reference Cas12a protein structures. Hierarchical clustering was then conducted using the “linkage” function (method = ‘average’) in the SciPy package,56 followed by tree visualization and annotation in the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) environment.

Phylogenetic analysis

To infer phylogenetic relationships, Cas protein sequences were first aligned with MAFFT (v7.490) under default settings. The alignment was then used to construct a maximum-likelihood tree with FastTree (v2.1.11).57 Finally, the tree was visualized and formatted using the iTOL platform.58

Plasmid construction for protein expression

All Cas12a orthologs identified in this study, along with LbCas12a, were codon-optimized for E. coli, synthesized, and cloned into the pET-28a(+) vector via NcoI/HindIII sites (GenScript). The coding sequences of AsCas12a and AsCas12a Ultra were PCR-amplified from LentiCRISPRv2-AsCas12a (Addgene,#188492) and px330-puro-AsCas12a Ultra, respectively, and subsequently cloned into pET-28a(+). For mammalian expression, PcuCas12a and AsCas12a Ultra were human codon-optimized, synthesized, and cloned into the px330-puro(+) vector via AgeI/BamHI sites (GenScript). The PcuCas12a MAX variant was generated by site-directed mutagenesis (Fast Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit, TIANGEN, 4992901) of the wild-type PcuCas12a sequence and subsequently cloned into pET-28a(+) for prokaryotic expression. For eukaryotic expression, the PcuCas12a, PcuCas12a MAX, and AsCas12a Ultra coding sequences, each with their corresponding crRNA expression cassettes, were assembled into the px330-puro backbone (Addgene #110403). Complete plasmid maps, crRNA sequences, and primer sequences are provided in Table S5.

Protein expression and purification

Cas12a orthologs (including LbCas12a, AsCas12a, and AsCas12a Ultra) were expressed and purified from E. coli. Constructs were transformed into BL21(DE3) cells (AngYuBio, G6030). A starter culture was grown overnight and used to inoculate 2 L of LB medium. At OD600 ≈ 0.6, expression was induced with 0.5 mM IPTG, and cells were cultured at 16°C for 16 h. Cells were harvested, resuspended in lysis buffer (500 mM NaCl, 20 mM HEPES, 20 mM Tris, 10 mM imidazole, 1 mM DTT, 1% Triton X-100, 0.005 mg/mL lysozyme, pH 7.5), and lysed by sonication. After centrifugation, the clarified lysate was subjected to affinity purification on Ni-NTA agarose (Qiagen, 30210). Proteins were washed and eluted with 50 mM and 300 mM imidazole in Tris buffer. The eluted fractions were concentrated and further purified by size-exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 column (cytiva) on an ÄKTA pure system. Peak fractions were pooled, concentrated, and analyzed by SDS-PAGE. Protein concentration was measured spectrophotometrically (DS-11 FX+, DeNovix), and aliquots were stored at −80°C.

crRNA preparation via in vitro transcription

crRNAs were generated by in vitro transcription. First, DNA templates were amplified by PCR using primers designed with a T7 promoter and scaffold (upstream) and a 15-bp overlap plus a 20- to 23-bp target-specific region (downstream; Table S4). The PCR products were purified (Qiagen, 28104) and used as templates for transcription with the HiScribe T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (NEB, E2040L). After transcription, reactions were treated at 37°C for 15 min with DNase I (NEB, M0303L) to remove DNA. RNA was then purified using the Monarch RNA Cleanup Kit (NEB, T2040L), quantified (DS-11 FX+, DeNovix), aliquoted, and stored at −20°C. All crRNA sequences are listed in Table S4.

Assay of Cas12a cis-cleavage activity in vitro

The cis-cleavage activities of 21 purified Cas12a orthologs, along with LbCas12a, AsCas12a, and AsCas12a Ultra (benchmark controls), were evaluated in NEBuffer r2.1 at 37°C. A standard reaction (20 μL) contained 250 nM Cas12a protein, 500 nM in vitro transcribed crRNA, and 200 ng of a 750 bp target DNA amplicon (Table S4). To assess cleavage kinetics, reactions were sampled at 0, 0.5, 2, 10, and 20 min. To test substrate length dependence, cleavage of shorter amplicons (100, 150, 200 bp) and a supercoiled 3.5-kb plasmid was evaluated after a 10-min incubation.

The in vitro cleavage activities of wild-type PcuCas12a and the engineered PcuCas12a MAX variant were directly compared under the same buffer conditions. Reactions were sampled at 0, 0.5, 2, 5, 10, and 20 min. All reactions were stopped by adding 1 μL Proteinase K (Beyotime, ST532) followed by incubation at 55°C for 10 min. Products were resolved on 2% agarose gels (BIOWEST, BY-R0100), and cleavage efficiency was quantified via grayscale analysis using ImageJ (v1.8.0). Cleavage efficiency59 was calculated as follows:(Equation 1)CleavageEfficiency(%)=100×1−b+ca+b+c,where “a” represents intact bands, and “b + c” represent cleavage products.

Assessment of gene-editing efficiency via T7E1 assay and high-throughput sequencing

Editing efficiencies of PcuCas12a, its variants, and AsCas12a Ultra were evaluated in HEK293T cells. Cells were transfected in 24-well plates with 500 ng Cas12a plasmid and 250 ng crRNA plasmid using jetPRIME (Polyplus, 101000046), followed by 72 h 2.5 μg/mL of Puromycin selection (InvivoGen, 58-58-2). Genomic DNA was extracted with the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN, DP304), and target loci were PCR-amplified using the primers in Table S4.

For T7E1 analysis,59 200 ng of PCR product was denatured, re-annealed, and digested with T7 endonuclease I at 37°C for 30 min (NEB, M0302L). After Proteinase K treatment (Beyotime, ST532), products were separated on 2% agarose gels, and editing efficiency was calculated by grayscale analysis (ImageJ v1.8.0) using the formula described for the in vitro cleavage assay.

For high-throughput sequencing, PCR amplicons were adapter-ligated and sequenced on the Hi-Tom platform.60 Data were analyzed with CRISPResso261 to quantify indels and base substitutions.

Genome-wide off-target profiling by GUIDE-seq

Off-target activities of PcuCas12a MAX and AsCas12a Ultra were profiled genome-wide using an adapted GUIDE-seq protocol.31 Briefly, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with targeting plasmids and a double-stranded oligodeoxynucleotide (dsODN) via Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000015). After 3-day Puromycin selection (2.5 μg/mL), genomic DNA was extracted. DNA was fragmented to ∼500 bp with NEBNext dsDNA Fragmentase (NEB, M0348L) and then processed through end-repair, dA-tailing, and adapter ligation (including an 8-nt UMI) using the VAHTS Universal DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina V3 (Vazyme, ND607-01). dsODN-containing fragments were enriched by nested anchored PCR. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 and analyzed with the GUIDE-seq bioinformatics pipeline (https://github.com/aryeelab/guideseq).

In vitro transcription and preparation of PcuCas12a MAX mRNA and Tyr-crRNAs

For mRNA synthesis, PcuCas12a MAX plasmids were linearized with FastDigest EcoRI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, FD0274), gel-purified, and used as templates for transcription with the mMESSAGE mMACHINE T7 Ultra Kit (Life Technologies, AM1345). For crRNA synthesis, DNA templates containing T7 promoters were PCR-amplified, gel-purified, and transcribed using the MEGAshortscript T7 Kit (Life Technologies, AM1354). All RNA products were purified with the MEGAclear Kit (Life Technologies, AM1908), eluted in RNase-free water, aliquoted, and stored at −80°C. Before microinjection, aliquots of PcuCas12a MAX mRNA and Tyr-crRNAs were centrifuged at 18,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were transferred to RNase-free 0.2-mL PCR tubes.

Evaluation of PcuCas12a gene-editing efficiency in a mouse model

To evaluate the gene-editing efficiency of PcuCas12a in vivo, we performed zygote microinjection in mice. Superovulated 8-week-old B6D2F1 females were mated with B6D2F1 males. Zygotes were collected from oviducts 21 h after human chorionic gonadotropin administration. A mixture of PcuCas12a MAX mRNA (50 ng/μL) and Tyr-targeting crRNAs (100 ng/μL) was microinjected into the cytoplasm of fertilized eggs in M2 medium using a FemtoJet microinjector (Eppendorf) under constant flow. Following injection, embryos were cultured overnight in M16 medium supplemented with amino acids at 37°C under 5% CO2 and subsequently transferred into the oviducts of 8-week-old pseudopregnant ICR females at 0.5 days post coitum. After birth, candidate mice were genotyped via high-throughput sequencing (HTG). All primers used for genotyping are provided in Table S5. All mouse experiments were reviewed and approved by the Animal Management and Ethics Committee of Huazhong Agricultural University.

Porcine fetal fibroblast transfection and selection

Porcine fetal fibroblasts (PFFs) were isolated from ∼35-day-old Duroc female fetuses and maintained in DMEM supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum at 38.5°C. For transfection, approximately 5 ×107 PFFs were trypsinized, resuspended in 100 μL electroporation buffer, and electroporated with 10 μg of pX330-PcuCas12a MAX-crRCD163 plasmid (encoding a crRNA targeting CD163 exon 7; Table S4) using a Celetrix Electroporation (LE+) under program 540 volts. Immediately after electroporation, 500 μL pre-warmed medium was added, and cells were seeded into 10-cm dishes at a density of 10 cells/cm2. After 3 days of culture, pooled cells were isolated and transferred to 6-well plates. Upon reaching confluence, 10% of the pooled cells was lysed in 10 μL lysis buffer (0.45% NP-40, 2 mg/mL Proteinase K) at 56°C for 1 h, followed by heat inactivation at 95°C for 10 min, and the lysate was directly subjected to PCR genotyping. The cells were maintained and expanded in 6-well plates. Upon reaching confluence, the pooled population of CD163-edited PFFs was cryopreserved for subsequent use.

CRISPR-PcuCas12a MAX-mediated knockout of CD163 gene in pigs

Gene-edited pigs were generated through SCNT using an expedited protocol.35 First, male Duroc fetal fibroblasts (PFFs) were co-transfected with a PcuCas12a MAX plasmid and a CD163-targeting crRNA; the resulting pooled, unsorted edited fibroblasts were directly used as nuclear donors. Porcine ovaries were collected from an abattoir and transported at 35°C–39°C in saline. Oocytes were aspirated from follicles and matured in vitro for 42 h–44 h at 39°C. Subsequently, enucleation was performed in medium containing 7.5 μg/mL cytochalasin B (Sigma, C6762). A single donor cell was inserted into each enucleated oocyte, and fusion was induced by two electrical pulses (1.2 kV/cm, 30 μs) using an ECM 2001 Electro Cell Fusion & Electroporation System (BTX, USA). Next, reconstructed embryos were cultured for about 20 h at 39°C before being surgically transferred into the oviducts of recipient gilts on the day after estrus. Pregnancy was confirmed by the absence of estrus return or abortion, and piglets were delivered naturally. Following birth, candidate piglets were genotyped using Sanger sequencing. All primers used for genotyping are provided in Table S5. All pig experiments were reviewed and approved by the Animal Management and Ethics Committee of Huazhong Agricultural University.

Assessment of Cas12a trans-cleavage activity in vitro

Reaction conditions

A 20 μL reaction mixture was prepared containing 250 nM Cas12a protein, 500 nM corresponding crRNA, 400 nM ssDNA-FQ reporter (Tyhygene), 500 nM dsDNA target, and 1× rCutSmart buffer (NEB, B6004S). The dsDNA target was a 750-bp fragment amplified by PCR from the ASFV P72-pMD18T plasmid.33 Negative controls were performed in the absence of dsDNA target. All oligonucleotide sequences are provided in Table S4.

Incubation and fluorescence measurement

Reactions were incubated at 37°C for 15 min, followed by heat inactivation at 98°C for 2 min. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using an EnSpire microplate reader (PerkinElmer) with excitation and emission wavelengths set at 576 nm and 601 nm, respectively.

We then performed enzyme kinetic analyses to quantify the orthogonal trans-cleavage activities of LcoCas12a, FcaCas12a, EsoCas12a, and Mac2Cas12a. First, 20-μL reactions were assembled containing 10 nM Cas12a, 20 nM crRNA, 10 nM target DNA, and a series of concentrations (0–1000 nM) of the corresponding nucleotide-specific ssDNA-FQ reporter (AAAAA-FQ, TTTTT-FQ, TATAT-FQ, or TTATT-FQ) in 1× rCutSmart buffer (Table S4). Reactions were incubated at 37°C in a LightCycler 96 instrument (Roche), with fluorescence monitored every 10 s for 15 min. The fluorescence readings were converted to cleaved reporter concentration (nM) to generate product accumulation curves. Subsequently, the initial velocity (V0) for each reporter concentration was calculated from the linear portion (first 5%–10%) of these curves. Finally, V0 was plotted against reporter concentration [S], and the data were fitted to the Michaelis-Menten equation via nonlinear regression in GraphPad Prism (v9.5) to obtain kcat and Km.

Fluorescent trans-cleavage assay

Reactions (20 μL) contained 250 nM Cas12a ortholog (LcoCas12a, FcaCas12a, or Mac2Cas12a), 500 nM cognate crRNA, 500 nM dsDNA target, and 400 nM matching ssDNA-FQ reporter (ROX-, HEX-, or FAM-modified; Table S4). After incubation at 37°C for 15 min, fluorescence was measured on an EnSpire reader using excitation/emission wavelengths of 576/601 nm (ROX), 535/550 nm (HEX), or 492/517 nm (FAM). The same assay was performed across a temperature range of 16°C–65°C using ROX-labeled reporters and P72 dsDNA targets. Scaffold-modified crRNAs (Table S4) were tested under standard conditions, with fluorescence detection at 576/601 nm.

Nucleic acid preparation

Gene fragments for SVA VP1 (527 bp; KT007137.1) and CSFV E2 (507 bp; MW368990.1) were synthesized by Tsingke and cloned into pMD18T; the ASFV P72 fragment (MK333180.1) was prepared as described previously8 (Table S4). Clinical ASFV samples were obtained from infected pig swabs. Simulated SVA samples were prepared by harvesting supernatants from PK-15 cells 9 h post infection with titrated virus. For co-infection models, ASFV samples, SVA supernatants, and varying CSFV plasmid copies were combined in PBS. Nucleic acids were extracted with the QuickExtract DNA Extraction Kit (Lucigen, QE09050): samples in 80 μL buffer were incubated at 65°C (5 min) and then 95°C (5 min).

qPCR and RT-qPCR assays

The detection of ASFV was performed as previously described.8 For CSFV and SVA qPCR/RT-qPCR assays were carried out using NovoStart SYBR high-sensitivity qPCR SuperMix (Novoprotein, E099) on a CFX Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad). All primer sequences are listed in Table S4. For CSFV, cycling conditions were initial denaturation at 95°C for 60 s, followed by 35 cycles of 95°C for 20 s and 60°C for 45 s. For SVA, the Luna WarmStart RT Enzyme Mix (NEB, E3005L) was added prior to cycling; reverse transcription was performed at 55°C for 20 min, followed by the same cycling profile as for CSFV. Sensitivity and linearity were evaluated using standard curves. Samples with a quantification cycle (Ct) > 30 were considered negative. The LOD was defined as the lowest concentration that produced consistent positive results in replicate assays.

Integrated multiplex RPA-CRISPR-Cas12a detection assay

A triple-target detection assay was established by coupling multiplex RPA with orthogonal CRISPR-Cas12a trans-cleavage. First, multiplex RPA (or RT-RPA) was performed using a TwistAmp Basic Kit (TwistDx, TABAS03KIT). Each 25-μL reaction contained resuspended RPA mix, virus-specific primers (CSFV-E2, ASFV-P72, SVA-VP1; Table S4), template, and MgAc and was incubated at 39°C for 30 min. For RNA targets, 0.6 μL reverse transcriptase (100 U/μL) was included.

Subsequently, 2 μL of the RPA product was transferred to a 20-μL Cas12a detection reaction containing three ortholog-specific ribonucleoprotein complexes (LcoCas12a, FcaCas12a, Mac2Cas12a, each at 250 nM with 500 nM cognate crRNA), corresponding fluorescent reporters (ROX, HEX, FAM; Table S4), and rCutSmart buffer. The reaction was incubated at 37°C for 15 min and then heat-inactivated at 98°C for 2 min.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5). Unless otherwise stated, data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Group comparisons were conducted using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t tests, one-way ANOVA, or two-way ANOVA as appropriate (Table S6). For ANOVA analyses, post hoc multiple comparisons were performed with Dunnett’s or Šídák’s correction. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data met the underlying assumptions of the respective statistical tests used.
