Effects of systemically administered cholecalciferal and vitamin B complex on mental nerve injury in rats
Authors: Nergiz Yılmaz, Aynur Tüzüntürk, Oya Törün, Nihat Laçin, Selen Akyol Bahçeci, Fahri Emrah Soylu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to evaluate the effects of systemic cholecalciferol (vitamin D3) and vitamin B complex (B1, B6, B12) on crush-injured mental nerve in rats using histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses to assess nerve degeneration and regeneration. Thirty-two Wistar Albino rats were subjected to standardized mental nerve crush injury using a Yasargil aneurysm clip and divided into four groups: Control /Sham (Group 1), B vitamin (intraperitoneal) (Group2), D vitamin (oral) (Group 3), and combined (B + D) (Group 4). After 4 weeks, nerve samples were excised for histopathological evaluation (ghost cell and hyperchromatic nuclei counts) and immunohistochemical analysis (S100β and BIII tubulin scores). Data were analyzed using Generalized Linear Models with Bonferroni post-hoc tests. Treatment groups showed significantly lower ghost cell counts (e.g., Group 3: 15.33 ± 3.50 vs. Control: 53.67 ± 9.63, p < 0.001) and hyperchromatic nuclei counts (e.g., Group 4: 28.00 ± 5.40 vs. Control: 34.33 ± 4.37, p = 0.004) compared to controls. Immunohistochemically, Group 4 exhibited significantly higher ßIII tubulin scores (3.17 ± 0.39 vs. Control: 1.75 ± 0.46, p < 0.001), indicating enhanced axonal regeneration, while S100β scores were similar between Group 3 and 4 (1.83 ± 0.39, p = 1.000). After a 4-week healing period, cholecalciferol and vitamin B complex, particularly in combination, significantly reduced axonal degeneration and promoted early nerve regeneration in a rat mental nerve injury model. These findings suggest potential as adjunctive therapies for nerve recovery in oral and maxillofacial surgery, pending clinical validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20985-9.

Introduction

Peripheral nerve injuries (PNI) are frequent complications in oral and maxillofacial surgery interventions. There are various factors that caused injuries including mechanical such as crushing, incision and stretching, as well as thermal, ischemic, electrical, radiative and chemical1. Seddon was classified responses to nerve injuries in terms of severity of damage into three as neurapraxia, axonotmesis and neurotmesis. Axonotmesis, a form of crush injury to peripheral nerves, induces Wallerian degeneration in the distal segment of the affected nerve. Functional recovery following such injury is contingent upon the rate of axonal regeneration, which typically proceeds at approximately 1–3 mm per day. The overall duration of recovery, therefore, is variable and may extend over a period of weeks to several months, depending on the extent of the injury and the regenerative capacity of the nerve. Complete recovery is possible if the nerve ends are aligned2. Injuries to the mental nerve represent a significant clinical challenge in maxillofacial surgery, given its critical function in providing sensory innervation. As a terminal branch of the inferior alveolar nerve, the mental nerve is responsible for sensation to the chin, lower lip, and gingiva. Such injuries are commonly associated with surgical interventions, including dental implant placement, mandibular fracture repair, orthognathic procedures, and oncologic resections3–6. The manual application of force through clamps or sutures can vary based on the operator’s technique, the specific instruments used, and the duration of pressure applied. This lack of standardization can lead to inconsistent injury severity, making it challenging to compare results across experiments or ensure uniform damage to the nerve tissue7,8. Yasargil aneurysm clips, designed for permanent cerebral aneurysm obstruction, are increasingly used in experimental nerve injury models to produce standardized crush injuries9. The key advantage of these clips lies in their precisely calibrated closure force, which is achieved via electronic tuning during manufacturing. This ensures consistency in the severity of nerve injuries, addressing a common issue in crush injury studies that lack uniform pressure application9. Clinical recovery after crush type nerve injury is often incomplete and this process continues with negative symptoms. Although there is currently no gold standard agent other than conservative and surgical approaches in different types of injuries, many agents have been found to have positive and promising contributions to nerve recovery10–12 .

B-complex vitamins (B1, B6, and B12), often referred to as neurotropic B vitamins, exhibit significant biological effects on brain function and mood regulation, especially when combined for the treatment of peripheral neuropathy. Vitamin B1 supports brain function by aiding in glucose metabolism, preserving the integrity of neuronal membranes, and assisting in the production of myelin and several neurotransmitters, especially acetylcholine and serotonin. Meanwhile, Vitamin B6 is essential for neurotransmitter synthesis, facilitating the conversion of L-DOPA to dopamine, 5-hydroxytryptophan to serotonin, and glutamate to gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)13. Vitamin B12 has a particularly important function in the synthesis of myelin and, together with vitamin B6, in the methionine cycle14,15.

There are two natural forms of vitamin D: D2 (ergocalciferol) and D3 (cholecalciferol). In humans, the primary source is vitamin D3, which is generated in the skin through the conversion of 7-dehydrocholesterol under UV-B light exposure. There are vitamin D receptors in neurons and Schwann cells. Cholecalciferol has been shown to increase neurotrophin production and axon growth16. In addition, cholecalciferol has been reported to increase the expression of genes that trigger myelinization17.

In this study, it was aimed to investigate the effects of B vitamin complex, cholecalciferol (vitamin D3), and their combination on crush-type nerve injury, a common and often debilitating condition encountered in clinical practice. The primary focus was to assess how these nutrients, individually and together, may influence the recovery and regeneration of nerve tissue following such injuries. Crush-type nerve injuries, which occur when a nerve is compressed or pinched, can lead to significant motor and sensory deficits. By evaluating the therapeutic potential of B vitamins and cholecalciferol, the study sought to explore whether these compounds could enhance nerve healing, reduce inflammation, and improve functional outcomes, offering new insights for treatment strategies in cases of peripheral nerve trauma.

Methods

Ethical statement

Approval of ethics was given by Ege University Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee (2020-023). This study was funded by Izmir Katip Celebi University Scientific Research Projects Commission with the Project number 2020-TDU-DİŞF-0013.

Study design

All experimental procedures were conducted with strict adherence to the ARRIVE Guidelines (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments), as published in PLoS Biology (Volume 8, Issue 6, e1000412, 2010). Compliance with these guidelines ensures that animal studies are conducted and reported ethically, allowing for better reproducibility and scientific scrutiny. All animals received humane care in compliance with “The Principles of Laboratory Animal Care” formulated by the National Society of Medical Research and “The Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” published by the U.S National Institutes of Health (NIH). By following these guidelines, the research team aimed to uphold high standards of scientific rigor and transparency in their experimental protocols. 32 healthy, 3-month-old Wistar Albino male rats, an average weight of 250 g were used. Animals were obtained from Ege University Laboratory Animals Research and Application Center. Rats were divided into four groups with equal number of rats. In all study groups, after the left mental nerve was exposed, Yasargil aneurysm clip was used for 60 s to create a crush type nerve damage, and the operation area was closed with sutures.

Surgical procedure

General anesthesia was administered to rats with 90 mg/kg intramuscular ketamine hydrochloride (Ketalar; Pfizer, Istanbul, Turkey) and 10 mg/kg xylazine (Rompun; Bayer, Istanbul, Turkey). After the neck regions were shaved and antisepsis of the surgical field was provided, local anesthesia was performed around the surgical site with artricaine solution containing 1:200,000 epinephrine (Ultracain-DS hoechst Marion Roussel, Istanbul, Turkey) to ensure hemostasis. Dermal incision was made at the middle of the neck, the skin was dispensed with ecarteurs and the mental nerve was exposed with the blunt dissection by microsurgical clamps and crushed with Yaşargil aneurysm clip for 60 s (Fig.1). Following the completion of the surgical procedures, the muscle tissue was sutured using a 4/0 glycolide-based absorbable suture (Vicryl, Ethicon, Brussels, Belgium), and the skin was closed with a 4/0 silk suture (Doğsan, Turkey). Group 1 was control of the study groups and 1 ml of saline was given locally to the damaged area. The right mental nerve of the rats in the control group was the sham group and no damage was done to nerve. In Group 2, rats were systemically administered 1 mg/kg B vitamin complex (Norogrizovim I.M. 1 mg/1mL) intraperitoneally for 28 days. In Group 3, rats were administered oral cholecalciferol (Devit-3 Oral Drop 50000 IU/15 Ml) at a dose of 3500 IU/kg/week for a total of 4 times. In Group 4, rats were systemically administered 1 mg/kg B vitamin complex intraperitoneally for 28 days and oral cholecalciferol at a dose of 3500 IU/kg/week.

Postoperative care

To manage postoperative infection and pain, enrofloxacin (Baytril-K® 2.5 mg/kg, IM) and meloxicam (Maxicam® 1 mg/kg, IM) were administered once daily for five days. Additionally, a topical antibiotic (Neo Caf Spray, Intervent, MSD, Italy) was applied to the wound site once daily for five days to prevent localized infections.

Sacrification procedure

Rats were sacrificed by cervical dislocation after the 28-day healing period, the nerve tissue was excised and trasferred in 10% formaldehyde solution for histological examinations.

Histological analysis

For histopathological evaluation, after the sections were stained with hematoxilen and eosin, imaging was performed with a light microscope (BX 43, Olympus). The presence of hyperchromatic nuclei and ghost cells in the light microscope were indicators of degeneration. The areas were counted with Image J Program (US National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Sections were transferred to distilled water and the tissue borders were drawn using Dakopen (lot 008877, Thermofischer, MA, USA). After transferring the sections to phosphate buffer solution (PBS), they were treated with 3% H2O2 solution for 20 min. The sections were washed 3 times with PBS for 5 min each wash, then treated with blocking solution (lot 1754084 A, Life Technologies, Frederick, MD, USA) for 10 min to block nonspecific antibody binding. After removing the blocking solution, the tissues were incubated with primary antibodies against beta III-tubulin (ab18207, Abcam, Boston, USA) and S100 beta (ab52642, Abcam, Boston, USA) overnight at + 4 °C.

Primary antibodies S-100 and ß3-Tubulin were placed separately on sections on pre-labeled slides. They were left overnight at + 4 °C in the refrigerator. The next day, the sections were washed again with 3 × 5 PBS. Biotinylated secondary antibodies (for rabbit compatibility) (Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (HRP) (ab6721)), (for mouse compatibility) (Rabbit anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Secondary Antibody [Biotin] (NB720-B)) for 30 min to make the primary antibodies more visible. They were then washed again with 3 × 5 min PBS. Treated with streptavidin (Thermo Fisher STREPTAVIDIN- HORSERADISH, 1 MG S911) for 30 min, which formed a complex with biotin. Then washed again with PBS for 3 × 5 min. The secondary antibody was made visible using a chromogen called DAB (DAB Chromogen/Substrate Kit Scytek ACK125) for 5 min. The process was stopped with PBS for 5 min. The sections were washed 3 × 5 min with distilled water and stained with Mayer’s hematoxylin (Thermo Fisher 008011) for 1–5 min to stain the cell nuclei. The sections were then washed with 3 × 5 distilled water to remove excess stain. After being held in 80% alcohol for 1 min, they were slightly dried and left to clear in xylene for 30 min. Entellan was used for sealing. To visualize the immunohistochemical reaction, the sections were stained with DAB-Plus substrate kit (lot#1636518A, Life Technologies, Frederick, MD, USA) for 4–6 min. For contrast staining, Gill III Hematoxylin (cat#1051740500, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) staining was performed and the sections were washed under running water for 45 s, then mounted with Entellan mounting medium (lot#1079610100, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Images of the sections were acquired at various magnifications using a Zeiss Imager A2 light microscope. To evaluate axonal regeneration and growth, beta III tubulin primary antibody was used for the immunoreactivity of regenerated axons after nerve damage. The number of Schwann cell and inflammation was evaluated by S100B primary antibody. In immunohistochemical examination, scoring was performed by evaluating the ratio of positively stained cells and areas. Immunoreactivity intensities were scored as no staining (0), weak (1), moderate (2), intense (3) and very intense (4).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS for Windows, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were expressed as frequency (n), percentage (%), mean ± standard deviation (x̅ ± SD), minimum (min), and maximum (max) values. The Shapiro–Wilk test and Q-Q plots were utilized to assess the normality of quantitative data. For comparisons of independent groups with more than two subcategories, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied if the data were normally distributed. Post hoc comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s test when homogeneity of variances was confirmed. Correlations between categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson’s exact chi-square test for r × c tables. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Histopathological observations

Histopathologic parameters of our study were the number of ghost cells and hyperchromatic nuclei, which are indicators of axonal degeneration and edema. In the sham side of the control group, normal axon and vascular structures of the peripheral nerve were observed. In the study side of control group, severe axonolysis, vacuolized axons (Ghost Cell), intense hyperchromatic nuclei, inflammatory cells migrating out of the vascular structures and edema in the connective tissue were observed (Fig. 2). In Group 2, axon degeneration and edema were less than the control group (Fig. 3). In Group 3, axon degeneration was more than the Group 2, edema and the number of hyperchromatic nuclei were less than the control group (Fig. 4). In Group 4, ghost cells in the myelin sheath were less than the intact axons (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Consequences of peripheral nerve injuries are significant for both patient and clinician. Experimental studies had shown that medical treatment as well as surgical treatment can be effective in the nerve healing process3,18. A wide variety of animal and neural models have been used in studies on peripheral nerve regeneration19. Many different peripheral nerve injury models on facial, sciatic and hypoglossus nerves have been studied in the literature20,21. The most studied model is sciatic nerve model because of its dimensional advantages22,23. However, it is important to investigate models containing only sensory fibers in the head, as well as models using mixed nerves containing both sensory and motor fibers24. Moreover, another important factor is the clinical relevance of the study. The selection of the mental, facial or hypoglossal nerves seems to be more appropriate than the sciatic or median nerve if one is interested in conducting a trial in the clinical setting of maxillofacial surgery. Therefore, the rat mental nerve model was chosen in this study as one of the most injured sensory nerves in oral and maxillofacial surgery procedures.

Most of the methods reported in the literature25,26 for the crush type injury model could not provide a standardized force. In our study, crush injury was performed with Yasargil aneurysm clips22 that has a standardized compression force, with 1.77 N magnitude in this study, and the compression time was determined as 60 s according to the literature8,27. A study by Sarıkçıoğlu et al. highlighted the importance of this standardized method when using Yasargil aneurysm clips on rat optic nerves, proving its efficacy in creating reproducible injury severities for further neurological evaluation9.

Certain vitamins play a critical role in maintaining nerve health and promoting the recovery of injured nerves14,16,28. Kato et al. demonstrated that vitamin B complex significantly alleviated diabetes-like morphological abnormalities caused by acrylamide neuropathy, including mild Schwann cell atrophy and disintegration of the sciatic nerve myelin sheath28. Ehmedah et al. conducted a study using a rat femoral nerve injury model, where vitamin B complex was administered intraperitoneally. The results demonstrated a significant decrease in pro-inflammatory markers and an increase in anti-inflammatory cytokine expression, facilitating the resolution of neuroinflammation29.

Although there are many studies in the literature on therapeutic effect of cholecalciferol in central nervous system damage17,30, studies showing its effects on myelinization and regeneration of damaged peripheral nerve are limited31,32.

In a study conducted on the facial nerve of rabbits, histological analysis revealed that cholecalciferol significantly enhanced both functional recovery and myelination after 12 weeks of treatment. This suggests that cholecalciferol may play a beneficial role in nerve repair and regeneration, highlighting its potential therapeutic value in improving outcomes following nerve injuries. The observed improvements in both functional and structural recovery indicate that cholecalciferol could be a promising agent for promoting nerve regeneration. 33. Regarding the oral dose regulation of cholecalciferol, a comparative study in the literature with low (100 IU/kg/day) and high doses (500 IU/kg/day) of has shown more favorable results at high doses32–34. In our study, we administered high-dose (500 IU/kg/day) oral cholecalciferol which increases axonal regeneration and reduces neural inflammation.

The therapeutic effectiveness of the cholecalciferol and vitamin B complex was demonstrated in a significant way as a result to the histological methods used. Our utilization of ImageJ for semi-automated morphometric analysis aligns with the latest developments in nerve histomorphometry35,36. These studies highlight the value of consistent, repeatable techniques for measuring nerve regeneration, especially in peripheral nerve injury models. This study’s incorporation of semi-automated morphometric analysis highlights its usefulness as a tool for assessing therapeutic interventions in models of nerve injury. The significant reduction in ghost cells and hyperchromatic nuclei in the treatment groups, as observed through H&E staining, provided clear evidence of reduced axonal degeneration and edema compared to the control group (Table 1). These findings were corroborated by immunohistochemical results, which showed elevated beta III-tubulin expression in the combined treatment group, indicating enhanced axonal regeneration (Table 2). Similarly, the lower S100β scores in the treatment groups suggested a reduction in neuroinflammatory Schwann cell activity, aligning with the anti-inflammatory effects of the administered vitamins37–39. The integration of these histological and immunohistochemical data allowed for a comprehensive assessment of both structural and molecular changes in the injured mental nerve, reinforcing the synergistic effects of cholecalciferol and vitamin B complex. Our histological results’ consistency with previous studies37,40,41demonstrates the validity of our procedures, and the new use of cholecalciferol in this situation suggests that its function in nerve repair may be further investigated. Ghost cells, S100ß levels, and hyperchromatic nuclei are well-established markers of neuroinflammation, with increased numbers indicating higher levels of tissue damage and inflammatory response. The concurrent elevation of these markers strongly correlates with increased neuroinflammation and damage within the nervous tissue. Their presence is frequently used to evaluate the extent of injury and the efficacy of therapeutic interventions aimed at reducing inflammation and promoting nerve regeneration42,43. In our study, we observed a significant reduction in the number of ghost cells, S100ß levels, and hyperchromatic nuclei in the groups receiving the B vitamin complex. This finding aligns with previous research, which indicates that B vitamins, particularly when combined, can modulate neuroinflammation and contribute to better nerve health14,44. Our histopathological analysis further supports these results, as it demonstrated that both cholecalciferol and the B vitamin complex had a positive impact on reducing neural edema and inflammation (Table 2). These outcomes are consistent with prior studies, which have shown similar effects of vitamin supplementation on neuroinflammatory processes29. The ability of Schwann cells to switch between their mature, myelinating phenotype and the repair phenotype highlights their plasticity and importance in peripheral nerve healing. Studies have demonstrated that enhancing Schwann cell activity, either through biological therapies or pharmacological agents, can significantly improve outcomes in nerve injury models45,46. Gupta and Steward47 investigated the mitogenic effects of Schwann cells after injury in the sciatic nerve by S100ß primary antibody. It was observed that the number of Schwann cells reached the highest value in the fourth week in the experimental group and then started to decrease immediately. Our results corresponded with previous findings, as S100ß antibody immunoreactivity was significantly elevated in the control group (p < 0.001) compared to the other study groups (Tables 2 and 3). Neurotubules and neuroflaments are markers of axonal growth. Both cytoskeletal proteins can be immunohistochemically visualized with beta III tubulin48. In the study that investigated the effects of sciatic nerve injury on beta tubulin gene expression in rats, day 1,7, 14 and 28 after axotomy. It was concluded that the beta III tubulin levels of the experimental group were lower on day 1, but significantly higher on days 7, 14, and 28 compared to the control group49. Beta III tubulin level was found to be higher in the study groups compared to the control group in our study. Beta III tubulin level in the combined group was superior to the other groups (Table 2). Despite the benefits of our histological method, some drawbacks should be considered in account. The constantly changing processes of nerve regeneration, such as early axonal sprouting or minute alterations in myelin structure, may not be adequately captured by H&E staining, despite its effectiveness in evaluating general tissue morphology. However, immunohistochemical staining is more specific, it may vary depending on a number of factors, including staining intensity, tissue fixation quality, and antibody specificity41. Riberio et al. 50histologically examined rat sciatic nerves with crush-type damage and axonotmesis. Prior to the analysis, the rats were divided into two groups: one group received ganglioside treatment and the other did not. They assessed macrophage presence, Schwann cell hyperplasia, Wallerian degeneration, myelin sheath damage, and myelinated nerve fiber degeneration on days 8, 15, 30, and 60 post-injuries. While no difference in regeneration was observed between the two groups on days 8 and 15, a significant increase was detected in the treated group on days 30 and 60. Regarding degeneration, a significant decrease was reported in the treated group only on day 60. Furthermore, our study’s single time point of analysis (28 days after the injury) restricts our comprehension of the temporal dynamics of nerve repair. According to the earlier literature40,46,51, morphometric alterations like Schwann cell proliferation and axonal regrowth change dramatically over time. In animal models, the nerve segment expected to regenerate after injury is shorter than in human, and the regeneration rate is higher (2–3 mm/day), so the assessment methods for regeneration also differ from human52. According to the literature, a 4-week period was considered sufficient for the histological evaluation of nerve regeneration, and the evaluation results confirmed that this period was sufficient. Future studies could provide a more detailed examination of regeneration kinetics by including multiple time points (e.g., 7, 14, and 28 days), which could help determine the appropriate timing to take vitamin supplements.

Conclusion

This study indicates that systemic administration of cholecalciferol and vitamin B complex, especially in combination, significantly decreased axonal degeneration and promoted early nerve regeneration over a 4-week period after mental nerve crush injury in rats. Histopathological analysis revealed that treatment groups had significantly lower numbers of ghost cells (e.g., Group 3: 15.33 ± 3.50 vs. Control: 53.67 ± 9.63, p < 0.001) and hyperchromatic nuclei (e.g., Group 4: 28.00 ± 5.40 vs. Control: 34.33 ± 4.37, p = 0.004) than controls. Although S100β scores were comparable between the Group 3 and 4 (1.83 ± 0.39, p = 1.000), immunohistochemical results showed improved axonal regeneration in the combined group, with higher ß3tubulin scores (3.17 ± 0.39 vs. Control: 1.75 ± 0.46, p < 0.001). According to these findings, cholecalciferol and vitamin B complex might function in combination to decrease neuroinflammation and increase the growth of early axons. This suggests that these substances could be used as supplemental treatments to assist in the recovery of nerves following oral and maxillofacial surgeries. We recommend the combination of cholecalciferol and B-complex vitamins should be added to the clinical routine after surgical operations when nerve damage is expected. These results are limited to early nerve repair because of the single 4-week time point; longer-term preclinical research is required to verify long-lasting effects. Furthermore, the translational relevance of these findings may be strengthened through combining morphometric analysis with functional assessments like sensory testing or electrophysiological recordings. Clinical trials are necessary to confirm the safety and effectiveness of these supplements in individuals with mental nerve injuries; however, these preclinical results are encouraging.
