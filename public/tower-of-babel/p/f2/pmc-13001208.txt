De novo transcriptome and tissue-specific gene expression analysis in three types of barley following exploring the mechanism of quercetin content in purple barley (Hordeum vulgare)
Authors: Md. Mahmudul Hasan, Md. Sohel Mia, Jiazhen Yang, Tao Yang, Yawen Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBarley (Hordeum vulgare L.) is a major cereal crop and an important source of bioactive compounds, including flavonoids. Among these, quercetin is a biologically active flavonol with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties. Quercetin accumulation in barley is type-dependent and was detected exclusively in purple barley compared with yellow and black barley. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying quercetin biosynthesis and accumulation in purple barley remain unclear.ResultsIn this study, comparative transcriptome analysis was performed on yellow, black, and purple barley grains at the grain-filling stage to elucidate the transcriptional regulation of quercetin biosynthesis. RNA sequencing followed by de novo assembly resulted in the structural optimization of 6,393 genes, of which 4,986 were functionally annotated. A total of 12 alternative splicing event types and 561,743 putative single-nucleotide polymorphisms were identified. Differential expression analysis revealed 4,540 differentially expressed genes, with purple barley exhibiting the highest number of upregulated genes. Many of these genes were enriched in biological process, cellular component, and molecular function categories and formed dense protein–protein interaction networks. KEGG pathway analysis showed significant enrichment of phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways in purple barley. Furthermore, significantly higher expression of key quercetin biosynthesis genes, F3ʹH, F3ʹ5ʹH, FLS1, and FLS2, during grain maturation than the grain-filling stage might guide their regulatory roles in upward transition of quercetin accumulation into the seed along with maturity.ConclusionsThis study provides the first transcriptome-level comparison of quercetin-associated gene regulation among three tyeps of barley. The results reveal a coordinated transcriptional framework underlying quercetin biosynthesis in purple barley and offer valuable genetic resources for future functional studies and breeding strategies aimed at enhancing quercetin content in purple barley.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08414-z.

Introduction

Barley (Hordeum vulgare L.) is one of the four major global cereal crops and was domesticated approximately 10,000 years ago in the eastern Mediterranean region before spreading globally (http://faostat.fao.org), including China [1]. In China, barley is predominantly cultivated in Tibet, Qinghai, Sichuan, and Yunnan provinces, with Diqen Prefecture in Yunnan serving as a major production area due to its unique climatic conditions [2, 3].

Beyond its agronomic and nutritional importance, barley is also a valuable source of bioactive compounds, particularly flavonoids. Among these, quercetin is a major flavonol with well-established antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties that have a great impact in plant defense system and human health [4]. Owing to its multiple hydroxyl groups, quercetin efficiently scavenges free radicals, thereby reducee oxidative stress and cellular damage [5, 6]. It has also potential roles in neuroprotective mechanisms, lipid metabolism regulation., Quercetin is also applied in the cosmetic and food industries [7]. However, the low solubility and limited bioavailability of quercetin restrict its broader utilization, prompting interest in improving its natural accumulation and extraction efficiency [8]. Hence, increasing attention has focused on elucidating the genetic and molecular regulation of quercetin biosynthesis to enhance its natural accumulation through breeding and molecular strategies.

In plants, quercetin is synthesized through the phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways, which represent one of the most extensively studied secondary metabolic routes. The pathway is initiated by phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), which converts phenylalanine into cinnamic acid, followed by cinnamate 4-hydroxylase (C4H) and 4-coumarate–CoA ligase (4CL), generating p-coumaroyl-CoA as a central intermediate [9, 10]. This compound enters the flavonoid biosynthesis branch, where chalcone synthase (CHS) catalyzes the formation of chalcones, which are subsequently isomerized by chalcone isomerase (CHI) to produce flavanones [11].

Further hydroxylation and oxidation steps are mediated by flavonoid 3′-hydroxylase (F3ʹH), flavonoid 3ʹ,5ʹ-hydroxylase (F3ʹ5ʹH), and flavonol synthase (FLS), which lead to the formation of flavonols, including quercetin [12–16]. The functional roles of these enzymes and their transcriptional regulation have been well characterized in model plants and crops such as Arabidopsis thaliana [14], maize [15], rice [16], grape [17], and apple [18], where coordinated expression of PAL, CHS, F3H, F3′H, F3′5′H and FLS has been shown to directly regulate flavonoid accumulation, pigmentation, and stress adaptive responses.

In barley, quercetin content is variety/cultivar specific which is strongly associated with grain pigmentation. Among different types of barley, quercetin content is higher in purple and black barley than yellow barley [19, 20]. In addition, light, temperature, nutrient availability, and developmental stages further critically influence quercetin accumulation, where, higher levels are found in flowers and developing grains [21]. However, despite substantial knowledge of the biochemical pathway, the transcriptome-level regulatory mechanisms governing quercetin biosynthesis and accumulation in purple barley remain poorly understood.

Therefore, the current research was to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying quercetin accumulation in purple barley by (i) quantifying quercetin content in yellow, black, and purple barley, (ii) performing comparative transcriptome analysis at defined developmental stages, and (iii) identifying key genes, pathways, and regulatory networks involved in quercetin biosynthesis. The research provides transcriptome-level deep insights into quercetin regulation in barley and establishes a molecular foundation for future functional studies and targeted improvement of quercetin content in barley.

Materials and methods

Determination of quercetin content in yellow, black and purple barley by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

Seeds of yellow, black, and purple barley were dried and ground. Using methanol solvent, 1 g of the ground powder was utilized for extraction following ultrasonic treatment as a solvent for 30 min. Following the extract filtration, the filtrate was evaporated under reduced pressure. The residue was dissolved in double-distilled water and extracted again using ethyl acetate solvent. After completing the water phase evaporation, the residue was again dissolved in 10 mL of methanol and filtered on a 0.45 μm membrane filter. Quercetin content was measured by HPLC using a YMC-Pack ODS AM-303 column (5 μm, 250 mm × 4.6 mm i.d.). The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% (v/v) acetic acid in water and acetonitrile, and was applied under isocratic conditions with a water:acetonitrile ratio of 70:30. The flow rate was maintained at 0.8 mL min⁻1, and detection was performed at 280 nm. The 10 μL sample was injected for each measurement. Peak retention times and areas were considered to calculate the quercetin content.

Plant materials, RNA extraction, and library preparation

Yellow barley (variety: Yundamai No. 12), black barley (variety:Yungongmai No.4), and purple barley (variety: Ziguangmangluoerleng) were grown under natural field conditions at the Yuxi Experimental Station in Yunnan, China. Standard agricultural practices were followed throughout the cultivation period. During plant growth, the average temperature ranged between 10 and 22 °C. Daytime temperatures usually reached 20–25 °C, while nighttime temperatures remained around 8–12 °C. Noticeable temperature differences between day and night were observed, with cooler conditions early in the season that gradually became warmer later. Relative humidity remained moderate, ranging from 55 to 70%. Rainfall was limited because the crop was grown toward the end of the dry season, and only occasional light rain occurred. On average, plants received 6–8 h of sunlight per day. Most days were sunny or partly cloudy, with very few rainy days. Climatic information for the experimental area was obtained from the China Meteorological Data Service Center (CMDC: http://data.cma.cn) [22].

Immature spikelets were collected during the grain-filling stage, approximately 30 days after flowering, which occurred around 120 days after sowing in mid-April. Similarly,for evaluating the expression of the selected genes at physiological maturity stage in barley, spikelet was harvested at 45 days after flowering consequences on 135 days after planting. Immediately after harvesting, the samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until further processing. Total RNA was isolated from yellow barley (c), black barley (h), and purple barley (z) using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For each barley type, three independent biological samples were prepared for RNA sequencing.

The quality and quantity of the extracted RNA were evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer. After quality confirmation, the RNA samples were sent to a commercial sequencing facility in Beijing, China, for library preparation and RNA-Seq analysis. During library construction, mRNA was separated using oligo(dT) beads and fragmented into smaller pieces. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using random primers, followed by second-strand synthesis. The cDNA fragments were then purified, repaired, ligated with sequencing adapters, size-selected, and amplified by PCR to generate final libraries. Library concentration and insert size were checked using standard fluorometric and bioanalyzer methods, and accurate quantification was performed by qPCR. High-throughput sequencing was conducted on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform, producing paired-end reads of 150 bp, with at least 20 million reads generated for each sample [23].

Sequencing, assembly, and annotation

The TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v4-cBot-HS (Illumina) was utilized to cluster the index-coded samples on a cBot Cluster Generation System. Following clustering and library preparations, paired-end reads were generated using the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform. Low-quality, adapter-containing, and poly-N reads were removed from the draft data to generate the clean reads. In addition, Q20, Q30, and GC contents of the clean data were carefully computed. Using the Tophat2 program (parameters set: -N 5 -p 30 -i 20), all downstream analyses were done using clean, high-quality data that were perfectly aligned to the reference genome: Hv_IBSC_PGSB_v2 [24]. The length of the mentioned reference genome was 4.83 Gbp, which had a 4 Mbp N50 contigs value, and 7 scaffolds pseudomolecules [25].

The longest transcript of each gene was named as unigenes. To evaluate the potential role of mRNA, all observed unigenes were blasted in the NR (NCBI-non-redundant protein sequences) to screen the homolog genes having established functions [26]. Following that, the Blast2GO web server (http://www.blast2go.de) was utilized for obtaining the best gene ontology (GO) terms [27]. For identification, functional classifications, and molecular pathway analysis, the assembled unigenes were also evaluated in the Swiss-Prot (a manually annotated and reviewed protein sequence database), NT (NCBI nucleotide sequences), Pfam (Protein family), KOG (euKaryotic Ortholog Groups)/COG (Clusters of Orthologous Groups of proteins), and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) Orthology databases. The RNASeq data were enumerated in Table S1.

Alternative splicing event quantification

Alternative splicing (AS) profile (b-1.0.4) software [28] was performed 12 types of alternative splicing. These included alternative exon ends (AE), intron retention (IR), multi-intron retention (MIR), multi-exon skipping (MSKIP), skipped exon (SKIP), alternative 5′ first exon (TSS), alternative 3′ last exon (TTS), and approximate exons (XAE, XIR, XMIR, XMSKIP, and XSKIP) events.

Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) calling

Before sorting and deleting duplicates, SAMtools fixmate was utilized for fixing and filling the mate information. Then, along with SAMtools v0.1.18, Picard-tools v1.41 was also utilized for sorting and removing the duplicate reads, and merging bam alignment findings of each sample. The GATK2 software was utilized to call SNPs [29]. The original vcf files were filtered using GATK standard filtering (clusterWindowSize: 10), and only SNPs having a distance higher than five were kept. Variants were excluded based on the cut off value of MQ0 > 4 and (MQ0/(1.0 × DP) > 0.1); QUAL < 30.0; and QD < 5.0 or HRun > 5.

Differential expression analysis

Gene expression is time and condition-specific. Genes or transcripts with significantly varying expression levels are called differentially expressed genes (DEGs) or DETs under different circumstances. Following StringTie, the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped (FPKM) optimized the transcript reconstruction and gene expression [30]. Using StringTie, each sample was annotated to fresh transcripts without following the -e argument. After merging the files of all gene transfer format (gtf) from individual samples into a cumulative gtf file, the -e argument was used to calculate the normalized expression value over the samples.

Differential expression pattern was analyzed using DESeq2 program (v1.10.1) [31]. DESeq2 utilized a negative binomial distribution model to identify digital gene expression patterns. Here, the treatment group, having three biological replicates were compared with the control group. DESeq2 solved the compensating problem for library size disparities while retaining the library size impact. In DESeq2, genes with FPKM values of 0 were eliminated before determining the scaling factor of each sample. Differentially expressed genes were determined by comparing FPKM values and scaling factors. DEGs were calculated following an absolute log2 (Fold Change) larger than one and a False Discovery Rate (FDR) less than 0.01. Fold change was considered the expression ratio between samples/groups. FDR was calculated by correcting the significant difference in p-value. Differential expression analysis in transcriptome sequencing used an independent statistical hypothesis test for the expression level of several genes, which may also result in false positives. In a hypothesis test, the significance level based on p-value was corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg correction model.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis

The DEG sequences were blasted (blastx) against the genomes of model plant species (Arabidopsis thaliana), whose protein data were available in the STRING database (http://string-db.org/), to obtain protein–protein interactions for the DEGs. Then, the PPI of these DEGs was visualized using Cytoscape [32, 33]. Here, the full STRING network, edge evidence, and the lower confidence parameter (0.150) were set [34].

GO and KEGG functional annotation of the DEGs

Gene Ontology enrichment analysis of the DEGs was done by the GOseq R packages based on Wallenius non-central hyper-geometric distribution [35], which can adjust for gene length bias in DEGs. We have used the KOBAS [36] software integrated in KEGG to assess the statistical enrichment of differentially expressed genes in KEGG pathways. Further, to explore the quercetin biosynthesis regulatory genes in the flavonoids biosynthesis pathway, the online KEGG database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) was utilized [37, 38].

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) validation for expression of major genes involved in quercetin biosynthesis

To explore the expression patterns of major genes involved in quercetin biosynthesis in barley, the expression patterns of F3ʹ5ʹH, F3ʹH, FLS1, and FLS2 genes were observed in grain and leaf tissue under grain filling and maturation stages in barley. To obtain the specified tissues in the mentioned stages, barley was cultivated in the field under natural conditions at Yunnan Yuxi city Experimental Station, Yunnan, Kunming, China, following recommended intercultural operations. For gene expression analysis during the grain-filling stage, immature barley spikelets were collected 30 days after flowering (120 days after planting). For the physiological maturity stage, spikelets were collected 45 days after flowering (135 days after planting). Then, the qRT-PCR experiment was performed to evaluate the expression of the selected four quercetin biosynthesis genes (F3ʹ5ʹH, F3ʹH, FLS1, and FLS2) in grain and leaf tissues.

The total RNA was isolated from barley leaf and grain tissues utilizing an E.Z.N.A. Plant RNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Inc., USA), following the manufacturer's guidelines. The isolated RNA was subsequently reverse transcribed into first-strand cDNA using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix in qPCR Kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd, China) with a 20 μL reaction volume. The specific primers for quantitative PCR were synthesized utilizing Primer (version 5.0; Premier, Canada), employing HvActin as the reference gene. The HvActin gene was selected as the eference genes since it showed stable expression across different tissues and developmental stages [39]. The primer sequences were enumerated in Table S1. Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted utilizing the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix Kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., China) and a fluorescence quantification kit on a CFX96 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, USA). The relative gene expression patterns were evaluated using the 2−ΔΔCT technique [40].

Alternative splicing event quantification

The 12 distinct types of AS among the three types of barley, more specifically AE, IR, MIR, MSKIP, SKIP, TSS, TTS, XAE, XIR, XMIR, XMSKIP, and XSKIP might guide their potential roles in altering protein structures (Table 3). The conserved AS types among three barley types might guide their conserved genomic structure following consistent AS modules (Fig. 4). TSS event was the most abundant AS type across all three sets of transcriptomes, following TTS and IR events. However, in exon-skipping–related events, the XMSKIP occurred at lowest frequency. Discussion

Since transcriptome analysis guides the roles of differentially expressed genes interacting different signaling pathways for synthesizing different metabolites, comparative transcriptome analysis of yellow, black, and purple barley was conducted to elucidate the molecular basis of quercetin biosynthesis and accumulation in purple barley.. Following HPLC analysis, the quercetin content was exclusively detected in purple barley grains, highlighting a distinct metabolic characteristic of this barleyr.

Following de novo transcriptome assembly, numerous novel genes, many of which are annotated to secondary metabolite–related pathways were identified based on eggNOG and KOG analyses. Notably, a substantial number of unigenes are associated with the phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways, which are known to give rise to quercetin and related flavonoids [41]. Similar enrichment of these pathways has been reported in flavonoid-rich crops such as maize [15], rice [16], grape [42], and apple [18], indicating a conserved biosynthetic framework across the plant species. Our findings suggest that enhanced transcriptional activation of these pathways might contribute to the elevated quercetin accumulation in purple barley.

Alternative splicing analysis revealed that TSS and TTS events are predominant across all barley types. Although overall AS patterns are conserved, these events may fine-tune gene expression affecting transcript stability, localization, and translational efficiency [43, 44]. Similar regulatory roles of AS in flavonoid-related genes were observed in tomato [45] and grape [46], suggesting that AS may acts as an additional layer of post-transcriptional regulation contributing to metabolic output.

Single-nucleotide polymorphism analysis showed a relatively low SNP density across most genes, with a subset of nonsynonymous mutations that may potentially affect protein structure or function [23]. Such sequence variation may lead to functional diversification of enzymes or regulatory proteins involved in secondary metabolism, thereby contributing to phenotypic differences in quercetin accumulation among different barley types. Comparable associations between SNP variation and flavonoid content have been identified in maize [47] and soybean [48], indicating that both regulatory and coding sequence variation could influence flavonoid biosynthesis.

Differential gene expression analysis revealed that purple barley exhibited the highest number of upregulated genes, particularly those involved in secondary metabolism [49]. This observation, together with clear transcriptional separation in principal component analysis, indicates that quercetin accumulation is associated with coordinated upregulation of biosynthetic genes rather than isolated gene-level changes [50, 51]. Such transcriptional reprogramming of flavonoid biosynthesis was observed in pigmented rice [52] and purple maize [53], supporting the conserved role of pathway-wide activation in flavonoids accumulation.

Protein–protein interaction analysis revealed denser interaction networks among differentially expressed genes in purple barley compared with black and yellow barley, suggesting coordinated regulation of proteins associated with secondary metabolism [54]. Similar network-level coordination among flavonoid biosynthetic enzymes and regulatory proteins were characterized in flavonoid-rich crops like maize [15], grape [42], and Arabidopsis [55], where tightly connected protein networks facilitate efficient metabolic flux through the phenylpropanoid and flavonoid pathways. The enhanced connectivity observed in purple barley likely reflects a conserved regulatory strategy for flavonoid accumulation, while its higher interaction density suggests a more integrated network contributing to elevated quercetin levels [55]. These interaction patterns provide a systems-level basis for identifying regulatory targets to enhance quercetin biosynthesis in barley.

In our findings, the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway (ko00940) contained the second-highest number of annotated unigenes, indicating its central role in secondary metabolism in purple barley. Consistently, 17 unigenes were assigned to the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway (ko00941), supporting transcriptional activation of flavonoid biosynthesis and its association with the purple grain phenotype and elevated quercetin accumulation [31]. Similar enrichment of phenylpropanoid and flavonoid pathways was reported in maize [15], rice [16], grape [42], and apple [18], indicating a conserved flavonoid biosynthetic framework across crops. However, the stronger pathway enrichment observed in purple barley suggests enhanced transcriptional activation associated with its elevated quercetin accumulation.

The qRT-PCR expression analysis of key genes involved in the quercetin biosynthesis pathway, in grain and leaf tissues, revealed significant upregulation during grain maturation, consistent with quercetin accumulation patterns. Similar developmental stage–dependent activation of flavonoid biosynthetic genes were found in rice [16], maize [15], and grape [42], indicating a conserved regulatory mechanism, while the pronounced upregulation observed in purple barley suggests a potentially stronger or more sustained transcriptional control contributing to its distinctive quercetin accumulation.

Taken together, our results indicate that quercetin accumulation in purple barley arises from the coordinated action of multiple regulatory layers, including transcriptional reprogramming, alternative splicing–mediated post-transcriptional control, sequence-level variation, protein–protein interaction networks, pathway-level enrichment, and experimental validation by qRT-PCR. This study provides the first comprehensive transcriptome-based insight into the molecular regulation of quercetin biosynthesis in purple barley and establishes a valuable foundation for future functional characterization, marker-assisted breeding, and metabolic engineering aimed at enhancing flavonoids more specially quercetin content.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the significant higher accumulation of quercetin in purple barley compared with yellow and black barley. Comparative transcriptome analysis conducted during the grain-filling stage enabled systematic elucidation of the molecular basis underlying differences in quercetin accumulation in above mentioned three types of barley. Based on RNA-seq data, a total of 6,393 genes were structurally optimized, of which 4,986 were functionally annotated. In addition, transcriptome analysis identified 12 types of AS events and 561,743 putative SNPs, the majority of which were located in intronic regions.

A total of 4,540 differentially expressed genes among three types of barleymight reflect how transcriptional differences associated with different types of barley for differential metabolite accumulation. Purple barley exhibited a higher number of upregulated genes and more extensive PPI networks, indicating enhanced metabolic coordination. Functional enrichment analyses guide how notable DEGs involved in BP, CC, and MF regulatet growth, development, and secondary metabolism in different barley. Notably, significant enrichment of genes involved in the phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways in purple barley might support their central roles in quercetin biosynthesis. Furthermore, higher expression of the key quercetin biosynthetic genes F3′5′H, F3′H, FLS1, and FLS2 at the grain maturation stage than the grain-filling stage might reveal their their contribution to quercetin accumulation in the seed.

Overall, this study provides transcriptome-level insights into the molecular regulation of quercetin biosynthesis in barley and identifies key candidate genes associated with high quercetin accumulation in purple barley. However, transcriptome data alone cannot directly confirm protein abundance, enzymatic activity, or metabolic flux. Therefore, future studies integrating proteomics, metabolomics, and functional validation of candidate genes will could guide more precise and usefly routes to fully elucidate the regulatory mechanisms governing quercetin accumulation. Collectively, these findings provide valuable genetic resources and regulatory clues for the improvement of quercetin content in barley through molecular breeding approaches such as marker-assisted selection or targeted gene editing.
