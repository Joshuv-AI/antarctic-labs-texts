Regional travel distance is not associated with operative mortality in acute type A aortic dissection
Authors: Anthony V. Norman, Raymond J. Strobel, Sanjana Challa, Kristen Wells, Andrew M. Young, Alexander M. Wisniewski, Raza M. Ahmad, Michael A. Mazzeffi, Alan M. Speir, Mohammed A. Quader, Jared P. Beller, Leora T. Yarboro, John A. Kern, Kenan W. Yount, Nicholas R. Teman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesFew data are available to examine the impact of travel distance on outcomes in acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD). We hypothesized that longer travel would increase mortality after ATAAD repair.MethodsWe studied the impact of travel distance ≤100 miles before ATAAD repair between July 2011 and September 2022 using a regional collaborative database. Patients were stratified into quartiles, and the longest and shortest distance quartiles were compared. High-volume centers were defined as those averaging >10 ATAAD repairs annually. Multivariable and hierarchical logistic regression models were fit to identify preoperative and intraoperative risk factors associated with operative mortality.ResultsWe identified 1285 patients who underwent ATAAD repair: 320 in the longest-distance quartile and 335 in the shortest. The longest-distance quartile had greater hospital transfer rates (76% vs 26%, P < .001). There was no difference in major morbidity (52% vs 53%, P = .78) or operative mortality (22% vs 22%, P = .95). High-volume centers were associated with lower mortality (odds ratio [OR], 0.68; 95% CI, 0.46-0.99; P = .048). Age (OR, 1.03; 95% CI, 1.01-1.04, P < .001), cardiopulmonary resuscitation (OR, 2.73; 95% CI, 1.31-5.69, P = .007), cardiogenic shock (OR, 1.99; 95% CI, 1.24-3.22, P = .005), longer cardiopulmonary bypass time (OR, 1.01; 95% CI, 1.01-1.01, P < .001), greater temperature nadir (OR, 1.05; 95% CI, 1.01-1.09, P = .006), and intraoperative blood transfusion (OR, 1.16; 95% CI, 1.08-1.24, P < .001) were associated with greater mortality. Distance (OR, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.98-1.00; P = .073) and hospital transfers (OR, 1.08 95% CI, 0.74-1.58; P = .69) were nonsignificant.ConclusionsHospital transfers and travel distances up to 100 miles for ATAAD repair did not increase operative mortality.

Central Message

Patients can travel up to 100 miles for ATAAD repair without worse outcomes. Transporting patients to high-volume centers, when feasible and without significant delays, can improve outcomes.

Perspective

ATAAD is a time-sensitive emergency that benefits from early repair. Limited data are available describing the impact of travel distance on outcomes despite it being one of the first barriers to surgical care. We demonstrated patients may safely travel up to 100 miles for repair with similar outcomes. Taking the time to travel to a high-volume center may afford better outcomes.

Acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) is a cardiovascular emergency warranting immediate intervention. Nonoperative management is associated with a nearly 60% risk of mortality. This risk of death is time dependent, with mortality rates increasing between 0.5% and 2% per hour without surgery.1,2 Although reported operative mortality rates generally range from 18% to 26%,3, 4, 5 modern registry studies have shown rates can be as low as 4.4% if repair is performed within 48 hours.1 Systems-based interventions have been implemented to improve outcomes. Centers of excellence have been developed, with greater surgeon and hospital volume facilitating surgical expertise.6, 7, 8 Hospital networks have been trialed to streamline diagnostics, transportation, and intervention.9,10 Nevertheless, ATAADs can occur abruptly in a variety of community settings. Travel distance to a high-volume referral center is an important consideration for hospitals less equipped to offer immediate repair or deliver appropriate postoperative care.

Few studies have explored the impact of travel distance on outcomes in ATAAD repairs. One Japanese study found it may be safe to pursue a prehospital travel distance up to 100 kilometers without increasing mortality.11 This notion may hold true for patients transferred to more equipped facilities.12 Other studies suggest the mode of transportation has little impact on mortality in patients with ATAAD.13,14 However, these findings are contradictory to the key principle that increased time before aortic repair is associated with increased mortality.1,2 Thus, the aim of our study was to conduct a statewide analysis of the association between preoperative travel distance and postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing ATAAD repair. We hypothesized that longer travel distance would increase operative mortality after ATAAD repair.

Methods

Patient Data

The Virginia Cardiac Services Quality Initiative (VCSQI) includes 17 hospitals in Virginia and contains 99% of all adult cardiac surgeries in the region. Standard Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) definitions were used for all study variables. Institutional STS data were voluntarily submitted by each center and compiled in a central database for quality improvement and clinical research. This study was approved by the University of Virginia's Institutional Review Board (Protocol #23305, July 14, 2021).

All patients who underwent open surgical repair for ATAAD between July 2011 and September 2022 were extracted from the VCSQI database. We included all patients who had hospital and home ZIP code data recorded. Geodesic travel distances were calculated on the basis of the centroid of ZIP codes for the patient's home and the hospital where the surgery was performed. Patients with a travel distance of >100 miles were excluded to improve internal validity and avoid including data from out-of-state visitors which may falsely represent large travel distances. Patients were stratified into quartiles based on travel distance, and the top (longest distance) and bottom (shortest distance) quartiles were compared. These data were merged with Economic Innovation Group Distressed Community Index (DCI) data, which has been previously described. DCI estimates socioeconomic distress by ZIP code, with a score of zero indicating a nondistressed community and a score of 100 indicating a maximally distressed community.15 Outcomes measured included STS operative mortality and major morbidities including postoperative renal failure, stroke, reoperation for any reason, deep sternal wound infection, and prolonged postoperative ventilation (>24 hours).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables with skewed distributions are presented as median (Q1, Q3), and normally distributed variables as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical variables are presented as n (%). Differences in continuous variables with skewed distributions were compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and normally distributed variables by Student t test. Categorical variables were compared by χ2 or Fisher exact tests as appropriate. Missing data were imputed for variables with less than 10% missing data using the median or mode. Variables with greater than 10% missing data were not analyzed. High-volume center status was defined as hospitals performing on average >10 ATAAD repairs per year.6,7 A multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify pre- and intraoperative variables associated with operative mortality after ATAAD repair. Variables were selected on the basis of clinical relevance and statistical significance. Models included the entire study cohort, or all four quartiles, and travel distance was included in the model as a continuous variable. A hierarchical logistic regression with a generalized linear regression model was used to analyze the effect of center level clustering and was treated as a random effect. SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute) statistical software was used for analysis.

Comment

In a statewide analysis, we explored the effect of travel distance on postoperative outcomes for patients who underwent open surgical repair of ATAAD. In the univariate analysis, the shortest and longest quartile groups had similar rates of mortality and major morbidity. After risk adjustment for the entire cohort, travel distance and transfer status were not associated with operative mortality. High-volume center status was associated with decreased operative mortality. These results build upon previous findings and highlight the importance of establishing hospital networks and high-volume centers to improve postoperative morbidity and mortality for patients undergoing ATAAD repair.6,10,12,13

Izumisawa and colleagues11 studied the impact of travel distance from the scene to the hospital by ambulance in Japan with travel distances up to 100 kilometers. Similar to our findings, they found travel distance had no association with operative mortality. Although it may delay surgery, hospital transfer has also not been known to routinely increase operative mortality in ATAAD.13,16 Goldstone and colleagues12 found in a study of Medicare beneficiaries that inter-hospital transfer to a high-volume aortic center was associated with decreased mortality.

The impact of high-volume centers and surgeons has been previously reported.6, 7, 8,17 Chikwe and colleagues6 reported that surgeon and institution volume had a greater impact on outcomes than many common patient comorbidities. In our study, the majority of ATAAD repairs were performed at high-volume centers, which was associated with 32% lower odds of mortality. Developing the infrastructure of a high-volume center includes not only high-volume aortic surgeons, but multidisciplinary teams (vascular surgeons, critical care physicians, radiologists, etc), standardized operative techniques, and high volumes of elective aortic operations. In one center's experience, implementing these practices contributed to a decrease in mortality from 33.9% to 2.8% in just over a decade.7 However, these efforts extend beyond the individual center, with many centers coordinating with neighboring hospitals.

Many high-volume centers, including several in the current study, have established aortic codes or protocols to facilitate early diagnosis, mobilization of personnel, and rapid transfer if necessary. Implementation has been shown to decrease times to diagnosis and the operating room by up to 50% and reduces complications including prolonged mechanical ventilation, stroke, and mortality.9,10 Establishing transfer patterns has created several high-volume centers in the state of Virginia. Furthermore, all VCSQI centers are within 100 miles of a high-volume center, as seen in Figure 3. The distribution of these centers creates a network spanning most of the state and allowing the VCSQI to perform 99% of all cardiac operations in Virginia. As such, this model may serve as a template for establishing statewide cooperatives and policies to best serve communities at large. Establishing high-volume centers and developing the infrastructure to create a network of neighboring hospitals can be a daunting but worthwhile task. Hospitals have found success in adapting existing ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction and stroke networks when building referral programs for ATAAD repair.13

Integral to regional systems are emergency responders. Helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS) have increased the ability to expedite transportation from farther distances and provide personnel to initiate medical care with proven benefits.18,19 Although this has borne out in the trauma literature, there are fewer data available for patients with ATAAD. Several studies indicate HEMS do not reduce mortality in patients with aortic dissections.13,14 Manzur and colleagues13 showed that comorbidity burden was associated with mortality in their California-based regional transport system, but travel mode or distance was not. They postulated current systems are designed to use HEMS for patients with greater acuity or those traveling from much farther distances, which is determined on a case-by-case basis.

Social and structural barriers to adequate care may exist for patients needing ATAAD repair. Socioeconomic distress and limited access to cardiac surgery are associated with worse outcomes15,20,21 with data suggesting this may apply to ATAAD.22,23 Distress scores of >60 have been associated with increased delays in diagnosing ATAAD.23 In our study, the top quartile was more distressed than the bottom quartile. However, the median distress score was 60.7. This may explain why DCI did not reach statistical significance in our model. Nevertheless, prior series as well as the Virginia Department of Health appear to demonstrate an overlap where more distressed communities also appear to be more rural.24,25 Prior studies have shown that patients from rural communities experience worse postoperative outcomes in cardiac surgery.26,27 While it is not a surprise that fewer patients came from rural communities, it is plausible there are socioeconomic and geographic barriers that prohibit ATAAD patients from reaching the hospital and ultimately undergoing aortic repair. This may be an area worthy of future investigation.

Ultimately, once the patient is in the operating room, factors related to establishing cardiopulmonary bypass and hypothermic circulatory arrest are integral to postoperative outcomes.28 Importantly, there was no difference in the selection of axillary or femoral cannulation, extent of hypothermia, duration of circulatory arrest, cerebral perfusion strategy, or cardiopulmonary bypass time between the top and bottom quartiles. There was higher use of aortic cannulation in the top quartile in our study. However, this approach has been shown to be a safe alternative to femoral cannulation with good results.29,30 Nevertheless, this may speak to the standardization of processes which can occur at high-volume centers.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, these data were reviewed in a retrospective fashion and may be subject to unmeasured confounding. Second, we were unable to control for mode of transportation. Third, the Society of Thoracic Surgeons does not have a risk calculator for patients with an aortic dissection as they do for other patients undergoing cardiac surgery, which limits our risk-adjusted analysis. Lastly, these data do not include patients who died at the scene or in transit, which may limit the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusions

Traveling up to 100 miles for ATAAD repair was not associated with increased operative mortality in patients undergoing open surgical repair for ATAAD in Virginia (Figure 5). When feasible, patients should undergo repair at high-volume centers to decrease operative mortality.
