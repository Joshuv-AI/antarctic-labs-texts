Posture and location preferences and gastrointestinal homeostasis in Holstein cows with tongue-rolling behavior
Authors: Chenyang Li, Guangyong Zhao, Jian Wang, Jie Gao, Xianhong Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTongue-rolling is the most common stereotypic behavior in dairy farming and is often considered an indicator of poor animal welfare. This study aimed to investigate the posture and location preferences of tongue-rolling cows and to compare digestion profiles between tongue-rolling and non-tongue-rolling cows. A total of 755 cows from four barns were selected for behavioral observation using scan sampling over a 7-day period to record the postures and locations associated with tongue-rolling. Subsequently, 10 tongue-rolling cows (TR group) and 10 non-tongue-rolling cows (CON group) were selected to analyze intake composition, apparent nutrient digestibility, rumen fermentation parameters and fecal microbiota.ResultsResults showed a 28.90% prevalence of tongue-rolling in the observed herd. Tongue-rolling was most frequently expressed in the stall area (40.59% in the morning, 41.99% in the afternoon) and a standing posture (82.80% in the morning, 89.28% in the afternoon). There was no significant difference in intake preference between TR and CON cows. Although ruminal pH was significantly lower in TR cows compared to CON cows (P < 0.05), other rumen fermentation parameters were not significantly affected. 16S rRNA sequencing results indicated that the fecal microbial composition was relatively similar between TR and CON cows; however, at the genus level, the abundances of norank_o_Bacteroidales (P = 0.045), Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group (P = 0.037), and norank_f_Erysipelatoclostridiaceae (P = 0.049) were significantly lower in TR cows than in CON cows, suggesting a potential dysbiosis that could affect gut barrier function. Compared with CON cows, TR cows exhibited significantly reduced digestibility of neutral detergent fiber and ether extract (P < 0.05).ConclusionOverall, tongue-rolling behavior in dairy cows may be associated with altered nutrient utilization and subtle gut microbial shifts, highlighting its potential value as an indicator of compromised digestive efficiency and welfare status, and providing new insights into the understanding of this behavior.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05729-9.

Background

With the advancement of large-scale intensive farming, dairy cows are frequently subjected to adverse factors such as high stocking density, environmental constraints, and psychological stress, which contributes to the high prevalence of stereotypic behaviors [1–6]. Stereotypic behaviors are defined as repetitive, invariant behavior patterns with no apparent function, and are typically classified as abnormal behaviors and indicators of compromised animal welfare [7–10]. Tongue-rolling is the most prevalent stereotypic behavior in dairy cows, characterized by the repetitive rotation or extension of the tongue either inside or outside the open mouth while the cow is neither eating nor ruminating, and is observed across different physiological stages [11–13]. In intensive dairy systems, cows are often fed high-concentrate diets that reduce eating and rumination time while limiting opportunities to express natural foraging behaviour. Consequently, tongue-rolling has been proposed as a substitute activity arising from unsatisfied foraging and rumination motivation [10, 12–15].

However, existing literature has primarily focused on the prevalence of tongue-rolling behavior, with limited attention to the contexts in which it occurs. The spatial location and posture associated with tongue-rolling may provide valuable insights into its underlying causes. For example, whether tongue-rolling is expressed predominantly in feeding areas, where competition and feeding motivation are high, or in resting areas, where social interactions are limited, may help clarify the factors associated with its expression. In addition, understanding where cows preferentially perform tongue-rolling may provide useful information for herd management, particularly when regrouping animals or introducing cows into new social groups.

Tongue-rolling may also be associated with digestive physiology. High-concentrate feeding systems increase the risk of gastrointestinal disturbances in ruminants [16, 17], and previous studies have reported lower ruminal pH in cows exhibiting tongue-rolling behaviour [4, 12]. These findings suggest that tongue-rolling may be linked to digestive discomfort or altered gastrointestinal function. Similar hypotheses have been proposed for oral stereotypies in other species, such as crib-biting and wind-sucking in horses [18].

Accumulating evidence, particularly from human studies, suggests the association between gut microbiota and stereotypic behaviors, making this an emerging field of study [19, 20]. The gut microbiota communicates bidirectionally with the brain through neural, immune, and endocrine pathways, thereby influencing behavioral expression. This relationship extends across different segments of the digestive tract, although evidence from farm animals remains relatively limited. For instance, the abundances of Coprococcus, Clostridium, Firmicutes, and Ruminococcaceae are elevated in the gut microbiota of pigs exhibiting tail-biting behavior [21, 22], and the abundances of Bacteroidota and Spirochaetota are increased in the rumen of tongue-rolling cows [4]. Nevertheless, research regarding the relationship between tongue-rolling behavior and fecal microbiota in dairy cows is still lacking.

Therefore, this study was designed to provide a comprehensive characterization of tongue-rolling behavior from both external behavioral manifestations and internal physiological perspectives. Specifically, we quantified posture and location preferences of cows exhibiting tongue-rolling behavior and examined differences in digestive physiology between tongue-rolling and normal behavior cows. We hypothesized that tongue-rolling behavior is expressed non-randomly, with affected cows showing preferences for specific locations and postures within the housing environment. Furthermore, we hypothesized that tongue-rolling cows differ from normal cows in feeding preferences, which may subsequently influence rumen conditions and fecal microbial communities, thereby contributing to the development or maintenance of this stereotypic behavior.

Materials and methods

Animal welfare statement

The experiment was conducted at a commercial dairy farm in Bayannur, Inner Mongolia, China. The farm keeps Holstein cows for milk production. All experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Beijing, China, approval number IAS2023-68). The experimental dates were from July to August, 2025. The owner of the dairy farm was fully informed of the study objective and procedures, and consented to the use of the animals in this research.

Animal, diet and management

This study was conducted in a large-scale free-stall cowshed, as illustrated in Fig. 1, which comprised four separate barns. After excluding cows that were transferred in or out during the experimental period, a total of 755 Holstein dairy cows were included in the study (days in milk = 138 ± 106, parity = 2.26 ± 1.43, body condition score = 3.15 ± 0.21; mean ± SD). Each barn was equipped with 292 stalls, 8 water troughs, and headlocks along the feeding alley. The sand bedding material was leveled daily by farm staff. Additionally, a bucket of salt and a bucket of NaHCO3 (baking soda) were provided outside the corner formed by both the feeding and milking alleys for ad libitum consumption (Fig. 1). Each barn was thoroughly disinfected once a week to maintain hygiene. Cows were milked three times daily starting at 08:00, 16:00, and 23:00. Milking was conducted sequentially by barn (Barn II, Barn I, Barn III, and then Barn IV), with the milking process for each barn lasting approximately 20 min. During each session, an automated milking system recorded the milk yield and days in milk for each cow. While cows were away for milking, one staff member operated a bulldozer to clean the manure, and another staff member distributed the total mixed ration (TMR). TMR was delivered to the barns during the morning and afternoon milking sessions while the cows were absent. To ensure ad libitum intake, a small top-up of TMR was dispensed 3 h after both the morning and afternoon deliveries. The formulation and nutritional composition of the TMR are presented in Table 1. Health inspections were performed by veterinarians once daily after the morning milking. Upon returning to the barn, cows were restrained in headlocks for approximately 30 to 60 min for clinical examination. Body condition score was assessed weekly for all cows by the veterinarians according to the method of Edmondson et al. [23].

Behavioral observations

Tongue-rolling behavioral observations were conducted by two trained observers using the scan sampling behavior observation [4, 25]. It is important to note that repeated circular movements of the tongue occurring while the cow is feeding or ruminating are not classified as tongue-rolling [13, 26]. Prior to the formal observation period, a two-day pilot study was conducted to standardize the assessment of tongue-rolling behavior, ensuring a high level of inter-observer agreement. Subsequently, a 7-day formal behavioral observation period was conducted. Observations were carried out daily during two-time blocks: 08:10 ~ 11:10 and 14:10 ~ 17:10. During each behavioral observation, Observer 1 started from one end of the cowshed, observing the behaviors of cows in Barn I and Barn II. After approximately 10 min, the observer reached the opposite end, then turned back to observe the cows in Barn IV and Barn III, returning to the starting point after another 10 min to complete one full observation round. A 10-minute rest interval was taken before the next round commenced. Observer 2 followed the same route starting 10 min after Observer 1 started (the observation routes are illustrated in Fig. 1). Whenever a cow exhibited tongue-rolling behavior, the following data were recorded: cow ID, posture (standing or lying), and location (headlock, aisles, water trough, stall, salt and NaHCO3 buckets). The layout of these locations is shown in Fig. 1. Notably, the location was recorded as “headlock” only when the cow’s head was extended through the headlock; otherwise, it was recorded as “aisles”. During each scan round, all cows present in the barn were assessed once, and tongue-rolling behavior was recorded only when observed at that specific moment. Individual cows were not continuously followed throughout the observation period.

Each observer performed 6 observation rounds in each time block, resulting in a total of 24 observation rounds per day. During each round, if a barn was empty due to the cows being away for milking, the observer maintained the established route and observation pace, skipping the unoccupied barn and proceeding to the next. In both the morning and afternoon sessions, each barn was skipped exactly once. Consequently, each barn was effectively observed 22 times per day throughout the study period. The high frequency of scan sampling throughout the day was designed to mitigate potential temporal sampling bias in behavior detection. The observation schedule of the two observers is shown in Supplementary Table 1 for reference.

Animal selection and sample collection

Following the 7-day observation period, a total of 154 observation rounds were completed for each barn. To ensure that the selected cows exhibited stable tongue-rolling behavior and to provide sufficient data for further digestive function analysis, cows were screened based on their tongue-rolling frequency (calculated as the number of tongue-rolling occurrences observed per cow divided by 154). Only cows with a tongue-rolling frequency exceeding 5% were selected for the TR group (A total of 18 cows). Among cows with tongue-rolling frequencies exceeding 5%, second-parity cows represented the largest subgroup. To control for the potential confounding effect of parity, only second-parity cows were considered for inclusion. Importantly, only 10 healthy cows met both inclusion criteria (tongue-rolling frequency > 5% and parity = 2), and all eligible cows were therefore included in the TR group. After selecting the TR cows, 10 cows with the same parity, similar days in milk with TR cows, and no tongue-rolling behavior were randomly selected as control group (CON, n = 10, parity = 2). Sample collection began on the first day after the completion of behavior observations and cow selection.

TMR samples were collected after feed distribution, while cows were away for milking. Approximately 100 g of TMR was gathered from five different points within each barn. After sampling all four barns, the subsamples were thoroughly pooled to create a single representative daily TMR sample. This process was repeated for three consecutive days, resulting in three TMR samples. The samples were then analyzed for concentrate-to-forage ratio and nutrient content.

Immediately following the sampling of TMR and the return of cows from milking, the TMR in front of TR and CON cows was temporarily cleared to prevent feeding. Fecal samples were obtained via rectal fecal sampling performed by experienced veterinarians. To minimize environmental contamination and oxygen exposure, the initial portion of feces was discarded, and subsequent feces were collected. Approximately 150 g of feces was collected from each cow, and 10 mL of 10% sulfuric acid was added to prevent ammonia nitrogen volatilization. Fecal samples were placed in individual glass dishes and dried in a forced-air oven at 60 °C for 48 h. This procedure was repeated for three consecutive days, resulting in three fecal samples from each selected cow. Additionally, on the first day of collection, a separate aliquot of fresh feces was placed in a 2 mL cryovial, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently stored at -80 °C for microbial analysis.

Immediately after fecal collection, rumen fluid was obtained using an oral stomach tube by a veterinarian to minimize animal distress. The initial 100 mL of aspirated rumen fluid was discarded to avoid saliva contamination. Subsequent samples were filtered through four layers of gauze into 50 mL centrifuge tubes and temporarily stored on dry ice. Once all cows were sampled, the rumen fluid from each cow was aliquoted into four 10 mL cryovials, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and transferred to a -80 °C freezer for long-term storage and subsequent analysis.

Upon completion of fecal and rumen fluid collection, the selected cows were permitted to feed. The ort samples were collected after the cows had been feeding for 20 min, when a small amount of feed still remained in front of them. Specifically, when the cow extended its head forward perpendicular to the headlock, the orts within a 50-cm range on either side of the head’s midline was thoroughly mixed, of which approximately 400 ~ 500 g was collected. This process was repeated for three consecutive days, resulting in three ort samples from each selected cow. The samples were then analyzed for concentrate-to-forage ratio.

Concentrate-to-roughage ratio of TMR and orts

The TMR and ort samples collected from selected cows post-feeding were analyzed using a four-tier Penn State Particle Separator (PSPS) [27]. The pore sizes of the top three sieves were 19 mm, 8 mm, and 4 mm, respectively. The procedure of the PSPS was as follows: each TMR or ort sample was placed on the top sieve and the sieves were shaken in one horizontal direction five times followed by a one-quarter turn rotation of the separator box, with no vertical motion applied, and this procedure was repeated seven times to give a total of eight sets (40 shakes).The feed retained on each layer was weighed individually to calculate the proportion of each fraction. The proportion of feed retained on each sieve layer for the orts of each selected cow was calculated and compared with that of the daily TMR sample to analyze the dietary intake composition, expressed as the difference obtained by subtracting the proportion in the orts of each selected cow from that in the TMR. After particle size analysis, the TMR samples were recombined following particle size analysis and retained for subsequent nutrient content determination.

Apparent nutrient digestibility

Apparent digestibility of nutrients was determined using acid-insoluble ash (AIA) as an internal marker [28]. To prepare samples for analysis, TMR and fecal samples from the three consecutive collection days were first dried in a forced-air oven at 60 °C for 48 h, and then ground to pass through a 0.425 mm sieve. Subsequently, a single composite TMR sample was created by thoroughly mixing equal amounts of the ground sample from each of the three days. Similarly, a composite fecal sample for each cow was created by mixing equal amounts of its three corresponding daily ground samples.

The samples were analyzed for dry matter (DM), crude protein (CP), ether extract (EE), neutral detergent fiber (NDF), and acid detergent fiber (ADF). The AIA content was determined using 4 N hydrochloric acid, following the method described by van Keulen and Young [28]. All other nutrient components were analyzed according to the standard procedures of the Association of Official Analytical Chemists [29]. where A1 represents the AIA content in the diet (%), A2 represents the AIA content in the feces (%), N1 represents the content of the specific nutrient in the diet (%), and N2 represents the content of the corresponding nutrient in the feces (%).

Rumen fermentation parameter analysis

After rumen fluid collection, ruminal pH was immediately measured using a portable pH meter (PB-10, Sartorius, Germany) with a resolution of 0.01 pH units and an accuracy of ± 0.01 pH units, following calibration with standard buffer solutions. Frozen rumen fluid samples were used to determine the concentrations of acetate, propionate, isobutyrate, butyrate, isovalerate, valerate, and caproate by gas chromatography, while ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N) and microbial crude protein (MCP) concentrations were measured using colorimetric methods. Total volatile fatty acids (VFA) were calculated as the sum of the measured short-chain fatty acids.

Fecal microbiota analysis

Total genomic DNA was extracted from fecal samples of dairy cows using a commercial DNA extraction kit (B518225-0100, Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The hypervariable region V3-V4 of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified with forward primer 338F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and reverse primer 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’) on an ABI GeneAmp 9700 PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The PCR amplification cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 3 min; followed by 27 cycles of denaturing at 95 ℃ for 30 s, annealing at 55 ℃ for 30 s and extension at 72 ℃ for 45 s; and single extension at 72 ℃ for 10 min.

The PCR product was extracted from 2% agarose gel and purified using the PCR clean-up kit (MJYH, shanghai, China) according to manufacturer’s instructions and quantified using Qubit 4.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Purified amplicons were used for library construction with the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit (Bioo Scientific, Austin, TX, USA) and sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). After quality filtering and sequence assembly, operational taxonomic units (OTU) were clustered using UPARSE (version 7.1; http://drive5.com/uparse/) at a 97% sequence similarity threshold [30, 31]. Taxonomic assignment of OTU was performed using the RDP Classifier (version 2.11; http://sourceforge.net/projects/rdp-classifier/) with a confidence threshold of 70%.

Statistical analysis

For the analysis of dairy cow behavioral data, preliminary data processing was performed using Microsoft Excel 16.104 to calculate the prevalence of tongue-rolling behavior.

Behavioral data were recorded at the event level. Because multiple tongue-rolling events could originate from the same cow across different scan rounds, cow ID was included as a random effect in all mixed models to account for within-animal correlation and avoid pseudo-replication. In addition, to examine temporal variation in tongue-rolling behaviour, time of day (morning vs. afternoon) was included as a categorical fixed effect in the model.

A generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with a binomial distribution and a Logit link function was used to investigate posture preference in tongue-rolling behavior (standing vs. lying) [32]. Posture was specified as a binary response variable. All statistical analyses were performed using the statsmodels package in Python 3.10. By default, this package determines reference categories based on alphanumerical order; consequently, “Lying” was set as the reference category for posture (lying = 0, standing = 1), and “afternoon” served as the reference baseline for the time fixed effect. Posture preference was evaluated using the odds ratio (OR) with a 95% credible interval (CI); a 95% CI excluding 1 indicates a significant difference. The model was formulated as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{logit}\left(p_{ij}\right)=\mathrm{ln}\left(\frac{p_{ij}}{1-p_{ij}}\right)=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\mathrm{Time}_{ij}+\mu_{j}$$\end{document}

where pij denotes the probability that the jth tongue-rolling event of cow i occurred in the standing posture; β0 is the fixed intercept representing the overall log-odds of standing posture during the afternoon; β1 is the fixed effect coefficient for time of day; Timeji represents the time of day, and µj is a random intercept for Cow i, assumed to follow a normal distribution.

A Bayesian multinomial logistic mixed model (MLMM) was used to examine the location preference of tongue-rolling behavior (headlock, aisles, water trough, stall, salt and NaHCO3 buckets) [33]. MLMM analyses were conducted in Python 3.10 using the Bambi package. Location was specified as a multinomial response variable, with the most frequently observed location set as the reference category. For the fixed effect of time, “afternoon” was set as the reference baseline (determined by default alphabetical order). Log-odds of tongue-rolling behavior occurring at other locations were estimated relative to the reference location, and time effects were estimated relative to the afternoon baseline. Model significance was assessed using the 95% highest density interval (HDI) of the posterior distribution. When the 95% HDI of the log-odds (or the time coefficient) for a given parameter did not include 0, a significant difference relative to the reference category (or a significant time shift) was inferred. The model was formulated as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{log}\left(\frac{P\left(Y_{ij}=k\right)}{P\left(Y_{ij}=r\right)}\right)=\alpha_{k0}+\alpha_{k1}\mathrm{Time}_{ij}+\mu_{ik},k{\neq}r$$\end{document}

where Yij denotes the location category of the jth tongue-rolling event of cow i; k represents a non-reference location and r denotes the reference location (the most frequently observed location); P (Yij = k) is the probability of tongue-rolling occurring at location k; αk0 is the intercept for location k, representing the baseline log-odds relative to the reference location in the afternoon; αk1is the fixed-effect coefficient for time of day; and µik is the random effect of cow i for location k.

This behavioral preference analysis reflects relative differences in the occurrence of tongue-rolling behavior across postures and locations, rather than absolute preferences.

Bioinformatic analyses were conducted on the Majorbio Cloud platform (http://cloud.majorbio.com). Within this platform, α diversity indices were calculated using Mothur [34], and between-group differences in α diversity were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on the Bray-Curtis distance metric was performed to evaluate similarities in microbial community structure among samples, and differences in community structure between groups were further assessed using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA). Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size was used to identify bacterial taxa with significantly different abundances between groups from the phylum to species level (LDA score > 2, P < 0.05) [35].

Data regarding dietary intake composition, apparent nutrient digestibility, ruminal VFA, NH3-N, and MCP were first tested for normality. For the dietary intake composition, data from the second sieve were not normally distributed and were consequently analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. All other data were normally distributed and analyzed using the student’s t-test. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normally distributed data are presented as means ± SD. Non-normally distributed data are presented as the median (interquartile range). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Prevalence of tongue-rolling behavior

During the behavioral observation period, a total of 640 tongue-rolling events were recorded from 224 dairy cows, with tongue-rolling cows accounting for 29.67% (224/755) of the entire herd.

Posture and location preferences of tongue-rolling cows

The GLMM indicated that dairy cows exhibited a significant posture preference when expressing tongue-rolling behavior (Table 2). Tongue-rolling was predominantly performed in a standing posture rather than lying in both the morning and afternoon. In addition, the preference for the standing posture was significantly stronger in the afternoon than in the morning.

Richness and diversity of the fecal microbiota

In this study, 16S rRNA gene sequencing was used to characterize the fecal microbiota of dairy cows. A total of 1,094,738 high-quality sequences were obtained, with an average read length of 412 bp.

Across the 20 fecal samples, a total of 2,282 OTU were identified, of which 1,570 OTU were shared between the two groups. The numbers of unique OTU were 391 in TR cows and 321 in CON cows (Fig. 3A). As shown in Table 7, no significant differences were observed between TR and CON cows in the Ace, Chao1, Sobs, Shannon, or Simpson indices. PCoA based on the Bray-Curtis distance revealed no clear separation between the TR and CON groups (Fig. 3B). This observation was confirmed by a PERMANOVA test, which showed no significant difference in the overall community structure between the two groups (P > 0.05).

Discussion

Prevalence of stereotypic behaviors and preference for tongue-rolling

Stereotypic behaviors occur in farm animals across different physiological stages [6, 10]. Previous literature has generally suggested that tongue-rolling is the most common oral stereotypic behavior in dairy cows [4, 11, 36, 37]. In addition, the prevalence of tongue-rolling observed in this study was approximately 30%, which is comparable to the levels reported by Robbins et al. [11] and Li et al. [6].

Standing and lying are two core natural behaviors in the daily life of dairy cows. Under free-stall housing conditions, cows lie down both during the day and at night, although lying time is generally longer at night [38]. We found that cows exhibited a clear preference for standing when expressing tongue-rolling behavior, regardless of morning or afternoon, which may be related to thermoregulation and behavioral prioritization. Given that this study was conducted during the summer, cows were exposed to mild heat stress (average THI = 73.49). Previous studies have shown that during heat stress, dairy cows reduce lying time and increase standing time to enhance heat dissipation by increasing body surface area exposed to air and avoiding warm bedding materials [39–42]. Therefore, as cows spend more time standing, there is a greater temporal opportunity for a wider range of non-resting behaviors, including oral stereotypies, to be expressed. A study by Leliveld et al. [43] on dairy farms in the Lombardy region of Italy (with similar latitude and summer THI to the experimental farms in this study) found that during summer, dairy cows spent approximately 35% of their daytime in a lying posture. Consequently, the standing-to-lying time distribution for dairy cows during the day was approximately 1.8:1. In contrast, the current study indicates that when dairy cows exhibit tongue-rolling behavior, the predicted probability ratio of standing-to-lying posture is approximately 4:1 (morning) to 9:1 (afternoon). This ratio is notably higher than the cows’ typical daily activity distribution, reflecting a stronger preference for expressing tongue-rolling behavior while standing.

In addition, the probability of tongue-rolling during lying was lower (13.75%), which may reflect behavioral prioritization during this posture. When cows are lying down, they commonly engage in resting and rumination, both of which are biologically important behaviours [44, 45]. This may partly explain why tongue-rolling was observed more frequently during standing than lying. However, rumination activity was not directly measured during behavioural observations; therefore, the potential relationship between rumination and tongue-rolling can only be inferred from previous studies and should be interpreted with caution. Previous studies have reported that rumination activity exhibits an increasing trend following the cessation of feeding, typically reaching its peak approximately six hours post-feeding [46]. In our study, the afternoon observation period coincided with this approaching peak in rumination. Consequently, this likely further reduced the probability of observing tongue-rolling behavior in a lying posture during this period.

Another important finding of this study was that tongue-rolling behavior most frequently occurred in the stall area (40.59% in the morning, 41.99% in the afternoon). Typically, the stall area occupies the largest proportion of a dairy cow’s daily time budget, serving as the primary site for resting and rumination [47]. In addition, sand was used as the bedding material on the farm where this study was conducted. Compared with hard flooring surfaces, sand bedding provides a softer lying and standing substrate, which can alleviate mechanical load on the hooves associated with prolonged standing. Consequently, the substantial amount of time cows spend in the stall area naturally increases the temporal opportunity for tongue-rolling to be observed there. The comfort provided by sand bedding may further encourage cows to remain in this area, thus contributing to the high frequency of the behavior being recorded in this location. Compared with other areas of the barn, the stall area is generally characterized by lower levels of competition and reduced crowding. Therefore, cows exhibiting tongue-rolling behavior may preferentially occupy areas with less competition and avoid competitive interactions with other individuals. However, this interpretation remains speculative and requires further investigation. Future studies are needed to determine whether stereotypic cows differ from non-stereotypic cows in their responses to social competition and group dynamics. If confirmed, these findings may have practical implications for dairy herd management, particularly when regrouping animals or introducing cows into new social groups.

Moreover, the water trough area was the second most common location for tongue-rolling in this study. During heat stress, drinking behavior patterns change, with cows spending more time near water troughs area [42, 48], which may further increase the likelihood of tongue-rolling behavior being expressed in the water trough area. In this study, a significant effect of time was observed exclusively in the headlock area, a finding attributable to farm management practices. As described in the Materials and Methods, in contrast to the afternoon where no health checks were performed, headlocks were routinely locked during feeding following the morning milking to facilitate veterinary health checks, and cows were released only after the entire barn had been inspected. Consequently, during this period, many cows had finished feeding but were unable to retreat from the headlocks. This physical restraint likely explains the significantly higher probability of tongue-rolling behavior in the headlock area during the morning compared to the afternoon.

Intake composition in dairy cows

It has been demonstrated that increased roughage provision leads to a reduction in non-nutritive oral behaviors among calves [49–52]. In addition, researchers speculated that tongue-rolling cows might prefer concentrates over roughage, based on observations of lower rumen pH, reduced milk protein percentage, and shorter feeding duration in these animals [4, 6, 12]. However, the present study did not support this speculation. No significant differences were found in the composition of feed consumed by TR and CON cows, indicating that the feeding habits of TR cows did not differ from those of the control group.

Nevertheless, these findings are subject to several limitations. First, due to farm management constraints, the selected TR and CON cows remained in their original group-housed barns throughout the study and were not transferred to separate experimental barns. Consequently, their feeding behavior was inevitably influenced by neighboring cows [53]. Second, the orts were sampled after being thoroughly mixed. However, perfect homogeneity is difficult to achieve, and variations in the mixture, along with the potential for smaller, denser particles (i.e., concentrates) to settle or segregate during this process, may have introduced sampling errors. Finally, although the concentrate-to-forage ratio of the TMR was generally consistent along the feed bunk, it was not perfectly identical at every location, which may also have impacted the results. Based on these factors, more rigorous experiments are required to investigate the specific dietary intake composition of tongue-rolling cows. Future studies utilizing individual housing for TR and CON cows would likely yield superior research outcomes.

Ruminal environment in dairy cows

Tongue-rolling behavior not only exhibits posture and location preferences but may also be associated with physiological functions. As mentioned above, the stall area is the primary location for rumination. Therefore, exhibiting tongue-rolling in the stall may displace time that would otherwise be spent ruminating. Based on this, we further examined the ruminal environment in TR and CON cows.

Rumen fermentation following feed intake results in the production of VFA and lactic acid [54], which consequently creates an acidic ruminal environment [55]. During rumination, cows secrete large amounts of saliva, which helps neutralize ruminal acidity [55]. If the rumen’s buffering capacity cannot keep pace with the production of acidic compounds such as VFA and lactic acid, these acids accumulate and lower ruminal pH [56]. Ruminal pH influences both body health and microbial balance [55, 57, 58].

In our previous studies, based on the lower production performance and immune function observed in tongue-rolling cows, we speculated that tongue-rolling may reflect an excessively acidic ruminal environment, with cows performing this behavior to alleviate the physiological stress associated with ruminal discomfort [6, 13]. In the present study, we found that ruminal pH in TR cows was significantly lower than in CON cows, which is consistent with previous studies [4, 12]. Although the difference in ruminal pH between TR and CON cows was significant, both groups remained within the normal physiological range, and the absolute difference was minimal. The lack of significant differences in other fermentation parameters, such as VFA, NH3-N, and MCP, can likely be attributed to the fact that the ruminal pH in both groups remained within the optimal range (e.g., 6.5-7.0) for microbial digestive function [59, 60].

Fecal microbiota and nutrient digestibility

The relationship between the gut microbiome and psychological or behavioral traits is an emerging research area [19, 20, 61]. Human studies have demonstrated associations between specific microbial taxa and various psychiatric disorders or behaviors [62–64]. In recent years, studies on the relationship between the gut microbiota and stereotypic behaviors in animals have also increased. In the study by Sun et al. [4], tongue-rolling cows showed a decreased abundance of Firmicutes and an increased abundance of Bacteroidota in the rumen compared with cows without tongue-rolling behavior. Research on tail-biting, a common stereotypic behavior in pigs, found no significant difference in gut microbial α-diversity between tail-biting pigs and control pigs [21, 22]. However, certain taxa within the phylum Firmicutes, including the order Clostridiales, the families Lachnospiraceae, and the genus Ruminococcus, were enriched in the gut microbiota of both tail-biting pigs and their victims [21, 22]. Similarly, studies in horses with abnormal behaviors revealed increased gut Firmicutes abundance compared with control horses [65].

In the present study, consistent with findings in tail-biting pigs [21, 22], we observed no significant differences in fecal microbial α-diversity indices between TR and CON cows (P > 0.05). Furthermore, β-diversity analysis also revealed no significant separation in the overall community structure (Fig. 3B, PERMANOVA P > 0.05), suggesting that tongue-rolling is not associated with a large-scale shift in the gut microbial community. Bacillota (Firmicutes) and Bacteroidota were dominant in both TR and CON cows, consistent with previous reports [66]. However, unlike previous findings in the rumen, no significant differences in Bacillota (Firmicutes) or Bacteroidota abundance were observed in the feces of TR and CON cows in the present study, which may reflect differences between rumen and fecal microbiota. Indeed, the rumen and fecal microbiota of the same cow can differ significantly [67–70]. For example, the abundances of Firmicutes and Actinobacteria are significantly lower in the rumen than in feces [70].

At the genus level, the abundance of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group was significantly lower in TR cow feces than in CON cows. Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group belongs to the family Lachnospiraceae. As reviewed by Homer et al. [61], changes in Clostridiaceae, Lachnospiraceae, Lactobacillaceae, and Oscillospiraceae within Firmicutes show the strongest correlations with behavioral and psychiatric traits in humans and animals, with Lachnospiraceae showing changes across all analyzed species. Lachnospiraceae is a core gut bacterial family, accounting for approximately 50% of Firmicutes, and is one of the primary producers of VFA [71–73]. Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group is crucial for maintaining gastrointestinal health. It has been shown to preserve intestinal barrier integrity and attenuate inflammation, with its abundance positively correlated with enhanced gut barrier function [74–76]. Therefore, the decreased abundance of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group in TR cows may be indicative of differences in gut health and barrier function, although the present study does not allow conclusions regarding causality. Moreover, Niu et al. [77] reported significant differences in fecal microbiota among pigs with varying NDF digestibility levels. Specifically, the abundance of the Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group was significantly higher in pigs with high NDF digestibility than in those with low NDF digestibility. Consistent with these findings, TR cows in the present study exhibited both a reduced abundance of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group and lower NDF digestibility compared with CON cows. However, because the present study was observational in nature, these results indicate an association rather than a direct causal relationship. Further studies are required to determine whether alterations in gut microbiota contribute to changes in fiber digestibility and tongue-rolling behaviour, or whether these factors are influenced by common underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study integrated behavioral, physiological, and microbial analyses to provide a multi-faceted characterization of tongue-rolling behavior in dairy cows. Specifically, TR cows exhibited a strong preference for a standing posture and were more likely to be located in the stall area. Furthermore, although ruminal pH was significantly lower in TR cows, the magnitude of this difference was insufficient to alter other fermentation parameters. Additionally, 16 S rRNA sequencing revealed that while the overall fecal microbial structure was similar between TR and CON cows, the abundance of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group was significantly reduced in TR cows, which may suggest a potential decline in gut barrier function and intestinal health. This reduction was also consistent with the observed decrease in NDF digestibility in TR cows. In summary, tongue-rolling exhibited distinct posture and location preferences. Furthermore, compared with non-tongue-rolling cows, tongue-rolling cows showed lower neutral detergent fiber digestibility and differences in fecal microbial composition. These findings extend current knowledge of tongue-rolling behavior by providing new information on its behavioral expression and its relationships with digestive function and gut microbiota in dairy cows.
