Treatment with saponins from Panax japonicus decreases jejunal inflammation in mice fed a high-fat diet
Authors: Yaoyan Dun, Jiao’e Dong, Hanyu Xiang, Zhaoqi Chen, Huiwen Wang, Zhengguo Xiong, Min Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND/OBJECTIVESChronic high-fat diet (HFD) consumption induces low-grade intestinal inflammation. Although saponins from Panax japonicus (SPJs) have anti-inflammatory properties, the mechanisms by which they mitigate HFD-induced jejunal inflammation remain unclear. This study examined whether SPJs alleviate inflammation by modulating lipid metabolism and endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) in the jejunum.MATERIALS/METHODSHFD-fed mice were administered high-dose SPJs extract. The jejunal triglyceride (TG) levels, gene expression related to TG synthesis (glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase 3 [Gpat3] and diacylglycerol O-acyltransferase 2 [Dgat2]) and lipolysis (adipose triglyceride lipase [Atgl]), cytosolic lipid droplets (CLDs) deposition, ERS markers, and inflammatory responses were assessed.RESULTSHFD feeding induced TG accumulation, upregulated Gpat3 and Dgat2, downregulated Atgl, and promoted CLDs deposition in jejunal epithelial cells. High-dose SPJs attenuated these changes. Furthermore, SPJs suppressed HFD-induced ERS and inflammation in the jejunum. In particular, short-term HFD exposure initially enhanced the antioxidant responses without significant ERS activation.CONCLUSIONSPJs ameliorate HFD-induced jejunal inflammation by modulating lipid metabolic genes, reducing intracellular lipid accumulation, and alleviating ERS. These findings highlight the role of lipid-driven ERS in gut inflammation and the potential of SPJs as a therapeutic intervention.Keywords: Saponins, Panax, high-fat diet, jejunum, inflammation, endoplasmic reticulum stress, oxidative stress

INTRODUCTION

The global rise in obesity, partly attributed to dietary lipid changes, is linked to intestinal lipid absorption via chylomicron synthesis or storage in cytosolic lipid droplets (CLDs) [1,2]. Excessive lipid intake induces lipotoxicity and endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS). Under homeostatic conditions, the ER chaperone glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78) binds to and inhibits ERS sensors (protein kinase RNA-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase [PERK], activating transcription factor 6 [ATF6], inositol-requiring enzyme 1 [IRE1]). Under stress, GRP78 dissociates, however, activating these sensors and potentially triggering chronic inflammation [3,4,5,6,7,8]. In addition, chronic high-fat diet (HFD) consumption leads to jejunal lipid accumulation, ERS, and inflammation, as evidenced by elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines in HFD-fed mice and obese individuals [9,10,11,12]. Therefore, mitigating intestinal epithelial lipid accumulation and ERS is a promising therapeutic strategy for alleviating diet-induced inflammation.

Various compounds ameliorate HFD-induced small intestinal inflammation through distinct but complementary mechanisms. These therapeutic approaches can be categorized based on their primary targets along the “microbiota–metabolism–immunity” axis disrupted by a HFD [13]. For example, at the metabolic level, supplementing phenyllactic acid (PLA) can activate the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ) pathway to restore lipid homeostasis, while inhibiting fatty acid (FA) oxidation or promoting lipid droplet formation can counteract lipotoxicity [14]. At the neuroendocrine and signaling levels, inhibiting tryptophan hydroxylase 1 reduces serotonin (5-HT) overproduction, and administering indole derivatives enhances aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) signaling, both of which contribute to barrier integrity and anti-inflammatory responses [15]. At the immune level, administering recombinant interleukin (IL) 22 directly restores the function of group 3 innate lymphoid cells, promoting mucosal repair [16]. Furthermore, at the ecological level, prebiotic fibers enrich indole-producing bacteria, fostering a microbiota environment conducive to AhR activation [15]. Ultimately, a multimodal therapeutic strategy that simultaneously targets the PLA-PPAR-γ axis, the indole-AhR pathway, the 5-HT synthesis pathway, and the IL-22-mediated immune response—potentially combined with dietary fiber intervention—may synergistically restore gut homeostasis. This integrated approach addresses the interconnected triad of HFD-induced microbiota disruption, metabolic dysregulation, and immune dysfunction, effectively mitigating the development of systemic metabolic diseases.

Despite the promising findings, the investigation of natural saponins—particularly those that target the critical interplay between lipid metabolic dysregulation (lipid accumulation and ERS) and intestinal epithelial inflammation—is still relatively limited. The root of Panax japonicus, a traditional medicine used for regulating lipids and reducing inflammation [17,18,19], is a rich source of bioactive saponins, referred to as saponins from P. japonicus (SPJs). SPJs inhibit hyperlipidemia, systemic inflammation, and ERS in metabolic tissues [20,21,22]. In contrast to the agents previously mentioned, the potential of SPJs to specifically alleviate HFD-induced intestinal inflammation by reducing epithelial lipid accumulation and ERS has not been examined.

Therefore, this study examined the effects of SPJs on HFD-induced intestinal inflammation and elucidated the underlying mechanisms, focusing on lipid metabolism and ERS in the intestinal epithelium.

Although chronic HFD induces jejunal ERS, the acute response after a single high-fat exposure is unclear. In addition, although CLDs accumulate in the intestinal epithelium during HFD [23,24,25], their mechanistic link to ERS (especially in the jejunum) remains contentious [26,27,28,29], potentially because of variations in pre-sacrifice fasting. Accordingly, the mice received a single 200 μL olive oil gavage to address these gaps and specifically investigate the temporal relationship between acute CLD accumulation and ERS induction. Jejunal tissues were collected under controlled fasting conditions at 1, 2, and 6 h post-gavage.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals and SPJs treatment

Male C57BL/6 mice (4–6 weeks old) were sourced from China Three Gorges University's Experimental Animal Center. The mice were individually housed in ventilated cages under controlled conditions (22 ± 1°C, 55–65% humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle). All procedures followed the institutional animal care guidelines (China Three Gorges University, Ethics Approval No. 2017030E).

Fresh rhizomes of P. japonicus were obtained from Enshi Chunmuying Medicinal Materials Base (Hubei, China). The total saponins (SPJs) were extracted and purified as described elsewhere [17,30] with minor modifications. The roots were sequentially extracted (60% ethanol, water, petroleum ether, ethyl acetate, and n-butanol); the crude extract was chromatographed on silica gel and eluted with ethanol gradients (30%, 60%, 90%, and 0% v/v). The 90% fraction was collected and analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography with ultraviolet detection.

The mice were randomized into 4 groups (n = 8/group): 1) normal chow diet (NCD) + vehicle (0.5% CMC-Na); 2) HFD (35% fat, 26% protein, 26% carbohydrate) + vehicle; 3) HFD + low-dose SPJs (50 mg/kg); 4) HFD + high-dose SPJs (150 mg/kg). The SPJs/vehicle were administered daily via oral gavage for 14 weeks. A vehicle control group (HFD + 0.5% CMC-Na) was included to account for the potential effects of the chronic oral gavage procedure on the intestinal physiology. This group underwent identical handling and gavage procedures as the SPJs-treated groups, allowing for the distinction between compound-specific effects and procedural influences. The doses of SPJs (50 and 150 mg/kg) were selected based on a clinical equivalent dose conversion and validation in previous animal models of metabolic diseases [17,31,32,33,34,35]. The mice were anesthetized and euthanized by cervical dislocation. The jejunal tissue was collected: one portion fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for histology, the remainder flash-frozen (−80°C).

In a parallel experiment (n = 6/time-point), the mice received a single oral gavage of 200 μL olive oil. The jejunal tissues were collected under anesthesia 1, 2, and 6 h post-gavage, processed immediately, and stored at −80°C.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

The jejunal tissue samples were fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h, routinely processed, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a 4 μm thickness. The sections were mounted onto glass slides. After deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through a graded series of ethanol (e.g., 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%), the sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, counterstained with eosin, dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and coverslipped. A morphological examination and image acquisition were performed using an optical microscope (Olympus BX53; Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The jejunal tissue samples were primarily fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at 4°C for 12 h, followed by post-fixation in 1% osmium tetroxide at room temperature for 1 h. After fixation, the samples were washed in 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) for 45 min, dehydrated in a graded series of ethanol (e.g., 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%), and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (80 nm) were cut using an ultramicrotome, stained with saturated aqueous uranyl acetate and Reynold's lead citrate, and examined using a Hitachi H-7500 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi High-Technologies Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) operating at 80 kV.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The jejunal tissues from all experimental groups were fixed in 4% PFA (24 h), embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a 4 μm thickness. After deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through a graded series of ethanol (e.g., 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%), heat-mediated antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). The sections were blocked (room temperature, 1 h) using 5% non-fat dry milk or 3% bovine serum albumin in PBS. The sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer, targeting the following: nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) p65 (#6956; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), IL-1β (ab9722), phosphorylated IRE1α (p-IRE1α; ab48187), ATF4 (ab184909), and nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2; ab137550) (all from Abcam, Cambridge, UK); tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α; sc-52746), phosphorylated PERK (p-PERK; sc-32577), GRP78 (sc-13968), C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP; sc-56107), and ATF6α (sc-166659) (all from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA); FOXO3a (RLT1763), FOXO1 (RLT1757), and phosphorylated eIF2α (p-eIF2α; RLP0093) (all from Ruiying Biological, Suzhou, China). After thorough washing in PBS (3 × 5 min), the sections were incubated with the appropriate horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (room temperature, 1 h). After additional PBS washes, the antigen–antibody complexes were visualized using 3,3'-diaminobenzidine as a chromogen with hematoxylin counterstaining. The images were acquired using an optical microscope at 400× magnification. Five random fields per section were captured for quantification. The percentage of positive staining area was analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) in a blinded manner.

Assay of the triglyceride (TG) content

The TG levels in the jejunal tissues were quantified using a commercially available kit (Triglyceride Assay Kit, Catalog No. A110-1-1; Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, tissue samples were homogenized (10% w/v) in ice-cold PBS (0.1 M, pH 7.4) using a homogenizer. The homogenate was centrifuged at 2,500 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was collected, and the TG concentration was determined spectrophotometrically.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

RNA was extracted from both jejunum tissues using the RNAiso Plus reagent kit, adhering to the manufacturer's guidelines. The purified RNA was converted to cDNA using the PrimeScript reagent from Takara. Wuhan Aoke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. synthesized the primers detailed in Table 1. The PCR procedure commenced with a denaturation phase for 5 min at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation for 15 s at 95°C and annealing for 1 min at 60°C. Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase served as a reference gene. The relative gene ratios were determined by applying the 2(−ΔΔCT) formula.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics software (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Multiple group comparisons were analyzed by 1-way analysis of variance, followed by the least significant difference post hoc test when the significance was detected. The statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

RESULTS

Effects of the SPJs treatment on the lipid metabolism of enterocytes in the jejunum of HFD-fed mice

The small intestine is the main site of lipid digestion, absorption, and metabolism, and previous studies have shown that high dietary fat intake leads to lipid deposition in this organ [36,37,38]. H&E staining revealed substantial jejunal epithelial lipid droplet (CLDs) accumulation in HFD-fed mice compared to the normal NCD structure; the SPJs treatment significantly reduced this deposition (Fig. 1A). Consistently, the jejunal TG levels were significantly elevated by the HFD and were attenuated by SPJs (Fig. 1B). Correspondingly, the HFD increased the mRNA expression of the key lipogenesis genes (glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase 3 [Gpat3], diacylglycerol O-acyltransferase 2 [Dgat2]) while suppressing the lipolysis/catabolism genes (Atgl, monoacylglycerol O-acyltransferase 1 [Mogat1]) in the jejunum. The SPJs intervention effectively reversed these HFD-induced perturbations in lipid metabolic gene expression (Gpat3, Dgat2, Atgl, and Mogat1) (Fig. 1C).

Effects of the SPJs treatment on the inflammation of the jejunum in HFD-fed mice

Considering the established association between ERS and inflammation [41,42], this study investigated the alterations in jejunal inflammatory cytokines using IHC. NF-κB p65, TNF-α, and IL-1β proteins were localized predominantly in the cytoplasm of the jejunal epithelium. HFD feeding induced pronounced upregulation of these inflammatory mediators, which was partially attenuated by SPJs treatment (both doses) (Fig. 3A). Quantitative real-time PCR analysis revealed a substantial increase in jejunal IL-22 mRNA expression in the HFD-fed mice (Fig. 3C); this diet-induced elevation was ameliorated by the SPJs treatment. Collectively, these findings suggest that SPJs ameliorate diet-induced jejunal inflammation.

Dynamic lipid droplet changes in the murine jejunal enterocytes observed by TEM and H&E staining following acute high-dose oral olive oil challenge

The mice received a single 200 μL olive oil gavage to standardize dietary fat intake and assess acute responses [23,24,25]. The jejunal tissues were harvested at 1 h, 2 h, 6 h, and 12 h post-gavage. Quantitative EM analysis revealed time-dependent CLDs dynamics (Fig. 4A, C, and D): significant accumulation of predominantly small CLDs at 1h, decreasing the counts with larger droplets by 6 h, and complete clearance at 12 h. H&E staining corroborated this pattern (Fig. 4B): jejunal enterocytes exhibited pale, vacuolated cytoplasm at 1 h post-gavage, with a slight reduction at 6 h. By 12 h, a uniform eosinophilic staining and normal epithelial architecture were restored, matching those of the vehicle controls. These findings establish a validated model of acute postprandial lipid handling—characterized by initial absorption/saturation (1 h), droplet coalescence (6 h), and metabolic clearance (12 h), consistent with reported lipid handling phases [43].

Dynamic expression of ERS-associated proteins in the mouse jejunum following acute high-dose oral olive oil challenge

IHC was performed to assess the ERS pathway activation in jejunal IECs following acute olive oil gavage. p-PERK was cytoplasmic, p-eIF2α localized to cytoplasm/nucleus, and ATF4 was nuclear (Fig. 5). ATF6α and p-IRE1α exhibited cytoplasmic expression (Fig. 6A). Quantitative analysis showed no significant alterations in p-PERK, p-eIF2α, ATF4, ATF6α, or p-IRE1α levels in high-fat-challenged mice compared to the controls at 1 h, 6 h, or 12 h post-gavage (P > 0.05). Therefore, acute lipid overload does not activate the PERK/eIF2α/ATF4, ATF6, or IRE1 branches of the unfolded protein response in jejunal epithelial cells, suggesting compensatory unfolded protein response (UPR) regulation or pathway-independent lipid handling.

Dynamic expression of antioxidant stress-associated proteins in mouse jejunum following acute high-dose oral olive oil challenge

This study investigated the oxidative stress responses via IHC for Nrf2, FOXO1, and FOXO3a in the jejunal epithelia after acute lipid challenge. All factors exhibited cytoplasmic localization (Fig. 7). Nrf2 expression increased significantly at 1 h post-gavage versus the controls (P < 0.05), returning to the baseline by 12 h. Similarly, FOXO1 and FOXO3a also showed marked elevation at 1 h, normalizing at 6 h. This coordinated, transient activation of cytoprotective transcription factors at 1 h post-challenge indicates an acute adaptive response to lipid-induced oxidative stress in intestinal epithelial cells.

DISCUSSION

The jejunum, as the primary site of dietary lipid absorption, is involved in the dynamic handling of TG through CLDs [43,44,45]. Acute lipid challenge (olive oil gavage) elicited time-dependent CLDs dynamics in jejunal enterocytes: substantial CLDs accumulation at 1 h post-gavage, significant reduction at 6 h, and near-complete clearance by 12 h. Similarly, chronic HFD feeding induced marked CLDs accumulation in jejunal epithelial cells compared to the normal chow controls. Intervention with SPJs significantly attenuated this HFD-induced CLDs deposition in the jejunal epithelium. These coordinated temporal patterns, which were observed in the acute challenges and chronic dietary models, establish CLDs as transient storage organelles central to jejunal lipid homeostasis, with their accumulation pathologically sustained during prolonged high-fat exposure.

The SPJs treatment significantly ameliorated jejunal inflammation and ERS compared to the HFD-fed vehicle control group, which underwent identical gavage procedures. Hence, the beneficial effects were attributable to SPJs rather than to non-specific procedural influences.

The intestinal TG metabolism involves complex enzymatic regulation [46]. The dietary lipids are absorbed via cluster of differentiation 36 and resynthesized into TG via MAG or G3P pathways in enterocytes, using enzymes (MGAT, GPAT, and DGAT) and chaperones (fatty acid-binding protein 1/2). Newly synthesized TGs are stored transiently as CLDs or incorporated into pre-chylomicrons via microsomal triglyceride transfer protein for Golgi maturation, secretion, and systemic delivery. CLDs catabolism occurs via lipolysis (ATGL, hormone-sensitive lipase, and monoglyceride lipase) or lipophagy (lysosomal acid lipase). Under chronic HFD, jejunal TG content increased significantly, accompanied by upregulated lipogenic genes (Gpat3, Dgat2) and downregulated lipolytic Atgl [47,48]. The SPJs treatment effectively reversed these HFD-induced alterations. Considering that SPJs also reduce the serum lipids in HFD mice, these findings suggest that SPJs likely suppress jejunal lipid absorption, synthesis, and CLDs storage, attenuating systemic lipid dissemination. The precise mechanisms require further investigation.

An analysis of the lipid metabolism gene expression data (Fig. 1C) revealed notable variability among biological replicates, particularly within the HFD group. This heterogeneity likely reflects genuine biological differences in the individual responses to chronic HFD feeding rather than technical noise. Diet-induced obesity models produce diverse metabolic phenotypes even in genetically similar rodents, attributable to variations in the gut microbiota composition, baseline metabolic rate, and adaptive transcriptional responses in metabolic tissues [49,50]. As the primary site of fat absorption, the jejunum is exposed directly to dietary stress. Individual differences in lipid-handling efficiency (e.g., in chylomicron synthesis or lipid droplet turnover) naturally translate to variable expression of key regulatory genes such as Gpat3, Dgat2, and Atgl. Significantly, this inter-individual variability appears attenuated in SPJs-treated groups, particularly at higher doses. This supports the hypothesis that SPJs intervention shifts the mean response but promotes metabolic homeostasis, reducing HFD-induced dysregulated variance and yielding a more consistent, healthier lipid metabolic gene expression profile. Therefore, the observed variability itself provides important insights; it shows the destabilizing effect of a HFD on intestinal lipid regulation while highlighting the stabilizing and normalizing capacity of effective intervention.

Although the ER harbors key lipid-metabolizing enzymes and regulates lipid homeostasis, chronic stress disrupts this balance. Previous studies have confirmed diet-induced ERS activation (elevated p-PERK, p-eIF2α, ATF6α, IRE1α, CHOP, and GRP78) in metabolic tissues such as the liver and pancreas [3,51,52,53]. Consistent with this, increased protein expression was observed across all 3 UPR pathways (PERK, ATF6, and IRE1) in the jejunal epithelium of chronically HFD-fed mice. In particular, SPJs intervention attenuated this ERS response. Surprisingly, acute olive oil challenge (1 h, 6 h, and 12 h post-gavage) did not activate PERK, ATF6, or IRE1 signaling in enterocytes. This paper proposes that enterocytes possess a rapid lipid-buffering capacity via transient CLDs formation. This system sequesters excess free FAs during physiological absorption, mitigating lipotoxic pressure through controlled lipolysis and preventing acute ERS. These findings show that jejunal enterocytes efficiently handle acute lipid loads without triggering canonical UPR pathways, maintaining homeostasis through CLDs dynamics. Therefore, the threshold for ERS induction appears to depend on the exposure duration and the functional capacity of the lipid-buffering machinery, including CLDs biogenesis regulators like DGAT1 and perilipins—highlighting the unique adaptability of the intestine to dietary lipid flux.

Lipotoxic ERS can be pro-inflammatory because some studies have shown that it can lead to the release of pro-inflammatory extracellular vesicles [3,53]. Studies in the HFD-fed mice have also revealed low-grade inflammation in several organs, including the intestine [12,54,55]. Furthermore, in mammals, NF-κB serves as a pivotal transcription factor controlling the inflammatory responses. The activation of NF-κB prompts its relocation to the nucleus, leading to the upregulation of inflammatory genes, including TNF-α and IL-1β. A metabolic environment rich in saturated fatty acids (SFAs) can also increase the expression of inflammatory factor IL-23, which induces Th17 cells to produce cytokines such as IL-22 [53]. In line with earlier findings, this study also observed elevated levels of NF-κB, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-22 in the jejunum of mice consuming a HFD, with this upregulation being mitigated by the SPJs treatment.

Long-term HFD feeding in mice enhances the expression of genes involved in intestinal FA oxidation. This metabolic adaptation in intestinal epithelial cells may attenuate the HFD-induced elevation of circulating lipid levels. In addition, CLDs can contact organelles, such as the mitochondria, mediating the transport of FAs into the mitochondria for oxidation. Nevertheless, the regulatory mechanisms for the allocation of intestinal FAs between TG transport and oxidation remain to be clarified.

Several studies have reported that excessive SFAs reprogram mitochondrial metabolism, triggering an overload of the electron transport chain and a reactive oxygen species (ROS) burst. ROS subsequently attack membrane phospholipids enriched in polyunsaturated FAs, triggering a chain reaction of lipid peroxidation that exacerbates oxidative stress. Consequently, antioxidant pathways such as the Nrf2 signaling axis may be induced to counteract the cellular damage and restore homeostasis [56,57,58]. The results showed that a single high-dose olive oil gavage did not induce significant ERS in the jejunal tissue of mice. Therefore, this study examined the molecular markers associated with antioxidant stress in the jejunum. These findings reveal the transient upregulation of antioxidant defense proteins (Nrf2, FOXO1, FOXO3a) in intestinal absorptive cells at 1 h post-high-fat challenge, followed by a return to the baseline levels by 6–12 h. This temporal pattern suggests that early-phase high-fat exposure rapidly activates the adaptive antioxidant responses, which then subside as lipid-processing mechanisms engage. This study hypothesized that initial lipid overload induces acute oxidative stress, triggering this protective cascade, while the later-phase resolution may be mediated by chylomicron-dependent lipid efflux—a process that alleviates intracellular lipid accumulation and associated stress.

Although direct ROS quantification was not performed in this study, the observed early antioxidant induction suggests the mitigation of severe ERS, consistent with reports linking Nrf2 activation to maintenance of ER homeostasis [58,59]. Critically, the data raise an unresolved question: Does persistent low-level oxidative stress persist beyond the decline of antioxidant protein expression during prolonged high-fat feeding? Such residual stress could represent a key driver of chronic intestinal cell dysfunction and metabolic pathology. Future studies should quantify the longitudinal ROS dynamics and integrate multi-omics approaches to determine if 1) sustained subclinical oxidative injury underlies long-term HFD toxicity, or 2) compensatory mechanisms (e.g., enhanced mitochondrial uncoupling or lipid droplet sequestration) fully restore redox homeostasis.

This investigation specifically targeted the jejunum to examine HFD-induced intestinal inflammation based on 3 compelling rationales. As the primary site of dietary lipid absorption, the jejunum is particularly vulnerable to HFD, exhibiting pronounced lipid accumulation and ERS [60,61]. Furthermore, jejunal inflammation manifests primarily through metabolic triggers rather than the immune-centric inflammation characteristic of the ileum [62], aligning with the observed upregulation of ERS markers and pro-inflammatory cytokines associated with metabolic pathways. Because SPJs are designed to alleviate lipid accumulation and ERS, their therapeutic effects would be most detectable at the initial site of lipid overload—the jejunum. The experimental results confirmed that SPJs significantly reduced the CLDs and ERS markers specifically within the jejunal epithelium, effectively attenuating inflammation. Although this study focused on the jejunum as the principal site of fat absorption, the fundamental cellular mechanisms targeted by SPJs—reduction of lipid accumulation and ERS—represent core aspects of enterocyte homeostasis. Therefore, the therapeutic benefits of SPJs are likely to be applicable to other intestinal segments susceptible to HFD-induced damage, but potential regional variations warrant further investigation to understand the segment-specific responses.

While this study showed that SPJs effectively attenuate the key upstream events in HFD-induced jejunal pathology—namely, lipid metabolic dysregulation, ERS, and local inflammation—it is important to note its limitations. The absence of direct functional assessments of the intestinal barrier integrity (e.g., in vivo permeability assays and comprehensive tight junction protein analysis) and gut microbiota profiling significantly constrains the mechanistic interpretation of SPJs-mediated protection. Specifically, the lack of barrier function data prevents establishing a direct causal link between the observed attenuation of ERS and local inflammation and the actual improvement in gut barrier integrity. Although the decrease in inflammatory and ERS markers strongly suggests improved barrier function, it is unclear whether SPJs directly enhance the tight junction assembly or reduce the intestinal permeability without functional validation. Furthermore, the absence of microbiota analysis limits the understanding of the potential role of gut microbial communities in SPJs-mediated protection. Considering the well-established crosstalk between the gut microbiota, barrier function, and metabolic health, it is unclear if SPJs exert their effects directly by modulating the intestinal epithelium or indirectly via reshaping the gut microbial ecosystem. The gut microbiota could contribute to the observed metabolic improvements by producing beneficial metabolites or modulating the immune responses of the host. Consequently, although the findings provide important mechanistic insights within the jejunal epithelium, the functional consequences of SPJs intervention on the gut barrier integrity and systemic inflammation remain to be confirmed. Future studies incorporating these functional endpoints, including comprehensive barrier function assessments and multi-omics analyses of the gut microbiota, will be crucial for validating the therapeutic potential of SPJs for improving the gut barrier integrity and overall metabolic health in the context of obesity.

The experimental design used distinct lipid sources to model different physiological conditions: olive oil (rich in monounsaturated fatty acids, MUFAs) for acute gavage studies and a lard-based diet (high in SFAs) for chronic high-fat feeding. This approach was designed to simulate transient high-volume lipid challenges and sustained metabolically stressful conditions. Although this methodology effectively showed that chronic HFD surpasses the ERS activation threshold not reached by acute MUFAs exposure, the differential potency of SFAs versus MUFAs in inducing ERS is a significant variable. The established stronger ERS-inducing capacity of SFAs suggests that the specific FA composition, not just the duration of lipid overload, potently influences the intensity of the unfolded protein response. Consequently, the specific “threshold” for ERS induction observed in the present study is intrinsically linked to the lipid qualities used—primarily SFAs in the chronic setting and MUFAs in the acute challenge.

Therefore, the generalizability of the conclusions regarding acute versus chronic ERS thresholds may be influenced by the specific FA profiles used. Using an SFA-rich bolus for an acute challenge might lower the threshold and potentially trigger ERS even after short-term exposure. On the other hand, a chronic diet rich in MUFAs might raise the threshold, delaying or attenuating ERS onset compared to the SFA-based HFD model. These findings show that lipid quantity and quality are critical determinants of intestinal ERS homeostasis. The differential effects observed suggest a rational dietary strategy: MUFAs-rich diets, such as olive oil, may promote better enterocyte tolerance for efficient fat absorption without triggering acute ERS pathways, whereas chronic SFAs consumption likely lowers the ERS activation threshold, promoting metabolic dysfunction. This insight aligns with the documented benefits of Mediterranean diets and emphasizes that dietary fat quality is a pivotal factor for maintaining intestinal metabolic health, alongside quantitative considerations. Future studies systematically comparing different FA types under acute and chronic feeding regimens will be essential to define more universal principles governing the lipid-induced ERS thresholds in the intestine.

Mice fed a HFD exhibited substantial accumulation of variably sized lipid vacuoles within the small intestinal enterocytes. Treatment with SPJ, particularly at high doses, significantly attenuated this lipid vacuole aggregation compared to the HFD group. Furthermore, prolonged HFD consumption induced ERS and inflammation in the murine small intestine, pathologies that were effectively reversed by SPJs intervention. In particular, short-term exposure to a high-fat environment enhanced the antioxidant stress response in intestinal enterocytes without altering the ERS levels. These observations provide novel insights into the adaptive resistance pathways used by enterocytes during early exposure to a HFD and delineate the distinct cellular stress responses elicited by acute versus chronic HFD. Collectively, these results establish SPJs as a promising therapeutic candidate for mitigating HFD-induced intestinal inflammation and its associated sequelae.

The data show that intestinal epithelial cells in mice require at least 12 h to complete lipid processing after a high-fat meal. This finding aligns with previous observations showing near-complete depletion of cytoplasmic lipid droplets in enterocytes within 12 h of oral administration of 300 μL olive oil [43]. Collectively, these results suggest that an interval of at least 12 h between successive high-fat meals is physiologically advisable to ensure adequate lipid clearance by the intestinal enterocytes.
