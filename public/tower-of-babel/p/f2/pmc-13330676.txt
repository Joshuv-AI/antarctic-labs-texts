Structure-based identification of small-molecule stabilizers targeting mutant TP53 (Y220C) in breast cancer: a pharmacophore modeling, molecular docking and dynamics study
Authors: Peter Jerome Ishmael V Paulino, Mohammad Tasyriq Che Omar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMutations in TP53, particularly the Y220C variant, destabilize the protein and impair tumor-suppressor function, driving breast cancer progression. Restoring the structural integrity of TP53 Y220C using small-molecule stabilizers represents a potential therapeutic strategy.MethodsPharmacophore modeling based on the co-crystal ligand binding site guided screening of a ligand library using the Pharmit platform. Molecular docking, ADME, and toxicity predictions filtered drug-like and safe candidates. Molecular dynamics simulations (MDS) and per-residue MM/PBSA analyses assessed binding stability, structural fluctuations, and energetic contributions.ResultsTP53 Y220C was found to be highly expressed in breast cancer. Pharmacophore-guided virtual screening and molecular docking identified several promising compounds with favorable drug-likeness and safety profiles. Among these, three ligands, including CPD0001, CPD0136, and CPD0330, demonstrated stable binding and favorable interactions with the Y220C cavity. Molecular dynamics analyses revealed that CPD0001 and CPD0136 maintained superior structural stability, reduced conformational fluctuations, compact cavity organization, and coordinated residue motions throughout the simulation period, closely resembling the co-crystal ligand. In contrast, CPD0330 demonstrated moderate stabilization with comparatively higher flexibility. MM/PBSA and per-residue energy decomposition analyses further supported the strong binding affinity and stabilizing potential of CPD0001 and CPD0136. CPD0330 showed moderate stabilization comparable to co-crystal ligands.ConclusionsOverall, CPD0001 and CPD0136 emerged as promising small-molecule stabilizers capable of reinforcing the TP53 Y220C mutant cavity and maintaining structural stability comparable to the co-crystal ligand. Background

Breast cancer is a highly heterogeneous malignancy and a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among women globally 1. Despite improvements in early detection and targeted therapies, resistance to conventional treatments and disease recurrence remain major challenges, particularly in aggressive subtypes such as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) 2. At the molecular level, disruptions in tumor suppressor pathways, oncogenic signaling, and DNA repair mechanisms contribute significantly to breast cancer initiation and progression. Among these, mutations in the TP53 gene — which encodes the p53 tumor suppressor protein — are among the most prevalent genetic alterations observed in breast tumors 3.

p53 plays a central role in maintaining genomic stability by regulating cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, senescence, and DNA repair in response to cellular stress. Its function is tightly controlled through a complex network of transcriptional and post-translational mechanisms 4. Loss of p53 function through genetic alterations, particularly missense mutations within the DNA-binding domain, is associated with a failure to execute these tumor suppressor programs, thereby facilitating cancer cell survival and progression 5. In breast cancer, TP53 mutations are detected in approximately 30–35% of cases and rise to nearly 80% in TNBC and basal-like subtypes, correlating with aggressive clinical features and poor therapeutic response 6.

A large proportion of TP53 missense mutations result in structurally destabilized proteins that adopt aberrant conformations, losing their native DNA-binding functions while sometimes gaining oncogenic activities such as promoting invasion or therapy resistance 7. One such mutation, Y220C, is particularly noteworthy because it creates a surface cavity adjacent to the DNA-binding interface by replacing a tyrosine residue with cysteine at position 220. This substitution destabilizes the local fold without directly impacting the DNA contact surface; importantly, it generates a mutation-specific pocket that can be exploited for small-molecule binding 8. The presence of a structurally defined crevice distinguishes Y220C from other p53 mutations and has stimulated significant interest as a druggable target.

Historically, p53 was considered a challenging target for small molecules due to its lack of deep binding pockets in the wild-type structure. However, mutations such as Y220C that create distinct cavities have shifted this perception, offering opportunities for structure-guided drug discovery 9. The goal is not to inhibit a hyperactive oncogene but to restore a broken tumor suppressor — a paradigm that fundamentally differs from traditional anticancer drug design. The premise is that small molecules can bind inside the mutation-induced pocket, stabilize the local structure, reduce conformational flexibility, and partially restore wild-type function, thereby reactivating p53-mediated tumor suppression 10.

Several preclinical studies support this concept. For example, small molecules capable of stabilizing mutant p53 have been shown to increase thermal stability, improve DNA binding, and activate downstream p53 target genes in cellular models 11. These findings suggest that pharmacological reactivation of mutant p53 could suppress tumor growth or sensitize cancer cells to existing therapies. The structural characteristics of the Y220C pocket — comprised of both hydrophobic regions and potential hydrogen-bonding partners — enable rational design of ligands with complementary features, guiding efforts toward high-affinity binding and stabilization.

Translating this strategy into the clinic, the compound PC14586 (rezatapopt) is the most advanced example of a mutant p53 stabilizer, specifically targeting Y220C. Early clinical evaluation indicates tolerability and preliminary antitumor activity in patients with tumors harboring this mutation 12. Its clinical progress provides an important reference framework for contextualizing the present findings and assessing the mechanistic relevance of the identified compounds as potential stabilizers of mutant p53. Collectively, these clinical observations support the feasibility of p53 reactivation and highlight the need for continued discovery of structurally diverse stabilizers with improved efficacy and safety profiles.

Despite encouraging progress, identifying effective small-molecule stabilizers remains complex. Mutant p53 proteins exhibit dynamic conformational landscapes, and achieving both high affinity and favorable pharmacokinetic properties is challenging. Moreover, canonical high-throughput screening approaches may miss novel scaffolds that engage key pockets optimally. Therefore, integrating computational methods such as pharmacophore modeling, virtual screening, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations represents a powerful approach to accelerate early hit identification and refine candidate selection.

Pharmacophore modeling captures the essential chemical features required for ligand-target interaction, acting as a 3D filter to enrich compound libraries for molecules that match the structural and chemical blueprint of the binding pocket 13. When based on co-crystal structural information, pharmacophore hypotheses can reveal spatial arrangements of hydrogen bond donors/acceptors, hydrophobic features, and other interaction hotspots, guiding ligand selection more intelligently than simple shape or fingerprint similarity approaches.

Once pharmacophore hits are identified, molecular docking provides a means to predict how these compounds fit into the target pocket and estimate relative binding affinities. Docking algorithms rank candidate ligands based on energetics and complementarity to the receptor, helping prioritize molecules for further evaluation. However, docking predictions are inherently static and do not account for protein flexibility or temporal interaction stability.

Molecular dynamics simulations extend docking by modeling the behavior of protein–ligand complexes over time, revealing how interactions persist, fluctuate, or dissolve under simulated physiological conditions. MD helps elucidate key dynamic processes such as hydrogen-bond lifetimes, conformational flexibility, and solvent effects, which are critical for understanding whether a ligand can truly stabilize a mutant protein 14. Combining MD with free energy calculations, such as MM/PBSA or FEL analysis, further quantifies the energetic favorability of binding and identifies dominant low-energy conformational states that may correlate with functional stabilization.

An equally important consideration in early-stage drug discovery is the evaluation of drug-likeness and safety. In silico ADME and toxicity profiling filters out candidates with undesirable pharmacokinetic or toxicological properties before costly experimental validation. Compounds that comply with established medicinal chemistry filters such as Lipinski's rule of five, Ghose, Veber, Egan, and Muegge criteria are more likely to exhibit favorable bioavailability and systemic behavior, thereby increasing the probability of translational success 15. Toxicity predictions — including acute toxicity estimates and potential organ-specific liabilities — provide early warnings that can be addressed through scaffold modification or alternative prioritization.

Recent advances in biomedical research have transformed cancer therapeutics through the integration of experimental and computational platforms. Emerging 3D organoid systems and organ-on-chip models provide more physiologically relevant tumor microenvironment simulations, improving translational accuracy in drug discovery 16. In parallel, nanomedicine and biomaterial-based systems have shown promising anticancer and targeted delivery potential, particularly in breast and other cancer models, as demonstrated by recent studies on chitosan-based, zinc oxide, and nanosulfur-doped nanocomposites 17, 18, 19. Alongside these developments, computational approaches such as pharmacophore modeling, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and network pharmacology have become essential tools in cancer drug discovery, enabling efficient screening and optimization of therapeutic candidates 20, 21. Collectively, these integrated strategies reflect a shift toward precision and computationally driven oncology research.

Although mutant p53 stabilization represents a promising therapeutic strategy, most existing computational studies targeting TP53 Y220C rely primarily on molecular docking alone, without incorporating protein dynamics, pharmacokinetic behavior, or toxicity evaluation. This limits the translational reliability of identified candidates and leaves a critical gap in the rational design of stable and drug-like Y220C stabilizers. Given that the Y220C mutation creates a structurally destabilized cavity that can potentially be rescued through small-molecule binding, a more integrated computational framework is required to accurately capture binding stability and conformational dynamics. We hypothesize that a multi-layered structure-based approach combining pharmacophore modeling, virtual screening, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and ADMET profiling can identify small molecules capable of effectively stabilizing the TP53 Y220C mutant cavity. Accordingly, the present study aims to systematically discover and prioritize potential Y220C stabilizers with favorable binding affinity, dynamic stability, and drug-like properties, thereby providing a rational basis for further experimental validation and development of targeted breast cancer therapeutics.

Methodology

Analysis of TP53 Y220C expression in breast cancer

RNA-seq data for breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA) were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), 22 and samples harboring the TP53 Y220C mutation were identified using mutational information from the cBioPortal database (https://www.cbioportal.org/). Log2-normalized expression values of TP53 Y220C were extracted for tumor and normal breast tissue samples. Statistical comparison between the Y220C tumor group and normal tissues was performed using Welch's t-test to account for unequal variances, with significance defined as p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using the R programming environment.

Pharmacophoric features of human TP53 Y220C–A1ARZ structure

A structure-based pharmacophore model was generated from the TP53 Y220C–A1ARZ co-crystal complex (PDB ID: 9BR4) 23 using the web-based Pharmit platform (http://pharmit.csb.pitt.edu/) 24. The protein-ligand complex file was directly uploaded to the server, and pharmacophoric features were automatically extracted to define key ligand-binding interactions. The resulting features were further filtered based on their involvement in stable hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic contacts within the binding pocket, considering only interactions satisfying appropriate geometric constraints (distance and orientation criteria). Pharmit was chosen for its ability to efficiently generate robust pharmacophore queries and perform virtual screening against large compound libraries.

Identification of compounds through structure-based pharmacophore screening

The generated pharmacophore model was applied to screen Pharmit's integrated compound libraries for potential small-molecule modulators 24 The virtual screening encompassed approximately 305 million unique compounds, representing more than 1.4 billion possible conformations. All compounds meeting the pharmacophore criteria were retrieved and exported in SDF format for subsequent computational analyses.

Ligand library curation

The compounds obtained from virtual screening were processed and curated using RDKit (v2023.03.1). Structural standardization was performed with the MolStandardize module, including charge neutralization, normalization of functional groups, and removal of salts. Only the largest molecular fragment of each compound was retained to preserve structural completeness. Tautomeric forms were standardized, and canonical SMILES representations were generated. Redundant molecules were eliminated using unique InChIKey identifiers, producing a cleaned, non-redundant ligand library ready for downstream computational analyses.

Identification of binding sites and molecular docking

The ligand-binding pockets and key interaction residues of the TP53 Y220C protein (PDB ID: 9BR4) were first predicted using PrankWeb (https://prankweb.cz/), which employs machine learning to analyze structural features and highlight regions with a high likelihood of small-molecule binding. In parallel, co-crystal ligand interactions and known protein–protein interfaces were examined to identify critical residues contributing to binding. Based on these analyses, the docking grid was defined. With the following parameters: center_x = 126.2246, center_y = −66.2732, center_z = 44.3202, size_x = 25.3576, size_y = 21.6729, and size_z = 22.1150, ensuring that all relevant interaction sites were encompassed.

Molecular docking and virtual screening were carried out using PyRx (https://pyrx.sourceforge.io/). Protein and ligand structures were prepared and converted to PDBQT format, and site-specific docking was performed within the defined grid. Binding affinities for each ligand–protein complex were recorded, and the resulting binding poses were examined. Molecular interactions, including hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, and other non-covalent interactions, were visualized and analyzed using BIOVIA Discovery Studio, allowing identification of the key determinants of ligand recognition and binding stability.

Validation of docking

The docking procedure was validated using the co-crystallized ligand A1ARZ from the TP53 Y220C complex (PDB ID: 9BR4). A1ARZ was extracted from the crystal structure, prepared, and re-docked into the rigid binding site of TP53 Y220C using AutoDock Vina, with the grid centered on the coordinates predicted by PrankWeb. The accuracy and reliability of the docking protocol were assessed by calculating the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) between the re-docked pose and the original crystallographic conformation using Discovery Studio Visualizer.

Pharmacokinetic, drug-likeness, and toxicity prediction

The top-ranked compounds identified through molecular docking were further evaluated for their pharmacokinetic properties and toxicity profiles to ensure suitability as potential drug candidates. SwissADME was used to assess drug-likeness parameters, including gastrointestinal absorption and adherence to established rules such as Lipinski, Ghose, Veber, Egan, and Muegge 15. Compounds exhibiting structural features associated with nonspecific or promiscuous binding were removed by filtering for Pan-Assay Interference Compounds (PAINS) 25 Additionally, potential toxicological liabilities were predicted using the ProTox 3.0 server 26 This systematic ADMET and toxicity screening allowed the selection of compounds with favorable pharmacokinetic profiles, low predicted toxicity, and high potential for further computational and experimental validation.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the TP53 Y220C–ligand complexes were conducted using GROMACS 2025.3 with the CHARMM36 force field. Ligand parameters were generated using the CHARMM General Force Field (CGenFF) 27. Each system was solvated in a cubic box with TIP3P water molecules, neutralized by adding appropriate counter ions, and subjected to energy minimization to remove steric clashes. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm for up to 50,000 steps until convergence was achieved with a maximum force threshold of <100 kJ·mol−1·nm−1, effectively removing steric clashes. The system was then equilibrated in two stages. First, a 500 ps NVT equilibration was carried out at 300 K using the V-rescale thermostat to stabilize temperature. This was followed by a 2 ns NPT equilibration at 300 K and 1 bar using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat with isotropic pressure coupling and a coupling constant of 2 ps to ensure stable pressure conditions. Finally, a 200 ns production molecular dynamics simulation was conducted under periodic boundary conditions for trajectory analysis. Production simulations were carried out for 200 ns for each complex. Trajectories were analyzed to assess structural stability and dynamics, including root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), buried SASA, ligand–protein distances (average and minimum), and hydrogen bonding using standard GROMACS analysis tools.

Principal component-based conformational dynamics analysis

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to examine the dominant collective motions of the protein-ligand complexes. The covariance matrix of Cα atomic fluctuations was computed and diagonalized using the gmx covar and gmx anaeig tools in GROMACS. The resulting eigenvectors were further analyzed with the Bio3D package in R 28. To visualize the directionality and magnitude of major conformational changes, extreme projections along the principal components were represented as porcupine plots in PyMOL.

Conformational free energy and residue motion correlation analysis

The free energy landscape (FEL) of the protein-ligand complexes was generated from the first two principal components using the gmx sham module in GROMACS and visualized with Matplotlib in Python to identify low-energy conformational states. To evaluate residue-level coordinated motions, dynamic cross-correlation matrices (DCCM) were calculated using the Bio3D package in R, 28 after converting the trajectory files to .dcd format with VMD. Additionally, three-dimensional correlated and anticorrelated motion networks were constructed and visualized using Bio3D in R and PyMOL, providing insights into coordinated residue dynamics and allosteric communication within the complexes.

MM-PBSA-based binding free energy and per-residue decomposition analysis

The binding free energies of the TP53 Y220C–ligand complexes were estimated using the Molecular Mechanics/Generalized Born Surface Area (MM/GBSA) approach implemented in g_mmpbsa. Calculations were performed on snapshots extracted from the 200 ns molecular dynamics trajectories. Binding energies were determined using the standard thermodynamic relationship: ΔG_binding = G_complex − (G_protein + G_ligand). This analysis provided a quantitative assessment of ligand-binding affinities and the stability of the complexes, enabling comparison of lead compounds (CPD0001, CPD0136, CPD0330) with the co-crystallized reference ligand (A1ARZ).

Results

Differential expression of TP53 Y220C in breast cancer

As shown in Fig. 1, TP53 Y220C expression (log2 normalized) was substantially upregulated in breast tumor samples compared with normal breast tissues. Statistical analysis using Welch's t-test revealed a significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.0425), with a log2 fold change (log2FC) of 1.07 in tumor samples (Fig. 1). These results indicate that TP53 Y220C is substantially upregulated in breast cancer, suggesting that this mutant may contribute to tumorigenesis and disease progression. Given its elevated expression and potential functional impact on p53 activity, TP53 Y220C may represent a biologically relevant variant and a promising target for therapeutic intervention in breast cancer.

Pharmacophoric features of human p53 Y220C mutant in complex with PC-9859 (A1ARZ)

To elucidate the key molecular determinants of ligand recognition, a pharmacophore model of the human TP53 Y220C mutant in complex with A1ARZ was generated (Fig. 2A–B). The model revealed two hydrogen bond donors (HBD1 and HBD2) and five hydrophobic features (Hydrophobic 1–5) within the binding pocket, indicating a combination of polar and nonpolar interactions that stabilize ligand binding. The spatial arrangement of these features was determined by their Cartesian coordinates and radii (Table 1), providing a 3D representation of the binding cavity. Notably, the hydrogen bond donors (HBD1 at X = 124.267, Y = −70.779, Z = 44.466 Å; HBD2 at X = 124.277, Y = −62.983, Z = 48.106 Å) were positioned to facilitate optimal hydrogen bonding with the ligand, while the hydrophobic points clustered within the pocket to form van der Waals interactions that complement the polar contacts.

Visualization using Discovery Studio Visualizer (DSV) and the TP53 Y220C–A1ARZ crystal structure revealed that the hydrogen bond donor sites interact with THR150 and CYS220, whereas the hydrophobic features engage residues LEU145, VAL147, PRO151, PRO153, PRO222, and PRO223. Virtual screening of small-molecule libraries based on TP53 Y220C pharmacophore modeling

Based on the pharmacophore model of the TP53 Y220C–A1ARZ complex, virtual screening was conducted across multiple chemical libraries to identify potential small-molecule stabilizers. The screening encompassed databases including PHARMIT, ChEMBL (16 compounds), ChemDiv (2 compounds), ChemSpace (5 compounds), Enamine (1 compound), Mcule (56 compounds), MolPort (17 compounds), PubChem (333 compounds), LabNetwork (2 compounds), and ZINC (21 compounds). Following the removal of redundant entries, a total of 360 unique compounds were obtained. Supplementary Table 1 provides detailed information on these compounds, including their Compound IDs, SMILES notations, and corresponding Molecule IDs within the respective databases. These compounds serve as promising candidates for subsequent structure-based evaluation and rational drug design targeting the TP53 Y220C mutant.

Identification and validation of key binding site residues in TP53 Y220C via co-crystal analysis and molecular docking

The identification of key amino acid residues within the TP53 Y220C binding pocket was performed using a systematic two-step approach. Initially, residues involved in co-crystal interactions (LEU145, VAL147, ASP48, THR150, PRO151, PRO153, CYS220, PRO222, PRO223, and THR230) were characterized (Fig. 3A) and subsequently validated using the PrankWeb server to confirm their functional relevance. Based on these validated residues, a docking grid box was generated to define the search space, with dimensions set as follows: center_x = 126.2246, center_y = −66.2732, center_z = 44.3202, size_x = 25.3576, size_y = 21.6729, and size_z = 22.1150. To evaluate the accuracy of the docking protocol, the native co-crystal ligand was re-docked into the defined binding pocket (Fig. 3B). The resulting ligand interactions involved residues PHE109, LEU145, TRP146, VAL147, THR150, PRO151, PRO152, PRO153, GLY154, THR155, VAL157, CYS220, GLU221, PRO222, PRO223, ASP228, CYS229, THR230, and LEU257. Comparison with the original co-crystal interactions revealed a set of common residues, namely PHE109, LEU145, VAL147, THR150, PRO151, PRO153, CYS220, PRO222, PRO223, and THR230. The co-crystal ligand and the docked pose were subsequently superimposed (Fig. 3C–D), and the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) between them was determined to be 1.509 Å. This low RMSD indicates a high degree of structural concordance, confirming the reliability of the docking procedure and supporting the validity of the identified binding site residues for subsequent virtual screening and structure-based ligand design studies.

ADME profiling and drug-likeness evaluation of pharmacophore-based screened compounds

The ADME and drug-likeness profiles of all pharmacophore-derived compounds were systematically evaluated using SwissADME, and the complete results are presented in Supplementary Tables S2. Comprehensive analysis of physicochemical parameters, lipophilicity, solubility, pharmacokinetics, and medicinal chemistry filters was performed to ensure the selection of candidates with favorable drug-like characteristics. Among the screened molecules, 46 compounds exhibited zero violations across Lipinski, Ghose, Veber, Egan, and Muegge criteria, indicating optimal molecular weight, polarity, lipophilicity, flexibility, and hydrogen bonding properties consistent with good oral bioavailability (Supplementary Tables S3). In addition, most of these compounds demonstrated acceptable GI absorption, manageable lipophilicity (consensus Log P within recommended limits), and satisfactory synthetic accessibility scores, further supporting their pharmaceutical feasibility. Importantly, none of the selected compounds showed critical PAINS alerts, minimizing the risk of false-positive biological activity. Compared to the co-crystal ligand, which did not comply with all drug-likeness filters, the selected 46 compounds displayed superior overall pharmacokinetic and medicinal chemistry profiles. These findings strengthen the reliability of the virtual screening workflow and support the prioritization of these drug-like candidates for subsequent docking and further biological evaluation.

CPD0330 (−8.3 kcal/mol) displayed multiple hydrogen bonds with LEU145, VAL147, ASP148, SER227, and ASP228, and retained critical interactions with THR230, PRO151, GLU221, PRO222, PRO223, and CYS229. CPD0001 and CPD0349 (both −8.2 kcal/mol) showed extensive overlap with the A1ARZ interaction profile. Notably, both compounds engaged CYS220 and THR230 and shared conserved hydrophobic contacts with PHE109, LEU145, TRP146, VAL147, THR150, VAL157, PRO222, PRO223, ASP228, CYS229, and LEU257. CPD0001 additionally formed carbon hydrogen bonds with PRO223 and GLU224.

Ligands with binding affinities of −8.1 kcal/mol (CPD0078, CPD0202, and CPD0252) consistently maintained interactions with CYS220 and THR230 and preserved contacts with PRO151, GLU221, LEU145, TRP146, VAL147, THR150, PRO222, PRO223, ASP228, and CYS229. CPD0078 and CPD0202 both formed hydrogen bonds with ARG110, THR150, and ASP228, whereas CPD0252 established a hydrogen bond with VAL147 and a carbon hydrogen interaction with LEU145.

CPD0136 (−8.0 kcal/mol) and CPD0077 (−7.9 kcal/mol) also maintained the conserved interaction network characteristic of the Y220C pocket. CPD0136 formed hydrogen bonds with PRO151, CYS220, and GLU221, while CPD0077 established hydrogen bonding with CYS220 and ASP228 and a carbon hydrogen interaction with PRO151. Both compounds preserved extensive hydrophobic contacts with LEU145, TRP146, VAL147, THR150, PRO152, PRO153, THR155, PRO222, PRO223, and LEU257, closely resembling the co-crystal binding pattern.

Comparative analysis across all ligands revealed a highly conserved interaction network centered on CYS220, THR230, GLU221, PRO222, and PRO223, with frequent contributions from LEU145, TRP146, VAL147, THR150, ASP228, and CYS229. Engagement of the mutated residue CYS220 was consistently observed among nearly all ligands, underscoring its critical role in anchoring and specificity within the mutant cavity. The substantial overlap of binding pocket residues with those of A1ARZ confirms that these compounds occupy the canonical Y220C crevice and mimic the structural stabilization mechanism of the co-crystal ligand.

Overall, the docking results demonstrate that the screened compounds maintain the essential interaction framework required for effective stabilization of the TP53 Y220C mutant, with several ligands showing strong conservation of hotspot residues comparable to the reference ligand A1ARZ.

These findings suggest that CPD0136 is a promising modulator of TP53 Y220C.

Structural compactness and surface exposure of TP53 Y220C complexes

The compactness of TP53 Y220C in complex with the selected ligands was evaluated using the radius of gyration (Rg). The average Rg values for CPD0001, CPD0136, and CPD0330 were 1.68 ± 0.01 nm (1.64–1.76 nm), 1.67 ± 0.008 nm (1.64–1.71 nm), and 1.70 ± 0.008 nm (1.66–1.73 nm), respectively. Notably, CPD0136 exhibited a slightly lower average Rg value and a narrower fluctuation range compared to the other complexes, suggesting enhanced structural compactness and stable folding behavior. In agreement with the RMSD and RMSF analyses, these findings further support CPD0136 as a stable TP53 Y220C modulator that preserves protein structural integrity during molecular dynamics simulations.

The solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) of TP53 Y220C and the ligand–protein buried SASA were analyzed to further evaluate structural stability and binding interactions. The average SASA values for CPD0001, CPD0136, and CPD0330 were 114.29 ± 3.63 nm2 (103.06–128.07 nm2), 108.22 ± 2.20 nm2 (100.36–116.80 nm2), and 111.54 ± 2.30 nm2 (103.28–121.34 nm2), respectively. Collectively, the Rg and SASA analyses, together with RMSD and RMSF results, indicate that CPD0136 effectively preserves TP53 Y220C structural compactness while forming a stable and favorable interaction interface, further supporting its potential as a promising TP53 Y220C modulator.

Principal component analysis and correlated motions of TP53 Y220C complexes

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the Bio3D package to investigate the dominant collective motions of TP53 Y220C in complex with the selected ligands (Fig. 8). The essential dynamics were examined through scree plots, projection along the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2), comparative PC1–PC2 conformational sampling plots, dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM) analysis, porcupine plots, and three-dimensional correlated/anticorrelated motion mapping. The scree plot analysis demonstrated that the first two principal components captured a substantial proportion of the total conformational variance in all systems. The co-crystal ligand A1ARZ exhibited the highest contribution along PC1 (38.27%) and a moderate contribution along PC2 (12.31%) (Fig. 8D), indicating that a large portion of the protein motion is dominated by a single collective mode in the reference complex. In contrast, the tested ligands showed lower variance along PC1: 20.44% for CPD0001 (Fig. 8A), 26.94% for CPD0136 (Fig. 8B), and 18.73% for CPD0330 (Fig. 8C). PC2 contributions were 16.68%, 11.29%, and 10.41% for CPD0001, CPD0136, and CPD0330, respectively (Fig. 8). Although CPD0136 exhibited higher PC1 variance than CPD0001 and CPD0330, its contribution remained notably lower than that of the co-crystal complex, indicating reduced dominance of large-scale collective motion compared with A1ARZ. The redistribution of variance across PC1 and PC2 in the ligand-bound systems suggests modulation of the intrinsic dynamic behavior of TP53 Y220C upon ligand binding.

MM/PBSA binding free energy analysis

To quantitatively evaluate the binding affinity and energetic contribution of the selected small-molecule stabilizers toward mutant Tumor protein p53 (Y220C), the MM/PBSA approach was applied to the 200 ns of the molecular dynamics simulations.MM/PBSA analysis over the full 200 ns trajectory revealed that all four ligands bound favorably to the TP53 (Y220C) mutant, with total binding free energies (ΔTOTAL) of −20.85 kcal/mol (CPD0001), −31.59 kcal/mol (CPD0136), −18.00 kcal/mol (CPD0330), and − 25.92 kcal/mol (co-crystal) (Fig. 12 A–D). Across all complexes, van der Waals (ΔVDWAALS) and electrostatic (ΔEEL) interactions were the principal stabilizing forces, while polar solvation (ΔEPB) partially opposed binding and nonpolar solvation (ΔENPOLAR) contributed modestly to overall stability. CPD0136 exhibited the strongest van der Waals and electrostatic interactions, but also incurred the largest polar solvation penalty, suggesting a more constrained binding environment. It should be noted that the polar solvation term reflects the desolvation cost upon ligand binding within the protein pocket and does not directly indicate aqueous solubility of the compound. CPD0001 and the co-crystal complex displayed a balanced interplay of stabilizing forces and solvation effects, resulting in favorable overall binding and thermodynamic stability. In contrast, CPD0330 showed the weakest total binding energy, reflecting moderate stabilization consistent with its increased conformational flexibility observed in FEL and SSE analyses. Collectively, these results indicate that CPD0001 effectively stabilizes the Y220C mutation site while maintaining a favorable energetic profile comparable to the co-crystal ligand, supporting its potential as a promising small-molecule stabilizer. Per-residue energy decomposition

To further elucidate the molecular determinants of ligand stabilization, per-residue MM/PBSA energy decomposition (Fig. 13 A–D) was performed for CPD0001, CPD0136, CPD0330, and the co-crystal ligand, identifying key residues that act as energetic “hotspots” within the Y220C cavity. Across all complexes, residues lining the binding pocket—particularly LEU50, TRP51, VAL52, SER54, THR55, PRO56–PRO58, GLY59, ARG61, VAL62, ARG107, PRO124, CYS125, GLU126, PRO127–PRO128, CYS134, THR135, and LEU162—contributed most significantly through van der Waals and electrostatic interactions. Hydrophobic residues such as TRP51 and VAL52 provided strong nonpolar contacts, while polar and charged residues including SER54, THR55, CYS125, GLU126, and ARG107 supplied complementary electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding interactions. The consecutive proline clusters (PRO56–PRO58, PRO127–PRO128) contributed rigidity and maintained the geometry of the binding cavity, facilitating optimal ligand accommodation.

CPD0001 and the co-crystal ligand engaged these residues most effectively, consistent with their favorable total MM/PBSA binding energies, while CPD0136 showed strong stabilizing interactions with minimal polar solvation penalty, highlighting its efficient accommodation within the Y220C pocket. In contrast, CPD0330 exhibited weaker contributions from these residues, correlating with its lower total binding energy and moderate stabilization of the Y220C cavity. Collectively, these residue-level insights corroborate the FEL, SSE, and MM/PBSA analyses, highlighting CPD0001 and CPD0136 as promising small-molecule stabilizers capable of restoring structural integrity to the mutant TP53 binding site.

Discussion

Breast cancer is driven by multiple genetic aberrations, among which mutations in the TP53 gene are particularly prevalent and correlate with aggressive disease and poor prognosis. The TP53 mutation Y220C creates a surface pocket that destabilizes the DNA-binding domain, making the protein structurally unstable and functionally compromised. This mutation has been recognized as a druggable hotspot, and small-molecule stabilizers that bind within this pocket have shown potential for rescuing mutant p53 function by restoring thermal stability and enabling transcriptional activity — a strategy that may suppress tumor growth in cancers harboring this variant 29, 30. Recent mechanistic studies further demonstrate that selective small molecules engaging the Y220C cavity can not only stabilize local structure but also rescue allosteric communication pathways critical for DNA binding 31. In addition to direct stabilizers, emerging approaches such as acetylation targeting chimeras (AceTACs) have been developed to modulate post-translational modifications of p53 Y220C, leading to enhanced anticancer activity in preclinical models 32. Therefore, targeting mutant p53—particularly common and clinically relevant variants like Y220C—represents a compelling therapeutic avenue in breast cancer research.

The elevated expression of TP53 Y220C in breast tumors underscores its potential role in tumor progression. TP53 mutations are common in breast cancer and are frequently associated with aggressive behavior and poor prognosis 33, 34. Missense mutations such as Y220C not only abolish normal tumor suppressor activity but may also exert gain-of-function effects that promote proliferation and survival. Importantly, the Y220C mutation creates a structural cavity within the DNA-binding domain, rendering the mutant protein thermodynamically unstable yet pharmacologically targetable 35. This mutation-induced pocket provides a unique opportunity for structure-based drug design aimed at restoring p53 stability and function. Therefore, the biological relevance of TP53 Y220C in breast cancer is supported both by its oncogenic impact and its well-characterized druggable structural features.

The identification of well-defined hydrogen bond donor and hydrophobic features within the Y220C binding pocket reinforces the structural tractability of this mutant as a therapeutic target. The Y220C substitution generates a mutation-induced cavity that reshapes the local microenvironment of the DNA-binding domain, creating a pocket enriched with hydrophobic residues while retaining strategically positioned polar contacts. Such a combination of hydrophobic clustering and directional hydrogen bonding is consistent with previously reported structural analyses, which emphasize that effective stabilization of p53 Y220C requires complementary occupation of the cavity together with specific polar anchoring to enhance thermodynamic stability 35. Therefore, the pharmacophore features identified in this study are structurally and mechanistically aligned with established principles of mutant p53 reactivation.

From a drug discovery perspective, pharmacophore-guided virtual screening enables selective enrichment of compounds that match the essential spatial and chemical characteristics of the Y220C pocket, thereby increasing the probability of identifying stabilizing ligands. Because mutant p53 reactivation relies on precise cavity occupation rather than conventional enzyme inhibition, capturing the three-dimensional arrangement of hydrophobic and hydrogen-bonding features is critical for functional rescue 36. The use of diverse chemical libraries further enhances scaffold diversity, which is advantageous given the dynamic and partially solvent-exposed nature of the Y220C crevice. Collectively, this strategy integrates structural biology with rational ligand selection, supporting the identification of small molecules with the potential to restore mutant p53 stability and function in breast cancer.

The systematic validation of the TP53 Y220C binding pocket strengthens the structural rationale for subsequent screening and docking analyses. The Y220C mutation generates a mutation-induced cavity that reshapes the local architecture of the DNA-binding domain, exposing hydrophobic residues while preserving strategically positioned polar amino acids. Previous structural studies have demonstrated that successful stabilization of this mutant depends on accurate targeting of this crevice and engagement of residues surrounding CYS220, which forms the core of the mutation site 8. Therefore, confirming the functional relevance of these residues prior to large-scale docking enhances the reliability of structure-based ligand discovery and reduces the likelihood of false-positive binding predictions.

The enrichment of drug-like candidates following ADME and medicinal chemistry filtering further strengthens the translational potential of the identified hits. In mutant p53 reactivation strategies, physicochemical balance is critical because effective compounds must not only bind within a relatively hydrophobic pocket but also maintain favorable pharmacokinetic properties for systemic administration. Previous efforts to stabilize Y220C have highlighted the challenge of optimizing both cavity binding and drug-likeness simultaneously 8, 37. Thus, integrating drug-likeness filters early in the workflow increases the probability that identified stabilizers are pharmacologically viable rather than merely structurally compatible.

From a mechanistic standpoint, the consistent engagement of conserved pocket residues—particularly those proximal to CYS220—supports the concept that ligand-induced stabilization of the Y220C cavity can restore local structural integrity. Small molecules that occupy this crevice have been shown to increase the thermal stability of the mutant protein, thereby partially rescuing its transcriptional activity and downstream tumor suppressor functions 35, 38. The identification of compounds exhibiting interaction patterns comparable to or stronger than reference stabilizers suggests potential for enhanced cavity occupation and improved stabilization dynamics. Collectively, these findings align with the established paradigm that Y220C is a structurally druggable hotspot and validate the applied computational strategy as a rational approach for discovering mutant p53 stabilizers. By combining binding site validation, pharmacokinetic prioritization, and interaction-focused docking analysis, the study provides a coherent framework for advancing selective modulators of TP53 Y220C toward experimental and therapeutic evaluation in breast cancer.

The combined evaluation of toxicity and dynamic stability provides a scientifically robust approach for lead prioritization. Higher predicted LD₅₀ values indicate a safer pharmacological profile, which is critical for compounds intended to stabilize mutant p53 over extended periods without off-target effects 34. Concurrently, molecular dynamics analyses demonstrate that effective stabilizers reduce both global and residue-level fluctuations, maintaining the compactness and solvent accessibility of the Y220C DNA-binding domain. This is mechanistically important, as the Y220C mutation destabilizes the pocket, and successful ligands must restore local structural integrity without perturbing the overall fold 35. CPD0136 exemplifies these characteristics, forming extensive interactions within the mutation-induced cavity while preserving protein architecture, aligning with established strategies for pharmacological reactivation of mutant p53 36. Collectively, these findings support a rationally selected lead with both structural and pharmacological potential for therapeutic development in breast cancer.

The combined hydrogen-bond, distance-based, and essential dynamics analyses provide a mechanistic rationale for the superior stability of CPD0136 within the TP53 Y220C binding pocket. Stable hydrogen bonding and high occupancy indicate persistent polar interactions that anchor the ligand, while consistent minimum and average distances and an elevated number of contacts reflect extensive van der Waals and electrostatic engagement. These features collectively contribute to a tightly bound ligand–protein complex, minimizing unproductive fluctuations.

Principal component and dynamic cross-correlation analyses further support this conclusion, showing that CPD0136 reduces large-scale collective motions and promotes coordinated residue-level dynamics relative to both the co-crystal ligand and other candidates. The restrained motions observed in PCA and porcupine plots suggest enhanced conformational stabilization of the mutation-induced cavity, which is critical for restoring the structural integrity of Y220C and potentially rescuing p53 function 35, 36. In contrast, ligands with higher variance or less coherent correlations may partially occupy the pocket but fail to achieve comparable stabilization. Therefore, integrating hydrogen-bond persistence, contact density, and dynamic flexibility provides a comprehensive framework to rationalize CPD0136 as a lead candidate, demonstrating both strong binding and structural reinforcement of TP53 Y220C.

The computational analyses collectively support a mechanistic rationale for targeting TP53 Y220C with small-molecule stabilizers. Effective ligands must balance local stabilization of the mutation-induced cavity with preservation of the global fold, maintaining the dynamic flexibility necessary for p53's DNA-binding function 39. Energetically, optimal stabilization arises from complementary van der Waals and electrostatic interactions with key hotspot residues, while avoiding excessive solvation penalties that could compromise conformational adaptability 40. Structurally, maintaining the β-sandwich core and loop integrity ensures that ligand binding does not disrupt essential secondary structure elements, a requirement for functional reactivation. These principles explain why ligands that achieve strong, persistent contacts within the pocket while supporting coordinated residue-level dynamics—like CPD0001 and CPD0136—are particularly promising, as they can reinforce the structural integrity of TP53 Y220C without imposing undue rigidity. Overall, integrating energetic, structural, and dynamic considerations provides a rational framework for selecting small molecules capable of reactivating mutant p53 in breast cancer 41.

This study benefits from a robust and integrative computational workflow, combining pharmacophore modeling, virtual screening, ADME/toxicity profiling, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, free energy landscape analysis, and MM/PBSA calculations. Such a comprehensive approach enables systematic identification of small-molecule stabilizers for TP53 Y220C while providing mechanistic insights into binding energetics, structural stability, and key residue interactions. The use of multiple dynamic and stability metrics—including RMSD, RMSF, SASA, hydrogen bonding, PCA, and FEL—adds rigor to the evaluation, and the prioritized compounds, particularly CPD0001 and CPD0136, exhibit favorable drug-like properties, predicted safety, and strong binding potential.

In addition to pharmacological efficacy and binding stability, biocompatibility and safety are critical parameters for translational drug development. Although experimental toxicity evaluation was not performed in this study, in silico ADMET and toxicity predictions provide preliminary insights into the biosafety of the identified compounds. Recent studies have indicated the importance of integrating eco-toxicity and biocompatibility assessments in early-stage drug and nanomaterial design to ensure safe biomedical translation and environmental compatibility 42, 43, 44, 45.

In this context, the predicted low toxicity and favorable drug-like properties of CPD0001 and CPD0136 suggest a potentially acceptable safety profile for further development. However, these findings remain computational predictions and require experimental validation to confirm biological activity, pharmacokinetic behavior, and off-target effects through in vitro and in vivo studies. The study is further limited by its entirely in-silico nature, including the inherent approximations of ADMET and toxicity models, which may not fully capture complex biological and systemic responses and may differ from actual in vivo pharmacokinetic behavior. A 200 ns molecular dynamics simulation may not fully capture slower or rare conformational transitions, which could require longer timescales for more comprehensive sampling of protein–ligand dynamics. In addition, only a subset of candidate ligands was subjected to extended molecular dynamics and free energy analyses, which may have excluded other potential stabilizers. Furthermore, the focus on the TP53 Y220C mutant restricts broader applicability to other p53 variants and cancer types, and potential off-target interactions with wild-type p53 or other TP53 mutants were not evaluated, highlighting the need for future comparative and experimental studies. For in vitro validation, TP53 Y220C–engineered breast cancer cell models (e.g., MCF-7 or MDA-MB-231 expressing mutant p53 Y220C), along with wild-type TP53 controls, are recommended to assess mutant-specific stabilization and selectivity of the identified compound.

Conclusion

This study presents a comprehensive structure-based computational approach for identifying small-molecule stabilizers targeting the TP53 Y220C mutant in breast cancer. Through integrated pharmacophore modeling, virtual screening, ADMET evaluation, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, MM/PBSA, and free energy landscape analyses, key candidates were systematically prioritized. Among them, CPD0001 and CPD0136 demonstrated the most favorable binding affinity, stable interactions with hotspot residues, and consistent structural stability, indicating their potential as effective Y220C stabilizers.

Despite these promising findings, the study is limited by inherent constraints of computational modeling, including force-field approximations, limited conformational sampling, and the absence of biological system complexity. Therefore, experimental validation is required to confirm the predicted binding stability and functional effects. Future studies should include in vitro and in vivo investigations to evaluate pharmacological efficacy, pharmacokinetics, and toxicity profiles, followed by structure–activity relationship (SAR)-guided optimization of lead compounds to improve potency, selectivity, and safety. Comprehensive biosafety assessments will also be essential for clinical translation. Overall, CPD0001 and CPD0136 emerge as promising lead candidates for further development as TP53 Y220C-targeted therapeutics in breast cancer.
