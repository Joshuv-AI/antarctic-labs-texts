Association of procalcitonin levels with multidrug-resistant bloodstream infections: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Durga Shankar Meena, Gopal Krishana Bohra, Manika Malik, Naman Lodha, Deepak Kumar, Naresh Midha, Vibhor Tak, Amit Kumar Rohila, Mithu Banerjee, Mahendra Kumar Garg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProcalcitonin (PCT) is widely used for early detection of bloodstream infections. However, limited data correlate PCT levels with infection aetiology, especially in identifying multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens.MethodsThis prospective study included patients with bloodstream infections (BSIs) from January 2022 to April 2024. Patients with positive blood cultures and simultaneous PCT testing were enrolled. The primary objective was to evaluate the association between PCT levels and MDR bacteremia. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of PCT.ResultsA total of 317 patients with positive blood cultures met the inclusion criteria. Of these, 186 (58.7%) had MDR bacteremia, while 131 (41.3%) were due to non-MDR pathogens. Gram-negative (GN) bacteremia accounted for 238 cases, and Gram-positive (GP) bacteremia for 79 cases. Median PCT values were significantly higher in MDR bacteremia (9.6, IQR: 3.4-17.1 ng/mL) compared to non-MDR (1.5, IQR: 0.5-4 ng/mL, p < 0.001). ROC analysis showed an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.71 for PCT and 0.47 for CRP in predicting MDR bacteremia. For GN bacteremia prediction, the AUC was 0.61 for PCT and 0.39 for CRP. PCT values were highest in urinary tract infections, followed by pneumonia and soft tissue infections (11.75, 5.65, and 5.1 ng/mL, respectively).ConclusionsPCT demonstrated moderate ability to discriminate MDR bacteremia. Background

Bloodstream infections (BSIs) are a significant public health issue and a leading cause of sepsis-related mortality [1]. A recent meta-analysis estimated that 49 million cases of sepsis occur globally each year, with a mortality rate of 26.7% [2]. India bears a substantial portion of this burden, contributing around 11 million cases and 3 million deaths annually [2]. In Indian ICUs, 45% of deaths are attributed to infections caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) microorganisms [3]. Early diagnosis and pathogen identification are crucial for improving patient outcomes in BSIs. However, while blood cultures remain the gold standard for diagnosing BSIs, they are limited by low sensitivity and lengthy turnaround times (often exceeding 24 h) [4].

Procalcitonin (PCT), a biomarker for sepsis, has shown promise in guiding antibiotic therapy in patients with BSIs [5, 6]. Compared to CRP, PCT levels rise more rapidly following bacterial infection, making it a useful biomarker in severe bacterial infections [7]. Additionally, studies suggest that PCT may differentiate between Gram-negative (GN) and Gram-positive (GP) bacteremia [8, 9]. Recent literature has explored the relationship between PCT and MDR BSIs, though findings have been inconsistent [10–12], due to small sample sizes and heterogeneous data. Given these uncertainties, this study aims to evaluate the association between PCT levels and MDR BSIs. We assess the diagnostic performance of PCT, with particular emphasis on infection focus and bacteremia aetiology.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient population

This prospective observational study was conducted in the Department of Internal Medicine at a tertiary referral centre in Western India from January 2022 to April 2024. The inclusion criteria were: (a) age ≥ 18 years, (b) bloodstream infections (BSIs) defined by the isolation of a pathogenic organism from one or more blood cultures in the setting of clinically suspected sepsis [13], and (c) simultaneous collection of blood samples for PCT and microbiological analysis. Exclusion criteria included: (a) autoimmune conditions associated with elevated PCT levels (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, vasculitis) and non-infective systemic inflammatory response syndromes such as acute pancreatitis, burns, or trauma, (b) polymicrobial BSIs, (c) blood culture contamination, and (d) end-stage renal disease (ESRD). For cases with persistent bacteremia, only the first episode was considered. Coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) and other common skin contaminants were included only if isolated from at least two blood cultures consistent with sepsis. MDR infections were defined as non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories [14]. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee and conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (1964). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Variables analysed

All eligible patients diagnosed with BSIs were enrolled. The following data were prospectively collected: demographic information, clinical and laboratory findings, comorbidities, infection focus, and microbiological data (aetiology and antimicrobial susceptibility). Among laboratory parameters, baseline PCT and CRP levels, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) Score, and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) were collected. Complications, including septic shock, intensive care unit (ICU) stay, and 30-day mortality, were also recorded. The SOFA and qSOFA scores were calculated at the time of initial clinical assessment, corresponding to the time of blood culture sampling and baseline PCT measurement. Serum PCT levels were measured at the time of initial clinical evaluation, concurrently with blood culture collection during the same clinical assessment episode. Baseline PCT samples were obtained prior to initiation or escalation of antibiotic therapy whenever feasible. Only baseline PCT values obtained at presentation were analysed. All PCT measurements were performed using the same electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) platform (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions to ensure assay standardization.

Procalcitonin assays and blood culture processing

Serum procalcitonin levels were measured using an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The assay’s lower detection limit was 0.05 ng/mL. Blood samples (8-10 mL) were collected aseptically and inoculated into BacT/ALERT bottles (bioMérieux, France) and incubated in an automated system for at least five days. Bottles that flagged positive were immediately removed, and the time to detection (TTD) was recorded. Positive samples were subjected to Gram staining and cultured on blood and MacConkey agar plates. After 16-18 h of incubation at 37 °C, colony growth was assessed. Microorganisms were identified using the VITEK-2 system. Antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method, following Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive data were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables, depending on data distribution. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. For non-parametric data comparison, we used the Mann-Whitney U test. Comparisons among three or more datasets were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. PCT thresholds were evaluated based on commonly used clinical thresholds reported in previous sepsis and bloodstream infection studies, along with ROC curve analysis and Youden index assessment. Variables for multivariable logistic regression were selected based on clinical relevance and prior literature related to MDR bloodstream infections and elevated PCT levels. These included age, SOFA score, Charlson Comorbidity Index, hospital-acquired infection, gram-negative bacteremia, urinary tract infection source, serum creatinine, and PCT levels. To reduce overfitting, the number of variables included in the final model was limited according to the total number of MDR events. A predefined statistical analysis plan was followed before performing the final analysis. Missing data were minimal and were handled using complete-case analysis without imputation. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was carried out using SPSS software.

Results

Patient characteristics

This study included a total of 317 patients with blood culture positivity who met the eligibility criteria. The demographic and clinical characteristics, and outcomes of all patients are summarised in Table 1. 62.8% of patients were males; the community-acquired infections accounted for 63.7% of cases. Out of 317 patients, 186 (58.7%) had MDR bacteremia. GN bacteremia accounted for the majority of cases (238 patients; 75.1%) in this study. The urinary tract (35%), abdomen (18.9%), and lower respiratory tract (13.6%) were among the common sources for BSIs. Escherichia coli (37.2%), Staphylococcus aureus (17.4%), and Klebsiella pneumoniae (9.8%) were the frequently isolated microorganisms. Patients with MDR isolates had higher median SOFA scores (3.5 vs. 3), qSOFA scores (1 vs. 0), and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) scores (3 vs. 2) compared to non-MDR BSIs (Table 1). The overall mortality rate was 15.5%. GP bacteremia episodes revealed a higher mortality rate (21.5%) compared to GN bacteremia (13.4%). However, we did not find this difference statistically significant (p = 0.10).

PCT in Multidrug-resistant bacteremia

In this study, we found a significantly high serum PCT levels in MDR bacteremia (median: 9.6 ng/mL, IQR: 3.4-17.1) compared to non-MDR bacteremia (median: 1.5 ng/mL, IQR: 0.5-4) (p < 0.001, Fig. 1A). In extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing bacteraemia, the PCT levels were also raised compared to the ceftriaxone-susceptible Enterobacteriaceae group (11.3 vs. 1.9 ng/mL, p = 0.001). Additionally, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) isolates had higher PCT levels compared to methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) (4.8 vs. 1.5 ng/mL, p = 0.032). CRP levels did not differ significantly between the MDR and non-MDR groups (165.5 vs. 150 mg/L, p = 0.348). The NLR levels also did not differ significantly between the two groups (p = 0.335). For predicting MDR bacteremia, the area under the ROC curve (AUC) for PCT was 0.71 (95% CI: 0.65-0.75; p = 0.001; Fig. 2A). However, the AUC for CRP was only 0.47 (95% CI: 0.41-0.51, p = 0.298; Fig. 3A).

Table 2 summarised the sensitivity, specificity, negative predictive value (NPV), and positive predictive value (PPV) of CRP and PCT in predicting MDR bacteremia. CRP > 50 mg/L showed 87% sensitivity, 20% specificity, 60% NPV, and 61.8% PPV for MDR bacteremia. In comparison, PCT levels > 2.5 ng/mL revealed a sensitivity of 78%, specificity of 60%, NPV of 64%, and PPV of 73.6%. The overall diagnostic accuracy of PCT > 2.5 was 71% for MDR bacteremia (Table 2). In multivariate logistic regression analysis, PCT > 2.5 ng/mL was independently associated with MDR bacteremia (OR 3.8, 95% CI: 1.6-8.7, p = 0.002, Table 3). The other variables included in the model are shown in Table 2). In a subgroup analysis limited to gram-negative bacteremia, median PCT levels remained significantly higher in MDR isolates compared with non-MDR isolates (9.43 ng/mL [IQR: 2.75-18.93] vs. 1.50 ng/mL [IQR: 0.70-7.25], p < 0.001).

PCT in gram-negative bacteremia

PCT levels were significantly increased in GN bacteremia (median: 6.9 ng/mL, IQR: 1.5-16.9) compared to GP isolates (median: 2 ng/mL, IQR: 0.3-6.1) (p = 0.022, Fig. 1B). Notably, we found higher CRP levels in GP bacteremia than in GN cases (178 mg/L vs. 153 mg/L), although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.158). Similarly, no significant difference was found in other sepsis markers (NLR, SOFA, and qSOFA scores) between GN and GP BSIs (P > 0.05 for all). The AUC for PCT in predicting GN bacteremia was 0.61 (95% CI: 0.56-0.66) (Fig. 2B). The AUC for CRP was 0.39 (95% CI: 0.35-0.45) (Fig. 3B). This indicates a poor predictive performance of PCT and CRP for GN bacteremia. For GN Bacteremia, PCT levels > 2.5 ng/mL had a sensitivity of 65%, a specificity of 44%, a NPV of 30%, and a PPV of 78%, with an overall diagnostic accuracy of 60%. Conversely, CRP levels > 50 mg/L demonstrated 84% sensitivity, 12% specificity, an NPV of 33%, and a PPV of 73%.

Procalcitonin in different foci of infection and microorganisms

PCT levels showed significant variation across different infection foci (p = 0.015, Table 4). The highest PCT levels were observed in UTIs, followed by lower respiratory tract infections and soft tissue infections. We found the lowest PCT concentrations (median: 1.3 ng/mL) in bacteremia with an abdominal focus. Among GN Bacteremia, the PCT levels also differed significantly (p = 0.005) in different microorganisms. Escherichia coli bacteremia showed the highest median serum PCT levels (11 ng/mL), followed by Acinetobacter baumannii (10.9 ng/mL) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (9.6 ng/mL). The PCT levels were lowest in Salmonella typhi among all GN isolates (1.02 ng/mL). Notably, the PCT concentrations did not differ significantly among GP isolates (p = 0.384). Discussion

Procalcitonin is a peptide precursor of calcitonin consisting of 116 amino acids. PCT is typically present in low concentrations in healthy individuals (< 0.1 ng/ml) [15]. Since PCT was first identified as a potential biomarker for bacterial sepsis in 1993, it has received substantial interest through multiple studies as a tool in identifying bacterial infections and guiding antibiotic therapy in severe bloodstream infections [16–18]. Previous literature suggests that PCT-guided antibiotic strategies may reduce antibiotic exposure and treatment duration in certain clinical settings. However, their impact on clinical outcomes, particularly mortality, remains inconsistent across studies. [19–20]. Similarly, controversy remains regarding its diagnostic utility in bacteremia, its ability to predict the causative pathogen, and its role in identifying the focus of bloodstream infections. To date, there is insufficient evidence to support PCT as a reliable tool for antibiotic initiation in BSIs. In this study, the diagnostic performance of PCT in predicting MDR bacteremia was moderate. The sensitivity and specificity values do not support its use as a standalone diagnostic tool. Therefore, PCT should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical parameters rather than in isolation. Its potential utility lies in early risk stratification. Particularly in identifying patients at higher risk of MDR infection, when microbiological data are pending.

The role of PCT in predicting MDR BSIs has been studied in earlier literature [10–12]. In 2016, Watanabe et al. reported elevated PCT levels in ESBL infections compared to non-ESBL cases [10]. Although PCT levels did not differ significantly between MRSA and MSSA isolates in this study. The sample size in this study was relatively small (n = 41) [10]. A subsequent study from China in 2016 found no significant differences in PCT concentrations between MDR and non-MDR isolates. However, the number of MDR strains analysed was also relatively small (n = 21) [11]. Conversely, Lin et al. reported a significant difference in PCT levels among MDR and non-MDR isolates in burn patients with sepsis (2.76 vs. 1.01 ng/ml, with 51 patients harbouring MDR isolates) [12]. In this study, Acinetobacter baumannii, Klebsiella, and Pseudomonas were common MDR pathogens [12]. These findings are consistent with the current study. Although the majority of MDR isolates in the current study were from the Enterobacteriaceae family. We also observed significantly higher PCT levels in MRSA isolates compared to MSSA. Previous studies reported an AUC of 0.81 for predicting MDR isolates [12], which is notably higher than the AUC of 0.71 found in the current study. The predominance of gram-negative microorganisms and the inclusion of polymicrobial BSIs may explain this discrepancy [12]. The PCT cut-off in the prior study was set at 1.42, with a sensitivity of 91% and specificity of 89% [12]. In our study, a PCT threshold of > 2.5 showed a sensitivity of 78% and specificity of 60%, which was inferior compared to an earlier study [12].

Bacterial virulence is an important determinant of PCT stimulation. Studies have revealed increased inflammation and elevated cytokine levels (IL-1, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-alpha) in MDR isolates [21, 22]. These cytokines are potent stimulators of PCT, especially in cases of GN bacteremia. However, further research is required to consolidate this hypothesis. In severe BSIs, mortality rates are high. An early risk stratification is required in such cases for effective management. Additional complexity arises due to the time taken for blood culture positivity. Moreover, blood culture has poor sensitivity in many cases. This necessitates the evaluation of biomarker-based risk stratification. However, the result of this study highlights the limitations of using PCT as a surrogate marker for resistance. PCT can be used as an adjunctive biomarker rather than a predictor of MDR pathogens. Although elevated PCT levels were associated with MDR bacteremia in the current study, we did not find its sufficient diagnostic utility for standalone clinical use. PCT is a marker of host inflammatory response rather than antimicrobial resistance. The observed association is likely indirect in the current study. It may reflect differences in infection type, bacterial burden, or host response, rather than resistance mechanisms themselves. Combining PCT with clinical and other laboratory parameters may improve the prediction in practice. Future studies should focus on developing and validating such models.

The PCT levels in GN bacteremia were significantly elevated in the current study. This result was consistent with previous reports [8–12, 23]. In a large study by Leli et al., PCT levels were increased in GN bacteremia, compared to GP bacteremia [23]. However, PCT had a limited ability to discriminate specific pathogens or resistance profiles [23]. The differential activation of toll-like receptors (TLRs) by various microorganisms (TLR4 by lipopolysaccharides in GN bacteria and TLR2 by teichoic acids in Gram-positive bacteria) leads to varied cytokine release. This varied cytokine release contributes to the differential PCT stimulation [24, 25]. GN bacteremia induces a higher release of interleukins (IL-6 and IL-8) compared to GP bacteremia, which could explain the variation in PCT levels [26, 27]. In contrast, CRP had poor specificity in the current study. It limits the utility of CRP in predicting MDR and GN bacteremia. Similar to earlier studies, PCT levels were highest among UTI patients in this cohort [8–11]. The predominance of GN microorganisms (particularly Enterobacteriaceae) with MDR isolates may account for the elevated PCT in UTI cases. Escherichia coli and Acinetobacter species showed the highest PCT levels compared to other isolates. Although median PCT values were lower in earlier studies, this is likely due to the smaller proportion of MDR isolates [12, 28]. Another key finding was the consistent trend of lower PCT levels in salmonella bacteremia compared to Enterobacteriaceae in the current study and in previous reports [29]. This may be explained by the smaller amount of IL-6 and TNF-alfa release in enteric fever compared to E. coli bacteremia [30].

This study has some important limitations. It was conducted at a single centre with a relatively small sample size. This may limit its generalisability. Only baseline PCT levels were evaluated. Serial measurements (PCT kinetics) could have provided more insight into the diagnostic role of PCT. Composite predictive models (integration of PCT with other clinical and laboratory parameters) may be more useful in predicting MDR bacteremia. Although we adjusted for the severity of illness using the SOFA score, other important determinants of PCT levels were not evaluated. Among these factors, bacterial burden (inoculum), timing of source control, and PCT measurement relative to the course of infection are common. These factors may vary between MDR and non-MDR infections and could contribute to residual confounding. Therefore, the observed association between PCT and MDR bacteremia should be interpreted with caution. Large, multicentre studies are required to validate these findings and assess generalisability across different patient populations.

Conclusions

PCT demonstrated moderate discriminatory ability for MDR bacteremia. However, given the observational nature of study and the modest diagnostic performance, PCT should not be considered a standalone predictor of MDR bloodstream infections. PCT levels should be interpreted alongside clinical and microbiological findings. Further research is necessary to explore the utility of PCT in combination with other biomarkers.
