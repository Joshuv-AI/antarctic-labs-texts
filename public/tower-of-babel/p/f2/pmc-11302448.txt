A case report of reversible left ventricular dysfunction due to azathioprine hypersensitivity syndrome
Authors: Daniel Lüscher, Micha T Maeder, Eva Scheler, Vincenzo Nuzzi, Debbie Falconer, Giulia Elena Mandoli, Elton Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypersensitivity reaction is a rare side effect during immunosuppressive treatment with azathioprine (AZA). Some cases of cardiac involvement have already been reported but causality is notoriously difficult to prove.Case summaryWe present the case of a 68-year-old man with two episodes of reversible left ventricular (LV) dysfunction. One month after treatment initiation with AZA, he developed non-specific symptoms, including mild chest pain. In the context of elevated cardiac biomarkers and markers of inflammation, echocardiography showed depressed systolic LV function. Biventricular dysfunction was shown on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR), but neither myocardial oedema nor late gadolinium enhancement was documented. There was full recovery of LV function after AZA discontinuation. Very similar clinical course and echocardiography findings were observed early after restarting AZA treatment. After definitive cessation of AZA, systolic LV function recovered again and remained stable throughout long-term follow-up.DiscussionHypersensitivity reaction with cardiac involvement due to AZA is rare. The exact mechanisms underlying AZA-related cardiac dysfunction are still not completely understood, and causality is often difficult to prove. However, because of re-exposure to the drug, which, considered retrospectively, was inappropriate, the effect was clearly apparent in our patient. Knowledge of this potentially life-threatening side effect of AZA treatment is important. AZA must be discontinued promptly if there is any evidence of hypersensitivity reaction.Keywords: Case report, Azathioprine, Hypersensitivity reaction, Left ventricular dysfunction

Introduction

Azathioprine (AZA) is a frequently used immunosuppressive agent. However, the drug has a variety of potentially dangerous side effects including a hypersensitivity reaction (HSR) in individuals without prior exposure to the drug.1 Cardiac involvement is rarely part of an HSR, but some cases have already been published.2–6 We report a case of recurrent severe left ventricular dysfunction associated with the use of AZA. Reversibility after discontinuation and re-occurrence after re-exposure strongly suggest causality.

Case presentation

A 68-year-old man was transferred to our institution because of ST-segment elevations in the precordial ECG leads. At the referral clinic, the patient presented with severe immobilizing lumbar pain radiating to the legs for three days (started after shoveling snow). Mild chest pain was only reported after specific questioning. The ECG revealed persistent ST-segment elevation in ECG leads V2-5 (Figure 1). Vital signs were normal except for mild hypotension (blood pressure, 90/63 mmHg; heart rate, 92 b.p.m; oxygen saturation, 94% at room air; temperature, 36.6°C). There were no clinical signs of heart failure. Laboratory assessment revealed evidence of inflammation (C-reactive protein (CRP) 232 mg/L [normal range <8], procalcitonin 0.82 µg/L [<0.5], leukocytes (Lc) 17.1G/L [4–10], 83% segmented neutrophils [25–78%]) and elevated cardiac biomarkers: high-sensitive troponin I (hs-TnI) 609 ng/L [<18], B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) 634 ng/L. Aortic dissection was ruled out by computed tomography angiography. Echocardiography showed reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) with diffuse hypokinesia (LVEF, 35–40%).

ECG at initial presentation.

The non-smoking patient had no history of heart disease, family, and functional history was unremarkable. Diabetes mellitus was diagnosed as an incidental finding (HbA1c 6.9% [4.0–5.7]) and represented the only cardiovascular risk factor. Personal medical history was only significant for an ocular myasthenia gravis, which was in remission on immunosuppressive therapy consisting of prednisone (10 mg daily) and AZA (150 mg daily).

The initial working diagnosis was acute perimyocarditis. Although clarification regarding coronary artery disease (by CT coronary angiography or invasive angiography) would have been justified at that point, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) was primarily planned (considering diffuse LV dysfunction on echocardiography and atypical symptoms). Treatment with prednisone was continued and AZA was withheld. Analgesic therapy was established, and heart failure therapy was initiated cautiously (lisinopril, 5 mg daily; no betablocker in the context of myasthenia gravis). Blood and urine cultures remained sterile. A lumbar spine magnetic resonance imaging revealed no evidence of spondylodiscitis.

Cardiac and inflammatory markers decreased quickly (Figure 2), the ECG normalized, and no relevant arrhythmias were documented. CMR on Day 3 showed biventricular dysfunction, but there was no myocardial oedema and/or late gadolinium enhancement. Supportive therapy was continued, and the patient was discharged on Day 8 after admission.

Diagram showing the course of LVEF, CRP, and high-sensitive troponin I over time.

Two weeks later, the patient was doing well with only mild dyspnoea on exertion. Inflammatory markers and hs-TnI were within the normal range. One month later echocardiography showed complete recovery of LVEF (56%; Supplementary material online, Video S1), and lisinopril was continued. At that time, AZA (150 mg daily) was restarted. Shortly afterwards, the patient complained of diffuse arthralgia and a stabbing sensation in the chest. Only three days after AZA re-initiation, the patient presented again in the emergency department. Similarly to the initial presentation, the ECG showed ST-segment elevation, and laboratory analysis revealed increased cardiac and inflammatory markers (hs-TnI, 888 ng/L; CRP 244 mg/L, Lc 17.2 G/L). Left ventricular ejection (LVEF 38%) and global longitudinal strain were depressed again (Figure 3; Supplementary material online, Video S2). Coronary artery disease was excluded by CT coronary angiography. A second CMR demonstrated overall the same findings as the first one: decreased LVEF but no focal or diffuse myocardial oedema and no late gadolinium enhancement (Figure 4; Supplementary material online, Video S3). At that time, HSR to AZA was suspected, and AZA was stopped permanently. Prednisone pulse therapy (50 mg daily) was started. Cardiac and inflammatory markers declined rapidly. The patient was discharged on 10 mg prednisone daily. Clinically there was a very quick recovery, the LVEF normalized as well and remained normal during a follow-up of 2 years. Myasthenia gravis was treated with corticosteroids alone, which was sufficient to induce long-term remission.

Discussion

The present case is an instructive illustration of the clinical course of an AZA-related HSR with cardiac involvement. Such an adverse reaction is rare but represents a serious side effect, which often mimics infection, with symptoms including fever, arthralgia, abdominal pain, nausea and vomiting; or is noticeable due to cutaneous reactions. AZA-related HSR is dose-independent and often appears within the first month after drug initiation.1,7 Since AZA itself is a prodrug, it must be converted into active metabolites, into 6-mercaptopurine (6-MP) and then into 6-thioguanine nucleotides. The latter are responsible for dose-dependent cytotoxic side effects, such as myelosuppression or hepatotoxicity. Impaired inactivation of 6-MP (by thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT) and xanthine oxidase) leads to increased dose-dependent side effects, for example, in individuals with reduced TPMT activity (10% of the population). There are conflicting results in the literature as to whether TPMT level is also related to dose-independent side effects, such as HSR.7,8 However, TPMT activity was not determined in our patient.

A major strength of our pseudo-experimental case is the fact that there was re-exposure to the trigger factor. This was, in fact, an error that became evident only in the retrospective assessment when AZA was identified as the causative agent. The cardiac effect of AZA-related HSR can be even more pronounced after re-exposure. Reintroduction of AZA may lead to a stronger and more prompt reaction.2 Repeated cardiac imaging is another strength. Remarkably, no typical findings of inflammation were detected on CMR although imaging was performed in the early stages of the disease. In contrast to our findings, evidence of myocarditis was indicated on CMR in two other cases of AZA-related cardiotoxicity.3,4 A more low-grade inflammation process may explain the absence of pathological CMR findings in our patient. Endomyocardial biopsy is the gold standard in the diagnostic work-up of myocardial inflammation, but because of rapid and complete recovery during the first episode, we decided against it. Therefore, histological information is not available.

A few cases of heart failure and recovery after cessation of AZA treatment have been described in the literature. Most patients showed symptoms of hypotension or circulatory shock.2,5,6 In some cases endomyocardial biopsy was performed, and an eosinophilic infiltrate was found.3,6 In our patient, the eosinophilic count, at least in the peripheral blood count, was always normal. By contrast, mature neutrophil granulocytes were increased, which has been described in other patients with HSR.8

The knowledge of AZA-related HSR and possible cardiac involvement is especially important, because AZA can be used to prevent rejection after heart transplant or to treat certain forms of myocarditis itself.3 In such cases the declining cardiac function could be easily attributed to the underlying condition.

Due to the small number of cases of AZA-related HSR with significant cardiac involvement, a number of important aspects are currently unknown. These include the natural course without treatment or interruption of AZA therapy, risk factors of AZA-related cardiotoxicity, the underlying pathomechanisms of cardiac involvement, and potential preventive measures.

If patients being treated with AZA are developing symptoms of cardiac disease, an AZA-related hypersensitivity reaction should be suspected. Our case illustrates that the most important step is to withhold the causative drug at an early stage.
