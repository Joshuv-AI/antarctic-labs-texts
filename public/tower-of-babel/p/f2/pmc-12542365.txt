FTO enhances OSCC progression via m⁶A-dependent stabilization of PKM2 mRNA through YTHDF2 modulation
Authors: Jie Wu, Lina Liu, Bo Xu, Ru Wang, Wensheng Ma, Jiayin Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

N6-methyladenosine (m⁶A) modification represents a pivotal layer of epitranscriptomic regulation in cancer. Fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO), an m⁶A demethylase, has been implicated in several malignancies. While FTO’s involvement in OSCC is emerging, its regulation of glycolytic metabolism remains uncharacterized. Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is one of the most common subtypes of head and neck cancers, accounting for over 90% of oral malignancies worldwide. Despite advances in surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, the five-year survival rate for patients with advanced OSCC remains below 50%, primarily due to local recurrence, lymph node metastasis, and therapeutic resistance [1]. These clinical challenges underscore the urgent need to identify novel molecular targets that can improve prognostic precision and guide therapeutic intervention [2].

In the past decade, epitranscriptomic regulation, particularly N6-methyladenosine (m⁶A) modification, has emerged as a crucial determinant of RNA fate in cancer biology [3–5]. m⁶A influences RNA splicing, export, stability, and translation [6], mediated by the coordinated actions of methyltransferases (“writers”), demethylases (“erasers”), and m⁶A-binding proteins (“readers”) [7–9]. Among the erasers, fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) was originally identified as an m⁶A demethylase regulating metabolism and obesity [10, 11], but has since been implicated in the progression of several malignancies. Aberrant FTO expression has been shown to promote tumor growth and therapy resistance by modulating the fate of m⁶A-marked oncogenic transcripts in leukemia, breast cancer, and liver cancer [12–14]. Although FTO’s functions in OSCC are partially described [15], its mechanistic links to mRNA stability and metabolic reprogramming require elucidation.

A key metabolic enzyme implicated in cancer progression is pyruvate kinase isoform M2 (PKM2), a rate-limiting glycolytic enzyme central to the Warburg effect, characterized by elevated glycolytic flux even under normoxia [16, 17]. PKM2 not only catalyzes the final step of glycolysis but also exerts non-metabolic functions, including regulation of transcription factors and modulation of cell signaling pathways. Its upregulation has been associated with enhanced proliferation, migration, angiogenesis, and immune evasion in multiple solid tumors, including OSCC [18]. In addition, PKM2 has been implicated in resistance to therapy and tumor microenvironment remodeling [19], highlighting its multifaceted contribution to oral carcinogenesis. While transcriptional and post-translational regulation of PKM2 has been well documented, its post-transcriptional regulation via m⁶A-dependent pathways remains poorly defined. Given that the m⁶A reader YTHDF2 mediates selective decay of methylated transcripts [20], it is plausible that FTO may stabilize PKM2 mRNA by reducing YTHDF2-dependent degradation.

In this study, we investigated the role of FTO in OSCC with a focus on its regulation of PKM2 expression through m⁶A demethylation and YTHDF2 modulation. We hypothesized that FTO-mediated m⁶A erasure prevents YTHDF2 recognition of PKM2 mRNA, thereby enhancing its stability and protein abundance. To address this hypothesis, we systematically assessed FTO expression in clinical OSCC samples and cell lines, evaluated the phenotypic consequences of FTO overexpression and knockdown in vitro and in vivo, and examined the molecular associations between FTO, YTHDF2, and PKM2 transcripts using RNA immunoprecipitation and mRNA decay assays. Collectively, our findings define an FTO/m⁶A/YTHDF2/PKM2 axis that promotes OSCC malignancy by sustaining glycolytic reprogramming and tumor progression. These insights extend current knowledge of FTO’s oncogenic role in oral cancers and underscore its potential as a therapeutic vulnerability in metabolically reprogrammed tumors.

Materials and methods

The detailed information of reagents and instruments used in this study is listed in Table S1.

Patient cohort and tissue collection

This study enrolled 56 patients with histologically confirmed oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) who underwent surgical resection at the Stomatological Hospital of Tianjin Medical University between January 2012 and December 2021. Inclusion criteria comprised primary OSCC cases without prior radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or hormone therapy, and complete clinical records. Patients with concurrent malignancies or immune-related diseases were excluded. Tumor tissues and paired adjacent normal tissues (collected >5 cm from the tumor margin) were obtained during surgery. Tissue samples were pathologically graded and staged according to the morphological characteristics of cells under the microscope and the 8th edition of the UICC/AJCC TNM staging system [21]. After stratified sampling based on the grading results, a total of 40 tissue samples were selected, with 10 samples in each group representing grade I, grade II, grade III-IV and normal tissue. All samples were subjected to immunohistochemical analysis. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tianjin Medical University (IRB number: TMUhMEC20230409), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives. The characteristics of patients are listed in Tables S2.

Immunohistochemical (IHC)

Immunohistochemical staining was performed on 3.0-µm paraffin-embedded sections of OSCC and adjacent normal tissues. Following deparaffinization and graded rehydration, antigen retrieval was carried out in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide. Sections were then blocked with 10% normal serum containing 1% BSA at room temperature. Primary antibody against FTO (1:2500) was applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and DAB visualization. Hematoxylin was used for nuclear counterstaining. Stained sections were observed under a light microscope at 200× magnification, and FTO expression was quantified using Image-Pro Plus software by calculating the integrated optical density (IOD) from ten randomly selected fields per sample. All IHC scoring and field selections were conducted in a blinded manner by two independent investigators, and quantitative criteria were standardized to ensure reproducibility and minimize observer bias.

Cell culture and maintenance

The human oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) cell lines SCC15, SCC25, CAL27, and the head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) cell line FaDu, as well as normal human oral keratinocytes (HOK), were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. When cells reached approximately 80% confluence, they were digested with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA and subcultured at a 1:4 ratio. For cryopreservation, cells were resuspended in freezing medium composed of 90% FBS and 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and stored in liquid nitrogen after controlled-rate cooling.

Plasmid construction and shRNA-mediated knockdown of FTO and YTHDF2

shRNA sequences targeting human FTO and YTHDF2 were designed using the RNAi Designer tool and inserted into the pLKO.1-TRC vector (See Fig. S1, Additional File 2). Recombinant constructs were verified by restriction digestion and sequencing. Lentiviral particles were generated by co-transfecting HEK-293T cells with shRNA plasmids and packaging plasmids. Viral supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 h, filtered, and used to infect SCC25 and CAL27 cells with polybrene. Stable cell lines were established by puromycin selection, and knockdown efficiency was confirmed by RT-qPCR and Western blotting. For transient overexpression, FTO expression plasmid was transfected into SCC25 and CAL27 cells using Lipofectamine 3000, and cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for subsequent analyses. The reaction systems involved in the cell line construction process are listed in Tables S6-S17.

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted and treated with ezDNase to remove genomic DNA, then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the SuperScript IV VILO Master Mix. Quantitative PCR was performed using the SYBR Green-based SuperReal PreMix Plus on an ABI 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System. Gene-specific primers for FTO, PKM2, YTHDF2, and GAPDH (internal control) were used. Thermal cycling conditions were: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and relative expression was calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the normalization control. The reaction systems and primer sequences are listed in Table S18-S21.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from cells or tumor tissues using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with 1 mM PMSF and 1 mM phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (20–30 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE (8–10% gels depending on the target protein) and transferred to NC membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1.5 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies against FTO, PKM2, YTHDF2, and GAPDH. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent, and band intensities were quantified with ImageJ software. The compositions of resolving gel are listed in Table S22-S23.

Cell proliferation assay (CCK-8)

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. SCC25, CAL27, and their FTO-knockdown derivatives were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1,000 cells per well in 100µL of complete medium. At 0, 24, 48, and 72 h,10µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured with a microplate reader, and proliferation curves were generated based on optical density values to assess relative growth rates.

Cell migration and invasion assays

Cell migration was assessed by wound healing assay, in which SCC25 and CAL27 cells were seeded into six-well plates, grown to near confluence, and scratched with 200µL pipette tips; wound closure was photographed at 0, 6, 12, and 24 h. Cell invasion was evaluated using Transwell chambers precoated with Matrigel (1:10 dilution) at 37 °C for 30 min. After 24 h of serum starvation, 2 × 10⁴ cells suspended in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber, and the lower chamber contained medium with 20% FBS. Following overnight incubation, non-invaded cells were removed, and invaded cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained sequentially with 1% and 0.1% crystal violet, and counted in five random microscopic fields.

Seahorse XF Glycolysis stress test

Cellular glycolytic activity was assessed using the Seahorse XFp Analyzer following the standard Mito Stress Test protocol. SCC25 and CAL27 cells were seeded into XFp miniplates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. Prior to measurement, cells were washed and equilibrated in XF assay medium at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator for 1 h. During the assay, oligomycin, FCCP, and rotenone/antimycin A were sequentially injected to evaluate extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) dynamics. ECAR values were recorded in real time and normalized to protein content per well. The configuration systems are listed in Table S24.

Dot blot assay for global m-A methylation

Total RNA was extracted, quantified, and diluted to final concentrations of 400, 200, and 100 ng. Samples were centrifuged, denatured at 95 °C for 3 min, and rapidly chilled on ice. Equal volumes (2 µL) of RNA were dotted onto NC membranes and baked at 37 °C for 30 min to immobilize RNA. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with anti-m⁶A antibody (1:250) overnight at 4 °C. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:10,000) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using ECL, and RNA loading was assessed by methylene blue staining of the same membrane.

RNA Immunoprecipitation followed by qPCR (RIP‒qPCR)

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) was performed using lysates from SCC25 and CAL27 cells. After lysis in buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors, clarified supernatants were incubated with magnetic beads conjugated to either anti-FTO antibody, anti-YTHDF2 antibody, or control IgG at 4 °C. Following extensive washing, RNA was extracted from the immunoprecipitates by phenol–chloroform and ethanol precipitation. Purified RNA was reverse transcribed, and enrichment of PKM2 mRNA was quantified by RT-qPCR. Results were normalized to input RNA and expressed as fold enrichment relative to IgG control. The buffer systems are listed in Table S25-S26.

mRNA stability assay using actinomycin D

To assess PKM2 mRNA stability, SCC25 and CAL27 cells were seeded in six-well plates and treated with 30 µL of 1 mg/mL actinomycin D to inhibit transcription. Cells were collected at 1, 3, and 6 h post-treatment, and total RNA was extracted and reverse-transcribed to cDNA. PKM2 mRNA levels at each time point were quantified by RT-qPCR and normalized to GAPDH. The relative decay rate of PKM2 mRNA was then calculated to evaluate transcript stability.

NF-κB pathway analysis by Western blot

Western blot was performed to evaluate the expression of NF-κB pathway–related proteins in SCC25 and CAL27 cells with or without FTO knockdown. The examined proteins included p-IKKα/β, NF-κB p65, p-NF-κB p65, IκBα, p-IκBα, with α-tubulin used as the loading control. Band intensities were quantified by ImageJ, and expression levels were normalized to α-tubulin.

In vivo xenograft tumor model

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. Male BALB/c nude mice (4–5 weeks old, SPF grade, 20 in total) were subcutaneously injected with 1 × 10⁶ SCC25 cells stably expressing shFTO or control shNC in 0.1mL PBS (10 mice per group). Tumor length and width were measured every 3 days for 27 days. At the endpoint, mice were sacrificed, and tumors were excised, photographed, and weighed for tumor burden analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 20.0. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way ANOVA or repeated measures ANOVA was used for group comparisons, and LSD-t test was applied for pairwise comparisons. Categorical data were expressed as percentages and compared using the chi-square test. A p-value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

In accordance with the prevailing conceptions of FTO’s function in OSCC [15], this study has substantiated heightened FTO expression and its consequent influence on proliferation and metastasis; detailed data overlapping with prior reports are therefore presented in the Supplementary Information. Specifically, immunohistochemistry and Western blotting confirmed elevated FTO in OSCC tissues and selected cell lines; higher FTO associated with adverse clinicopathologic features and shorter overall survival; FTO knockdown reduced CCK-8 growth, migration, invasion, and colony formation; and xenograft assays showed diminished tumor burden upon FTO silencing (See Table S3-S5, Additional File 1; Fig.S2-S4, Additional File 2).

RIP‒qPCR demonstrated significant enrichment of PKM2 transcripts in FTO immunoprecipitates relative to IgG, indicating an association between FTO and PKM2 mRNA (Fig. 1A). Consistently, silencing FTO decreased PKM2 protein abundance in both SCC25 and CAL27 (Fig. 1B). Given YTHDF2’s role in recognizing m⁶A-modified transcripts for decay, RIP–qPCR using YTHDF2 antibodies revealed enrichment of PKM2 mRNA in YTHDF2 complexes (Fig. 1C), and YTHDF2 knockdown increased PKM2 protein levels (Fig. 1D). Modulating FTO altered YTHDF2–PKM2 co-association: FTO knockdown enhanced, whereas FTO overexpression reduced, PKM2 mRNA enrichment in YTHDF2 immunoprecipitates (Fig. 1E–F). These data support a model in which FTO-mediated m⁶A demethylation attenuates YTHDF2 recognition of PKM2 transcripts, thereby sustaining PKM2 expression.

Actinomycin D chase assays further showed that depleting YTHDF2 prolonged the half-life of PKM2 mRNA, consistent with reduced transcript decay upon loss of the m⁶A reader (Fig. 2A). In parallel, dot-blot analysis indicated that FTO knockdown increased global m⁶A levels, in line with its demethylase activity (Fig. 2B). Functionally, Seahorse XF glycolysis stress tests demonstrated that FTO-deficient cells exhibited lower extracellular acidification rates (ECAR), reflecting diminished glycolytic flux (Fig. 2C). Finally, Western blotting revealed that FTO knockdown coincided with increased phosphorylation of IKKα/β, IκBα, and p65, indicative of NF-κB pathway activation (Fig. 2D–E). Collectively, these findings indicate that FTO sustains PKM2 expression via an m⁶A/YTHDF2-dependent mechanism and concordantly supports glycolytic metabolism with coincident activation of NF-κB signaling in OSCC.

Discussion

This study delineates the oncogenic role of the fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) and uncovers a novel epitranscriptomic regulatory axis involving FTO, YTHDF2, and PKM2 that drives tumor progression via m⁶A-dependent modulation of glycolytic metabolism. Specifically, our findings suggest that FTO enhances OSCC malignancy by reducing m⁶A methylation of PKM2 mRNA, thereby limiting its recognition by the m⁶A reader YTHDF2 and stabilizing PKM2 transcripts. Consistent with prior reports implicating FTO in multiple cancers including acute myeloid leukemia, breast cancer, and hepatocellular carcinoma [22–24], we observed that FTO expression is significantly upregulated in OSCC tissues and positively correlated with adverse clinicopathological features such as larger tumor size, advanced stage, lymph node metastasis, and local recurrence. These data reinforce the broader oncogenic role of FTO and highlight its prognostic potential in OSCC.

Through functional assays in vitro and in vivo, we demonstrated that FTO knockdown markedly suppressed OSCC cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and glycolysis, whereas FTO overexpression enhanced these phenotypes. These effects were tightly associated with altered PKM2 expression, a glycolytic enzyme central to the Warburg effect by favoring aerobic glycolysis over oxidative phosphorylation [25]. As PKM2 elevation is a common feature of malignancies and correlates with poor prognosis [26–28], our results provide new insight into metabolic reprogramming in OSCC through an FTO/PKM2 axis. Consistent with this specificity, analysis of the UALCAN database did not reveal significant correlations between FTO expression and other glycolytic enzymes such as HK2 or LDHA, further underscoring PKM2 as the predominant downstream effector of FTO in OSCC.

Mechanistically, RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays followed by qPCR showed significant enrichment of PKM2 mRNA in FTO complexes compared to IgG controls, supporting a regulatory association. Importantly, FTO knockdown increased m⁶A levels on PKM2 transcripts and enhanced their enrichment in YTHDF2 immunoprecipitates, consistent with accelerated YTHDF2-mediated decay. Conversely, FTO overexpression diminished this association. Moreover, YTHDF2 silencing extended PKM2 mRNA half-life and increased PKM2 protein levels, supporting its role as a key effector in this process. Taken together, these findings establish a demethylase–reader–effector cascade through which FTO stabilizes PKM2 transcripts to promote glycolysis.

The choice to focus on YTHDF2 was informed by its established function in facilitating mRNA degradation via m⁶A recognition [29, 30]. While other readers such as YTHDF1 or IGF2BP proteins also regulate mRNA fate [31, 32], YTHDF2 has been consistently shown to mediate mRNA decay in various malignancies [33–35], rendering it a biologically plausible effector in this context. Our data reinforce the mechanistic paradigm wherein FTO promotes oncogene expression by disrupting the m⁶A-dependent mRNA decay machinery.

Our work also refines the understanding of FTO’s relationship with NF-κB signaling. We observed that FTO knockdown increased phosphorylation of IKKα/β, IκBα, and NF-κB p65, consistent with pathway activation. NF-κB is a central mediator of inflammation, survival, and immune evasion in OSCC [36, 37]. Notably, in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, PKM2-driven lactate has been shown to inhibit NF-κB activity and thereby facilitate Galectin-9–mediated immunosuppression [38]. Accordingly, the reduction of PKM2 after FTO depletion could lower lactate output and relieve this inhibitory tone on NF-κB, providing a plausible explanation for the increased phosphorylation we observed. Whether this effect is mediated primarily by changes in PKM2 abundance or by its metabolic products remains unresolved; targeted measurements of glycolytic metabolites together with NF-κB reporter assays will be required to establish causality.

Compared to prior work, our study introduces a distinct mechanistic insight. Li et al. demonstrated that FTO promoted OSCC progression by regulating YAP demethylation [15]. In contrast, our findings reveal an FTO/m⁶A/YTHDF2/PKM2 axis that specifically governs glycolytic reprogramming (Fig. 3). This metabolic dimension expands the known pro-tumorigenic effects of FTO beyond proliferation and migration, thereby positioning our study as complementary yet novel. The integration of metabolism into FTO biology broadens the mechanistic landscape of m⁶A regulation in OSCC.

When placed within the broader context of m⁶A-mediated tumor biology, our results resonate with studies showing that m⁶A regulators can remodel tumor phenotypes. For example, Wu et al. reported that m⁶A modification of circCUX1 confers radioresistance in hypopharyngeal squamous carcinoma via the caspase-1 pathway [39], while Liang et al. demonstrated that ALKBH5-regulated m⁶A modification of circCCDC134 promotes cervical cancer metastasis through HIF1A transcriptional activation [40]. Similarly, a recent study showed that m⁶A-mediated IRS1 promotes OSCC progression through the p53/LINE-1 signaling pathway, further highlighting the diversity of m⁶A-dependent oncogenic mechanisms in this cancer type [41]. Our work expands this framework by demonstrating how m⁶A demethylation of metabolic enzyme transcripts can remodel cancer cell metabolism, thereby linking epitranscriptomic regulation to glycolytic adaptation.

Despite its strengths, this study has limitations. First, although RIP-qPCR confirmed PKM2 transcript enrichment in FTO- and YTHDF2-containing complexes, these approaches do not establish direct binding or identify site-specific interactions. Advanced techniques such as CLIP-seq or biotin-labeled RNA pull-down [42] will be needed to map precise sites of regulation. Second, although PKM2 downregulation following FTO loss strongly impaired glycolysis, rescue experiments by overexpressing PKM2 in FTO-deficient cells were not performed and would more conclusively establish PKM2 as a driver rather than a passenger in this pathway. Third, we did not test a catalytically inactive FTO mutant, which would directly confirm that its demethylase activity is essential for the observed effects. Fourth, our IHC analyses were based on a limited cohort size. To minimize imbalance among clinical subgroups (16/13/27 cases), we employed a subsampling approach to ensure comparability, while blinding of IHC scoring and standardized quantitative criteria were applied to reduce bias. Nonetheless, larger blinded cohorts with standardized quantification are warranted to strengthen statistical robustness.

In conclusion, our study identifies a previously uncharacterized FTO/m⁶A/YTHDF2/PKM2 regulatory axis that promotes OSCC progression by stabilizing PKM2 mRNA and enhancing glycolytic activity. This work not only broadens the understanding of epitranscriptomic regulation in OSCC but also supports the development of novel therapeutic strategies targeting FTO or its downstream metabolic pathways. Recent efforts have yielded several small-molecule inhibitors of FTO, including FB23-2, which effectively suppresses FTO demethylase activity and demonstrates antitumor efficacy in acute myeloid leukemia models [43, 44]. Importantly, preclinical studies have shown that FTO inhibition can enhance the therapeutic index of radiotherapy in head and neck cancers, suggesting potential translational relevance for OSCC [45]. In addition, emerging studies indicate that FTO also regulates ferroptosis sensitivity in OSCC, for instance by suppressing ACSL3 and GPX4, and that nanosword-based nanomaterials can trigger ferroptotic cell death in oral carcinoma [46]. Together, these findings underscore the promise of FTO inhibition as a clinically actionable strategy, particularly in combination with conventional therapies or ferroptosis-inducing approaches, for patients with OSCC and other m⁶A-dysregulated malignancies.
