TRADE: tolerability of adjuvant abemaciclib at 6 months after initial dose escalation in patients with early-stage HR-positive/HER2-negative breast cancer☆
Authors: I Schlam, D Trapani, S-E Kim, H Heiling, M Faggen, N Sinclair, P Sanz-Altamira, C Battelli, S Berwick, S Lo, J Acevedo, S Sinclair, A Malcolm, L Varella, S Sammons, S Schumer, PD Poorvu, E Wallace, E Pasternak, N Tayob, SM Tolaney, EL Mayer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdjuvant abemaciclib decreases recurrence rates and improves overall survival for patients with high-risk early-stage hormone receptor (HR)-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative breast cancer. However, toxicity, particularly diarrhea, may lead to dose reductions and early discontinuation. Primary results of the TRADE trial demonstrated that early dose escalation of abemaciclib can help patients reach and maintain the target dose of 150 mg twice daily (b.i.d.) by 12 weeks on therapy. In this article, we report a preplanned analysis of clinical outcomes at 24 weeks of the study.Patients and methodsTRADE is a prospective, investigator-initiated, single-arm, phase II trial that enrolled patients with early-stage node-positive HR-positive/HER2-negative breast cancer who were candidates for adjuvant abemaciclib. Patients initiated abemaciclib at 50 mg b.i.d. for 2 weeks, then 100 mg b.i.d. for 2 weeks, before escalating to 150 mg b.i.d. for the planned 2-year duration of therapy. We report key endpoints at 24 weeks.ResultsAmong 89 evaluable patients, 16 (18.0%) had discontinued abemaciclib by 24 weeks on therapy, including 7 (7.9%) who discontinued for adverse events. Among the 73 participants (82.0%) who were still receiving abemaciclib at 24 weeks, 47 (64.4%) were receiving 150 mg b.i.d., 18 (24.7%) were receiving 100 mg b.i.d., and 8 (11.0%) were receiving 50 mg b.i.d. Among all 89 assessable participants, 29 (32.6%) required at least one abemaciclib dose reduction, including 22 participants (24.7%) who had initially reached the 150 mg b.i.d. dose and then reduced. Patient-reported outcomes indicated no clinically meaningful change in quality of life from baseline through month 5 on therapy.ConclusionsFindings from the adjuvant TRADE study at 24 weeks of follow-up continue to demonstrate the utility of an early dose-escalation strategy during the initiation of adjuvant abemaciclib in optimizing patient persistence with and tolerability of therapy. Longer-term follow-up will continue to evaluate treatment outcomes over the planned 2 years of therapy.Key words: abemaciclib, breast cancer, dose escalation, HR-positive/HER2-negative (HR+/HER2−), TRADE

Introduction

For patients with high-risk early-stage hormone receptor (HR)-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative breast cancer, the addition of 2 years of adjuvant abemaciclib to standard endocrine therapy (ET) can significantly improve long-term outcomes, including invasive disease-free survival and overall survival.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 However, abemaciclib can be associated with toxicities that may interfere with patients completing the planned 2 years of therapy. In the monarchE trial, 18.5% of participants receiving abemaciclib and ET discontinued abemaciclib early due to adverse events (AEs), with toxicity leading to dose holds and reductions in 61.7% and 43.4% of participants, respectively. The majority of these events occurred in the first 12 weeks of therapy, and more than half of the discontinuations occurred without prior dose reduction.6 To maximize the therapeutic benefit of adjuvant abemaciclib and other cyclin-dependent kinases 4 and 6 (CDK4/6) inhibitors, it is imperative to pursue strategies to improve the tolerability of these agents in combination with ET.

The investigator-initiated single-arm phase II TRADE study was designed to explore the utility of early dose escalation of adjuvant abemaciclib in combination with ET for patients with high-risk early-stage HR-positive/HER2-negative breast cancer. The study met its primary endpoint; compared with monarchE, the early dose-escalation approach allowed more patients to reach and maintain the target abemaciclib dose and avoid early discontinuations during the first 12 weeks of therapy.7 This report describes 24-week follow-up, including abemaciclib dose holds, reductions, or early discontinuation, as well as toxicity and patient-reported outcomes, among participants on the TRADE trial.

Patients and methods

Study design

The details of the TRADE trial were published with the primary analysis.7 Briefly, TRADE (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT06001762) is an investigator-initiated, single-arm, phase II trial conducted at 12 sites within the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute network. The trial enrolled patients with stage II-III node-positive HR-positive/HER2-negative breast cancer who were clinically eligible for the addition of 2 years of adjuvant abemaciclib to standard adjuvant ET of provider choice. ET may have been initiated before enrollment in the trial or at the time of enrollment.

Treatment

Trial participants initiated abemaciclib using an early dose-escalation schedule. Participants initiated abemaciclib at 50 mg twice per day (b.i.d.) for the first 2 weeks, then increased to 100 mg b.i.d. for the next 2 weeks, before increasing to the full dose level of 150 mg b.i.d. at cycle 2 day 1 for the remainder of the planned 2 years of therapy. Each treatment cycle lasted 28 days (4 weeks).

Escalation to each higher dose level of abemaciclib required the absence of ongoing grade 3/4 hematologic or nonhematologic toxicity or ongoing nonhematologic grade 2 toxicity related to abemaciclib that did not resolve with maximal supportive measures. Diarrhea management followed protocol-defined supportive care recommendations. Patients were educated at enrollment and instructed to initiate antidiarrheal therapy (e.g. loperamide) at the first sign of symptoms, maintain hydration, and notify the study team. Patients who required an abemaciclib dose reduction for any reason were discouraged from re-escalating the dose during the first 12 weeks of treatment. Management of abemaciclib dosing after 12 weeks was per standard of care.

Endpoints

The primary objective of the TRADE trial was a composite endpoint at 12 weeks consisting of the rate of abemaciclib discontinuation for any reason and/or inability to reach or maintain the target standard dose of 150 mg b.i.d.; these results were previously reported.7 Participants were assessable for the primary endpoint if they received at least one dose of adjuvant abemaciclib and they did not experience a disease recurrence within the first 12 weeks. In this article, we report the results of a preplanned secondary analysis conducted at 24 weeks to evaluate the durability and tolerability beyond the primary 12-week endpoint. Patient-reported quality-of-life data were collected using the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy – Breast (FACT-B) instrument.8

Statistical considerations

Details regarding the design of the primary study endpoint, including choice of historical control and power calculations, were previously published.7 The patient-reported quality-of-life analysis using the FACT-B instrument employed the same clinically meaningful threshold described in the monarchE trial: ≥0.5 × standard deviation of baseline, with improving or worsening quality of life indicated by an increasing or decreasing FACT-B score, respectively.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice Standards and the Declaration of Helsinki. Institutional review board approval was obtained at Dana-Farber/Harvard Cancer Center and at all participating sites. All patients provided written informed consent before any study-related procedures.

Results

Clinical outcomes

Discontinuation of protocol therapy was calculated among all 89 participants assessable for the primary endpoint (a total of 90 participants were enrolled in the study; patient characteristics are described in Table 1).7 Abemaciclib was discontinued in 6 patients (6.7%) during the first 12 weeks of therapy and in an additional 10 patients (11.2%) between weeks 12 and 24, for a cumulative discontinuation rate of 18.0% (n = 16) by 24 weeks of exposure (Figure 1). Among patients who discontinued abemaciclib, the median time from treatment initiation to discontinuation was 3.5 months (range: 0.9-4.6). The most common reasons for early discontinuation were protocol-specified unacceptable AEs [n = 7; 7.9%, 95% confidence interval (CI) 3.2% to 15.5%, including pneumonitis, transaminitis, diarrhea, and allergic reaction] and patient preference/withdrawal of consent (n = 5; 5.6%; Table 2). Among those who discontinued for reasons other than AE, 33.3% (3 of 9) had a prior dose reduction, and one patient had been unable to escalate to 150 mg. Therefore, of the nine patients who discontinued treatment, one patient was receiving 50 mg b.i.d., three patients 100 mg b.i.d., and five patients 150 mg b.i.d.

Among the 73 participants (82.0%) who were still receiving abemaciclib at 24 weeks on study, 47 (64.4%) were receiving the target dose (150 mg b.i.d.), 18 (24.7%) were receiving 100 mg b.i.d., and 8 (11.0%) were receiving 50 mg b.i.d. (Figure 1 and Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108247).

Among all 89 assessable participants, 29 (32.6%) required at least one abemaciclib dose reduction by 24 weeks on study. This included 22 participants (24.7%) who had initially reached the target dose of 150 mg b.i.d. before requiring a dose reduction. In total, 12 participants (13.5%) never reached the target dose of 150 mg b.i.d.

Adjuvant ET was administered according to institutional standard of care and was not included as part of protocol therapy. By 24 weeks on study, 3 of 90 participants (3.3%) had discontinued ET for any reason.

AEs

AEs were evaluated in the full safety population, which included all 90 participants who initiated abemaciclib on study. The most common grade ≥2 treatment-emergent AEs occurring during the first 24 weeks of treatment were diarrhea [n = 30 (33.3%); grade 3: n = 5 (5.6%)], neutropenia [n= 28 (31.1%); grade 3: n = 3 (3.3%)], and fatigue [n = 24 (26.7%); grade 3: n = 0]. One participant experienced grade 4 neutropenia (1.1%; Table 3). No grade 5 AEs were reported. The monthly incidence rates of grade 2-3 diarrhea at 8-12 weeks, 12-16 weeks, 16-20 weeks, and 20-24 weeks were 16.5%, 12.0%, 10.1%, and 11.0%, respectively (Figure 2).

Patient-reported outcomes

Using the threshold rule, there was no clinically meaningful change in the FACT-B score from baseline through month 5 on therapy (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108247). The percentage of patients who answered the I have diarrhea item with the responses ‘somewhat’, ‘quite a bit’, or ‘very much’ increased from baseline to cycle 3 of treatment, then began to decrease by cycle 6 (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108247). Similarly, the percentage of patients who answered the item I am bothered by side effects of treatment with ‘somewhat’, ‘quite a bit’, or ‘very much’ increased from baseline to cycle 3 of treatment, then began to decrease by cycle 6 (Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108247). Notably, 50% of patients reported being bothered by the adverse effects of treatment at baseline, whereas 25% reported diarrhea at baseline. The incidence and severity increased with initiation of treatment but stabilized and declined after the second month.

Discussion

Despite optimal adjuvant ET, patients with high-risk early-stage HR-positive/HER2-negative breast cancer face the possibility of distant recurrence for many years after diagnosis.9 The addition of a CDK4/6 inhibitor to adjuvant ET reduces the risk of recurrence, as shown with both ribociclib and abemaciclib, and improves overall survival with abemaciclib in patients with higher-risk disease.4,5,10 These data led to US Food and Drug Administration approvals for both abemaciclib, based on monarchE study, and ribociclib, based on NATALEE study, in the early-stage setting.4,5,10 The efficacy of adjuvant abemaciclib and other CDK4/6 inhibitors may be hampered by toxicity, which can lead to early discontinuation in some patients. Thus, improving the tolerability of these agents in combination with adjuvant ET remains an area of clinical need.

The initial report from the TRADE study demonstrated a significant improvement in the ability of patients with high-risk early-stage HR-positive/HER2-negative breast cancer to reach and/or maintain adjuvant abemaciclib dosing by 12 weeks of therapy over rates reported at the same timepoint from monarchE. These results support clinical use of the TRADE strategy by clinicians starting patients on adjuvant abemaciclib therapy. This updated report of the TRADE trial continues to support the utility of an early dose-escalation approach of adjuvant abemaciclib. At 24 weeks on study, 82.0% of participants were still receiving adjuvant abemaciclib. Among those continuing on abemaciclib at 24 weeks, 64.4% were receiving the target dose of 150 mg b.i.d., compared with 70.8% at 12 weeks.7 Toxicity is the primary nonrecurrence reason for early discontinuation of a CDK4/6 inhibitor. By 24 weeks on the TRADE trial, 18% of patients had discontinued abemaciclib early, and 7.9% (95% CI 3.2% to 15.5%) discontinued due to unacceptable AEs. In monarchE, 10.6% of patients (95% CI 9.44% to 11.74%) had discontinued abemaciclib at 6 months due to AEs. Long-term follow-up of the TRADE study continues to evaluate the number of patients who complete the full 2 years of abemaciclib therapy, either at the full dose or a reduced dose, as well as overall discontinuations.

In monarchE, the rate of abemaciclib discontinuation at 6 months was 25.8%, including 18.5% due to AEs.6 The most common AEs leading to discontinuation or dose reduction were diarrhea, fatigue, and neutropenia.6 These findings were consistent with the toxicity profiles reported in landmark trials in patients with metastatic breast cancer.11, 12, 13, 14, 15 In TRADE, the discontinuation rate at 6 months (24 weeks) was 18.0%, including 7.9% due to AEs. Diarrhea was less frequent, with grade ≥2 diarrhea reported in 33.3% of patients and grade 3 diarrhea in 5.6%, compared with any-grade diarrhea in 83.5% and grade 3 diarrhea in 7.8% in monarchE.6 Although these cross-trial comparisons should be interpreted with caution given differences in study design, patient populations, and toxicity reporting, these observations suggest that an early dose-escalation strategy may be associated with improved tolerability and lower early discontinuation rates.

In TRADE, by 24 weeks on therapy, 32.6% of participants required at least one abemaciclib dose reduction. Among the 73 participants still receiving abemaciclib at 24 weeks, 64.4% were taking the target dose of 150 mg b.i.d., 24.7% were taking 100 mg b.i.d., and only 11.0% were taking 50 mg b.i.d. In comparison, in both monarchE and NATALEE, 43.4% and 23% of patients required dose reductions of abemaciclib or ribociclib due to toxicity, respectively. Data from the monarchE and NATALEE trials of adjuvant abemaciclib and ribociclib, respectively, suggest that dose reduction of a CDK4/6 inhibitor in combination with adjuvant ET does not significantly reduce the efficacy of the CDK4/6 inhibitor; this observation has also been reported in the metastatic setting.16,17 These findings raise the important question of whether maintaining the full dose of 150 mg b.i.d. is necessary to achieve optimal clinical benefit. In this context, the TRADE abemaciclib dose-escalation strategy provides an opportunity for individualized dose optimization, allowing patients to reach and maintain a tolerable dose that supports treatment persistence while minimizing toxicity. Accordingly, dose reductions should be considered a clinically appropriate strategy and are preferable to early discontinuation, with the goal of maximizing overall treatment exposure rather than maintaining a specific target dose. This approach may also be preferred by patients, as it limits potential exposure to severe toxicity.

At the primary analysis of the TRADE trial, no clinically meaningful difference in patient-reported quality of life during the first 8 weeks on therapy was observed.7 This pattern was maintained in this 24-week update, in which patients reported no clinically meaningful difference in quality of life from baseline through month 5 on therapy. The percentage of patients who reported being bothered by side effects of treatment, particularly diarrhea, peaked during cycle 3 of therapy and began to decrease by cycle 6. This finding supports prior data from monarchE,6 which suggest that the toxicity of abemaciclib in combination with ET is often greater during the first 12 weeks of therapy and then begins to diminish. Longer-term follow-up is necessary to evaluate whether this pattern holds.

This study has several limitations. The single-arm design does not allow for definitive conclusions regarding the impact of the dose-escalation strategy on tolerability or efficacy. Comparisons with historical data, including monarchE, should be interpreted with caution given differences in study design and patient populations. In addition, TRADE has a modest sample size, and follow-up remains limited, restricting assessment of long-term treatment persistence and clinical outcomes.

Novel endocrine therapies are also entering the adjuvant treatment landscape. The phase III lidERA Breast Cancer trial evaluated the oral selective estrogen receptor degrader (SERD) giredestrant in patients with early-stage HR-positive/HER2-negative breast cancer, finding at a median follow-up of 32.3 months an improvement in invasive disease-free survival with giredestrant over provider choice ET; overall survival data are not mature.18 In lidERA, eligibility allowed a broader population of early-stage patients than in monarchE or NATALEE, and adjuvant CDK4/6 inhibition was not utilized. If oral SERDs are approved in the adjuvant setting, it will be critical to determine the efficacy and safety of combining these agents with CDK4/6 inhibitors. This is particularly important given the similarity in hazard ratios observed at time of first report across several recent adjuvant trials in the early setting for HR-positive/HER2-negative breast cancer, underscoring the need to demonstrate incremental benefit when novel endocrine therapies are combined with CDK4/6 inhibitors.4,5,18 Longer follow-up will also be needed to determine whether oral SERDs improve overall survival, as has now been established with abemaciclib.4,5 Treatment selection will likely need to consider recurrence risk, patient preferences, comorbidities, and financial factors, given the potential for financial toxicity with extended and increasingly complex therapy.

In summary, the results from the 24-week analysis of the TRADE trial continue to support use of an early dose-escalation approach when initiating adjuvant abemaciclib in combination with ET, to improve the tolerability of this treatment combination and to reduce early discontinuations. Longer follow-up of the TRADE study will continue to evaluate how the benefits of this approach extend through the planned 2 years of therapy, in addition to planned biomarker analyses.
