Smartwatch-derived sleep estimates in individuals with probable sleep bruxism: an exploratory pilot study
Authors: Kuddusi Akbulut, Funda Gökçe Akbulut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSleep bruxism (SB) is sleep-related movement disorder potentially affecting sleep architecture. This exploratory pilot study evaluated smartwatch-derived sleep estimates in young adults with a probable SB phenotype versus controls.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 30 age- and sex-matched participants (18–50 years) were evaluated. The probable SB phenotype was defined by a triple-validations approach consisting of combined self report and clinical signs agreed upon by two clinicians. Controls were age- and sex-matched. Sleep estimates were recorded at home for 7 consecutive nights using a consumer-grade smartwatch (Apple Watch Series 8); nights with < 240 min of recorded sleep or substantial signal loss were excluded and repeated. Group comparisons used appropriate parametric/nonparametric tests and effect sizes (Cohen’s d).ResultsParticipants were analyzed (15 in probable SB group, 15 controls; 86.7% female in each). Total sleep time and light sleep estimates did not differ between groups. Compared with controls, the probable SB group had significantly reduced estimated durations of REM sleep (82.7 ± 18.9 vs. 111.5 ± 17.2 min; p < 0.001; d = 1.59) and deep sleep (49.1 ± 9.8 vs. 60.7 ± 6.9 min; p < 0.001; d = 1.37), with greater device-estimated sleep fragmentation (WASO median 27.0 vs. 3.0 min; p < 0.001; awakenings median 5 vs. 1; p < 0.001). Awakenings correlated positively with wake duration (r = 0.898) and negatively with REM duration (r = − 0.573) (both p < 0.001).ConclusionsIndividuals with probable SB exhibited altered device-estimated sleep patterns, featuring reduced deep/REM sleep and increased fragmentation. Consumer wearables may offer exploratory, adjunctive data on sleep continuity in dental practice but cannot replace physiological sleep staging. Findings warrant confirmation with concurrent polysomnography.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov; Registration No: NCT07453121. 25/02/2026.

Background

Bruxism is a repetitive jaw-muscle activity characterized by clenching or grinding of the teeth and/or by bracing or thrusting of the mandible. It has two distinct circadian manifestations, occurring either during sleep (sleep bruxism) or during wakefulness (awake bruxism) [1]. Sleep bruxism (SB) is widely classified as a sleep-related movement disorder within the International Classification of Sleep Disorders (ICSD-3) [2, 3] Current epidemiological data suggests that SB is a common phenomenon, with a prevalence in adults ranging approximately from 8% to 13%, although these rates can vary significantly depending on the diagnostic criteria employed [1, 4]. Unlike awake bruxism, which is often associated with psychosocial stress, SB is increasingly understood as a complex physiological event linked to the central nervous system and sleep-arousal mechanisms [5].

The etiology of SB is multifactorial, involving biological, psychological, and exogenous factors; morphological causes such as occlusal discrepancies have largely been replaced by evidence favoring central regulation [4, 6]. Research indicates that SB episodes are frequently preceded by physiological changes such as increased heart rate and brain activity, suggesting a strong temporal link with sleep micro-arousals [4, 7, 8]. Recent conceptual frameworks suggest a potential bidirectional relationship between sleep fragmentation and motor activity: while micro-arousals can trigger bruxism events, the resulting motor and autonomic bursts may further disrupt sleep continuity.

SB most commonly occurs during non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep and is characterized by rhythmic masticatory muscle activity (RMMA) arising within micro-arousal periods, during which the brain transiently shifts toward a wake-like state [9]. Experimental evidence shows that artificially induced micro-arousals triggered RMMA and teeth grinding in 70% of SB patients but not in controls, confirming the arousal–bruxism link [8]. However, evaluating this relationship using consumer-grade accelerometers presents inherent challenges. Such devices may misclassify the motor activity associated with bruxism as wakefulness or movement artifacts, rather than capturing true physiological sleep architecture [10, 11, 12]. Nonetheless, because SB is inherently associated with autonomic arousal and muscle activity, we hypothesize that multi-night smartwatch monitoring may capture a distinctive, device-estimated signature of sleep continuity variations that differentiates individuals with a probable SB phenotype from non-bruxers.

Polysomnography (PSG) with audio-video recording and electromyography (EMG) remains the gold standard for definitive SB diagnosis and physiological sleep architecture analysis [13]. However, its technical complexity, high cost, and laboratory setting limit widespread use. The artificial environment may also induce a “first-night effect,” altering typical sleep patterns and potentially masking habitual bruxism activity [14, 15]. These constraints have driven growing interest in home-based wearable alternatives for longitudinal observation.

Consumer-grade smartwatches utilizing photoplethysmography (PPG) and accelerometry have emerged as promising tools for naturalistic sleep monitoring [16, 17]. Systematic reviews identify the Apple Watch as the most widely used commercially available device capable of sleep staging [18, 19]. Despite these advances, a substantial knowledge gap remains regarding how accurately smartwatches reflect SB-associated variations in sleep estimates and whether they could serve as adjuncts to PSG.

Therefore, the aim of this exploratory pilot study was to evaluate the association between a clinically defined probable SB phenotype and smartwatch-derived sleep architecture estimates compared to healthy controls. Based on the established link between SB and sleep fragmentation, we hypothesized that individuals with probable SB would exhibit increased device-estimated wakefulness and altered sleep-stage estimates (e.g., reduced deep and REM sleep estimates) compared to controls, acknowledging that these metrics reflect device-derived algorithms rather than true physiological architecture.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical considerations

This study was conducted as an exploratory, cross-sectional pilot investigation. The study protocol was designed in strict adherence to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Cappadocia University (Date: 13/11/2024, Decision No: 24.19 G. O). Prior to data collection, written informed consent was obtained from all participants following a detailed explanation of the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits.

Participants and sample size estimation

The study population was selected from individuals presenting to the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Faculty of Dentistry, Cappadocia University. Individuals who applied to the clinic between January 1, 2025, and December 31, 2025, were screened for eligibility, and a total of 52 volunteers who met the inclusion criteria were initially identified as potential candidates.

In this study, the age range of 18–50 years was intentionally selected to enhance internal validity by reducing the confounding effects of aging on sleep architecture, given that sleep patterns become naturally more fragmented with age and deep sleep decreases markedly in older adults [20].

Addressing the sample size, an a priori power analysis was conducted (G*Power v3.1) for the primary outcomes (smartwatch-derived sleep stage estimates). Based on a pilot evaluation aiming to detect a large effect size (d = 1.0) with 80% power and an alpha error of 0.05, the calculation indicated that a minimum of 15 participants per group was required to achieve statistical significance. However, given the exploratory pilot nature of this study, it should be acknowledged that the derived effect size estimates are subject to sampling variability and potential inflation inherent to small sample sizes.

To evaluate the association between the probable SB phenotype and device-estimated sleep architecture, and to minimize the potential confounding effects of other sleep disorders, strict clinical criteria were applied.

Diagnosis of probable sleep bruxism

All diagnostic approaches for sleep bruxism have inherent limitations when used in isolation. The 2013 international consensus stated that patient self-report alone yields only a “possible” diagnosis; adding a clinical examination-based diagnosis supports a “probable” diagnosis, whereas definitive diagnosis requires PSG, which remains the gold standard [1]. Accordingly, the term “probable SB” was used in the present study. However, it is fundamentally important to acknowledge that without instrumental confirmation (e.g., polysomnography), the ability to attribute observed sleep estimates specifially to current bruxism-related motor events is inherently limited, as the true presence and frequency of RMMA cannot be definitively determined.

Because PSG could not be performed in this home-based monitoring study, we implemented a rigorous triple-validation approach to strengthen diagnostic reliability and minimize potential misclassification bias. To ensure maximum diagnostic accuracy, all clinical examinations were performed jointly by two experienced specialists- a prosthodontist and an oral and maxillofacial surgeon.

The diagnosis of “Probable Sleep Bruxism” was established only upon the clinical consensus of these two expert observers, requiring the simultaneous presence of the following:

Positive self-report: A history of regular clenching/grinding during sleep (at least 3–4 nights per week) confirmed by a bed partner/roommate. Reports of audible tooth-grinding sounds during sleep are particularly suggestive of SB, as normal orofacial muscle activity does not typically produce such noise [22]. Lavigne et al. reported that bed-partner reports of grinding noise, when validated against PSG, showed 78% sensitivity and 94% specificity as a stand-alone measure [23].Clinical findings: At least one objective sign on intraoral examination, including abnormal tooth wear (attrition grade ≥ 2), masseter hypertrophy, or tongue/cheek indentations.

Importantly, it must be explicitly acknowledged that clinical signs such as tooth wear, masseter hypertrophy, and mucosal indentations may partly reflect historical bruxism activity or comorbid awake bruxism, rather than exclusively current sleep-related motor activity. Although our requirement for concurrent expert clinical consensus and frequent nocturnal reports from bed-partners was designed to mitigate this issue, the potential for misclassification bias remains a notable limitation. By evaluating these three components simultaneously, we aimed to identify a phenotype likely experiencing active nocturnal bruxism. Nevertheless, to avoid any implicit suggestion of a causal or mechanistic linkage between true SB episodes and sleep architecture changes, we have maintained a highly cautious interpretation, framing our findings strictly as associative observations within a clinical “probable SB” cohort.

The control group was matched for age and sex and comprised individuals without a history of clenching/grinding (confirmed by a sleep partner) and without clinical findings supportive of SB on the consensus examination performed by the same two specialists.

Wearable device and sleep assessment protocol

Sleep architecture estimates were collected using the Apple Watch Series 8 (Apple Inc., Cupertino, CA, USA). The device performs sleep estimation by integrating movement data from a triaxial accelerometer with cardiovascular signals derived from PPG (e.g., metrics related to heart rate variability).

Data were processed using the native Apple Sleep Stages algorithm (available on watchOS 9.0 and later). Validated algorithms within the Apple HealthKit framework were used to provide estimates for wakefulness, REM sleep, light sleep, and deep (slow-wave) sleep stages. Although some previous studies have reported acceptable agreement for this device in healthy populations [24, 25], the current evidence base does not support the use of consumer-grade smartwatches for the accurate physiological differentiation of sleep stages—particularly REM and deep sleep—at an individual level. Crucially, in a population characterized by motor activity during sleep (such as SB), accelerometry-based algorithms are highly susceptible to the misclassification of wakefulness and movement-related artifacts. Therefore, it must be acknowledged that some wakefulness and awakening estimates in this study may partly reflect device-related classification bias or movement effects rather than true physiological awakenings. The data derived from the smartwatch in this study must be strictly interpreted as device-derived estimates and exploratory algorithms rather than true physiological sleep architecture.

Procedure

Participants were instructed to wear the device on their non-dominant wrist for 7 consecutive nights in their own home environment. To prevent the “first-night effect” and minimize behavioral changes, an adaptation night was allowed before data collection, and device screens were set to “Theater Mode” (dimmed/silent) [14].

The same device and assessment protocol were applied identically to both the Probable SB phenotype group and the control group, ensuring comparability of measurement methods across groups.

Data reliability and dropout analysis

To maintain data integrity, nights with recordings shorter than 4 h (240 min) or with substantial signal loss were excluded, and data collection was restarted to ensure completion of 7 consecutive nights. For each participant, valid nights were averaged to obtain a more robust representation of habitual sleep architecture estimates rather than night-to-night variability.

Although 52 participants were initially recruited, a strict quality control process resulted in a final sample of 30 participants (15 Probable SB, 15 Controls). The reasons for this attrition (dropout) were transparently documented to address potential selection bias:

Final analyses were conducted only on the 30 participants who provided complete, high-quality sleep data. Several measures were implemented to minimize potential sources of bias. Selection bias was addressed through strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, including the elimination of OSA risk and confounding systemic conditions. The first-night effect was mitigated by allowing an adaptation night prior to data collection. Attrition bias was addressed through transparent reporting of all dropouts and their reasons.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequency (n, %) for categorical variables. Given the small sample size, the assumptions for parametric testing were robustly evaluated. The normality of data distribution was strictly assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test alongside visual inspection of Q-Q plots.

To ensure statistical consistency:

Additionally, to determine the practical significance of the differences observed between the relatively small groups, Effect Size (Cohen’s d) was calculated for all statistically significant pairwise comparisons. Effect sizes were interpreted as small (d = 0.2), medium (d = 0.5), and large (d = 0.8). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Importantly, given the exploratory pilot nature of this study, no correction for multiple testing was applied. Consequently, the reported p-values should be interpreted with caution, as the lack of correction increases the risk of Type I error. No subgroup analyses or interaction tests were performed, as the study was not powered for such comparisons.

Sleep architecture

Comparisons of smartwatch-derived sleep estimates between the groups are presented in Table 2 (Table 2). Regarding overall sleep quantity, no statistically significant differences were observed in Total Sleep Time (TST) (p = 0.331) or estimated light sleep duration (p = 0.683) between the probable SB phenotype and control groups. Similarly, estimated deep sleep duration was significantly lower in the probable SB phenotype group (49.1 ± 9.8 min) compared to controls (60.7 ± 6.9 min) (p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.37). Sleep fragmentation and correlations

Smartwatch-derived sleep continuity estimates showed significantly greater fragmentation in the probable SB phenotype group. The median duration of estimated Wake After Sleep Onset (WASO) was significantly prolonged in the probable SB group (27.0 min) compared to the control group (3.0 min) (p < 0.001). While the low WASO in controls may reflect the device’s limited sensitivity to quiet wakefulness, the substantial increase in the probable SB group suggests a detectable pattern of device-estimated sleep disruption associated with the phenotype.

Furthermore, the median number of estimated awakenings was significantly higher in the probable SB phenotype group (5.0) compared to controls (1.0) (p < 0.001).

Correlation analysis revealed significant associations between device-derived sleep fragmentation parameters and estimated sleep stages. A strong positive correlation was found between the number of estimated awakenings and the duration of wakefulness (r = 0.898, p < 0.001), indicating that frequent arousals were associated with prolonged estimated wakefulness (Fig. 2). Conversely, a significant negative correlation was observed between the estimated number of awakenings and estimated REM sleep duration (r = − 0.573, p < 0.001), suggesting that increased sleep fragmentation is associated with a reduction in estimated REM sleep quantity (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This pilot study examined sleep architecture characteristics in adults with a clinically probable Sleep Bruxism phenotype using a consumer-grade, multisensor smartwatch. The main finding of this study was that, although no significant difference in total sleep time was detected between groups, the smartwatch-derived estimates of internal structure and quality of sleep differed significantly. The probable SB group exhibited significantly reduced estimated durations of REM and deep (N3) sleep, alongside increased device-scored sleep fragmentation and prolonged WASO. These results suggest an association between a probable SB phenotype and variations in device-estimated sleep continuity, indicating that wearable technology might capture exploratory proxy signatures of these architectural characteristics in a home-based setting. However, consumer sleep technologies have not yet been rigorously validated against physiological polysomnography for individual-level sleep staging, and there is currently no consumer sleep technology approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for this specific purpose [26]. Therefore, these devices cannot be used for the diagnosis or mechanistic evaluation of sleep disorders. Nevertheless, wearable technologies that track sleep data should be viewed strictly as exploratory, adjunctive tools that may facilitate patient -clinician interaction when presented within the context of a comprehensive clinical evaluation.

Interpretation of sleep architecture alterations

The observed differences in restorative sleep stage estimates (deep/N3 and REM) in our study are consistent with the micro-arousal framework proposed in SB pathophysiology. The literature indicates that RMMA episodes are frequently preceded by physiological arousals involving cortical and autonomic activation, and that SB episodes occur predominantly during the lighter stages of NREM (N1–N2) [7, 8, 10].

These micro-arousals can abruptly shift the sleeping individual—without full awareness—from deep sleep or REM sleep to lighter sleep stages (N1/N2) or to wakefulness [10].

However, because SB is fundamentally characterized by jaw muscle activity and autonomic activation, it is crucial to consider measurement-related bias. The smartwatch’s wake-detection heavily relies on accelerometry; thus, rhythmic masticatory movements may be algorithmically misclassified as wakefulness or physical movement. Consequently, the increased wakefulness estimates and sleep-stage variations may partly reflect this movement-related classification bias rather than definitive, PSG-confirmed alterations in physiological sleep architecture.

The significantly shorter estimated durations of deep sleep (49.1 min vs. 60.7 min) and REM sleep (82.7 min vs. 111.5 min) in the probable SB group support the hypothesis that recurrent bruxism events may be associated with disruptions in the maintenance of continuous restorative sleep. This finding is particularly consistent with the “hyperarousability” theory, which posits that individuals with a probable SB phenotype maintain a higher level of central nervous system vigilance, which may be assosiated with lower device-estimated durations of deeper sleep stages [7, 27]. The negative correlation between awakening frequency and REM duration further suggests an association between device- estimated sleep fragmentation and limited progression into REM cycles, though it must be reiterated that this correlation may partly reflect mathematical coupling within the device’s proprietary algorithm rather than a purely physiological phenomenon [28].

Considered alongside views that certain motor phenomena observed during REM may overlap with subclinical manifestations of REM sleep behavior disorder, these findings suggest that the associations between probable SB and sleep architecture characteristics should be addressed within the framework of a multidimensional central regulation disturbance involving autonomic pathways, rather than a simple unidirectional cause-and-effect model.

Sleep fragmentation and clinical symptoms

Sleep continuity estimates appeared to be markedly impaired in the probable SB group estimates; estimated nocturnal awakening frequency and WASO were significantly increased compared with controls, whereas the control group exhibited a more consolidated sleep pattern. These findings suggest that, in individuals with a probable SB phenotype, sleep integrity and architecture may be characterized by significant variations rather than differences in total sleep duration. The strong positive association between awakening frequency and wake duration (r = 0.898; p < 0.001) is consistent with the observation that frequent awakenings are accompanied by longer cumulative wakefulness, though as previously noted, this likely partially reflects mathematical coupling within the scoring algorithm. The significant negative correlation between awakening frequency and REM duration (r = − 0.573; p < 0.001) similarly suggests that device-scored fragmentation may be associated with disruptions in REM progression and/or REM continuity.

Regarding potential measurement-related biases, accelerometer-based wearables are sensitive to movement signals when classifying wakefulness. Therefore, rhythmic jaw movements accompanying SB likely contribute to some episodes being misclassified as “wake” (movement artifacts). While algorithms that incorporate cardiovascular signals (such as PPG/HRV) alongside accelerometry may capture portions of the sympathetic activation and autonomic arousals known to physiologically accompany RMMA episodes [7], the inability to reliably differentiate true physiological wakefulness from motor artifacts remains a fundamental limitation. Consequently, rather than establishing a definitive physiological phenotype, the detected pattern should be cautiously interpreted as a device-derived signature of sleep fragmentation consistent with the arousals expected in SB.

Wearable technology as a screening tool: clinical applicability

Although PSG is the gold standard in dental sleep medicine, its large-scale use in routine clinical practice is limited by practical constraints such as cost, accessibility, and the “first-night effect” [14, 15]. In clinical practice, the diagnostic approach for individuals suspected of having SB largely relies on patient/sleep-partner reports combined with clinical examination findings. Similarly, treatment outcomes are monitored substantially through subjective reports. In this context, wearable technologies enabling repeated longitudinal home measurements stand out as complementary tools providing data with greater ecological validity, and their increasing adoption suggests they may offer clinicians a practical supplementary data source [26].

However, expectations regarding their clinical applicability must be carefully moderated. The group identified with a probable SB phenotype in our study exhibited a device-estimated pattern of fragmented sleep, characterized by reduced REM and deep sleep estimates alongside increased awakenings and prolonged WASO. Because the Apple Watch integrates cardiovascular metrics derived from PPG in addition to accelerometry, epochs classified as “awake” or “light sleep” may indeed reflect clinically meaningful autonomic arousals rather than motion artifacts alone [7]. Accordingly, in a patient presenting with examination findings suggestive of SB, observing a similar sleep pattern may serve as an exploratory, adjunctive indicator to facilitate patient-clinician discussion regarding SB-associated variations in sleep continuity. It is critical to emphasize that smartwatch-derived data cannot support definitive clinical decision-making and do not provide diagnostic certainty. These devices should be used strictly as an exploratory complement to clinical assessment, with formal PSG referral remaining essential, particularly when comorbid sleep disorders must be robustly ruled out.

Strengths of the study design

Several methodological decisions were implemented to reduce confounders and enhance the reliability of home-based data collection. Restricting the age range to 18–50 years and excluding systemic and psychiatric comorbidities minimized major clinical confounders that could independently be associated with variations in sleep architecture—particularly the age-related reductions in deep sleep that could obscure variances associated with the probable SB phenotype [20, 28]. Excluding medications known to alter sleep physiology and lifestyle factors disrupting the sleep–wake rhythm similarly reduced the likelihood that observed fragmentation could be attributed to causes other than the suspected SB condition. A key strength of our study was the rigorous triple-validation approach used for the probable SB classification [1]. To minimize misclassification bias and ensure diagnostic reliability, we required the simultaneous presence of positive self-report confirmed by a bed partner (specifically regarding current grinding sounds) and objective clinical findings. Furthermore, all clinical examinations were performed jointly by two experienced specialists—a prosthodontist and an oral and maxillofacial surgeon—to ensure consensus in accordance with international grading standards [1].

Regarding data quality, seven consecutive nights of home monitoring were preferred over single-night measurement, with an adaptation night provided to mitigate the first-night effect [14]. This approach reduced night-to-night variability and increased the likelihood that the derived measurements reflected habitual sleep estimates. Device wear on the non-dominant wrist served as an established strategy to minimize motion-related noise, and sleep staging via the watchOS Apple Sleep Stages algorithm was grounded in validation studies reporting acceptable agreement with PSG [22, 23]. To manage signal loss inherent to home-based recordings, predefined quality-control criteria were applied: nights with fewer than 240 min of recording or substantial signal loss were excluded and repeated until the seven-night criterion was met. Averaging valid nights per participant reduced the influence of night-specific fluctuations. Although excluding 22 of 52 initially screened candidates reduced the sample size, transparent reporting of exclusion reasons—including protocol deviation, withdrawal of consent, non-compliance, and discomfort—enables assessment of potential selection bias.

Limitations

Given the exploratory pilot and cross-sectional nature of the study, these findings should be interpreted considering several constraints. A primary methodological consideration is the reliance on the “probable SB” diagnostic framework [1]. We acknowledge that clinical markers such as tooth wear and masseter hypertrophy are cumulative in nature and may reflect historical bruxism activity or comorbid awake parafunctional habits rather than current, active nocturnal motor events. This inherent limitation introduces a potential source of misclassification bias. While we attempted to mitigate this through a triple-validation approach—requiring concurrent expert consensus from two specialists and bed-partner confirmation of grinding sounds—the absence of instrumental confirmation (PSG/EMG) remains a fundamental constraint. Consequently, our findings characterize a clinical ‘probable SB phenotype’ and its associated smartwatch-derived sleep estimates, rather than providing definitive physiological evidence of sleep architecture changes directly by current nocturnal motor activity. Furthermore, although STOP-Bang and BMI-based exclusions reduced the risk of severe apnea, mild-to-moderate OSA, as well as other comorbid sleep disorders such as insomnia (including COMISA phenotypes) or circadian rhythm disturbances, could not be fully eliminated. Attributing sleep fragmentation primarily to SB in this context may represent an oversimplification, as the interplay between SB and undiagnosed comorbid conditions is highly complex.

Concerning sample size and generalizability, the pilot design and limited sample size (n = 30) may have constrained statistical power and the ability to detect small-to-moderate effects. More importantly, although large effect sizes were observed in our primary outcomes, these estimates should be interpreted with caution. Pilot studies with small sample sizes are inherently prone to sampling variability and effect size inflation; thus, the true magnitude of these differences may be less pronounced in the general population. Importantly, the very high proportion of female participants (86.7%) significantly restricts the external validity of our findings, as sleep architecture and SB manifestations can vary by sex; thus, our cohort may not accurately represent the broader community. Relatedly, strict exclusion and quality-control criteria favored individuals with higher protocol adherence, potentially introducing selection bias. Moreover, given the exploratory nature of this pilot study, no correction for multiple testing was applied. This significantly increases the risk of Type I error; therefore, the reported p-values should be interpreted with caution.

With respect to device-related variability, factors such as wrist fit, PPG signal quality, skin characteristics, and environmental conditions contribute to measurement error that cannot be fully standardized. The proprietary nature of the sleep-staging algorithm limits reproducibility assessment across software versions. More importantly, accelerometry-based methods are highly susceptible to misclassifying bruxism-related movement artifacts as wakefulness, and they may also underestimate quiet wakefulness, both of which affect WASO estimates and between-group comparisons. Additionally, because all sleep architecture variables were generated concurrently by the same proprietary wearable-based algorithm, some degree of mathematical coupling or algorithmic interdependence may exist. This interdependence could artificially inflate the observed correlations among derived metrics, such as awakening frequency and REM sleep duration. Finally, uncontrolled environmental factors in the home setting—including noise, light, temperature, and screen use—introduce residual night-specific variability. While these naturalistic conditions enhance ecological validity, they represent a residual confounder for internal validity.

Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; it cannot be determined whether the probable SB phenotype mechanistically drives sleep fragmentation or whether existing sleep fragmentation and arousal instability reciprocally facilitate SB episodes.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this exploratory, cross-sectional pilot study, young adults with a probable sleep bruxism (SB) phenotype exhibited a less favorable smartwatch-derived sleep architecture profile despite having a similar total sleep time. This device-estimated profile was characterized by reductions in REM and deep sleep estimates, alongside increased sleep fragmentation. Although consumer-grade smartwatches cannot replace polysomnography and do not provide diagnostic certainty, they may serve as practical, exploratory, and adjunctive tools for the non-invasive acquisition of habitual sleep estimates in the home setting. From a dental practice perspective, recognizing potential variations in sleep continuity among individuals with a probable SB phenotype may help shift care beyond purely mechanical tooth protection toward a more comprehensive framework that encourages interdisciplinary sleep health evaluation. Future longitudinal studies incorporating simultaneous PSG validation, planned with predefined endpoints, and conducted in larger, more sex-diverse cohorts are needed to confirm and extend these strictly associative findings.
