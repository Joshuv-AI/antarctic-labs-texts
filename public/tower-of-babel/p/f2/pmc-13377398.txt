Advanced Medical Care at Home Among Patients With Acute Heart Failure
Authors: Cheng-Wei Huang, Dan N. Huynh, Bing Han, Janet S. Lee, Ming-Sum Lee, Alyssa T. Millan, Kiet V. Lieu, Romina S. Rosen, Mehran R. Sina, Christopher C. Subject, Khang A. Nguyen, Huong Q. Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionAmong patients initially hospitalized for acute heart failure, does an advanced medical care at home (AMCAH) program offer comparable safety and quality compared with continued brick-and-mortar (BAM) hospitalization?FindingsIn this cohort study including 307 propensity-matched pairs, there were no differences in all-cause readmission, mortality, or guideline-directed medical therapy utilization at 30 and 60 days after discharge between patients from AMCAH and those who continued BAM hospitalization.MeaningThese findings suggest that in patients hospitalized with acute heart failure, an AMCAH program with clinician-determined eligibility is a reasonable alternative to continued BAM hospitalization, with comparable safety and quality after discharge.

Introduction

Acute hospital care at home is a care framework that allows for hospital-level care in the home setting.1 Adoption of acute hospital care at home increased rapidly following the passage of the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) acute hospital care at home waiver in 2020 in response to the COVID-19 pandemic.2,3 Acute hospital care at home, with its patient-centered focus, improves patient comfort and satisfaction by supporting a faster recovery in the home setting.1 At the same time, it reduces the potential risk of hospitalization-related complications, such as nosocomial infections, pressure ulcers, and deconditioning.1,4

Heart failure (HF) is the second most common cause of hospitalization, accounting for 4.1% of all hospitalizations in 2018.5 Prevalence of HF is also increasing and is expected to affect 8 million people in the US by 2030.6 Considering the mounting HF disease burden, acute hospital care at home has emerged as an attractive alternative to the traditional brick-and-mortar (BAM) hospital care model for patients with acute HF.7 HF represents the largest diagnosis-related group based on a CMS report on the Study of the Acute Hospital Care at Home Initiative.1 Despite literature supporting the safety of acute hospital care at home,1,8 contemporary evidence specific to HF within the US remains limited,4,7,9 and concerns regarding patient safety remain.10 Furthermore, evaluation of quality metrics specific to HF, such as utilization of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT), have been even more limited.4

Ongoing evaluation of the safety and quality of acute hospital care at home is necessary to ensure that processes are refined and improved upon before additional scaling. Study findings have important implications, including assuaging concerns regarding the safety and quality of care with respect to GDMT, given the increasing emphasis on GDMT initiation and utilization in acute HF.11,12 Therefore, we sought to evaluate the association of an acute hospital care at home–level program for acute HF with readmission, mortality, and GDMT utilization after discharge within a large integrated health system.

Methods

This cohort study was approved by the Kaiser Permanente Southern California (KPSC) institutional review board, and informed consent was waived because the research was low risk, the research could not practically be conducted with individual consent, and the waiver did not adversely affect the rights and welfare of the participants. This study is reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cohort studies.

Setting

This study is based in KPSC, a large integrated health system with 13 service areas that encompasses more than 200 medical clinics and 16 medical centers. KPSC serves more than 4.8 million members representative of the diverse population of Southern California.13,14 KPSC has an integrated electronic health records (EHR) system that allows for complete capture of both care within the health system and claims data. All study variables for this study were extracted from KPSC EHR and the operational database for the KPSC acute hospital care at home–level program, also known as advanced medical care at home (AMCAH).15

Briefly, the KPSC AMCAH program started as an acute care at home program managed locally by each service area. It was standardized in February 2023 as a regional program with centralized management and support, with a staggered rollout across each service area through October 2024 (Figure 1). The program enrolled patients from clinic, emergency department, observation, and inpatient settings and includes mobile phlebotomy, intravenous medication administration, nursing visits, remote patient monitoring, virtual case management, and virtual physician rounding to oversee patient care daily.

Eligibility is determined on a case-by-case basis by AMCAH physicians, although general exclusion criteria specific to HF are considered, such as unstable arrythmia, severe valvular disease, recent myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, worsening kidney function, dyspnea at rest after initial treatment, altered mentation beyond baseline, and repeated implantable cardiac device firings. Additional considerations include social factors (risk for safety or nonadherence) and equipment limitations (eg, oxygen requirement >5 L).

Design and Population

This was a retrospective cohort study of patients initially hospitalized for HF at 1 of 11 service areas. The study cohort was derived from an existing database of adult (age ≥18 years) nonpregnant member (≥1 year KPSC insurance membership) patient hospitalizations for acute HF that was collected retrospectively to examine Medication Initiated at Discharge to Reduce Readmission and Mortality in Heart Failure (MIDTERM-HF).16 The database since inception has been refreshed as needed to capture more recent hospitalizations with AMCAH exposure. HF hospitalizations included both observation and inpatient encounters, given similarity in care provided within our system, and was defined by a principal discharge diagnosis of HF by International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) code.6,16 This approach was internally validated with 200 EHR reviews, which revealed a positive predictive value of 97% based on Framingham criteria and 98.5% based on treating clinician assessment.17

From the MIDTERM-HF cohort, we created 2 cohorts and performed both intra– and inter–service area comparisons to enhance comparability and generalizability. For the intra–service area comparison, we compared patients discharged from AMCAH with patients discharged from continued BAM hospitalization within each service area between February 2023 and December 2024. We restricted this cohort to the first hospitalization of each patient within periods that regional AMCAH had been implemented for each service area (ie, the eligibility period for each service area differed based on AMCAH implementation) (Figure 1 and Figure 2A). For the inter–service area comparison, we compared patients discharged from AMCAH in service areas where AMCAH was implemented with patients discharged from BAM hospitalization in service areas where AMCAH had yet to be implemented, akin to a stepped-wedge design, between February 2023 and October 2024. We restricted again to the first hospitalization of the study period (Figure 2B).

For both cohorts, we subsequently excluded patients who were receiving dialysis, died during hospitalization or were discharged to hospice, or were discharged to a nonhome setting to capture AMCAH-eligible patients. We also excluded encounters where a patient was subsequently enrolled in AMCAH for a non-HF diagnosis based on operational records (eg, patient hospitalized initially for HF but subsequently enrolled in AMCAH for continued management of pyelonephritis). Finally, we excluded encounters where the patient did not have complete 60-day KPSC insurance membership on discharge and those with missing data to allow for complete case analysis (Figure 2). We chose a hospitalized cohort to increase comparability in a safety-first analysis through examining results in a population that definitively required hospitalization, recognizing that this would only capture a subset of AMCAH patients who were enrolled from the hospital setting.

Patient characteristics, including race and ethnicity, were based on self-report and extracted from EHRs. Race and ethnicity was classified as Asian, Pacific Islander, or other (eg, American Indian or Alaska Native, multiple races or ethnicities, and unknown race or ethnicity); Black, Hispanic, or White. Race and ethnicity were assessed to describe the study sample, assess for representativeness, and as a proxy for social constructs.

Exposure of Interest

The exposure was AMCAH for continued HF care following initial hospitalization. Patients in AMCAH are referred to as the AMCAH group, whereas those who continued BAM hospitalization are referred to as the BAM group.

Outcomes of Interest

The primary safety outcome was a composite of 30-day all-cause care escalation, readmission, or mortality. Care escalation, while unique to AMCAH, was included to more fully capture potential AMCAH-related adverse events as part of our effort to evaluate program-level safety. Care escalations were defined as any escalation necessitating hospitalization during AMCAH and were coded as a distinct event at time 0 to ensure its inclusion. Time 0 was defined as the AMCAH discharge date (or disenrollment date for escalations) for the AMCAH group and the hospital discharge date for the BAM group.

Secondary safety outcomes included the individual components of the primary composite outcome at 30 days, days alive and out of hospital (DAOH) by 30 days, the composite outcome and its individual components at 60 days, and DAOH by 60 days. The DAOH metric was chosen to give a comprehensive assessment of overall utilization that may not be captured by readmissions alone.18

The primary quality outcome was GDMT score at 30 days. The secondary quality outcome was GDMT score at 60 days. The GDMT score has been supported by the American College of Cardiology in assessing GDMT utilization.19 For our study, we limited our GDMT utilization assessment to 4 classes of GDMT, including renin-angiotensin-system inhibitors, β blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor, and assigned the same score for each class and dose as suggested by Fiuzat et al,19 which resulted in a scale from 0 to 9 (eTable 1 in Supplement 1).

Statistical Analysis

For both intra– and inter–service area comparisons, cohort characteristics were first described descriptively with mean and SD or absolute count and percentages, as appropriate. For intra–service area comparison, we used greedy nearest-neighbor 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) without replacement to account for imbalances between groups. PS was derived via logistic regression and included variables, selected a priori based on clinical experience and data availability.20,21 For inter–service area comparison, we further matched by month and year, in addition to performing PSM that otherwise mimicked the intra–service area comparison. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) before and after PSM were compared to assess covariate balances, where a SMD cutoff of 0.25 was considered balanced, given our modest sample.

Using the PSM sample, we estimated associations between AMCAH and study outcomes using appropriate scales: odds ratios (ORs) for binary outcomes and mean differences for quantitative outcomes. To adjust for matching pairs, we applied generalized estimating equations for binary outcomes and random effects for quantitative outcomes. Traditional multivariable regressions without PS were fitted as sensitivity analyses to check the influence of PSM to estimates and statistical inference. Unadjusted cumulative incidence function (CIF) curves were plotted to visually assess the time to event for all-cause escalations and readmissions accounting for competing risk of mortality. A 2-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed in SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute). Data were analyzed from April 2025 through March 2026.

Results

In the intra–service area comparison, there were 4237 patients (mean [SD] age, 75.7 [13.0] years; 2260 [53%] male), including 309 patients (7%) in the AMCAH group (Figure 2A). Prior to matching, the AMCAH group had a higher Elixhauser comorbidity score compared to the BAM group (mean [SD] 9.2 [2.9] vs 7.7 [2.9]). The mean (SD) length of stay (LOS) in AMCAH was 4.7 (3.9) days.

After PSM, 714 individuals (307 pairs; mean [SD] age, 75 [12.6] years; 374 [61%] male) were matched for the intra–service area comparison, and all SMD were less than 0.25 (Table 1; eFigure in Supplement 1). The PSM intra–service area sample included 62 Asian, Pacific Islander, or other patients (10%); 109 Black patients (18%); 176 Hispanic patients (29%); and 267 White patients (43%). The index hospitalization LOS was shorter in the AMCAH group compared to the BAM group (mean [SD] 3.5 [2.5] vs 4.5 [3.1]). There were 191 patients (40%) with HF with reduced ejection fraction.11

In the inter–service area comparison, there were 5489 patients, including 242 (4.4%) in the AMCAH group (Figure 2B). Prior to matching, the AMCAH group had a higher Elixhauser comorbidity score (mean [SD], 9.3 [2.9] vs 7.7 [2.9]) and more HF hospitalizations in the prior year (mean [SD], 1.3 [1.6] vs 0.8 [1.5]) compared with the BAM group. After PSM, 239 pairs were matched, and all SMD were less than 0.25 (eTable 2 and eFigure in Supplement 1). No differences were seen at 30 or 60 days for all outcomes or in the CIF curve for all-cause escalation or readmission (eTable 3 in Supplement 1; Figure 3B). In sensitivity analyses using multivariable regression, results were largely consistent, with the exception for 60-day GDMT score in the intra–service area (β = 0.16 [95% CI, 0.00 to 0.08]; P = .048) and inter–service area (β = −0.19 [95% CI, −0.38 to 0.00]; P = .045) comparisons between the AMCAH and BAM groups (eTable 4 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

In this retrospective PSM cohort study, continued care with AMCAH compared with continuous BAM hospitalization following initial hospitalization for acute HF was not associated with significant differences in readmission or mortality, DAOH, or GDMT scores at 30 or 60 days. These findings were consistent across both intra– and inter–service area comparisons and sensitivity analyses using traditional regression.

Our study adds to the growing literature highlighting AMCAH as a promising alternative to BAM models for patients with HF. Existing literature have reported comparable if not lower readmissions and mortality rates with AMCAH in HF.4,9 There are important differences between our study and prior studies. First, our study is 1 of the first to perform a rigorous comparative analysis with a control group and PSM. Second, our study focused on patients initially hospitalized as part of our goal to ascertain safety, which should alleviate potential selection bias regarding hospitalization needs.4,9 Moreover, as an integrated health system, our value-based care approach and lack of encounter-level incentives should mitigate potential concerns of enrolling lower-acuity patients in AMCAH for revenue capture and thereby strengthen the comparability of our 2 groups.22 Finally, our study population represented a higher-acuity segment of the patients with HF within our system, as evident by the higher Elixhauser index and GDMT score within the AMCAH group prior to matching and higher 30-day readmission rates even compared to previously reported rates within our organization (22%-23% vs 18%),23 which in turn provides added reassurance regarding the safety of enrolling patients who were more severely ill into the AMCAH program. Simultaneously, our escalation rate (approximately 5%-6%) was lower compared with prior reports (10%-16%), although definitions differ.4,9

We believe the inclusion of care escalation is critical for a comprehensive safety assessment of an acute hospital care at home program. We acknowledge that this captures an event that would otherwise not be captured with BAM. While this decision was intended to be conservative, it may also reduce the ability to detect meaningful between-group differences, although individual components were directionally consistent. To include an in-episode event, we assigned it to time 0, recognizing that such assignment may potentially distort temporal associations and should be taken into consideration when examining our time-sensitive analyses. Notably, we explicitly chose a time-agnostic approach for our primary outcome analysis.

It was recognized that PSM on variables at discharge does not completely address selection biases with AMCAH enrollment. AMCAH patients likely reflect a subset of patients who are more severely and chronically ill with more longitudinal care engagement. While the shorter index hospitalization LOS of approximately 1 day upfront may in part be a shift in care to the AMCAH setting, it may also reflect near-term clinical trajectory, which may be incompletely accounted for, especially considering clinician eligibility determination. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as supporting the safety of clinician judgement in selecting patients for acute hospital care at home, an integral component of our AMCAH program, rather than intrinsic AMCAH equivalence to BAM.

The shorter upfront index hospitalization LOS by approximately 1 day was followed by a LOS of approximately 4.7 days on AMCAH. Longer LOS (2.1-3.0 days) with acute hospital care at home for HF have been previously reported in the CMS report.1 Irrespective, these durations should not be interpreted in isolation, nor was our study designed to assess cost. The full outcomes of acute hospital care at home, including the throughput impact, reduction of hospitalization-related complications, and subsequent downstream utilization, are likely inadequately captured through these numbers alone. Furthermore, differences in rapidity of diuresis and GDMT titration may have also been a factor. Alternatively, the AMCAH LOS may reflect a subset of patients who were more hypervolemic on initial presentation and necessitated a longer duration for diuresis, a theory supported by patients in the AMCAH group being more severely ill, as previously noted.

We wanted to explore quality outcomes beyond typical safety outcomes for our study to assess whether AMCAH is an appropriate alternative for patients with acute HF, a group with high morbidity and mortality burden but also with significant opportunities for intervention, as highlighted by recent gaps for GDMT in HF with reduced ejection fraction.24,25 GDMT represents 1 of the most modifiable factors to reduce adverse outcomes among patients with HF.11 First, it was reassuring to see that GDMT utilization was similar if not higher at time 0 in the AMCAH group compared with the BAM group prior to PSM. Second, comparable rates of GDMT utilization after discharge further support that GDMT initiation and titration in a less intensely monitored setting during acute HF is a viable option that did not require subsequent deescalation.4,7 Our results highlight the opportunities to further optimize GDMT utilization, especially in the second month after discharge, given only a slight increase in scores between 30 and 60 days in both groups. Our GDMT score should be interpreted with the understanding that our study included patients with HF with an ejection fraction of at least 40%, who have a weaker basis for GDMT initiation.11

We also found that the penetration of AMCAH was only 6% to 7%. This was likely a result of a multitude of factors, including uncertainty with a newer program by both clinicians and patients, as well as operational scaling challenges,10 although this lower rate likely also helped reduce the indication biases in the intra–service area comparison. Given our findings, further investigation into strategies to improve adoption is necessary. Simultaneously, we recognize that findings from our integrated health system may not be immediately applicable to other health systems, given the unique nature of our integrated health system model and value-based care approach, which allows us to continue AMCAH despite an evolving regulatory landscape.26,27 Regardless, our experiences remain valuable as a demonstration of safety and quality in support of AMCAH and for other health systems aspiring to provide more value-based care.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study had numerous strengths, including a diverse patient population. We pursued both an intra– and inter–service area comparison and sensitivity analyses, supporting the robustness of our findings. We had a comprehensive list of variables, including socioeconomic factors, which likely has even greater relevance for AMCAH. Furthermore, we are 1 of the first studies to evaluate GDMT utilization rigorously.

Limitations of our study must also be considered and include its observational design. We had a modest sample, although our study remains 1 of the larger comparative analyses on this topic to date.4,9 The modest sample also precluded a more stringent SMD for covariate balance (ie, <0.1), although consistent results were seen with traditional regression methods. There may be residual confounding from lack of severity surrogates, such as New York Heart Association class and B-type natriuretic peptide. Clinician-determined eligibility on a case-by-case basis and continuous program and eligibility refinement may also limit generalizability.

Conclusions

The findings of this cohort study including a diverse cohort of patients initially hospitalized for acute HF suggest that an AMCAH program with clinician-determined eligibility was a reasonable alternative to continued BAM hospitalization with no differences in rates of readmission, mortality, or GDMT utilization up to 60 days after discharge. These results support further scaling and adoption of AMCAH.
