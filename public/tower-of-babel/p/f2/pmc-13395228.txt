Childhood-onset schizophrenia co-occurring with 3-hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA hydrolase deficiency: A rare dual diagnosis successfully treated with clozapine
Authors: Neetu Garg, Shobit Garg, Mohan Dhyani
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Childhood-onset schizophrenia (COS), defined by psychosis onset before age 13, is a rare and severe variant associated with profound cognitive deficits, delayed development, and a poorer long-term prognosis than adult-onset illness.[1] Mitochondrial dysfunction is an increasingly recognized contributor to schizophrenia pathophysiology.[2] 3-Hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA hydrolase (HIBCH) deficiency is an ultra-rare autosomal recessive disorder of valine catabolism; toxic metabolite accumulation causes neurotoxicity and developmental regression, classically with Leigh-like features, though phenotypic variability including milder forms without MRI abnormalities is well described.[34] We report an adolescent with COS co-occurring with HIBCH deficiency, illustrating diagnostic complexity, pharmacological challenges, and shared neurobiology.

A 17-year-old female presented to our tertiary center with a 5-year history of progressive psychiatric symptoms including social withdrawal, declining self-care, disorganised behavior, muttering to self (s/o auditory hallucinations), and persecutory beliefs; she was the eldest of two siblings, with past history notable for congenital heart disease at birth (details unavailable) and delayed speech milestones. Poor attention, slow learning, and social withdrawal throughout childhood led to school discontinuation after 8th grade. Overt psychosis began at age 12 with auditory hallucinations (commanding and commenting voices), persecutory delusions, disorganized behavior, poor self-care, and flattened affect. Neurological examination was unremarkable. DSM-5 criteria for COS were met; the baseline Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) was 87 (moderate-to-severe), Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) was 22/30, and brain MRI showed no structural abnormality.

Given the atypical early-onset course, documented developmental delays, progressive cognitive decline, and inadequate response to multiple antipsychotics, targeted metabolic screening was initiated to exclude an underlying inborn error of metabolism.[5] Urinary gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) revealed elevated homoserine and 2,3-dihydroxy-2-methylbutyrate (23DH2MB)—a pathognomonic pattern for HIBCH deficiency.[6] Serum lactate was normal (1.2 mmol/L). Whole exome sequencing (WES) returned negative, consistent with reports highlighting biochemical screening’s diagnostic priority over WES.

Pharmacological management was challenging, with antipsychotics trialled sequentially: Trifluoperazine (5 mg/day) caused rapid extrapyramidal symptoms (EPSs); aripiprazole (10 mg/day) caused intractable akathisia; paliperidone (6 mg twice daily) yielded minimal benefit. Each trial was conducted at recommended therapeutic doses before discontinuation. First-line second-generation antipsychotics were deferred given metabolic concerns (congenital heart disease history) and EPS vulnerability. Clozapine was initiated given failure of multiple antipsychotic trials largely due to intolerance to EPS, in the setting of early-onset psychosis, and titrated to 25 mg twice daily. Tolerability was good with no major adverse effects. Positive and negative symptoms diminished substantially, social engagement improved, disorganized behaviors resolved, and follow-up PANSS fell from 87 to 50, with sustained clinical stability.

This case underscores the challenges when COS coexists with a rare metabolic disorder. Our patient’s chronic, progressive course distinguishes primary COS from metabolic encephalopathy, with HIBCH deficiency acting as a biological amplifier—worsening glutamatergic and dopaminergic dysregulation through mitochondrial stress.[27] Antipsychotic intolerance may reflect mitochondrial effects on dopamine pathways. Phenotypic heterogeneity in HIBCH deficiency—including forms without seizures or MRI changes—means the diagnosis may be missed without targeted urinary metabolite screening.[346] Clozapine’s broad receptor profile conferred advantage in this metabolic context.[8] Three takeaways emerge: (i) Metabolic and genetic screening should be considered in early-onset psychosis with developmental or neurological features, (ii) HIBCH deficiency can co-occur with primary psychiatric illness rather than causing psychosis de novo, and (iii) clozapine can achieve meaningful gains in treatment-resistant COS even with metabolic comorbidity. Collaborative research into shared mitochondrial pathways is warranted.
