New insight about early miscarriage: a prenatal data-based study
Authors: Yuanhang Zhu, Yaming Liu, Chunying Ren, Jing Liu, Qian Yang, Naiqi Li, Ting Wang, Ping Zhang, Jing Xue, Chenchen Ren, Ling Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly miscarriage, a common pregnancy complication predominantly occurring before 12 weeks, is conclusively linked to embryonic chromosomal abnormalities. Given the rising proportion of pregnancies at advanced maternal age, a contemporary re-examination of the relationships among early miscarriage, sex chromosomal anomalies, and maternal age is imperative to optimize clinical counseling strategies.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed cases from the Medical Genetics and Prenatal Diagnosis Center at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (July 2022–December 2024). Inclusion criteria required fetal samples with < 10% maternal cell contamination confirmed by fluorescent PCR-capillary electrophoresis. Exclusion criteria included absence of CNV-seq/CMA testing results, discordant results, incomplete documentation, or donor oocyte pregnancies. Cases were stratified into early miscarriage (EM) group and non-EM (NEM) group. Chromosomal abnormalities (triploidy/tetraploidy, uniparental disomy and aneuploidies involving chromosomes 21, 18, 13, X, and Y), fetal sex, and maternal age were compared between groups.ResultsIn a cohort of 21,311 fetuses undergoing genetic testing, the overall prevalence of sex chromosome aneuploidies (SCAs) was 2.07%. Fetuses with EM exhibited significantly higher SCAs prevalence (7.5%) compared to NEM (1.4%, P < 0.001). Stratified analysis demonstrated elevated 45, X/mosaic 45, X frequency in EM (7.03% vs. 0.40% NEM, P < 0.001), whereas XYY/XXY/XXX/mosaic XXX was significantly lower in EM (0.34% vs. 0.97% NEM, P = 0.002). The EM rate increased with maternal age from 15 to 27 years, stabilized around 13% thereafter, and showed minor fluctuations. Maternal age was significantly lower in cases with 45,X compared to other cases (29.88 ± 4.15 vs. 31.43 ± 4.50 years, P < 0.001).ConclusionsThis observational study identified an overall SCAs prevalence of 2.07% in the cohort. Beyond sex chromosome trisomies, autosomal trisomies and 45, X demonstrated higher prevalence in the EM compared to NEM. A positive correlation existed between chromosomal trisomies prevalence and advancing maternal age, whereas 45, X exhibited inverse association with maternal aging. Distinct age-stratified pattern emerged: early miscarriage rates increased with maternal age before 27 years, stabilizing thereafter at approximately 13% without further age-dependent fluctuations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08096-z.Keywords: Early miscarriage, Sex chromosome aneuploidies, Prenatal diagnosis, Advanced maternal age, Fluorescent PCR

Background

The rising prevalence of pregnancies at advanced maternal age (AMA) has emerged as a significant public health concern, with epidemiological studies demonstrating a 3-fold increase over the past decade [1]. These AMA pregnancies poses significant maternal and fetal risks [2], including miscarriage [3]. Miscarriage is one of the most common complications of pregnancy and generally defined as pregnancy loss before gestational threshold of viability [4]. A systematic review demonstrated that the cumulative incidence of miscarriage in clinically recognized pregnancies reached 15.3% [5].

From a gestational chronology perspective, miscarriage is categorized into early and late subtypes. Early miscarriage is defined as spontaneous pregnancy loss before 12 weeks’ gestation, whereas late miscarriage refers to spontaneous fetal demise occurring between 12 and 24 weeks’ gestation [6, 7]. Epidemiologic data indicate that approximately 80% of clinical miscarriage are early [8]. The etiology of early miscarriage is primarily embryonic aneuploidy [9], found in about 60% of cases. Autosomal trisomies are the most frequent, followed by monosomy X and triploidy [10, 11]. Late miscarriage is more frequently associated with complex maternal pathophysiological factors including antiphospholipid syndrome congenital uterine anomalies, cervical insufficiency, intrauterine infections, polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS), and placental insufficiency [12, 13].

However, current understanding of the role of specific types of aneuploidies in miscarriage remains incomplete. In particular, studies on sex chromosome aneuploidies (SCAs) in miscarriage remains unclear, largely due to methodological limitations. Most studies focus exclusively on products of conception without incorporating non-abortive control specimens [10, 11]. To clearly delineate the anomalies that may contribute to early pregnancy loss from those that typically preclude survival to prenatal diagnosis, a focused analysis is necessary. Therefore, this study specifically investigates fetal chromosomal abnormalities compatible with survival into the prenatal diagnostic period (including trisomies 13, 18, 21, X and Y). By utilizing a large prenatal diagnostic cohort and comparing early miscarriage cases to non-miscarriage controls from the same clinical setting, we aim to clarify the distinct association of these viable chromosomal abnormalities with early miscarriage and to analyze their relationship with maternal age.

Materials and methods

Participants

This retrospective study analyzed data from the Department of Medical Genetics and Prenatal Diagnosis at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University, involving cases between July 2022 and December 2024. Fetal samples collected in this study included amniotic fluid, embryonic/fetal tissue, fetal blood (intracardiac or cordocentesis-derived) and chorionic villus sampling, all obtained for diagnostic purposes. Sample acquisition was clinically indicated for: AMA, miscarriage evaluation, adverse obstetric history, harmful prenatal exposures, abnormal prenatal screening results (including maternal serum screening (MSS) or non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT)), fetal structural anomalies, or abnormal soft markers. Critically, all samples were derived exclusively from singleton pregnancies.

Eligibility criteria required fetal samples to undergo maternal cell contamination assessment via fluorescent PCR-capillary electrophoresis (fPCR-CE) followed by confirmatory chromosomal analysis via copy number variation sequencing (CNV-seq) or chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA). Exclusion criteria included: (1) absence of CNV-Seq/CMA testing, (2) discordant results between fPCR-CE and CNV-Seq/CMA, (3) incomplete clinical documentation, and (4) maternal cell contamination > 10%, (5) donor egg pregnancy.

fPCR-CE

fPCR-CE was used to identify whether fetal samples are contaminated with maternal cells, but also to detect the ploidy of chromosome 21, 18, 13, X and Y in sample cells. A multiplex fluorescent PCR coupled with capillary electrophoresis-based DNA fragment analysis was employed to simultaneously amplify 30 genetic markers in a single PCR reaction. The amplified products were separated by capillary electrophoresis to obtain genotyping results for each genetic marker. Based on the genotyping data, the average number of target chromosomes per cell was determined, thereby assessing the chromosomal ploidy (e.g., diploidy, triploidy, or aneuploidy) and uniparental disomy (UPD). The 30 genetic markers’ loci included Xp22.2, Xp22.11/Yp11.31, Xp11.21/11q22.3, 21q21.1, 21q21.3, Xq21.1/3p24.2, 21q22.3, 21q22.13, Xq12, Xq24, Xq13.1, 21q21.1, 21q22.3, Xp22.2, 21q22.3, Yq11.21/1q43, 18q21.32, 18q22.1, 18p11.31, 18q21.2, 18q12.3, 18q23, Yp11.31, 13q33.3, 13q31.1, 13q32.1, 13q14.3, 13q21.1, and 13q14.11.

CNV-seq and CMA

CNV-seq and CMA were employed to detect chromosomal copy number variations (CNVs). CNV-seq achieves ≥ 10% mosaicism detection sensitivity, while CMA’s resolution for mosaicism is limited to ≥ 30%. All testing procedures were performed by certified professionals in the Department of Medical Genetics and Prenatal Diagnosis at our institution, adhering strictly to standardized operating protocols. The results were interpreted and classified in accordance with the joint guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Clinical Genome Resource.

Study design

In this study, fetuses were classified into two groups: early miscarriage (EM), defined as spontaneous pregnancy loss before 12 weeks’ gestation with gestational age confirmed by both last menstrual period (LMP) and ultrasound concordance; and the non-early miscarriage group (NEM), comprising fetuses with either: (a) Documented viability at ≥ 12 weeks’ gestation, or (b) First diagnosis of embryonic/fetal demise occurring after 12 weeks’ gestation. All enrolled fetuses underwent fPCR-CE testing, with specimens showing abnormal results being systematically compared against CNV-seq or CMA findings. Concordant results were directly validated, while discordant cases underwent repeat fPCR-CE testing. Specimens achieving consistency upon retesting were confirmed, whereas persistently discordant cases were excluded from analysis. The specimen inclusion workflow is detailed in Fig.1. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with standardized protocols by certified laboratory personnel.

It is noteworthy that our analysis was limited to aneuploidies (including tetraploidy and triploidy) and uniparental disomy involving chromosomes 21, 18, 13, X, and Y. Numerical abnormalities in other chromosomes (e.g., chromosomes 1, 2) were excluded from the scope of this study. All references to “abnormal” or “normal” in the study results specifically pertain to the ploidy status of the aforementioned five chromosomes. To ensure consistent and clear presentation of cytogenetic findings, standardized abbreviations are employed throughout this manuscript: 45,X is abbreviated as XO; trisomy 21 (47, XN, + 21) as T21; trisomy 18 (47, XN, + 18) as T18; trisomy 13 (47, XN, + 13) as T13; 47,XXY as XXY; 47,XYY as XYY; 47,XXX as XXX; 48,XXXX as XXXX; 48,XXXY as XXXY; and 49,XXXXY as XXXXY. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (Approval No. 2025-032-01).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software. Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the cohort samples. According to the data distribution characteristics, continuous variables were expressed as mean±standard deviation (SD) and the independent sample t test was used to compare the two groups. For categorical variables, expressed as frequency (percentage), and the chi-square (χ2) test was used to compare the differences between two groups. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to quantify the association between chromosomal abnormalities and EM. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) was employed to quantify linear associations between maternal age and EM. To examine whether a nonlinear relationship exists between age and the risk of early miscarriage and to quantify the change in risk before and after a specific threshold, we performed a piecewise logistic regression analysis using 27 years of age as the prespecified cut-point. P ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Overall characteristics of the participants

This study enrolled 21,311 fetuses with maternal ages ranging from 15 to 52 years (mean age 31.42 ± 4.99 years). Among them, 2360 cases experienced EM, 18,951 showed NEM. Chromosomal abnormalities included 1243(5.83%) cases of aneuploidy (including compound aneuploidy), 205(0.96%) cases of polyploidy and 8(0.04%) cases of uniparental disomy (UPD). Among them, 442 (2.07%) cases of SCAs (including XO, XYY, XXY, XXX, XXXX, XXXY, XXXXY, mosaic XO, mosaic XXX, XO/T13, XO/T21, T21/mosaic XXX, T21/mosaic XO, mosaic XXY/mosaic T2) and 241(1.13%) cases of XO/mosaic XO, 57(0.27%) cases of XYY, 92 (0.43%) cases of XXY, 42(0.20%) cases of XXX/mosaic XXX. Difference of fetal sex between EM and NEM

Fetal sex is determined by sex chromosomes. For XO, fetuses without a Y chromosome were classified as female, while those with one or more Y chromosome were classified as male. Overall, the proportion of male fetuses in EM (1143/2360, 48.43%) was lower than that in NEM (10054/18951, 53.05%) (χ²=17.97, P < 0.001). To eliminate potential confounding from undetermined fertilization origins in XO conceptuses, we excluded all pure and mosaic XO cases from the analysis. Subsequent evaluation revealed no statistically significant difference in male fetus proportion between EM (1143/2192, 52.14%) and NEM (10052/18875, 53.25%, χ²=0.974, P = 0.324)。To further investigate the association between fetal sex and EM, we analyzed differences in fetal sex distribution between EM and NEM in common aneuploidies, including T21/mosaic T21, T18/mosaic T18, and T13/mosaic T13, but there was no statistical difference (all P > 0.05).

Notably, our analysis revealed significant sex distribution disparities in autosomes trisomic cohorts: male predominance was observed in T21/mosaic T21 fetuses (60.44% vs. 52.33% in non-T21/mosaic T21 counterparts), while T18/mosaic T18 cases demonstrated reduced male proportion (43.31% vs. 52.61% in non-T18/mosaic T18). However, no significant sex disparity was observed in T13/mosaic T13 cases compared to non- T13/mosaic T13 counterparts (Table 3). Additionally, among 10 cases of mosaic XO, we observed 2 cases of XO/XY mosaicism and 8 cases of XX/XO mosaicism. All 8 cases of uniparental diploidy were female. Among 204 triploid cases, female fetuses (XXX = 89) account for 43.63% and male fetuses (XYY = 4, XXY = 110, XXYY = 1) account for 56.37%.

Difference of maternal age between EM and NEM

In this study, the mean maternal age in EM was 31.78 ± 4.43 years, higher than that in NEM (31.37 ± 5.05 years; t = 4.11, P < 0.001), while Pearson correlation analysis revealed inverse association between maternal age and EM (r = -0.025, P < 0.001). To minimize confounding from younger populations, we restricted analyses to individuals aged ≥ 21 years. Pearson correlation demonstrated a persistent but negligible inverse correlation between maternal age and EM (r = -0.016, P = 0.02). Subsequent analysis of the ≥ 24 years subgroup revealed no statistically significant association (r = 0.003, P = 0.664).

Furthermore, we constructed a trend graph of maternal age versus EM rate (Fig.2, Figure S1). The graph demonstrates that between 15 and 27 years of age, the EM rate exhibits an upward trend with advancing maternal age, while beyond 27 years, the rate stabilizes at approximately 13% followed by minor fluctuations. Piecewise regression analysis identified 27 years of age as a significant threshold in the age effect on early miscarriage risk (extra-age effect: OR = 1.322, 95% CI [1.252, 1.397], P < 0.001). Specifically, among women aged 27 years and younger, each year closer to 27 (i.e., with increasing age) was associated with a significantly higher risk of miscarriage (OR = 0.775, 95% CI [0.737, 0.814], P < 0.001). For women older than 27 years, each additional year of age was associated with a further slight increase in risk on this elevated baseline (overall OR = 1.025).

Discussion

This study analyzed the prevalence of SCAs in products of conception using all-cause prenatal genetic testing cohorts. Observed frequencies aligned with prior reports with 45, X (4.5%-15.49%), XXX (0.54%) and XXY/XYY (0.1–0.3%) [9, 10, 14, 15]. The significantly higher overall prevalence of SCAs in the EM group, primarily driven by the striking overrepresentation of 45, X/mosaic 45, X, underscores a fundamental difference in the embryonic viability associated with various sex chromosome abnormalities. This pattern suggests that 45, X is highly incompatible with early embryonic development, leading to pregnancy loss. In contrast, the lower prevalence of sex chromosome trisomy in the EM group indicates that these anomalies are more frequently compatible with survival into the prenatal diagnostic period.

In assessing maternal age-related risks, EM cases exhibited marginally elevated maternal age compared to NEM controls, yet correlation analyses revealed no significant association between advancing maternal age and EM occurrence. This contrasts with existing literature [2, 16], potentially attributable to cohort heterogeneity, though Frederiksen LE et al. report differential miscarriage rates only for women aged ≥ 40 years versus 20–34 years [17]. Our piecewise regression analysis identified 27 years as a statistically significant threshold in the relationship between maternal age and early miscarriage (EM) risk (extra-age effect OR:1.322). This model quantifies a shift in risk pattern: for women aged 27 years and younger, each year closer to 27 was associated with a progressively higher risk (OR:0.775). Beyond age 27, each additional year was associated with only a slight further increase in risk on this already elevated baseline (overall OR:1.025). This differs from A M Nybo Andersen’s population-based findings [3], who reported higher pregnancy loss rates among women aged > 30 years. The observed risk plateau beyond age 27 should be interpreted with caution, as the limited number of very advanced-age pregnancies potentially constrained the analysis. This pattern could be interpreted as either a biological threshold or a reflection of this sample limitation, requiring verification in larger studies.

Consistent with established trends, autosomal trisomy rates positively correlated with maternal age, while 45, XO and triploidy frequencies exhibited inverse age-dependent patterns, initially reported by Pendina AA [11]. Advanced maternal age exhibits a robust association with fetal chromosomal trisomies, consistent with established reproductive theories positing that aging compromises critical mechanisms governing meiotic chromosome segregation in human oocytes. This age-related meiotic errors predominantly manifest as chromosome non-disjunction events, culminating in genetically unbalanced gametes [11, 18]. However, the observed negative correlation between maternal age and the prevalence of 45, X challenges conventional paradigms of age-dependent chromosomal segregation failures, necessitating further investigation into alternative etiological mechanisms.

Furthermore, our stratified analysis revealed divergent maternal age patterns: no significant age difference existed between EM and NEM in chromosome trisomy cases, whereas 45, X demonstrated elevated maternal age in the EM. Coupled with our findings of null correlation between maternal age and EM overall, alongside the established positive association between maternal age and chromosome trisomy, we postulate that previously reported maternal age-EM correlations may stem from selection bias in cohort composition, specifically underrepresentation of non-abortive specimens. Crucially, this study systematically included all prenatal cytogenetic testing cases within the defined timeframe, ensuring enhanced population representativeness through comprehensive sampling of both EM and NEM pregnancies.

Fetuses with 45, X demonstrated characteristic clinical indications for genetic testing, including EM, severely increased NT (frequently accompanied by hydrops fetalis and cystic hygroma), aligning with prior findings [19–21]. For sex chromosome trisomies, primary indications were high-risk NIPT for SCAs, reflecting widespread clinical implementation of NIPT [14, 22]. Notably, we documented novel associations between sex chromosome trisomies and both markedly increased NT and structural/soft marker abnormalities, findings unreported in prior literature.

While no overall sex disparity existed between EM and NEM cases with autosomal aneuploidies, significant differences emerged in T21, T18, and non-T21/T18 subgroups—a previously undescribed phenomenon. Prenatal studies on mosaic 45, X are limited. In our cohort of 10 mosaic 45, X cases, two exhibited 45, X /XY. This distribution aligns with postnatal reports showing a 45, X /XX to 45, X /XY ratio of 205:19 [23], suggesting that X chromosome may exhibit higher susceptibility to loss compared to the Y chromosome during early development, necessitating rigorous mechanistic investigations in embryogenesis.

We observed a predominance of female conceptuses in EM, contrasting with the established male predominance at conception and their higher gestational attrition rates [24]. Supporting evidence shows an extreme male-to-female ratio (248:100) among fetuses surviving beyond 16–19 weeks, which sharply declines to 130:100 by week 20 and stabilizes near parity (100:100) after 36 weeks [25]. As our study focused on early pregnancy loss (< 12 weeks), the hypothesis of increased miscarriage probability for female conceptuses during this period requires further validation through expanded cohort studies accounting for potential confounding factors. Notably, after excluding all 45, X cases, no significant sex distribution difference existed between EM and non-EM groups. This pattern might indicate preferential Y-chromosome loss in EM with 45, X. However, evidence suggests 45, X viability critically depends on the parental origin of the retained X chromosome, with maternal-origin X conferring higher survival [26]. Thus, the relationship between fetal sex and early loss in 45, X conceptuses warrant further investigation.

Study Implications

This study employed all-cause prenatal genetic testing cohorts to establish a 2.07% population prevalence of sex chromosome aneuploidies, contributing to public health awareness. Comparative analysis revealed significantly higher rates of autosomal aneuploidies and 45, X in EM versus NEM, while sex chromosome trisomies demonstrated lower prevalence in EM. Although maternal age was elevated in the EM, no linear correlation existed between maternal age and EM rates, despite the established association of advanced maternal age with increased chromosomal aberration risks. This dissociation provides critical insights for addressing age-related fertility challenges. Notably, the observed inverse relationship between 45, X prevalence and maternal aging contradicts the established pattern of age-related chromosomal segregation errors in oocytes, offering clinically relevant evidence for fundamental research. Furthermore, our analysis of clinical manifestations associated with sex chromosome aneuploidies identified severely increased NT and early miscarriage as characteristic features of 45, X cases, whereas current prenatal detection of sex chromosome trisomies primarily relies on high-risk NIPT results and markedly increased NT, though the latter association requires further confirmation through larger case-control studies.

Study strengths and limitations

This study demonstrates several strengths. Notably, all chromosomal results were obtained following rigorous maternal blood contamination assessment, effectively eliminating confounding effects from maternal cells and ensuring high reliability of cytogenetic findings. Furthermore, the comparative analysis utilized comprehensive prenatal data from a unified time period, incorporating a more representative population sample compared to studies limited to postnatal data, thereby minimizing selection bias. Additionally, the investigation of sex chromosome aneuploidies incorporated parallel analyses of viable common trisomies such as trisomies 21, 18, and 13, while excluding other lethal autosomal aneuploidies, enhancing result generalizability and diagnostic context.

This was a retrospective observational study, and its findings should be interpreted with consideration of its inherent limitations. First, exclusion of cases with incomplete clinical data and the omission of normal pregnancies and fetuses undergoing solely chromosomal karyotyping may introduce potential selection bias. Second, the relatively small sample size of pregnancies in individuals aged ≥ 40 years, reflective of the overall lower pregnancy rate in this demographic, may compromise the precision of conclusions regarding advanced maternal age-associated chromosomal abnormalities and the relationship between maternal age and early miscarriage. Finally, this study did not incorporate adjustments for maternal and paternal confounders such as comorbidities or lifestyle behaviors; therefore, interpretations regarding the miscarriage-chromosomal abnormality relationship warrant caution due to potential residual confounding.

Conclusions

This observational study identified an overall prevalence of sex chromosome aneuploidies as 2.07% in the population, with specific distributions of 45, X /mosaic45, X (1.13%), XYY (0.27%), XXY (0.43%) and XXX/mosaic XXX (0.20%). Beyond sex chromosome trisomies, autosomal trisomies and particularly 45, X was found to be markedly elevated in the early miscarriage compared to non-early miscarriage. Additionally, an analysis of maternal age indicated a positive correlation with the prevalence of sex autosomal trisomies, whereas an inverse association was observed for 45, X. Notably, while the relationship between maternal age and early miscarriage risk in this cohort exhibited a non-linear pattern, a distinct age-dependent pattern was observed: early miscarriage rates increased with maternal age before 27 years, followed by stabilization without age-related fluctuations beyond this threshold.
