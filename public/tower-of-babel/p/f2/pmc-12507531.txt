Solving Diagnostic Dilemma in an Old Stroke Patient: A Case Study of Binswanger's Disease
Authors: Himel Kumar Biswas, Md Abdul Kader Zilani, Mussarat Rahman Khan, Mohammed Wahidur Rahman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBinswanger's disease (BD) is a rare vascular subcortical dementia characterized by small vessel ischemic changes in the white matter of the brain. The diagnosis is made from neurological and radiological study with discrimination from other cerebrovascular pathologies. Early diagnosis leads to effective treatment and follow‐up, both resulting in better disease management and outcome.

Introduction

Binswanger's disease (BD) is characterized by cerebrovascular changes in the hemispheral white matter, also known as subcortical arteriosclerotic encephalopathy (SAE) or subcortical leukoencephalopathy. It is an infrequently recognized dementia of the elderly involving cerebral white matter vessels and a diffuse subcortical demyelination, sparing the cerebral cortex [1]. It is a form of small vessel vascular dementia that may be caused by variable factors, mainly emphasizing chronic essential hypertension, long‐standing type 2 diabetes mellitus, previous cerebrovascular accidents, as well as old age [2]. This disease is characterized by the loss of executive function of the brain, which encompasses poor memory, intellectual disability, changes in mood, and mental deterioration [3, 4]. Neurological examination, as well as radiological evidence, is important to aid diagnosis with other cerebrovascular diseases. Solving diagnostic dilemmas leads to successful treatment and follow‐up with the disease prognosis.

Case History/Examination

A 69‐year‐old diabetic and hypertensive gentleman, with a previous history of acute ischemic stroke 3 years ago, presented with a two‐year history of dementia and tremor with vital signs (pulse‐67 bpm, Blood Pressure 130/70 mmHg, Temperature‐98.4F, Urine output‐Adequate, SpO2‐98% in room air) in our hospital. Recently, he experienced a new onset of gait disturbances, dysarthria, and decreased orientation and generalized weakness. Upon examination, the patient was diagnosed with sub‐cortical vascular dementia, specifically BD, based on clinical and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) findings.

Our patient's previous medical history includes poorly controlled type‐2 diabetes mellitus and hypertension, which both are significant risk factors for small vessel disease. Three years prior, he suffered from an acute ischemic stroke with partial recovery of motor function. Over the past 2 years, there has been a noticeable cognitive decline, with memory loss, impaired executive function, and the onset of tremors.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment)

The new symptoms such as gait disturbance, dysarthria, generalized weakness, and reduced orientation prompted further investigations. Neurological examination revealed bradykinesia, moderate cognitive impairment, and motor incoordination. Routine laboratory investigations were within normal limits except for elevated blood sugar (RBS‐12.7 mmol/L) and cholesterol levels (Table 1). An MRI of Brain (Figure 1) showed T2‐weighted FLAIR imaging demonstrated hyperintensities in the deep white matter, particularly in the periventricular regions, bilaterally and symmetrically; enlargement of the lateral ventricles which is consistent with subcortical atrophy. These findings denote the characteristic of the diagnosis of subcortical vascular dementia, consistent with BD.

The patient was started on a conservative management plan targeting his underlying vascular risk factors, including glycemic control, antihypertensive therapy, antiplatelet agents, and rehabilitation process to prevent further strokes. Cognitive rehabilitation and physical therapy were initiated to help with motor symptoms and cognitive deficits.

Through the progressive nature of BD, the prognosis is generally poor. However, controlling vascular risk factors may slow disease progression. The patient was regularly followed up closely to monitor for further cognitive and functional decline.

Conclusion and Result

BD, though rare, should be considered in elderly patients with a history of stroke and risk factors such as diabetes and hypertension who present with cognitive and motor decline, helping to establish a diagnosis and solve a diagnostic dilemma. Early diagnosis through radiological and clinical evaluation is essential for management, although it has a poor prognosis. Comprehensive care, focusing on controlling underlying vascular risk factors, may improve quality of life and slow further disease progression.

Discussion

White matter pathology associated with arteriolosclerosis was reported for the first time in 1904, but received little attention as autopsies in the elderly were rarely performed, limiting confirmation of the observations made by Otto Binswanger [5]. In Binswanger's encephalopathy, white matter pathology is commonly accompanied by ventricular enlargement and lacunar infarctions within the basal ganglia and thalamus [6]. Gross pathological examination demonstrates variable degrees of white matter involvement, which may remain limited to superficial and subcortical regions or extend deeper into the periventricular areas. On microscopic evaluation, two distinct forms of capillaropathy are typically identified, predominantly affecting subcortical structures: obstruction of the vascular lumen and capillary stagnation secondary to chronic retrograde venous outflow, a condition that sustains persistently elevated intravascular filling. Both mechanisms contribute to diffuse ischemia of the subcortical white matter and promote the development of multiple pericapillary microinfarcts.

This resulting cognitive decline and dementia, which are largely explained by a “disconnection syndrome,” whereby subcortical white matter injury disrupts cortical–subcortical neural pathways [7]. In addition, dysfunction of the basal ganglia and thalamus further exacerbates the clinical manifestations, leading to the characteristic neurocognitive and neurobehavioral deficits associated with Binswanger's disease [8].

This disease is a form of vascular dementia which is commonly associated with long‐standing diabetes, hypertension and previous cerebrovascular events [1, 9, 10]. Our patient's history of diabetes, hypertension, and previous stroke was a significant contributing factor. The hyperintensities on T2‐FLAIR images, combined with the patient's clinical presentation, were key to making the diagnosis [11]. Diagnosing Binswanger's disease necessitates a comprehensive, multimodal strategy. Isolated biomarkers are insufficient for definitive diagnosis. However, integrating clinical data with imaging and auxiliary tests can be beneficial for patients presenting with cognitive impairment and neurological symptoms of uncertain or unknown origin [12].

Sub‐cortical vascular dementia presents with cognitive decline, motor symptoms (e.g., tremor, gait disturbance), and psychiatric features [13]. The cognitive decline is often gradual but can be aggressive with sudden deteriorations after small strokes [1]. In this case, the patient's stroke 3 years prior, along with poorly controlled vascular risk factors, likely contributed to the progression of the disease.
