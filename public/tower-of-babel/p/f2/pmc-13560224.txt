Performance of 4 artificial intelligence chatbots in responding to multiple choice questions in operative dentistry
Authors: Thilla Sekar Vinothkumar, Syed Nahid Basheer, Sabari Murugesan, Abdulaziz Algadhi, Nassreen Hassan Albar, Saleem Abdulrab, Mohammed Sultan Alak’hali, Esam Halboub
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe accuracy and consistency of artificial intelligence (AI) based chatbots and their dependability in the field of dental education are questionable. This study was aimed to evaluate the performance of four different chatbots in answering multiple-choice questions (MCQs) in operative dentistry.MethodsRelying on textbooks in operative dentistry, a three-membered panel of experts developed 150 MCQs, which a fourth expert screened to yield a final 110 MCQs. These questions were input into GPT-4o, Grok 3, Gemini Advanced and Claude 3.7 Sonnet in two rounds with a gap of one-week interval. The proportion of correct answers reflected the performance of these chatbots. Inter- and intra-chatbot consistencies were analysed using the McNemar test and Cohen’s Kappa.ResultsIn the first round, Grok 3 and Gemini Advanced answered 86.4% of the MCQs correctly, while GPT-4o and Claude 3.7 Sonnet answered 85.5% correctly. In the second round, the performance of GPT-4o and Claude 3.7 Sonnet improved, answering 87.3% and 91.8%, respectively. Intra-chatbot consistency ranged from fair (Kappa = 0.33) for Claude 3.7 Sonnet to substantial for GPT-4o. Inter-chatbot consistency ranged from 0.34 to 0.54 in the first round and 0.44 to 0.66 in the second round.ConclusionThe assessed chatbots showed promising performance in answering MCQs in operative dentistry and improved over time. The assessed chatbots can be used as adjuncts in the education process of operative dentistry while carefully considering their inherent limitations.Clinical RelevanceDetermining the accuracy and, consequently, the dependability of the most widely used AI-based chatbots in responding to dental queries is essential for dental students. Dental students must interpret chatbots’ responses with caution and use them as supplementary tools alongside the standard resources such as textbooks and guidance from mentors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08276-9.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Chatbots, Multiple choice questions, Dental education, Operative dentistry

Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is a branch of computer science that focuses on developing systems capable of performing tasks typically requiring human intelligence, such as comprehending natural language, problem-solving, and reasoning [1]. AI systems utilize large datasets and complex algorithms to learn from data and generate predictions or choices, thereby mimicking human cognitive processes [1, 2]. AI-driven systems are increasingly implemented in various medical specializations, enhancing diagnostic accuracy and optimizing treatment protocols [3, 4]. In dentistry, studies have shown that deep learning algorithms have surpassed traditional methods in interpreting dental radiographs, resulting in enhanced accuracy in diagnosing conditions such as dental caries and periodontal diseases [5].

The integration of AI into dental education is a rapidly evolving field that promises to revolutionize the dental students’ training [6]. Chatbots are tools powered by AI and large language models (LLMs) that uses advanced natural language processing (NLP) technologies to interpret, understand, and respond to human language [7, 8]. Generally, they can be categorized as “rule-based” and “AI-based” chatbots, which can participate in constrained and unconstrained human-to-brain-like conversations, respectively [9]. Kavadella et al. [10] used ChatGPT to educate undergraduate dental students, highlighting that it efficiently engaged the students and enhanced their learning experience. Surveys have shown that dental students use chatbots as their learning companions to find answers to problems, especially while preparing for exams [11].

One of the most frequently used chatbots is ChatGPT (Chat Generative Pre-Trained Transformer; OpenAI, San Francisco, USA), an advanced conversational model that utilizes AI to generate instant, human-like responses [12]. ChatGPT's latest versions, such as GPT-4o, demonstrate enhancements in understanding and generating human-like text, based on extensive datasets that incorporate text from books, articles, and internet sources [13]. Grok 3 is a sophisticated AI advancement that employs a transformer-based architecture typical of many modern large language models, utilizing self-attention mechanisms to process input data more effectively and understand the context [14]. A recent study reported that Grok 3 achieved a notable accuracy of 93% when evaluated against multiple-choice questions on blood physiology, demonstrating its reliability in medically relevant inquiries [14].

Gemini Advanced is an advanced LLM developed by Google, distinguished by its multimodal intelligence, robust safety protocols, and application-specific optimization, particularly within medical contexts [15]. Gemini Advanced employs an integrated multimodal AI approach, that incorporates both language processing capabilities and visual data interpretation [15]. Meanwhile, Claude 3.7 Sonnet (Anthropic) is a decoder-only LLM model optimized to generate text based on given prompts rather than interpreting input from distinct sources [16].

Using AI to simulate realistic clinical scenarios, enabled dental hygiene students to apply their theoretical knowledge, thereby enhancing learning outcomes [17]. Generally, when responding via chatbots, users prefer multiple-choice questions (MCQs) over traditional open-ended questions, as the former reduces cognitive load and allows for more focused engagement with specific knowledge areas [18, 19]. While the effectiveness of chatbots in answering MCQs in dental diagnostics and oral pathology is promising, challenges remain, particularly regarding their limited ability in understanding nuanced clinical contexts [20, 21]. A recent study compared the performance of four different chatbots in answering MCQs in endodontics. It concluded that further training and development are required to improve their accuracy and reliability in this field [22]. A scoping review on the application of LLMs in dental education concluded that this remains in its early stage and emphasized the importance of establishing standardized and technically sound guidelines for integrating LLM in dental education [23].

A preliminary study compared the effects of free and paid versions of ChatGPT on the validity, reliability, and authenticity of overall 20 MCQs and 20 open-ended clinical questions in operative dentistry and endodontics for undergraduate dentistry education and found the values to be comparable [24]. Nevertheless, one must be prudent in accepting the answers provided by chatbots, especially in life sciences. Various matters of concern include security, chatbot dependability, uneven response times, and the possibility of sharing inaccurate information due to incorrect responses [25].

In general, chatbots are seen as reliable information sources and have transformed digital communication [26]. Therefore, it is crucial to determine the accuracy and consistency of AI-based chatbots in answering questions correctly to advise students and educators about the reliability of the most popular chatbots. Moreover, previous studies were not conducted exclusively in the specialty of operative dentistry to assess the capability of multiple chatbots in answering sizeable number of MCQs. Hence, this study was planned to evaluate and compare the performance of four chatbots in answering MCQs related to various topics in operative dentistry. The study compared the accuracy and consistency of various chatbots upon answering the most common assessment tool (MCQ) used in undergraduate dentistry education.

Methods

Study design and approval

This study was a cross-sectional design performed based on the responses from a publicly available software platform with no direct involvement of human or animal subjects, nor breach in data privacy. Hence, informed consent and ethical approval were not required as per the institutional and national standards.

Multiple choice questions development

A three-membered panel of academicians and clinical staff specialized in operative dentistry developed 150 MCQs in consensus. These professionals were selected based on their contributions to the subject, more than 10 years of teaching experience, and academic credentials. Each MCQ had four alternatives as potential answers, with only one being the correct answer. Every question was thoughtfully selected from the preclinical and clinical syllabi prescribed for the third-, fourth-, and fifth-year students of undergraduate dental program to reflect a variety of difficulty levels and important topics in the domain of operative dentistry. Another academician and consultant critically revised these 150 MCQs and removed 40 MCQs due to doubts and uncertainty. The remaining 110 MCQs were discussed among the panel and the consultant for final approval. The references used for developing these MCQs were Sturdevant’s Art and Science of Operative Dentistry (Sixth [27] and Seventh edition [28]) and Fundamentals of Operative Dentistry—A Contemporary Approach (Fourth edition [29]). The MCQs and their correct answers were included as a (Supplementary Material 1).

AI language models

The subscribed versions of the utilized chatbots were accessed through the major application programming interface (API), aiming to replicate real-world scenarios. A new chat was started for each MCQ in any of the used chatbots. In brief, GPT-4o (OpenAI) was accessed via its API at https://openai.com/chatgpt/. Grok 3 (xAI) was accessed via the X application on an iOS device. The API of Gemini Advanced (Google AI), developed by Google, was accessed using Gemini application on an iOS device. The chat session was reset before submitting each new MCQ. Claude 3.7 Sonnet (Anthropic) was accessed via the Claude mobile application on an iOS device. The MCQs were input to the selected chatbots on the same day (May 1, 2025), and this was considered the first round of obtaining the responses. This exact procedure was repeated one week later and was considered the second round of obtaining the responses. A single operator (XX) administered the chatbots in both rounds.

Statistical analysis

Data were input into an Excel sheet, from which they were imported for analyses into IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). As the outcomes were qualitative, they were expressed as frequencies and percentages. The outcomes were paired in nature (the same questions were assessed by the same chatbots on two different occasions, and assessed using different chatbots at a single time point). Hence, the data were analysed for potential discordance/disagreement (within and between groups) using the McNemar test, while the overall agreement was assessed using Cohen’s Kappa test. Cohen's Kappa statistic has values between −1 and 1, where 1 represents perfect agreement and −1 represents the opposite. Cohen's Kappa values less than 0.2 represent slight agreement, values from 0.2 to 0.4 represent fair agreement, values from 0.41 to 0.6 represent moderate agreement, values from 0.61 to 0.8 represent substantial agreement, and values greater than 0.8 represent great (perfect) agreement [30]. The P value was set at < 0.05.

Results

Figure 1 and Table 1 summarize the results of the different chatbots in both rounds. Overall, 33 questions were answered incorrectly by at least one chatbot. The performance of the chatbots in round 1 was close to one another. Grok 3 and Gemini Advanced answered 95 questions correctly (86.4%) each, while GPT-4o and Claude 3.7 Sonnet answered 94 questions correctly (85.5%) each. In the second round, the performance of GPT-4o and Claude 3.7 Sonnet improved; the former answered 96 questions (87.3%) and the latter answered 101 questions (91.8%). In contrast, Gemini Advanced deteriorated, answering 94 questions (85.5%), while Grok 3 remained steady with 95 correct answers (86.4%).

Discussion

Various domains under the ‘applications of LLMs in dental education’ include dental examination, education information resources, perception of LLM in dental education, assessing student’s work, preclinical support, generation of test question, and virtual patient [23]. The current study evaluated the performance of four AI models, namely GPT-4o, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, Grok 3 and Gemini Advanced, in answering MCQs on operative dentistry for the undergraduate dental education thereby targeting the dental examination. Basically, preclinical training, clinical practice, and specialized training are the usual stages in the planned progression of dental education and LLMs have showed promising performance in offering broad support throughout these stages [23]. Hence the questions from all the three stages of dental education were included in the current study.

AI is rapidly transforming healthcare, medical, and dental education by enhancing diagnostic accuracy, optimizing treatment plans, and improving student learning outcomes [6, 18, 25]. A recent study found that chatbots with natural language processing (NLP) skills might successfully guide students and answer typical questions in neuroscience [31]. Such technologies not only increase knowledge but also minimize stress on dental students by providing instant answers to MCQs in a variety of medical and dental settings [20, 31]. Given the type of questions (MCQs) tested, the present study is currently among the most thorough evaluations of chatbots' accuracy and consistency in operative dentistry [26]. Chatbots were chosen based on their popularity or how well they performed on the benchmarking. Overall, the percentages of the correct answers in our study ranged from 85.5% to 91.8%, irrespective of the round.

In this study, Claude 3.7 Sonnet correctly answered the highest rate of questions (91.8%) in the second round, although its performance was similar to that of other chatbots in the first round. A previous study yielded similar results while comparing four different AI-driven LLMs (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, GPT-3.5 & GPT-4–1106, Copilot, and Gemini 1.5 Flash) with students in accurately answering United States Medical Licensing Examination-style MCQs, [31]. Notably, the self-training of Claude 3.7 Sonnet focuses on the efficient utilisation of both general and domain-specific datasets; it emphasizes providing contextually appropriate responses through techniques like retrieval-augmented generation, which retrieves relevant information to support inference and decision-making [32]. Previous research reported that Claude 3.5 Sonnet excels over many other AI models in medical examinations by performing similarly to some human practitioners in generating accurate discharge summaries for patients with renal insufficiency [33].

In the present study, the overall consistency was almost perfect for ChatGPT-4o. Several studies have assessed how accurately AI chatbots could answer medical and dental questions [18, 34, 35]. In a pilot study comparing Google Bard, ChatGPT4, ChatGPT 3.5, Llama, Sage, Claude 2 100 k, Claude-instant, Claude-instant-100 k, and Google Palm, chatbots answered 78% true-or-false questions in the field of paediatric dentistry covering a wide range of topics, reflecting a lower accuracy when compared to human clinicians (96.67%). Notably, the assessed chatbots, except ChatGPT-3.5, demonstrated acceptable consistency, suggesting their stability in responding to multiple queries in paediatric dentistry [35]. Abdulrab et al. [22] compared ChatGPT-4o, MedGebra GPT-4o, Gemini Advanced and Meta Llama 3 in answering endodontic MCQs over two rounds and found higher rates of correct answers by ChatGPT-4o and MedGebra GPT-4o. Another study evaluated ChatGPT-4's responses to the frequently asked questions about dental amalgam in accordance with the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) criteria. The ChatGPT-4's responses were consistent with those of the FDA, underscoring reasonable accuracy and reliability [36]. Although the chatbot can deliver accurate information, opinions on specific recommendations may differ, indicating complexities in dental therapeutic guidance [36], which might misguide the students.

The idea behind conducting two rounds of assessment of these chatbots was to evaluate their consistency over time, in addition to accuracy. Interestingly, Claude 3.7 Sonnet showed the best improvement, followed to a lesser extent by ChatGPT-4o. While Grok 3 remained unchanged, Gemini Advanced deteriorated. Such results suggest that, although it is assumed that these chatbots are self-updating and their performance would improve over time, this is not always true. These findings align with those of earlier research, which reported increased accuracy of ChatGpt-4o in the second round (90%) for answering MCQs in the speciality of Endodontics [22]. Similarly, Goodman et al. found that ChatGPT improved significantly over an 8–17 days period in the context of answering medical queries [37]. Such a trend of improved performance over time can be attributed to the ongoing updates and refinements of algorithms, as well as the repetitive user feedback through reinforcement learning. Moreover, the large and diverse training dataset allows the chatbots to understand the nuances and complexities of medical language and concepts [37]. Another possible cause is that the knowledge-based datasets associated with the chatbot are constantly updated. These findings underscore the importance of continually updating and refining the datasets of LLM models to stay current with the latest breakthroughs in NLP [38, 39]. Eventually, the learning achievement, knowledge retention, explicit reasoning, and learning interest of chatbots will be improved by their respective developers, which in turn will aid the students in fostering these skills [18, 23, 40]. The educators or students should infer the output of AI-based chatbots with caution by verifying them with the standard resources like textbooks. The chatbots need to evolve further by constantly self-updating themselves from the authentic resources.

Intra-chatbot reliability revealed substantial to perfect consistency for all chatbots except Claude 3.7 Sonnet. That is, as mentioned earlier, because the performance of Claude 3.7 Sonnet improved significantly in the second round, unlike the other chatbots, which either improved very slightly, remained constant, or even deteriorated, albeit slightly. In contrast, the inter-chatbots' reliability revealed moderate to substantial consistency, likely reflecting the different technologies used in developing, self-learning, and updating these chatbots. The training of Grok 3 undergoes a two-phase process—pre-training followed by fine-tuning [14]. In the pre-training phase, Grok 3 is exposed to a diverse and extensive dataset in contrast to specific dataset related to the dental specialty. Fine-tuning is crucial for models like Grok 3, enabling it to specialize in particular tasks by training on more targeted datasets related to areas such as medical and dental terminology or other subject matter expertise [14]. This feature could be the probable reason for its inadequate improvement over the course of one week. Similarly, regarding the differential diagnosis, research has highlighted that occasional inaccuracies do occur with Gemini Advanced when producing comprehensive lists of answers [41].

It is worth noting that 33 questions were answered incorrectly by at least one chatbot, irrespective of the round. This number represents 30% of the questions, which is a substantial percentage, underscoring the risk that learning students may face when utilizing such chatbots. Furthermore, the lower reliability between these chatbots in answering MCQs jeopardizes the learning process when they give different answers to a given question. This finding is consistent with prior research, which mentioned that inaccurate responses from generative AI might confuse medical students, causing them to learn incorrect medical facts or concepts [14]. Regarding this concern, it is crucial to thoroughly validate AI tools, instruct students in the critical evaluation of AI outputs, and ensure that human experts supervise the content produced by AI [42].

Generally, these chatbots excel in different domains such as reasoning speed, reasoning approach, or coding. Moreover, other factors like cost of subscription, utility, real-time data from social media platform (X-Twitter) play a major role in differentiating them. On the other hand, the LLMs pose technical and application related challenges, especially inaccurate information, potential harm to student’s knowledge and ability, etc. [23].

Strengths and limitations

By choosing these four models for comparison on the most common assessment tool (MCQ), we intended to identify the chatbot with optimal accuracy and consistency that best suit the workflow of an undergraduate dental education. Another strength is varying the difficulty level of the MCQs widely encompassing almost the entire undergraduate curriculum in operative dentistry. The study used a controlled approach by employing a predetermined set of questions to compare how well the four chatbots answered. While this study provides insights into the performance of the chosen AI chatbots in operative dentistry, the results are neither generalizable to the present or future AI chatbots due to the limited breadth of AI programs tested, nor to the types of questions. Another limitation of this study is the evaluation of a MCQs alone in just two rounds without involving open-ended or image-based questions. Moreover, the time interval between the two rounds was short (one week), which is likely insufficient for assessing improvement in chatbots’ performance. However, it is proposed that these chatbots are constantly self-updated. This study evaluated the most popular chatbots, using single best answers in MCQs. Performance might vary with other question formats, such as true or false questions, case-based scenarios, or open-ended responses. LLMs undergo frequent updates, resulting in changes in their performance over time. Further periodic investigations are required using different types of questions in multiple rounds to validate the results obtained in this study. Moreover, additional research is needed to evaluate the models' dependability and clinical significance using a higher number of questions with different formats and increasing their difficulty levels.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study found that the performance of the assessed chatbots in answering MCQs in Operative Dentistry was nearly identical in the first round. As far as the accuracy is concerned, Claude 3.7 Sonnet surpassed that of the other chatbots while the consistency of ChatGpt-4o was almost perfect. With the continuous evolution of technology, chatbots are increasingly playing supportive roles in education, including undergraduate dental education by helping students to understand the subject and substitute conventional literature sources. However, even the best-performing models made errors, emphasizing the need for caution when utilizing them as learning aids. Ultimately, AI chatbots should not be considered a substitute for human mentors and textbooks but rather as a beneficial supplement to their teaching efforts.
