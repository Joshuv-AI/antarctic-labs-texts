Clinical Outcomes of Balloon Pulmonary Angioplasty in Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Hypertension: A Retrospective Cohort Study From a Latin American Care Center

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) is a distinct subtype of pulmonary hypertension arising from persistent major pulmonary artery obstruction after pulmonary embolism. Pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA) remains the standard of care, but up to 40% of patients in European and US registries are inoperable; balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA) has emerged as a more accessible interventional modality for suitable patients. This retrospective single‐center cohort study evaluated functional improvement, hemodynamic response, and mortality in patients with CTEPH ineligible for PEA who underwent BPA at a Colombian institution (May 2022–October 2025). Risk was stratified using COMPERA 2.0. The primary outcome was improvement of at least one New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class; secondary outcomes were change in mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) and all‐cause mortality during follow‐up. Of 33 patients (median age 63 years; 70% female), 5 (15.1%) were high risk at baseline, 16 (48.4%) intermediate‐high, 10 (30.3%) intermediate‐low, and 2 (6%) low risk. After BPA, 19 patients (58%) improved NYHA class (95% CI, 39.2%–74.5%), and mPAP fell from 60 (IQR 50–68) to 40 mmHg (IQR 37–48) after a median of three sessions (IQR 2–4) (mean paired reduction 14.05 mmHg, 95% credible interval 9.81–18.07; Hedges’ g = 1.22, 95% CI 0.77–1.66). Two deaths (6.0%) occurred, both in high‐risk patients. BPA improved NYHA class and pulmonary hemodynamics in severe, inoperable CTEPH, with a low complication rate despite advanced baseline disease. COMPERA 2.0 stratified baseline severity but did not predict the magnitude of hemodynamic response to BPA, consistent with the mechanical nature of the disease.Keywords: cardiac catheterization, hemodynamics, pulmonary artery, pulmonary embolism, vascular resistance

Introduction

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) represents a distinct subset of pulmonary hypertension (PH) caused by mechanical obstruction of the major pulmonary arteries by organized fibrotic tissue [1]. Although pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA) remains the definitive treatment for eligible patients, restoring patency of the pulmonary vascular tree and offering excellent long‐term survival, approximately 40% of patients reported in registries are not candidates for this surgical intervention due to technical complexity, comorbidities, restricted PEA access, or patient preference [2, 3]. In this context, balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA) has gained attention.

The BPA technique was initially described in 1988 [4, 5, 6]. The procedure has demonstrated both efficacy and safety in the RACE trial, the MR‐BPA trial, the ICA BPA registry, and in studies involving elderly patients [7, 8, 9, 10]. However, non‐inferiority has not yet been established, as the GO‐CTPEH study is still ongoing [11]. It is currently an option for patients with inoperable disease, residual disease following PEA, predominantly distal disease, and in symptomatic chronic thromboembolic disease in the absence of PH [1]. Despite the growing international adoption of BPA and its recent upgrade to Class I, Level B in the European Society of Cardiology guidelines, several significant gaps remain in our understanding of patient outcomes across different clinical settings [12, 13]. Most published BPA series originate from single, high‐volume centers, predominantly in Asia and Europe, which limits the generalizability of findings to other health systems and populations [3]. Although defined treatment goals have been established in European and U.S. consensus statements, definitions of technical success and primary endpoints still vary widely across studies, often favoring hemodynamic surrogates over patient‐centered outcomes, thereby hindering meaningful cross‐study comparisons [14, 15]. Furthermore, data on outcomes and survival predictors in patients undergoing BPA remain limited, particularly in contemporary cohorts managed with current risk‐stratification tools such as the COMPERA 2.0 assessment. Notably, evidence from Latin American (LATAM) populations remains limited, representing an important and underexplored gap in the existing literature [16, 17, 18].

We report our institutional experience with BPA in patients with severe CTEPH deemed ineligible for PEA at a tertiary‐care center in Colombia. In this real‐world cohort from an underrepresented LATAM setting, we evaluated the functional, hemodynamic, and safety outcomes of BPA, as well as survival, and examined risk stratification according to COMPERA 2.0.

Methods

Study Design

This study analyzes a retrospective, single‐center cohort of both inpatients and outpatients with CTEPH who underwent BPA at our tertiary‐care center in Colombia, South America. The objective was to characterize the demographic and clinical profiles, as well as functional outcomes and mortality of this population. The study was approved and supervised by the Centro de Investigaciones Clínicas (CIC) of our institution.

Methodological Framework and Reporting Guidelines

This study was designed within the framework of fundamental prognosis research, as proposed in the Prognosis Research Strategy (PROGRESS 1) [19]. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [20].

Participants

This database included ambulatory and hospitalized patients aged 18 years and older with a confirmed diagnosis of CTEPH, according to current international guidelines [12]. Patient recruitment was conducted retrospectively, covering the period from May 2022 to October 2025. We included patients with CTEPH who completed the BPA treatment protocol, including hemodynamic and functional assessments, were evaluated in a multidisciplinary PH meeting, and were not considered for PEA. Patients were excluded for this analysis if they met any of the following criteria: (a) incomplete medical records, or (b) missing baseline assessment.

Institutional Patient Selection Process

A multidisciplinary PH team meeting is held weekly at our institution to evaluate patients with CTEPH and to determine the appropriate therapeutic strategy. This multidisciplinary team includes pulmonologists, cardiologists, intensive care specialists, cardiovascular surgeons, thoracic radiologists, and interventional cardiologists. Patient selection is based on an integrated assessment of clinical data and standardized review of diagnostic studies, including transthoracic echocardiography, ventilation–perfusion scintigraphy, pulmonary computed tomography (CT) angiography, and pulmonary arteriography. According to current evidence, PEA is considered the first‐line treatment [12]. BPA is reserved for patients deemed technically inoperable or for those with persistent or recurrent PH after PEA, as well as selected patients with severe disease.

There is substantial experience with PEA at our institution. During the same period in which this cohort was enrolled (May 2022 to October 2025), a total of 36 patients were evaluated by the multidisciplinary PH team and underwent PEA by the cardiovascular team.

Balloon Pulmonary Angioplasty ‐ Technical Description

For BPA, vascular access was achieved via the femoral or jugular vein under strict echocardiographic guidance. After gaining access through a short 6‐Fr sheath, we selectively cannulate the target segment using a guiding catheter, usually a 6‐Fr multipurpose MPA or MPB guiding catheter with side holes (APT Medical), telescoped through a 70 cm 8‐Fr Flexor introducer (Cook Medical), which provides better support. Low‐profile coronary angioplasty semi‐compliant balloons (2–5 mm) mounted on 0.014‐inch guidewires targeted segmental and subsegmental branches, with a maximum balloon/vessel ratio of 0.8/1.

Angiographic findings guided lesion selection and, in selected cases, pulmonary CT angiography and pulmonary perfusion helped identify target vessels to revascularize areas with the most significant perfusion defects. When addressing lesions in proximal branches with a higher diameter (6–10 mm), we first use small coronary balloons. Then we progress to peripheral angioplasty balloons mounted on 0.018‐inch or 0.034‐inch guidewires, generally adding fusion angiographic/CT‐scan images to control the angioplasty site, especially when the peripheral balloon cannot pass freely through the guiding catheter due to its size. It has to be advanced through the 70 cm sheath (Figure 1).

BPA was staged across multiple sessions (typically 3–4), with a minimum interval of 2 days between procedures. Due to their greater physiological impact, lower lobe vessels are prioritized, with the right lung taking precedence over the left. Segment selection followed this systematic angiographic and perfusion‐guided protocol rather than operator preference; the predominance of A10/A8 treatment reflects this prioritization. We initially treated type A (ring‐like) and type B (web) lesions with near‐complete success, and type C (subtotal occlusions) with an 86.5% success rate. By comparison, type D (total occlusions) and type E (tortuous) lesions have success rates of 52.2% and 63.6%, respectively, and carry a higher risk of complications. Because of this higher risk, these latter lesion types were avoided, especially in patients with poor pulmonary hemodynamics [21]. We performed as many sessions as necessary to treat all types A, B, and C lesions present in each patient.

Data Collection

Information was collected from each participant's electronic medical records. Sociodemographic data, comorbidities, and medical history were recorded at the initial assessment. Baseline functional evaluation included 6‐min walk distance (6MWD) and New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class. Laboratory parameters included complete blood count, comprehensive metabolic panel, and N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide (NTproBNP) levels. Echocardiographic measurements comprised left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), right atrial area, right ventricular basal diameter, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion, and estimated systolic pulmonary arterial pressure. Hemodynamic parameters were obtained through right heart catheterization and included mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP), pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), cardiac output and cardiac index, and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure.

Treatment variables included the total number of BPA sessions per patient, the number of pulmonary artery lesions treated, the specific pulmonary artery branches targeted using standardized segmental anatomical nomenclature (A1–A10 segmental arteries), and preprocedural and postprocedural hemodynamic measurements.

Follow‐up data included vital status, NYHA functional class, hemodynamic parameters, oxygen requirement, anticoagulation therapy, and PH medications. These data were obtained from follow‐up visit records and the most recent invasive right heart catheterization. For patients who were unable to attend in‐person follow‐up visits, such as those living in other cities or regions of the country, clinical information was collected via telephone interviews as part of the routine follow‐up program. Although data collection was originally planned for a 1‐year follow‐up period, challenges in data acquisition led to an analysis including data with a median follow‐up of 1.6 years (interquartile range [IQR] 0.3–2.9) and a mean of 1.7 years (standard deviation [SD] 0.7). All data were securely stored using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) platform.

Risk Stratification

Patients were categorized using the COMPERA 2.0 four‐stratum risk assessment tool, which was developed and validated in pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) and has not been formally validated as a prognostic or predictive instrument in CTEPH [12, 16]. Non‐invasive variables analogous to those in COMPERA have nonetheless shown prognostic value for survival in CTEPH populations, including medically managed cohorts, cohorts treated with BPA, and a large multicenter registry in which the four‐stratum model retained statistically significant, though modest, discriminative value specifically within the group 4 subgroup [17, 22, 23]. In the absence of a risk‐stratification tool developed specifically for CTEPH, we used COMPERA 2.0 solely as a standardized descriptor of baseline clinical severity within our cohort. Given the sample size, this study was neither designed nor powered to develop or validate a predictive model of hemodynamic or functional response to BPA, which represents a distinct methodological objective pursued by other groups using larger, dedicated cohorts [18]. Furthermore, COMPERA 2.0 was not used to select candidates for the procedure; this decision was based exclusively on the anatomical and angiographic characteristics of the lesions, as assessed by the multidisciplinary team. Each variable (functional class, 6MWD, and NT‐proBNP) was assigned a score from 1 to 4 points, and the risk score for each patient was calculated as the mean of all point values. Risk categories were defined as follows: low risk, < 1.50 points; intermediate‐low risk, 1.50–2.49 points; intermediate‐high risk, 2.50–3.49 points; and high risk, ≥ 3.50 points.

Outcomes Measures

The primary outcome was NYHA functional class improvement, defined as a decrease of at least one NYHA functional class from baseline to the final follow‐up assessment, measured at least 1 month after the last BPA. Secondary outcomes included hemodynamic improvement assessed by change in mPAP from baseline, all‐cause mortality during the follow‐up period, and procedural safety outcomes. Hemodynamic reassessment was ideally performed via right heart catheterization at 3–6 months after the last BPA session; when this follow‐up catheterization was not available, the most recent hemodynamic measurement obtained during the final BPA session was used instead. We recorded both non‐specific and procedure‐related (thoracic) complications.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Continuous variables were presented as medians with IQRs for non‐normally distributed data. Categorical variables were expressed as absolute frequencies and percentages. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms.

Baseline characteristics were compared across COMPERA 2.0 risk categories using the Kruskal–Wallis H test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, due to small expected cell counts in some risk groups. Functional outcome measures were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. NYHA functional class comparisons were performed using the Pearson chi‐square test when adequate expected cell frequencies were present.

Paired changes in mPAP were assessed with a paired‐samples Student's t‐test using only complete pre and post–data. Effect size was estimated with Hedges’ g* and its 95% confidence interval. A Bayesian paired t‐test with a Cauchy prior on the standardized effect size was also conducted. We report the log Bayes factor (BF01) and the posterior mean pre–post difference with its 95% credible interval. Analyses and visualizations followed the conventions of the ggstatsplot R package [24, 25].

All statistical tests were two‐sided, and statistical significance was defined as a p‐value less than 0.05. Missing data were addressed using complete case analysis; no imputation methods were applied. Sample sizes reflect the actual number of observations available for each analysis. The extent of missing data ranged from 1 to 14 observations per variable, and specific sample sizes were reported for each analysis to ensure transparency.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) via RStudio 2024.12.1 + 563. Data visualizations were created using R and BioRender (BioRender. com).

Ethical Considerations

The study complies with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Comité de Ética en Investigación Biomédica (CEIB), the institutional review board (IRB) of FVL (Approval No. 2024.055; May 2, 2024). As the coordinating institution, the CEIB of FVL waived the requirement for patient consent, given that no additional interventions were planned for the participants and all information was obtained from medical records.

Results

69 patients with CTEPH were evaluated by the multidisciplinary PH team during the study period; of those, 33 met the inclusion criteria and underwent BPA; all of them were included in the final analysis. A total of 36 patients who were managed with PEA were excluded. No patients received combined management (PEA and BPA), and no cases were excluded due to missing critical data (Figure 2).

Procedural Details

A total of 128 BPA sessions were performed across 33 patients, with a median of three sessions per patient (IQR: 2–4). The median number of lesions treated per patient was 12 (IQR: 10–20). The A10 and A8 segments were the most frequently treated, in 91% and 82% of patients, respectively. Treatment of the A6 segment was more frequently treated among high‐risk patients (80%) than among other groups (p = 0.018) (Table 1). Supporting Information S1: Figure S1 illustrates the pattern of the four most commonly treated pulmonary vascular segments (A10, A8, A9, and A4). 11 patients received treatment in all four segments. Four patients underwent combined treatment of A8 and A10 without treatment of A4 or A9.

Low‐risk patients underwent more BPA sessions (Median: 5.5, IQR: 5–6, p = 0.18) and had a higher number of total lesions treated (Median: 28, IQR: 16–30, p = 0.37) compared with high risk patients (Median: 17, IQR: 12‐20). One patient required mechanical circulatory support with femoro‐femoral veno‐arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation with a blood flow rate of 2 L/min, gas flow of 2 L/min, and an FiO2 of 100%, because the first BPA attempt was not tolerated due to profound systemic hypotension associated with right heart dysfunction documented by transesophageal echocardiography, and despite the use of inhaled nitric oxide, milrinone, noradrenaline, and vasopressin.

Functional Outcomes

A total of 19 patients (58%) demonstrated an improvement of at least one NYHA functional class at follow‐up (95% CI, 39.2%–74.5%). Baseline NYHA class distribution differed significantly between patients who improved and those who did not (p = 0.001). No patients with baseline NYHA functional class II showed improvement (0 of 7), whereas 79% of patients who improved had baseline NYHA functional class III, and 21% had baseline NYHA functional class IV (Table 2 and Figure 3). The largest transition originated from baseline NYHA functional class III (n = 21), with notable patterns of improvement. All patients with baseline NYHA functional class IV demonstrated some degree of improvement. In contrast, none of the seven patients with baseline NYHA functional class II improved (Table 2).

Hemodynamic Outcomes

mPAP decreased from a median of 60 mmHg (IQR: 50–68) before the first BPA session to 40 mmHg (IQR: 37–48) after the final BPA session, representing a median within‐patient reduction of 13 mmHg (IQR: 10–23) (mean paired reduction 14.05 mmHg, 95% credible interval 9.81–18.07; Hedges’ g = 1.22, 95% CI 0.77–1.66). PVR similarly decreased from a median of 13.0 Wood units (WU) (IQR: 11.0–18.6) to 9.0 WU (IQR: 7.0–17.0), with a median reduction of 3.9 WU (IQR: 2.0–5.0). Mortality

There were no intraprocedural deaths. Two deaths (6%) occurred after BPA (95% exact binomial CI, 0.7%–20.2%). During the last BPA procedure, the first patient had a vascular injury with hemoptysis that was successfully treated using balloon tamponade, but died later and during the same admission in the intensive care unit from infectious complications secondary to pneumonia 3 weeks later. The second patient died 1 year after the last intervention due to severe right heart failure. Both deaths occurred in patients classified as high risk, corresponding to a 40% mortality rate within this subgroup (2 of 5 patients; 95% exact binomial CI, 5.3%–85.3%), whereas no deaths were observed in the other risk categories (p = 0.04). Mortality was similar in patients who demonstrated functional improvement (5%) and those who did not (7%), with no statistically significant difference (p > 0.99). Overall, in‐hospital mortality was 3.0% and post‐discharge (ambulatory) mortality was 3.1%.

Discussion

This cohort study of patients with CTEPH managed with a BPA protocol represents one of the largest reported experiences in LATAM to date. We classified 33 patients using the COMPERA 2.0 risk assessment tool and evaluated changes in NYHA functional class, hemodynamic response, and treatment intensity in a real‐world setting. More than half of the patients demonstrated improvement in NYHA functional class, and the majority showed a reduction in mPAP following BPA, which typically involved three sessions and 12 segmental interventions per patient. We observed a low overall mortality rate (in‐hospital mortality and follow‐up mortality), with both deaths occurring among high‐risk patients, a pattern consistent with more advanced baseline disease, albeit derived from a small number of events. The per‐session complication rate was also low. These findings, obtained in a cohort with more severe baseline disease and a lower per‐session complication rate than previously reported registries, contribute to a deeper understanding of BPA management in inoperable patients or those not eligible for PEA in Colombia and LATAM, underscoring the utility of the BPA protocol as an effective, real‐world strategy within a comprehensive care approach in an underrepresented region.

The patients in our analysis had more severe baseline disease compared with those reported in other major international registries. A critical finding of this study is the cohort's advanced baseline disease severity, with a baseline mPAP of 60 mmHg (IQR 50–68) and a PVR of 13.0 WU (IQR 11.0–18.6). In contrast, lower pulmonary artery pressures have been reported in international studies, including the RACE study (mPAP 46.5 mmHg; PVR 9.59 WU), the MR‐BPA trials (mPAP 38.1 mmHg; PVR 8.06 WU), and the International BPA Registry (mPAP 42 mmHg; PVR 7.55 WU) [7, 8, 10]. Similarly, data from other LATAM cohorts, including studies from Chile (mPAP 51.1 mmHg) and Brazil (mPAP 51 mmHg), also showed baseline pressures lower than those observed in our cohort [26, 27]. This greater disease burden may be a consequence of very severe prevalent cases, including patients who were previously considered not to be candidates for invasive procedures and are now referred for multidisciplinary evaluation at our institution.

These findings also underscore the greater disease severity observed in some LATAM populations and highlight the importance of generating evidence from an underrepresented region. Such advanced clinical presentations are likely multifactorial and may reflect delays in diagnosis or referral to specialized centers, consistent with patterns observed in other cardiovascular diseases in the region and potentially related to healthcare inequities, delayed diagnosis, and limited access to specialized care [28, 29, 30]. In this context, our findings meaningfully contribute to the literature, as we observed NYHA functional class improvement in 58% of patients and a median mPAP reduction of 13 mmHg (IQR 10–23) despite advanced disease severity. Achieving these outcomes in a severely ill population suggests that BPA can yield substantial benefits even at the most severe cases in the CTEPH spectrum, although these results derive from a single‐center cohort of modest size and should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive evidence of generalizable efficacy. However, significant challenges remain, including the need to perform more BPA sessions and treat all lesion types, such as chronic total occlusions, as recently demonstrated in large international BPA registries [10].

Additionally, we observed a nuanced functional response in our patients. The lack of improvement among patients with NYHA functional class II is intriguing and aligns with findings from the ICA BPA registry, which demonstrated the greatest benefit in those with higher mPAP and greater NYHA functional class limitation [10]. In this subgroup, persistent symptomatic limitations are likely multifactorial, potentially driven by comorbidities, deconditioning, or residual microvasculopathy not amenable to mechanical revascularization, rather than reflecting a true absence of benefit from BPA [31]. The lack of statistical significance may be attributed to the small number of patients in this group. A possible floor effect of the NYHA classification may also contribute, as the scale may lack sensitivity to detect clinically meaningful changes in patients with milder symptoms [32]. In contrast, the 71.4% (15 of 21) improvement rate among patients with baseline Class III disease highlights the substantial functional impact of BPA in individuals with a higher symptomatic burden.

Furthermore, the safety of interventional procedures is an important consideration when balancing the benefit/risk ratio. In our cohort, the incidence of complications was lower than that reported in other registries. In our study, complications occurred in 9 of 128 sessions (7% of all sessions and 18.2% of all patients), which is lower than the rate reported in the French registry, where complications occurred in 113 of 1006 sessions (11.2% of all sessions and 46% of all patients) [33]. This finding is relevant because the complication rate did not increase despite treating more severe patients in the LATAM region, within its inherent limitations, supporting this approach as a viable option for treating this class of patients. Nonetheless, the single case requiring VA‐ECMO support in our cohort underscores the high‐risk nature of BPA in patients with severe right ventricular dysfunction and supports the need for on‐site mechanical circulatory support capability when treating the most severe end of the spectrum.

COMPERA 2.0 is a four‐stratum risk score developed and validated for PAH (WHO group 1), specifically for risk reassessment during follow‐up, and has not been formally validated as a prognostic or predictive instrument in CTEPH [12, 16]. Nevertheless, its components may serve as surrogate markers of right ventricular function. In the absence of a risk‐stratification score specific to CTEPH, these non‐invasive variables have been applied descriptively in CTEPH populations, including medically managed cohorts and BPA‐treated cohorts [17, 22, 23]. Moreover, the four‐stratum model retained statistically significant, although modest, discriminatory value for survival in a large multicenter registry that included patients with group 4 PH [34]. Notably, a larger, dedicated BPA cohort of 139 patients using formal predictive‐modeling methodology found that COMPERA 2.0 could predict post‐BPA hemodynamic outcome with modest discrimination (area under the curve 0.65) and adequate calibration [18]. In our study, we observed a low mortality rate in the overall population; however, mortality reached 40% in the high‐risk category, whereas no deaths occurred in the other groups. Given the small number of events (2 deaths among 5 high‐risk patients) and the resulting extremely wide confidence interval, this association should be interpreted cautiously and for descriptive purposes only, rather than as evidence supporting inferential claims. Moreover, all risk groups showed hemodynamic improvement in mPAP and PVR, with no differences between groups. This null finding should not be interpreted as evidence that COMPERA 2.0 is unrelated to hemodynamic response, as larger, purpose‐built cohorts using formal predictive‐modeling methods have reported a modest association [18]. Rather, it likely reflects the limited statistical power of our sample (n = 33), which was neither designed nor powered to develop or validate a predictive model. In our cohort, COMPERA 2.0 was used solely to characterize baseline clinical severity. Therefore, BPA may be proposed as a treatment option across the baseline risk spectrum, while risk‐adapted predictive modeling of hemodynamic response remains an open question for larger, purpose‐built studies.

Additionally, BPA has been described as an effective strategy for managing large‐vessel obstruction, reducing right ventricular afterload, and potentially improving parameters associated with right ventricular dysfunction [35, 36]. Based on this biological plausibility, the hemodynamic and functional improvements achieved with BPA could also be expected to favorably affect the components of COMPERA 2.0, including functional class, NT‐proBNP, and 6‐min walk distance, because these variables reflect overall clinical status and right ventricular function. This raises the possibility that COMPERA 2.0 may be useful not only for baseline risk assessment but also for longitudinal follow‐up, a hypothesis partially supported by the predictive performance described above [18]. However, this potential application was not evaluated in the present study, and larger, adequately powered cohorts are needed to determine its clinical utility. This caution is further supported by our finding that high risk patients had a higher rate of A6 segment treatment, which likely reflects a greater lesion burden and a more extensive distal disease distribution rather than the predictive utility of the risk score itself. These findings reinforce that, in our cohort, anatomical lesion characteristics remain a more established determinant of clinical heterogeneity than COMPERA 2.0 strata alone.

An evolving aspect of CTEPH management is the integration of interventional procedures such as BPA with targeted PH medical therapy. A multimodal treatment strategy is increasingly favored, with agents such as riociguat used either as a bridge to intervention or to treat residual disease, potentially addressing microvascular pathology that is not amenable to BPA [37]. Although many patients in our series were receiving PH‐specific medications, the retrospective design of our study precludes a reliable assessment of their contribution to the observed outcomes. The optimal therapeutic strategy, whether upfront combination therapy, sequential treatment, or BPA alone, remains an important unanswered question. Recent evidence supports the use of a sequential treatment approach in selected patients with CTEPH, but further prospective studies are required to define the most effective strategy [37].

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several important strengths. First, it provides systematic data on a rare disease from an underrepresented region, contributing valuable evidence from LATAM. Second, we used COMPERA 2.0 risk stratification, providing a standardized framework for baseline assessment, applied here as a marker of baseline disease severity in CTEPH populations and enabling comparability with other cohorts assessed using the same standardized tool. Third, our institution's established multidisciplinary PH team, which includes pulmonologists, cardiologists, intensive care specialists, cardiovascular surgeons, thoracic radiologists, and interventional cardiologists, ensured rigorous patient selection and treatment decisions. Fourth, we documented detailed anatomical information about treated segments using standardized nomenclature, allowing transparent reporting of lesion complexity and treatment patterns. Fifth, our cohort predominantly comprised severe cases with a baseline mPAP of 60 mmHg, suggesting that BPA can achieve meaningful clinical benefit even in patients with advanced disease, an observation, derived from a single‐center cohort of modest size, that may be relevant for centers managing late‐presenting patients with a low complication rate. Finally, the study adhered to established reporting guidelines (STROBE) and used complete‐case analysis, with transparent reporting of sample sizes for each outcome.

Meanwhile, we present several limitations. Our case volume of 33 patients is relatively small and does not permit a systematic assessment of outcomes; in particular, the COMPERA 2.0 risk strata were markedly unbalanced (2 low‐risk and 5 high‐risk patients), and conclusions drawn from these subgroups, including the observed hemodynamic changes, should not be assumed to be generalizable. Hemodynamic follow‐up protocols varied among patients, reflecting the constraints of routine clinical practice. The absence of a control group is not considered a limitation in this analysis, as BPA has been shown to provide greater benefit than PH‐specific medical therapy in patients with CTEPH [7, 8, 38]. Our finding that mortality was confined to the high‐risk group should be viewed as a hypothesis‐generating observation that warrants validation in larger cohorts. Similarly, the descriptive comparison of mortality between functional responders and non‐responders (5% vs. 7%) should not be interpreted as evidence of association, given the very small number of events in each group. A formal comparison with the 36 patients managed with PEA during the same period was not possible, as detailed clinical and hemodynamic data for these patients were not systematically collected, given that our inclusion criteria and data extraction protocol were specific to the BPA cohort. Selection between PEA and BPA was determined by the multidisciplinary team based on operability, and we acknowledge this as a potential source of selection bias. Furthermore, COMPERA 2.0 was developed and validated in PAH and has not been formally validated as a predictive tool in CTEPH; in our study it was used exclusively to describe baseline clinical severity, not to predict hemodynamic response or guide candidate selection for BPA, which were based on anatomical lesion characteristics. Quality‐of‐life assessment was not performed in this study.

Clinical Implications and Future Directions

Our findings have direct implications for both patient management and program development. We demonstrate that the COMPERA 2.0 score is a useful marker of baseline disease severity; however, it lacks domains specific to CTEPH, such as lesion characteristics and the total number of occlusions, and did not predict the magnitude of hemodynamic response to BPA in our cohort. The observation that mortality occurred exclusively in the high‐risk group, despite improvements in hemodynamics, highlights the importance of baseline risk stratification for prognosis, even though it does not anticipate treatment response. This suggests that baseline risk assessment, rather than post‐procedural hemodynamic response alone, is critical for guiding long‐term care. Furthermore, our study demonstrates the successful implementation of a BPA program in a new healthcare setting, providing a strong rationale for expanding access to BPA in LATAM. This expansion should be supported by institutional commitment and structured operator training.

Conclusion

BPA led to functional and hemodynamic improvements in these patients with inoperable CTEPH, demonstrating a favorable safety profile, a low periprocedural complication rate, no periprocedural deaths, and a low follow‐up mortality rate. These findings support the feasibility of BPA even in patients with advanced disease, although the modest sample size and single‐center design warrant confirmation in larger, multicenter cohorts before broader generalization. BPA appears to be a promising treatment option that delivers meaningful clinical benefits in high‐risk populations in real‐world, resource‐variable settings such as ours, supporting continued, structured development of specialized BPA programs in underrepresented regions such as LATAM.
