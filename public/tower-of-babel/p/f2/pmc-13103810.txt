Friedelin Enhances Gelatin/Nanohydroxyapatite Scaffold Performance by Inhibiting Osteoclastogenesis and Promoting Osteogenesis
Authors: Nguyen Minh Trang, Da Eun Lee, Eun-Nam Kim, Gil-Saeng Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Friedelin is a naturally occurring compound known for its anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and wound-healing properties, positioning it as a potential candidate for the development of advanced biomaterials in bone tissue engineering. In this study, we first investigated the biological effects of friedelin (FD) on osteoclast differentiation induced by RANKL (receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-Β ligand)-induced osteoclast differentiation, as well as its protective role in osteoblast function under lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammatory conditions. Furthermore, composite scaffolds composed of gelatin/friedelin (Gel/FD) and gelatin/nanohydroxyapatite/friedelin (Gel/HA/FD) were fabricated via the freeze-drying. These scaffolds were characterized in terms of morphology, chemical composition, swelling behavior, porosity, degradation rate, rheological properties, and biocompatibility. To evaluate their osteogenic potential, Saos-2 cells were cultured on the scaffolds, and markers of osteogenic differentiation were assessed through mRNA expression analysis and calcium deposition assays. Overall, the findings suggest that friedelin is a promising bioactive molecule for the design of functional biomaterials intended for bone regeneration applications.

Introduction

Bone homeostasis is preserved through a continuous and well-regulated balance between bone resorption and bone formation by osteoclasts and osteoblasts, respectively. This dynamic remodeling process is essential for preserving skeletal strength, repairing microdamage, and enabling adaptation to mechanical stress throughout life. With aging, the skeleton progressively loses bone mass, largely due to an imbalance in bone remodeling, in which the pace of bone resorption exceeds that of bone formation. Although the exact mechanisms underlying age-related bone loss are still being investigated, increased activity and differentiation of osteoclasts have been identified as major contributing factors. , A central regulator of osteoclastogenesis is the receptor activator of RANKL, which is produced by cells of the osteoblast lineage and binds to its receptor RANK on the surface of myeloid progenitor cells. This interaction triggers downstream signaling cascades that promote osteoclast differentiation, activation, and survival. In the physiological context, RANKL-stimulated osteoclastogenesis is fundamental for bone remodeling by enabling the removal of aged or damaged bone while maintaining skeletal integrity. However, excessive or dysregulated activation of this pathway contributes to pathological bone resorption and the development of age-associated skeletal disorders.

Large bone defects resulting from tumor removal, severe infections, congenital abnormalities, or traumatic injuries continue to present significant challenges in orthopedic medicine. To manage such conditions, surgical reconstruction using conventional bone grafting strategies, including autografts, allografts, xenografts, and metallic implants, are commonly employed. Despite their widespread use, each of these approaches has inherent limitations, such as immune rejection, risk of disease transmission, donor site morbidity, or inadequate integration with host tissue. Moreover, traditional grafts often fail to replicate the complex architecture, mechanical properties, and biological functions of native bone, thereby limiting their capacity to support complete and functional regeneration. , Bone tissue engineering represents a key area within tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, aiming to develop functional substitutes capable of repairing or regenerating damaged bone tissue. This approach combines biomaterial scaffolds with cells and bioactive molecules to promote the natural healing process. Engineered constructs are designed to mimic the architectural and biological attributes of native bone, thereby enabling effective integration with host tissue. As a fundamental element of bone tissue engineering, scaffolds enable a temporary three-dimensional matrix that promotes the cell attachment, proliferation, migration, and differentiation, while permitting the exchange of nutrients, oxygen, and metabolic waste. Scaffolds composed of gelatin and hydroxyapatite have attracted considerable interest due to their favorable biological properties, notably high biocompatibility, osteoconductivity, tunable biodegradability, wide availability, cost-effectiveness, and ease of fabrication. Despite these strengths, the practical application of such scaffolds remains limited by several material-related challenges. Their inherent brittleness, low mechanical strength, and tendency to degrade rapidly under physiological conditions can compromise their structural integrity before new bone tissue has fully formed. Overcoming these limitations is essential for advancing their use in load-bearing or long-term bone repair applications.

Recently, natural products have attracted growing interest in biomedical and pharmaceutical research, particularly in bone tissue engineering, owing to their intrinsic biocompatibility, bioactivity, and favorable safety profiles compared with synthetic compounds. Bioactive natural compounds and materials, including marine-derived biosilica and plant-derived polyphenols such as phloridzin, esculetin, and nuciferine, as well as polysaccharide-based biomaterials such as chitosan, alginate, and gelatin, have been successfully integrated into electrospun scaffolds, injectable hydrogels, and bioprinted constructs to promote osteogenic differentiation, angiogenesis, and immunomodulation. − These advances collectively underscore the significant potential of natural product–based systems as promising and sustainable candidates for next-generation bone tissue engineering applications.

Friedelin (friedelan-3-one, FD) is a naturally occurring pentacyclic triterpene that was first isolated from tree bark in 1807 using alcohol-based extraction methods. At the time of its discovery, it was referred to as “cork alcohol” due to its source and chemical nature. Since its initial discovery, friedelin has been isolated from a wide range of plant species across various botanical families. In addition to higher plants, it has also been reported in lower organisms, including mosses, lichens, algae, and fungi. Recently, FD has emerged as a promising bioactive compound in pharmaceutical and biomedical research, largely attributed to its minimal cytotoxicity, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anticancer, neuroprotective, hepatoprotective, antimicrobial antidiabetic effects, and wound healing properties. , An increasing number of scientific studies have demonstrated its potential therapeutic benefits across various biological systems. Reported activities include anti-inflammatory, anticancer, antioxidant, and hepatoprotective effects, as well as neuroprotective, antimicrobial, and antidiabetic actions. ,,− Importantly, FD has been shown to attenuate the progression of collagenase-induced tendinopathy in mice, an effect that is associated with the activation of selective autophagic pathways targeting p65. These multifaceted properties position FD as a strong candidate for further investigation in the development of novel, naturally derived therapeutic agents.

In this study, we investigated the biological effects of FD on osteoclastogenesis stimulated by receptor activator of RANKL. This included an assessment of osteoclast differentiation and the expression of key osteoclast-specific genes and transcription factors involved in bone resorption. In parallel, we evaluated the protective role of FD on osteoblast function under inflammatory conditions simulated by LPS exposure. To determine its osteoprotective effects, we analyzed alkaline phosphatase activity and the mRNA levels of several osteogenic markers, including alp, col1a1, opn, osx, runx2, and ocn, which are essential for bone matrix formation and mineralization. Following the biological evaluation, composite scaffolds were fabricated by incorporating FD into gelatin (Gel/FD) and into a gelatin/nanohydroxyapatite matrix (Gel/HA/FD) using the freeze-drying technique. Then scaffolds were thoroughly characterized to determine their swelling behavior, morphological structure, chemical composition, porosity, degradation rate, and overall biocompatibility. To further assess their osteogenic potential, human osteoblast-like Saos-2 cells were seeded onto the scaffolds. Osteogenic differentiation was evaluated through the analysis of osteogenic gene expression profiles and calcium deposition, providing insights into the capacity of the scaffolds to support bone tissue regeneration.

Results and Discussion

FD Inhibits the RANKL-Induced Osteoclast Differentiation and Function

Since cytotoxicity is an undesirable property of natural compounds, the cytotoxicity of FD were assessed before further investigation. , As shown in Figure S1, RAW264.7 macrophages were treated with FD across a wide concentration range (1–100 μM). The results indicated that FD exhibited cytotoxicity at concentrations of 40 μM and above. Therefore, concentrations ranging from 1 to 20 μM were chosen for subsequent experiments to evaluate the effects of FD. Osteoclasts represent multinucleated cells that originate from mononuclear precursors of the monocyte and macrophage lineage found in the bone marrow and bloodstream. In response to cytokines such as M-CSF and RANKL, these precursors migrate to bone surfaces, where they fuse, become TRAP-positive, and differentiate into mature osteoclasts capable of resorbing bone. This process, which plays a central role in bone remodeling and inflammatory osteolysis, is marked by significant changes in gene expression, cell structure, and metabolic activity. − As illustrated in Figure A,D,E, RANKL stimulation effectively induced osteoclast differentiation. However, treatment with FD markedly inhibited this process in a dose-dependent manner. This inhibitory effect was further supported by the observed decrease in TRAP activity in FD-treated macrophages, indicating a suppression of osteoclastic differentiation and function (Figure F). Osteoclast migration from the bone marrow and circulation to the bone surface is a crucial step in initiating bone resorption. This directed movement allows osteoclasts to reach sites where bone breakdown is needed or becomes excessive in disease. Disruption or enhancement of this migration plays a significant role in the bone-resorptive progression. RANKL treatment enhances the migration of RAW264.7 cells, whereas cotreatment with FD significantly reduces this migration in a dose-dependent manner (Figure B,G). To further validate the inhibitory effect of FD, a transwell assay was performed, demonstrating that FD suppresses RANKL-induced migration of osteoclasts in a concentration-dependent manner (Figure C,H).

FD Suppresses the Osteoclastogenic Gene Expression and Transcription Factors Induced by RANKL Stimulation

RANKL is a main cytokine which plays a crucial role in regulating the differentiation, activation, and function of osteoclasts. When RANKL binds to RANK, it initiates a signaling cascade by promoting receptor trimerization and recruitment of the adaptor protein TRAF6. This interaction activates downstream pathways that are crucial for osteoclast development. RANKL signaling leads to the upregulation of critical transcription factors, such as c-Fos. In addition, RANKL enhances the expression of several osteoclast-specific genes, namely acid phosphatase 5 (acp5), dendritic cell-specific transmembrane protein (dcstamp), cathepsin K (ctsk), and ATPase H+ transporting V0 subunit d2 (atp6v0d2), all of which contribute to the differentiation and maturation of functional osteoclasts. As shown in Figure A, RANKL stimulation led to an upregulation of c-Fos and cathepsin K (CTSK) protein expression. However, treatment with FD effectively downregulated the expression of these osteoclast-associated proteins. In addition, FD treatment significantly suppressed the expression of key osteoclast marker genes, including ctsk, dcstamp, acp5, and atp6v0d2, as illustrated in Figure B.

FD attenuates the RANKL-induced osteoclast-specific genes and transcription factors. (A) CTSK and c-Fos proteins were quantified by Western blot. (B) mRNA expression level of osteoclast-specific genes was analyzed by RT-PCR. The data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation of three experiments independently. # p < 0.05 vs control group; *p < 0.05 vs RANKL-treated group; **p < 0.01 vs RANKL-treated group; ***p < 0.001 vs RANKL-treated group.

FD Treatment Enhances Cell Proliferation, Promotes Migratory Activity, and Accelerates Wound Healing Capacity

MC3T3-E1 cells are a well-established murine preosteoblast cell line extensively used to investigate early stages of osteogenic differentiation, extracellular matrix maturation, and mineralization under controlled in vitro conditions. This cell line is particularly suitable for evaluating initial osteoblast lineage commitment and early osteogenic responses to biomaterial cues. , Since cytotoxicity is an undesirable characteristic in vitro, the effects of FD on MC3T3-E1 cells were evaluated to further assess its biocompatibility. The cytotoxicity assay demonstrated that FD exhibited no significant cytotoxic effects on MC3T3-E1 osteoblasts at concentrations up to 20 μM, indicating its biocompatibility within this range (Figure S2). Moreover, in comparison with the LPS only treated group, FD had a protective effect on the MC3T3-E1 cell line by increasing the cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner (Figure A). Osteoblasts play a central role in bone regeneration by producing the extracellular matrix essential for bone repair and remodeling. Successful bone healing requires these cells to proliferate, differentiate, and actively promote the mineralization process within the extracellular matrix to restore bone strength and function. Wound healing assays revealed a marked reduction in the healing area following LPS treatment, suggesting impaired cellular migration. However, cotreatment with FD significantly restored the wound closure capacity (Figure B,D). To further evaluate the migratory potential of preosteoblasts, a transwell migration assay was performed. The results showed that FD enhanced cell migration in a concentration-responsive manner (Figure C,E). Taken together, these findings suggest that FD promotes osteoblast proliferation, stimulates cell migration, and facilitates wound healing, indicating its potential therapeutic value in bone regeneration.

FD Significantly Enhances and Accelerates Osteogenic Induction, Promoting Precursor Cell Differentiation toward the Osteoblastic Lineage

Osteoblast differentiation is marked by a tightly regulated sequence of gene expression changes involving several key markers. Genes such as osteocalcin (ocn), alkaline phosphatase (alp), osteopontin (opn), and type I collagen (col1a1) are expressed at distinct stages, reflecting the progression of osteoblast maturation and bone matrix formation. , Among the regulatory factors involved, Runx2 (Runt-related transcription factor 2) plays a pivotal role in driving osteoblast lineage commitment and differentiation. Runx2 expression is initially low in uncommitted mesenchymal stem cells. As these cells begin to commit to the osteogenic lineage and transition into preosteoblasts, Runx2 levels increase significantly, reaching a peak during the early stages of differentiation. As the cells mature into fully functional osteoblasts, Runx2 expression gradually declines, allowing for the expression of late-stage osteogenic markers. In addition to its role in differentiation, Runx2 contributes to the proliferation of osteoprogenitor cells by directly regulating the expression of fibroblast growth factor receptors (Fgfr2 and Fgfr3), further underscoring its importance in bone development. , As illustrated in Figure A, treatment with LPS significantly suppressed the protein expression of RUNX2 and ALP, both of which play essential roles in osteoblast differentiation and bone formation. Interestingly, the presence of FD was able to counteract this effect, restoring the expression levels of these key osteogenic markers. In support of these findings, FD treatment also resulted in a dose-dependent increase in the mRNA expression of several osteogenic genes, including opn, runx2, alp, osx, ocn, and col1a1 (Figure B), which are widely recognized as indicators of osteoblast maturation and function. Moreover, FD significantly enhanced ALP enzymatic activity in MC3T3-E1 cells, further confirming its pro-osteogenic effect (Figure C). Taken together, these results suggest that FD not only mitigates the inhibitory effects of inflammatory stimuli such as LPS but also actively promotes osteogenic differentiation, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic agent for enhancing bone regeneration.

Chemical Characterization of the Scaffold

Considering the demonstrated effectiveness of FD in both suppressing RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation and enhancing osteogenic activity, FD was subsequently incorporated into gelatin (Gel) and gelatin combined with hydroxyapatite (Gel/HA), both of which are well-established biomaterials extensively used in the field of bone tissue engineering. In this study, FD was incorporated into scaffolds based on gelatin alone as well as those composed of gelatin and hydroxyapatite, in order to evaluate its potential as a natural compound-based biomaterial for promoting bone regeneration. To investigate the molecular interactions among gelatin, hydroxyapatite, and FD within the composite scaffolds, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was conducted, and the resulting spectra are presented in Figure A. The FTIR analysis revealed characteristicabsorption bands corresponding to functional groups within the scaffolds. Additional absorption bands were observed in the region between 1232 and 1637 cm–1, which further support the presence of specific chemical interactions among the scaffold components. , In the full FTIR spectrum, the band at 2931 to 2934 cm–1 was considered as CH2 asymmetric stretching. The predominant band observed at 1633 cm–1 is attributed to desolvated or buried amide carbonyl groups, which are more prominent in the scaffolds containing HA. In contrast, this band appeared with lower intensity in the Gel/FD scaffold, indicating reduced interaction or the presence of these specific carbonyl groups in the absence of HA, which is similar to N–H bond at between 1231 and 1632 cm–1. The most notable differences in the FTIR spectra among the Gel/HA, Gel/FD, and Gel/HA/FD scaffolds were observed in the region between 952 and 1078 cm–1. This spectral range corresponds primarily to the phosphate (PO4 3–) stretching vibrations, which are indicative of the presence and interaction of hydroxyapatite within the composite scaffolds. The variation in peak intensity and position within this region suggests distinct structural or compositional differences resulting from the incorporation of FD and HA in the respective scaffold formulations. Figure B shows the physical appearance of all scaffold samples. Visual inspection reveals that the Gel/HA scaffold appears translucent, whereas the incorporation of FD imparts a slight yellow coloration to the scaffolds. Furthermore, the micromorphology of the scaffolds was investigated to understand their structural integrity. As depicted in Figure C, the morphological analysis demonstrated that the scaffolds possess a highly interconnected, 3D porous network resembling a sponge-like structure. This heterogeneous pore distribution is conducive to providing sufficient space for cell infiltration and nutrient exchange. The presence of HA within the Gel/HA and Gel/HA/FD scaffolds was further validated by EDS analysis, which identified the characteristic elemental signals of Ca, P, and O. The presence of Ca and P peaks, alongside O, confirms the presence of the successful incorporation and uniform retention of HA particles within the gelatin-based matrix. Following the structural characterization, the physical properties influenced by these microstructures were evaluated. The swelling capacity of scaffolds plays a crucial role in supporting cellular functions, as it contributes to the formation of a hydrated three-dimensional structure that facilitates cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation. This swollen matrix enhances cell migration and promotes the efficient exchange and retention of nutrients and gases, which are essential for sustaining cellular activity and tissue regeneration. The swelling capacity of the fabricated scaffolds was evaluated, and the results are illustrated in Figure D. During the first hour of immersion in PBS, all scaffold groups demonstrated a rapid initial uptake of water. The swelling ratio at this time point was recorded as 3064.8 ± 704.1% for the Gel/HA scaffold, 2739.9 ± 287.5% for the Gel/FD scaffold, and 3372.3 ± 558.0% for the Gel/HA/FD scaffold. This initial water absorption is primarily attributed to the hydrophilic characteristics of gelatin, particularly the presence of carboxyl groups in its amino acid residues, which readily interact with water molecules. After 24 h, the swelling ratios continued to increase, reaching 4732.4 ± 258.6% for Gel/HA, 3977.1 ± 636.6% for Gel/FD, and 4125.8 ± 538.7% for Gel/HA/FD. Despite this continued absorption, no significant change in swelling behavior was observed after 72 h, indicating that the scaffolds had reached a state of equilibrium (Figure D). Previous studies have proposed that a high content of hydroxyapatite can reduce the swelling ability of polymer-based scaffolds by occupying pore spaces and forming a denser network, thereby limiting fluid penetration. However, the results of this study implied that the concentration of hydroxyapatite used did not substantially affect the swelling performance of the scaffolds. This observation is consistent with earlier findings reported in the literature. Porosity is a critical parameter in scaffold design, as it enables the efficient exchange of gases, nutrients, and metabolic waste, while also supporting the infiltration of blood vessels. The overall porosity, along with the size, shape, and interconnectivity of the pores, plays a fundamental role in determining not only the mechanical strength of the scaffold but also its biological performance and integration with surrounding bone tissue at the implant interface. , The results of the porosity assessment showed that the Gel/HA, Gel/FD, and Gel/HA/FD scaffolds exhibited porosity values of 63.37, 42.30, and 58.27%, respectively, as shown in Figure E. The incorporation of hydroxyapatite significantly improved scaffold porosity, which can be attributed to its hydrophilic and polyanionic nature that enhances water uptake, ice crystal formation, and pore stabilization during freeze-drying. , When friedelin was introduced into the Gel/HA system (Gel/HA/FD), the pore-reinforcing effect of hydroxyapatite partially counteracted the pore-filling and densifying effect of friedelin, resulting in an intermediate porosity that remained higher than Gel/FD but slightly lower than Gel/HA. The rate at which a bone scaffold degrades is a key factor in its overall performance and is largely determined by its chemical makeup, structural design, and the techniques used during fabrication. A well-regulated, gradual degradation process is essential to provide ongoing mechanical strength and biochemical cues that support tissue regeneration until the newly formed bone fully replaces the scaffold. As shown in Figure F, the degradation rates of the scaffolds on day 14 were measured at 16.63% for Gel/HA, 17.89% for Gel/FD, and 37.22% for Gel/HA/FD, respectively. Importantly, the moderately higher degradation observed for the Gel/HA/FD scaffold does not compromise scaffold function during long-term cell culture, where partial scaffold resorption is considered desirable to facilitate tissue remodeling and regeneration. Previous studies have reported that moderate scaffold degradation can promote extracellular matrix deposition and mineralization by creating space for cell infiltration and newly formed tissue, while simultaneously enabling sustained release of bioactive components. , In addition to controlled degradation behavior, the viscoelastic properties of the scaffolds were evaluated by rheological analysis (Figure G,H). Under amplitude sweep conditions at 1 Hz and 22 °C, scaffolds exhibited a well-defined linear viscoelastic region at low strain levels, where the storage modulus (G′) remained higher than the loss modulus (G″). Since 1% strain was within the linear viscoelastic region for all groups, subsequent frequency sweep measurements were conducted at 1% strain. In frequency sweep measurements (0.1–100 rad/s), G′ consistently exceeded G″ across the tested frequency range with minimal frequency dependence. These results demonstrate that all scaffolds formed stable elastic networks. The higher G′ values in FD-containing groups indicate that FD contributed to mechanical reinforcement. The combined incorporation of HA and FD preserved viscoelastic stability, supporting the structural integrity of the scaffold for tissue regeneration.

Cytocompatibility, Anti-Inflammatory Properties, and Antioxidant Activity of the FD-Based Scaffold

Saos-2 cells are a well-established human osteoblast-like model commonly used to assess osteogenic differentiation, mineralization, and biomaterial-cell interactions in vitro. Notably, Saos-2 cells are well characterized and capable of producing a mineralized extracellular matrix while expressing key bone formation markers, including alkaline phosphatase and osteogenic genes, making them a widely accepted in vitro model for bone tissue engineering studies in a human-relevant context. , The biocompatibility of the scaffolds was assessed by measuring the viability of Saos-2 cells cultured on each material, as shown in Figure A. To evaluate this, an MTT assay was conducted at two time points, including day 3 and day 7, allowing for the observation of cell proliferation over time. The results demonstrated that both Gel/FD and Gel/HA/FD scaffolds supported significantly higher levels of cell growth compared with the Gel/HA scaffold alone. This suggests that the incorporation of friedelin may enhance the ability of the scaffold to support osteoblast-like cell viability and proliferation, indicating improved biological compatibility and a more favorable environment for bone tissue regeneration. In addition, to evaluate the anti-inflammatory potential of the FD-loaded scaffolds, a nitric oxide (NO) assay was performed. The results revealed that the Gel/FD composite significantly reduced NO production, indicating a notable reduction in inflammation levels compared with the control (Figure B). The generation of ROS and free radicals during oxidative stress can interfere with normal bone healing processes and impair various cellular functions. This detrimental effect may be mitigated by integrating bioactive compounds into the scaffold, which can help modulate the oxidative environment and support tissue regeneration. In the present study, the antioxidant activity of the FD-functionalized scaffolds was assessed by evaluating their ability to inhibit ABTS free radicals (Figure C). Our data indicate that, in comparison to the Gel/HA scaffold, both Gel/FD and Gel/HA/FD scaffolds exhibit only a modest effect. To further validate the antioxidant potential of the FD-loaded scaffolds, reactive oxygen species (ROS) staining was performed. Saos-2 cells were seeded onto the scaffold surfaces, and oxidative stress was induced using hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), a well-known agent that promotes cellular oxidative damage and senescence. , This experimental setup allowed for the evaluation of each scaffold ability to counteract oxidative stress. As illustrated in Figure D, both the Gel/HA and Gel/FD scaffolds exhibited notable antioxidant effects by reducing ROS accumulation in the treated cells. However, the Gel/HA/FD composite demonstrated the most pronounced antioxidant activity, suggesting a synergistic effect between hydroxyapatite and friedelin in mitigating oxidative stress. Collectively, the ABTS and DCF-DA assays provide complementary information on the antioxidant behavior of the FD-loaded scaffolds, indicating that while their direct chemical radical-scavenging activity is modest, they effectively attenuate intracellular oxidative stress under biologically relevant conditions.

Expression Levels of Osteogenic Genes, Corresponding Protein Production, and Mineralization Analysis

The osteogenic potential of Saos-2 cells was evaluated by examining multiple indicators, including ALP enzymatic activity and calcium mineral deposition. ALP, a metalloenzyme attached to the cell membrane via glycosylphosphatidylinositol anchors, is widely recognized as an initial and essential marker of osteoblast differentiation. This suggests that the incorporation of friedelin enhances the osteogenic response of cells beyond that provided by gelatin and hydroxyapatite. ALP activity, ARS staining analysis revealed that the Gel/FD scaffold exhibited a notable reduction in calcium deposition compared with the Gel/HA scaffold, as illustrated in Figure A,B, suggesting a diminished capacity to promote mineralization in the absence of hydroxyapatite. The incorporation of HA into the Gel/FD scaffold (Gel/HA/FD) resulted in increased calcium deposition, indicating a partial recovery of pro-mineralization capacity.

Conclusions

This study underscores the multifaceted role of FD in bone tissue regeneration. FD was revealed to significantly inhibit RANKL-stimulated osteoclast differentiation and downregulate the expression of osteoclast-specific genes and transcription factors, indicating its potential to suppress bone resorption. Concurrently, FD enhanced osteoblast function by restoring ALP activity and clearly upregulating the expression of key osteogenic genes, namely opn, runx2, alp, osx, ocn, and col1a1, which are critical markers for osteogenic differentiation and bone matrix formation. The FD-loaded scaffold, when compared with the Gel/HA scaffold, exhibited improved swelling behavior and provided a more supportive microenvironment for cellular activity. This enhancement is attributed to the bioactive properties of FD, which, in combination with HA, conferred significant antioxidant activity to the scaffold. This functionalization not only supported the proliferation and differentiation of Saos-2 cells but also promoted extracellular matrix mineralization, a key step in bone tissue formation. Overall, these findings suggest that the FD-loaded Gel/HA scaffold holds significant promise as a multifunctional biomaterial for bone tissue engineering, offering both antiresorptive and pro-osteogenic effects mediated through the upregulation of osteogenic gene expression that are essential for successful bone regeneration.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Cell culture media, including Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), α-Minimum Essential Medium (α-MEM), and were sourced from Welgene Bioscience (Daegu, Korea). Recombinant soluble RANKL (sRANKL) protein was purchased from PeproTech EC, Ltd. (London, UK). 2,2′-biazobis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS), Crystal Violet (C0775), the Acid Phosphatase Leukocyte Kit (387A-1KT), the Acid Phosphatase Assay Kit (CS0740), 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT), polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes and Griess reagent (G4410) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The Bradford protein assay reagent and enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection reagents were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Primary antibodies against β-actin (Cat# sc-47778), cathepsin K (CTSK; Cat# sc-48353), c-Fos (Cat# sc-7202), alkaline phosphatase (ALP; Cat# sc-365765), and runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2; Cat# sc-390351) were obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, TX, USA). TRIzol reagent was obtained from JBI (Seoul, Korea), while the TOPscript RT DryMIX (dT18 plus) reverse transcription kit was provided by Enzynomics (Daejeon, Korea). The qPCR reagent TB Green Premix Ex TaqII (Tli RNaseH Plus) was acquired from Takara Bio (Tokyo, Japan).

Cell Culture

MC3T3-E1 cell lines were procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), while RAW264.7 and Sao-2 cells were purchased from the Korean Cell Line Bank (Seoul, Republic of Korea). RAW264.7 and Saos-2 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM). While MC3T3-E1 cells were cultured with Minimum Essential Medium-α (α-MEM). In addition, the culture media was enriched with 10% 100 U/mL penicillin G, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), and cultures were preserved at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in an incubator.

Cell Viability and Proliferation

Cells were distributed in 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h in an incubator. Then, cells were treated with FD (1–5 μM) with/without RANKL (50 ng/mL) or LPS (1 μg/mL) for additionally 24 h. MTT (5 mg/mL) was added, followed by dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) treatment to dissolve formazan crystals. A microplate reader (TECAN infinity Pro 2000, Männedorf, Switzerland) was used to measure the cell viability at an absorbance of 540 nm. For cell proliferation, Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was used. In particular, 10 μL CCK-8 was added to each well and incubated for 1 h before being measured at the absorbance of 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Osteoclast Differentiation

RAW264.7 cells (1 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in 24-well plates using DMEM medium. After 24 h of cell adherence, the medium was converted to α-MEM supplemented with recombinant RANKL (50 ng/mL) in the presence/absence of FD at concentrations ranging from 1 to 20 μM. The treatment was maintained for 5 days, with the culture medium refreshed on day 3, maintaining the same concentrations of RANKL and/or FD as initially applied.

Tartrate-Resistant Acid Phosphatase Staining and Activity

RAW264.7 cells (1 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in 24-well plates according to the cell culture procedure followed by the osteoclast differentiation assay. On day 5, the Acid Phosphatase Assay Kit was utilized to analyze TRAP activity. Briefly, the reaction mixture was transferred to a 96-well plate and incubated for 30 min, which subsequently was terminated by adding 0.5 N NaOH. Absorbance was measured at 405 nm using a microplate reader, and the TRAP activity was measured according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and PBS washing, cells were incubated with the Acid Phosphatase Leukocyte Kit for 1 h protected from light. Multinucleated cells (≥3 nuclei) exhibiting red staining were identified as TRAP-positive osteoclasts under an optical microscope.

Transwell Migration and Scratching Assay

Cells (1 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in 24-well transwell plates according to the mentioned cell culture procedure. Cells were then treated with the indicated conditions for 5 day. Migrated cells were stained using Crystal Violet and visualized under an optical microscope.

For the wound healing assay, cells were plated in 6-well plates. A scratch was created using a sterile pipet tip, followed by treatment as mentioned. The wounded area of migrated cells was assessed on day 5.

Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) Activity

Osteogenic differentiation was induced by culturing MC3T3-E1 and Saos-2 cells in osteogenic medium containing 5 mM β-glycerophosphate, 100 mM ascorbic acid, and 10 nM dexamethasone for 7 days. For ALP activity analysis, cells were rinsed with PBS, scraped into 1 mL PBS, and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 5 min. Pellets were resuspended in 100 μL ALP buffer (100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 9.5, 1% Triton X-100). After dilution, 50 μL of each sample was mixed with 50 μL of p-nitrophenyl phosphate (pNPP) substrate. Absorbance was monitored at 405 nm, and results were normalized to protein content determined by the BCA assay (Sigma).

Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cell lysates using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the TOPscript RT DryMIX (dT18 plus) kit (Enzynomics, Korea). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using TB Green Premix Ex TaqII (Tli RNaseH Plus; Takara Bio, Japan) on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCT method: expression = 2–ΔΔCT, where ΔΔCT = (CT target – CT gapdh). Primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

Western Blotting

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors, incubated on ice for 30 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was measured using the Bradford assay. Equal amounts of denatured protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C. After washing with TBS-T, membranes were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies. Protein bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and visualized with the ImageQuant LAS 4000 system (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). Band intensities were semiquantified using ImageJ software.

Synthesis of FD-Functionalized Scaffolds

Gelatin/nanohydroxyapatite (Gel/HA) scaffolds were fabricated using a modified protocol based on previously reported methods, incorporating carbodiimide (EDC/NHS) cross-linking before freeze-drying. To prepare the Gel/HA composite, Gel (5% w/v) was dissolved in deionized (DI) water under constant stirring at 40 °C and 200 rpm. Once fully solubilized, HA was added at 10% w/w relative to the gelatin mass, and the mixture was further stirred for 6 h at 40 °C and 400 rpm to ensure uniform dispersion. Aliquots of the resulting Gel/HA solution (800 μL) were cast into 48-well plates, frozen at −20 °C for 24 h, and subsequently lyophilized for 72 h. The dried scaffolds were then cross-linked by immersion in an EDC/NHS solution (4:1 w/w) prepared in an acetone/DI water mixture (4:1 v/v). Cross-linking was performed for 24 h, comprising 6 h at room temperature followed by 18 h at 4 °C. The final EDC/NHS concentration was maintained at 0.7% w/v, with the solvent volume adjusted proportionally to scaffold mass (100 mL per gram of scaffold). Post-cross-linking, scaffolds were thoroughly washed three times with DI water to remove residual cross-linking agents and stored in DI water at 4 °C. A final freeze-drying step was carried out to obtain the purified, cross-linked scaffolds. To prepare the Gel/FD scaffold, FD (3 mM) was added to a gelatin solution (5% w/v) and mixed at 400 rpm. For the Gel/HA/FD scaffold, this FD-containing solution was combined with the previously prepared Gel/HA mixture. Both formulations were stirred at 40 °C for 1 h, then 800 μL of each was cast into 48-well plates, frozen, and freeze-dried.

Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy Analysis

The functional groups present in the scaffolds was determined using an ALPHA-P spectrometer (Bruker Optics Inc., Billerica, MA, USA) in the 400–4000 cm–1 wavenumber range at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C).

Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM)

Morphological and elemental characterization of the scaffolds were investigated using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (Tescan, Czech Republic) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (Oxford, UK). Prior to observation, the scaffolds were sputter-coated with a thin gold layer to provide adequate surface conductivity. SEM micrographs were captured under high vacuum at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV and a working distance of 5 mm. To identify the constituent elements specifically calcium (Ca), phosphorus (P), oxygen (O), nitrogen (N), and carbon (C) EDS analysis was conducted at 10 kV with a working distance of 10 mm.

Porosity Assessment

Porosity was evaluated via liquid displacement by immersing the scaffolds in absolute ethanol for 45 min at room temperature (RT). Porosity was calculated using the following equation: Porosity(%)=V1−V3V2−V3×100 Where: V 1, the initial volume of ethanol; V 2, the volume of ethanol and ethanol-immersed scaffold; V 3, the volume of remaining ethanol after discarding the scaffold.

Swelling Ratio Assessment

Swelling behavior was evaluated by immersing the scaffolds in PBS (pH 7.4) at RT for up to 72 h (1, 3, 6, 24, and 72 h). Water uptake was calculated as follows: Swelling(%)=Ww−WdWd×100 Where: W d, the weight of the dry scaffold; W w, the weight of the wet scaffold

Degradation Rate Assessment

Scaffolds were incubated in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C for 14 days. Thereafter, the scaffolds were rinsed with DI water and dried overnight at 50 °C. Degradation was then calculated using the following equation: Degradation(%)=Wi−WdWd×100 Where: W d, the weight of the dry scaffold; W i, the weight of the incubated scaffold.

Rheological Analysis

Rheological measurements were performed using an ARES-G2 rotational rheometer (TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA) equipped with a 8 mm parallel plate (Stainless steel). All experiments were conducted under low-amplitude oscillatory shear within the linear viscoelastic region (LVER). Measurements were run in triplicate. The LVER was initially determined by an amplitude sweep test over a strain range of 0.1–100% at a fixed frequency of 1 Hz and 22 °C. Subsequently, frequency sweep tests were performed at a constant strain of 1% and 22 °C over an angular frequency range of 0.1–100 rad/s.

NO Synthesis Measurement

The generation of NO was detected using Griess reagent. Briefly, 1× Griess Reagent was added to each scaffold seeded with Saos-2 cells, then allowed to react for 15 min. The NO generation was measured at the absorbance of 540 nm.

Antioxidant Performance Measurement

The antioxidant properties of the scaffold were evaluated by ABTS free radicals assay following the supplier’s guidelines. Briefly, after reacting at 37 °C for 15 min, the mixture was measured at 405 nm. The PBS solution was used as the blank control.

Evaluation of Intracellular ROS

Cells were seeded onto scaffolds and cultured for 14 days. Following this period, Saos-2 cells were treated with or without hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 100 μM) to induce oxidative stress. ROS production was assessed using the fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCF-DA). Cells were exposed to DCF-DA for 15 min at 37 °C in the absence of light. After incubation, ROS generation was monitored in real time using the live cell analysis system.

Calcium Deposition Assessment

Saos-2 cells were seeded on the scaffolds and cultured under the osteogenic differentiation conditions. After 14 days of induction, the medium was removed, and cells were rinsed with PBS and fixed in 95% ethanol for 15 min at RT. Extracellular matrix (ECM) mineralization was then stained with Alizarin Red solution (40 mM, pH 4.2). Excess dye was washed off with distilled water, and calcified nodules were visualized under a light microscope. The dye was extracted from the scaffold, and absorbance was measured at 405 nm using an ELISA reader. Alizarin Red concentration was quantified using a standard curve.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using Prism (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA) and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All experiments were independently performed in triplicate. Statistical significance was assessed using Student’s t-test test or one-way ANOVA, with p-values reported accordingly. Comparisons not marked with significance symbols indicate no statistically significant difference (p ≥ 0.05).
