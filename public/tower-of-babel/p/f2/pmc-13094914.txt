Maternal care behaviors for preterm babies: Insights from a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Sonya Sequeira, Kanagavalli Rajgopal, Prakash Robert Mario Saldanha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:The primary role of nurses in a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) is to ensure that the mothers are competent enough to take care of their preterm babies at home. The current study aims to evaluate the extant practices and methods of mothers in the care of preterm babies and provides important inputs into discharge decisions of a NICU nurse.MATERIALS AND METHODS:In this descriptive cross-sectional study, 194 mothers of preterm babies were selected using a purposive sampling technique. A rating scale on preterm baby care was employed to gather data on the prevalent practices by mothers.RESULTS:The overall practice scores of mothers in preterm care showed a mean of 31.66 ± 2.86. Among these mothers, 137 (70.6%) demonstrated moderate practice levels, and 57 (29.4%) showed good practice. Area-wise analysis indicated the following mean scores: Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC), 11.82 ± 1.63; breastfeeding practices, 8.30 ± 1.07; mother-baby interaction, 2.49 ± 0.84; hand hygiene, 1.43 ± 0.67; and administration of oral supplements, 1.20 ± 0.89.CONCLUSION:The current study results revealed the prevalence of suboptimal practice in major areas of preterm care. Thus, a health education package was developed to help mothers gain knowledge and practice in preterm baby care. The authors emphasize the need for creating better healthcare education for mothers and suggest NICUs to encourage adopting healthy practices by mothers in preterm.

Introduction

A preterm baby is defined as a neonate born 37 weeks before gestation. Based on gestational age, preterm babies can be categorized as extremely preterm (<28 weeks), very preterm (28–32 weeks), and moderate to late preterm (32–37 weeks).[1] One out of every 10 newborns in the United States was reported to be born prematurely in 2021, with the preterm birth rates rising from 10.1% to 10.5%.[2] Developing countries contribute to 60% of premature births globally. India reported 13.4 million preterm infants in 2020, resulting in 1 in 10 infants being “born too soon”.[3] About 1 million newborn deaths reported globally in 2021 were attributed to preterm birth-related complications. About 75% of such cases could have been avoided with affordable treatments, including wearing warm clothing, breastfeeding, and providing basic respiratory care by mothers to the babies.[1]

Preterm babies face numerous challenges during the neonatal period, including respiratory problems, feeding difficulties, poor temperature regulation, and an increased susceptibility to infections, which often require admission to a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). The mother-baby bonding is interrupted by admission to the NICU.[4] World Health Organization (WHO) advocates the use of Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC) to effectively meet preterm babies’ needs for warmth, breastfeeding on demand, stimulation, protection from infection, and mothers’ attention.[5]

Post-discharge from the hospital, a premature baby faces several health-related challenges. Mothers need to be provided with adequate information and timely support on preterm baby care well before discharge.[67] Frequent hospitalizations cause feeding difficulties and jaundice in the infants; besides, 7.4% of re-hospitalizations occur due to preventable conditions such as diarrhea, infection, and milk aspiration.[8910] Mothers feel equally traumatized by the unexpected birth of a preterm baby. The preterm needs NICU admission for survival, and the care is predominantly provided by the neonatal nurses. Limited NICU access in many areas disrupts mother-preterm infant bonding, a core dyadic need. Mothers express their trauma from witnessing the preterm baby in the NICU. A prolonged NICU admission sets feelings of helplessness and fear in the mother. The mothers often struggle with insecurities related to the care of the preterm baby, building up stress and anxiety. Therefore, there is a need for seeking active engagement of mothers in caring for preterms in the NICU as it reduces stress, increases self-confidence, and develops competency.[111213141516]

Another challenge the mothers undergo is a lack of awareness in taking care of the preterm at home post-discharge or on their own. The situation is aggravated by factors such as the history of childbirth, the mother’s socioeconomic and health conditions. Mothers face several problems in identifying the special needs of the preterm baby. Moreover, the idea of committing errors can torment mothers at home. To resolve the matter, mothers need the necessary knowledge and skills to care for a preterm baby independently at home. As the necessary skills are gradually acquired, the mother gains confidence to participate actively in the health care of the preterm baby.[111213141516] Mothers need to be prepared to care for a preterm baby before the mother-preterm dyad is discharged from the hospital.[151617]

The nurses at a NICU are responsible for ascertaining a smooth hospital-to-home transition of the mother and the preterm baby. An in-hospital evaluation of the mothers’ perceptions, knowledge, and practices reveals mothers’ preparedness for discharge from the hospital. It is necessary to develop suitable instruments and guidelines to evaluate a mother’s predischarge readiness to care for the preterm infant at home.[161718]

Preterm birth is the predominant cause of morbidity and mortality among infants. Maternal and child healthcare services continue to face serious challenges and concerns about preterm births because premature babies need additional medical care during their hospital stay, as well as after their discharge from the hospital. The responsibilities of taking care of a preterm baby fall primarily upon the mother. Therefore, proper feeding, maintaining a stable body temperature, good hygiene, preventing infections, and early stimulation are all critical components for the survival and healthy development of preterm infants. However, many mothers report difficulty in taking proper care of their preterm babies on account of limited knowledge, emotional stress, low self-confidence, and inadequate post-natal guidance. Furthermore, some mothers’ caregiving practices are influenced by their cultural beliefs, and/or barriers to accessing follow-up support services. Though huge advancements have been made in the medical management of preterm infants, studies are inadequate in understanding mothers’ views and daily caregiving practices after mothers take their preterm babies home. Therefore, assessment of the way mothers care for their preterm babies is needed to identify gaps and strengthen maternal support and education programs.

Though the literature review reveals that examination of preterm birth primarily investigates clinical outcomes and neonatal survival rates, studies investigating maternal caregiving practices in the home environment are limited. As numerous existing studies are based on hospital-setting observations, they may not accurately represent real-life caregiving experiences.

Additionally, limited research has investigated mothers’ perspectives on the care of their preterm babies and the manner in which these perspectives affect their caregiving practices. Furthermore, context-specific evidence investigating the relationships between maternal socio-demographic characteristics and caregiving practices is lacking. These limitations indicate that a structured study with a larger group of mothers would allow for a better understanding of their experiences and challenges in taking care of preterm babies. This study has endeavored to assess the behavior and practices of mothers in caring for preterm babies to provide important inputs into discharge decisions. The structured study was conducted for a larger group of mothers.

Materials and Procedure

Study design and setting

In this descriptive cross-sectional study design, 194 mothers of preterm babies were admitted to the postnatal ward of a selected hospital, Mangalore, India. The data were collected from July 2021 to November 2022.

Study participants and sampling

The participants were selected based on the specified inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Inclusion criteria: The following were the study inclusion criteria:

The study samples were selected using a purposive sampling technique. The instruments used for data collection were the baseline characteristics of the preterm baby and the mother. The mother’s practice in the care of a preterm baby was assessed in the NICU with a 3-point rating scale. The content validity of the scale was ascertained by nine subject experts. The valid suggestions given by the subject experts on the scale were considered. The final scale consisted of baseline characteristics of the preterm baby with six items and baseline maternal characteristics with ten items. Overall, the rating scale on preterm baby care had 25 items. Scale reliability was determined by pretesting it on ten samples. The inter-rater reliability based on the intraclass correlation coefficient was 0.85. This indicates the scale’s reliability.

Sample size estimation

The sample size for the study was determined considering the results of previous research on mothers’ practice, where moderate practice (P = 40%) was found to be a predominant behavior.[19] Assuming that a population proportion Z for a 95% confidence interval is 1.96 and the margin of error is 7%, the calculated sample size was 188 mothers of preterm babies, and rounded off to 200. The final sample size was 194 mothers of preterm babies, as six mothers withdrew their consent.

Ethical consideration

Prior ethical permission was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (Protocol No.: YEC-1/2021/027). The study samples were selected based on the defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Purposive sampling was employed to select 200 mothers of preterm babies. The purpose and duration of the study were explained clearly to the participants. Prior informed written consent was obtained from the respondents, who were assured of anonymity and confidentiality of information obtained. The data were collected using a baseline proforma and the rating scale from 200 mothers of preterm babies. The behavior of the mothers was observed upon their visit to the preterm baby inside the NICU. Six out of the 200 mothers withdrew their consent subsequently. Finally, the data collected from 194 mothers of preterm babies were compiled for data analysis.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27.0. Demographic data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, frequency, and percentage. The association of mothers’ practice with selected demographic variables was assessed using a Chi-square test. Statistical significance was denoted with a P value < 0.05, and the data were represented using tables and graphs appropriately.

The practice of clothing the baby showed that 158 (81.4%) mothers always used a diaper/napkin; 115 (59.3%) always put on a cap/bonnet/scarf covering the preterm baby’s head; 109 (56.2%) always mummified their babies, and 89 (45.9%) always kept the baby and their clothes clean. However, 105 (54.1%) mothers maintained clean babies and clothes sometimes. In hand hygiene, 163 (84%) mothers never followed all six steps of handwashing, and 151 (77%) mothers never followed the hand rub for 5 min. The majority of mothers (170, 87.6%) used hand sanitizer both before and after touching the baby.

In the breastfeeding technique, 131 (67.5%) mothers followed the practice of cleaning the breast properly; 109 (56.2%) used appropriate positioning for feeding; 132 (68%) checked the latching of the baby to the breast; 141 (72.7%) woke the baby between feeds; 102 (52.6) switched to the second breast after one is empty. Most of the mothers, 151 (77.8%), always burped the baby after feeds. In the administration of oral supplements to the preterm baby, 130 (67%) mothers sometimes followed the practice of measuring the correct dose of the supplement, and 102 (52.6%) adopted appropriate techniques while administering supplements. In the interaction of mother and baby, 134 (69.1%) mothers sometimes interacted with their babies while awake/feeding; 143 (73.7%) were able to recognize baby’s engagement cues sometimes, and 108 (55.7%) never recognized baby’s disengagement cues.

Association of the practice of mothers with selected baseline characteristics of preterm babies

A statistically significant association was found in age, education, residence, number of children, type of delivery, parity and previous exposure to preterm care with χ2 = 23.13 (P < 0.01), χ2 = 12.80 (P = 0.005), χ2 = 7.97 (P = 0.005), χ2 = 54.64 (P < 0.01), χ2 = 7.32 (P = 0.007), χ2 = 85.60 (P < 0.01), and χ2 = 7.1 (P = 0.008), respectively. There was no association observed with occupation χ2 = 3.39 (P = 0.07), type of family χ2 = 0.23 (P = 0.63), and monthly family income χ2 = 2.0 (P = 0.37), respectively.

Discussion

An extensive literature search was done on the care of the preterm baby. The researcher came across multiple research studies on the knowledge of mothers about preterm baby care. There are only a few studies on practice. In the present study setting, each mother was taught care of a preterm baby, which included Kangaroo Mother Care, clothing the baby, hand hygiene, breastfeeding technique, measurement, and oral administration of supplements such as vitamins and minerals’ interaction with the infant. The mothers were guided with the practice during each contact with the preterm baby, but the compliance with the practice was not assessed. In view of assessing the pre-discharge preparedness of the mothers, a descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted on practices of mothers with preterm babies.

In this study, the practice of preterm baby care was assessed among 194 mothers. The results revealed the overall mean of practice scores among mothers was 31.66 ± 2.86, with a mean percentage of 63.33%. Furthermore, 70.6% mothers had moderate practice, 29.4% had good practice, and none had poor practice. The study findings are corroborated by the pretest results of a quasi-experimental study conducted by Rajeswari, who assessed the effectiveness of multimodal intervention on comprehensive home care management of preterm babies among mothers of preterm at a pediatric tertiary care hospital, Chennai.[19] In this study, 60% of mothers had a poor practice score, and 40.0% had a moderate practice score. Further, the results are also supported by a descriptive study conducted in Andhra Pradesh by Anusha.[20] The knowledge and practice regarding the care of preterm babies were assessed among 30 mothers. The study findings on prevalent practices revealed that 4 (13.3%) mothers showed poor practice, 19 (63.3%) had fair practice, and 7 (23.4%) had good practice.[20] This reveals the prevalence of suboptimal practice among mothers nearing discharge. This would have a negative impact on the hospital-to-home transition of the mother and preterm dyad, as the mother needs to take care of the preterm baby independently. The findings suggest that mothers’ practice in preterm baby care needs to be assessed individually, and necessary support needs to be provided by nurses.

In the present study, results of the area-wise practice on care of preterm babies reveal that in the practice of KMC- placement of the baby and clothing for the baby, the mean was 11.82 ± 1.63, and 6.43 ± 1.09, with mean percentages of 84.43% and 80.35%, respectively. This suggests good practice. In addition, item-wise practice on KMC revealed that the following components were taken care of always: placing the preterm baby in a vertical position 194 (100%), direct skin-to-skin contact on the chest 174 (89.7%), preterm babies positioned with legs flexed in a frog position 135 (69.6%), cheeks in contact with the mothers’ chest 132 (68%), preterm baby fixed firmly to the chest 135 (69.6%). Though 96 (49.5%) mothers checked the breathing of the baby frequently, 95 (49%) mothers followed it sometimes, 94 (48.5%) mothers frequently stimulated the baby, and 98 (50.5%) did it sometimes. However, the findings were not in line with a descriptive study to assess the knowledge, attitude, and practices of mothers regarding premature baby care in a community, Lahore, Pakistan, in which inadequate practice was observed in the areas of KMC and 53 (34.7%) mothers concurred that extra thermal care for preterm includes KMC, baby hats, blankets, overhead heaters, and incubators for preterm babies.[21] Furthermore, in the present study, the mean was 8.30 ± 1.07 with a mean percentage of 69.16% in the area of breastfeeding feeding, which shows moderate practice. In regard to the practice of mothers regarding feeding techniques, 131 (67.5%) mothers followed cleaning the breast properly before feeding; 109 (56.2%) took an appropriate position for feeding; 132 (68%) checked the latching of the baby to the breast; 141 (72.7%) woke the baby between feeds; 102 (52.6%) switched to the second breast after one became empty. Most of the mothers, 151 (77.8%), always burped their babies after feeds. These findings were again contradicted by the earlier study that showed poor practice where, out of 150 mothers, only 54 (36%) were neutral about the extra support for breastfeeding, e.g. expressing and cup or tube feeding, supplemented breast milk if the indication was important for preterm babies.[21] This suggests that mothers are discharged without being empowered to take care of their preterm babies. If mothers are not prepared well enough to take care of their preterm babies while in the hospital, the care would be compromised after discharge. It would add to the burden of under-5 morbidity and mortality rates of a country.

The present study showed a statistically significant association between the practice of mothers in preterm baby care with baseline characteristics of preterm babies, such as plurality χ2 = 10.48 (P = 0.001) and birth order χ2 = 81.36 (P < 0.01). Similarly, a highly significant association was noticed between mothers practice with baseline maternal characteristics such as age χ2 = 23.13 (P < 0.01), education χ2 = 12.80 (P = 0.005), residence χ2 = 7.97 (P = 0.005), number of children χ2 = 54.64 (P < 0.01), type of delivery χ2 = 7.32 (P = 0.007), parity χ2 = 85.60 (P < 0.01), and previous exposure to preterm care χ2 = 7.1 (P = 0.008). The findings were contradicted by a study conducted by Anusha, where the study found no statistically significant association between practice scores of mothers with socio-demographic variables such as age (2.04), religion (2.96), type of family (2.73), educational status (15.51), occupation (12.59) family income (9.49), parity (12.59), gestational age of preterm (5.99), weight of the baby (9.49).[20] In a study by Kapti et al.,[22] identified that post-discharge, maternal factors such as young age, low educational level, rural living, lack of partner support, and unfavorable conditions of the preterm baby, such as very low baby weight, could increase stress in mothers with preterm babies.

This study highlights the need for assessing the competence of mothers in preterm baby care before discharge from the hospital. It also places importance on creating suitable awareness in mothers by nurses, the government, and society to have healthy preterm infant development. Periodic assessment of the mothers will enable nurses to identify mothers who need further guidance. Salam et al.[23] explored gaps and opportunities for improved care for preterm babies among adolescent mothers and communities in rural Bangladesh and employed a combination of factors, including local knowledge, socio-cultural practices, and health systems challenges that influence knowledge of, and care for, preterm babies among adolescent and adult mothers. The policies for discharge of a preterm baby from the NICU differ, as several factors contribute to the discharge. A Cohort study by Behera et al.[24] on preterm neonates in eastern India reported that birth weight and gestation are important factors for the discharge of very low birth-weight preterm babies. Nearing discharge, an evaluation of mothers’ preparedness in preterm baby care is necessary to identify any gaps. The discharge criteria of the mother-preterm dyad must include a thorough evaluation of the mother’s skills in preterm baby care. Zores et al.[25] evaluated a tool, “Step by step with my baby”, to involve parents in preterm infant care. The existing resources related to the management of preterm babies for mothers are found to be limited in developing countries and are a major constraint.[20] The mothers should be empowered in caring for their preterms in the hospital, and any concerns need to be addressed before discharge. A confident mother would help a preterm baby catch up in growth and attain well-being.

Analysis of the views and practices of 194 mothers of preterm babies in this research revealed a wide range of views about feeding, thermal care, hygiene, follow-up care, and recognizing vulnerability symptoms. This finding highlights a need for structured, mother-focused educational programs. Therefore, this study will provide nurses and other neonatal healthcare providers with the necessary information they can use when tailoring counselling and discharge education to meet the specific needs and concerns of the mothers. This will boost their confidence and ability to provide adequate care for their preterm babies in the home setting.

The results of this study demonstrate that maternal viewpoint assessment should be incorporated into all routine neonatal care provided to mothers of preterm babies. Understanding the viewpoints of mothers will enable healthcare systems to develop supportive postnatal programs, for example, follow-up counselling and community-based monitoring. In addition, incorporating maternal feedback into the planning of neonatal care may lead to better continuity of care and fewer preventable complications among preterm infants.

This study contributes to the limited number of studies that have examined maternal viewpoints toward caring for preterm babies. The results of this study support family-centered and mother-inclusive care models, thereby supporting the belief that maternal viewpoints will continue to be an essential component of successful neonatal care strategies.

Future studies should focus on developing and evaluating structured health education or awareness packages to address the identified gaps in knowledge and attitudes of mothers. Interventions and longitudinal studies could also investigate the relationship between maternal viewpoints and the quality of care provided to preterm infants, as well as the impact on the health of those babies.

Relevance of the study

Mothers play a vital role in the survival and healthy growth of their preterm babies and are responsible for providing care for them outside of a hospital. By using systematic methods for collecting views of 200 mothers, this study provides meaningful insights into the behavior, needs, and challenges of mothers caring for preterm babies. Hence, the findings of this study are very relevant to professionals who work in healthcare, policymakers, and program developers working to create effective, mother-centered neonatal care interventions to positively affect the outcome of preterm infants.

Conclusion

Mothers need to be aware of preterm birth and its consequences. The burden of this responsibility can be endured only when the mothers are well-prepared to care for the preterm baby. The practice of preterm baby care requires great skill and guidance. If the mother is not guided well enough on preterm baby care in the hospital, then the readiness of the mother to be independent at home is difficult. An assessment of mothers’ practice on care of the preterm baby may guide further action. In the present study, though many mothers demonstrated moderate practice in care of preterm babies, they were not found in areas of feeding techniques, mother and baby interaction, hand hygiene, and administration of oral supplements. Therefore, a health education package was developed in order to help mothers gain knowledge and practice on preterm baby care.
