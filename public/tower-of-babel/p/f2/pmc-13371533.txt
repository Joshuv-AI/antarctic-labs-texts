Global burden of antimicrobial-resistant Staphylococcus aureus lower respiratory tract infections in older adults: a secondary analysis of the MICROBE database (1990–2021)
Authors: Kunchen Wei, Qingzhong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs) remain a leading cause of global infectious disease mortality, with Staphylococcus aureus emerging as a particular concern among older adults. The global spread of antimicrobial resistance, especially methicillin resistance (MRSA), represents a critical public health issue and limits treatment options in the elderly. In this study, we quantified global mortality trends of S. aureus LRTIs in older adults (≥ 70 years) from 1990 to 2021.MethodsWe analyzed MICROBE database data (1990–2021) for deaths and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) from S. aureus LRTIs in older adults (≥ 70 years) across 204 countries and territories. We extracted antimicrobial resistance-attributable deaths and DALYs, defined as excess mortality from resistant versus susceptible infections. Geographic aggregation followed the Global Burden of Disease framework at global, regional (21 regions), and super-regional (7 super-regions) levels. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS and GraphPad Prism, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05.ResultsS. aureus LRTI deaths in older adults increased threefold from 1990 to 2021. In 2021, LRTIs caused 3.54 million deaths globally, with 1.94 million deaths (54.8% of all-age deaths) among older adults. Sub-Saharan Africa showed the highest death and DALY rates, while high-income countries had the lowest. S. aureus caused 387,158 LRTI deaths worldwide (10.9% of all LRTIs mortality). Among older adults, S. aureus caused 250,347 deaths, with the largest absolute number in high-income settings but the highest mortality rate in sub-Saharan Africa (74.7 per 100 000). MRSA remains a persistent threat over three decades. The significant regional disparities and sustained upward trends underscore the urgent need for targeted interventions, improved antimicrobial stewardship programs, and strengthening infection prevention strategies, especially in high-risk regions and vulnerable elderly population.Clinical trialNot applicable.

Introduction

Lower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs) remain a leading cause of global infectious disease mortality, primarily involving the trachea, bronchi, and pulmonary parenchyma. In 2019, LRTIs caused 2.49 million deaths worldwide, with 1.23 million among older adults (≥ 70 years). Although Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae type b, influenza virus, and respiratory syncytial virus are major pathogens, Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) is of particular concern [1]. Older adults are particularly vulnerable to S. aureus LRTIs due to immunosenescence, a higher prevalence of comorbidities such as COPD and diabetes, the escalating burden of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), and frequent contact with healthcare facilities. This Gram-positive coccus colonizes human skin and mucosal surfaces and causes community- and hospital-acquired infections, including pneumonia, bloodstream infections, and infective endocarditis [2].

The global spread of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in S. aureus, especially methicillin resistance (MRSA), represents a critical public health issue [3]. Treatment options for MRSA are limited in older adults due to drug toxicity and comorbidities. Additionally, antimicrobial overuse has driven expanding resistance, with epidemic MRSA strains resistant to various β-lactam antibiotics and increasingly resistant to aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, and macrolides [4]. This multidrug resistance severely restricts therapeutic options. The persistence of S. aureus is also driven by its complex molecular pathogenesis. The bacterium can utilize a diverse array of virulence factors, including adhesins and toxins, to evade host immune defenses and establish infection [5].

Given these challenges, we used the MICROBE database to analyze the global burden and pathogen profile of LRTIs. The analysis spans global, regional, and super-regional levels across 21 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) regions and 7 super-regions. We focused on S. aureus-associated LRTIs, including MRSA, in older adults. The objective is to generate contemporary, evidence-based data to guide the development of effective infection prevention and control (IPC) strategies and antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) programs.

Materials and methods

Data sources

We used data from the Multidisciplinary Investigation of Clinical Research Outcomes and Burden Estimates (MICROBE) database, developed by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME, University of Washington), which provides standardized estimates of fatal and nonfatal outcomes of infections, pathogens, and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) worldwide. The methodology underlying MICROBE has been previously described in The Lancet [6]. Data are publicly accessible for non-commercial research via registration (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/microbe/) and acceptance of the Free Non-Commercial User Agreement. Ethical approval was not required for this analysis of aggregated, de-identified data.

Data collection

We analyzed MICROBE estimates from 1990 to 2021 on deaths and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) associated with 22 pathogens and 84 pathogen–drug combinations across 204 countries and territories. Indicators included absolute death, mortality per 100,000, and age-standardized DALY rates. Age-standardization was performed using the GBD world population standard to facilitate comparisons among diverse populations [6]. DALYs were calculated as the sum of years of life lost (YLLs) and years lived with disability (YLDs) [6].

We extracted AMR-attributable deaths and DALYs, defined as excess mortality from resistant versus susceptible S. aureus infections. The burden of AMR was estimated using two counterfactual scenarios: (1) the associated burden (based on a counterfactual of no infection), and (2) the attributable burden (based on a counterfactual of infections caused by susceptible pathogens). In this study, we focused on AMR-attributable mortality. This burden was modeled by multiplying the number of infectious deaths by the mortality Population Attributable Fraction (PAF), which is derived from the prevalence of resistance and the excess relative risk (RR) of death [6]. We extracted data for specific resistance profiles defined by the MICROBE database, including Methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), and S. aureus strains resistant to Fluoroquinolones, Macrolides, Trimethoprim (sulfamethoxazole), and Vancomycin. We included pathogen–drug combinations where first-line antimicrobials were ineffective. Estimates are reported with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs). Geographic aggregation followed the GBD standard framework at three levels: global, 21 regions, and 7 GBD super-regions.

Statistical analysis

Data were prepared in Microsoft Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) and analyzed with SPSS (version 27.0.1; IBM Corp, 2020) and GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Temporal trends were assessed using linear regression and evaluated based on 95% Uncertainty Intervals (UIs), and differences in mortality proportions and resistance rates between age groups and regions were assessed using Pearson’s χ² tests. Two-tailed p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Global LRTIs burden in older adults, 2021

In 2021, LRTIs were the leading infectious cause of death worldwide, accounting for 3.54 million deaths (95% UI: 3.22–3.86 million). Older adults accounted for 1.94 million of these deaths (54.8% of total). This group had the highest age-specific mortality rate at 391.7 per 100,000 (95% UI: 345.7–437.8) (Table 1). Men experienced greater mortality than women, both in absolute deaths (1.92 vs. 1.61 million) and in rates (48.7 vs. 41.1 per 100,000) (Table 1).

Sub-Saharan Africa recorded the greatest burden, with the highest all-age mortality (61.9 per 100,000 [95% UI: 52.0–71.8]) and DALY rate (3,417.8 per 100,000 [95% UI: 2,698.3–4,137.4]). S. aureus-associated LRTIs in Older Adults, 2021

In 2021, Staphylococcus aureus was responsible for 250,347 deaths from lower-respiratory-infections among adults aged 70 years and older worldwide (95%UI: 219,686–281,009) (Table 2). Regional analysis revealed a distinct dichotomy: while high-income regions bore the largest absolute number of deaths, sub-Saharan Africa experienced the highest mortality rates. The largest absolute toll was observed in High-income (77,295.7 deaths [95%UI: 65,738–88,853.4]), whereas the highest mortality rate occurred in sub-Saharan Africa (74.7 per 100,000 [95%UI: 66.4–83.0]). DALY rates were also greatest in sub-Saharan Africa (1,096.1 per 100,000 [95%UI: 975.7–1,216.6]) (Table 2). Among deaths attributable to antimicrobial-resistant bacterial infections in 2021, S. pneumoniae (51,912 deaths; 24.3%) and S. aureus (42,625; 20.0%) prevailed, together with gram-negative pathogens, such as Acinetobacter baumannii (34,297; 16.1%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (30,077, 14.1%), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (22,821; 10.7%). These five pathogens caused 181,731 deaths, or 85.2% of LRTIs mortality in this group. Gram-positive pathogens as a whole contributed 44.3% of all deaths (Fig. 2).

S. aureus-associated LRTIs in Older Adults, 1990–2021

S. aureus-associated LRTI deaths increased by 200% (a threefold increase) over three decades, from 83,363 in 1990 and peaking at 260,691 in 2019, before declining slightly to 250,347 in 2021. Mortality rates similarly increased from 41.3 per 100,000 in 1990 to a peak of 56.3 in 2015, before declining to 50.6 in 2021. DALY rose continuously, from 1.15 million in 1990 to 3.37 million in 2019, with a slight decline to 3.29 million in 2021, a 184.5% increase over 30 years. The DALY rate rose from 601.19 per 100,000 in 1993 to 724.5 in 2015, declining to 664.53 in 2021 (an 8.3% reduction from peak) (Fig. 3).

S. aureus-associated antimicrobial resistance mortality in older adults, 1990–2021

Methicillin resistance (MRSA) mortality trends varied by resistance mechanism (Fig. 4). MRSA remained the largest contributor, with mortality rising sharply from 11.1 per 100,000 in 1990 to 17.3 in 2005, then stabilizing and settling at 15.2 in 2021— 36.4% above the 1990 baseline. Fluoroquinolone resistance increased from 0.28 per 100,000 in 1990 to 5.72 in 2015, representing a 1,922% increase before declining by 44.5% in 2019. In 2021, rates remained 1,073% above 1990 (3.32 per 100,000). Trimethoprim resistance declined progressively from a peak of 2.00 in 1995 to 1.08 in 2021 (-45.8%). Vancomycin resistance decreased consistently, with mortality decreasing from 1.11 in 1990 to 0.79 in 2021.

MRSA-associated mortality in older adults, 1990–2021

Between 1990 and 2021, MRSA-related mortality in older adults worsened markedly (Fig. 5). Deaths increased from 22,481 to 75,038, with mortality rising from 11.1 to 15.2 per 100,000. Rates in this age group exceeded those in younger populations (P < 0.001) (Fig. 6). In 2021, MRSA mortality among older adults (15.18 per 100,000) was 6.7 times higher than in adults aged 50–69 (2.27 per 100,000), 9.5-fold higher than in children under 5 years (1.60 per 100,000), and over 70-fold higher than in those aged 5–49 (0.21 per 100,000). DALYs mirrored these trends with sustained growth throughout the study period (Figs. 5 and 6).

Discussion

Antimicrobial resistance is a defining public health threat of the twenty-first century, with profound social and economic consequences [7]. This analysis quantifies the global S. aureus LRTIs burden across 204 countries from 1990 to 2021, disaggregated by 21 GBD regions and seven super-regions. Temporal trends from 1990 to 2021 confirm that S. aureus resistance—notably MRSA—remains a substantial and persistent threat among older adults and in specific high-burden regions.

S. aureus was the second leading bacterial cause of LRTIs mortality in 2021, accounting for nearly 387,000 deaths, or 10.9% of all LRTIs mortalities. Gram-positive organisms caused 43.5% of global LRTI deaths, with S. aureus representing nearly one-quarter of this burden. Among older adults, S. pneumoniae and S. aureus remained as the leading pathogens. These findings align with international surveillance data showing that most S. aureus infections occur in individuals aged ≥ 65 years, with incidence highest among those ≥ 85 years [8]. Multiple clinical studies support advanced age as an independent risk factor for mortality from S. aureus infection [9–11]. The observed male predominance likely reflects accelerated immunosenescence compounded by a higher burden of comorbidities, such as COPD, driven by historical smoking and occupational exposures [12].

Significant regional heterogeneity is evident in the distribution of Gram-positive pathogens responsible for LRTIs [13–17]. While S. pneumoniae often dominates, S. aureus consistently ranks among the top pathogens across Asian, European, and African settings, though its relative prevalence varies by local epidemiology and healthcare infrastructure.

Our 2021 estimates attribute 250,347 LRTI deaths in older adults to S. aureus worldwide (95%UI: 219,686–281,009), with the highest absolute burden in High-income (77,295.7 deaths [95%UI: 65,738–88,853.4]). This reflects both the region’s vast population base (> 2 billion) and its rapid demographic aging. East Asia is now the world’s oldest region; in Japan, over 30% of the population is ≥ 65 years, the group at highest risk for S. aureus pneumonia [18]. Structural disparities in healthcare further compound the burden: a multicenter study spanning 289 Chinese cities (2001–2019) documented regional imbalances in resources, with higher hospital bed density and fiscal investment in eastern regions compared to central and western areas [19]. Antibiotic misuse has also driven accelerated dissemination of resistant strains.

In high-income countries, absolute mortality is lower, but age-standardized rates are paradoxically the highest, driven largely by hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP). Intensive interventions, such as intubation and surgery, combined with high MRSA prevalence (exceeding 50% of S. aureus isolates in USA’s intensive care units), amplify mortality risk. Outbreaks in nursing homes further exacerbate mortality; with fatality rates in infected elderly residents reaching 30% [20]. Conversely, sub-Saharan Africa recorded the highest DALY rate (183.1 per 100,000), reflecting limited healthcare access, low vaccine coverage, environmental exposures, and co-endemic infections [21].

Long-term trends in older adults reveal a sustained, upward trajectory in both absolute deaths and mortality from S. aureus-associated LRTIs between 1990 and 2021. The transient decline in 2020–2021 aligns with global reports of reduced transmission of respiratory pathogens due to COVID-19 non-pharmaceutical interventions [22, 23]. Notably, MRSA-related mortality in 2021 remained 36.4% higher than in 1990, underscoring its entrenched role in driving global mortality burden.

MRSA emergence in community settings during the 1990s prompted intensive global surveillance and rapid implementation of infection-prevention measures [24]. The North-American ST8/USA300 clone, initially confined to hospitals, soon become the dominant community-associated MRSA (CA-MRSA) lineage across the continent and subsequently worldwide [25]. In addition to mecA-mediated methicillin resistance, USA300 commonly carries erm and msrA genes, conferring cross-resistance to macrolides and tetracyclines and thereby limiting treatment options. Although international surveillance data indicate declining MRSA mortality between 2005 and 2012, the burden in older adults remain substantial. The recent emergence of vancomycin-resistant S. aureus (VRSA), vancomycin-intermediate S. aureus (VISA), and heterogeneous VISA (hVISA) has become a major global concern [26].

Current treatment options for MRSA include vancomycin, clindamycin, linezolid, and new agents; however, their utility remains limited in older adults due to comorbidities [27, 28]. Over the past decade, the introduction of newer glycopeptides (telavancin, dalbavancin, and oritavancin) has expanded the therapeutic arsenal, but their widespread use in treating LRTIs among the geriatric population is often constrained by indication-specific limitations, such as the nephrotoxicity risk of telavancin and the restriction of dalbavancin to skin infections, as well as their high costs [29–33]. Beyond conventional antimicrobials, novel multidimensional approaches are under active investigation, including combination regimens, bacteriophage–antibiotic synergy, natural-product adjuvants, and photothermal therapies [34–38]. The persistently high mortality rates underscore the urgent need for Antimicrobial Stewardship (AMS) programs specifically tailored for long-term care facilities, alongside rigorous Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) measures to curb transmission.

Limitations

This analysis has several limitations. First, MICROBE estimates depend on surveillance data quality, which varies globally; their accuracy is inevitably constrained by deficiencies in primary data and by underlying methodological assumptions. True uncertainty may exceed reported 95% UIs. Second, estimates for low-resource regions, particularly sub-Saharan Africa, rely heavily on predictive modeling due to sparse primary surveillance data, which may affect the accuracy of burden estimates. Third, the aggregation of data at the GBD regional level may mask significant country-level variations in healthcare access and resistance patterns. Finally, we could not stratify by specific risk factors within the elderly population, nor could we definitively distinguish between colonization and pathogenic infection in the aggregated dataset. In older adults, the high prevalence of S. aureus colonization in long-term care settings complicates this distinction, potentially leading to diagnostic challenges and inappropriate antimicrobial use in clinical practice. Nevertheless, estimating this burden remains clinically relevant, as colonization often serves as a critical reservoir for transmission and a significant risk factor for subsequent invasive infection in this vulnerable group [39, 40].

Conclusions

This analysis demonstrates increasing S. aureus LRTIs burden in older adults globally. Our findings underscore the urgent need to prioritize targeted interventions and antimicrobial stewardship to prevent the growing burden of resistant pathogens. There is a critical need for improved microbiological surveillance and data collection infrastructure, particularly in high-burden, low-resource regions such as sub-Saharan Africa, where enhancing affordable diagnostic capacity is essential to inform local treatment guidelines.
