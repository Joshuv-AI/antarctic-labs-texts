Sulfotransferase signaling sustains fibroblast identity and antagonizes therapeutic cardiac reprogramming
Authors: Michaela R. Romero, Sean Murphy, Yu-Ling Chang, Aashna Lamba, Sara Ancel, Alicia Llorente, James Marchant, Chun-Teng Huang, Caroline Kumsta, Peter Andersen, Sanjeev Ranade, Ahmed I. Mahmoud, Chulan Kwon, Hudson H. Freeze, Peter D. Adams, Alessandra Sacco, Mark J. Ranek, Brooke M. Emerling, Yu Xin Wang, Alexandre R. Colas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Differentiated cells maintain their identity through active mechanisms that suppress alternative cell fates, but disrupting these barriers can enhance direct reprogramming for organ repair. Among the regulators of cell fate stability, glycosylation-associated genes have emerged as barriers to cardiac reprogramming. Here we show that carbohydrate sulfotransferases are central fate-stabilizing regulators, with CHST7 acting through CD44 to control nuclear JUNB levels, chromatin binding, and downstream transcriptional activity. Integrated RNA-seq and ATAC-seq analyses reveal that CHST7 maintains open chromatin at JUNB- and CTCF-enriched loci while restricting accessibility at MEF2C-enriched regions, collectively reinforcing fibroblast identity and suppressing cardiac fate acquisition. We further identify PIP4K2C as a downstream effector whose inhibition enhances cardiac reprogramming efficiency and improves myocardial repair in vivo. These findings define a sulfotransferase-dependent barrier to cell fate conversion with therapeutic implications for heart regeneration.

Introduction

Maintenance of cell identity is essential for differentiated cells to perform specialized functions and sustain multicellular life over time. Although generally stable under homeostatic conditions, groundbreaking studies1–4 have shown that cell identity can be reprogrammed towards alternate lineages through the forced expression of defined, lineage-specific transcription factor combinations. However, these studies also revealed that under control conditions, reprogramming efficiency is limited as only a small fraction (<10%) of fate challenged cells successfully convert to the destination cell type5–7. These observations suggest the existence of robust barrier mechanisms opposing cell fate deviation in response to identity challenges, thereby reinforcing the stability of differentiated cell states. However, in this context, the molecular nature and mechanistic architecture of such mechanisms remain poorly understood and currently limit the use of cell fate reprogramming for therapeutic purposes8,9.

We10 and others11,12 have shown that transcription factors play a central role in safeguarding fibroblast identity during cell fate reprogramming. A key mechanism by which transcription factors oppose reprogramming is through the activation of specialized gene programs that constrain cellular plasticity, thereby enforcing lineage stability and inhibiting fate conversion. Among these, carbohydrate sulfotransferase 2 (CHST2), emerged as a potent downstream barrier effector regulated by the AJSZ transcription factors10. Notably, CHST2 encodes a carbohydrate sulfotransferase13 that catalyzes the transfer of sulfate groups to the 6-O position of N-acetylglucosamine residues on specific proteoglycans14. Together, these findings suggest that yet-to-be-identified proteoglycans, and/or their modulation by glycosaminoglycan (GAG) modifications, might play a previously unrecognized role as key determinants of fibroblast identity maintenance during cell fate destabilization events.

Proteoglycans are extracellular matrix proteins consisting of a core protein synthesized in the endoplasmic reticulum and processed through the Golgi apparatus, where GAG chains undergo extensive elongation, epimerization and sulfation14–16. Golgi-resident sulfotransferases such as CHST2 catalyze GAG sulfation, thereby modulating ligand-binding affinities to growth factors, cytokines and extracellular matrix components17. These interactions, in turn, regulate proteoglycan activity and downstream signaling pathways implicated in diverse biological processes including, tissue morphogenesis18, stem cell homeostasis19,20, cell growth and differentiation21,22. However, the role of such cellular components in regulating cell fate stability in fibroblasts, the primary target cell type for regenerative approaches using fate reprogramming, has not been explored and is the focus of this study.

Using an established cardiac reprogramming assay10,23 as a model system to investigate mechanisms of cell fate stability, we systematically interrogated the proteoglycan biosynthetic pathway and uncovered carbohydrate sulfotransferases as an unrecognized class of reprogramming barriers, with CHST7 emerging as the most potent effector with conserved activity in mouse and human fibroblasts. By integrating a systems-level approach with systematic functional testing and orthogonal biochemical validation, we show that CHST7 mediates its barrier function by potentiating CD44-dependent signaling, thereby modulating the levels, chromatin binding, and transcriptional activity of the downstream fate-stabilizing factor JUNB. In this context, epistasis analysis reveals that the CHST7/CD44/JUNB fate-stabilizing axis operates as a cell state-independent, self-reinforcing regulatory circuit in both mouse and human fibroblasts. Furthermore, integrative RNA- and ATAC-seq analyses reveal that CHST7 stabilizes fibroblast identity by maintaining accessibility at JUNB/CTCF motif-enriched chromatin while restricting accessibility at regions enriched for the reprogramming factor MEF2C, thereby promoting fibroblast-specific gene expression while restricting activation of alternative lineage programs. Finally, we identify PIP4K2C as a critical downstream effector mediating the barrier function of the CHST7/CD44/JUNB axis. Remarkably, targeted inhibition of PIP4K2C combined overexpression of cardiac transcription factors, MEF2C, GATA4 and TBX5 (MGT), significantly enhanced cardiac function and repair compared with MGT alone, as assessed by echocardiography and CODEX spatial proteomics one month after myocardial infarction (MI). Collectively, this study identifies a CHST7-dependent sulfotransferase pathway that reinforces fibroblast resistance to cell fate reprogramming and unveils a promising target to enhance cardiac repair after injury.

To test this hypothesis, we systematically profiled all GAG biosynthetic genes expressed above 1 transcript per million (TPM) in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) (Fig. 1b), a threshold used to exclude transcriptional noise and retain biologically meaningful gene expression24. Next, we evaluated the role of each of these genes for their ability to modulate MEFs abiliy to reprogram into cardiomyocyte-like cells following MGT overexpression10,23. Briefly, individual siRNAs targeting each of the 31 genes involved in GAG biosynthesis were transfected into MEFs one day prior to doxycycline-induced MGT overexpression. Reprogramming efficiency was measured on day 3 by quantifying the percentage of Myh6-eGFP⁺ cells. Consistent with our initial observations, the two most potent barrier hits (P < 0.05; Fig. 1c, d), siChst7 and siChst2, target two carbohydrate sulfotransferases that catalyze the 6-O-sulfation of GAGs25.

The top barrier hit, CHST7, was selected for validation and cloned into a P2A–mCherry overexpression construct, enabling selective tracking of CHST7-overexpressing cells (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). Consistent with the barrier function described above, CHST7-overexpressing MEFs exhibited a ~40% reduction in reprogramming efficiency compared to mCherry controls (Fig. 1e, f). Next, we assessed whether this barrier role is conserved in human cells. CHST7 knockdown significantly enhanced reprogramming efficiency in human cardiac fibroblasts, as evidenced by increased expression of cardiac markers (ACTC1, NPPA, and NPPB) at day 3 and a marked increase of TNNT2⁺ staining area at day 21 following MGT induction compared to control conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2a–c). Together, these results identify CHST7 as a robust barrier to cardiac reprogramming conserved from mouse to human fibroblasts.

Carbohydrate sulfotransferases, such as CHST7, are Golgi-resident enzymes where they catalyze GAG sulfation26; however, the subcellular localization of endogenous CHST7 in fibroblasts has not been defined. Confocal imaging using a siRNA-validated antibody (Supplementary Fig. 2d, e) shows that CHST7 predominantly localizes to the perinuclear compartment in MEFs (Fig. 1g), where a subset of its signal (~30%) colocalizes with the trans-Golgi marker Golgin97 (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 2f, g), consistent with a role in Golgi-associated GAG sulfation.

Finally, to directly test the role of 6-O sulfation in the regulation of cell fate reprogramming competence, we treated MEFs with SULF2, an extracellular endosulfatase that selectively removes 6-O sulfate groups from GAGs27, to determine whether it would phenocopy CHST7 knockdown effect. Consistent with this model, SULF2 treatment dose-dependently enhanced cardiac reprogramming efficiency (Fig. 1i, j). Together, these findings identify CHST7 and the regulation of 6-O sulfation, as a previously unrecognized mechanism controling fibroblast competence for cardiac reprogramming.

CHST7 interacts with CD44 to restrict cardiac reprogramming

Carbohydrate sulfotransferases catalyze the transfer of sulfate groups onto specific proteoglycans, thereby modulating their signaling properties and associated downstream functions17. Based on this biochemical paradigm, we next hypothesized that CHST7 might oppose cardiac reprogramming by modulating the activity of yet-to-be identified barrier proteoglycans (Fig. 2a). To identify such proteoglycans, we profiled all known proteoglycan-encoding genes28 in MEFs (Fig. 2b). Next, to test whether CD44 is a likely substrate of CHST7, we examined their relative subcellular localization in MEFs using a siRNA-validated CD44 antibody (Supplementary Fig. 3d, e). Although CD44 is broadly distributed at the plasma membrane and within intracellular compartments as previously described29,30, colocalization analysis revealed an overlap between CHST7 and a subset of CD44 signal within the perinuclear region (Fig. 2e). Three-dimensional reconstruction further showed that ~32% of the CHST7 signal colocalizes with CD44 (Supplementary Fig. 3f, g). Consistent with this observation, proximity ligation assays (PLA) generated a robust and specific signal in the perinuclear compartment, indicating close association between CHST7 and CD44 in this region of the cell (Fig. 2f, g). Finally, co-immunoprecipitation using CD44 as bait in human dermal fibroblasts, followed by CHST7 immunoblotting, independently validated their physical interaction (Supplementary Fig. 3h). Together, these results support a model in which CD44 is a likely substrate for CHST7, with both proteins physically interacting in the perinuclear region of fibroblasts.

To test whether this interaction might modulate CD44 function, we overexpressed CHST7 and quantified CD44 levels by immunofluorescence. CHST7 overexpression led to a marked increase in CD44 abundance per cell (~1.8-fold, P < 0.001) relative to mCherry controls (Fig. 2h, i and Supplementary Fig. 3i), consistent with a model in which CHST7 promotes CD44 stabilization and/or accumulation in fibroblasts. Finally, to determine whether CD44 is required for CHST7-mediated barrier activity, we overexpressed CHST7 in MEFs and assessed cardiac reprogramming efficiency following CD44 knockdown or siControl treatment. Notably, siCd44 increased reprogramming efficiency by 47% in CHST7-overexpressing cells relative to siControl (Fig. 2j, k), supporting a model in which CD44 functions as a key downstream effector of the CHST7-dependent barrier to reprogramming (Fig. 2l).

CHST7/CD44 fate stabilizing axis controls JUNB levels, chromatin binding distribution, and transcription

Proteoglycan sulfation regulates signal transduction by altering ligand-receptor interactions and thereby modulating downstream transcription factor activity and associated gene expression17,31. To test this hypothesis, we first conducted RNA-seq in unchallenged MEFs under control and CHST7 knockdown conditions. This approach identified 1968 differentially expressed genes (Padj <0.001) in CHST7 knockdown MEFs, including 947 upregulated and 1021 downregulated transcripts as compared to controls (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Data 1), suggesting a major role for CHST7 in regulating fibroblasts transcriptome. Next, gene ontology analysis (EnrichR) on the top 100 most Chst7-dependent genes (0.02–0.57-fold, p-value < 0.001), revealed significant enrichment for terms associated with the cytoskeleton components, adherens junctions, and collagen-containing extracellular matrix (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Data 2), supporting a central role for CHST7 as a positive regulator of genes regulating fibroblast function and morphology.

Next, to identify candidate transcription factors mediating CHST7-dependent gene regulation, we queried the TRRUST v2 database32, which curates experimentally validated transcription factor–target gene interactions, using these top 100 most CHST7-dependent genes. This analysis identified four transcription factors, SRF, SMAD3, HIF1α and JUNB, as top candidates mediating CHST7-dependent transcription (P < 0.01; Fig. 3d and Supplementary Data 3). Next, given the role of CHST7 as a barrier to cardiac reprogramming, we hypothesized that transcription factors mediating its downstream effects would similarly function as barriers to reprogramming. We previously performed a genome-wide screen of transcription factors for their ability to modulate cardiac reprogramming efficiency10. Among the four candidates identified above, only JUNB knockdown markedly enhanced reprogramming efficiency as compared to control conditions (Fig. 3e, f). Collectively, these findings identify JUNB as the main transcription factor mediating CHST7-regulated transcription during cardiac reprogramming.

To test the model that CHST7 acts upstream of JUNB, we assessed whether JUNB levels are regulated by CHST7 in unchallenged fibroblasts. Notably, CHST7 knockdown led to a marked reduction in JUNB protein levels, as measured by immunofluorescence (Fig. 3g, h), accompanied by a corresponding decrease in Junb mRNA expression (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Consistent with these observations, CHST7 overexpression led to a marked increase in JUNB levels (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d), thereby identifying CHST7 as an upstream regulator of Junb/JUNB transcription and protein expression under basal conditions. In this context, and consistent with a model in which CHST7 mediates its fate stabilizing role via CD44, knockdown of CD44 also resulted in reduced Junb/JUNB mRNA (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f) and protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 4g, h).

Finally, in addition to regulating JUNB levels, and given that JUNB was recently shown to oppose cardiac reprogramming through its interaction with the chromatin10, we investigated whether CHST7 might also modulate JUNB chromatin occupancy during reprogramming. CUT&RUN profiling performed at day 1 post-MGT revealed that >30% of JUNB binding sites were redistributed upon siChst7 compared to siControl (Fig. 3i), indicating that CHST7 influences not only JUNB protein levels but also its chromatin occupancy. Notably, motif enrichment and chromatin composition analyses at commonly or differentially bound JUNB regions were largely unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 5a–e), suggesting that the intrinsic biophysical properties governing JUNB engagement with the chromatin remain largely unaltered. However, integrative analysis of JUNB CUT&RUN and RNA-seq datasets at day 1 post-MGT revealed that changes in JUNB occupancy at core promoter/TSS regions strongly correlate with differential gene expression (Fig. 3j and Supplementary Fig. 5f), supporting a model in which, CHST7 regulates JUNB binding distribution and associated transcription during reprogramming. Collectively, these data identify JUNB as the main intracellular effector of CHST7, acting downstream of CD44 to reinforce resistance to cell fate conversion (Fig. 3k).

Feedback regulation sustains CHST7/CD44/JUNB barrier pathway activity in fibroblasts

Extensive work (reviewed in refs. 33,34) has established that maintenance of a differentiated state relies on the sustained expression of lineage-specific gene programs. Based on this regulatory logic, we hypothesized that the fate stabilizing CHST7/CD44/JUNB axis, functions as a self-reinforcing regulatory module that promotes fibroblast identity through continuous signaling input.

Supporting this model, our results above (Fig. 3) show that both CHST7 and CD44 positively regulate JUNB expression/levels. Next, to assess reciprocity within the pathway, we examined whether JUNB positively reinforces Chst7 expression. In unchallenged fibroblasts, JUNB knockdown led to a marked reduction in Chst7 transcript levels (Fig. 4a), supporting the existence of a self-sustaining positive feedback loop involving CHST7, CD44 and JUNB in MEFs at ground state. Next and consistent with an active role for this pathway in promoting lineage stability during cell fate challenges, Chst7 expression dependence on JUNB persisted during MGT-mediated reprogramming (Fig. 4b). To assess the conservation of this regulatory architecture, we examined whether CHST7 expression in human fibroblasts depends on JUNB. Notably, as in MEFs, JUNB knockdown led to a significant reduction in CHST7 mRNA levels in human cardiac fibroblasts during cardiac reprogramming (Fig. 4d). Finally, to assess whether JUNB might directly regulate CHST7 expression, we queried previously published JUNB ChIP–seq dataset from human fibroblasts at ground state10. Consistent with a proximal regulatory role, JUNB binding was detected at CHST7 core promoter region (Fig. 4e). Together, these findings support a model in which the CHST7/CD44/JUNB axis is deployed as a self-sustaining regulatory module that stabilizes fibroblast identity and actively opposes reprogramming toward alternative cell fates (Fig. 4f).

CHST7 opposes cardiac reprogramming by modulating chromatin accessibility and repressing cardiogenic programs in fibroblasts

Fate stabilizing transcription factors such as JUNB safeguards cell identity by maintaining a chromatin accessibility profile that resists reprogramming factors activity and prevents activation of alternative transcriptional programs10. Thus, given that CHST7 positively regulates JUNB expression (Fig. 3), we next hypothesized that CHST7 might act upstream of this chromatin-based barrier mechanism.

To test this, we performed ATAC-seq at day 1 post-MGT induction and assessed differential chromatin accessibility in control and siChst7 conditions in MEFs. This approach revealed that under reprogramming conditions, CHST7 knockdown led to widespread chromatin closing at regions enriched for CTCF (>6-fold) and JUNB (>2.5-fold) motifs (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Data 4). Notably, ~20% of these regions overlapped with promoter or transcription start site (TSS) elements (Fig. 5b, f and Supplementary Data 5), potentially implicating CHST7 in the promotion of transcriptional activity at these loci. Gene ontology analysis of genes associated with these promoter/TSS regions revealed an enrichment for proliferation-associated pathways (Fig. 5c), consistent with a reduction of proliferation observed as cells undergo transdifferentiation35,36. In contrast, regions that gained accessibility upon CHST7 knockdown were enriched for reprogramming factor MEF2C (~3-fold) and JUNB (>4-fold) binding motifs (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Data 6), suggesting the generation of a chromatin accessibility profile that is more permissive to MEF2C binding. Enrichment for JUNB motif at these regions was also consistent with our previous observations10 that JUNB binds at regions of closed chromatin enriched for reprogramming transcription factor motifs in ground state fibroblasts. Newly accessible regions were predominantly located in intergenic and intronic sequences (Fig. 5e, g and Supplementary Data 7), suggesting that CHST7 restricts reprogramming factor access to distal regulatory elements rather than promoter-proximal regions. Collectively, these findings identify CHST7 as a chromatin regulator that promotes accessibility at regions enriched for CTCF and JUNB binding motifs while restricting accessibility at regions enriched for the reprogramming factor MEF2C. Next, to determine how these chromatin accessibility changes might contribute to alter gene expression during the reprogramming process, we first identified a gene signature that associates with successful cardiac reprogramming. Briefly, RNA-seq was performed on FACS-sorted Myh6-eGFP⁺ (reprogrammed) and Myh6-eGFP⁻ (refractory) cells at day 3 following MGT induction (Supplementary Fig. 6a–c). Differential gene expression analysis identified 688 upregulated (p-value < 0.05 and log2FoldChange > 1.0) and 815 downregulated (p-value < 0.05 and log2FoldChange < −1.0) transcripts in reprogramming cells as compared to refractory cells. Cell type association analysis (Enrichr37, CellMarker 2024) revealed that downregulated genes were primarily associated with a fibroblast identity, whereas upregulated genes were associated with a cardiomyocyte identity (Supplementary Fig. 6d–g). Collectively, these data define a gene signature associated with the transition from a fibroblast to early cardiomyocyte-like state during cardiac reprogramming.

Thus, to assess how CHST7-regulated chromatin accessibility impacts fibroblast and cardiac gene expression during reprogramming, we performed RNA-seq at day 3 post-MGT induction under siControl and siChst7 conditions. CHST7 knockdown led to marked repression of fibroblast-enriched genes, including Adamts2, Pdgfra/b, and Thbs2 (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Data 8). In this context, supporting a direct role for JUNB in regulating fibroblast-specific gene expression downstream of CHST7, analysis of published ChIP–seq datasets10 from human dermal fibroblasts revealed JUNB occupancy at the core promoter regions of these genes, indicating that JUNB might directly regulate their transcription (Supplementary Fig. 7a–d). Conversely, CHST7 knockdown induced robust upregulation of cardiomyocyte-associated genes, including Actc1, Myh6, and Tnnt2 (Fig. 5i). Notably, these genes are well-established transcriptional targets of MEF2C, GATA4, and TBX538–42, suggesting that CHST7 knockdown generates a chromatin landscape permissive to the activity of these reprogramming transcription factors. Together, integrated ATAC-seq and RNA-seq analyses indicate that CHST7, acting through JUNB, stabilizes fibroblast identity by maintaining open chromatin at regions enriched for JUNB and CTCF motifs while preserving a closed chromatin state at loci enriched for MEF2C motifs. In turn, this chromatin conformation sustains fibroblast-specific transcriptional programs and limits reprogramming factor access to target DNA, thereby constraining cardiac reprogramming (Fig. 5j).

To identify such CHST7-regulated effectors, we integrated RNA-seq datasets from days 0, 1, and 3 post-MGT induction in both siControl and siChst7 conditions. This analysis revealed 14 genes that were consistently downregulated upon CHST7 knockdown (Fig. 6b), suggesting that their regulation by Chst7 is cell state independent. We next evaluated their function as barriers to cardiac reprogramming in MEFs and identified PIP4K2C, a lipid kinase that phosphorylates phosphatidylinositol 5-phosphate (PI(5)P) to generate phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PI(4,5)P₂)43, as the most potent barrier (Fig. 6c, d). Next, to further validate these findings, we assessed the reprogramming potential of Pip4k2c heterozygous MEFs compared to wild-type controls (Fig. 6e). Consistent with the results above, qPCR analysis at day 8 revealed a robust upregulation of cardiomyocyte-specific genes alongside a concomitant downregulation of fibroblast markers in Pip4k2c heterozygous cells (Fig. 6f, g). Collectively, these results identify PIP4K2C as the most potent CHST7-regulated barrier to cardiac reprogramming.

Finally, to position PIP4K2C within the CHST7-regulated pathway, we examined whether its expression depends on CD44 and JUNB. qPCR analysis revealed a significant reduction in Pip4k2c transcript levels following knockdown of either CD44 or JUNB in MEFs (Fig. 6h), suggesting that PIP4K2C functions as the main downstream barrier effector of the CHST7/CD44/JUNB fate stabilizing pathway (Fig. 6i).

PIP4K2C knockdown enhances cardiac reprogramming-mediated heart repair

Inhibition of mechanisms opposing cardiac reprogramming has been shown to improve heart repair and function post-injury10,44. Our data above (Fig. 6) identified PIP4K2C as the most potent downstream barrier regulated by the fate stabilizing CHST7/CD44/JUNB pathway. Thus, we next investigated whether targeting PIP4K2C could enhance cardiac reprogramming-induced repair following myocardial infarction (MI). First, we confirmed that PIP4K2C is expressed in the heart post-MI (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Next, we identified an shRNA that efficiently knocked-down PIP4K2C and enhanced (>2-fold, p-value < 0.001) cardiac reprogramming efficiency in MEFs (Supplementary Fig. 8b–e).

To assess whether loss of PIP4K2C enhances cardiac reprogramming-mediated heart repair post-MI, we injected viral vectors encoding MGT alone (MGT) or together with shRNA targeting PIP4K2C (MGT+shPip4k2c) into the infarct border zone using an echo-guided delivery at four days post-injury (Fig. 7a). We first validated PIP4K2C knockdown by immunofluorescence, which revealed broad regions of reduced expression within the injured area of the heart (Supplementary Fig. 8f, g). In this context, echocardiographic analysis at 4 weeks post-MI showed a marked improvement in cardiac function in MGT+shPip4k2c-injected hearts compared to MGT alone, with left ventricular ejection fraction increasing from 24.9% to 58.6% and fractional shortening from 11.7% to 30.6% (p-value < 0.001) (Fig. 7b–d). To delineate the cellular mechanisms underlying the observed functional improvement, we performed highly multiplexed immunofluorescence imaging using CODEX (Akoya PhenoCycler)45 at 4 weeks post-MI, employing a validated panel of 36 antibodies optimized for cardiac and regenerating tissue (Supplementary Data 9). Single-cell quantification of fluorescence signal followed by unbiased clustering identified ten main cell types in the heart post-injury, including cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts, endothelial and immune cells (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b), further resolved into subtypes of cardiomyocytes and fibroblasts (Fig. 7e). Notably, comparison between MGT and MGT+shPip4k2c conditions revealed significant shifts in relative abundance, with an increase in TNNT2high cardiomyocytes and a reduction in three discrete fibroblast subpopulations (Fig. 7f). Consistent with these observations, orthogonal immunofluorescence staining for TNNT2 and COL1A corroborated these findings (Fig. 7g, h and Supplementary Fig. 9c, d). Collectively, these results demonstrate that post-MI MGT+shPip4k2c treatment shifts the cellular composition of the injured heart from MGT alone, marked by increased cardiomyocyte content and reduced fibrotic cell populations.

To further dissect the cellular rearrangements induced by MGT+shPip4k2c treatment, we performed spatial neighborhood analysis45 to compare the abundance and composition of major tissue compartments in the heart (Supplementary Fig. 9e, f). The unbiased clustering of local cellular composition resulted in three spatial neighborhoods; cardiac tissue, border zone, and fibrotic regions that make up the scar. Consistent with the changes in cellular composition, MGT+shPip4k2c treatment led to an expansion of cardiac tissue in the left ventricle (LV), accompanied by a concomitant reduction in both the border zone and fibrotic regions (Fig. 7i, j).

In addition to the changes in neighborhood size, their cellular composition was also substantially altered, with the fibrotic compartment displaying the most pronounced reorganization in MGT+shPip4k2c-treated hearts (Fig. 7k). Interestingly, within fibrotic regions, we observed an increased proportion of cardiomyocytes (>2-fold) and a concomitant reduction in fibroblasts (~2-fold) in MGT+shPip4k2c as compared to MGT conditions (Fig. 7l, m). Collectively, these findings identify fibrotic regions as principal targets of MGT+shPip4k2c therapy, demonstrating its capacity to attenuate fibrosis, enhance cardiomyocyte content, and restore cardiac function. Together, our results indicate that inhibition of the CHST7/CD44/JUNB pathway via PIP4K2C knockdown increases the efficacy of cardiac reprogramming post-MI, supporting PIP4K2C inhibition as a promising therapeutic strategy to promote heart repair after injury.

Discussion

Here, we identify and functionally reconstruct a previously unrecognized CHST7/CD44/JUNB signaling axis that enforces fibroblast identity and restricts cardiac reprogramming in mouse and human fibroblasts. Mechanistically, this axis operates as a self-reinforcing regulatory circuit that limits chromatin accessibility at regulatory elements enriched for reprogramming transcription factors and CTCF motifs, thereby stabilizing transcriptional programs that oppose lineage conversion, in part through induction of downstream barriers such as PIP4K2C. Notably, shRNA-mediated inhibition of downstream pathway effector, PIP4K2C, after MI enhances MGT-driven functional recovery and reduces fibrosis. Together, these findings establish fate-stabilizing pathways as tractable therapeutic targets to promote adult tissue repair.

Sulfotransferases, sulfation and the regulation of cell fate stability

Systematic interrogation of the GAG biosynthesis pathway identifies carbohydrate sulfotransferases, CHST2 and CHST7 (Fig. 1) as most potent barriers to cardiac reprogramming in fibroblasts. These findings imply that sulfation, rather than other types of GAG modifications such as chain elongation or epimerization, represents the main modification by which GAG biosynthesis-associated genes control cell fate stability in fibroblasts. Interestingly, while CHST2 and 7 display potent activities, other highly expressed CHSTs such as CHST1, 3, 11, 12, and 14 did not show any barrier activity, suggesting that the involvement of CHSTs in the regulation of cell identity maintenance is family member specific. In this context, although each CHST displays some preferred sugar residues and linkage positions (e.g., CHST2 for GlcNAc-6-O, CHST7 for GalNAc-6-O, and CHST11/12/14 for GalNAc-4-O)26, a substantial overlap in function also exists, for example, CHST3 and CHST7 both catalyze GalNAc-6-O sulfation on chondroitin sulfate26 and yet exhibit distinct barrier to cardiac reprogramming properties. Collectively, these observations suggest that the unique barrier to cardiac reprogramming properties of CHST2 and CHST7 relative to other family members, are likely dictated by their specific abilities to mediate sulfation on specific core proteins rather than preferred sugar residues and linkage position. However, in this context, the molecular basis for core protein substrate selectivity remains to be defined.

From a biochemical perspective, sulfation, unlike other types of post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation46, is biochemically stable thereby providing a durable informational layer to cells47. Notably, this modification is added within the Golgi but displayed and functionally relevant at the cell surface. Thus, specific proteoglycan sulfation patterns enable a defined cellular state to program the signaling properties of the plasma membrane by determining ligands and co-receptors affinity for their cognate receptors48,49. In this context, we propose that sulfation confers both programmability and biochemical stability, endowing the molecular properties necessary to regulate signaling pathways that enforce cell fate stability.

Identification and reconstruction of CHST7/CD44 fate stabilizing pathway in fibroblasts

While the biochemical function of carbohydrate sulfotransferases is well understood; the identification of physiologically relevant core-protein substrates has been hindered by the lack of scalable discovery methods50,51. To overcome this challenge, here, we applied a systems-level workflow integrating functional screening, quantitative confocal imaging, and orthogonal biochemical assays to systematically interrogate the entire proteoglycan core-protein repertoire28, leading to the identification of CD44 as the most potent and physically interacting CHST7-regulated proteoglycan mediating a barrier to cardiac reprogramming function (see Fig. 2). Consistent with our findings, previous work52 has shown that inflammatory stimuli (LPS/IFNγ) in mouse macrophages suppress Chst7 mRNA expression and reduce CD44 sulfation levels, supporting our model that CHST7 contributes to modulate CD44 sulfation levels.

Moreover, a central role for CD44 in stabilizing fibroblast identity is also supported by our initial screen (Fig. 1) that identified the hyaluronan-degrading enzymes HYAL1 and HYAL2 as potent agonists of cardiac reprogramming. These findings suggest that high-molecular weight hyaluronan (HA) which is the main ligand of CD4453, agonizes CD44 signaling and promotes cell fate stability in fibroblasts. Consistent with this model, treatment of MEFs with bovine hyaluronidase increased reprogramming efficiency as compared to vehicle treated controls (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). This is further supported by RNA-seq analyses showing marked downregulation of the HA synthase, Has2, and concomitant upregulation of the HA-degrading enzyme, Hyal1, during cardiac reprogramming (Supplementary Fig. 10c–f). Moreover, FACS-sorted Myh6-eGFP⁺ cells at day3 post-MGT (see Supplementary Fig. 6) displayed substantially lower Has2 expression than refractory Myh6-eGFP⁻ cells (Supplementary Fig. 10g), further supporting an antagonistic role for HA/CD44-mediated signaling in cardiac reprogramming. Finally, and consistent with these observations, naked mole rats, which secrete exceptionally high-molecular-weight HA, display remarkable high cell fate stability and resistance to OKSM-induced iPSC reprogramming and oncogenic transformation54,55. In this context, the authors also show that this intrinsic enhancement of cell fate stability is mediated by HA/CD44 signaling, as ectopic expression of HYAL2 or antibody-mediated inhibition of CD44 increases oncogenic transformation frequency. Together, our findings and previous studies support a model in which CHST7 modifies the activity of an HA/CD44 signaling axis that in turn stabilizes cell fate identity in fibroblasts.

CHST7 stabilizes cell identity through JUNB levels regulation, chromatin accessibility restriction, and barrier gene activation

Although the extracellular environment is known to influence cell plasticity55, the underlying mechanisms mediating this process in the context of cell fate reprogramming have remained undefined in fibroblasts. In Fig. 3, we identify JUNB as the main intracellular mediator of the CHST7/CD44-regulated fate-stabilizing pathway. In this context, we show that a key function of CHST7 and CD44 is to regulate JUNB nuclear levels and modify its chromatin binding distribution and associated transcription. Previous studies56,57 have shown that CD44 signaling can activate MAPK/JNK pathways that promote phosphorylation, activation and nuclear accumulation of the AP-1 transcription factor c-JUN, suggesting a broader role for CD44 in regulating AP-1 transcriptional activity. However, the molecular mechanisms by which CHST7 regulates JUNB chromatin binding pattern remain to be defined.

In this context, and consistent with a role for CHST7 in regulating JUNB activity, our ATAC-seq analysis (Fig. 5) reveals that CHST7 restricts chromatin accessibility at regulatory regions enriched for the reprogramming factor motif, MEF2C, while maintaining accessibility at sites enriched for JUNB and CTCF motifs. Notably, JUNB motifs were enriched across both opening and closing chromatin regions, further supporting a central role for JUNB in the control of chromatin dynamics during cell fate reprogramming and positioning CHST7 as an upstream modulator of this process. These findings extend our previous work10 demonstrating that JUNB maintains reprogramming transcription factor motif-enriched regions in a closed chromatin state, thereby limiting transcription factor access to target DNA and constraining cell fate transitions. However, in this context, the precise mechanistic delineation of how transcription factors such as JUNB maintain these regions in a closed state remains to be established.

Finally, in parallel with its role in regulating chromatin accessibility, our data demonstrates that CHST7 exerts broad control over transcriptional programs (Fig. 3). In this context, a core function of CHST7 is to promote the expression of downstream fate-stabilizing barrier genes, including the most potent effector PIP4K2C (Fig. 6). Consistent with this model, Pip4k2c expression was also dependent on both CD44 and JUNB, identifying it as the main downstream effector of the fate-stabilizing pathway. In support of a role in cell identity maintenance, analysis of a large melanoma patients cohort revealed that loss of PIP4K2C promotes liver-specific metastasis58. At the cellular level, PIP4K2C functions as a lipid kinase that modulates phosphoinositide signaling, thereby influencing membrane trafficking, cytoskeletal organization, and stress responses43. The mechanism by which PIP4K2C enforces cell fate stability, however, remains to be determined. Collectively, these findings position CHST7 as an upstream regulator of fibroblast fate stability that operates through a CD44/JUNB signaling axis to establish a chromatin accessibility landscape refractory to reprogramming factors and to induce transcriptional barriers to cell fate conversion, including PIP4K2C.

PIP4K2C a novel therapeutic target to promote heart repair post-injury

Cardiac reprogramming holds considerable promise for the treatment of both acute59 and chronic60,61 heart injuries; however, its clinical translation has been limited by low efficiency62. Here, we identify PIP4K2C as a novel target to enhance cardiac reprogramming-mediated repair and function after injury (Fig. 7). Multiplexed immunofluorescence imaging and spatial single cell analysis (Fig. 7) reveals cellular mechanisms of cardiac function improvement and demonstrates that MGT+shPip4k2c treatment restored cardiac tissue neighborhood and ameliorated the formation of the fibrotic scar, as compared to MGT alone. In addition, the proportion of cardiomyocytes within fibrotic neighborhoods also increases with MGT+shPip4k2c, supporting a repair mechanism driven by enhanced reprogramming efficiency in vivo. In addition, recent drug discovery efforts63 have established PIP4K2C as a tractable and degradable target, further underscoring its potential as a therapeutic candidate. In conclusion, this study, together with previous work10,44, reinforces the therapeutic concept that targeted inhibition of genes controlling cell fate stability represents a promising strategy to enhance the efficacy of direct reprogramming–based organ repair.

Methods

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with relevant ethical regulations and institutional guidelines. Study protocols were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Johns Hopkins University.

iMGT-Myh6-eGFP-MEFs and screening assays

Immortalized Dox-inducible iMGT-Myh6-eGFP-MEFs (MEFs) were described previously23. These cells were acquired from Dr. Li Qian laboratory at UNC. The cells were cultured in plates precoated with 0.1% gelatin (Stem Cell Technologies, #7903) and maintained in Fibroblast Culture Medium (FCM) consisting of DMEM (Corning, #10-013-CV), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; VWR, #89510-186), and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (A/A; (solution contains 10,000 units/mL of penicillin, 10,000 μg/mL of streptomycin, and 25 μg/mL of Gibco Amphotericin B), Gibco, #15240062) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. One day prior to siRNA transfection (day −1), cells were detached by addition of 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Thermo Fisher, #25200056) for 3 min at 37 °C, and then washed in FCM, centrifuged at 400 × g, and resuspended in Induced-CM Reprogramming Medium (iCRM), consisting of DMEM, 20% Medium 199 (Gibco, #11150-059), 10% FBS, and 1% A/A. Cells were plated in 384-well plates at 103 cells/well and transfected with an siRNAs directed against mouse genes regulating steps of proteoglycan biosynthesis (Dharmacon-Horizon Discovery; ON-TARGETplus-siRNA library, see Supplementary Data 10 for target sequence details). The next day (day 0), 1 μg/mL doxycycline hydrochloride (Dox; Sigma, #D3072) diluted in iCRM was added to the cells to induce MGT expression. On day 3, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and processed for immunostaining, microscopy, and Myh6-eGFP quantification. All experiments were performed in quadruplicate. For follow-up analyses, the cells were collected either on day 1 or day 2 post-transfection for RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis, additionally samples were processed for immunostaining, microscopy, and target protein analysis.

CHST7 overexpression in iMGT-MEFs

For CHST7 overexpression, iMGT-MEFs were transduced with a lentiviral construct encoding pLV-Puro-CMV>mChst7 [NM_021715.1]:P2A:mCherry (VectorBuilder, VB250909-1794xqc), with viral particles produced by the SBP Viral Core. Cells were infected on day −1, or on day −2 in experiments combined with siRNA treatment. Cardiac reprogramming was induced by doxycycline administration on day 0. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde on day 3 and processed for imaging analysis.

SULF2 recombinant protein treatment in iMGT-MEFs

Recombinant Sulfatase-2 (SULF2; Novus Biologicals, H00055959-P02) was diluted in vehicle buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 10 mM reduced glutathione, pH 8.0). iMGT-MEFs were treated with SULF2 at final concentrations of 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, or 0.5 μg ml⁻¹ on day −1. Cardiac reprogramming was induced by doxycycline administration on day 0. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde on day 3 and processed for imaging analysis.

Human primary cardiac fibroblasts (HCFs)

Human cardiac fibroblasts isolated from normal, adult heart tissue were obtained from Lonza (Lonza; NHCF-V-Human Ventricular Cardiac Fibroblasts; #CC-2904) and cultured in plates precoated with 0.1% gelatin and maintained with HCF growth medium consisting of FBM Basal Medium (Lonza, #CC3131) and FGM-3 SingleQuot Supplements (Lonza, #CC4525). For cardiac reprogramming, the cells were allowed to reach 80% confluency, harvested using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA as described above, resuspended in HCF growth medium, added to 384-well plates at 103 cells/well, and transfected with the indicated siRNAs (Dharmacon-Horizon Discovery; ON-TARGETplus-siRNA; see Supplementary Data 11 for target sequence details). The next day (day 0), the cells were transduced by addition of 1 µL/well of mouse MGT retrovirus6 diluted in 5ul of STEMdiff Cardiomyocyte Maintenance Kit medium (Stem Cell Technologies, #05020). Cells were collected on day 3 for qRT-PCR experiments or day 21 for immunostaining post-MGT. During the days of incubation in 384-well plates, 50% of the medium was exchanged with STEMdiff Cardiomyocyte Maintenance Kit medium (Stem Cell Technologies, #05020) every other day starting at day 3 up to day 21.

siRNA transfection

Mouse and human siRNAs were purchased from Dharmacon and added at a final concentration of 25 nM. A non-targeting negative control siRNA was obtained from Dharmacon (referred to as siCT or siControl; Dharmacon, #D-001810-10-20). Transfection was performed using Opti-MEM (Gibco, #31985070) and Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Gibco, #13778150) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. siRNA transfection was performed on day −1.

Immunostaining and fluorescence microscopy

Cells were fixed with 4% PFA in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 15 min and blocked with blocking buffer consisting of 10% horse serum (Life Technologies, #26050088), 0.1% gelatin, and 0.5% Triton X-100 (Fisher Scientific, #MP04807426) in PBS for 30 min. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and followed by secondary antibodies with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Sigma, #D9542, 1:1000) for 1 h in the dark at room temperature. Cells were washed with PBS between each step. Cells were imaged with an Image Xpress confocal microscope (Molecular Devices) and fluorescence was quantified with Molecular Device software. Experiments were performed in at least quadruplicate.

For confocal immunofluorescence imaging, MEFs were seeded onto chamber slides (Ibidi, #81201) at 6000 cells per well and fixed 48 h after plating with 4% PFA in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 10 min. Cells were blocked for 30 min as described above. Primary and secondary antibodies were applied as described above. PBS washes were performed between each step. Images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 980 microscope equipped with Airyscan2 and Zen 3.8 software. Quantification of colocalization was performed using Imaris software (Bitplane).

Mouse heart sections were fixed with 4% PFA (Alfar Aesar, #43368) for 10 min. Sections were permeabilized and blocked in 20% goat serum (Life Technologies, 16210-072) and 0.3% Triton X-100 (Promega, #H5142) in PBS for 1 h. Incubation with the primary antibody was performed overnight at 4 °C. Sections were washed with PBS. Then, the secondary antibody was left for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 minutes. Slides were mounted with Fluoromount-G (Southernbiotech, #0100-01).

The primary antibodies used were: rabbit polyclonal anti-TNNT2 (Sigma, #HPA017888, 1:200); rabbit anti-Chst7 (Abcam, # Ab154726, 1:200); mouse anti-Golgin97 (Cell Signaling, #CDF4, 1:200); mouse-anti-Cd44 (Novus Biologicals, #NBP-1-47386, 1:200); rabbit anti-JUNB (Abcam, #Ab128878, 1:200); rabbit anti-Collagen I (Abcam, #Ab21286, 1:200); mouse anti-Collagen I alpha I (Novus Biologicals, #NB600-450, 1:100); rabbit anti-Pip4k2c (Proteintech, #17077-1-AP, 1:200);

The secondary antibodies used were: Alexa Fluor 488 goat-anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Invitrogen, A11008, 1:500); Alexa Fluor 488 donkey-anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (Invitrogen, A21202 1:500); Alexa Fluor 568 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Invitrogen, A11011, 1:500); Alexa Fluor 568 goat-anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (Invitrogen, A10037, 1:500); Alexa Fluor 680 donkey-anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (Invitrogen, A10038, 1:500); and Alexa Fluor 680 donkey-anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Invitrogen, A10043, 1:500).

Whole-cell lysate extraction, immunoprecipitation, and western blotting

Human dermal fibroblasts (ATCC; CRL-2097, CCD-1079Sk) were seeded at 5 × 105 cells per 10 cm dish and transfected with siChst7 or siControl as described above. At 48 h post-transfection, cells were washed twice with cold PBS, harvested, and centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. For protein extraction, cell pellets were resuspended in ice-cold Pierce IP lysis buffer (87787) containing Halt™ Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (100X) (78440). Lysates were transferred to pre-chilled tubes and were incubated on ice for 10 min with intermittent mixing, followed by centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was measured using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher, #PI23225).

Co-IP assays were performed using Dynabeads™ Protein A Immunoprecipitation Kit (ThermoFisher, 10006D) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 50 µL of protein-A-coated Dynabeads were incubated with 2 µg of either anti-CD44 antibody (Bio-Rad, MCA2726) or isotype IgG control (Bio-Rad, MCA929) in binding/wash buffer for 10 minutes at room temperature with rotations. Lysates were then added and incubated overnight at 4 °C with rotation. Following this incubation, beads-lysate-antibody mixture was washed three times with 200 µL washing buffer. After the final wash, proteins were eluted by adding 20 µL elution buffer supplemented with LDS sample buffer and reducing agent, followed by heating at 70 °C for 10 min. Eluates were collected using a magnetic rack.

For Western blotting, ~10 µg of Co-IP samples and whole cell lysates were resolved on 4–12% NuPage Bis-Tris mini gels at 200 V for ~1 h and transferred to membranes using the iBlot 2 Dry Blotting System (Thermo Fisher) with method P0. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD44 (Novus Biologicals, #NBP1-47386, 1:1000) and GAPDH (Cell Signaling, # D4C6R, 1:1000). Secondary antibodies (1:10,000) were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Blots were imaged using the LI-COR Odyssey CLx imaging system.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using Zymo Research Quick-RNA MircoPrep Kit (Zymo Research, R1051) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. RNA concentration was measured by Nanodrop (Thermo Scientific). Aliquots of 1 μg of RNA were reverse transcribed using a QuantiTect Reverse Transcription kit (Qiagen, #205314), and qPCR was performed with iTaq SYBR Green (BioRad, # 1725122) using a 7900HT Fast Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystem). Gene expression was normalized to that of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) for human and mouse samples using the 2–ΔΔCt method. Human and mouse primer sequences for qRT-PCR were obtained from Harvard Primer Bank. Primers were: CHST7 (#23238227c3), Chst7 (#11096336a1), CD44 (#48255942c1), Cd44 (#26337169a1), JUNB (#44921611c1), Junb (#6680512a1), Pip4k2c (#17298686a1), ACTC1 (#113722123c1), MEF2C (#301069383c3), GATA4 (#172072611c1), TBX5 (#31652233c1), NPPA (#23510319a1), NPPB (#83700236c1), TNNI3 (#151101269c1), TNNT2 (#48255880c1), Myh6 (#289803014c3), Tbx5 (34098933a1), Adamts2 (#33563307a1), Cd248 (#16923944a1), Cxcl12 (#7305465a1), Cyp1b1 (#6753568a1), Igfbp6 (#6680387a1), Lox (#6754568a1), Pdgfra (#6755012a1), Pdgfrb (#6679259a1), Podn (#32526861a1), Thbs2 (#6755779a1), Actc1 (#14192922a1), Fabp3 (#6753810a1), Myh6 (#6754774a1), Tnnc1 (#6678369a1), Tnnt2 (#6755843a1), Ttn (#27817830a1), GAPDH (#378404907c1), and Gapdh (#6679937a1).

Retrovirus and lentivirus preparation

Large-scale retrovirus production was performed at the SBP Viral Vector Core Facility SBP as previously described64. Briefly, for retrovirus preparation, pMX-MGT6, Retro-Gag-Pol, and pMD2.G plasmids were co-transfected into HEK-293T cells at a ratio of 3:2:1. For lentivirus preparation, lentivector DNA plasmid for shPip4k2c (pLKO.1 vector with sequence from Broad Institute #TRCN0000024703; target sequence: GAGTCCTTCATCGATGTCTAT) was co-transected with pCMVDR8.74 and pMD2.G into HEK-293T cells using the calcium phosphate method. UltraCulture serum-free medium (Lonza) supplemented with 1 mM L-glutamine (Life Technologies) was used to re-feed transfected cells, and the supernatant was collected every 24 h from day 2 to day 4 after transfection. Viral supernatants were pooled, passed through a 0.22-μm-pore filter, concentrated, and purified by 20% sucrose gradient ultracentrifugation at 21,000 rpm for 2 h at 4 °C. The pellet containing concentrated viral particles was resuspended in PBS, aliquoted, and kept at −80 °C.

RNA-seq and data analysis

MEFs were added to 384-well plates at 103 cells/well and transfected with siRNAs (siControl and siChst7) in iCRM. The next day (day 0), samples were collected, and RNA was extracted using Zymo Research Quick-RNA MircoPrep Kit for the baseline timepoint. For the samples with MGT overexpression, 1 μg/mL Dox diluted in iCRM was added to the cells. On day 1 and day 3 post-MGT, cells were collected, and RNA was extracted using Zymo Research Quick-RNA MircoPrep Kit. Cells were pooled from 40 wells per sample, and three biological replicates per condition were analyzed.

Library preparation was performed by the La Jolla Institute for Immunology Next Generation Sequencing Core using the NEB poly(A) mRNA-seq Library Prep kit. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina SP 200-cycle flow cell as paired-end 2 × 150 bp reads to a target depth of ~30 million reads per sample.

Bulk RNA sequencing was used to determine transcript levels. Fastq reads were mapped to mm10 with HISAT265. Counts were then assembled using Subread featureCounts (1.5.2)66. Differential gene expression was analyzed using the DESeq267 in R was used for differential expression analysis. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed using PANTHER version 12.0 classification68,69 and enrichR (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/).

CUT&RUN assay

The Cleavage Under Targets & Release Using Nuclease (CUT&RUN) assay was performed using the CUT&RUN Assay Kit (#86652S, Cell Signaling Technology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with modification for our sample types, cell numbers and type of antibody.

Briefly, iMGT-MEFs (siControl and siChst7) were harvested at day 1 post-doxycycline treatment and frozen at –80 °C in 90% FBS with 10% DMSO using a controlled-rate freezing container. Approximately 250,000 cells were used per condition, including experimental samples and controls. For each biological group, we included a positive control (H3K4me3), and a negative control (rabbit or mouse IgG). After thawing, cells were bound to activated concanavalin A (Con A) beads and incubated with primary antibodies targeting gene of interest. Following overnight incubation at 4 °C, samples were incubated with pAG-MNase for targeted chromatin digestion. DNA fragments were released by calcium activation and stopped using provided buffers. Crosslinks were reversed with SDS and proteinase K at 65 °C. DNA was purified using the Qiagen MinElute PCR Purification Kit (#28006) and quantified with a Qubit fluorometer. Fragment size distribution was assessed using the Agilent 2200 TapeStation with High Sensitivity D1000 ScreenTape. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB #E7645L) along with NEBNext® Multiplex Oligos for Illumina® (Dual Index Primers Set 1, NEB #E7600S). DNA libraries were cleaned up using AMPure XP reagent, Bechman Coulter, A63881). Libraries were sequenced on the Element Biosciences AVITI platform with 2 × 75 bp paired-end reads, targeting approximately 10 million reads per sample. The following antibodies were used for CUT&RUN: H3K4me3 Rabbit mAb (1:50; Cell Signaling Technology, #9751), IgG XP® Isotype Control (1:20; Cell Signaling Technology, #66362), JUNB Rabbit mAb (1:33; Cell Signaling Technology, #3763). All conditions were performed with two technical replicates. Reads were mapped using bowtie2 (2.5.1) then sorted and indexed with samtools (1.16.1). Peaks were called with MACS3 (3.0.0b3). Overlap analysis was performed with bedtools (2.31.0). Motifs were analyzed with Homer (5.1). Code is available at https://github.com/smurph50/Chst7.

Chromatin accessibility

Bulk ATAC-seq experiments were performed with MEFs at day 1 post-MGT overexpression, MGT+siControl and MGT+siChst7. MEFs were added to 384-well plates at 103 cells/well in iCRM and transfected with siRNAs. The next day (day 0), 1 μg/mL Dox diluted in iCRM was added to the cells to induce MGT expression. Cells were collected 1 day later with trypsin-EDTA. Cells from 40 wells were pooled to obtain ≥5 × 105 cells per sample, with two biological replicates per condition. Cells were washed with FCM, centrifuged in conical tubes, and the pellets were frozen in Freezing Medium (DMEM, 10% DMSO, 20% FBS), transferred to cryotubes, and placed in Mr, Frosty containers (Thermo Fisher) at −80 °C. Next, samples were processed with the Active Motif ATAC-seq Kit (#53150) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA cleanup, library preparation, and indexing were performed with kit reagents. Libraries were sequenced on the Element Biosciences AVITI platform using paired-end 2 × 75 bp reads, with a target depth of ~50 million reads per sample. Next, Fastq files containing raw reads were mapped with Bowtie2 to the mm10 genome. Bam files were sorted and indexed with Samtools70 before MACS371 peak calling. Bedtools72 was used for comparing accessible regions in different samples. Peaks were annotated with HOMER. Tracks were visualized using Integrative Genome Viewer 2.8.12. All scripts for this analysis are available on GitHub [https://github.com/smurph50].

Mouse MI model

Experiments were performed in 14- to 18-week-old male mice (Jackson lab, strain 000664). To generate the mouse MI model, mice were intubated and anesthetized with isoflurane gas. The chest cavity was exposed by cutting the intercostal muscle and then the left coronary artery was ligated with a 5–0 silk suture.

Echo-guided retro/lenti-viruses injection

Four days after MI, 6 μL of virus-containing solution (PBS) was injected into the boundary between the infarct and border zone at one site with a 32-gauge needle using echo-guided visualization as described in ref. 73. The mouse surgeon was blinded to the study and no mortality after MI was noted.

Echocardiography

Cardiac function was analyzed with transthoracic echocardiography (Visual Sonics, Vevo 2100) at 4 weeks after MI (n = 10–13 per group). Mice were anesthetized with low dose isoflurane for echocardiographic examination. Two-dimensional targeted M-mode traces were obtained at the papillary muscle level. Left ventricular internal diameter during diastole (LVDd) and left ventricular internal diameter during systole (LVDs) were measured in at least three consecutive cardiac cycles. EF and FS were calculated with the Teichholtz formula. Average baseline prior to injury for EF and FS was 78.15% and 45.12% respectively.

Tissue processing and staining for CODEX

Antibody staining was performed using Antibody Mix 1 by adding DNA-barcoded antibodies (Supplementary Data 12) into in S2 buffer together with 5 μL of each of 1 mg/ml mouse IgG (Sigma), 1 mg/ml rat IgG (Sigma), 1 mg/ml rabbit IgG (Sigma), Sheared salmon sperm DNA (10 mg/ml in H2O; Thermo Fisher), Mixture of non-modified CODEX oligonucleotides at a final concentration of 0.5 mM in TE buffer (IDT) solutions.

Tissue sections (10 μm cryosection) were thawed in Hydration Buffer (S1) for 2 min and washed twice. Subsequently, sections were fixed in S1-diluted 1.6% PFA for 10 min and washed with S1 three times. 5 min washed was performed with S2 buffer and then the Antibody Mix 1 was added and incubated overnight at 4 °C in a hydration chamber. Coverslips were washed twice with S2 buffer and fixed in S1-diluted 1.6% PFA for 10 min. Three washes with PBS were then performed and a final fixation step with 200 mg/ml BS3 (bis(sulfosuccinimidyl)suberate; Thermo Fisher) in anhydrous DMSO (Sigma) for 20 min. the coverslips were again washed three times with PBS and stored at 4 °C in Storage Solution (S4) until CODEX imaging.

CODEX was performed on a Phenocycler Open system (Akoya) on a Keyence BZ-810 automated microscope in 21 cycles using the layout described in Supplementary Data 12. Fluorescent cDNA probes in Cy3 and Cy5 (5 μL of a 10 mM stock in 250 μL of plate buffer) for the matching antibody barcode was loaded into a 96-well plate as per Phenocycler protocol. The entire heart section was captured in tiles with optical sectioning (3 × 2.4 μm z-slices).

For each CODEX experiment, a single cross section of post-MI heart was sectioned on a gelatin-coated coverslip, stained and imaged in its entirety.

Imaging data was processed using CRISP-CODEX processor45 to register (across image tiles and imaging cycles), deconvolve, and generate stitched extended-depth-of-field images of the entire heart section. Cells were segmented using a modified version of CellSeg74 that has been optimized for CRISP output (https://github.com/will-yx/CellSeg-CRISP). Quantified per cell staining intensity from images were pre-processed using HFcluster45 (https://github.com/will-yx/HFcluster/tree/anndata) for analysis in Scanpy75. Harmony was used to integrate replicate data across 8 experiments. Cell types were clustered by expression similarity of Ki67, ECL, WGA, TNNT2, DMD, CD90, PDGFRa, aSMA, CD45.2, Dysf, CD44, Thbs4, Col1, Ter119, Sca1, CD31, Postn, CD68, Fn1, ERTR7., Ly6C using Leiden (resolution = 2) into 22 clusters and manually annotated by marker expression and spatial arrangement into 11 cell types. Cell neighborhoods76 were determined by the composition of each cell and its nearest 40 neighbors, then clustered based on the relative abundance of each cell type (Leiden, resolution = 0.2) which yielded 11 neighborhoods. Each neighborhood was then annotated based on enriched cell types and its spatial localization in the MI heart. Cell type enrichment was calculated as log2fold enrichment of its abundance in each neighborhood over the total abundance in the whole tissue.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise stated. Group comparisons were made using unpaired Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. All experiments were independently reproduced at least twice.
