Response to: The Long Game in Ulcerative Colitis: Mirikizumab’s Four-Year Outcomes
Authors: David B Clemow
Published: 2026

In acknowledging and thanking the authors of the editorial entitled “The Long Game in Ulcerative Colitis: Mirikizumab’s Four-Year Outcomes”1 that discusses the article published in Inflammatory Bowel Diseases, “Mirikizumab four-year sustained and durable efficacy and safety in ulcerative colitis: final findings from the LUCENT-3 open-label extension study,”2 please note the following input.

It is agreed that mirikizumab may “offer patients durable efficacy and long-term safety” with “stable maintenance dosing.”1

In relation to discussing missing data imputation methods, it is mentioned from the paper that 13% (40 of 316 total patients) of discontinuations in the long-term extension (4 years of continuous treatment) were due to patients switching to a commercially available drug. Please note that this equates to 30% (40/132) of discontinuations. Discontinuations due to lack of efficacy were 4.4% of the total population (14/316); 10.6% of discontinuations (14/132) (Figure 1).2 Of note, the percentage of discontinuations for geopolitical logistical reasons was 17% (23/132).

In the paper, for transparency, data from the observed case (OC), modified nonresponder imputation (mNRI), and nonresponder imputation (NRI) methods are provided. We agree with the editorial commentary on the pros and cons of the different methods. Being aware of which type of method is being used is important when reading about long-term data so that it can be put into proper context. In the paper, it is noted that “as efficacy endpoints are assessed further into long-term extension studies, the OC data become particularly valuable because they minimize bias introduced by patient discontinuations due, not to lack of efficacy or AEs, but to reasons such as ‘approved drug available for indication.’”2 This is why, while all the data are shown, OC data were a focus of the abstract.

In the editorial, while OC and NRI data are mentioned for the overall week 52 maintenance clinical remitter population (W52 Remitters), NRI data in the week 52 maintenance clinical responder population is noted for the biofailed vs bionaive at baseline subgroup analyses: “four-year clinical remission was 38% for biologic-failed patients versus 47% for biologic-naive patients, as determined by NRI.”1 Note that mNRI data were not shown for the subgroup analyses because the patient numbers (N) became small enough to limit the statistical robustness of this method. Because of limitations with long-term NRI data, particularly in subgroup analyses with lower N, it is also important to note OC data in the W52 Remitters: clinical remission was 82% for biologic-failed patients vs 76% for biologic-naive (Not Biologic Failed) patients (Figure 3) 2; for the W52 Responders: 73% vs 67%.

This additional information may help readers compare results across these data, aiding understanding of the editorial comment that mirikizumab “offers sustained efficacy across a wide variety of patients” and the paper’s statement that “across biologic failed and bionaive subgroups, efficacy results were generally similar.” 1
