ArcFace-enhanced few-shot meta-learning for industrial fault diagnosis using multi-modal representations with lightweight 1D CNNs
Authors: Mehedy Hasan Rony, Harikrishnan Ramiah, Jeevan Kanesan, H. P. D. Shiran Madhuranga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Machine Health Monitoring Systems (MHMS) often struggle in industrial environments due to scarce labelled data, limited fault classes, and the overfitting tendency of large deep models. This study introduces a lightweight multi-Skip 1D CNN (MS-1D-CNN) built with depth-wise separable convolutions and skip connections, coupled with an ArcFace-enhanced Few-Shot Learning (FSL) framework and multi-modal feature extraction to ensure discriminative and robust representations under limited data. ArcFace enforces margin-based class separation, while episodic FSL training enhances generalization with only a few samples per class. The framework employs a two-stage protocol: meta-training on the clean CWRU dataset to learn stable fault-related patterns, followed by meta-testing on noisy industrial vibration data to evaluate real-world generalization without overfitting. Both single-sensor signals and image-based representations are utilized during evaluation to verify the framework’s adaptability across various data modalities. The proposed framework achieves near-perfect accuracy with only 1–5 samples per class, confirming their robustness, strong generalization capability, and suitability for deployment in data-constrained industrial MHMS applications.

Introduction

Machines have been integral to human progress since ancient times, and their importance continues to grow, particularly in industrial sectors1,2. The advent of Industry 4.0 and the rise of the Industrial Internet of Things (IoT) have revolutionized manufacturing by enabling data collection and analysis from connected machines3. This transformation has made machine health monitoring4 a critical field of study, as the complex operational environments of industrial equipment can lead to costly failures5,6.

Machine Health Monitoring System (MHMS) plays a vital role in modern manufacturing systems, fully capitalizing on the advancements of the big data era. Unlike traditional physics-based models7, which rely on top-down modeling approaches, data-driven machine health monitoring introduces a bottom-up framework for fault detection (diagnosis) and predicting future operational conditions, including remaining useful life (prognosis)1,8. However, the complexity and noise inherent in real-world industrial environments make it difficult to develop accurate physical models. Additionally, most physics-based models lack the ability to adapt using real-time sensor data, reducing their flexibility and practical effectiveness.

In recent years, there has been a significant focus on deep learning-based methods for health monitoring and maintenance (MHM) systems, particularly in the context of machinery performance and reliability. One of the critical applications of these systems is the identification of bearing faults, as bearings are integral components in many mechanical systems and play a crucial role in their overall functionality.

Machine faults, especially in components like bearings, which account for a large proportion of mechanical failures, can cause significant financial losses and operational downtime9,10. The impact of faults and machine anomalies will become apparent when utilized in a large-scale industrial facility. Rolling element bearings play a key role in diagnosing faults within rotating machinery, with bearing failures accounting for around 45–55% of all mechanical equipment failures11,12. Furthermore, bearing issues are the cause of 90% of failures in small rotary machines13. On average, large manufacturing companies face losses of $532,000 per hour of downtime and $172 million per plant annually due to machinery breakdowns14.

Many physics-based models cannot readily incorporate online measurement data, thereby limiting their flexibility and long-term applicability. Conversely, the rapid evolution of sensing systems and computational resources has driven growing interest in data-driven approaches for condition monitoring.

With the rapid advancement of sensors and the Industrial Internet of Things (IoT), vast amounts of industrial data are now available, enabling progress in data-driven intelligent diagnostics15,16. Traditional diagnostic methods typically involve two stages: (1) feature extraction, where wavelet transform is one of the signal analysis techniques17, variational mode decomposition18, and empirical mode decomposition19 are used to manually extract features, and (2) fault classification, using models like artificial neural networks (ANNs)19 or support vector machines (SVMs)20. However, manual feature extraction struggles to capture complex system dynamics, and shallow classifiers cannot effectively learn high-dimensional nonlinear relationships.

In contrast, deep learning21 has gained popularity for its ability to automatically extract features from raw data through multiple hidden layers22, addressing these limitations. Among deep architectures, CNNs23 and LSTMs are particularly effective for learning spatial–temporal and long-sequence features, respectively, and have shown strong performance in machinery fault diagnosis. For example, Zhang et al.24 introduced a deep convolutional neural network featuring a wide first-layer kernel to improve fault identification accuracy. Jiang et al.25 proposed a multiscale CNN architecture capable of capturing hierarchical features for wind turbine gearbox fault diagnosis. Wen et al.26 converted vibration signals into image representations and applied a LeNet-5-based two-dimensional CNN for fault classification. In addition, Yuan et al.27 employed an LSTM-based framework for both fault diagnosis and remaining useful life prediction of aero engines, while Yin et al.28 Further enhanced LSTM performance using a cosine loss function for wind turbine fault diagnosis.

Recently, Tama et al.29 showed that graph neural networks are effective in capturing the topological relationships inherent in vibration signals. Zhang et al.30 transformed one-dimensional vibration data into two-dimensional images and applied a GRU-based approach, reporting an 8.6% improvement in classification accuracy under noisy environments. Xie et al.31 combined multi-sensor measurements into RGB image representations and achieved 99.12% accuracy using a residual CNN architecture. Lei et al.32 described the progressive evolution of fault diagnosis techniques from traditional machine learning to deep learning and further to transfer learning, identifying context-aware diagnosis as a promising future trend. Souza et al.33 demonstrated the practicality of low-cost, single-sensor CNN-based monitoring for Industry 4.0 applications, although system performance under high-speed extreme conditions exceeding 15% was not investigated. Khan et al.34 provided a comprehensive review of statistical and deep learning-based motor bearing diagnosis methods, highlighting the necessity of improved feature-space mapping to enhance model generalization. Finally, Jin et al.35 addressed slewing-bearing fault prediction by integrating industrial case libraries with multi-physics monitoring data and proposed a dynamic risk scoring framework for effective yaw and pitch system health management. Although deep learning–based diagnostic methods have demonstrated impressive performance, their success typically relies on the availability of large-scale labeled datasets. In practical industrial applications, however, fault data are often limited, unevenly distributed, and costly to collect, which makes conventional deep models prone to overfitting under data-scarce conditions. These constraints significantly hinder the deployment of data-hungry learning frameworks in real-world scenarios.

To overcome the scarcity of labelled samples in equipment fault diagnosis, learning strategies such as Zero-Shot Learning (ZSL) and Few-Shot Learning (FSL) have attracted growing interest. ZSL focuses on recognizing unseen fault classes by exploiting auxiliary knowledge, including semantic attributes or shared latent spaces, thereby facilitating knowledge transfer across fault categories without the need for labelled training examples. For example, Yang et al.36 introduced the ADT-ZSID model, which constructs universal fault attributes and performs similarity-based inference for unseen fault diagnosis in high-voltage circuit breakers. FSL, on the other hand, improves adaptability under limited labeled data using metric learning and meta-learning techniques37.

Consequently, applying deep learning to machine health monitoring systems (MHMS) remains challenging in real-world scenarios.

1) In real industrial environments, machines rarely operate for extended periods under faulty conditions; consequently, most collected data represent normal operation, resulting in highly imbalanced datasets with limited fault samples.

2) Manual fault labeling requires expert knowledge and substantial time investment, making it costly and impractical to generate large-scale annotated datasets for model training.

3) The scarcity of labeled fault data often causes overfitting in deep learning models, leading to unstable training and poor generalization to unseen conditions.

4) Industrial data are frequently affected by sensor noise, varying load conditions, and environmental disturbances, which complicate model training and reduce diagnostic accuracy.

5) Domain shifts between laboratory datasets (CWRU) and real-world machinery often cause performance degradation, as models trained in controlled settings may not generalize well to cross-domain applications.

6) The high computational cost and extensive number of parameters in deep learning models hinder their real-time deployment on edge and embedded industrial platforms.

To mitigate the challenge of limited labelled data, numerous few-shot and semi-supervised deep learning approaches have been proposed38–40. For example, Razavi-Far et al.41 developed a semi-supervised fault diagnosis method (Tri-DBN) that exploits unlabelled charging device data, while Che et al.42 introduced a semi-supervised multitask DCGAN for vibration signal analysis. Vinyals et al.43 proposed a metric-based matching network for few-shot learning, and Zhang et al.44 employed a Siamese network for bearing fault diagnosis. Furthermore, Yue et al.45 improved feature representation by integrating a multiscale wavelet convolutional module with metric learning to identify unseen fault classes using only a few labelled samples.

Mechanical fault diagnosis under conditions like data imbalance, domain shift, and limited labeled samples has been thoroughly studied in related works. For instance, Jian et al.46 proposed a prototype-guided supervised contrastive learning framework with dynamic temperature modulation to enhance model generalization under imbalanced multi-domain conditions by learning domain-invariant representations. In another study, Jian et al.47 introduced a two-stage learning framework for imbalanced semi-supervised domain generalization that integrates multi-scale convolutional feature extraction, adversarial domain learning, and pseudo-labeling to improve diagnostic performance with scarce labelled data. Similarly, Jian et al.48 developed a long-tailed multi-domain generalization approach to address imbalanced fault distributions and cross-domain scenarios in rotating machinery diagnosis. Furthermore, Jian et al.49 proposed a domain generalization network capable of learning transferable and domain-invariant features, improving fault recognition reliability across varying operating conditions. These studies highlight the importance of designing robust feature learning strategies to achieve accurate fault diagnosis in practical industrial environments with limited labeled data. Other studies have explored multi-view and feature fusion strategies to enhance fault diagnosis performance. For example, a multichannel-based multiview shallow fusion framework integrates complementary information from multiple signal representations to improve classification accuracy and robustness, particularly under complex conditions. This demonstrates the importance of leveraging diverse feature spaces to achieve reliable fault diagnosis50.

Although these methods have demonstrated promising results, these approaches often rely on complex training strategies, and they often struggle to establish accurate decision boundaries, and fluctuations in data distribution can significantly degrade their performance, thereby limiting industrial applicability. Their performance may degrade under extremely low-shot conditions or significant domain shifts, limiting their effectiveness and reliability in practical industrial applications. With the rapid advancement of IoT technology, vast amounts of unlabeled data have become available, offering new opportunities to enhance model robustness. Motivated by this, a novel Few-Shot Learning Framework (FS-Framework) is proposed, integrating supervised discriminative representation learning with angular-margin classification. This framework develops and fine-tunes a discriminative learning pipeline that produces compact and well-separated feature representations, improving diagnostic accuracy even with limited labeled samples.

1) Normalized embeddings. Two realizations are introduced: (i) A lightweight MS-1D-CNN followed by a minimal decision layer for waveform or PSD inputs, and (ii) an image-based encoder fine-tuned with ArcFace. This design enhances inter-class separability and intra-class compactness for both 1D and image representations.

2) Cross-domain zero-shot transfer through meta-learning.

A cross-domain zero-shot meta-learning strategy is proposed, where meta-training is conducted on the CWRU dataset and meta-testing is performed directly on real industrial datasets without any additional fine-tuning. This setting evaluates the true generalization capability of the learned embeddings under domain shift, demonstrating strong performance across unseen industrial conditions.

3) Lightweight separable Conv1D architecture for real-time implementation.

The proposed MS-1D-CNN employs depth-wise separable Conv1D layers to significantly reduce the number of trainable parameters and computational complexity. This lightweight design achieves efficient inference with minimal latency, making the framework suitable for real-time partial discharge monitoring in industrial environments.

4) Few-shot supervised discriminative inference for industrial data.

For real-time deployment, a few-shot supervised discriminative inference scheme is implemented on the industrial dataset. The model constructs class prototypes from limited support samples and classifies new queries via cosine similarity in the learned embedding space, enabling rapid adaptation to new fault classes with minimal data.

Literature review

Few-shot machinery fault diagnosis

Few-shot machinery fault diagnosis has recently gained attention as it seeks to identify faults with limited labelled data. The concept of few-shot learning (FSL), which enables model training with minimal samples, was initially introduced in the 1980s and has since advanced significantly in tackling data scarcity challenges51. In industrial applications, FSL models are particularly advantageous as they can efficiently extract discriminative features and achieve high accuracy even with small datasets52. For instance, Zhang et al.44 developed a CNN-based few-shot learning framework specifically designed for bearing fault diagnosis.

Li et al.53 applied meta-training to optimize initialization parameters across multiple fault tasks, enabling rapid and accurate diagnosis under unseen conditions. Wang et al.54 enhanced meta-transfer learning with domain adaptation to improve generalization across variable operating conditions. Wan et al.55 combined contrastive learning on unlabelled data with fine-tuning on limited labelled samples, while Siamese network-based approaches have also been widely explored. Xu et al.56 developed a Siamese model with a contrastive loss to increase inter-class separability and reduce intra-class variation. Generative methods have also been applied; for instance, Xie et al.57 introduced a self-attention metric learning approach with multiscale feature fusion (MSFF), whereas Liu et al.58 proposed TScatNet, a time scattering convolutional network using Morlet wavelets, which provided domain-invariant features and reduced the need for extensive hyperparameter tuning.

Nanqing Dong59 used domain adaptation methods with one-shot learning to address domain adaptation in one-shot image categorization. In a related effort, Daehwan Lee et al.52 optimized the WDCNN architecture to achieve high accuracy under few-shot learning scenarios. To further improve computational efficiency, depthwise separable convolution layers were adopted to reduce the number of parameters while maintaining classification performance.

Zheng et al.60 proposed a Siamese Network–based bearing fault diagnosis approach that relies on pairwise similarity learning; however, its performance is constrained by the high computational overhead associated with sample pairing. A Brain-Inspired Meta-Learning framework based on Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) was presented by Wang et al.61, who showed great efficacy in few-shot circumstances. In order to increase diagnostic performance in situations with limited data, Vu et al.62 created a dual-branch architecture that combines Mahalanobis distance measurements with Cross-Attention Transformers. Zhang et al.63 employed Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML) to enhance artificial fault generalization, achieving a notable 25% accuracy improvement over Siamese-based methods, though its cross-domain adaptability remained limited. Lin et al.64 Further extended this approach by proposing GMAML with multi-kernel attention, attaining 97.6% precision and 40% faster task adaptation, albeit at the expense of increased computational complexity and labelled data requirements. Li et al.65 combined SNNs with meta-contrastive learning to address fine-grained heterogeneous fault classification, achieving 92.8% accuracy. Wang et al.66 presented the FSM3 metric-based meta-learning model, which surpassed baseline methods by 18% in 5-shot tasks but did not explicitly address domain alignment. Shao et al.67 enhanced cross-domain diagnosis via ANIL-optimized residual networks, improving feature reuse by 31%. Ren et al.68 introduced a Capsule Autoencoder framework that reduced the required number of training samples from over 100 to 15 while decreasing training time by 58%. Finally, Cheng et al.69 proposed an MMFSL framework that leveraged multimodal data generation to raise industrial fault diagnosis accuracy from 80.9% to 97.5%.

A. Few-shot learning (FSL)

Enabling models to learn new tasks or classes from a limited number of labelled samples is the main goal of few-shot learning (FSL). In order to prevent overfitting when there is a lack of data, its efficacy depends on learning compact, discriminative feature representations and appropriate distance measures70. Metric-learning-based and meta-learning-based approaches are the two main types of FSL techniques. Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML) is a popular framework for meta-learning, which trains a model to quickly adapt by “learning how to learn.” In order for the model to perform well on new jobs with just a few gradient modifications, MAML optimizes a shared initialization across tasks. In this study, a dual-model few-shot learning (FSL) framework is developed to enhance bearing fault diagnosis performance across both benchmark and real-world industrial environments. The framework incorporates two backbone architectures FSL MS-1D-CNN (ArcFace) and FSL ResNet50 (ArcFace), each designed to extract discriminative temporal representations from vibration signals. The MS-1D-CNN model leverages multiple skip connections to facilitate efficient gradient propagation and feature reuse, thereby improving the network’s ability to capture subtle fault characteristics within complex temporal patterns. In contrast, the ResNet50 model employs deeper residual blocks that enable hierarchical feature extraction, providing a strong baseline for comparison and cross-domain adaptation. Both architectures are integrated with an ArcFace angular-margin classifier, which enforces enhanced inter-class separability and intra-class compactness within a 128-dimensional embedding space—properties that are critical for robust few-shot recognition under limited labeled data conditions. The meta-learning framework is trained on the CWRU bearing dataset to acquire generalized fault representations and subsequently evaluated on an industrial real-time vibration dataset collected under varying operational conditions. This cross-domain meta-train/meta-test paradigm allows assessment of the models’ transferability and generalization ability when exposed to unseen domains. Experimental results, including K-way N-shot classification and t-SNE visualization of learned embeddings, demonstrate that the proposed FSL architectures effectively capture domain-invariant fault features, achieving reliable performance in industrial scenarios where data scarcity and distribution shifts are prevalent.

Improved siamese network (MS-1D-CNN) for few-shot learning

In the first stage, this study enhances the lightweight MS-1D-CNN by integrating a separable-convolutional backbone with multi-skip fusion and coupling it to an ArcFace classification head, model architecture showed in Fig. 2. This design promotes stronger intra-class cohesion and greater inter-class separation. The conventional softmax layer is replaced by an additive angular-margin softmax (ArcFace; scale \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:s$$\end{document}, margin \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:m$$\end{document}) applied to L2-normalized encoder features, thereby yielding margin-aware prototypes and improving closed-set separability without increasing the parameter burden (~ 76k parameters, ~ 296 KB). Meta-training is carried out on ten CWRU classes using windowed and z-normalized signals, with episodic sampling employed to emulate few-shot conditions. Class partitions are grouped by file identifier to ensure disjoint support and query sets, thereby eliminating data leakage. Evaluation follows the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:K$$\end{document}-shot protocol \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:K\:\in\:\:\{1,\:3,\:5,\:10,\:20\}$$\end{document}, with 15 query samples per class, and performance is reported using averaged accuracies, aggregated confusion matrices, and t-SNE visualizations of the 128-dimensional L2-normalized encoder embeddings. This design provides a controlled, like-for-like comparison with the earlier Siamese-based framework71, thereby isolating the contribution of the ArcFace classifier.

Lightweight MS-1D-CNN model architecture.

The additive angular-margin softmax (ArcFace) loss, which uses class weights and L2-normalized feature embeddings, is used to train the encoder. The loss increases inter-class angular separation in the embedding space while enforcing tighter intra-class clustering through the introduction of an angular margin and scaling factor . The learnt features’ discriminative structure is directly improved by this margin-based constraint, which increases their suitability for prototype-based few-shot inference. Specifically, the enhanced angular separability reduces class overlap during cosine similarity-based classification by producing more compact and well-separated class prototypes. As a result, under cross-domain transfer from CWRU to field data, the ArcFace goal helps achieve more consistent and dependable performance in a variety of shooting situations.

In the second stage, the framework is extended to field data in order to evaluate robustness under real-world operating conditions. Following meta-training on the CWRU dataset, the ArcFace-trained encoder is frozen and adapted to two additional fault classes acquired from field measurements. Signals are divided into windows that do not overlap, and for every K-shot episode, K support windows per field class are embedded and averaged to form cosine-normalized prototypes. Query windows are then classified by cosine similarity with temperature scaling. Evaluation is performed under both an ungrouped setting (random support/query selection) and a grouped setting where support and query originate from different pseudo-groups in a 50/50 split, thereby simulating a realistic domain shift. To minimize the gap in distribution between the target (field) and source (CWRU) domains, Adaptive Batch Normalization (AdaBN) is applied once using unlabeled mixed field windows before few-shot testing. Embedding quality and cross-domain alignment are further assessed through t-SNE visualization after AdaBN, while classification performance is summarized across five trials per K using accuracy and 2 × 2 confusion matrices.

The best-performing solution from the metaheuristic optimisation procedure, which is mostly determined by validation accuracy in noisy environments, is the final hyperparameter configuration used in this study. The final model configuration was chosen using the converged parameter regions of PSO and GWO since they consistently outperformed GA in terms of convergence and stability. The chosen hyperparameters were further confirmed over several runs and datasets, including the CWRU and field data situations, without further manual adjustment to guarantee robustness. This approach guarantees that the reported performance is a systematically optimised and validated configuration appropriate for few-shot and cross-domain defect diagnosis tasks rather than the outcome of arbitrary parameter selection.

Experimental setup

1) The suggested few-shot learning framework is trained on a workstation with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4050 GPU and implemented in Python using the Keras deep learning package. Practically speaking, the framework is meant to represent real-world industrial settings where a lot of unlabeled vibration data is easily accessible yet labeled fault samples are hard to come by and expensive.

There are two steps in the training process. First, representative characteristics are extracted from unlabeled vibration signals using self-supervised learning. A total of 12,000 training samples are produced by randomly selecting a subset of raw signals and augmenting them using various signal treatments. The network can learn inherent signal features at this level that are not dependent on fault labels. Using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001, pretraining is carried out for 100 epochs with a batch size of 32.

In the second stage, the pretrained model is adapted to specific fault diagnosis tasks under few-shot conditions. Only a limited number of labeled samples are used for training, consistent with practical scenarios where fault data collection is constrained by operational cost and safety considerations. To reduce the risk of overfitting during this adaptation process, a smaller learning rate of 0.0006 is adopted, while the batch size is maintained at 32. The network is trained for 5000 iterations to ensure stable convergence despite the limited data availability.

Model performance is evaluated using classification accuracy, defined as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{A}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{y}=\frac{\mathrm{T}\mathrm{P}+\mathrm{T}\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{T}\mathrm{P}+\mathrm{F}\mathrm{P}+\mathrm{T}\mathrm{N}+\mathrm{F}\mathrm{N}}$$\end{document}.

where TP, FP, TN, and FN denote the numbers of true positive, false positive, true negative, and false negative samples, respectively. To account for the inherent randomness introduced by few-shot sample selection, each experiment is repeated five times using different randomly constructed few-shot training sets, and the average accuracy is reported as the result.

Experiments are carried out on both a publicly accessible bearing fault dataset and a real-world industrial dataset to verify the efficacy and robustness of the suggested methodology, allowing evaluation under controlled laboratory conditions as well as practical industrial environments.

Dataset description and data preparation

The efficacy of the suggested approach is assessed under controlled but generally acknowledged benchmark settings using the Case Western Reserve University (CWRU) bearing dataset. Four bearing health states normal operation, ball fault, outer race fault, and inner race fault are represented by vibration signals in the dataset. Ten categories total one normal class and nine fault classes are produced by providing three defect sizes (7 mils, 14 mils, and 21 mils) for each type of fault Fig. 4.

Fig. 4(a) Experimental platform and (b) fault type for CWRU dataset.

(a) Experimental platform and (b) fault type for CWRU dataset.

In this study, vibration signals acquired from the drive-end bearing are used, as they are commonly considered more informative for fault diagnosis. The data were collected at a sampling frequency of 12 kHz under four different motor load conditions ranging from no load to 3 hp, thereby introducing variability that reflects realistic operating environments.

To construct training and testing samples, the raw vibration signals are segmented using a sliding window approach with a fixed window length of 2048 sampling points. This segmentation strategy enables the generation of sufficient samples while preserving local temporal characteristics of the vibration signals. After segmentation, each class is configured to contain 60 samples for training and 176 samples for testing, ensuring a balanced class distribution.

A small number of labeled samples are chosen at random from each class’s training set to replicate few-shot diagnostic scenarios. Models are trained in several experimental situations using 1, 3, 5, 10, and 20 samples per class. Seventy samples per class are selected at random from the testing set for evaluation. This experimental approach is intended to mimic real-world industrial scenarios where there is enough data for performance evaluation but only a small number of defect samples are available for model construction.

Computational complexity analysis

To further evaluate the practicality of the proposed approach, computational complexity was analyzed in terms of training time, inference latency, memory consumption, and model size, as illustrated in Fig. 11.

The results show that the backbone architecture is the primary factor influencing computational cost. The transition from BaselineCNN to MultiSkipCNN increases training time significantly, from approximately 29–37 s to around 123–125 s on the CWRU dataset and from 781 to 833 s to approximately 2914–2976 s on the PU dataset. This increase is expected due to the additional multi-scale feature extraction layers, which introduce higher representational capacity at the cost of increased computation.

In contrast, the additional overhead introduced by ArcFace is relatively modest. For both datasets, the difference in training time between Softmax and ArcFace remains small compared to the backbone-induced cost. This indicates that the improved discriminative capability provided by ArcFace is achieved with minimal additional computational burden, making it a cost-effective enhancement.

Inference latency analysis reveals a similar trend. The baseline models achieve the lowest latency due to their simpler architecture, while the MultiSkip-based models exhibit higher inference time. However, the increase remains moderate and scales smoothly with K. Importantly, the proposed MultiSkipCNN + ArcFace configuration demonstrates comparable or slightly improved latency compared to MultiSkipCNN + Softmax in some cases, suggesting that the ArcFace head does not introduce significant inference overhead.

Limitations and future work

Even if the suggested framework performed well, there are a few things to be aware of. The existing method may not adequately capture intricate and structured distribution shifts between laboratory and industrial environments because it is based on a relatively straightforward domain adaptation strategy based on AdaBN. Furthermore, the study’s industrial dataset only includes a small number of failure types, which limits how thorough real-world validation may be. Furthermore, due to data confidentiality restrictions, comprehensive details about the industrial dataset cannot be completely shared, which could have an impact on reproducibility and direct comparison with alternative approaches.

In order to effectively handle cross-domain variability in industrial settings, future research will concentrate on creating more sophisticated and organised domain adaptation techniques. To increase the effectiveness of the model and its capacity for generalisation, the integration of knowledge distillation techniques will also be investigated. Furthermore, for more thorough validation, the industrial dataset must be expanded to include a wider variety of defect categories. It is anticipated that these paths will further improve the suggested framework’s resilience and usefulness in actual deployment situations.

Conclusion

This paper proposed a Few-Shot Learning (FSL) approach integrated with a lightweight MS-1D-CNN model for machine health monitoring under limited labeled data availability. By combining separable convolutions and skip connections with meta-learning strategies, the model effectively captured discriminative fault features while maintaining low computational complexity. Experimental validation on both the benchmark CWRU dataset and real-world industrial data confirmed superior performance, achieving more than 99% accuracy across various few-shot settings. The experimental results demonstrate the strong robustness and generalization ability of the proposed framework, indicating its suitability for practical real-time fault detection in industrial applications.
