Anomalous enhancement of humid CO2 capture by local surface bound water in polar carbon nanopores
Authors: Zhe Zheng, Yong-Sheng Wang, Miao Wang, Guo-Hua Zhao, Guang-Ping Hao, An-Hui Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRemoval of confined space carbon dioxide (CO2) that is in low concentration and with coexisting water is necessary but challenging by physical adsorption method. To make the removal process effective, rendering the nanopore surface hydrophobic to resist water is the popular way. Instead of preventing water from occupying the nanopores, in this work, we propose to utilize the guest water for the spatially selective formation of local surface bound water and further induce the preferential CO2 capture. We observe an anomalous enhancement of CO2 capture performance under humid conditions over carbon nanopores with spatially selective polar sites. It is evidenced that the surface bound water is formed at non-CO2-selective areas of polar carbon nanopores, thus creating additional CO2 trapping sites. This work may inspire the design of environment tolerable materials for molecular separation and purification under harsh conditions.Subject terms: Chemical engineering, Porous materials

Introduction

Removal of low concentration carbon dioxide (CO2) in confined spaces (e.g., space stations, submarines, etc.) is necessary since the accumulated CO2 could easily reach 3000 ppm and above in poorly ventilated conditions that may lead to serious health risks to the human body1. To address this issue, current methods mainly rely on solid or liquid phase amine absorption2,3, which requires relatively high energy for its regeneration, and may suffer from the health risk due to amine leaking. The physical adsorption method based on tailored porous materials has attracted wide interest due to its relatively safe operation and easy regeneration4,5. However, the low concentration of CO2 as well as the severe competitive adsorption of water make its capture highly challenging6.

Porous materials that are suitable for dilute CO2 removal are often composed of highly polar adsorption sites to enhance the interaction toward CO2, thus enabling an expected adsorption capacity7. Thus, the introduction and alternation of metal cations in zeolites, open metal centers in metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) and nitrogen-doping sites in porous carbons etc., have been extensively explored to create such CO2-philic local environment8–14. However, in most cases, such CO2-philic sites are even more attractive to water because of water’s smaller molecular size and larger dipole moment (CO2−3.3 Å, 0 esu cm; H2O-2.6 Å, 1.85 × 10−18 esu cm)15. This leads to the dilemma that the capture materials with excellent CO2 capture under dry conditions often fail to work under humid conditions. Because of this, in practical CO2 capture technology, the pre-removal of water is required ahead of CO2 removal unit9. This in turn increases the setup complexity and operational energy consumption.

The popular way to resist water moisture is to construct a hydrophobic surface of adsorbent12–14. However, the hydrophobic adsorbents still suffer from performance reduction by more than 40% under high humidity conditions such as 75% relative humidity (75% RH) compared to that for dry gas feed, particularly after multiple adsorption cycles14. Although the design of adsorbent with separately H2O-specific and CO2-specific sites can maintain the CO2 capture performance under humid conditions, the presence of water did not show additional enhancement for CO2 capture, and its regeneration is still energy-consuming10. Very recently, Katz et al. reported an exceptional zeolite adsorbent, named K-MER, over which CO2 can beat water in the competitive adsorption under wet conditions16. The key for such CO2-selective adsorption behavior was linked to the unique microenvironment of the adsorption sites in K-MER that can preferentially trap CO2 instead of water. Nevertheless, robust physical adsorbents that could selectively capture CO2 in the presence of water or even could enhance CO2 capture by the coexisting water are still rarely seen.

The enhancement of CO2 capture by coexisting water was indeed observed in amine-decorated sorbents, such as amine-grafted MOFs and zeolites17,18. However, such enhancement mainly stems from chemical reactions by forming ammonium carbamate (e.g., 0.5 CO2 per amine group) or bicarbonate (e.g., 1.0 CO2 per amine group)19,20. For such a chemical way, its thermal stability and cycle stability remains a problem21. Recent computational and experimental results reveal that confined water in nanopores can form ultrathin water nanofilms22. The water orientation in the nanofilms depends on the local interactions between water and nanopore surface, which include the extended hydrogen bond, induced dipole interactions, etc. Of notice, under highly confined conditions, the surface bound water can be immobile and highly stable23,24.

Based on the above understanding, herein, we propose to utilize the coexisting water by selectively forming compact water nanofilms in non-CO2-selective areas, which could in-situ transform into CO2-selective adsorption sites. We previously reported the most ever ultra-polar carbon nanopores (UPC)25–27, which represent one ideal model to verify our proposal. In UPC, the heteroatom terminated sites distribute at the lapping area of carbon nanolayers that feature rich edges. In principle, the rich edges would readily induce water nucleation in such lapping area and form local water nanofilms. By controlling the status of local nanosized water, we aim to create additional trapping sites for CO2 adsorption.

Results

Screening of carbon nanopores with different polarity

Following the above proposal, we first screened a large set of adsorbents by conducting the dynamic breakthrough experiments using gas feed composed of 3000 ppm CO2 in N2 under dry and humid conditions. UPC indeed exhibited the expected CO2 removal performance under dry condition as well as in the first cycle under humid condition of 75% RH (Fig. 1a). However, it gradually lost the selective CO2 capture performance, and the CO2 breakthrough time decreased by ∼60% in eight cycles. Although this performance is still better than the benchmark adsorbent of zeolite 13X that completely lost the CO2 capture performance in two cycles due to micropore filling by water induced by the overcrowded polar sites, the expected enhancement by the coexisting water did not appear (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Based on the above attempts, we realized that spatially selective water nanofilms at the targeted area of nanopores would be a prerequisite rather than the full filling. To reach such state, it requires the fine regulation of the density and distribution of polar sites in the nanopore walls. The heteroatom doping sites such as nitrogen/oxygen, N/O-containing sites are believed to create local dipoles to enhance the attraction to polar molecules25,27. While the specific O-doping sites like carboxyl groups were evidenced to show a relatively strong attraction to water28. Thus, we chose the thermal reduction method to tune the surface polarity by selective modification of the polar groups. We investigated the surface compositions of this series of materials as well as their influence on surface polarity (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). The sample obtained from H2 reduction for 1 h at 500 °C was denoted MPC; while for milder or harder thermal reduction, the samples were denoted MPC-L (1 h, 300 oC, H2) and WPC (3 h, 500 oC, H2). For MPC, the content of the carboxyl group decreased by 25% compared to UPC (Fig. 1b); while the changes of C and N are less than 3.8% (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). The surface polarity was first compared by water contacting behaviors (Supplementary Fig. 4), which all show rather hydrophilic property. The polarity difference of the polar carbon nanopores was then quantified by comparing the slope of the water vapor adsorption isotherms within 20% RH. We defined the polarity of the most polar sample of UPC as unit 1, based on which the polarities of other less polar samples were normalized. The calculated values are 0.85, 0.56 and 0.48 for MPC-L, MPC and WPC, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 5, 6 and Supplementary Table 3).

Then, we compared the CO2 capture performances for this series of samples with well-controlled polarity gradient under humid condition. All the polar nanoporous carbon adsorbents indeed showed the enhancement for CO2 capture as compared to their performance under dry condition (Supplementary Fig. 7). Among them, MPC-H revealed the largest enhancement of CO2 capture performance under humid condition, which outperform by as high as 87% compared to its dry feed condition (MPC-D) (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 8). The effects of varied humidity were scanned, and all show the enhancement of CO2 capture performance even at 90% RH (Supplementary Fig. 9). We next compared our MPC-H with other popular benchmarks of CO2 capture materials such as zeolite of 13X, MOFs of SIFSIX-18Ni, NbOFFIVE-1-Ni. The performance over MPC-H is also in sharp contrast to the benchmarks that showed clear performance reduction under humid condition even in one cycle (Fig. 1d)9,14,29. In addition, the enhancement for MPC-H is also different from the nonpolar activated carbons (AC) (Supplementary Fig. 10). The hydrophobic feature of AC indeed ensures the initial stability in the first cycle; however, it finally lost ∼60% of CO2 capture performance in four cycles (Supplementary Fig. 11).

We believe such anomalous enhancement over MPC-H with polar carbon pores should link to a distinct water facilitated CO2 adsorption behavior (Fig. 1e, left) that is different from the polar crystalline pores (Fig. 1e, middle) and the amine-decorated pores (Fig. 1e, right) mentioned above. We proposed the water facilitated CO2 capture mechanism based on the understanding of the carbon slit-type pore structure and the unique polar property. Microscopically, the slit-type pores of carbon adsorbents are dominated with different slit sizes30,31. At the lapping area of carbon nanolayers, the slit sizes are larger (pore size distribution, PSD ∼0.8–1 nm32) and the edge polar sites are rich25. Such larger slit pores are less effective to trap CO2, but the rich polar edges at the lapping area can readily induce water to nucleate and coalesce, forming surface bound water25,27. Because of the formed surface bound water, the larger ineffective size would become narrower. Potentially, it could turn the non-CO2-selective areas to CO2-selective adsorption sites. Notably, the formation of surface bound water was unlikely to affect the original narrow slits (PSD ∼0.35-0.4 nm) that are suitable for selective CO2 adsorption.

Verification of the stability and generality of the proposed mechanism

Next, we further assessed the adsorbent performance and the stability during adsorption cycling, and we also tried to verify the generality of our proposed mechanism. For MPC-H, the enhancement on CO2 capture performance was well remained in successively 20 rounds of dynamic breakthrough test (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 12). This indicates that the adsorbed CO2 can be reversibly removed by Ar purging at 298 K, and the introduced water remains rather stable under such condition. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 13, the fluctuation in the last ten cycles was even smaller than the initial cycles. This is probably because the formation of surface bound water gradually reached the stable state during cycling under humid condition. To further evaluate the stability of MPC-H, we tested one sample that aged in air for 6 months. The enhancement on CO2 capture over the aged sample under humid conditions remained almost the same as the fresh MPC-H (Supplementary Fig. 14). The results again confirm the robust structure stability of MPC-H.

Then we verified the generality of the proposed mechanism by changing the coexisting vapors or other kinds of carbon adsorbents with polar pores. We first compared the CO2 capture performances by replacing water with methanol or ethanol that are also polar but with different molecular dimensions (Fig. 2b). In the presence of methanol (sample denoted MPC-M), the CO2 breakthrough time increased by 25%; however, the ethanol involved sample (MPC-E) revealed a reduction of the breakthrough time by 44%. This means that methanol can also create additional adsorption sites suitable for CO2 adsorption, while the larger molecular size of methanol than water reduces the effective volume for CO2 accommodation. The performance reduction over MPC-E was probably due to that the much larger ethanol blocked or fully occupied the nanopores of MPC. To verify our proposed mechanism, we tried to enlarge the pore size of MPC by steam activation following the established method33. After activation, the proportion of larger pores at 0.7-0.9 nm clearly increased for the activated MPC (denoted MPC-ac) as compared to its pristine sample (Supplementary Fig. 15). We again conducted the breakthrough test under the identical condition, and found that the CO2 breakthrough time was indeed increased from 18 min/g for MPC-ac to 24 min/g for ethanol involved MPC-ac (denoted MPC-ac-E) (Supplementary Fig. 16). In parallel, we validated another kind of carbon adsorbent (PANC) that is derived from polyacrylonitrile precursor, which shows a similar surface polarity and pore size as that of MPC (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 17). We observed the enhancement effect for CO2 capture under humid condition, showing a 22% longer breakthrough time than that under dry condition (Fig. 2d). These results confirmed the generality of our proposed mechanism on water facilitated CO2 capture over polar carbon nanopores in the presence of water or other polar molecules with suitable molecular size.

Understanding the enhancement effect of CO2 capture under humid condition

In this section, we tried to uncover the origin of such anomalous enhancement effect. Firstly, we monitored the pore structure changes of the samples during the formation of surface bound water film at different stages. The different developing stages can be reflected by the different water content. At dry condition, the sample of MPC exhibits a narrow PSD at ∼0.35 nm, a wide PSD at ∼0.5 nm and a weak but even wider PSD at ∼0.8 nm; increasing water content led to the increasing intensity of the PSD at ∼0.35 nm and the PSD at ∼0.5 nm, however, the weak wide PSD at ∼0.8 nm almost disappeared (Supplementary Fig. 18). Thus, the performance enhancement in the presence of water can be well explained by the formation of additional adsorption sites suitable for CO2 capture since the narrow carbon slits centered at 0.35 nm and 0.5 nm are effective for CO2 capture. Accordingly, the formation of surface bound water led to the slight reduction of larger micropore volume of around 16% based on the results of CO2 adsorption at 1.0 bar at 273 K. Consistently, the significant loss of N2 adsorption uptake of 84% again evidenced the selective formation of surface bound water at the larger lapping area in MPC (Supplementary Fig. 19).

The above results supported that the surface bound water was spatially formed at the larger lapping area of MPC instead of a complete pore filling even at a high humidity of 75% RH. We then employed the solid nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) technique to analyze the status of the confined water in polar carbon nanopores. Solid NMR is particularly useful to probe the gradually filling behavior as water diffuses into the material’s nanopores28. Figure 3a shows the microscopic record of water nucleation and growth inside the micropore of MPC. In NMR spectra, the peak at ∼7.5 ppm indicates the formation of water clusters; another peak located at ∼2.9 ppm represents the coalesced water clusters, which is the evidence on the formation of local surface bound water34. Under 15% RH, the introduced water is mainly in the form of clusters and the clusters started to coalesce. As reaching 75% RH, dominated is the coalesced water clusters. Of notice, the local water in confined nanopores is different from the condensed water formed due to the capillary effect in larger pores (PSD of CMK-3: ∼4 nm35). Together with all the above results, we believe that the formation of local surface water is spatially selective, which mainly occupies the area with large nanopores that are N2 accessible but non-CO2-selective. In turn, the modified area by the local surface bound water turned to CO2-selective.

Next, we conducted the in-situ Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) to further record the changes in the state of surface bound water as CO2 diffusing into MPC-D and MPC-H. For MPC-D, the peaks at ∼2335 cm−1 and 2360 cm−1 were observed, which are ascribed to the physically adsorbed CO2 (Fig. 3b)10. For MPC-H, two absorption peaks appeared at ∼2960 cm−1 (broad) and 2920 cm−1 (small but visible) (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 21), which are likely relevant to the intensive O–H…O stretching of the surface bound water in amorphous multimers state36,37. We also found that the two bands first became intenser after the introduction of CO2, and then became weaker but still stronger than that in the absence of CO2. We reason that this transition reflected the relocation of guest CO2 in the nanopores with surface bound water. In this way, additional CO2 can be captured in the originally non-CO2-selective areas.

Since carbonic acid in solid form can form from CO2 and water in certain conditions such as astrophysical environments38, we also wondered that whether transient carbonic acid might form in our case, i.e. CO2 adsorption in the presence of water in the polar nanopores of MPC-H. Thus, we tried to significantly increase the time resolution of infrared spectrum to see the featured signal (Supplementary Fig. 22). However, we did not see any featured peaks that could link to carbonic acid (∼1735 cm−1); other carbonic acid relevant signals were also absent, such as CO32- (∼1658 cm−1) and HCO3- (∼1618 cm−1) (Supplementary Fig. 23). The presented signals at ∼2335 cm−1 and 2360 cm−1 can be assigned to the physically adsorbed CO2 (Supplementary Fig. 24). The peaks at ∼2960 cm−1 and 2920 cm−1 were observed that are relevant to the O-H…O stretching of the surface bound water (Supplementary Fig. 25). Since the interaction between water and CO2 in nanopores is intricate and highly dynamic, it is worthy to explore with advanced techniques to unveil the interactions between the guest CO2 and the surface bound water under highly confined nanopores.

Furthermore, the presence of the spatially selective surface bound water in polar carbon pores coincides well with the observed enhancement effect of CO2 capture (Fig. 4a). We further compared the adsorption kinetics for the samples before and after the formation of surface bound water. With surface bound water, MPC-H reached equilibrium in CO2 adsorption much faster than MPC-D (Fig. 4b). The diffusional time constants of CO2 and N2 for MPC-H were calculated to be 1.83 × 10−3 s−1 and 1.86 × 10−3 s−1 respectively, which increased by 10% and decreased by 43% as compared to that of MPC-D (Supplementary Figs. 26 and 27). These results highlight the higher selectivity towards CO2 in the presence of local surface bound water, which is highly required for dilute CO2 capture.

The application under simulated confined space conditions

In the following, we made efforts to show the application potential for the removal of CO2 in humid conditions from the confined space such as space station. Normally, a four-bed vacuum temperature swing adsorption (VTSA) process is used to remove the CO2 exhaled by the crew of a space station (Supplementary Fig. 28). In the four-bed process, two of the beds are packed with zeolites for water removal; the other two are used for CO2 removal. For MPC-H, it showed the enhancement effects for CO2 capture under humid conditions, and thus the two water removal beds are not required. Thus, the two-bed vacuum swing adsorption (VSA) process was suitable for our case.

Based on the understanding of MPC-H adsorbent, we first verified its feasibility by Aspen Adsorption simulation (Fig. 5a). A confined space with a volume of 20 m3 and humidity of 75% RH was selected as the simulation scenario. We made the assumptions: (1) 3 crew members were working in the space; (2) the average amount of CO2 breathed by one person in one hour is set as 20 L39. The CO2 adsorption beds work alternately at the pressure of 1.3 bar and a flow rate of 2000 L/min, then, the outlet gas (<50 ppm CO2) after the MPC-VSA process will be discharged into the space station (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Figs. 29–32 and Supplementary Tables 4–6). In the meantime, another bed was regenerated by vacuum and purging. The CO2 concentration of confined space could be decreased from 3000 ppm to 1000 ppm within 15 min, and decreased to 500 ppm within 60 min (Fig. 5c).

The working capacity and temperature were also recorded, and compared to the benchmark 13X. Under steady-state conditions, water will no longer be adsorbed, and the fluctuation of bed temperature and capture capacity is less than 0.3% (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 33). In contrast, a reduction of work capacity of 56% was observed on 13X due to the temperature rising to 326 K (Supplementary Figs. 34–38). This result is consistent with the reported result40. Under the same gas processing capacity, we conducted simulations for 13X-VTSA to compare the operational energy consumption (Supplementary Figs. 39–41 and Supplementary Tables 7 and 8). Regarding the 13X-VTSA process, the total energy consumption is mainly from gas compressing, vacuum pumping, heating for adsorbent regeneration, together with cooling for feed gas. The regeneration energy is required as high as 41.3 MJ/kg CO2 for 13X-VTSA, in which the energy for the heat regeneration is dominated, accounting for 53.9% (Supplementary Fig. 42 and Supplementary Table 9). In contrast, the MPC-VSA process benefits from a simpler adsorbent regeneration process, with energy demands primarily from gas compressing and vacuum pumping. Under identical conditions, the MPC-VSA process without the need of removing water moisture and heating regeneration could reduce 54.2% of the energy consumption, according to 18.9 MJ/kg CO2, indicating the evident advantages (Fig. 5e).

Discussion

The physical adsorption is one of the effective strategies for capturing CO2 from the ambient air to curb the deleterious effect of greenhouse gases and maintain spatial air quality. Although there are already some physisorbents have pore surfaces with the requisite thermodynamics and kinetics to capture trace CO2, the competition adsorption between water and CO2 in actual humid environments can result in over 40% loss of CO2 capture capacity. On the other hand, during the regeneration process of the adsorbents, the adsorbent must completely desorb water to release the adsorption sites, which occupies most of the energy consumption of the regeneration. In this context, we have rationally designed a polar porous carbon to achieve sustained capture of trace CO2 with enhanced stability, renewability, and affordability in humid conditions. By regulating the surface chemistry of the adsorbents, it was found that the anomalous enhancement of humid low concentration CO2 capture in polar carbon pores. This work provides an alternative solution to address the trade-off between the CO2 adsorption capacity and stability in humid conditions for CO2 removal.

Different from other amine-decorated sorbents that use water to improve amine utilization and promote CO2 capture, the carbon nanopores with nice polarity allow for the spatially selective formation of local surface bound water, around which it creates additional CO2 capture sites instead of occupying CO2 adsorption sites for the normal way. This anomalous enhancement can be stably maintained during the CO2 capture process and repeatedly validated in similar porous carbons. The simulation results show that the adsorbent exhibits stable capture ability and fast capture rate during the CO2 capture process based on VSA processes. Because there is no need to remove water moisture, it makes the VSA process highly energy efficient and competitive compared to the traditional VTSA process. We believe that this method may advance the design of suitable adsorbents for molecular separation and purification under humid conditions.

Methods

Chemicals and reagents

All chemicals were purchased from commercial suppliers and used as received. 4,4’-Bipyridine (C10H8N2, 98%) was purchased from Aladdin Co., Ltd. Iron nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3·9H2O, 98.5%), copper chloride dihydrate (CuCl2·2H2O, 99%), methanol (CH3OH, 99.5%) and ethanol (CH3CH2OH, 99.5%) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Activated carbon (AC) and zeolite 13X were purchased from Kuraray Co., Ltd and Luoyang Jianlong Micro-Nano New Materials Co., Ltd, respectively. High purity Ar (99.999%), H2 (99.999%), CO2 (99.999%) and CO2/N2 gas mixture (0.3/99.7, vol%) were purchased from Dalian special gases Co., Ltd.

Preparation of polar, medium polar, nonpolar adsorbents and other control samples

The preparation of ultrapolar carbon (UPC) was following a known procedure25. In brief, the UPC was derived from coordinated polymer precursor, which was prepared from the rapid coordination reaction between 4,4’-bipyridine and CuCl2·2H2O. After carbonizing at 500 °C in Ar and subsequent washing with 4 M nitric acid, the sample of UPC was obtained. Medium polar carbon (MPC) and weak polar carbon (WPC) were obtained through thermal reduction of UPC at 500 °C in H2/Ar gas mixture (1/9, vol%) for 1 and 3 h, respectively. MPC-L was obtained from the thermal reduction of UPC at 300 °C in H2/Ar gas mixture (1/9, vol%) for 1 h. MPC was activated at 800 °C for 2 h with steam following the established method33. The resultant sample was denoted MPC-ac.

To verify the generality of this method, the polyacrylonitrile-based carbon (PANC) was prepared for comparison following a known procedure41. Firstly, a certain amount of acrylonitrile was disolved in acetone. Then, the initiator of azobis(isobutyronitrile) was introduced and the polymerization was proceeded at 70 °C for 2 h under Ar protection. Next, the polyacrylonitrile precursor was stabilized in air at 230 °C for 2 h. Followingly, the polymer precursor was carbonized in Ar at 800 °C for 2 h and subsequently activated at the same temperature for another 2 h in the presence of CO2. The resultant sample was denoted PANC.

Mesoporous CMK-3 was prepared through a nanocasting method35. Typically, furfuryl alcohol (FA) was used as carbon precursor, and oxalic acid was introduced to the FA solution. A certain amount of FA solution was loaded into ordered mesoporous silica template of SBA−15 using the incipient wetness impregnation at room temperature, followed by polymerization at 50 °C for 24 h. After drying, the composite was carbonized at 850 °C for 4 h in Ar. Then, the SBA−15 template was leached out with aqueous NaOH solution, resulting in the final mesoporous CMK-3.

Materials characterizations

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra were acquired using a Escalab XI+ photoelectron spectroscopy system. The binding energy scale was calibrated using the C 1 s peak at 284.8 eV. 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were collected on a spectrometer (Bruker Avance III HD) operating at 400.13 MHz, using a Bruker 4-mm probe and a rotor spinning frequency of 10 kHz. In-situ Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was recorded on a spectrometer (Bruker Vertex 70 v). H2O and N2 adsorption isotherms at 298 K were measured with a VSTAR vapor sorption analyzer (Quantachrome). CO2 adsorption isotherms at 273 K were measured with an automated gas sorption analyzer (Quantachrome Autosorb-iQ). Pore size distribution, PSD was determined from the adsorption branches of CO2 isotherm collected at 273 K using the non-localized density functional theory (NLDFT) model. The time-resolved adsorption profiles were collected on an Intelligent Gravimetric Analyzer (IGA, Hiden).

Dynamic breakthrough measurements

The dynamic breakthrough experiments were conducted in a homemade dynamic gas breakthrough setup. The separation of CO2/N2 mixtures was performed on a fixed-bed adsorber (a stainless-steel tube with an inner diameter of 7 mm and a length of 6 cm) at 1 bar and 25 °C, which was controlled by a pressure controller and a thermostatic water bath. The pressure drop was not observed in adsorption column. Firstly, the sample was activated by Ar purging at a flow rate of 15 mL/min. Then, the breakthrough experiment was performed by abruptly switching from Ar to the CO2/N2 gas mixtures. CO2/N2 gas mixtures feed in breakthrough tests were composed of 3000 ppm CO2 in N2, with dry or 75% RH and flow rate of 10 mL/min, unless otherwise specified. The gas stream at the outlet of the column was analyzed online by using a mass spectrometer (Hiden HPR-20 EGA). In addition, for dynamic breakthrough cycling tests, the samples were regenerated with an Ar flow of 50 mL/min at 25 °C after CO2 adsorption reached saturation. All samples were degassed at 300 °C overnight before experiment. In addition to the coexisting water moisture, we also evaluated the dynamic breakthrough performance in the presence of methanol (MPC-M) or ethanol (MPC-E) under the conditions identical to that for water.

Aspen adsorption simulations

Aspen adsorption employs a spatial discretization method to address partial differential equations governing mass, momentum, and energy balances between gas and solid phases. This approach is combined with a selected isotherm model. In this simulation, the first-order Upwind Differencing Scheme 1 (UDS1) is chosen, utilizing 20 nodes for discretization. The Implicit Euler method is applied to numerically solve the differential equations, yielding precise results within a concise simulation timeframe.
