Oral contraceptive use and its sociocultural determinants in Iranian women: a secondary cross-sectional analysis from the PARS cohort study
Authors: Zahra Sharifi, Shayan Yousufzai, Mohammadreza Akbari, Hossein Poustchi, Abdullah Gandomkar, Fatemeh Malekzadeh, Shohra Qaderi, Hossein Molavi Vardanjani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study addresses a gap in global research by exploring sociocultural factors and health outcomes related to oral contraceptive pill (OCP) use among middle-aged Iranian women, where non-prescription access is common. It aims to identify determinants of OCP use and its association with chronic diseases in this demographic.MethodsThis study was a secondary cross-sectional analysis which was conducted using baseline data from the Pars Cohort Study, launched in 2012 and included a sample of 4,034 married middle-aged women aged 45–64 years residing in Fars Province, Iran. To evaluate the factors influencing the current use of OCPs as the preferred contraceptive method among married women aged 45–64 years old in our cohort, Poisson regression analysis was employed. This analysis was adjusted for various demographic, reproductive, medical, and behavioral variables.ResultsOverall, 60.5% of women were current OCP users. Usage declined with age and increased with socioeconomic status. Higher use was also linked to Fars ethnicity, obesity, and multiparity. Lower use was associated with literacy and stillbirth history. OCP users tended to marry younger and had fewer postmenopausal years. OCP use was linked to a reduced risk of liver disease. other comorbidities showed no significant associations.ConclusionOCP use remains high among middle-aged Iranian women, influenced by age, SocioEconomic Status (SES), ethnicity, and reproductive history—unlike global trends post-menopause. Non-prescription availability and cultural norms may sustain misconceptions about contraceptive need, highlighting the need for targeted education and policy changes to support informed reproductive health decisions.ImplicationOCP use, as a method of contraception remains high among middle-aged Iranian women, driven by sociocultural factors and non-prescription access. Unlike global trends, the use of OCPs persists post-menopause, indicating gaps in reproductive health literacy, education, and policy reforms are needed necessary support informed contraceptive choices and address disparities in similar low-resource settings. Additionally, efforts to improve reproductive health literacy should include culturally tailored educational campaigns, pharmacist training to ensure informed dispensing of OCPs, and expanded community-based counseling services—especially for peri- and post-menopausal women.Keywords: Contraceptives, oral; Social determinants of health; Women's health; Middle aged; Iran

Introduction

Oral contraceptive pills (OCPs) are a cornerstone of modern family planning, with 58.7% of married or in-union women worldwide using modern contraceptive methods as of 2022 reported by World Health Organization (WHO) [1]. However, substantial regional disparities in OCP utilization persist, driven by differences in healthcare access, educational resources, and cultural attitudes [2].

In Iran, 18.4% of married women rely on OCP as their primary contraceptive method [3]. This widespread availability, facilitated by Iran’s decentralized primary healthcare system, has led to high OCP use in semi-urban areas like Fars Province, raising public health concerns about potential overuse among middle-aged and postmenopausal women, associated health risks (e.g., cardiovascular complications), and gaps in reproductive health literacy. Despite global research on OCP use, studies focusing on middle-aged women in such contexts remain limited.

Discontinuation is linked to side effects such as mood change (35.5%), nausea (16.2%), and pregnancy intentions (22%) [3]. In contrast, continuation is associated with prior OCP use, access to reliable information, satisfaction, employment, and literacy [3, 4]. Cultural perspectives in Iran are shaped by the religious endorsement of family planning within marriage, social acceptance juxtaposed with youth-related stigma, and a well-established Ministry of Health program that promotes modern contraceptives through education, counseling, and accessibility [2, 3].

Despite their widespread use, OCPs are associated with a range of risks, from common side effects—such as breakthrough bleeding [5], nausea, headaches [6], abdominal cramping, breast tenderness, and changes in vaginal discharge or libido [7, 8]—to severe complications, including diabetes [9], hypertension [10], stroke [11], myocardial infarction [12, 13], and breast and cervical cancers [14]. Women over the age of 35 who use high-dose formulations face increased risks of venous thrombosis [15, 16], pulmonary embolism, cholestasis, and gallbladder diseases [17]. Additionally, those aged 45 and older may experience a reduced life expectancy due to cardiovascular mortality [18]. Notably, in Iran, OCPs are often obtained without prescriptions [19], which contrasts with global norms that typically require medical oversight [20, 21].

At the time of data collection in 2012, OCPs were widely available without a prescription through Iran’s decentralized primary healthcare system and commercial pharmacies, contributing to high participation rates (approximately 95% of eligible adults). Since January 30, 2022, an executive order from the Ministry of Health has restricted contraceptive provision in public health centers to cases where pregnancy threatens a woman’s health; however, OCPs remain available over-the-counter in pharmacies without a prescription as of 2025 [22].

This study builds upon findings from the Pars Cohort Study [22], which revealed that more than half of women over the age of 40 in the region use hormonal contraceptives. It aims to examine the prevalence and patterns of oral contraceptive pill (OCP) use specifically for contraception, as well as the associated sociodemographic, reproductive, and comorbidity profiles among married middle-aged women in a semi-urban area of southern Iran where over-the-counter access remains widespread.”

By elucidating the interplay of sociodemographic, cultural, and health-related determinants, this study aims to descriptively and exploratively analyze the prevalence, patterns, and sociocultural determinants of OCP use, along with associated health outcomes, among married middle-aged women aged 45–64 years old in the PCS, a large-scale, population-based cohort in southern Iran.

Methodology

Study design

This study employed a secondary cross-sectional analysis of baseline data from the Pars Cohort Study (PCS), a population-based cohort established in 2012–2014 in Valashahr, Fars Province, Iran, to investigate non-communicable diseases among adults aged 40 and older [23]. The methodology of the PCS has been previously detailed elsewhere [23], and it enrolled approximately 95% of the eligible adult population residing in its catchment area.

Settings

The study was conducted in the Valashahr district of Fars Province, Iran, a semi-urban region comprising one small town and approximately 10–15 surrounding rural villages. This area was selected for the PCS, initiated in 2012, due to its representativeness of semi-urban populations in southern Iran. Iran’s decentralized primary healthcare system facilitated widespread access to OCPs without prescription until 2022, contributing to high participation rates (approximately 95% of eligible adults) in this cohort. Place of residence was not directly analyzed as a variable but was indirectly captured through the socioeconomic status (SES) index, which included residence size and housing ownership as indicators.

Participants

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study included middle-aged married women aged 45–64 years from the PCS, a prospective cohort study conducted in Valashahr, Fars Province, Iran, from 2012 to 2014. Exclusion criteria were single women (n = 120) and those with missing data on OCP use (n = 788), yielding a final sample of 4,034 participants. Moreover, menopausal type (natural, defined as spontaneous cessation of menses for ≥ 12 months without surgical intervention, vs. surgical, including unilateral/bilateral oophorectomy, hysterectomy, or both) was not an inclusion/exclusion criterion but was analyzed as a covariate to assess its association with OCP use.

Survey tool and variable definitions

Data were collected using a structured, nurse-administered questionnaire comprising five sections aligned with the study objectives:

1. Demographic Information:

This section covered age, ethnicity (classified as Fars or non-Fars), and literacy status (with individuals considered literate if they had attended at least elementary school, and illiterate if they had not). Socioeconomic status was calculated using multiple correspondence analysis (MCA) based on household assets, housing ownership, residence size, and the education level of the household head. Furthermore, SES was derived using an asset-based index constructed via multiple correspondence analysis (MCA), incorporating household amenities, housing ownership, residence size, and the education level of the household head. The SES was analyzed as a continuous variable.

2. Reproductive History:

Captured variables included parity, age at first marriage, menopausal status, and history of stillbirths or abortions. Menopausal status was classified as either:

• Natural menopause: Spontaneous cessation of menstruation for ≥ 12 months without surgical intervention, excluding any history of oophorectomy or hysterectomy.

• Surgical menopause: Resulting from procedures such as unilateral/bilateral oophorectomy, hysterectomy, or combined hysterectomy with oophorectomy.

3. Medical History:

Included nurse-verified chronic conditions—such as diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and liver disease—confirmed through both self-report and clinical records. Mental health status was assessed via physician diagnosis of depression or anxiety based on DSM-5 criteria (Mental health status was assessed by physician-diagnosed depression or anxiety based on DSM-5 criteria, ensuring standardized clinical diagnoses.

4. Behavioral Factors:

Assessed lifestyle-related variables including smoking, substance use (e.g., alcohol, opium, tobacco), physical activity levels, and body mass index (BMI), categorized according to WHO classifications.

5. OCP Use:

Focused on current OCP use, duration, and intent. To ensure validity, general physicians confirmed whether OCP use was for contraceptive purposes. Only participants with physician-verified contraceptive intent were included in the final analysis (see Supplementary File 1).

Data collection

All data were collected according to standardized protocols to minimize measurement error, recall bias, and misclassification. Interviews were conducted face-to-face by trained nurses, who also performed physical assessments using calibrated instruments and validated medical data through clinical record review. Socioeconomic status was operationalized using multiple correspondence analysis (MCA), a dimensionality reduction technique that generated continuous scores from categorical asset-based indicators.

OCP use was defined as the current, regular use of oral contraceptive pills in the weeks immediately preceding the interview, with physician confirmation of contraceptive purpose. Chronic diseases were recorded only when corroborated by both self-report and medical documentation, thereby enhancing diagnostic validity.

Statistical analysis and tools

Data analysis was conducted using STATA software. The prevalence of OCP use for contraception was calculated and expressed as a proportion with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to describe usage patterns across the study population. To evaluate associations between OCP use and independent variables, univariate analyses were performed using chi-squared tests. These independent variables included demographic characteristics, reproductive history, medical conditions, and behavioral factors. Variables with a p-value less than 0.05 in univariate analyses were retained for inclusion in the multivariable model. SES, one of the key exposures, was operationalized using MCA, a dimensionality reduction technique that converted categorical indicators—such as housing ownership, household amenities, and educational attainment—into a continuous SES index.

For multivariable analysis, Poisson regression with robust standard errors was employed to estimate adjusted prevalence ratios (PRs) and their corresponding 95% CIs. This approach was chosen for its suitability in handling cross-sectional binary outcomes and its capacity to produce interpretable effect estimates without the inflation risk associated with odds ratios. A backward elimination strategy was used to iteratively remove variables with p-values ≥ 0.05 from the full model, while retaining covariates of theoretical importance, such as SES, ethnicity, reproductive and menopausal indicators, chronic disease status, and behavioral characteristics.

To assess the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted by applying alternative model specifications and stratifying the sample by menopausal status. Missing data were addressed using multiple imputation for variables with less than 10% missingness under the assumption of missing at random. For variables with more than 20% missing data, complete case analysis was employed to minimize the risk of bias from potentially unreliable imputations.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. It represents a secondary cross-sectional analysis of baseline data derived from the Pars Cohort Study—a large, population-based cohort established to investigate non-communicable diseases in southern Iran (Fig. 1).

Demographic characteristics were strongly associated with OCP use. Younger women reported significantly higher usage, with prevalence decreasing from 68.8% among those aged 40–50 to 42.3% in women aged 60 and older. SES also played a notable role, with women in the high-SES group showing greater use (68.4%) compared to those in the low-SES group (53.2%). Interestingly, literate women were less likely to use OCPs (54.6%) than their illiterate counterparts (69.6%). Ethnicity was another significant factor, as Fars women reported higher usage (62.4%) compared to non-Fars women (57.7%). In terms of reproductive health, multiparity (having three or more pregnancies) was linked to higher OCP use (62.9%) relative to women with fewer than three pregnancies (34.8%). Conversely, women with a history of stillbirth were less likely to use OCPs (49.1% vs. 60.9%). The type of menopause experienced showed a minor difference: women with natural menopause reported slightly lower OCP use (51.1%) compared to those with surgical menopause (50.0%). No significant differences were found with respect to abortion history or menstrual regularity.

Behavioral and general health factors also influenced OCP use. Obese women had a significantly higher prevalence of use (70.7%) compared to non-obese women (59.0%), while the association with being overweight was weaker (63.5% vs. 59.5%). Other behavioral factors, including alcohol, cigarette, opium, and tobacco use, as well as self-reported depression and anxiety, showed no significant associations with OCP use.

Multivariable Poisson regression analysis revealed several significant predictors of OCP use, as detailed in Table 2. Compared to women aged 40–50, those aged 50–60 had a lower adjusted prevalence ratio (PR) of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.82–0.94), and women aged 60 and above had an even lower adjusted PR of 0.68 (95% CI: 0.60–0.78). High SES remained positively associated with use (adjusted PR: 1.10, 95% CI: 1.02–1.18), while literacy continued to show a negative association (adjusted PR: 0.90, 95% CI: 0.85–0.95). Fars ethnicity was associated with increased OCP use (adjusted PR: 1.06, 95% CI: 1.01–1.12).

Among reproductive health variables, multiparity increased the likelihood of OCP use, with an adjusted PR of 1.04 per additional pregnancy (95% CI: 1.02–1.05). In contrast, a history of stillbirth decreased OCP use (adjusted PR: 0.81, 95% CI: 0.69–0.94). Obesity remained a significant predictor (adjusted PR: 1.09, 95% CI: 1.02–1.16). Anxiety showed a marginal association (adjusted PR: 1.06, 95% CI: 1.00–1.12), and natural menopause was borderline associated with lower OCP use (adjusted PR: 0.94, 95% CI: 0.90–1.00). Variables that did not show significant associations in the adjusted models included low-middle and middle-high SES, menstrual regularity, substance use, overweight status, physical activity, and history of depression.

Regarding health outcomes, OCP use was inversely associated with liver disease (adjusted PR: 0.17, 95% CI: 0.03–0.85). Although not statistically significant, there was a trend toward increased prevalence of diabetes (adjusted PR: 1.19, 95% CI: 0.96–1.47) and cancer (adjusted PR: 1.43, 95% CI: 0.70–2.91); however, the precision of the cancer estimate was limited by a small number of cases. No significant associations were found for other health conditions such as heart disease or hypertension (Table 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the prevalence and sociocultural determinants of oral contraceptive pill (OCP) use among middle-aged Iranian women using data from the Pars Cohort Study. By identifying key factors associated with OCP use, we sought to inform public health strategies for improving reproductive health outcomes.

This study indicates that nearly two-thirds of middle-aged women (aged 45–64 years old), including those who are menopausal or nearing menopause, continue to utilize OCPs as their primary contraceptive method. Furthermore, our cohort exhibited a significantly high prevalence of OCP usage, approximately 66%, among women in this age group. This prevalence is 8.5 times higher than the 7.8% reported by Farias et al. (2016) in Brazil [23]. This notable discrepancy may be attributed to the over-the-counter availability of OCPs in Iran, in contrast to Brazil’s prescription-restricted access, which created systemic barriers that impeded the adoption of hormonal contraceptives. Additionally, in Brazil, female sterilization emerged as the predominant contraceptive method, accounting for 36.1% of usage, thereby displacing OCPs [23]. In contrast, our study context revealed minimal competition from alternative contraceptive methods, which likely contributed to the sustained preference for OCPs among women during the perimenopausal phase. While comparisons with countries like Brazil provide useful context, differences in health system structures, cultural beliefs, and contraceptive availability must be considered when interpreting cross-national differences.

The widespread use of OCPs among women in this study highlights the importance of understanding the factors driving this high prevalence. Our analysis identified key contributing factors across three domains: demographic characteristics (including age, socioeconomic status, education, and ethnicity), reproductive history (such as age at marriage, parity, abortion history, menstrual regularity, and menopausal status), and behavioral factors (including substance use, weight status, and mood disorders). These findings suggest that OCP use in this demographic is influenced by a complex interplay of social, biological, and behavioral determinants.

The analysis also examined the relationship between OCP use and various health outcomes, including cancer, diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular diseases. Although most associations were not statistically significant, in contrast to findings from other Iranian cohorts such as the Tabari Cohort Study which reported an association between OCP use and hypertension [24], the findings contribute to a broader understanding of the potential health implications associated with long-term contraceptive use.

Demographic variables critically influenced OCP utilization patterns, with age demonstrating an inverse association to OCP consumption consistent with established clinical concerns regarding cardiovascular and thrombotic risks in women over 35 years [13]. Contrary to prior literature, this study revealed a positive association between high SES and OCP use. While previous studies consistently link contraceptive practices to SES gradients [25, 26], the elevated OCP prevalence among high-SES women in our cohort reflects synergistic factors including enhanced healthcare access and affordability that mitigate financial and systemic barriers (e.g., prescription costs and clinic availability noted by Molloy et al. [25]), enabling consistent contraceptive procurement despite age-related restrictions while prioritizing preventive consultations addressing age-specific risks like lipid metabolism [27]. Furthermore, superior reproductive health literacy facilitates nuanced understanding of risk-benefit trade-offs (e.g., thrombosis versus endometrial protection emphasized by Seibert et al. [13]), with Ibrahim et al. [28] demonstrating that higher education predicts 3.2-fold greater accuracy in appraising OCP risks/benefits. Finally, greater cultural agency and method autonomy permit rejection of permanent methods (e.g., SES-driven sterilization patterns documented by Mosher & Jones [29]) in favor of OCPs through economic independence and collaborative physician decision-making [28].

Educational attainment also influences contraceptive behavior. While improved education may enhance awareness of reproductive options, this study found that literate women were less likely to use OCPs. Education, serving as a proxy for financial autonomy and social status, appears to empower women by providing a broader range of contraceptive choices and potentially fostering a preference for alternatives to OCPs [29, 30].

Ethnicity has emerged as a significant demographic factor influencing the use of OCPs. Although the relationship between ethnicity and contraceptive behavior is complex and may be mediated by SES, age, and health awareness [31], this study supports the idea that ethnicity plays a role in the observed patterns of OCP utilization.

In the realm of women’s reproductive characteristics, multiparity is positively associated with the use of OCPs. This finding is consistent with evidence from low- and middle-income countries, where the number of children often influences contraceptive behavior. Multiparous women are more likely to engage with healthcare providers, have greater familiarity with contraceptive methods, and seek to limit additional pregnancies for health or economic reasons [30].

Regarding reproductive history, a history of stillbirth was associated with reduced use of OCPs, while a history of abortion showed no significant association. In the Iranian context, where voluntary abortion is legally restricted, the use of OCPs may be more prevalent as a strategy to prevent unintended pregnancies and the associated legal risks. This observation aligns with global trends indicating that abortion laws influence contraceptive behavior, with restricted access leading to a greater reliance on preventive measures such as OCPs [32].

Menstrual regularity did not demonstrate a significant association with the use of OCPs in this study, which contrasts with findings from other research indicating that hormonal contraceptives are frequently utilized to manage or suppress menstruation. This discrepancy may arise from differences in population characteristics, the types of OCPs employed, or varying definitions of menstrual regularity. Additionally, cultural attitudes and regional norms regarding menstruation and contraception may also play a role in these divergent findings [33].

Marriage age is another variable of interest, as previous research has demonstrated that contraceptive choices are influenced by social and cultural attitudes toward marriage and childbearing [33]. Consequently, the reproductive life stage and the timing of marriage are important factors to consider when analyzing the use of OCPs.

Behavioral factors demonstrated a limited influence on the use of OCP in this study. Alcohol consumption did not seem to directly impact OCP usage; however, it is known to impair judgment and increase the risk of contraceptive failure [34]. Similarly, the use of substances such as cigarettes, opium, and tobacco was not significantly correlated with OCP consumption, although these behaviors may have independent health consequences [35].

Our study found that weight status did not significantly influence the prevalence of OCP use among middle-aged Iranian women in the PCS. This suggests that neither overweight nor obese women were less likely to use OCPs compared to normal-weight women. However, the literature highlights that obesity may compromise OCP efficacy, potentially increasing the risk of unintended pregnancies. Trussell et al. (2009) reported that women with a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m² have a higher risk of OCP failure compared to normal-weight women, with an adjusted odds ratio indicating increased contraceptive failure rates [36]. Similarly, Robinson and Burke (2013) reviewed evidence suggesting that obesity alters the pharmacokinetics of hormonal contraceptives, leading to lower circulating levels of active ingredients, which may reduce effectiveness [37]. These findings emphasize the importance of counseling obese women about potential reduced OCP efficacy and considering alternative contraceptive methods when appropriate. Our analysis found marginal or no associations between OCP use and mood disorders such as anxiety and depression, which aligns with findings from recent large-scale studies. For instance, Lundin et al. (2022), in a Swedish cohort of over 264,000 women, reported no association between combined oral contraceptive (COC) use and depression (hazard ratio ≈ 1.0) [38]. Similarly, Toffol et al. (2012) observed no adverse mental health effects from hormonal contraception in a nationally representative Finnish sample [39]. However, de Wit et al. (2020) found an association with depressive symptoms specifically among adolescent users, though not across all young age groups [40].

While most women do not experience significant mood disturbances from hormonal contraceptives, a minority—particularly those with pre-existing mood disorders—may be more susceptible (Mu & Kulkarni, 2022) [41]. These findings underscore the importance of individualized clinical monitoring, as psychological responses to hormonal contraception vary and a one-size-fits-all approach may be inadequate.

Overall, the behavioral variables examined in this study did not show significant associations with the use of OCP, reinforcing the notion that demographic and reproductive factors play a more substantial role in influencing contraceptive behavior within this population.

Study’s strengths

This study leverages the robust framework of the PCS, a large-scale, population-based cohort with a high participation rate (approximately 95% of eligible adults), ensuring representativeness of middle-aged women in a semi-urban Iranian context. The sample size of 4,034 married women aged 45–64 years provides substantial statistical power to detect associations. Data quality is enhanced by nurse-administered questionnaires, physician-verified OCP use for contraceptive intent, and nurse-verified medical histories, minimizing recall bias and misclassification. The use of Poisson regression with robust variance and multiple correspondence analysis (MCA) for SES offers methodological rigor, producing interpretable prevalence ratios for common outcomes. The study’s focus on middle-aged women, including perimenopausal and postmenopausal groups, addresses an understudied demographic, contributing novel insights into OCP use patterns in a setting with non-prescription access until 2022.

Limitations

The cross-sectional design of this secondary analysis precludes causal inferences and limits exploration of subjective drivers, such as personal motivations or cultural beliefs, underlying OCP use decisions. Data were collected from 2012 to 2014, before Iran’s 2022 policy restricting public health center contraceptive provision, potentially limiting generalizability to current contexts. The reliance on baseline PCS data restricts the ability to capture longitudinal changes in OCP use or health outcomes. Additionally, while missing data were addressed using multiple imputation (< 10% missingness) or complete case analysis (> 20% missingness), potential residual bias from missing data cannot be fully excluded. The study’s focus on a semi-urban Iranian population may limit external validity to urban or non-Iranian settings with different healthcare access patterns.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that factors such as age, socioeconomic status, educational attainment, ethnicity, multiparity, history of stillbirth, menopausal type, body weight, age at marriage, and time since menopause are associated with OCP use. Also, variables including abortion history, menstrual regularity, substance use, physical activity, and mental health conditions demonstrated no significant association with OCP utilization. These definitive findings illuminate specific demographic and clinical predictors of contraceptive behavior, establishing an evidence base from which targeted public health strategies should be developed to optimize safe contraceptive decision-making in this population.
