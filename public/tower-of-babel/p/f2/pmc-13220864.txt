DHX36 is a regulatory switch in the interferon-mediated antiviral response
Authors: Lisa Weixler, Daniel Hilbig, Philipp S. Simon, Stefan Juranek, Julia Mahlberg, Martin Schlee, Eva Bartok, Markus Hafner, Katrin Paeschke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Innate immune and stress responses must be tightly regulated to prevent aberrant activation in the absence of pathogens. The RNA helicase DHX36 has been implicated in viral RNA sensing, but its role in immune regulation is not fully understood. Here, we show that DHX36 functions as a rheostat that restrains immune activation under homeostatic conditions while modulating antiviral signaling. Exposure to double-stranded RNA reduces DHX36 activity, enabling immune activation. In contrast, cells lacking DHX36 adopt a constitutively activated immune state characterized by accumulation of RNA G-quadruplex structures, protein kinase R (PKR)–dependent stress granule formation, and elevated interferon-stimulated gene expression. These cells also display enhanced responsiveness to the viral RNA sensor retinoic acid–inducible gene I (RIG-I) and more effectively suppress replication of a yellow fever virus replicon. Together, our findings position DHX36 as a key regulator of the type I interferon response, linking RNA structure surveillance to coordinated PKR- and RIG-I–dependent antiviral signaling and maintenance of immune homeostasis.

INTRODUCTION

The innate immune system is the first line of defense against pathogens and is key to the recognition and elimination of malignantly transformed cells. One central axis of innate immune signaling is the type I interferon (IFN) response, which establishes an antiviral state. Although a functional IFN pathway is essential for host defense, excessive or dysregulated IFN signaling can promote autoinflammation and autoimmunity (1–3). This functional duality of IFN signaling highlights the need to further investigate the molecular mechanisms that regulate the induction, amplification, and termination of type I IFN signaling.

Pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) initiate type I IFN induction after sensing specific nonhost or altered host structures, known as pathogen-associated or damage-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs or DAMPs, respectively) (4, 5). Nucleic acid sensing of DAMPs and PAMPs is a core feature of antiviral immunity and is known to be mediated by the endosomal Toll-like receptors (TLRs), the cytosolic receptor 2′3′-cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) synthase (cGAS), and members of the retinoic acid–inducible gene I (RIG-I)–like receptor (RLR) family (6, 7). The RLRs are members of the DExD/H-box helicase family and play a central role in sensing viral double-stranded RNA (ds-RNA) in the cytosol (6–8). The two signaling RLRs, RIG-I and MDA5, initiate phosphorylation of interferon regulatory factors 3 and 7 (IRF3 and IRF7) upon activation, thereby inducing the expression of type I IFNs (9, 10). Secreted IFNs activate the JAK/STAT (Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription) signaling pathway, which, in turn, drives the transcription of numerous interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) with antiviral activity (11–13). Among these ISGs is the dsRNA-dependent protein kinase R (PKR), which becomes activated upon binding dsRNA or single-stranded RNA (ss-RNA) with base-paired secondary structures (11, 12, 14). Upon activation, PKR dimerizes and autophosphorylates, leading to the phosphorylation of eukaryotic initiation factor-2α (eIF2α), an event that results in the global inhibition of canonical translation and promotes stress granule (SG) formation (15–17) (Fig. 1A).

Previous studies show that several other DExD/H-box helicase family members support antiviral signaling or even act directly as PRRs (18–20). In particular, our work and studies by others implicate the adenosine triphosphate (ATP)–dependent RNA helicase DHX36 (also known as RHAU, G4R1) in DAMP/PAMP recognition and the regulation of PKR activity (20–22). Knockout (KO) of DHX36 in mice has been shown to be embryonically lethal, indicating an essential role for DHX36 in early development (23, 24). In addition, we have previously shown that DHX36 KO in human embryonic kidney (HEK) cells induces SG formation and leads to the accumulation of endogenous DHX36 target RNAs, which are enriched in specific secondary structures known as RNA G-quadruplexes (rG4s) (22). Like other DExD/H-box helicases, DHX36 contains two RecA-like domains within its catalytic core that mediate nucleic acid unwinding via a conserved, ATP-dependent mechanism, enabling the resolution of G4 structures (25–27). rG4s are widespread in both mammalian and viral transcriptomes. Their recognition and unwinding are critical for proper RNA processing (28, 29), and unresolved rG4s can cause translational arrest, trigger SG formation, and activate the PKR-eIF2α-ATF4 pathway (22, 30, 31). Moreover, many human viruses also contain functional G4 motifs, and stabilizing these structures can impair viral replication, rendering viral G4s potential antiviral targets (32, 33). Multiple studies associate dysregulated G4 unwinding with various cancer types (34, 35), underscoring their broader relevance in health and disease.

Because the biological consequences of rG4 accumulation and its mechanistic connection to the enhanced stress response in DHX36 KO cells remain unclear, we investigated the role of DHX36 in the cellular response to viral stress. We demonstrate that viral stimuli intrinsically down-regulate DHX36 activity required for rG4 resolution, potentially serving as a prerequisite for an enhanced IFN response. Loss of DHX36 in unstimulated cells induces a basally immune-primed state, characterized by SG formation and driven by increased PKR activation, type I IFN induction, and ISG expression. Moreover, cells lacking DHX36 helicase activity exhibit heightened responses to viral stimuli in a RIG-I–dependent manner. Consistent with this enhanced antiviral state, DHX36-deficient cells displayed reduced yellow fever virus (YFV) replication in replicon assays. Together, these findings support a model in which DHX36 acts as a regulatory switch that is attenuated upon viral stress, thereby enabling robust antiviral responses through the coordinated activation of the PKR and RIG-I pathways.

RESULTS

DHX36 deficiency induces SG formation and activates PKR

Cells respond to stress through various mechanisms, from activating survival pathways to initiating programmed cell death (36). In our previous work, we demonstrated that the absence of DHX36 leads to elevated rG4 levels, mRNA accumulation, and inherent cellular stress, characterized by PKR phosphorylation and SG formation (22). However, the origin, nature, and consequences of this stress phenotype in DHX36 KO cells remain unclear. To investigate this, we analyzed the cellular stress response in CRISPR-Cas9–generated DHX36 KO HEK293 cells. As expected from our previous findings (22), DHX36 KO cells exhibited significantly elevated SG levels (Fig. 1B), detected by immunofluorescence (IF) using the SG marker protein G3BP1 (37).

SGs form in response to different cellular triggers. One of those is the stress-induced phosphorylation of eIF2α (38, 39). Therefore, we assessed the phosphorylation levels of both eIF2α as well as of PKR, the key kinase responsible for eIF2α phosphorylation upon viral sensing (15, 38). PKR phosphorylation is a sign of PKR activation and a hallmark of the antiviral stress response, which is typically induced upon binding to foreign dsRNA. As an ISG, PKR expression is also commonly up-regulated during viral infection (40, 41). Western blot analysis revealed increased phosphorylation of both eIF2α and PKR in DHX36 KO cells (Fig. 1, C and D, and fig. S1, A and B). However, total PKR protein levels were not elevated (fig. S1, A and C), suggesting that the increased PKR activation in DHX36-deficient cells occurs independently of transcriptional up-regulation. To evaluate whether this enhanced PKR activation directly contributes to the SG phenotype observed in DHX36 KO cells, stable PKR KO and DHX36/PKR double KO cell lines were generated in HEK293 cells (fig. S1D). All subsequent experiments were performed using at least three independent clones of each cell line to minimize potential bias from clonal variation. We performed IF staining against G3BP1 to evaluate the prevalence of SG foci and monitored eIF2α phosphorylation by Western blot. Accumulation of SGs in DHX36 KO cells was fully abolished by parallel KO of PKR (Fig. 1E and fig. S1E). In line with this, the increased eIF2α phosphorylation levels detected in DHX36 KO cells reverted to wild-type (WT) levels in DHX36/PKR KO cells (Fig. 1F), indicating that the formation of SGs in DHX36 KO cells is induced by increased PKR activation.

In addition, we observed that DHX36 KO cells exhibited faster growth and more rapid medium acidification, than WT cells, indicating increased metabolic activity. To quantify the survival and viability of DHX36 KO cells, we performed colony formation assays (CFAs) (42), along with temporal growth profiling and apoptosis analyses by live-cell imaging and the apoptosis marker annexin V. CFAs revealed a higher survival rate of DHX36 KO cells under unchallenged conditions, as evidenced by an increased number of colonies relative to WT cells (Fig. 1G and fig. S1F). Consistently, DHX36 KO cells showed elevated proliferation rates (Fig. 1H) as well as lower levels of apoptotic and dead cells compared to WT cells (Fig. 1I and fig. S1G). To assess whether the increased medium consumption observed in DHX36 KO cells may also be associated with increased mitochondrial abundance, we measured mitochondrial content by quantifying the expression of mitochondrial genes via quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and by IF following MitoTracker staining, which marks metabolically active mitochondria. Both qPCR analysis of COX1, CYTB, ND1, and ND6 (fig. S1H), as well as quantification of MitoTracker signal intensity (fig. S1I), revealed increased mitochondrial content in DHX36 KO cells. Given the role of the mitochondria in metabolism and survival signaling, the increased mitochondrial content in DHX36 KO cells coincided with enhanced metabolic activity and proliferation. Because SGs transiently repress mitochondrial and metabolic mRNA translation, the increased mitochondrial content in DHX36 KO cells may represent a compensatory adaptation to counteract SG-mediated translational inhibition under basal conditions.

We wondered whether the increased cell viability and proliferation capacity observed in DHX36 KO cells, together with elevated SG formation and enhanced PKR and eIF2α phosphorylation, may reflect a state of baseline immune activation—an “immune priming” effect—that would allow DHX36-deficient cells to respond more rapidly and effectively to virus-induced stress. Immune priming is described as a state in which an initial stimulus, in this case, DHX36 deficiency, induces a persistent immune response that does not fully return to baseline before a secondary stimulation or infection. As a result, primed cells typically mount a stronger response to an additional stimulus than naïve cells (43). To test this hypothesis, we challenged both WT and DHX36 KO cells with polyinosinic–polycytidylic acid [poly(I:C)], a synthetic dsRNA analog that induces a PRR-dependent antiviral stress response (44, 45), and analyzed the changes in SG abundance by IF in a time course experiment. We found that DHX36 KO cells exhibited elevated SG levels under basal conditions (Fig. 1B), whereas poly(I:C) treatment led to only a mild, nonsignificant increase in SG formation compared to WT cells (Fig. 1J and fig. S1J). We assumed that this comparable SG formation in poly(I:C)-challenged DHX36 KO cells correlates with similar levels of PKR and eIF2α phosphorylation. Unexpectedly, DHX36 deficiency resulted in a fivefold increase in PKR phosphorylation 24 hours after treatment with poly(I:C) (Fig. 1K and fig. S1K), whereas eIF2α phosphorylation was similarly elevated in both WT and DHX36 KO cells (Fig. 1L and fig. S1B).

These data indicate that SG formation in DHX36-deficient cells under basal conditions is driven by mild eIF2α activation, likely as a result of PKR activation. In contrast, the increased phosphorylation of PKR following poly(I:C) treatment does not lead to elevated eIF2α phosphorylation and consequently does not enhance SG formation. This suggests that, although DHX36 KO cells retain the molecular machinery necessary to activate the integrated stress response, alternative cellular mechanisms may prevent strong PKR-induced SG assembly in the absence of DHX36.

Loss of DHX36 primes the type I IFN immune response and enhances poly(I:C) responsiveness

Given that loss of DHX36 activates PKR, a key mediator of viral sensing and innate immunity, we hypothesized that DHX36 deficiency may induce broader immune activation. We quantified the expression of immune-related genes in DHX36 KO and WT cells by qPCR, revealing up-regulation of selected genes, including IFNB1, IRF7, and ADAR1, in DHX36 KO cells under basal conditions (fig. S2, A to C). To assess the impact of DHX36 loss on the transcriptional landscape of ISGs, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) experiments on WT and DHX36 KO cells, treated with poly(I:C) for 24 hours. A total of 1412 genes [Log Fold Change(LFC) > 0.75], including several within the JAK/STAT signaling pathway and numerous ISGs, were significantly up-regulated in DHX36 KO cells. Detailed analysis identified altered expression of several IFNs and other key components of the innate immune pathways, such as STATs, JAKs, IFNARs, IFITs, IFNB1, PKR, and RIG-I in DHX36 KO cells (Fig. 2A). To substantiate the impact of DHX36 on the type I IFN response, we measured IFN secretion following poly(I:C) treatment and observed a significant ~5-fold increase in DHX36 KO cells compared to WT cells (Fig. 2B). To quantify the dynamic regulation of ISG expression in response to poly(I:C), we measured transcript levels of IFNB1, IFIT1, RIG-I, and RSAD2 in WT and DHX36 KO cells over a 24-hour time course. All tested ISGs exhibited time-dependent up-regulation following poly(I:C) stimulation in both genotypes. Although the onset of transcriptional activation was comparable between WT and DHX36 KO cells, the overall amplification was consistently stronger in DHX36 KO cells (Fig. 2, C to F). Twenty-four hours post–poly(I:C) treatment, DHX36 KO cells showed up to fourfold higher expression of the respective ISGs compared to WT cells (Fig. 2, C to F). These findings were supported at the protein level, as exemplified by increased expression of RIG-I and IFIT1 in DHX36 KO cells (fig. S2, D to F).

The primed type I IFN response observed in unchallenged DHX36 KO cells, together with the elevated ISG levels following poly(I:C) treatment, led us to hypothesize that alterations in DHX36 activity may represent an additional layer of regulation in the antiviral immune response, acting through an as-yet undefined mechanism.

We previously demonstrated that G4-containing mRNA targets of DHX36 accumulate in the cytoplasm of DHX36 KO cells and remain translationally incompetent (22). Accordingly, we wondered whether such unresolved rG4 structures may act as ligands for cytoplasmic PRR activation, thereby contributing to constitutive activation of the innate immune system in DHX36 KO cells. To determine whether the type I IFN response observed in DHX36 KO cells results from the general accumulation of rG4 structures, we promoted rG4 formation in WT and DHX36 KO cells with the RNA-specific rG4-stabilizing agent carboxypyridostatin (cPDS) (46, 47). We first assessed whether rG4 stabilization by cPDS induced PKR activation. In WT cells, cPDS treatment modestly increased PKR phosphorylation compared to untreated controls, suggesting that global rG4 stabilization by cPDS can partially activate PKR. In contrast, no additional effect was observed in DHX36 KO cells, likely due to constitutive stabilization of DHX36 target rG4s in the absence of DHX36 (fig. S2G).

We next asked whether the elevated ISG expression observed in DHX36 KO cells (fig. S2, A to C) resulted from rG4 accumulation. ISG levels in cPDS-treated WT cells were assessed by qPCR, revealing a slight up-regulation of selected ISGs (fig. S2H). Whether this reflects direct rG4 stabilization within the affected transcripts or is due to activation of the IFN signaling pathway by accumulating rG4 structures remains to be elucidated.

Although cPDS broadly stabilizes rG4 structures, DHX36 preferentially targets rG4-forming sequences located in 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions (UTRs) of specific mRNAs (22). Although the changes in ISG expression upon cPDS treatment were not statistically significant, the observed trend mirrored the increased ISG expression seen in DHX36 KO cells. This suggests that cPDS-stabilized rG4s do not fully overlap with the DHX36 target repertoire but supports the notion that rG4 accumulation contributes to the induction of the IFN response. This trend was substantiated by 3′ RNA-seq data, which showed a modest increase in the expression of certain IFN-related genes in cPDS-treated WT cells (fig. S1I), whereas no expression changes were observed in similarly treated DHX36 KO cells (fig. S1J).

DHX36 is critical for maintaining RNA homeostasis by unwinding specific rG4 structures to ensure proper mRNA processing, localization, and translation. Loss of its helicase activity leads to the cytoplasmic accumulation of mRNA targets with unresolved rG4s, which may disrupt RNA metabolism and contribute to immune activation by impairing translation, promoting mislocalization, or interfering with SG dynamics or dysregulated stress responses (22, 29). We therefore hypothesized that, if immune activation in DHX36 KO cells resulted from the accumulation of DHX36 targets, and consequently specific rG4s, DHX36 activity must also be suppressed in WT cells during viral mimic stimulation. To test this, we compared RNA-seq data from untreated and poly(I:C)-treated WT cells, focusing on DHX36 target mRNAs previously identified by PAR-CLIP (22). Poly(I:C) treatment in WT cells resulted in increased levels of DHX36 target mRNAs (Fig. 2G), closely resembling the transcriptional profile of DHX36 KO cells (Fig. 2H). Moreover, the expression profile of DHX36 targets in DHX36 KO cells was nearly identical to that observed in poly(I:C)-treated WT cells (Fig. 2I).

Collectively, these findings suggest that immune activation in DHX36-deficient cells is primarily driven by the accumulation of unresolved DHX36 rG4 targets, rather than by a global increase in rG4 stability. This response may reflect an infection-induced functional inhibition of DHX36 helicase activity, which could act as a regulatory switch within the host antiviral defense.

DHX36 helicase activity promotes viral replication but is functionally inactivated by viral mimic–induced stress

On the basis of the accumulation of DHX36 targets after poly(I:C) treatment and the trend of IFN activation after rG4-stabilizing cPDS treatment, we speculated that HEK293 cells may have an inherent mechanism to suppress DHX36 in response to viral stress, enabling full activation of the virus-induced stress response. We hypothesized that DHX36 is either down-regulated or functionally inactivated upon viral stimulation. To test this hypothesis, we analyzed DHX36 transcript and protein levels in WT cells after poly(I:C) treatment. Although transcript levels showed a slight decrease (fig. S3A), protein levels remained stable over 24 hours (fig. S3, B and C), indicating no significant change in DHX36 expression or stability. Given that subcellular localization is a common mechanism for regulating protein activity (48), we also examined DHX36 cellular distribution but found no change in its cytoplasmic or nuclear levels posttreatment (fig. S3D).

To further investigate whether DHX36 helicase activity contributes to the enhanced type I IFN response, we transiently overexpressed WT DHX36 (DHX36 OE) and the catalytically inactive mutant DHX36-E335A (DHX36_EA) (49, 50) in DHX36 KO cells, as previously described (22). In the DHX36_EA mutant, helicase ATPase activity is disrupted by substitution of the glutamic acid (E) at position 335 within the RecA1 domain with alanine (A). qPCR analysis of selected ISGs, 24 hours after poly(I:C) treatment, revealed that DHX36 OE reduced ISG induction in DHX36 KO cells to WT levels or even lower (Fig. 3A), whereas expression of the DHX36_EA mutant resulted in IFIT1, RIG-I, and RSAD2 transcript levels comparable to those in DHX36 KO cells (Fig. 3B). These findings indicate that DHX36’s catalytic activity suppresses immune activation during homeostasis and that its helicase function is altered or inhibited in response to poly(I:C) treatment. To test whether the observed effect is a direct consequence of DHX36 inactivity rather than a coincidental or secondary effect of poly(I:C) treatment, we treated WT and DHX36 KO cells with the transcription inhibitor actinomycin D (AMD) in parallel with poly(I:C) stimulation and assessed the stability of the known DHX36 targets WAC and PURB. We previously showed that WAC and PURB are stabilized in nonstimulated DHX36 KO cells (22). If DHX36 is functionally inactivated upon poly(I:C) treatment, target mRNA stability is expected to be comparable between WT and KO cells. No differences were observed (fig. S3E), supporting the conclusion that the DHX36 KO–like phenotype induced by poly(I:C) reflects catalytic inactivation of DHX36 rather than a poly(I:C)-induced secondary mechanism.

Viral G4s have been shown to affect critical stages of the viral life cycle, and G4-stabilizing ligands have exhibited potent antiviral effects against different viruses including YFV (51, 52). To assess whether an effect on viral replication can be observed in DHX36 KO cells, we used a previously published YFV replicon system, which allows monitoring of viral replication through a Renilla luciferase (RLuc) reporter (51). DHX36 KO cells showed an ~2-fold decrease in YFV replicon levels 24 hours posttransfection compared to WT cells (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, transient overexpression of DHX36 increased the replicon replication threefold within the same period (Fig. 3C), consistent with the notion that DHX36 activity may be hijacked during infection to promote viral translation by resolving viral rG4 structures. On the basis of these findings, we tested whether loss of DHX36 directly mediated the observed effect on YFV replication rather than acting indirectly through secondary factors such as the primed immune state of DHX36 KO cells. To address this, we performed thermal shift assays (TSAs) using recombinantly purified DHX36. Direct binding of a ligand such as YFV replicon RNA or poly(I:C) to DHX36 is expected to result in a shift in its thermal stability. DHX36 bound to the replicon RNA, resulting in a significant thermal shift (ΔTmelt) of 4°C, whereas only a minor, nonsignificant shift of 1°C could be observed in the presence of poly(I:C) (Fig. 3D and fig. S3, F and G). An electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) of DHX36 and the YFV replicon confirmed the direct interaction of DHX36 and the viral replicon RNA (Fig. 3E).

These results indicate that loss of DHX36 activity contributes to increased antiviral activity, as reflected by reduced YFV replication during the early phase of infection in DHX36 KO cells, most likely due to loss of DHX36 helicase function, through an as-yet unidentified mechanism.

Given these findings, along with DHX36’s differential expression in certain cancers and previous suggestions of its immune-regulatory role (53, 54), we propose a dual role for DHX36 in immune regulation. On the one hand, its catalytic activity suppresses immune activation during homeostasis by preventing the accumulation of G4-containing RNAs in the cytoplasm but becomes functionally inactivated upon viral infection to enhance the antiviral response. On the other hand, DHX36 helicase activity may be inactivated to prevent the promotion of viral replication through the resolution of viral G4 structures. The primed immune state and stronger type I IFN response after poly(I:C) treatment further support the hypothesis that DHX36 functions as a negative regulator of type I IFN signaling. This regulatory function may allow fine-tuning of the antiviral response during infection while contributing to immune homeostasis and the prevention of autoimmunity under nonstimulated conditions.

DHX36 interacts with PKR but has limited impact on NF-κB signaling

Assuming that DHX36 helicase activity suppresses immune activation, we next sought to elucidate the mechanisms by which it inhibits type I IFN signaling. Given that the increased SG formation observed in DHX36 KO cells is likely driven by PKR activation (Fig. 1), and considering previous reports suggesting a physical interaction between PKR and DHX36 (55), we first focused on the impact of DHX36 deficiency on PKR activation and tested whether the interaction between DHX36 and PKR is dependent on the activation of PKR. Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) of N-terminally FLAG-tagged DHX36 from HEK293 cells revealed that DHX36 interacts with both PKR and phospho-PKR in an RNA-dependent manner, independently of PKR activation status (Fig. 4A and fig. S4A). Notably, this RNA-mediated complex formation occurred under both basal and poly(I:C)-stimulated conditions, suggesting that it is driven by endogenous RNA and is not a consequence of infection.

One downstream effect of PKR activation is the induction of the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) pathway (56, 57), which drives the expression of various cytokines and pro-inflammatory genes during the innate immune response. Activation of this pathway can occur through phosphorylation of its subunit p65 (56, 58–60), which can be triggered by multiple stimuli, including viral infection and ultraviolet (UV) light irradiation (61). In cancer cells, DHX36 has also been reported to activate NF-κB (21, 62). To assess whether DHX36 KO influences NF-κB signaling in our model, we exposed WT and DHX36 KO cells to UV irradiation and analyzed p65 phosphorylation levels over time. Despite elevated PKR activation in DHX36 KO cells (Fig. 1, C and K), p65 phosphorylation was similarly induced in both WT and DHX36 KO cells following irradiation or poly(I:C) treatment, with a slight trend toward reduced phosphorylation in poly(I:C)-treated DHX36 KO cells (Fig. 4, B and C, and fig. S4B). Consistent with this, expression of the NF-κB–dependent cytokines TNFα and IL8 increased upon poly(I:C) stimulation in both genotypes, although DHX36 KO cells showed a modest tendency toward lower expression under both basal and stimulated conditions (Fig. 4, D and E). In contrast, IL6 expression, another NF-κB target, was not substantially affected by poly(I:C) in either cell type (Fig. 4F).

Collectively, these findings suggest that, although there may be a slight—not statistically significant—reduction in NF-κB pathway activation in DHX36 KO cells, the pathway remains functionally intact. Robust induction of p65 phosphorylation by both UV irradiation and poly(I:C) treatment supports this conclusion. Thus, the observed type I IFN priming phenotype and enhanced IFNAR signaling in DHX36 KO cells are unlikely to result from differential NF-κB pathway activation.

DHX36 suppresses RIG-I and PKR activation

Having excluded differential PKR-DHX36 complex formation and NF-κB pathway activation as drivers of the DHX36 loss-of-function immune-priming phenotype, we turned our attention to the cytosolic viral RNA sensor RIG-I. A previous study has shown that DHX36 associates with RIG-I (55), and PKR has been reported to potentiate or modulate RIG-I signaling, whereas RIG-I activates type I IFN signaling and induces PKR expression as part of the broader antiviral program. This reciprocal regulation has led to the hypothesis that PKR and RIG-I form a feedback loop to fine-tune the antiviral immune response (63, 64). We therefore investigated DHX36’s potential regulatory influence on both PKR and RIG-I.

To this end, we generated stable RIG-I KO and DHX36/RIG-I double KO cell lines in HEK293 cells using the LentiCRISPRv2 system (fig. S5, A and B). All experiments were performed using at least three independent clones of each cell line to minimize potential biases arising from clonal effects. Given that DHX36 KO cells exhibited increased type I IFN release upon poly(I:C) treatment (Fig. 2B), we compared IFNB1 expression levels across all our cell lines following poly(I:C) stimulation to determine whether this effect is directly mediated by DHX36 loss or influenced by cross-talk with PKR or RIG-I. qPCR analysis revealed that IFNB1 expression was significantly elevated in both DHX36 KO and DHX36/PKR double KO cells under unstimulated conditions compared to WT cells, whereas no effect was observed in RIG-I or DHX36/RIG-I KO cells (fig. S5C). Upon poly(I:C) treatment, IFNB1 expression further increased in DHX36 KO cells and was even more pronounced in DHX36/PKR KO cells. In contrast, RIG-I single KO and DHX36/RIG-I double KO cells exhibited a near-complete loss of IFNB1 induction following poly(I:C) stimulation (Fig. 5A). These results highlight the requirement of RIG-I in the expression of IFN-β, both during loss of DHX36 and after poly(I:C) stimulation. Moreover, the elevated IFNB1 levels observed in DHX36/PKR KO cells compared to DHX36 KO cells suggest a DHX36-dependent, negative regulatory role of PKR in modulating RIG-I–mediated IFN-β induction. This is consistent with previous findings showing that PKR negatively regulates RIG-I–driven IFN-β expression (64).

To further investigate whether and how down-regulation of DHX36 activity influences the RIG-I– and PKR-related type I IFN response, and to pinpoint where DHX36 acts within the IFN signaling cascade, we measured the expression levels of selected ISGs we had found to be affected by DHX36 KO. ISG expression was assessed under basal conditions, after 24 hours of poly(I:C) treatment, and following 6 hours of IFN-α stimulation. Whereas poly(I:C) is expected to activate the full IFN signaling cascade, including upstream sensors such as RIG-I and PKR, IFN-α stimulation selectively activates the pathway downstream of IFNAR signaling, without activating RIG-I or PKR (65) (Fig. 5B).

qPCR analysis revealed elevated expression of the type I IFN/IFNAR signaling–associated ISGs IFIT1, RIG-I, ADAR1, and IRF7 in DHX36 KO cells following poly(I:C) treatment (Fig. 5, C to F). A similar trend was observed under unstimulated conditions, although basal expression levels of these genes remained extremely low in the absence of an immune trigger (Fig. 3, A to C, and fig. S5, D to G). Reminiscent of the observed effect on IFNB1 induction in Fig. 5A, the poly(I:C)-induced up-regulation of these ISGs was completely abrogated in both RIG-I KO and DHX36/RIG-I double KO cells, with the exception of ADAR1, whose expression levels remained comparable to those of WT cells (Fig. 5, C to F).

In contrast to the effect of RIG-I KO, parallel PKR KO did not abrogate the expression of the selected DHX36 KO–sensitive ISGs but instead restored their levels to those observed in WT cells, with the exception of IRF7, which remained strongly induced in DHX36/PKR KO cells (Fig. 5, C to F). Although general changes in ISG expression were observed following IFN-α treatment in the different KO cell lines compared to untreated conditions, no clear DHX36-dependent alteration was detected (fig. S5, H to K). We therefore conclude that DHX36 functions in the early antiviral immune response, acting upstream of the IFNAR signaling pathway. This supports the hypothesis that rG4 accumulation may trigger the observed type I IFN response. Moreover, our data suggest that the type I IFN response observed in DHX36 KO cells is likely driven by accelerated RIG-I activation—rather than by a direct effect of PKR—either as a secondary consequence of DHX36 loss, such as rG4 accumulation, or through regulation of RIG-I by PKR or DHX36 itself.

Together, our findings support a model in which DHX36 functions as a regulatory switch in the IFN-mediated antiviral response. Under homeostatic conditions, DHX36 resolves G4-containing RNAs in the cytoplasm, thereby preventing aberrant activation of RIG-I and PKR and contributing to immune homeostasis. Upon viral infection or stimulation with viral mimics, DHX36 becomes functionally inactivated, leading to the accumulation of unresolved G4 structures. These G4s promote RIG-I and PKR activation, resulting in eIF2α phosphorylation and induction of type I IFN signaling. In viral RNAs, unresolved G4 structures may additionally reduce translation efficiency (Fig. 6). This dual role positions DHX36 as both a suppressor of immune activation under basal conditions and an enhancer of antiviral signaling and defense during infection.

DISCUSSION

The viral stress response is a key determinant of infection outcome, governing whether viral replication is efficiently restricted or sustained. By analyzing the mechanisms that coordinate the activation and regulation of this response, our findings advance our understanding of how the helicase DHX36 engages cellular stress pathways, shapes innate immune activation, and influences homeostatic control.

We propose a model in which DHX36 fulfills two distinct functions within innate immunity: (i) acting as a suppressor of immune activation under homeostatic conditions and (ii) serving as a regulatory switch that, upon inactivation during viral infection, promotes antiviral signaling and limits viral replication by allowing the accumulation of unresolved rG4 structures in both cellular and viral RNAs (Fig. 6). This model provides a mechanistic framework for understanding how RNA structure, innate immunity, and viral resistance are intricately linked through helicase activity. Various DExD/H-box helicases have been shown to contribute to antiviral signaling or function directly as PRRs, indicating that innate RNA sensing is more widespread than previously assumed (18–20). Such diversity could allow for cell type– and ligand-specific tuning, introduce functional redundancy to counter viral antagonism, and uncover additional regulatory nodes, offering new opportunities to intrinsically or therapeutically fine-tune the IFN responses. Thereby, DHX36 helicase activity may contribute to the dynamic regulation of the innate immune response by preventing inappropriate activation of type I IFN signaling, which could otherwise lead to chronic inflammation or autoimmunity.

In the absence of DHX36, cells mount a robust stress response, marked by PKR and eIF2α activation and subsequent SG formation, without an external stressor (Fig. 1). SGs are dynamic cytoplasmic assemblies of stalled mRNAs and RNA binding proteins that form upon translational arrest in response to antiviral stress (66). DHX36 primarily binds to mRNAs with a high potential to form rG4s (22), and it has been proposed that G4s accumulate in SGs (30, 67), with both DHX36 and its target mRNAs found to be enriched in these compartments (22, 55). However, a recent study in human osteosarcoma cells reported that DHX36 depletion promotes SG assembly and delays their disassembly, independently of G4 content (68). Moreover, loss of DHX36 was shown to restore SG-like foci in cells otherwise unable to form SGs due to deletion of the key SG core proteins G3BP1/2 (68). These findings suggest that DHX36 normally limits SG assembly, potentially by unwinding rG4 structures or disrupting RNA-RNA interactions (e.g., trans-rG4s) that would otherwise promote condensate formation. Such a function points to a more nuanced role for DHX36 in RNA homeostasis: Rather than solely resolving rG4s to support translation or RNA processing, DHX36 may regulate the phase behavior of cellular ribonucleoproteins by restricting RNA-RNA interactions that drive condensate formation. This aligns well with our observation that DHX36 KO promotes SG formation under nonstimulated conditions (Fig. 1) and supports our hypothesis that, upon viral stimulation, DHX36 becomes functionally inactivated (Figs. 2 and 3), triggering a robust stress response potentially driven by cytosolic RNA accumulation (Fig. 2) and activation of PKR and RIG-I (Fig. 5). Furthermore, it can be speculated that DHX36 translocates to SGs or other liquid-liquid phase-separated (LLPS) structures as part of a regulatory mechanism: Upon viral infection, its helicase activity is inhibited, reducing target specificity and impairing G4 resolution, thus allowing RNAs to accumulate in the cytoplasm, where they may act as endogenous ligands for PRRs such as PKR and RIG-I—priming the innate immune response and promoting SG formation. Under basal conditions, DHX36 may dynamically shield its target RNAs, limiting their cytosolic exposure and preventing their accumulation in LLPS structures.

rG4 accumulation likely contributes to both the elevated SG formation and enhanced type I IFN response observed in unchallenged DHX36 KO cells (fig. S2). This interpretation is supported by the observation that treatment of WT cells with the rG4-stabilizing ligand cPDS induced modest PKR phosphorylation and mild ISG expression (fig. S2). Notably, cPDS functions as a broad-spectrum rG4 stabilizer, promoting the formation of multiple G4s in a nonspecific manner. In contrast, DHX36 depletion results in the accumulation of its physiological, rG4-containing mRNA targets (Fig. 2). This implies that type I IFN induction may depend on a distinct subset of rG4 structures, those normally targeted by DHX36, rather than on generalized G4 accumulation. However, the response of cPDS-treated WT cells was markedly weaker than that observed in DHX36-deficient cells (Fig. 1), indicating that cPDS induced rG4 stabilization is not fully sufficient to mimic the consequences of DHX36 loss. Different explanations could explain this observation. One potential scenario is that residual DHX36 activity in cPDS-treated cells attenuates the response. Although cPDS binding is largely structure dependent rather than sequence specific (47), its binding specificity in cells remains poorly defined. Thus, rG4 structures stabilized by cPDS may not completely overlap with endogenous DHX36 targets. In addition, several studies have shown that small-molecule G4 ligands such as cPDS or PDS can alter G4-protein interactions and disrupt the binding dynamics of endogenous G4-binding proteins (69–71). If RIG-I and PKR function as G4-binding proteins, such interference could account for the comparatively weaker immune response observed in cPDS-treated WT cells relative to DHX36 KO cells.

One well-known example of an immune-regulating nucleic acid signature is A-to-I RNA editing, the enzymatic conversion of adenosine to inosine in dsRNA, catalyzed by the ISG ADAR1. This modification prevents aberrant activation of PRRs. When ADAR1 is absent, unedited dsRNA accumulates, triggering spontaneous type I IFN induction (72). ADAR1 is known to negatively regulate the activation of both RIG-I and PKR, for example, by competing for or editing their target RNAs (73), and has also been shown to bind and interact with G4s (74, 75). By analogy, DHX36 may provide an equivalent safeguard by helicase-driven resolution of rG4-structured RNAs, thereby reducing the pool of endogenous ligands available to RIG-I and PKR, and consequently restraining the type I IFN response. Nevertheless, it should be noted that both the mild immune priming and the subtle SG formation observed in nonstimulated DHX36 KO cells may reflect a broader consequence of disrupted RNA homeostasis rather than a DHX36-specific effect. Loss of any major RNA binding protein could be sufficient to perturb RNA dynamics and induce low-level SG formation. Moreover, SG levels remain very low under nonstimulated conditions, suggesting that their biological relevance in this context may be limited.

We demonstrate that elevated ISG expression upon poly(I:C) stimulation depends on DHX36 helicase activity as overexpression of a catalytically inactive mutant fails to revert the immune-activated state (Fig. 3). Consistently, poly(I:C) treatment of WT cells led to the accumulation of DHX36 mRNA targets, resembling the transcriptomic profile of DHX36 KO cells (Fig. 2). These findings suggest that poly(I:C) exposure may functionally inactivate DHX36 helicase activity and that such inactivation could be a prerequisite for robust type I IFN induction.

The immune-primed phenotype and robust type I IFN activation observed in DHX36 KO cells upon poly(I:C) stimulation are particularly notable when contrasted with the dampened immune activation and enhanced YFV replicon replication seen upon DHX36 overexpression (Fig. 3). These findings highlight a context-dependent role for DHX36 in both viral replication and innate immune regulation. Viral G4 structures are known to affect key stages of the viral life cycle, and G4-stabilizing ligands have shown potent antiviral activity against various viruses, including YFV (51, 52). We show that loss of DHX36 reduces YFV replicon replication, whereas transient overexpression markedly enhances it. Combined with biochemical evidence showing direct DHX36 binding to YFV replicon RNA (Fig. 3), these results suggest that YFV may exploit DHX36 helicase activity to resolve viral rG4s that would otherwise impede early replication. At the cellular level, impaired replication in DHX36-deficient cells likely reflects both a reduced capacity for viral rG4 unwinding and the presence of a primed antiviral state triggered by DHX36 loss.

Infections induce host-driven changes in protein expression and modulate protein activity through posttranslational modifications, which are known to fine-tune protein function as part of the cellular defense response (76, 77). As expected for common nucleic acid receptors, DHX36 also binds endogenous RNA (Fig. 4). This raises the question of whether PAMPs displace endogenous RNA during viral infection, potentially leading to DHX36 inactivation through conformational changes triggered by binding or release. Because DHX36 binds directly to the YFV replicon RNA but not to poly(I:C), we can exclude the possibility that poly(I:C) competitively interferes with its interaction with endogenous RNA targets (Fig. 3). However, further in-depth studies are required to clarify the precise mechanisms regulating DHX36 during viral infection. In the meantime, we propose that DHX36 activity may be modulated through posttranslational modifications—similar to eIF2α—through higher-order complex formation or multimerization, as described for PKR and IRF3, or via allosteric conformational changes that alter its catalytic activity and RNA binding properties.

A recent publication reported that DHX36 KO in cancer cells leads to increased double-strand break accumulation at DNA G4 sites and subsequent activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway (62), and another study demonstrated that DHX36 functions as an activator of NF-κB–dependent pro-inflammatory cytokines via the TLR adaptor protein MyD88 in MARC-145 cells (21). In contrast, our data show no substantial effect of DHX36 on NF-κB activation, and only a minor fraction of DHX36 localizes to the nucleus, with no detectable change upon viral stimulation (Fig. 4), arguing against a major regulatory role at the DNA level.

We showed that DHX36 KO alters the cellular stress response by modulating SG formation via a PKR-dependent mechanism as SG accumulation was rescued in DHX36/PKR double KO cells (Fig. 1). However, knocking out both DHX36 and PKR did not reverse the elevated expression of IFNB1 and only hampered the expression of most tested ISGs, indicating that PKR is not the primary driver of this transcriptional activation. Instead, the elevated IFNB1 transcripts, increased IFN secretion, and up-regulated ISG levels heavily rely on RIG-I pathway activation (Fig. 5). The absence of a DHX36-specific effect following IFN-α treatment further supports a role for DHX36 upstream of IFNAR1/2, modulating initial RIG-I–dependent sensing rather than IFNAR-mediated feedback. In this context, we propose that DHX36 limits RIG-I access to immunostimulatory RNAs—potentially via helicase-dependent RNA remodeling—thereby hampering IFN induction. These findings position RIG-I as one key driver of the poly(I:C)-induced type I IFN response, with DHX36 acting as a negative regulator that constrains RIG-I activation.

In contrast to our finding, a previous study by Yoo et al. (55) using mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) reported that DHX36 depletion impaired the RIG-I–mediated type I IFN response, evidenced by reduced IFNB1 mRNA levels and diminished IRF3 activation following poly(I:C) stimulation or Newcastle disease virus infection. Notably, the response appeared stimulus dependent: Whereas poly(I:C) suppressed IFNB1 expression, stimulation with a 5′ppp-RNA RIG-I ligand enhanced it. Yoo et al. further identified PKR as a DHX36 interactor contributing to the SG-associated phenotype observed in DHX36-deficient cells. Despite the opposing phenotypes, both our and their study implicate PKR as a key downstream effector (Fig. 4) and driver of the SG-associated phenotype (Fig. 1). Given that DHX36 regulates multiple critical pathways and its depletion causes diverse cellular defects (78), it is plausible that RNA interference or inducible Cre/LoxP-mediated KO may elicit different outcomes than the constitutive CRISPR-Cas9–mediated DHX36 KO used in our study. The discrepancies between studies may also reflect cell type–specific roles of DHX36, as multiple functions reported for DHX36 (24, 29, 78–81), suggesting that its role in antiviral signaling is highly context dependent.

To conclude, our data suggest a complex interplay between RNA, DHX36, PKR, and RIG-I, wherein DHX36 is functionally inactivated at its physiological targets during viral infection to promote a robust type I IFN response. We hypothesize that, in HEK293 WT cells, DHX36 may either directly suppress activation of PKR and RIG-I or act indirectly by resolving endogenous RNA secondary structures, such as G4s. In doing so, DHX36 likely prevents the cytosolic accumulation of structured RNA species that would otherwise be recognized nonspecifically by RIG-I and PKR, which would lead to type I IFN secretion, eIF2α phosphorylation, and subsequent SG formation. Whether unresolved rG4 structures are specifically recognized by RIG-I or PKR remains unclear.

Nevertheless, our data highlight DHX36 as a key regulator of immune homeostasis and the antiviral response. Given its dysregulation in various cancer types (78, 82, 83) and its role in immune evasion and malignant cell survival (62, 84), DHX36 may represent a promising target for cancer therapy and immune modulation. Furthermore, its involvement in cellular stress and emerging anti-inflammatory functions (54, 85) underscore the importance of investigating DHX36 in the context of autoimmune disorders and cancer progression.

Limitations of the study

Although we demonstrate that DHX36 negatively regulates the type I IFN response and supports viral replication in HEK293 cells, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all experiments were conducted in HEK293 cells, a nonimmune cell line that may not fully reflect the signaling context, RNA landscape, or antiviral dynamics of primary immune cells. Second, although rescue experiments confirmed the specificity of the DHX36 KO phenotype, the molecular mechanisms regulating DHX36 remain unresolved. Our findings raise the possibility that DHX36 activity is modulated by posttranslational modifications, protein-protein interactions, or allosteric conformational changes, but these hypotheses require direct investigation. Moreover, although our data suggest that G4-containing RNAs drive immune activation in DHX36-deficient cells, the endogenous RNA species responsible for triggering PKR and RIG-I remain unidentified. Last, in vivo validation is currently lacking due to the embryonic lethality of DHX36 KO mice (23, 24), limiting insights into tissue-specific functions and systemic immune responses. Addressing these questions will be crucial to refine our model of how DHX36 interfaces with PRRs and shapes antiviral defense.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell culture maintenance

WT HEK293 Flp-In T-REx cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and penicillin-streptomycin (100 U ml−1; Thermo Fisher Scientific). All experiments were performed in antibiotic-free medium. HEK293 Flp-In T-REx DHX36 overexpression cells, catalytically dead HEK293 Flp-In T-REx DHX36_E335A cells, and HEK293 Flp-In T-REx pFRT-FlagHA-DHX36-iso1 cells used in this study are published by Sauer et al. (22).

CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing to generate KO cells

HEK293 DHX36 KO, PKR KO, RIG-I KO, DHX36/PKR KO, and DHX36/RIG-I KO cell lines were generated by lentiviral transduction of HEK293 Flp-In T-REx cells, using the LentiCRISPRv2 system. For virus production. 3.8 × 106 HEK293T LentiX (Takara; #632180) were cultured in DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1 mM pyruvate (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Twenty-four hours before transduction, 25 μM chloroquine was supplemented. Lentivirus production was induced by polyethylenimine (PEI) transfection with 2 μg of the packaging vector psPAX2 (RRID:Addgene_12260), 1 μg of the viral envelop vector pMD2.G (RRID:Addgene_12259), and 4 μg of the pLenti-CRISPRv2 (RRID:Addgene_78852) or pLenti-CRISPRv2_neo (RRID:Addgene_98292) containing the respective guide RNA sequence (PKR KO: 5′-GTACTACTCCCTGCTTCTGA-3′; RIG-I KO: 5′-GAACTAACCTGCATGGTCTA-3′; DHX36 KO: 5′-CTAGAGAGCTGTAGTACACA-3′) in a 3:1 ratio (PEI μg:total DNA μg). Virus containing cell-free supernatants were harvested 48, 72, and 98 hours posttransfection and used for transduction of Flp-In HEK293 Flp-In T-REx cells. Selection and culturing of positive clones was conducted with puromycin (1 μg ml−1; InvivoGen) and/or neomycin (0.8 mg ml−1) in FBS-supplemented DMEM. All shown experiments in this manuscript were performed using at least three independent clones of each cell line to minimize potential biases arising from clonal effects.

Coimmunoprecipitation

HEK293 Flp-In T-REx pFRT-FlagHA-DHX36-iso1 cells were grown to ~70% confluency in 15-cm dishes. Protein expression was induced by addition of tetracycline for 24 hours. After 6 hours of poly(I:C) treatment, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and harvested by scraping in PBS. Cells were centrifuged at 500g for 5 min at 4°C. The cell pellet was resuspended in 500 μl of cold NP-40 lysis buffer [50 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 150 mM KCl, 2 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 0.5% (v/v) NP-40, and protease inhibitors]. Lysate were incubated for 15 min on ice and centrifuged at 14,000g for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred to a 2-ml tube, and 50 μl was removed as input and mixed with 10 μl of 6x SDS loading buffer. The remaining 450 μl of the lysate was incubated with 30 μl of anti-FLAG magnetic beads (Sigma-Aldrich; #M8823) in 1 ml of NP-40 lysis buffer on a head-over-tail rotator overnight at 4°C. The following day, beads were washed five times with cold NP-40 lysis buffer for 5 min each. Beads were then boiled in 35 μl of 1x SDS loading buffer for 10 min at 95°C, and eluates were used for Western blot analysis.

Western blot analysis

Protein lysates were separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and transferred to Protran BA83 nitrocellulose membranes (GE Healthcare). Membranes were blocked in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBS-T) [0.1% (v/v) Tween 20] supplemented with 5% (w/v) milk powder or bovine serum albumin (BSA). Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4°C with rotation, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature (RT) with rotation. Membranes were washed three times for 5 min with TBS-T after each incubation step. Signals were detected using a Gel Doc XR+ imaging system (Bio-Rad). Signal intensities were quantified using ImageJ (Fiji). Unless stated otherwise, signal intensities of proteins of interest were normalized to the corresponding TUBA4A, ACTB (actin), or GAPDH (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase) signals. Antibodies and dilutions used were as follows: anti-DHX36 (1:500; Santa Cruz; sc-377485); anti-FLAG (1:1000; Sigma-Aldrich; F1804); anti-PKR (1:1000; Abcam; ab32052); anti-p-PKR (1:1000; Abcam; ab32036); anti-p-IRF3 (1:1000; Abcam; ab76493); anti-TUBA4A (1:5000; Sigma-Aldrich; T5168); anti-RIG-I (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology; 3743); anti-IFIT1 (1:2000; Cell Signaling Technology; 14769S); anti-phospho-NF-κB p65 (Ser536) (1:500; Cell Signaling Technology; 3033); anti-NF-κB p65 (L8F6) (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology; 6956); anti-beta Actin (1:2500; Thermo Fisher Scientific; MA5-15739); anti-phospho-eIF2a (Ser51) (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology; 9721); anti-GAPDH (1:2000; Thermo Fisher Scientific; MA5-15738); anti-Histone H3 (1:5000; Abcam; ab1791); anti-mouse IgG, HRP-linked (1:10,000; Cell Signaling Technology; 7076P); anti-rabbit IgG, HRP-linked (1:10,000; Cell Signaling Technology; 7074P); IRDye 800CW Donkey anti-rabbit IgG (1:10,000; LI-COR; 926-32213); and IRDye 800CW Donkey anti-mouse IgG (1:10,000; LI-COR; 926-32212).

RNA-seq sample preparation and analysis

RNA for all sequencing experiments was isolated with TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For Illumina sequencing, rRNA depletion of the RNA pool was performed using the NEBNext rRNA Depletion Kit (New England Biolabs) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA synthesis was performed with the NEBNext Ultra Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs), and cDNA enrichment was facilitated using the indexed primers of the NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina (New England Biolabs). Sequencing reads were aligned to the hg38 human genome using RNA STAR (88). Quantification and differential expression of reads on the UCSC hg38 annotation were performed using featureCounts (89) and DESeq2 (90).

Detection of SGs

Sterile coverslips were coated with poly-d-lysine solution (100 μg ml−1) for 1 hour at 37°C and subsequently washed twice with PBS. Cells were grown to ~60% confluency and then transfected with poly(I:C) (1 μg ml−1). After incubation for the indicated time points, cells were briefly washed with PBS and fixed in 4% (v/v) formaldehyde for 10 min at RT. Cells were then washed and permeabilized in PBS supplemented with 1% (w/v) BSA and 0.2% (v/v) Triton X-100 for 30 min at 4°C. After two washes with cold PBS, coverslips were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-G3BP1 antibody (5 μg ml−1; BD; #611127) diluted in PBS supplemented with 2% (w/v) milk powder. The following day, samples were washed three times with PBS for 10 min at RT and incubated with a 1:1000 dilution of anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 secondary antibody (Life Technologies; ref. A11001) in PBS containing 2% (w/v) milk powder for 2 hours at RT. After three additional washes with PBS, coverslips were mounted using the Fluoroshield mounting medium containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Merck; ref. F6057). Fluorescence signals were detected at RT using a Leica fluorescence microscope.

Cell treatments

Indicated cell lines were grown to ~60% confluency before treatment. Poly(I:C) treatments were performed using poly(I:C) (1 μg ml−1; InvivoGen; tlrl-pic) delivered by transfection with Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, for transfections in a single 6-well plate, 75 μl of Opti-MEM medium was mixed with 2 μg of the poly(I:C) in reaction tube A. In reaction tube B, 75 μl of Opti-MEM medium was mixed with 4 μl of Lipofectamine 2000. Both tubes were incubated for 5 min at RT, followed by dropwise addition of mix A to mix B. The resulting mixture was incubated for 20 min at RT and subsequently added dropwise to cells in 2 ml of antibiotic-free DMEM. IFN-α was added directly to cells at ~60% confluency in antibiotic-free DMEM at a concentration of 10,000 U ml−1 for the indicated time periods. Similarly, cPDS treatment was performed at a final concentration of 10 μM cPDS (MedChemExpress; HY-112680A) for 24 hours. UV treatment was performed on medium-free cells by irradiation with UV light (20 J m−2) after washing cells with PBS. Following UV treatment, cells were replenished with prewarmed, antibiotic-free medium and incubated for the indicated time periods.

CFA and cell confluency measurements

For CFAs, HEK293 WT or DHX36 KO cell lines were seeded with a density of 800 cells per well and grown for 8 days on poly-d-lysine–coated 6-well plates in DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were stained and fixed with 0.4% (w/v) crystal violet dissolved in methanol. Colony count was normalized to the growth area. For cell confluency measurements, HEK293 WT and DHX36 KO cells were seeded with a density of 6000 cells per 96 wells and monitored over the indicated periods with the Incucyte Live-Cell Analysis System. To track apoptosis, cells were coincubated with the nontoxic apoptosis marker annexin V.

YFV replicon transduction by electroporation

Cells were trypsinized and washed with PBS. After centrifugation at 200g for 5 min, cells were resuspended at a density of 1 × 107 cells ml−1 in cytomix buffer (van den Hoff, 1992), supplemented with ATP and glutathione (1:50 each). The cell suspension (100 μl) was mixed with 2 μg of YFV replicon RNA. The cell suspension was transferred to electroporation cuvettes and electroporated using a Gene Pulser Xcell (Bio-Rad). Immediately after electroporation, cells were added to 3 ml of DMEM and seeded into 12-well plates (1 ml per well for 4 and 24 hours; 0.5 ml per well for 48 and 72 hours). For the 48- and 72 hour time points, wells were subsequently filled up to 1 ml with DMEM. Cells were then washed with 500 μl of PBS and lysed in 200 μl of lysis buffer (Promega). For measurement, 50 μl of each lysate was transferred in duplicate to a white 96-well plate. Renilla-Glo reagent (Promega) was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and 50 μl was added to each well. After 10-min incubation at RT, luminescence was measured using a Tecan Infinite M200 plate reader.

Type I IFN reporter assay

THP1-Dual TBK1/IKKε/IKKα/IKKβ−/− reporter cells featuring type I IFN-inducible secreted Lucia luciferase (6 × 104 per well of a 96-well plate) were seeded in 180 μl of RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal calf serum (FCS) and penicillin-streptomycin (100 U ml−1; complete RPMI). Then, 20 μl of the diluted or undiluted cell-free supernatant from HEK293 cells harvested 24 hours after stimulation, 20 μl of complete RPMI containing IFN-α (standard ranging from 4000 to 62.5 U/ml; Miltenyi Biotec), or medium only (blank) was added to the cells. After incubation for 24 hours at 37°C, the supernatant of the THP1-Dual reporter cells was collected. The supernatant (30 μl) was combined with 30 μl of coelenterazine solution (1 μg ml−1 in H2O) in a white 96-well F-bottom plate, and luciferase activity was measured using an EnVision 2104 Multilabel Reader device (PerkinElmer, Massachusetts, USA).

Differential scanning fluorimetry

Protein thermal stability was assessed by differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) using a Thermofluor assay on the Bio-Rad CFX384 real-time PCR system with the CFX Manager software. Reactions were prepared in 1x binding buffer [10 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 100 mM KCl, and 5% glycerol] with a total volume of 10 μl per well, containing 5x SYPRO Orange Protein Gel Stain (Invitrogen), 2 μM recombinant expressed DHX36, and 100 ng of YFV RNA or poly(I:C). Samples were centrifuged at 1000 rpm and subjected to a temperature gradient from 15° to 95°C at a rate of 1°C/min. Fluorescence was recorded using the FRET (fluorescence resonance energy transfer) channel at 330 nm. Melting temperatures (Tmelt) were determined as the inflection point of the fluorescence intensity curve.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

Protein-RNA interactions were assessed by EMSA under native conditions. Binding reactions contained 100 ng of YFV RNA and 1 μM recombinant DHX36 in 1x binding buffer [10 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 100 mM KCl, and 5% glycerol], in a total volume of 20 μl. Samples were incubated at RT for 30 min and then resolved on a 1% agarose gel in 1x TAE (tris-acetate-EDTA) buffer at 100 V for 90 min at 4°C.

Nucleic acids were visualized by staining the gel with SYBR Gold (Invitrogen) and imaged using a fluorescence gel documentation system.

Cytosolic and nuclear fractionation

Cells were washed with cold PBS and pelleted at 300g. Pellets were resuspended in 100 to 300 μl of hypotonic lysis buffer [HLB; 10 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.6), 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.3% NP-40, 10% glycerol, and 1x Halt inhibitor cocktail] and incubated on ice for 10 min. After brief vortexing, lysates were centrifuged at 800g for 8 min. The supernatant (cytoplasmic extract) was collected, and salt concentration adjusted to 150 mM NaCl. The nuclear pellet was washed twice with 300 μl of HLB, resuspended in 100 to 300 μl of nuclear lysis buffer [NLB; 20 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.6), 150 mM KCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.3% NP-40, 10% glycerol, 1x Halt inhibitor cocktail, and Benzonase (1 μl/ml)], and incubated on ice for 10 min with intermittent vortexing. Cellular extract were clarified by centrifugation at 16,000g for 10 min. Supernatants were transferred to fresh tubes and used for Western blot analysis.

Recombinant expression and purification of DHX36

Full-length human DHX36 was cloned into the pET28a(+) expression vector to generate a C-terminal His-tagged fusion protein. The construct was expressed in Escherichia coli NiCo21(DE3) (NEB) cells following induction with 0.1 mM IPTG (isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside) and overnight incubation at 16°C. After expression, cells were harvested by centrifugation at 6000g for 15 min at 4°C and resuspended in lysis buffer [10 to 20 ml/g; 40 mM Hepes (pH 7.0), 350 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, 15% glycerol, 0.1% NP-40, protease inhibitor cocktail, and Benzonase]. Cells were lysed by sonication on ice (10 min, 17 to 30% amplitude, and 40-s on/20-s off). The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 20,000g for 30 min at 4°C. The His-tagged protein was purified using the HisPur Cobalt Resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Eluted fractions were dialyzed against buffer containing 40 mM Hepes (pH 7.0), 350 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM DTT, and purity was assessed by SDS-PAGE.
