Engaging patient and family advisory councils (PFACs) in patient safety in healthcare organisations: a rapid scoping review
Authors: Qëndresa Rramani Dervishi, Yannick Blum, Larissa Brust, Matthias Weigl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEnhancing patient safety is a global priority and active involvement of patient and family advisory councils (PFACs) has been proposed as a key strategy in this endeavour. Preparing and supporting PFAC’s activities in the field of patient safety is a crucial step in sustainably implementing patient engagement in healthcare organisations. However, to what extent and through which means remains unclear. Considering these shortcomings, we aimed to synthesise the current literature base, identify and map existing resources designed to educate and support PFACs in patient safety within healthcare organisations.MethodsWe conducted a rapid scoping review. Drawing on predefined eligibility criteria, we reviewed peer-reviewed and grey literature on educational resources on patient-safety-related topics aimed at PFACs in healthcare organisations. We searched three databases (PubMed, Web of Science and Scopus) as well as websites of relevant stakeholders and institutions.ResultsOverall, we obtained 13 eligible sources. They featured resources ranging from reading materials to more intensive workshop sessions—combining active and passive learning approaches. Definition of key concepts and stakeholder roles were common topics in most sources. Yet, contents specific to patient safety showed considerable variability, likely due to context-specific approaches, local practices and lack of overall guidelines. Standardised resources tailored to PFACs’ unique needs in patient safety were largely absent.ConclusionThis review highlights the limited availability of comprehensive, well-documented resources for PFACs. It further corroborates the need for systematic approaches to support patient engagement activities and meaningful involvement in patient safety.Practice implicationsOur findings provide a first synthesis of the literature, inform future research as well as the development of respective patient engagement initiatives in patient safety. Moreover, they underscore the importance of developing standardised, yet adaptable resources to equip PFACs for their role in fostering safer healthcare systems.Trial registration numberDRKS00034733.

Background

Patient harm due to unsafe care remains a major global public health challenge.1 The WHO2 defines patient safety as ‘the absence of preventable harm to a patient and reduction of risk of unnecessary harm associated with healthcare to an acceptable minimum’ and emphasises its multifaceted nature, involving various stakeholders and structures.3 In the Global Patient-Safety Action Plan 2021–2030, the WHO proposes strategies to enhance patient safety, with particular emphasis on an active engagement of patients, family members and/or caretakers.1

Patient engagement is a fundamental pillar of patient-centred care; it can occur at multiple levels and be fostered using different approaches.4 5 A key approach—endorsed by the WHO and national healthcare quality agencies—is through patient and family advisory councils (PFACs).6 7 Notably, organisational-level patient and family involvement operates under different labels—such as patient advisory boards, patient councils or patient and public involvement (PPI)—that are not consistently or conceptually defined in the literature and vary by national and institutional context.8 9 With regards to PFACs, they usually include patients and caregivers acting as intermediaries between healthcare institutions and patients. Often described as ‘specialists by experience’,10 11 PFACs are considered as catalysts of change and are typically embedded within healthcare organisations to help improve the quality of care, shape decisions or consult policies at all levels.6 7 12 13 If implemented successfully, PFACs may support diverse quality and safety improvement initiatives, including facilitating communication, addressing patient feedback, improving physical space in hospitals, discussing healthcare leaders’ priorities and contributing to research.14 Involving PFACs has been associated with developing better education and information materials for patients,15 16 better health outcomes, better patient experience scores as well as improvement in specific clinical performance measures.17 18 Despite these promising findings, the literature is sparse and inconsistent, which confines reliable inferences on factors for successful introduction and sustainable implementation of PFACs to enhance quality and safety.19

Among key requirements of well-functioning PFACs is members’ knowledge or experience in healthcare topics. This may be pre-existing (eg, recruiting patients with domain knowledge) or acquired via education and training.20 Domain knowledge, however, is often vaguely defined, with no universally applicable standards. In line with this, a systematic review by Oldfield et al concluded that more data are needed to guide PFAC development, particularly regarding training and recruitment strategies.6 Educating, training and providing tools for both providers and PFACs, establishing metrics to assess their involvement and impact and employing documentation strategies to track engagement have also been highlighted in recent recommendations.21 22 Yet, healthcare organisations’ investment in patient engagement is limited.23 While PFACs may receive support, onboarding, training or coaching,20 24 these practices are not standardised and may vary between systems and contexts. Importantly, empowering patients and PFACs via different educational resources supports them to obtain a more active rather than a ‘tokenistic’ role in healthcare decision-making.3 25 26 For instance, the Italian PartecipaSalute project increased knowledge and engagement of patients in healthcare safety.27 28 While these preliminary results are encouraging, it remains unclear what kind of educational resources are available for PFACs or involved patient representatives acting in healthcare organisations.

Given the increasing call for PFACs as central partners in enhancing safety1 6 7 and the key role of patient-safety knowledge in facilitating this engagement,27 28 providing patient-safety education and support emerges as a logical step for healthcare organisations seeking to strengthen patient engagement and enhance safety. Although many organisations may provide educational resources, to the best of our knowledge, no comprehensive summary or review of such resources exists so far and that also facilitates patient engagement and enhances quality and safety through PFACs. Taking stock may provide valuable insights into topics and formats, as well as aspects that could be improved, and ultimately, could inform organisations on how to address patient-safety education for all stakeholders. Moreover, such a structured overview would also help and guide organisations and countries that have not yet established PFACs or patient engagement at the organisational level in patient safety.

Considering these shortcomings, the objective of this review was to identify, synthesise and map existing educational resources in patient safety for PFACs in healthcare organisations. Specifically, we aimed to answer the question: what existing educational resources are available to educate and support PFACs in patient-safety relevant topics in healthcare organisations?

Methods

Considering the high relevance of the topic, the absence of such overviews, the need for exploration and the time-sensitive need for evidence to inform our educational intervention, we applied a ‘rapid scoping review’ approach for reviewing and aggregating the scientific literature. We employed an iterative search strategy and followed established guidelines, including the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews and Cochrane recommendations for rapid reviews.2931 As recommended by the JBI methodology, we have attached a completed version of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews checklist.32

The review was primarily intended to inform the planning and content of an education intervention on patient safety for PFACs in healthcare organisations in Germany (as a part of a larger research project on patient engagement). During project planning, the need for such a structured overview became apparent. The research project under which the scoping review was conducted was preregistered in the German Clinical Trial Registry (DRKS; ID: DRKS00034733).

Patient and public involvement

No patients or the public were involved in the preparation of this rapid scoping review.

Search strategy and inclusion criteria

Given the absence of a universally accepted definition of PFACs, a working definition was developed to guide the search strategy and inclusion criteria of this scoping review. Therefore, we defined PFACs as formal or informal collective entities of patients, family members and/or members of the public that are involved primarily at the organisational level of healthcare organisations and contribute experiential knowledge to advisory, consultative or decision-shaping processes related to organisational structures, policies, quality improvement or patient safety.6 8 This definition is independent of terminology and includes bodies not explicitly labelled as PFACs (eg, patient advisory board, PPI committee and service user involvement board), provided that they enable collective participation beyond individual involvement and are oriented primarily towards organisational-level policies, strategies and changes, irrespective of the regularity or duration of engagement.

In this rapid scoping review, we considered peer-reviewed and grey literature. Peer-reviewed literature was searched in MEDLINE (PubMed), CLARIVATE (Web of Science) and Scopus, using all relevant keywords (see online supplemental appendix A), determined based on the objective and a previous review on PFACs,19 structured using the population, concept and context (PCC) framework. Our search query includes specific and general terms targeting articles on PFACs or comparable entities with educational resources on patient-safety and quality-improvement topics in healthcare organisations. We searched publications in German and English, including all types of publications up to 22 October 2024 (the date of this first search). Following reviewer feedback, this search strategy was expanded and the search was rerun (20 January 2026). The final search strategy is reported in online supplemental appendix A. Software Rayyan33 was used to organise and screen references retrieved from the databases. In the first round, a title and abstract screening was performed by one reviewer, followed by full-text screening, performed independently by two reviewers from the study team.

The grey literature search followed recommendations by Adams et al34 and was conducted between 15 June and 1 August 2024. We searched specialist associations, institutions and online platforms in English and German, using Google and Google Scholar and relevant search terms (see online supplemental appendix A). A prescreening was conducted using file names and descriptions, as well as the tables of contents. If there was any uncertainty about relevance, the entire document was examined.

In both searches, conflicts were resolved with the involvement of a third reviewer, and materials were included based on predefined inclusion criteria (see table 1).

Data extraction

A priori, a data-charting form was created and finalised through consensus among the authors. Two independent reviewers conducted a pilot extraction of data from three eligible articles and minor inconsistencies (eg, the level of detail in the extracted data) were resolved through discussion. The same reviewers extracted data from all included sources, and the data-charting form was iteratively updated. Finally, one reviewer combined and harmonised all extracted data. Following recommendations for scoping reviews,35 we extracted data on source characteristics as well as on PCC framework. Specifically, beside population characteristics (and sample size, where applicable), we extracted data on the format of resources, duration and intensity, setting and mode of delivery, the receiver, provider and topic of resources, and reported outcomes (where applicable), as well as country, facility level and specialty (see data-charting form in online supplemental appendix A).

Quality appraisal

To evaluate methodological rigour and inform future research, we critically assessed risk of bias for each included peer-reviewed publication using the mixed methods appraisal tool, recommended for use in reviews with heterogeneous publication base of different methods.36 37 Quality appraisal was performed by two independent reviewers; discrepancies were discussed until consensus was reached.

Data analysis

Extracted data were analysed using an inductive narrative approach. That is, we describe the included sources and create summarising categories based on the data. In line with our objectives, respective categories comprise characteristics of educational resources, including format of resource, covered topics, duration and intensity, provider and receiver of educational resources, learning approach as well as setting and mode of delivery.

Results

Search outcomes and general information on the included sources

Our updated literature search yielded 822 entries in PubMed, 52 in Web of Science and 538 in Scopus. After resolving duplicates, 1196 references were screened for title and abstract, and 62 publications underwent full-text screening. Grey literature search yielded 19 hits, all of which were full-text screened. In total, 81 sources were screened for full text, of which 13 met our inclusion criteria. Of these, 46.1% (6/13) were peer-reviewed articles and 53.8% (7/13) were grey literature (see figure 1). Details on the characteristics of the included sources are presented in table 2. Overall, the majority stemmed from the USA (46.1%; 6/13) and Canada (46.1%; 6/13), with only one study covering additional regions (1/13; 7.7%). All peer-reviewed articles were published after 2019 and grey literature sources no earlier than 2013.

Methodological quality of the peer-reviewed articles

We assessed methodological quality of six empirical studies, that is, peer-reviewed articles (two qualitative and four mixed-methods studies). One qualitative and one mixed-methods studies (2/6) were rated as appropriate in terms of their reporting in all points of the quality assessment.25 38 One qualitative study (1/2) did not clearly describe its methods and lacked coherence with regards to data processing and interpretation.39

Among mixed-methods publications, three (3/4) did not provide a clear rationale for using this methodology.4042 In addition, two of these (2/4) lacked integration, interpretation and appropriate reporting of the different methodological approaches.40 42 Detailed results on the quality assessment can be found in online supplemental appendix B.

Description of the reviewed educational resources

Format of educational resources

The reviewed literature mentioned various formats (see figure 2A), including extensive workshops, courses and/or modules, less extensive presentations/informative sessions, more engaging discussions, meetings and/or coaching sessions (mentioned in all six peer-reviewed articles), reading material, manuals, guides, handbook, paper hand-outs and hands-on tools (eg, checklists, list of guiding questions, templates, worksheets, exercises/surveys and informative videos). Most (12/13; 92.3%) sources combined different types of resources (eg, videos with exercises and reading material with tools).

Patient-safety-related topics covered in educational resources

Patient-safety-related topics frequently mentioned included definitions of main concepts and roles of involved actors—including advisors (ie, patients, family members or patient representatives in PFACs)—in enhancing safety and improving quality of care. Other common topics included identifying, designing and implementing projects engaging advisors in patient safety as well as providing examples of such engagements (see figure 2B for an overview of the topics). Fewer publications included specific safety-related topics, including emergency codes, error prevention and infection control, topics related to health conditions (eg, adolescent health and children’s health conditions), to legal and ethical decision-making (eg, patient capacity and substitute decision-maker concepts) as well as topics related to quality improvement (eg, A3 problem-solving thinking). Several grey literature sources also provided additional resources that could further support advisors in patient-safety and quality-improvement topics.

Patient safety and quality improvement were indirectly addressed through auxiliary topics, most commonly including communication and storytelling (10/13; 76.9%), general orientation on advisor roles and the respective healthcare system/organisation(s) (9/13; 69.2%), patient partnership, engagement and/or experience (8/13; 61.5%) and confidentiality/Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) training (5/13; 38.5%).

Duration, intensity and depth of educational resources

Four of six peer-reviewed articles reported durations ranging from brief sessions of 45 min to longer workshops or brainstorming sessions (>1 hour). The duration of online courses and workshops featured in grey literature varied as well, ranging from 90 min to several hours.43 No details on duration were openly available for e-learning modules.

Intensity and depth were rarely directly mentioned39 40 and were, therefore, extrapolated from format, setting and topic(s) covered. Shorter formats providing general definitions or brief information covered rather general topics. In this category fell the handbook,44 manual45 and guides,46 47 although they offer resources where patient and/or family advisors could find more specific information. Longer and more extensive formats (eg, coaching sessions) with inclusion of more specific topics allowed for a more in-depth coverage. Only one peer-reviewed article focused specifically on medication error disclosure with in-depth discussions and debriefing sessions.40 The majority (8/16; 61.5%) of the resources covered topics both in general and in-depth.

Providers and receivers of educational resources

In peer-reviewed articles, resources were typically compiled by interprofessional groups, consisting of the study team, experienced patient advisors, participants and in some cases other stakeholders such as leadership, managers or programme coordinators. These were delivered by trained actors and clinicians,40 managers, leaders and/or team coordinators25 38 and most often by facilitators who were usually experienced advisors253842 or participating advisors.39 42 Educational resources addressed interprofessional groups, including advisors and, most commonly, medical staff. Participating advisors were either members of already established PFACs (eg, 39 40) or volunteers who recently joined advisory groups (eg, 41 42). Most articles that reported demographic information described training for adult advisors,38 40 41 with only one focusing on young advisors.38

Materials stemming from grey literature were compiled and provided by state agencies and institutions4547 and hospital associations.43 44 48 49 They were aimed either solely for (prospective or current) advisors44 44 or for a wider audience, including advisors, leadership and medical staff.434648 Importantly, the e-learning platform of Hamilton Health Sciences is, to the best of our knowledge, not openly available. Similarly, the courses/workshops offered by the same institution are primarily meant for staff and associated advisors/volunteers, with occasional access for external members.

The mode of delivery refers to the method through which educational resources are provided, based on the medium of interaction and location. Most frequently, we identified in-person modes, followed by virtual, and a combination between in-person and virtual. In-person modes consisted mostly of workshops, presentation sessions, discussions and meetings, whereas the virtual setting comprised online courses, virtual workshops and e-learning. The setting refers to the organisational format based on the number and interaction of participants. The majority featured educational resources that were intended for individual and group settings, and only a few where both individual and group settings could apply. Educational resources for individuals included handbooks, guides, surveys and e-learning modules, whereas group-focused resources typically consisted of workshops, meetings and presentations.

Evaluation and outcomes

Within peer-reviewed articles, half (3/6) reported an evaluation of the provided resources. These evaluations were conducted via focus groups39 and evaluation surveys,41 42 with mainly assessing participants’ satisfaction and perceived feasibility. These evaluations were generally reported to be positive. Two articles also reported practice-related outcomes: information material that may support further development of resources39 and educational material for patients.42 In the remaining two peer-reviewed articles, the authors reported general satisfaction of the team and/or participants25 as well as expectations and perceptions of participants, pre- and post-training.40 While these could be conceived as an evaluation of the training, they are rather indirect measures. From the grey literature, only the guide by the Canadian Patient Safety Institute46 was rated for usability by ten patients and providers via a pilot study. All other publications did not provide information related to evaluation.

Discussion and conclusion

Discussion

Organisational patient engagement in PFACs can contribute to improvements in patient safety and quality of care. While numerous educational resources exist to support patients in safety initiatives,5052 an overview of resources specifically targeting patient representatives in organisational-level engagement was lacking. We, therefore, set out this rapid scoping review with the aim to identify and map existing educational resources in patient safety for PFACs in healthcare organisations. Our evidence synthesis indicates that the number of reported resources is still limited but growing, with considerable variation in their content and form. Our findings have several important contributions and may inform future efforts on promoting patient engagement in patient safety.

Importantly, our findings need to be interpreted against the backdrop of substantial conceptual and terminological heterogeneity in the field of organisational-level PPI in healthcare organisations. Across countries and research traditions, similar forms of collective patient engagement operate under different labels (eg, PFACs, PPI groups and advisory boards or councils), while the same term may also refer to different structures, purposes or levels of involvement.

Identified educational resources most commonly focused on defining stakeholder roles and key concepts related to patient safety and quality improvement, followed by more practical guidance on engaging advisors to enhance safety and improve quality. Less commonly covered were specific, safety-related topics (eg, infection control). Further topics included storytelling, confidentiality and information on internal functioning of respective organisations. While these contents primarily relate to interpersonal skills and the general purpose of PFACs, training in these areas may indirectly enable advisors to engage in safety and quality-improvement strategies as well.5355 Altogether, we found a substantial variability in the topics covered, along with the inclusion of more specific topics. This resonates well with previous research, supporting that safety-enhancing activities and measures are specific to local contexts, care environments and varying needs.56 At the same time, the variability in topics may also suggest that effective educational resources should be multifaceted. Furthermore, the observed variability could also be attributed to a prevailing lack of clarity among stakeholders concerning the actual role of PFACs in patient safety and, ultimately, uncertainty about suitable content that should be covered in relevant resources. More systematic and comprehensive approaches in the future may also take account of needs, with thorough analysis of respective expectations and requirements across involved stakeholders.57

It is also noteworthy that while a broader range of educational resources for PFACs exists, many of these focus on rather foundational aspects, such as establishing, developing and sustaining an advisory council, or on engaging patients in research projects.58 59 These resources were outside the scope of our review. We specifically targeted materials aimed at enabling PFACs to contribute to patient safety and quality of care initiatives in routine care. This further explains the limited number of resources we finally identified. In addition, following the expansion of our search strategy, solely publications describing a PFAC intervention by our own research group were retrieved.6062 These articles were not included in the results of this review, as its aim was to provide information for the development of an intervention by summarising existing evidence. Including publications that report on the outcomes or components of the intervention informed by this very review would, therefore, have conflicted with the original scope and intent of the study. Excluding these articles helped to preserve the conceptual separation between evidence synthesis and intervention development and to avoid circularity in the interpretation of the findings. Nevertheless, despite our efforts to establish an expanded, updated search, this illustrates that the evidence base for specific teaching and training resources on patient safety for members in these participation formats remains limited and unchanged.

Overall, most common resource formats were reading materials—mostly aimed for passive learning and discussions, meetings and workshops—aimed for active learning. All these varied notably in duration and intensity. Nearly all publications combined different formats and approaches. Discussions, workshops and meetings were most featured in peer-reviewed articles, often tied to specific projects or initiatives within healthcare organisations. In contrast, reading materials and tools were more prevalent in grey literature, which generally provided broader recommendations for healthcare organisations and PFACs. Although most of the sources did not report effectiveness or feasibility, the mere existence of such variety suggests that the type of training or material is adapted to fit local regulation, purpose, context or population.63 64 Different healthcare organisations may choose specific training types based on their individual needs. Future research should test if standardised resources and rather generic educational approaches are suitable across different contexts and PFAC activities. Moreover, future and systematic evaluation of different resources could help clarify which resources are best suited for different regulatory demands, contexts and stakeholder needs.

Another contribution of our review is an aggregated overview of educational resources aimed for use among PFAC stakeholders. Among peer-reviewed articles, resources were predominantly developed with and aimed for various stakeholders, for example, interprofessional groups. While our research question focused on educational resources for patients, families and caregivers, the finding that many programmes also target healthcare professionals highlights the coproductive components of PFAC work. Training often aims not only to build the capacity of individual parties (eg, patient and family advisors) but to jointly support all involved stakeholders, that is, professionals in collaborating effectively with patient representatives in organisational decision-making and patient-safety initiatives.65 This dual focus reflects the relational aspects of PFAC engagement and has implications for the design and evaluation of educational resources.18 Just two articles addressed solely advisors25 41 via individual coaching sessions on more general rather than safety-related topics. Furthermore, advisors were often assigned a double role by being both a trainer/facilitator and a trainee. This may help to take account of their domain-specific perspective and experiences that are key to fostering a successful collaboration between all stakeholders.54 However, patient safety and quality improvements often require some core knowledge on related concepts (eg, project management and quality improvement). In this regards, educational resources tailored specifically to PFACs could be more effective, bridging the knowledge gaps between PFACs and healthcare professionals; while some grey literature sources were intended specifically for PFACs, there were no details regarding how effective these resources are. Previous research has shown that initiatives aiming to empower patients in safety-related topics increase patient engagement3 27 28; therefore, investing in developing educational resources specifically for PFACs in safety-related topics emerges as a promising step in the endeavour to enhance patient engagement in safety.

Our results revealed a lack of systematic evaluations and outcomes of reviewed educational resources, which limits drawing clear conclusions about their short- or long-term effectiveness. In particular, most reported evaluation approaches focused primarily on participant satisfaction and perceived usefulness rather than on measurable changes in knowledge, behaviours, organisational practices or patient-safety outcomes.19 21 While satisfaction may be an important indicator of acceptance, it provides only limited insight into whether PFAC training actually contributes to meaningful engagement or improvements in safety and quality. This highlights the unwavering need for more theory-driven and outcome-oriented evaluation frameworks for PFAC education, linking training inputs to intermediate outcomes (eg, advisor competence and quality of collaboration) and downstream effects on organisational decision-making and patient safety. Without such approaches, it remains difficult to determine which types of educational resources are truly effective and should be scaled or sustained. Only a few studies reported evaluation or outcomes, focusing mostly on feasibility and satisfaction of the participants and, at least some, on scientific output.39 41 42 Notwithstanding the lack of comparability, reported educational resources were evaluated positively by participants—including patient and family advisors—in some cases, with long-standing impact for ongoing refinement and development.39 42 These results, although limited, given the low number of studies, suggest that education and training initiatives are welcomed by patients and their representatives. Moreover, our findings resonate well with previous research indicating that patients—not necessarily acting in a PFAC—in general are willing to obtain knowledge that further encourages their engagement in safety improvements.66

Another relevant aspect of the reviewed educational resources is their consistency and sustainability, that is, whether they are common practice or rather employed only within the limited time or frame of specific initiatives. Most of the included sources did not address this aspect, and some only implied it in more indirect ways. Since we hardly identified reliable information on continuity and follow-up studies, it is likely that the reviewed resources were project specific and were considered as a good practice rather than a standard requirement in routine care. However, for educational approaches and resources to be effective in the long-term and in a more overarching way, they should be offered continuously, under supportive contextual conditions with strategic and structural changes that ensure sustainability.25 Such organisation-level support could include appointing specific departments or staff liaisons to oversee patient engagement as well as to offer and coordinate educational activities.25 67

Finally, we found that almost all sources came from the USA or the Canadian healthcare context, whereas other settings around the globe were not represented. This could indicate that these systems, compared with others, are more invested and/or more transparent in reporting initiatives related to PFACs and patient safety. The latter is especially likely, given that assessing and reporting the engagement of patients and PFACs are well-known and pervasive challenges in the field.21 22 Despite all national specifics in healthcare, comprehensive and transparent reporting would provide valuable insights for patients and professionals evaluating a healthcare organisation’s patient engagement efforts (ie, for benchmarking against best practice). Moreover, this would facilitate knowledge sharing between institutions with realistic insights into the true state of patient engagement, implementation barriers and facilitators in PFACs work.68 In the light of WHO’s call for global action in enhancing patient safety and in engaging patients,1 it becomes imperative for other countries to evaluate their healthcare systems and the employed strategies in this regards. Here, our review work is valuable by providing a guide with regards to the existing resources.

Beyond the immediate scope of PFAC education and training, our findings also speak to broader efforts to advance quality and safety in healthcare systems. Recent global policy frameworks emphasise the need for more people-centred, participatory and coproduced models of care as a foundation for sustainable quality improvement.69 In this context, PFACs can be understood as an important organisational forum through which patients and families contribute to collective learning and decision-making. The educational resources identified in this review provide viable means to promote participation by fostering shared understanding, role clarity and collaborative competencies between patient representatives and healthcare professionals. At the same time, the observed variability and limited evaluation of the existing resources indicate that the translation of these policy ambitions into routine practice remains uneven, underscoring the need for more coherent and strategically embedded approaches to PFAC capacity building.

Strengths and limitations of this study

Our review has certain strengths and limitations. Though we chose a rapid scoping review approach, this synthesis is, to the very best of our knowledge, the first assessment of the literature base and evidence synthesis on this timely and highly relevant topic in the given context. Our review contributes to our current understanding of patient engagement in healthcare and provides an overview for future research or initiatives aiming to engage and train PFACs in patient-safety-related topics.19 Our review was conducted in line with established methodological standards and followed a rigorous selection procedure,29 35 drawing on peer-reviewed as well as grey literature sources. The specificity of our search strategy likely reduced the number of irrelevant hits; we cannot, however, exclude the possibility of selection bias or missing of suitable publications (ie, being published in different languages). Given that PFAC education and training materials are often developed for local use and primarily aimed at patients, families and caregivers, this language restriction is likely to have led to a systematic under-representation of locally produced resources, particularly in grey literature, and may have biased the geographic and thematic distribution of included materials. We identified only a limited number of eligible publications, and the majority came from grey literature, some of which are not accessible without active accounts on respective websites. Another strength was the application of a quality appraisal tool. Although this is uncommon for rapid scoping reviews,31 32 70 we deemed it important to provide a frame of reference regarding available publication base and respective methodological rigour. Most of the included peer-reviewed articles showed a high risk of bias in methodology and reporting. For our data analysis, we opted for an inductive narrative approach, given the lack of comparable publications. Modes, formats, settings and contents of educational resources differed substantially, which further limited systematic aggregations. Nevertheless, the strength of evidence should be substantiated in the future with more objective and rigorous approaches, such as with a systematic review or meta-analyses. Notwithstanding these limitations, our findings may serve as a valuable guidance for future research as well as for care practitioners.

Practice implications and conclusion

This rapid scoping review has several implications for practice. First, it provides an overview of available educational resources that could inform and inspire the development of similar approaches to support PFACs in patient-safety-related topics. Second, the review may inform organisations on strategies on how to engage, train and work with PFACs on patient safety. Third, the evidence synthesised in this review may guide future studies and systematic reviews on the topic such that more objective, reliable and practice-relevant evidence is generated—with regards to educating, training, supporting and engaging PFACs in patient safety. Further, the limitations of the existing resources highlighted in the review could help to improve existing or in development of more effective and feasible resources, all tailored to specific needs of involved stakeholders.

To conclude, although on its own, this review may be limited in affecting policy- or institution-level changes, we deem it may serve as a milestone that encourages long-standing efforts on how healthcare systems and organisations invest in engaging patients and PFACs in patient safety in a transparent and meaningful way.
