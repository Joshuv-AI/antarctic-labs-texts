Association Between Clonal Hematopoiesis and Cardiometabolic Disease
Authors: Brooke D’Mello, Neel Sheth, Ani Orchanian-Cheff, David Soave, Sagi Abelson, Minna Woo, Calvin Ke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClonal hematopoiesis (CH) is the age-related expansion of hematopoietic stem cells. CH encompasses mosaic chromosomal alterations or somatic mutations in leukemia-driver genes (CH of indeterminate potential). Although CH has been associated with overall mortality, driven largely by cardiovascular disease, its links to individual cardiometabolic endpoints remain unclear.ObjectivesThe aims of this study were to systematically quantify the associations of CH with incident myocardial infarction, stroke, cardiovascular mortality, and type 2 diabetes and to assess whether these associations differ according to CH driver gene.MethodsMEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane, and CENTRAL were searched from inception through June 2025 for observational studies reporting age-adjusted associations between CH and cardiometabolic outcomes in adults. Animal and phenome-wide association studies were excluded. Using random-effects models, HRs were generated for primary outcomes. The risk for bias was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale.ResultsEighteen studies encompassing more than 650,000 individuals were included, with 1 study of participants with hematological malignancy. CH was associated with increased incidence of myocardial infarction (HR: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.00-1.29), stroke (HR: 1.12; 95% CI: 1.03-1.23), cardiovascular mortality (HR: 1.20; 95% CI: 1.07-1.36), and type 2 diabetes (HR: 1.20; 95% CI: 1.02-1.41). In driver gene analyses, TET2 mutations were associated with a higher hazard of stroke (HR: 1.41; 95% CI: 1.03-1.93) and ASXL1 mutations with cardiovascular mortality (HR: 2.22; 95% CI: 1.37-3.60).ConclusionsCH is associated with cardiometabolic outcomes and may exhibit heterogeneity across mutations and clinical phenotypes, supporting its role as a somatic genomic marker of cardiometabolic risk. However, cautious interpretation and further study are required, as CH definitions were heterogeneous. (Association of Clonal Hematopoiesis with Type 2 Diabetes and Cardiovascular Disease: A Systematic Review and Meta Analysis; CRD420251156288)

Clonal hematopoiesis (CH) is defined as the age-related expansion of hematopoietic stem cells carrying somatic mutations or chromosomal alterations.1 CH is highly prevalent among individuals with cancer, in part because of genotoxic effects of cancer-directed therapies that promote clonal selection.2 Although CH has traditionally been linked to hematologic malignancy, evidence suggests that CH may have a role in cardiometabolic disease.1 There are 2 major forms of CH: CH of indeterminate potential (CHIP), involving mutations in leukemia-driver genes such as DNMT3A, TET2, and ASXL1, and mosaic chromosomal alterations (mCAs), consisting of large chromosomal gains, losses, or copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity.3 CHIP is typically defined by a proportion of sequencing reads supporting an acquired somatic mutation in canonical CHIP driver genes (variant allele frequency [VAF]) of at least 2%. Both CHIP and mCAs increase with age, affecting up to 20% of adults older than 70 years.1

Although CHIP increases the risk for hematologic malignancy, most excess mortality associated with CHIP is driven by cardiovascular disease rather than conversion to overt leukemia.2,4 However, prior epidemiologic studies were focused on major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) as a composite outcome, which may mask potential differences in associations and pathophysiological mechanisms across individual cardiovascular endpoints.5,6 In animal models, DNMT3A and ASXL1 drive macrophage-mediated atherogenesis in myocardial infarction (MI), while TET2 promotes plaque instability and neuroinflammation in stroke and increases IL-1β expression in white adipose tissue, contributing to obesity-related insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes (T2D).7,8 These mechanistic differences might result in differences in the associations of CHIP with various endpoints. For example, in a large study of 17,182 U.S. participants, CH was associated with higher T2D prevalence (OR: 1.32; 95% CI: 1.14-1.53) but not cardiovascular mortality risk (HR: 1.40; 95% CI: 0.88-2.24).1 However, subsequent analyses of 200,453 individuals in the UK Biobank suggested no association between CH and T2D prevalence (OR: 0.92; 95% CI: 0.83-1.02), and analyses of 10,623 Japanese individuals suggested a significant association between CH and cardiovascular mortality risk (HR: 1.36; 95% CI: 1.09-1.70).9,10 It is unclear whether these conflicting findings may be related to prior studies’ being underpowered for examining individual cardiovascular endpoints, or potential variation in CH definitions. Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to measure the associations of CH with incident T2D, MI, stroke, and cardiovascular mortality. We hypothesized that CH is positively associated with each of these outcomes, with the magnitude of these associations varying by VAF and driver gene.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of published observational studies. We registered our prespecified protocol with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD420251156288). We reported the findings according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses statement11 and the Meta-Analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.12

Data sources and searches

We developed a comprehensive search strategy using a combination of database-specific subject headings and text words for the concepts of CH in combination with MI, stroke, cardiovascular mortality or T2D. Our search terms included “clonal hematopoiesis,” “clonal mosaicism,” “diabetes,” “myocardial infarct,” “cerebrovascular accident,” and “cardiovascular death” (see Supplemental Table 1 for the full list of search terms). We limited searches to human adults and excluded conference proceedings. On June 23, 2025, a medical librarian searched Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid Embase, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (Ovid), and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (Ovid). No restrictions on language or publication date were set on searches. We screened the reference lists of included publications to identify additional eligible studies.

Study selection

We included studies meeting the following criteria: 1) primary observational research, including noninterventional components of randomized trials (eg, preintervention baseline characteristics, post-trial observational follow-up); 2) measured CH in participants by any detection method, such as whole-genome sequencing, whole-exome sequencing and targeted gene panels; 3) included at least 1 outcome variable (T2D, MI, total stroke, ischemic stroke, or cardiovascular mortality); and 4) reported age-adjusted measures of association with or without adjustment for additional covariates, between the exposure variable and any outcome variable. Because of heterogeneity in the definitions of these outcome variables across studies, we included all types of definitions for each outcome (eg, hospitalization data, chart review). We excluded studies that 1) were available only in abstract form; 2) included participants <18 years of age; 3) were based solely on animal models or in vitro experiments; or 4) were phenome-wide association studies, because of their exploratory nature and high risk for outcome misclassification.13

Data extraction and quality assessment

We screened abstracts and full-text papers independently and in duplicate using Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation), with discrepancies resolved by discussion with a senior author (M.W. or C.K.). We independently extracted data in duplicate and resolved discrepancies by consensus or discussion with a senior author. We collected the following information: first author, study design, country, cohorts, study population, total sample size, CH sample size, mean baseline age, follow-up duration, CH definition, CH detection method, CH sequencing depth, VAF detection threshold (eg, ≥2%, ≥5%, ≥10%), genes assessed (eg, DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1), type of effect estimate, effect estimate (point estimate and 95% CI) P value, effect estimates for specific genes (if available), and covariates used for adjustment of each effect estimate. We extracted HRs from time-to-event (Cox proportional hazards) models. For studies with multiple effect estimates from models with different covariates, we extracted the estimate adjusting for the most covariates. For studies that reported results pertaining to multiple cohorts, we created a separate entry for each cohort. We extracted data from all eligible studies regardless of cohort overlap. If multiple studies reported on the same cohort and outcome, we included the study reporting the smallest SE. If SEs were identical or not reported, we included the study with the largest sample size.

We assessed risk for bias independently and in duplicate (B.D. and N.S.) using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, which evaluates observational and cross-sectional study quality on the basis of participant selection, comparability of groups, and outcome assessment.14 Studies were considered high quality if they scored ≥7 out of 9 points for incident outcomes. The overall certainty of evidence for each outcome was further evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework adapted for prognostic studies, considering risk for bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.15

Statistical analysis

We summarized baseline characteristics descriptively and calculated sample size–weighted mean age and pooled SD across studies. For each outcome, we constructed a random-effects model to calculate a pooled HR with 95% CI by combining age-adjusted or multivariable-adjusted HRs and 95% CIs from each cohort. We did not conduct meta-analyses for outcomes with <3 cohorts, as per Meta-Analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology recommendations.12 Studies of cancer populations were analyzed separately, considering the high clonal burden and independently elevated cardiovascular risk associated with some hematological malignancies.16 We estimated between-study variance (τ2) using the restricted maximum likelihood method. In all analyses, we recalculated 95% CIs from the regression coefficients (logHR) derived from the reported effect estimates and their SEs. We decided a priori to use random-effects models for all meta-analyses to account for heterogeneity across cohorts, CH definitions, and detection methods. We estimated heterogeneity across cohorts using the I2 statistic. For outcomes reported in ≥10 cohorts, we assessed small-study effects by visual inspection of contour-enhanced funnel plots. Additionally, we evaluated funnel plot asymmetry using Egger’s regression test, considering P < 0.10 as indicative of potential small-study effects. We conducted subgroup analyses for all outcomes for which there were ≥3 cohorts from ≥2 studies reporting results stratified by VAF (unselected VAF [defined as studies including all reported CHIP above the study-specific minimum threshold] and VAF ≥ 10%) or by individual genes (eg, DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1). There were insufficient cohorts to conduct subgroup analyses for other genes. We repeated this procedure with prevalent T2D considered as a hypothesis-generating exploratory outcome (see Supplemental Table 2 and Supplemental Methods), and explored whether baseline diabetes prevalence modified the association between CH and cardiovascular outcomes by conducting a meta-regression among cohorts reporting baseline diabetes prevalence. To test the robustness of our findings, we conducted several sensitivity analyses: 1) leave-one-out sensitivity analyses for each outcome by sequentially omitting one cohort at a time to evaluate its influence on the pooled estimate; 2) removing cohorts with VAF thresholds less than VAF ≥ 2% to mitigate the effect of small clones; and 3) restricting to cohorts that defined CH using a minimum set of identical genes. We conducted all analyses using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) with the meta package.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Of the 1,470 records screened for eligibility, 108 full-text papers were reviewed. We included 24 independent cohorts (reported in 18 studies) in the systematic review, of which 23 cohorts from 17 studies contributed to the quantitative analyses (Supplemental Figure 1). Across studies, the sample size–weighted mean age was 60.0 ± 10.6 years (range: 46.5 ± 14.817 to 82.0 ± 2.818 years), and female participants constituted 50.6% (range: 18.7%19 to 100.0% [Women’s Health Initiative]17) of the pooled study population. Included studies were published between 2014 and 2025, with most (17 of 18) published during or after 2021 (Tables 1 and 2). Total stroke

CH was associated with a 12% increased hazard of total stroke (HR: 1.12; 95% CI: 1.03-1.23; I2 = 0.0%) across 12 cohorts (Figure 1B).17,21, 22, 23 Subgroup analyses by VAF threshold demonstrated similar pooled HRs among studies reporting unselected VAF (1.15; 95% CI: 1.04-1.27; I2 = 13.3%) and studies restricted to VAF ≥ 10% (1.16; 95% CI: 1.08-1.25; I2 = 0.0%) (Supplemental Figure 3A). Subgroup analyses by CHIP driver gene were available for 3 cohorts. TET2 was significantly positively associated with total stroke (1.41; 95% CI: 1.03-1.93; I2 = 14.7%), whereas ASXL1 (1.07; 95% CI: 0.78-1.47; I2 = 0.0%) and DNMT3A (1.03; 95% CI: 0.90-1.16; I2 = 0.0%) were not (Supplemental Figure 3B). In a single study of patients with multiple myeloma undergoing hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, CH was associated with an increased hazard of total stroke (HR: 3.02; 95% CI: 1.07-8.52).

Ischemic stroke

CH was not associated with ischemic stroke (HR: 1.09; 95% CI: 0.97-1.21; I2 = 45.5%) across 11 cohorts (Figure 1C).1,17,20,22, 23, 24 However, when stratified by VAF threshold, VAF ≥ 10% was associated with increased ischemic stroke (1.31; 95% CI: 1.04-1.65; I2 = 79.2%) (Supplemental Figure 4).

Cardiovascular mortality

CH was associated with a 20% increased hazard of cardiovascular mortality (HR: 1.20; 95% CI: 1.07-1.36; I2 = 53.7%) across 12 cohorts (Figure 1D).1,10,18, 19, 20,22,24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29 There was moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 53.7%). Sensitivity analyses and meta-regression

Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses for all outcomes were consistent with the main analysis (Supplemental Table 8). For cardiovascular mortality, removal of 2 studies with lower VAF thresholds (≥0.01% and ≥1.0%) did not materially alter the pooled estimate (Supplemental Table 9). Sensitivity analyses restricted to cohorts using a consistent CHIP gene panel were consistent with the main analysis (Supplemental Table 10). In a meta-regression, baseline T2D prevalence did not materially change the effect size of the association of CH with cardiovascular mortality (HR per 10% increase in diabetes prevalence: 1.00; 95% CI: 0.87-1.16; P = 0.97). Discussion

Our systematic review and meta-analysis of 24 cohorts provide the most comprehensive synthesis to date on associations of CH with individual cardiometabolic outcomes. We provide evidence linking CH to individual cardiovascular endpoints including MI and cardiovascular mortality, which have never been shown in prior meta-analyses.20,22 These findings align with those of meta-analyses that analyzed MACE as a composite outcome6 and contrast with a smaller meta-analysis that reported no significant association between CHIP and cardiovascular mortality.5 Furthermore, associations of CHIP with stroke and cardiovascular mortality were similar in magnitude despite being mediated by different genes—TET2 and ASXL1, respectively—which have been implicated in prior studies.17,22 We also found that CHIP is associated with incident T2D in noncancer populations, and stroke in people with multiple myeloma. Our findings underscore the potentially heterogeneous biological effects of distinct CH clones on cardiometabolic outcomes, demonstrate consistent direction and magnitude of the associations of CHIP with individual cardiovascular endpoints despite their different driver mutations, and highlight an important gap in studies investigating the relationship between CHIP and individual cardiovascular endpoints in oncology populations (Central Illustration).Central IllustrationCH and Cardiometabolic DiseaseThis systematic review and meta-analysis (>650,000 participants) evaluated associations between clonal hematopoiesis (CH) and incident cardiometabolic outcomes. CH and Cardiometabolic Disease

This systematic review and meta-analysis (>650,000 participants) evaluated associations between clonal hematopoiesis (CH) and incident cardiometabolic outcomes. CH was associated with increased risk for myocardial infarction, total stroke, cardiovascular (CV) death, and type 2 diabetes, with gene-specific signals observed for TET2 (stroke) and ASXL1 (cardiovascular death). HSC = hematopoietic stem cell.

Our meta-analysis is the first to our knowledge to demonstrating that CH is significantly positively associated with incident MI and cardiovascular mortality. Our findings contrast with those of another meta-analysis reporting an insignificant association.5 Few cohorts have been able to identify a positive association between CH and cardiovascular mortality, likely because of insufficient power.10,19,28 By pooling data across cohorts, we provide a more generalizable estimate of this relationship, though there was significant heterogeneity likely reflecting differences in participant characteristics and methodologies across studies. The association of CH with cardiovascular mortality was secondary to ASXL1, with DNMT3A and TET2 showing insignificant associations. Clinically, ASXL1-mutated CH has been linked to elevated cardiac injury biomarkers such as high-sensitivity troponin T and increased left ventricular mass, potentially contributing to risk for MI.35 Further research in larger cohorts is required to investigate whether MI is similarly linked to ASXL1 or with other genes such as JAK2V617F, as this mutation has been shown to increase platelet reactivity and neutrophil extracellular trap formation enhancing arterial thrombosis and plaque rupture in preclinical studies,36 and a cross-sectional study suggested a strong association with prevalent MI.4 For stroke, our findings align with those of a previous meta-analysis reporting a pooled estimate of the relationships of CH and total stroke of 1.16 (95% CI: 1.05-1.28) and CH and ischemic stroke of 1.18 (95% CI: 1.06-1.33) at unselected VAF.5 In our analyses, we report a significant association of ischemic stroke only for VAF ≥ 10%. This distinction may reflect our restriction to incident events, whereas the prior meta-analysis included both prevalent and recurrent cases.5 In gene-specific analyses, TET2 largely was responsible for the association of CH with total stroke, consistent with experimental evidence showing that TET2-deficient hematopoietic cells give rise to clonally expanded monocytes and macrophages that secrete higher levels of IL-1β and IL-6, amplifying vascular inflammation and plaque instability.37 Furthermore, IL-1β inhibition has been shown to reduce MACE, including stroke, in a clinical trial.38 These pathways may be particularly relevant to some cancer populations, such as multiple myeloma,16 as CH prevalence is elevated by exposure to cytotoxic therapies and aging in general.2 In the UK Biobank, cancer patients with CHIP had a higher risk for cardiovascular disease analyzed as a composite outcome (HR: 1.08; 95% CI: 1.02-1.13).39 Future studies are required to investigate the relationship between CH and individual cardiovascular endpoints in various cancer populations.40, 41, 42, 43

We also found significant associations between CH and incident T2D across 7 cohorts, consistent with a prior meta-analysis.31 We update this analysis using a random-effects model in which the previously analysis used a fixed-effects model, which allowed us to better account for between-cohort heterogeneity. Although gene-specific associations could not be formally pooled for T2D, emerging evidence supports heterogeneity at the driver gene level. In experimental models, Tet2-knockout mice showed IL-1β-driven adipose inflammation and insulin resistance.7 In one study examining the association of CH with prevalent T2D, mCAs were strongly associated with T2D approximately 4-fold higher than CHIP, though no studies examined the association of mCAs with cardiovascular disease, highlighting an important area for future research. Additionally, although our meta-regression did not identify baseline diabetes prevalence as a statistical effect modifier of the CH–cardiovascular mortality association, prior studies have likewise shown that the cardiovascular consequences of CH are not altered by baseline diabetes status.21

This study has many strengths. As a meta-analysis, it integrates data from 24 independent cohorts, increasing statistical power to detect associations. By harmonizing effect measures across diverse study designs and applying a random-effects model, we accounted for between-study heterogeneity. Additionally, the inclusion of VAF and driver-gene subgroup analyses enabled us to explore specific clonal characteristics that may modify disease risk. Finally, risk for bias as assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale classified all studies as having a low risk and the overall certainty of evidence evaluated using GRADE was moderate across all outcomes.

Study limitations

The number of available cohorts was limited for certain outcomes, particularly gene-specific associations. Variation in CH calling methods and definitions, genes included in targeted arrays, sequencing methods, VAF thresholds and ranges, adjustment covariates, and the definition and ascertainment method of outcomes likely contributed to heterogeneity across analyses. Acquisition of multiple CH mutations and the order in which they accrue may also be pathogenically relevant but could not be assessed, because of reliance on aggregate data rather than individual-level clonal profiles. Finally, inconsistent reporting of baseline diabetes prevalence also precluded formal meta-regression for some cardiovascular outcomes.

Conclusions

Overall, our study supports the role of CH as a somatic marker of cardiometabolic disease, with risk varying by CH type, driver gene, and clone size. Future mechanistic and interventional studies using standardized CH definitions and finer VAF strata are warranted to investigate how specific hematopoietic clones drive cardiometabolic risk, how somatic markers can be integrated into cardiometabolic risk assessment and prevention strategies and whether existing therapies might mitigate the burden of cardiometabolic disease associated with CH.PerspectivesCOMPETENCIES IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: CH is associated with increased risk for incident MI, stroke, cardiovascular mortality, and T2D with differential associations across driver mutations.TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Future studies should evaluate whether integrating CH status, subtype, and mutation burden into cardiometabolic risk prediction models improves clinical decision-making and whether targeted preventive or therapeutic interventions can mitigate excess cardiometabolic risk associated with CH.
