Objective postoperative knee stability after 1-stage and 2-stage revision anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction
Authors: Ryohei Uchida, Yukiyoshi Toritsuka, Tomohiko Matsuo, Masashi Kusano, Takayuki Tsuda, Wataru Ando
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRevision anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) procedures are performed either as 1-stage or 2-stage surgeries. But there has been no report of comparison in postoperative knee stability between 1-stage and 2-stage Revision ACLR. Therefore, the present study aimed to compare postoperative anterior stability after 1-stage and 2-stage surgeries.MethodsObjective anterior knee laxity was evaluated by knee arthrometer at 12+ months postoperatively among 35 patients who underwent revision ACLR.Results19 patients (Group 1: nine males and 10 females, mean age 26.4 ± 11.0 years) underwent 1-stage revision ACLR and 16 patients (12 males and four females, mean age 32.3 ± 10.6 years) underwent 2-stage revision ACLR with staged revision ACLR at six months after iliac bone grafting for large bone defects. At the final follow-up period (23 ± 9 months after 1-stage surgery and 21 ± 9 months after 2-stage surgery), procedures in two patients (10.5 %) after 1-stage surgery and one patient (6.7 %) after 2-stage surgery were found to have failed. KT side-to-side differences in anterior laxity at manual maximum force were 0.9 ± 1.1 mm and 0.9 ± 1.3 mm after 1- and 2-stage surgeries, respectively, with 94 % of patients after 1-stage surgery and 80 % of patients after 2-stage surgery showing anterior laxity values between −1 and 2 mm. We found no significant technique-dependent difference in objective anterior knee laxity between 1-stage and 2-stage revision ACLR.ConclusionsAfter a minimum of 12 months of follow-up, patients who underwent 1-stage and 2-stage revision ACLR acquired sufficient knee stability regardless of surgical technique, showing no other significant differences.

Introduction

The incidence of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury has increased over the past few decades,1,2 and the number of revision ACL reconstructions (ACLR) has also increased.3 But some authors have reported greater residual anterior laxity with 1-stage revision ACLR relative to the primary reconstruction4, 5, 6 because procedures in 1-stage revision ACLR become more technically demanding in case of tunnel enlargement or deviation from the optimal tunnel location. On the other hand, 2-stage revision ACLR is theoretically free from technically demanding procedures because bone tunnels are filled with grafted bone. Therefore, 2-stage surgery should be selected depending on the tunnel location and size.7, 8, 9, 10 Thomas et al.11 found no significant differences in subjective and objective laxity assessments between primary ACLR and 2-stage revision ACLR after bone grafting. Notably, only one study has compared 1-stage and 2-stage revision ACLR in the past8,12; however, their report evaluated only clinical scores and did not assess anterior laxity. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate postoperative anterior laxity by knee arthrometer and physical examination including Lachman and pivot shift tests after revision ACLR. We also compared postoperative anterior laxity between 1-stage and 2-stage revision ACLRs, hypothesizing that regardless of the revision ACLR technique (1-stage or 2-stage), proper indication and optimal tunnel creation could address sufficient knee stability without significant technique-dependent differences in postoperative anterior knee laxity.

Methods

Patients

This study was approved by the institutional review board and informed consent was obtained from each patient. This was a retrospective study of prospectively collected data. Our study subjects comprised 38 consecutive patients who underwent revision ACLR performed between August 2010 and March 2020 with a minimum of one year follow-up. We interviewed all patients with a failed ACLR to obtain a detailed medical history. They then underwent a clinical examination and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to determine the cause of graft failure. Indications for revision ACLR were (1) subjective instability or giving way during strenuous or daily activities, (2) positive Lachman and pivot shift tests, and (3) more than 3 mm side-to-side difference with KT-2000 knee arthrometer (MEDmetric, San Diego, CA, USA) at maximum manual force. Contraindications were as follows: (1) arthrofibrosis: range of motion (ROM) deficit, (2) deep infection, and (3) severe osteoarthritis.

Indications for 1-stage and 2- stage revision surgery

According to our previous report, indications for 2-stage surgery were as follows: 1) On the tibial side, either enlargement of the tunnel >15 mm in diameter, which equivalents to the tunnel aperture >20 mm, or overlapping of the existing tunnel with the optimal tunnel, with positioning more than a half tunnel diameter posterior to the optimal position7,13,14; 2) On the femoral side, enlargement of the tunnel >15 mm in diameter, which equivalents to the tunnel aperture >15 mm, with positioning more than a half tunnel diameter anterior to the optimal position13,14 The assessment of the previous tunnel weas performed before the revision surgery on computed tomography (CT) images and confirmed by arthroscopy at revision surgery tunnel. These indications were applied for all patients, including 21 patients who underwent 1-stage revision ACLR surgeries (hereafter, 1-stage patients) and 17 patients who underwent 2-stage revision ACLR surgeries (hereafter, 2-stage patients). Notably, we lost two 1-stage patients and one 2-stage patient to follow-up by 12 months. Our final evaluations were performed in 19 patients after 1-stage surgery and 16 patients after 2-stage surgery. Surgical technique and postoperative regimen

Prior to revision surgery, existing tunnel position and direction, fixation devices, and grafts in the primary ACLR were confirmed on CT and MRI images.

Surgery was performed with the patient under general anesthesia in a supine position. First, arthroscopic evaluation of transplanted grafts, the meniscus, and articular cartilage was performed (Table 2). Second, to confirm the relationship between both the tibial and femoral tunnel apertures with the bony landmarks around the femoral and tibial ACL attachment areas, we debrided the remnant ACL graft and any associated scar tissue. 1-Stage revision surgery

For cases of 1-stage revision ACLR, an anatomic rectangular tunnel (ART) ACLR with bone-patellar tendon-bone autograft (BPTB) was performed in all patients. The ART ACLR procedure employed in the present study was very similar to that used in our previous reports and originated from the surgical procedure developed by Shino et al..15, 16, 17 Ipsilateral BPTB was selected for patients who had undergone primary ACLR using the hamstrings, while contralateral BPTB autograft was selected for those who had undergone primary ACLR using ipsilateral BPTB.

The remaining bony landmarks after primary ACL reconstruction were arthroscopically identified, example for resident's ridge and posterior/proximal cartilage margins on femur17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25 and the medial intercondylar ridge, anterior ridge, anterior horn of the lateral meniscus, central intercondylar ridge, and intercondylar tubercle on tibia.17,19,22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27 Two 5- to 6-mm tunnels for anteromedial and posterolateral bundle were created and he bone bridge between them was removed to create the rectangular socket on femur and tunnels on tibia.

For graft preparation, a BTB graft was harvested from the central portion of the patellar tendon with bone plugs 10–15 mm in length on both ends. The smaller tibial and larger patellar bone plugs were used for the femoral and tibial side, respectively. The graft was inserted through the tibial tunnel to the femoral rectangular socket, and fixed with BTB Tightrope (Arthrex, Naples, Florida) to locate the tendon-bone junction just at the femoral socket aperture. The tibial side of the graft was fixed with the knee at 20° of flexion with a Double Spike Plate (Smith & Nephew Endoscopy, Andover, MA) at 20 N using the ligament tensioner mounted on the metal shell boot fixed to the tibia with a bandage.17,27,28

Rehabilitation after 1-stage revision ART ACLR in this study was performed according to previous reports from our group.15,16,29,30 The knee was immobilized for a week with a brace. ROM exercise was started at two weeks. Partial weight bearing was allowed at three weeks, and full weight bearing was started at five weeks. Running was allowed at four months, followed by return to previous sports activity at 9–12 months.

Tunnel apertures were neatly exposed after debridement of the transplanted graft or its remnants, and hardware was removed if necessary. Clinical evaluation

At final follow-up, clinical evaluation was performed with a physical examination, including ROM testing with a goniometer, Lachman test, pivot-shift test, and instrumented anterior knee laxity measurement under manual maximum load at 30° of flexion with the KT-2000 knee arthrometer. Knee stability measurements were taken under general anesthesia preoperatively and at 1 year postoperatively as outpatients, by two surgeons with more than 15 years of experience. During KT-2000 arthrometer examination, the patient lay on an surgical or examination bed with the knees flexed at 20–30°. This position is similar to that for the Lachman test to enhance relative tibial displacement. Using the right leg as an example, the device was first secured to the lower leg by a hook-and-loop fastener. The patellar side was secured by the left hand of the examiner, and the examiner's right hand applied tensile force to the device until 134 N was achieved, at which time an indicator light lit up.

Plain radiographs in this study included the weight-bearing view during flexion.31 Incidence of radiographic osteoarthritis at the tibiofemoral joint was also assessed using the Kellgren-Lawrence (K-L) grade at the final follow-up period.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois). Chi-square test and Mann-Whitney U test were used to evaluate differences between the two groups. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare the values of the two periods. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

According to one of the findings at revision ACLR (Table 2), a significant technique-dependent difference was noted only for existing tunnel position; specifically, more patients who underwent 2-stage surgery presented with non-anatomic existing tunnel. With the exception of this outcome, we found no significant differences in graft source (ipsilateral or contralateral), meniscus treatment, or chondral injury between the two techniques (1-stage versus 2-stage).

Graft failure

At final follow-up, traumatic graft re-tear was identified in two patients (10.5 %) with abnormal anterior laxity after 1-stage revision surgery, while non-traumatic graft failure was found in one patient (6.7 %) after 2-stage revision surgery. Range of motion

Loss of knee extension ≤5° was observed in a total of three patients (9.4 %): one 1-stage patient (5.9 %) and two 2-stage patients (13.3 %). Meanwhile loss of knee extension >5° was observed in only one 2-stage patient (6.7 %). Loss of flexion was ≤5° in a total of six patients (18.8 %): three patients each (17.6 % and 20 % for 1-stage and 2-stage surgery, respectively), while one 2-stage surgery patient (6.7 %) showed loss of flexion >5°; the latter was one of the patients with loss of extension >5°. We found no significant difference in loss of extension/flexion between 1-stage and 2-stage patients (Table 3). However, all three patients for whom the duration from the primary to revision surgery was longer than 20 years showed ROM limitation (Table 1). Moreover, loss of extension/flexion was found in a total of seven patients in this study. Mean duration from primary to revision ACLR in these seven patients was 12.9 ± 8.7 years (range: 3–23 years) while that in the remaining 25 patients was 5.0 ± 4.6 years (range: 1–15 years). There was a significant difference (p = 0.0035).

Anterior laxity

Lachman test was negative in all patients, while the pivot shift test showed glide in three patients both in 1-stage and 2-stage patients (Table 3). In all patients with gliding pivot shift test, contralateral healthy knee had also gliding pivot shift test. Totally, the pivot shift test showed either negative or equivalent results to those of the contralateral healthy knees in all patients.

The KT side-to-side differences in anterior laxity at manual maximum force after 1-stage and 2-stage surgeries were satisfactory without significant difference. After 1-stage or 2-stage revision ACLR, preoperative instrumented knee laxity showed significant improvement (p < 0.05). Moreover sixteen 1-stage patients (94 %) and twelve 2-stage patients (80 %) showing values between −1 mm and 2 mm (Fig. 5). Radiographic findings

At the final follow-up, we found no significant difference in K-L grade between 1-stage and 2-stage patients. However, two patients (both 2-stage) were found to be grade 3. The duration from primary to revision ACLR was over 20 years for both patients, who also exhibited ROM limitation.

Discussion

The primary finding of the present study was that anterior knee laxity as evaluated by knee arthrometer or physical examination, including Lachman and pivot shift tests, among patients who underwent 2-stage revision ACLR was equivalent to that among patients who underwent 1-stage revision ACLR. In addition, no significant procedure-dependent differences were noted with regard to limitations in postoperative ROM or radiographic findings.

Postoperative objective knee stability

We evaluated the postoperative knee stability by Lachman and pivot shift test, and instrumented anterior knee laxity measurement with KT-2000. Lachman test and pivot shift test are mainly used to diagnose ACL injuries and to evaluate the postoperative anterior laxity after ACLR. These tests are manually operated, which is influenced by subjective factors, including the experience of examiners and compliance of patients, leading to varying sensitivities and specificities. On the other hand, the KT-2000 is the most widely used device for measuring knee laxity quantitatively. This instrumented measurement had the wide range in the sensitivity and specificity, but it could be justified based on the quality of the conducted studies, the experience of the examiner and the amount of force being utilized in each test.32

Generally, clinical results including instrumented anterior knee laxity after 1-stage revision ACLR are inferior to those following primary ACLR.4, 5, 6,33 Thomas et al. reported clinical outcomes including instrumented anterior knee laxity measurements with KT-2000 after 2-stage revision ACLR that were equivalent to those after primary ACLR (1.36 vs 1.2 mm).11 However, no previous study has compared postoperative objective anterior knee laxity following 1-stage revision ACLR to that following 2-stage revision ACLR. Accordingly, the present study is the first to report an assessment of objective anterior knee laxity as evaluated by knee arthrometer or Lachman/pivot shift tests, following either 1-stage or 2-stage revision ACLR. In the present study, instrumented anterior knee laxity at the time of at least 12 months of follow-up was 0.9 ± 1.1 (−2 to 3 mm) after 2-stage revision and 0.9 mm ± 1.3 (−1 to 3 mm) after 1-stage revision. Two previous reports of revision ACLR that used a “rectangular” tunnel. Shino et al.34 demonstrated stability as assessed by KT-1000 of 1.0 ± 1.5 mm (1–4 mm) after 1-stage revision ART ACLR. In our previous report of 2-stage revision ART ACLR, stability as measured by KT-2000 was −0.4 mm ± 1.0, with a range of −2 to 1 mm.7 These results were considered satisfactory and similar to those of the present study. Moreover, several previous studies have reported instrumented laxity following conventional revision ACLR.35, 36, 37, 38 In these studies, 6–22 % of patients showed side-to-side differences in KT measurement greater than 5 mm,35,38 whereas none of the patients exhibited these following revision ART ACLR in our series.

In addition to instrumented laxity, anterior laxity as evaluated by Lachman/pivot shift tests was evident in our patients. Lachman test results were negative in all patients, regardless of the technique (1-stage or 2-stage revision ACLR). The pivot shift test showed either negative or equivalent results to those of the contralateral healthy knees of all patients in both groups. Moreover, none of the patients showed 2+ or 3+ pivot shifts; this was in contrast to previous studies of revision ACLR, which reported that approximately 20 % of patients showed a 2+ or 3+ pivot shift.35,38 ART ACLR even with a single bundle BTB enable a function like a double bundle reconstruction, by making the cross section of tunnel and bone plug rectangular. As a result, the postoperative instability indicated by Pivot Shift test, which is often reported after single bundle BTB ACL reconstruction, could be resolved after revision ART ACLR. It has been proven in the past report that the knee stability as measured by manual testing and instrumentation can be achieved in primary ART ACLR without additional extra-articular procedures.23 Both 1-stage and 2-stage revision ACLR procedures using a “rectangular tunnel” showed superior stability measured by physical examination as well as knee arthrometer. This could be attributed to the features of the rectangular tunnel revision ACLR, the easy placement of the anatomic tunnel without tunnel overlap, mimicking fibers of the native ACL, a snug fit of the bone plug in the tunnel, or biomechanical superiority.17,34

Limitation in range of motion

We observed no significant technique-dependent difference between 1-stage and 2-stage patients in terms of loss of extension/flexion. However, all three 2-stage patients for whom the duration from the primary to revision ACLR was longer than 20 years showed loss of extension/flexion. Mean duration from the primary to revision ACLR in patients with ROM limitation was significantly longer than that among the remaining patients. Moreover, a K-L classification of grade 3 was found for two patients, both of whom had undergone 2-stage revision surgery and whose duration from primary to revision surgery was longer than 20 years. Although the exact timepoint of ACL graft failure could not be determined, it is possible that these patients had experienced ACL deficiency for a long time, which could have led to degenerative changes and ROM limitations. Our findings suggest the need for reconsideration of surgical indications and postoperative rehabilitation protocols, particularly for patients whose duration from primary to revision ACLR is relatively long.

Graft failure after revision surgery

Graft failure occurred in two 1-stage patients (10.5 %) and in one 2-stage patient (6.7 %), but no significant technique-dependent difference was observed in graft failure rate between the two patient groups. A previous comparative study noted failure rates following 1-stage and 2-stage revision of 10.3 % and 6.1 %, respectively.8 Shino et al.34 reported a failure rate of 5.8 % following 1-stage revision ART ACLR. These results are comparable to our present results.

Some past reports have shown that the 1-stage was not worse than 2-stage surgery.12 Furthermore, some reports have shown that the instrumented anterior laxity after 1-stage revision surgery was over 5 mm.35,38 Currently, there is little evidence on the choice of surgical techniques and postoperative results after each surgical technique. This study showed no difference in postoperative outcomes between 1-stage and 2-stage revision surgery with restoration of knee stability with either technique. In other words, proper indications and optimal tunnel creation using the anatomic rectangular tunnel technique in revision ACLR could improve clinical outcomes with regard to postoperative objective anterior stability in 1-stage and 2-stage revision ACL reconstruction. Notably, cases involving a long duration (>20 years) from primary to revision ACLR require careful consideration of a patient's ROM limitations following revision ACLR.

Particularly for 2-stage revision ACL reconstruction, proper bone grafting, sufficient bone healing, and anatomic rectangular tunnel creation could improve the restoration of knee stability.

Limitation

This study has some limitations. First, the present study included some cases for which the follow-up period was less than two years. In these cases, degenerative change or late graft failure could not be detected. Therefore, typically, a minimum of two years of follow-up is required to assess clinical outcomes. That said, our data pertaining to objective knee stability collected at a minimum of 12 months postoperatively could prove to be very helpful for surgeons required to make decisions regarding revision ACLR, especially in terms of which surgery to choose, 1-stage or 2-stage revision surgery. However, there was no evaluation of subjective scores in this study and thus additional longer-term follow-up on clinical scores should be performed. Second, the sample size was small, with a wide patient age range. There is a concern about the statistical power to detect subtle but clinically relevant differences. Therefore, the number of cases should be increased in the future, for additional subgroup analyses or adjustment to eliminate variability in patient background as well as multivariate analysis. Third, there were no evaluation with subjective score. Subjective score is very important to compare 1-stage and 2-stage surgery, However, objective knee stability was one of the most reliable postoperative outcomes for judgement of failure. Moreover, follow-up period was only minimum 12-month in this study and thus it was not meaningful to evaluate the subjective score. Follow up for at least 2 years and subjective scores should be needed.

Notably, our study focused on a single surgical technique for revision ACLR (e.g., graft type, tunnel creation, fixation device, and postoperative rehabilitation protocol), in contrast to other previous studies, which evaluated a variety of graft types.8,35,38 This is a strength of the present study which might outweigh the weakness of a smaller sample size.

Conclusions

After a minimum of 12 months of follow-up, patients who underwent 1-stage and 2-stage revision ACLR acquired sufficient knee stability regardless of surgical technique, showing no other significant differences in clinical outcome.
