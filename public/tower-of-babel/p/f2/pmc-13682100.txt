Background

Electronic health record (EHR) systems are now ubiquitous in healthcare, with the aim of improving quality, safety, and efficiency of care delivery. However, a significant unintended consequence has been the dramatically increased clerical burden and “pajama time” for clinicians, who now spend twice as much time on documentation and desk work as on direct patient care [1]. This phenomenon is a key contributor to the growing epidemic of clinician burnout, characterized by emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and a low sense of personal accomplishment [2]. Burnout negatively impacts individual clinicians, patient care outcomes, and health system performance through decreased productivity, increased errors, poorer quality of care, and higher turnover [2, 3]. The increasing adoption of EHRs has consistently been identified as a major driver of burnout [4–6].

In response to these challenges, many healthcare organizations have implemented human medical scribes to work alongside clinicians and assist with real-time EHR documentation [7–9]. Some systematic reviews have demonstrated the benefits of medical scribes, including decreased documentation time, improved clinician satisfaction, enhanced productivity, and in some cases, better patient experience [10, 11]. However, the traditional human scribe model has limitations. Scribes require extensive training, have high turnover, and incur substantial labor costs, making the model difficult to scale or sustain [12]. Quality of documentation is also variable and may not capture the full clinical context [13].

Ambient AI-driven scribe technology has recently emerged as a promising solution to automatically generate clinical notes from patient-clinician encounters [14–20]. Using advanced natural language processing and machine learning techniques, ambient AI-scribes can “listen in” on conversations, intelligently extract relevant information, and produce structured documentation that captures the clinical decision-making process [21]. This has the potential to significantly reduce documentation time, allowing clinicians to focus on direct patient care and other cognitively demanding tasks. Early research has demonstrated the feasibility of AI-scribes to generate high-quality clinical notes more efficiently compared to standard documentation practices [9, 22–25]. Unique advantages include 24/7 availability, customizability to clinician preferences, scalability across settings, and lower long-term costs [21].

However, current understanding of AI-scribes’ effectiveness and impact on clinician experience remains limited. Most studies to date have been small feasibility or proof-of-concept pilots, with a focus on technical performance metrics rather than clinically meaningful outcomes [22]. The usability, workflow integration, and clinician acceptance of this novel technology have not been systematically examined. Concerns have also been raised about the “black box” nature of AI algorithms, potential for perpetuating biases or errors in decision-making, and lack of transparency to clinicians and patients [26]. Poorly implemented AI-scribes thus have the potential to erode clinician trust, exacerbate burnout, and compromise patient safety.

To inform real-world adoption and policy around AI-scribes, a comprehensive synthesis of the evidence on both effectiveness and end-user experience is needed. This systematic review aims to critically evaluate the current scientific literature on AI-driven scribe technology from the perspective of clinicians practicing in outpatient settings. We will identify key benefits and challenges of existing AI-scribe systems and generate recommendations for future development and implementation efforts. Our results will help to optimize the technology to meet the needs of clinicians, enhance clinical care delivery, and combat the growing documentation burden.

Methods

This systematic review will be conducted following the methodological standards outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [27]. This established framework provides guidance on all aspects of systematic review conduct, from protocol development through evidence synthesis and reporting.

Review question

This systematic review aims to answer the question: What is the effectiveness and user experience of AI-driven scribe technology for clinicians practicing in outpatient settings?

We will conduct a comprehensive literature search in the following electronic databases from inception to December 2024: PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, CINAHL, MEDLINE, The Cochrane Library, PsycINFO, and Scopus.

Each database was selected for its unique coverage: PubMed/MEDLINE for biomedical literature, Web of Science for citation tracking, Embase for international and pharmaceutical research, CINAHL for nursing perspectives, PsycINFO for user experience and psychological aspects, Scopus for its broad interdisciplinary coverage, and Cochrane for high-quality systematic reviews that may reference relevant primary studies.

In addition to searching electronic databases, we will also conduct a comprehensive search of gray literature sources. This will include searches of clinical trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform), preprint servers (medRxiv, bioRxiv), conference proceedings, and dissertations/theses databases. We will also consult with experts in the field to identify any ongoing or unpublished studies. Including gray literature will help ensure that our review captures the most up-to-date and complete evidence on AI-driven scribe technology.

To ensure literature saturation, we will also hand-search reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews, as well as consult experts in the field.

For studies that include both eligible and ineligible populations (e.g., a mix of outpatient and inpatient clinicians), we will include the study if results are reported separately for the eligible population. If results are not stratified, we will include the study if the eligible population constitutes the majority (>50%) of the sample. Similarly, for studies conducted in mixed settings, we will include the study if outpatient settings predominate or if results are reported separately for outpatient settings. If it is unclear whether eligibility criteria are met, we will contact study authors for clarification.

Studies focused solely on inpatient settings will be excluded in order to focus the review on the impact of AI-scribe technology in outpatient clinical documentation. Outpatient settings have distinct documentation workflows, requirements, and challenges compared to inpatient care.

Study selection

Two independent reviewers will screen all titles and abstracts identified from the searches against eligibility criteria. Full-text articles of potentially relevant studies will then be retrieved and reviewed in detail for inclusion. Any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer. Reasons for exclusion will be recorded at the full-text stage. To ensure transparency and reproducibility in the study selection process, we will implement a prioritized and sequential exclusion approach as recommended by Saif-Ur-Rahman et al. [28]. All outcomes will be collected as reported in the primary studies, with no restrictions on measurement tools or time points. We will document the specific instruments used, timing of assessments, and any modifications made by study authors. This comprehensive approach will enable us to synthesize evidence across diverse study designs while maintaining the integrity of both quantitative and qualitative findings.

Data extraction

A standardized data extraction form will be developed and piloted on a sample of included studies to ensure consistency and accuracy of data collection. Two reviewers will independently extract key study information including study characteristics (authors, year, country, funding source, design, aims, methods), participant characteristics (sample size, clinician type/specialty, practice setting, patient population), AI-scribe characteristics (commercial vs. research system, technical approach, training data, EHR integration, implementation processes), comparator details, effectiveness and user experience outcomes (documentation time, EHR usage, productivity, perceived workload, usability, note quality, error rates, acceptability, satisfaction, adoption), key findings (benefits, challenges, barriers, facilitators), and study conclusions (limitations, implications for practice). A full list of data extraction variables is provided in Appendix 1. Disagreements in extracted data will be resolved through consensus or involvement of a third reviewer. When necessary, study authors will be contacted for additional information.

Risk of bias assessment

Included studies will be critically appraised for risk of bias using standardized tools appropriate for each study design. For randomized controlled trials, we will use the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool, which assesses bias across five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of reported results [30]. Each domain will be judged as low risk, some concerns, or high risk, with an overall risk of bias assigned based on the highest rating.

For non-randomized studies, we will use the ROBINS-I tool (Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies of Interventions) [31]. This evaluates bias across seven domains: confounding, participant selection, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and selection of reported results. Each domain is rated as low, moderate, serious, or critical risk, with an overall judgment based on the severity of bias across domains.

For qualitative studies, we will use the CASP Qualitative Checklist to appraise the appropriateness of qualitative methodology, data collection and analysis techniques, consideration of researcher reflexivity, ethical issues, and clarity of reporting [32]. An overall assessment of study quality will be made based on whether the study sufficiently addressed each criterion.

For mixed methods studies, we will apply relevant quantitative and qualitative appraisal tools to each component of the study, then make an overall judgment on the quality of integration and meta-inferences drawn [33].

Two reviewers will independently assess risk of bias, with disagreements resolved through discussion or a third reviewer. Risk of bias findings will be presented in detailed tabular format, incorporated into narrative synthesis and conclusions, and used to guide interpretation and assess certainty of the body of evidence.

All stages of the review process will be managed using Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) for screening titles, abstracts, and full texts, as well as for data extraction and risk of bias assessment. Covidence was selected for its collaborative features, automated duplicate detection, and integration with systematic review workflows. References will be managed using EndNote 20 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA) for organizing citations, detecting duplicates across databases, and formatting the final bibliography. The combination of these tools will ensure efficient and reproducible study selection and data management processes.

Data synthesis

Given the anticipated heterogeneity in study designs, clinical contexts, AI-scribe characteristics, and outcome measures, we expect that a quantitative meta-analysis will not be possible for most outcomes. As such, our primary approach will be a narrative synthesis of the evidence, stratified by outcome category (effectiveness vs. user experience) and further grouped by specific measures within those categories.

Within each grouping, we will qualitatively compare and contrast findings across studies, with detailed results tables to summarize key study characteristics and outcomes. We will examine both consistency and discrepancies in findings, exploring potential explanatory factors such as differences in AI-scribe design, implementation processes, population/setting, and study quality. We will identify overarching themes, important contextual influences, and knowledge gaps. Where appropriate, we will present summary statistics and visualizations of quantitative outcomes.

If a sufficient number of studies with comparable effectiveness outcomes and AI-scribe designs are identified, we will consider a quantitative meta-analysis. Documentation time is considered the outcome most feasible for pooling based on prior literature. We would calculate standardized mean differences with 95% confidence intervals, using a random effects model given anticipated conceptual heterogeneity. We would assess statistical heterogeneity using forest plots and the I2 statistic, and explore sources of heterogeneity via subgroup analyses (e.g., by AI-scribe system) and meta-regression if power allows. If meta-analysis is pursued, sensitivity analyses excluding high risk of bias studies would be conducted. Publication bias would be assessed through funnel plots and Egger’s test if at least 10 studies are included.

For qualitative outcomes related to user experience, we will use a thematic synthesis approach adapted from Thomas and Harden [34]. This involves line-by-line coding of participant quotes and author interpretations from each study, organizing codes into descriptive themes, and generating higher-order analytical themes that go beyond the primary studies. We will compare and synthesize themes across studies to develop a comprehensive understanding of clinicians’ experiences and perspectives using AI-scribes, as well as barriers and facilitators to adoption. Confidence in qualitative findings will be assessed using the GRADE-CERQual approach [35].

Throughout the synthesis process, we will maintain a reflexive and iterative approach, regularly discussing as a team to question assumptions, incorporate emerging insights, and consider alternative explanations. We will evaluate the certainty of evidence for each finding using the GRADE approach [36]. This involves considering risk of bias, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness, and publication bias, as well as contextual factors such as how review findings apply to specific populations, settings, and healthcare decisions. Contextual factors to be considered will include patient values and preferences, resource requirements, acceptability, feasibility, and impact on health equity as they relate to implementation of AI-scribes in outpatient clinical settings. Findings will be presented in a structured summary of findings table with quality of evidence ratings.

Discussion

This systematic review aims to comprehensively synthesize the current evidence on the effectiveness and user experience of AI-driven scribe technology from the perspective of clinicians in outpatient settings. As adoption of AI-scribes accelerates, a critical examination of real-world benefits and challenges is urgently needed to guide implementation and policy efforts. Our results will provide an evidence base for healthcare organizations considering deploying AI-scribes and identify priority areas for future research and development.

Several prior reviews have examined the impacts of human scribes on clinician and patient outcomes [10, 11, 37]. However, to our knowledge, this will be the first systematic review focused specifically on ambient AI-powered scribe systems. We anticipate that by reducing documentation burden, AI-scribes could help alleviate burnout, improve job satisfaction, and increase time spent on direct patient care. Early studies have demonstrated the potential for AI-scribes to generate high-quality notes more efficiently compared to standard documentation [23–25]. However, current AI-scribe models may lack the intuition, adaptability, and contextual awareness of human scribes [22]. The “black box” nature of AI algorithms and potential for perpetuating bias are key concerns that could erode clinician trust [26]. Clinicians may also find AI-scribes unacceptable if they are inaccurate, lack customizability, or disrupt established workflows [18].

Our review will have several methodological strengths. The search strategy is comprehensive, including multiple electronic databases, gray literature sources, and expert consultation.

Our eligibility criteria are broad, encompassing various study designs, specialties, clinical contexts, and commercial and research AI-scribe systems. This will provide a holistic view of the evidence landscape. We will use rigorous, standardized tools to assess risk of bias and incorporate these assessments into our synthesis and conclusions. A thematic synthesis of qualitative findings will provide rich insights into clinician perspectives and experiences.

Limitations include the likelihood of a small, heterogeneous evidence base given the nascent stage of AI-scribe technology. This may preclude quantitative meta-analysis or limit the generalizability of findings. The rapidly evolving nature of AI-scribes means that studies of earlier-generation systems may not reflect current capabilities. Publication bias favoring positive results is possible, though we will search extensively for gray literature. Finally, most studies to date have been conducted in a few academic medical centers in the USA, potentially limiting external validity.

This systematic review will provide a timely and comprehensive evidence synthesis on the effectiveness and user experience of AI-driven scribe technology among clinicians. Our findings will inform real-world adoption, identify challenges to be addressed, and guide future research and development of AI-scribes to optimally support clinicians and enhance care delivery. As the first systematic review on this topic, our results will provide a foundation for ongoing research and policy efforts to realize the potential of AI-scribes in clinical practice.
