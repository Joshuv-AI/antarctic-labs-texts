Which calcium preparation should we use in paediatric resuscitation? A comprehensive review of basic physiology, pharmacokinetics, and clinical evidence
Authors: Constantinos Kanaris, Swathy Subhash, Anna Camporesi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe optimal choice between calcium gluconate (CaGN) and calcium chloride (CaCl₂) for hypocalcaemia correction during paediatric critical illness and resuscitation remains debated. This literature review aimed to compare the efficacy and safety profiles of both preparations to determine an evidence-based clinical preference.MethodsA comprehensive review of in vitro, in vivo, and clinical trials involving critically ill patients was conducted, focusing on the pharmacokinetics, therapeutic efficacy (measured by the rise in ionised calcium, iCa2⁺), and comparative adverse event profiles, particularly the risk of extravasation injury.ResultsBoth CaGN and CaCl₂ effectively correct hypocalcaemia when equivalent elemental calcium doses are administered. CaCl₂ holds a significant dosing advantage in volume-restricted or extreme emergent scenarios due to its higher concentration of elemental calcium (27.2 mg/mL vs. 9.0 mg/mL for 10% solutions). Whilst the hepatic metabolism requirement for CaGN has been widely refuted, limited paediatric data suggest CaCl₂ may yield a greater response in mean arterial pressure (MAP) in critically ill children. Crucially, the safety profile favours CaGN; a substantial body of evidence indicates that the risk and severity of tissue necrosis following extravasation are markedly higher with CaCl₂.ConclusionThe selection of a calcium preparation must be conditional and context-dependent. We recommend reserving CaCl₂ for central venous administration in volume-restricted or extreme emergent settings, whilst CaGN is the preferred choice for peripheral line administration or routine maintenance due to its superior safety profile.Keywords: Hypocalcaemia, Calcium chloride, Calcium gluconate, Resuscitation, Cardiac arrest, Paediatric critical care

Introduction: the critical role of calcium in paediatric cardiopulmonary function

Calcium (Ca2+) is the most abundant divalent cation in the body and a fundamental regulator of physiological function, with its role in myocardial contraction placing it centrally in resuscitation science. In the plasma, total Ca2+ concentration is meticulously maintained within a narrow physiological range of 2.1–2.6 mmol/L. The biologically active fraction is the ionised calcium (iCa2+), which is unbound, metabolically active, and responsible for all physiologic effects, with a normal concentration ranging approximately from 1.12 to 1.32 mmol/L.

Calcium homeostasis and the influence of critical illness

The delicate balance of Ca2+ is regulated by complex hormonal feedback loops involving parathyroid hormone (PTH), vitamin D, and calcitonin, which aim to maintain a constant extracellular Ca2+ level. Approximately 55% of the total plasma Ca2+ is chemically inactive, either protein-bound (primarily to albumin) or complexed with anionic buffers such as citrate and phosphate. The concentration of iCa2+ is critically modulated by factors frequently encountered in critical illness. Plasma pH is a major determinant, as Ca2+ and hydrogen ions (H +) compete for cation binding sites on plasma proteins, especially albumin. Thus, systemic acidosis increases the fraction of unbound, active iCa2+, whilst alkalosis decreases it. Furthermore, changes in albumin concentration affect total serum calcium. For instance, hypoalbuminemia results in a decrease in the bound fraction, leading to a lower reported total serum calcium (termed pseudohypocalcaemia), although the physiologically relevant iCa2+ level often remains normal due to intact hormonal compensation [1, 2].

Calcium in cardiac excitation–contraction coupling

As a vital second messenger, Ca2+ is indispensable in the initiation of excitation–contraction coupling in cardiac and vascular smooth muscles. There is a steep electrochemical gradient across the cell membrane, with intracellular Ca2+ concentration maintained at a very low level (about 0.1 mmol/L) compared to the extracellular concentration (about 2.5 mmol/L) [3]. Cardiac contractility is maintained by a precise, beat-to-beat rise in intracellular Ca2+. Upon electrical excitation, Ca2+ enters the myocardial cell during the plateau phase of the action potential via L-type (long-lasting) calcium channels in the cell membrane. This influx triggers the release of significantly larger amounts of Ca2+ from the internal storage units, the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR), which ultimately initiates myofibrillar contraction. Diastolic relaxation, on the other hand, is an energy-dependent process achieved when Ca2+ is actively returned to the SR, mediated by other mechanisms including the Na + -Ca2+ exchange system of the cell membrane and a sarcolemmal calcium ATPase [4].

The immature paediatric myocardium is structurally and physiologically distinct from the adult heart, demonstrating a greater dependence on the influx of extracellular calcium for optimal function, especially during stress or critical illness. This fundamental difference often forms the physiological rationale for using exogenous Ca2+ to improve myocardial contractility and reverse low cardiac output states in children [5–7].

The paradoxical role of calcium in cellular injury

The dual nature of Ca2+ is most evident in the mitochondria. Whilst Ca2+ sequestration by the mitochondrial membrane uniporter is essential for ATP production via the Kreb cycle under normal conditions, the same mechanism can promote mitochondrial Ca2+ overload at high cytosolic Ca2+ levels. This can result in an exponential rise in Ca2+ within the organelle, severely impairing ATP generation and eventually leading to the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) and triggering cell death. This paradoxical effect is central to the debate on exogenous calcium administration during cardiac arrest, where ischaemic injury has already compromised cellular Ca2 + regulation [8–10].

Methodology

We undertook a narrative literature review examining the physiological rationale, pharmacology, and clinical evidence comparing calcium chloride (CaCl₂) and calcium gluconate (CaGN) in paediatric resuscitation. The purpose was to contextualise the evidence underpinning current practice and the ongoing areas of controversy, rather than to provide an exhaustive synthesis or quantitative effect estimate. To improve transparency and reproducibility, we incorporated selected systematic review features (for example, a pre-specified search strategy and explicit inclusion criteria). However, this work was not designed or reported as a full systematic review; formal risk-of-bias assessment and meta-analysis were not performed. Findings should therefore be interpreted as a structured narrative synthesis of the available literature.

Literature search strategy

The search strategy used a structured, non-exhaustive approach to identify clinical, pharmacological, and physiological literature relevant to the administration of calcium salts in acute care, with a focus on paediatric resuscitation. The core search was undertaken in MEDLINE and Embase (via the Ovid interface) and the Cochrane Library, selected to provide broad coverage of biomedical research, emergency care practice, and evidence syntheses. Calcium formulations, pharmacokinetics, and equivalence

In clinical practice, calcium chloride (CaCl2) and calcium gluconate (CaGN) are the two main preparations used, and understanding their formulation differences is paramount to safe and effective dosing.

Compositional and dosing differences

The key clinical distinction between the two salts is the concentration of elemental calcium (Ca2+) delivered per unit volume. CaCl2(CaCl2⋅2H2O) contains 27.2% elemental Ca2+ by weight. Consequently, a commercially marketed 10% solution of CaCl2 delivers a high concentration of 27.2 mg/mL elemental Ca2+ (1.36 mEq/mL). In contrast, a commercially marketed 10% solution of CaGN contains only 9.0 mg/mL of elemental Ca2+. This difference means that the volume of CaGN administered must be approximately three times that of CaCl2 to deliver an equivalent therapeutic dose of elemental Ca2+. It is important to note, however, that the strength and formulation of calcium preparations available in European markets may vary across countries and brands [11].

The Resuscitation Council UK recommends a dose of 0.5 mL/kg of 10% CaGN [12], whilst the equivalent dose of 10% CaCl2 is approximately one-third of this volume. In an emergency setting where dose calculation must be rapid and the time for drug administration minimized, this three-fold difference in volume has significant logistical and clinical implications, particularly for small infants where even a modest difference in fluid bolus volume can impact cardiorespiratory status.

The refutation of hepatic metabolism

Historically, a major point of contention regarding CaGN was the postulate that it required first-pass metabolism in the liver to dissociate the gluconate molecule and release active iCa2+ into the circulation [13]. If true, this would have decisively favoured CaCl2 in situations of circulatory shock, liver failure, or any critical state demanding immediate Ca2+ availability. This theory, however, has been conclusively refuted: in vitro studies showed immediate and equivalent ionisation of Ca2⁺ from both salts.

The definitive evidence came from the landmark study by Martin et al. [14], who administered equivalent doses of CaCl2 and CaGN to patients during the anhepatic phase of liver transplantation. They demonstrated that both salts were equally effective at treating hypocalcaemia and raising iCa2+ levels, concluding that the absence of hepatic function did not impair the effectiveness of CaGN. This finding confirmed that the theoretical concern regarding the metabolic requirement of CaGN is unlikely to be clinically valid, validating the use of CaGN in critical care settings.

The enduring controversy: calcium use in cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR)

The history of Ca2+ use in cardiac resuscitation spans over a century, marked by enthusiastic adoption followed by a stringent re-evaluation that ultimately led to its restricted use in modern CPR guidelines.

Early enthusiasm and routine adoption

The first reported use of Ca2+ for cardiac resuscitation dates back to 1904 [15]. Its profound inotropic effect made it a compelling agent. In the 1930s, canine studies by Hooker and Kouwenhowen on electrical defibrillation were interpreted as supporting CaCl2 in restoring a heartbeat after successful defibrillation [16]. By the 1950s, Ca2+ was already recommended for refractory ventricular fibrillation, with reports such as that by Kay and Blalock [17] detailing “excellent outcomes” after direct left ventricular injection of CaCl2 in children during cardiac surgery. This momentum led to the routine recommendation of Ca2+ use in the first edition of the AHA Standards for CPR and Emergency Cardiac Care (ECC) in 1974 [18].

Evidence of harm and the restriction of use

A renewed interest in the 1980 s prompted prospective human and animal studies that dramatically altered the clinical perspective. Bull and Band [19] were one of the earliest critiques of routine calcium use during resuscitation, influencing subsequent guidelines (AHA/ILCOR) to recommend against indiscriminate calcium use in cardiac arrest. Studies by Stueven et al. (1983, 1985) on prehospital cardiac arrests demonstrated that the routine use of Ca2+ showed no difference in outcomes and, crucially, suggested worse outcomes when added to standard treatment for cardiac arrest, particularly in cases of refractory asystole and electromechanical dissociation [10, 20–24].

The physiological explanation for this detrimental effect focused on the concept of cytoplasmic Ca2+ accumulation as a final common pathway of cell death, particularly in the context of ischaemia–reperfusion injury. During severe ischaemia, cells lose their homeostatic control, allowing Ca2+ to enter passively through damaged cell membranes. This intracellular Ca2+ excess, coupled with the ATP depletion of ischaemia, cannot be effectively extruded. Upon reperfusion, this excess Ca2+ is taken up by mitochondria, consuming residual energy and halting ATP production, leading to mitochondrial Ca2+ overload. The result is activation of damaging enzymes (proteases and phospholipases), myocyte contracture, and subsequent cell death, thereby mitigating any initial inotropic benefit [4, 25].

Current guidelines and discrepancies in paediatric practice

By 2000, the AHA and ILCOR published guidelines severely limiting the use of Ca2+, reserving it only for specific circumstances: documented hypocalcaemia, hyperkalaemia, hypermagnaesemia, and calcium channel blocker overdose. It was explicitly designated a Class III recommendation (not useful and may cause harm) for routine use in cardiac arrest [26].

Despite these stringent recommendations, clinical registry data demonstrate a persistent discrepancy between evidence and practice. A retrospective review of 1477 consecutive in-hospital paediatric CPR events found that Ca2+ was administered in 45% of cases. Furthermore, its use, even after adjusting for confounding factors, was associated with decreased survival to discharge and no associated improvement in neurological outcomes [27]. A more recent and focused analysis in 2022 from the AHA registry confirmed these poor survival rates associated with Ca2+ administration in children with heart disease during in-hospital cardiac arrest [28]. Furthermore, national trends show that, in some settings, the administration of Ca2+ during cardiac arrest has either persisted or increased over time in both the paediatric and adult populations, reiterating the challenges in translating high-level evidence into uniform clinical practice [29, 30].

Pathophysiological states justifying conditional calcium use

The ILCOR guidelines delineate specific pathologies where the anticipated benefit of reversing hypocalcaemia or countering electrolytic toxicity outweighs the risk of Ca2+ overload. These states necessitate a detailed understanding of the clinical implications for choosing CaCl2 versus CaGN [31].

Hypocalcaemia in the critically ill child

Hypocalcaemia is a common and dangerous electrolyte derangement in critically ill children. Specific aetiologies are often life-threatening and demand immediate correction. These include the following:

Neonatal asphyxia: Neonates, particularly those who are asphyxiated, experience an abrupt fall in plasma Ca2+ post-birth due to the cessation of placental transport. This often leads to clinically significant hypocalcaemia requiring correction.

Electrolyte toxicity and channel blocker overdose

In cases of hyperkalaemia or hypermagnesaemia, Ca2+ does not alter the serum concentration of the toxic electrolyte but acts as a membrane stabiliser to antagonise their cardiotoxic effects on the myocardium, effectively raising the threshold for arrhythmia. This action is time-critical and requires rapid administration. Similarly, overdose of calcium channel blockers (CCBs) directly blocks the L-type Ca2+ channels, rendering the myocardium dependent on large doses of exogenous Ca2+ to restore contractility and systemic vascular resistance. In these scenarios, the rapid administration of a highly concentrated solution, characteristic of CaCl2, via central access is generally preferred.

Ionization and haemodynamic response

Early in vitro and animal studies, such as those by Cote [32] and Heining et al. [34], consistently demonstrated that when equimolar amounts were studied, the ionisation of Ca2+ was immediate and equivalent for both gluconate and chloride preparations. These studies provide important mechanistic insights but rely on surrogate physiological outcomes. As previously discussed, clinical and anhepatic data confirm equivalent ionisation of Ca2⁺ from both salts [14].

However, clinical trials involving patients in extremis offer a more crucial comparison. The prospective, randomised, double-blinded study by Broner et al. [33], which enrolled 37 hypocalcaemic critically ill children, remains the only paediatric randomised study directly comparing CaCl₂ and CaGN with haemodynamic endpoints. The study demonstrated that both salts significantly increased ionised calcium concentrations, with CaCl₂ associated with a greater peak increase in iCa2⁺ and a greater short-term rise in mean arterial pressure (MAP).

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. The sample size was small, limiting statistical power, and the patient population was heterogeneous with respect to underlying diagnoses and severity of illness. MAP was assessed as a short-term surrogate marker of cardiovascular response rather than a patient-centred outcome, and the study was not designed or powered to evaluate survival, neurological outcomes, or adverse effects. Consequently, whilst the findings suggest a physiological and haemodynamic difference between preparations, they cannot establish clinical superiority.

Osmolarity and chemical stability

Beyond elemental concentration, the chemical properties of the solutions affect vascular tolerance. CaCl2 is significantly more hyperosmolar than CaGN, a factor that contributes to its chemical irritation and potential for phlebitis and extravasation injury. CaGN is often preferred for continuous infusion due to its relative isotonicity and stability in most intravenous solutions [35].

Adverse event profile: extravasation, tissue necrosis, and compartment syndrome

The single most significant limiting factor for the choice of Ca2+ preparation is the risk of local injury and tissue necrosis upon extravasation from a peripheral vein.

The mechanism and pathology of calcinosis cutis

Extravasation of Ca2+ salts into subcutaneous tissues can lead to a severe reaction known as calcinosis cutis due to the precipitation of calcium phosphate. The dangers have been long established, with the phenomenon first described by Virchow in 1855 [36]. The effects include localised soft tissue calcification, necrosis, cellulitis, osteomyelitis, and, in severe cases, compartment syndrome. The formation of amorphous masses or diffuse subcutaneous plaques typically takes days to weeks to fully develop, appearing as firm, white-to-yellow nodules on the skin. The underlying mechanism is complex but involves local tissue destruction, likely due to the corrosiveness and hyperosmolarity of the solution, followed by the deposition of calcium in the damaged, alkaline tissue environment [37–42].

Comparative risk and paediatric vulnerability

Whilst CaCl2 is widely reported in the literature as the higher-risk salt due to its greater elemental calcium concentration and osmolality, CaGN is by no means benign. Numerous papers report significant skin necrosis, serious complications requiring surgical debridement, and even compartment syndrome following the extravasation of calcium gluconate.

The paediatric population, especially neonates and small infants, is particularly vulnerable to extravasation injury due to small, fragile vein size and the potential for delayed recognition of compartment syndrome. Chen et al. [38] documented a case of calcinosis cutis complicated by compartment syndrome following CaGN extravasation in a neonate, underscoring the severity of this complication.

This established and substantial body of literature regarding extravasation dictates a critical preference for CaGN for routine peripheral administration, restricting CaCl2 to central access or life-saving emergencies where the circulatory status necessitates the most rapid, concentrated dose.

Synthesis and clinical recommendation

The selection of a calcium preparation in paediatric resuscitation requires balancing the need for high-concentration, small-volume delivery against the risk of local tissue damage. Much of the available comparative evidence relates to biochemical correction of ionised calcium or short-term haemodynamic responses rather than patient-centred outcomes.

The choice of calcium salt in paediatric critical care is highly conditional.

Emergent, haemodynamically unstable correction (e.g., hyperkalaemia, CCB overdose, massive transfusion): CaCl2 is the preferred agent due to its high elemental concentration and small volume, especially when administering via secure central venous access. Limited paediatric evidence suggests a greater short-term MAP response with CaCl₂; however, this evidence is derived from a single small physiological study and should not be interpreted as evidence of improved survival or neurological outcome.

Conclusion and future directions

The choice between CaCl2 and CaGN must ultimately be a balanced decision based on an assessment of vascular access, volume status, and the urgency of the haemodynamic deficit. Whilst the routine use of Ca2⁺ in cardiac arrest is no longer recommended, the selection between CaCl2 and CaGN for its limited, conditional indications remains a critical point of clinical practice. CaCl2 offers a compelling advantage in concentration and volume for emergency, centrally administered doses, whereas CaGN offers a safer profile for peripheral administration and compatibility.

The paediatric clinical evidence directly comparing CaCl₂ and CaGN is sparse and largely historical. Observations suggesting haemodynamic superiority of CaCl₂ are based predominantly on a single small paediatric trial and short-term surrogate endpoints. As such, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of clinical benefit.

Very few contemporary studies have examined these agents in modern paediatric critical care practice. The absence of large, well-powered randomised trials comparing both efficacy and safety represents a significant gap in resuscitation science.
