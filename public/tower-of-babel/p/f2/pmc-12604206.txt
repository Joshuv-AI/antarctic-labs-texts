Development and validation of a novel nerve-related prognostic model for gastric cancer based on bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing data
Authors: Lupeng Qiu, Sheng Yao, Zizhong Yang, Zishan Zhou, Yang Fei, Xinyong Zhu, Feide Liu, Yixin Gong, Shuang Li, Minglu Liu, Xiao Zhao, Shunchang Jiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastric cancer (GC) is still imposing a severe threat to human health. An increasing number of studies have found that neural activity plays an important role in the tumor microenvironment. However, the clinical implications of nerve-related genes (NRGs) remain largely unexplored.MethodsMatched GC and adjacent normal tissue from 8 patients were collected for single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis. Gene expression and patient information were downloaded from TCGA and GEO databases. A total of 441 NRGs were collected from the KEGG database, and LASSO regression analysis was used to construct the nerve-related risk score (NRRS). The survival, immune microenvironment, and mutation analyses were then carried out. Finally, scRNA-seq analysis was used to analyze the distribution of NRGs in GC patients.ResultsWe enrolled 441 NRGs and analyzed the association between NRGs expression and overall survival (OS). Finally, 8 NRGs highly associated with OS were identified to construct the NRRS model. Patients with low NRRS had significantly longer OS compared with high NRRS patients. We then analyzed the distribution of gene mutation landscape, enrichment annotation and immune infiltration in different NRRS subtypes. Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a malignant tumor of digestive system with high incidence, and is also an important cause of tumor-related death [1]. Recent studies revealed that GC is the fifth most diagnosed malignancy and the third leading cause of tumor-related death globally [2]. Although death rates from GC have declined over the past decades, it remains a severe threat to human health. At present, surgery, chemotherapy and immunotherapy are the main treatment methods for GC, and the prognosis of most patients remains poor [3–5]. With the rapid development of sequencing technologies, it is necessary to stratify GC patients more accurately to better guide treatment strategies and improve patient prognosis [6, 7].

The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays an important part in tumor progression and drug resistance and has become potential therapeutic targets for various malignant tumors [8, 9]. In the process of exploration of the TME, immune, endothelial, and tumor cells have always been the focus of attention [10]. Recently, the interaction between nerve and tumor development has been increasingly explored. Tumor cells can induce axon formation, attract nerves and stimulate nerve growth by secreting neurotrophic factors, axon-guiding molecules and vascular endothelial growth factors [11, 12]. In turn, nerve fibers can penetrate into the TME, secrete neurotransmitters and neurotrophic factors, and stimulate tumor growth and metastasis [13]. In view of the wide range of gastric innervation, it is necessary to explore the role of nerve-related genes (NRGs) in GC progression.

NRGs can be classified into four major categories: classical neurotransmitter system, neuroactive molecules, neurotrophic factors and axon-guiding molecules. Several studies have revealed that tumor cells can utilize the signaling pathways initiated by neurotransmitters to activate their own proliferation and diffusion. Additionally, neurotransmitters can also influence immune and endothelial cells to promote cancer progression. Huang et al. found that dopamine can inhibit GC invasion and migration by inhibiting EGFR-AKT-MMP-13 pathway through the activation of D2R [14]. Zhang et al. indicated that the activation of ADRB2 signaling pathway induced by stress hormones plays a key part in the progression and metastasis of GC. The ADRB2 antagonist can inhibit tumor growth by inhibiting the ERK-JNK-MAPK pathway [15]. It has been documented that endothelial cells can promote angiogenesis in response to exogenous neurotransmitter stimulation through their neurotransmitter receptors [16]. Additionally, previous studies have also clarified the mechanisms of neuroactive molecules, neurotrophic factors and axon-guiding molecules in tumor growth and progression [17–19]. Overall, the role of NRGs in tumor biology needs to be further studied to provide new opportunities for cancer therapy.

In this study, we enrolled 441 NRGs and analyzed the association between NRGs expression and prognosis of GC. Finally, eight NRGs closely related to prognosis were identified to construct a nerve-related risk score (NRRS), and the predictive ability of NRRS was further verified. We then analyzed the differences in gene mutation landscape, enrichment annotation and immune infiltration between the two NRRS subtypes. Additionally, we explored the potential role of NRRS in predicting the efficacy of chemotherapy and immunotherapy drugs in GC patients. We also constructed a prognostic nomogram combining NRRS and other clinical indicators. This study may help optimize precision treatment strategies and further improve the prognosis of GC patients.

Materials and methods

Data collection and processing

Messenger RNA (mRNA) expression profiles of GC patients were accessed from the TCGA and GEO databases. In total, 2347 GC patients from 11 independent public datasets were ultimately included in this study. Among them, ten datasets (TCGA-STAD, GSE62254, GSE15459, GSE57303, GSE29272, GSE84437, GSE14210, GSE34942, GSE13861, and GSE26942) with complete OS information were used to construct and validate the NRRS model. Three datasets (GSE13861, GSE26942, and GSE26253) encompassing RFS information were employed to validate the predictive power of NRRS for recurrence.

RNA-sequencing data from the TCGA database was transformed into the form of TPM for subsequent analysis [20]. For the datasets from GEO database, the original data was acquired and standardized using the RMA with the “affy” R package [21]. Finally, the GEO-meta dataset was merged with GSE62254, GSE15459, and GSE57303 datasets, all belonging to the Affymetrix GPL570 platform. After correcting batch effects using the ComBat algorithm, PCA analysis was conducted to ensure the success of subsequent steps (Fig. S1). Gene expression from each patient sample was converted to a z-score for all datasets. A detailed description of the 11 enrolled datasets was summarized in Table S1.

We included eight GC patients who underwent radical surgical treatment at the Chinese PLA General Hospital between October 2023 and December 2023 for single-cell sequencing analysis. All patients have been histologically confirmed by the hematoxylin-eosin staining method. Detailed clinical characteristics of individual patients that included in the scRNA-seq analysis was provided in Table S2. The written consent of each patient has been provided, and the study was approved by the Chinese PLA General Hospital. Additionally, we also collected four public scRNA-seq datasets for the validation analysis (Table S3).

Construction of the NRRS model

To further elucidate the prognostic significance of 441 NRGs, OS-related NRGs were determined by univariate Cox regression analysis with P < 0.05 in both the TCGA-STAD and GEO-meta datasets. The overlapping genes were extracted for LASSO regression analysis, and 8 NRGs highly correlated with OS were selected to develop the nerve-related risk score.

DEG and functional enrichment analysis

The R package “limma” was employed for DEG analysis [22]. GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were carried out using the “clusterProfiler” R package [23]. Afterwards, GSEA analyses were used to interpret the potential biological characteristics of GC patients.

Immune landscape description

The ssGSEA implemented in the “GSVA” R package was carried out to infer the infiltration levels of 28 immune cells within each sample [24]. To ensure stability, five other algorithms, including TIMER, quanTIseq, MCP-counter, xCell, and EPIC, were also employed to examine the consequence of immune cell infiltration [25–28]. In addition, 65 immunomodulators, including MHC-class family, immune checkpoints family, and several other molecules were collected to analyze the immune landscape of two NRRS subtypes [29, 30].

Gene mutations landscape

Tumors with higher tumor mutation load (TMB) were considered more prone to present higher levels of neoantigen, which can help the immune system recognize tumor features [31]. Genomic events of 348 GC patients were analyzed using the “maftools” R package, and the waterfall plot was used to show the mutational frequencies of the top 20 genes [32]. Additionally, we also analyzed the relationship between NRRS and gene mutation frequencies in the TCGA-STAD dataset.

Efficacy of chemotherapy and immunotherapy

To explore the role of NRRS in predicting chemotherapy sensitivity, the ‘‘pRRophetic” package was employed to estimate the IC50 of several conventional chemotherapy agents, including 5-Fluorouracill, Docetaxel, Oxaliplatin, and Lapatinib [33]. The lower the IC50, the higher the sensitivity of these drugs.

The TIDE approach was employed to predict the immunotherapeutic response [34]. In addition, Submap analysis was also utilized to evaluate the response rate to immunotherapy. The two independent datasets (PRJEB25780 and IMvigor210) were used to analyze immunotherapy responses according to the same NRRS formula [35, 36].

Single-cell isolation and sequencing

The samples were stored and transported at 4 °C immediately after the surgical removal. The gastric cancer and para-cancerous tissue were cut into small pieces of 2–4 mm3 respectively, and the tissue pieces were added to the gentleMACS C Tubes with tweezers and weighed, with each tube not exceeding 1 g. Mix was prepared according to sample weight, and 5 ml was prepared for each 1 g sample: 4.7 ml RPMI 1640, 200µL Enzyme H, 100 µl Enzyme R, 25 µl Enzyme A. Add Mix to the gentleMACS C Tubes, open the gentleMACS Dissociator, and select the program h_tumor_01 to run. After the program is finished, incubate at 37 °C for 30 min with MACSmix™ Tube Rotator, open the gentleMACS Dissociator, and select the program h_tumor_02 to run. Remove the gentleMACS C Tubes, pass the contents through the cell sieve, then add 4 ml RPMI 1640 to clean the cell sieve, and collect them into a 50 ml centrifuge tube. Add 20 ml of RPMI 1640 diluted dissociation solution, centrifuge 300 g for 7 min, and then evaluate the cell number and activity after resuspension with PBS.

Single‑cell sequencing analysis

Raw data of scRNA-seq were processed by Cell Ranger (version 7.1.0) to obtain the feature-barcode unique molecular identifier (UMI) matrices. The human genome GRCh38 was used as reference in our study. The “Seurat” R package was used for quality control, filtering and clustering analysis [37]. Cells with genes expression less than 500 or greater than 6000 or >15% mitochondria genes were excluded. Batch effects were then corrected with the “harmony” package. The Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) analyses were used for cluster visualization, and annotated cell clusters as known biological cell types using typical marker genes.

Statistical analysis

All statistical tests were conducted using R 4.1.0 software. The NRRS was established according to the correlation between NRGs and survival. Then the LASSO regression analyses were used to select highly OS-related NRGs on the basis of the value of lambda.min [38]. The “survminer” R package was applied to draw survival curves, and forest plot was depicted using the “forestplot” R package. The C-indices were compared with the “compare” R packages. The nomogram was constructed to calculate risk score and predict the probabilities of OS [39]. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Development and validation of the nerve-related prognostic model

Initially, we identified 441 genes related to the nerve-related pathways from the KEGG database (Table S4). To identify prognostic NRGs, univariable survival analyses were carried out to select OS-related genes. The results showed that 49 and 195 NRGs were significantly associated with OS in TCGA-STAD and GEO-meta datasets, respectively (Fig. 1A and B). Eventually, 29 overlapping genes were included for subsequent analysis (Fig. 1C). Figure 1D and E showed the results of univariate analyses of 29 OS-related NRGs in the TCGA-STAD and GEO-meta dataset, respectively. Subsequently, 8 out of 29 NRGs highly correlated with OS were selected through LASSO regression analysis (Fig. 1F-H), and the risk score formula was established using the TCGA-STAD dataset (NRRS = AGT * 0.040 + EPHB3 * (−0.040) + GNAI1 * 0.018 + LPAR2 * (−0.102) + LRRC4C * 0.014 + NPY1R * 0.072 + NRP1 * 0.175 + SEMA6A * 0.006). Meanwhile, the expression levels of 8 NRGs were further conducted between normal and gastric cancer tissues (Fig. S2), as well as between high- and low-NRRS subtypes (Fig. S3).

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that patients with low NRRS had significantly longer OS compared with high NRRS patients in the training dataset TCGA-STAD (n = 348, P < 0.001), and similar results were also observed in the validation datasets GSE62254 (n = 300, P < 0.001), GSE15459 (n = 192, P < 0.001), GSE57303 (n = 70, P = 0.008), GSE13861 (n = 65, P < 0.001), GSE29272 (n = 126, P = 0.007), GSE84437 (n = 433, P = 0.015), GSE14210 (n = 123, P = 0.015), GSE26942 (n = 202, P < 0.001), GSE34942 (n = 56, P = 0.024), and GEO-meta dataset (n = 562, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A-E and Fig. S4A-F). Likewise, low NRRS was also correlated with a significantly longer RFS in three datasets GSE13861 (n = 65, P = 0.002), GSE26942 (n = 202, P < 0.001), and GSE26253 (n = 432, P < 0.001) (Fig. S4G-I). Multivariable survival analyses indicated that NRRS remains a significant risk factor for OS (Fig. 2F-J). Then, the alluvial diagram showed that low NRRS patients had a higher proportion of TNM stage I-II and alive outcome (Fig. 2K-O).

Evaluation of the NRRS model

The distribution of NRRS, survival status, and heatmap of 8 selected NRGs were visualized in Fig. 3A-C and Fig. S5A-B. PCA analysis also showed a high degree of differentiation between the two NRRS subtypes (Fig. 3D-F and Fig. S5C-D). We then assessed the prediction accuracy of NRRS using the ROC curves. The results illuminated the distribution of NRRS, with 1-, 3-, and 5-year AUCs of 0.774, 0.819, and 0.821 in TCGA-STAD; 0.803, 0.756, and 0.787 in GEO-meta; 0.784, 0.713, and 0.704 in GSE62254; 0.687, 0.749, and 0.791 in GSE15459; 0.775, 0.667, and 0.658 in GSE57303, respectively (Fig. 3G-I and Fig. S5E-F).

To further verify the stability of our NRRS model, the C-index analysis was conducted. The C-index was 0.734 (95% CI, 0.685–0.783), 0.704 (95% CI, 0.657–0.751), 0.747 (95% CI, 0.705–0.789), 0.637 (95% CI, 0.540–0.734), 0.683 (95% CI, 0.591–0.775), 0.623 (95% CI, 0.582–0.664), 0.699 (95% CI, 0.656–0.742), 0.603 (95% CI, 0.539–0.667), and 0.702 (95% CI, 0.685–0.783) in the nine datasets, respectively. As displayed in Fig. S6, NRRS tended to have superior performance than other clinical features. These results suggest that NRRS could serve as a stable prognostic indicator for survival prediction.

Construction of the prognostic nomogram

To develop a quantifiable model for patient prognosis prediction, the OS nomogram was created using the training dataset TCGA-STAD. The total points of GC patients can be used to predict individual survival of 12-, 24- and 36-month. As depicted in Fig. 4A, NRRS contributed most to the OS nomogram compared to other clinical features. ROC analysis was employed to evaluate the predictive value of nomogram, with 12-, 24-, and 36-month AUCs of 0.781, 0.811, and 0.776 in the nomogram model (Fig. 4B). It appeared that the calibration curves showed good consistency between predicted and observed outcomes (Fig. 4C). Moreover, decision curve analyses also demonstrated a greater net benefit compared with other factors (Fig. 4D).

Correlation analysis between NRRS and clinical characteristics

We then examined the correlation between NRRS and clinical characteristics in the TCGA-STAD and GEO-meta datasets. In the former, grade, stage, and survival status were significantly related to NRRS, whereas age and gender were not (Fig. S7A). Similarly, significant differences were also observed in the GEO-meta dataset, including lauren classification, stage, and survival status (Fig. S7B). Overall, the higher the value of NRRS, the worse the degree of clinical features, once again confirming the predictive value of NRRS.

Identification of biological characteristics

To investigate the biological characteristics of NRRS, DEG analysis was performed between the two NRRS subtypes. Among the DEGs, 4252 and 720 genes were up- and down-regulated, respectively (Fig. 5A). The top 20 genes with greatest differences were selected for gene network analysis using GeneMANIA. As shown in Fig. 5B, these genes were closely associated in terms of co-expression, co-localization, and interactions. We next performed three pathway analyses, GO, KEGG, and GSEA, to elucidate the potential biological functions. For the GO term analysis, DEGs were primarily enriched in response to collagen-containing extracellular matrix, cell growth, regulation of angiogenesis, axon development, neuronal cell body, etc. (Fig. 5C). KEGG analysis revealed that DEGs were closely correlated with PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, regulation of actin cytoskeleton, cell adhesion molecules, etc. (Fig. 5D).

As illustrated in Fig. 5E and G, GSEA analyses showed that high-risk group mainly acted on immune and neural pathways, including activation of immune response, axon guidance, neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, synapse organization. While the low-risk patients primarily enriched in the metabolism-related pathways, such as ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling, DNA replication initiation, base excision repair, homologous recombination (Fig. 5F and H). Together, integrated analysis of biological characteristics revealed that NRRS may correlate with immune response and tumor growth.

Immune infiltration analysis

Since the distribution of NRRS is closely associated with immune-related pathways, we hypothesized that there are differences in immune cell infiltration between the two NRRS subgroups. Using the NES generated by ssGSEA approach, the correlation between NRRS and immune infiltrate abundance was explored (Fig. 6A and B). It is evident from the figure that high-risk group showed a significantly higher infiltration level of immune cells (Fig. 6G and Fig. S8B). To verify that the consequence of immune cell infiltration was not affected by the analysis approach, five other algorithms, including TIMER, quanTIseq, MCP-counter, xCell, and EPIC, were employed to estimate the results of immune cell infiltration (Fig. S8A).

As illustrated in Fig. 6C-F and Fig. S8C, the NRRS was significantly correlated with several immune cells, such as macrophage, MDSC, mast cell, regulatory T cell, and so on. Estimation from the RNA-seq data was utilized to quantify the TME, and the results showed that estimate, immune, and stromal scores were lower in the low NRRS subtype, while the tumor purity was lower in the high NRRS subtype (Fig. 6H and Fig. S8D).

Afterwards, the expression of 65 immunomodulators was further analyzed within the two NRRS subtypes (Fig. 7A). It was found that high NRRS patients tended to express higher abundance of costimulatory and coinhibitory molecules (Fig. 7B). These results suggest that high NRRS patients may overexpress immune checkpoint molecules to evade host immune surveillance and promote resistance.

Tumors with higher TMB are more prone to present higher levels of neoantigen, which are thought to help the immune system recognize and stimulate anti-tumor T cell proliferation and response. Similarly, IPS is a scoring algorithm that can integrate different immunophenotypes. The higher the IPS score, the stronger the sample immunogenicity. For these reasons, we next evaluated whether there were differences in TMB and IPS z-score between the two NRRS subtypes. Notably, patients with low NRRS had significantly higher TMB and IPS scores (Fig. 7E and F). In addition, the NRRS was higher in patients with MSS/MSI-L than that in the MSI-H group (Fig. S9A). We further analyzed the relationship between NRRS and TMB based on MSI status (Fig. S9B), and the results demonstrated that NRRS was negatively correlated with TMB in patients with MSS/MSI-L.

Genomic alterations of the two NRRS phenotypes

To study the genomic characteristics of NRRS model, we visualized the mutation frequencies in the TCGA-STAD dataset. The waterfall plot depicts the top 20 genes with greatest frequent variants (Fig. 7C). Subsequently, we compared the frequency differences between the two NRRS subtypes. In total, patients with low NRRS displayed higher mutation frequency, especially in TTN, SYNE1, ZFHX4, PCLO, and KMT2D (Fig. 7D).

Predictive value of chemotherapy and immunotherapy benefits

To explore the application possibilities of NRRS in the individualized treatment of GC patients, we utilized the pRRophetic method for the predictive of chemotherapy sensitivity. The IC50 value of several conventional chemotherapy agents for each GC patient was calculated to compare the differences in the two NRRS subgroups. As shown in Fig. S9E-H, compared with high NRRS patients, low NRRS subgroup was more sensitive to several chemotherapy agents, including 5-fluorouracill, docetaxel, oxaliplatin, and lapatinib.

Additionally, previous analyses indicated that patients with low NRRS had higher TMB and IPS scores, suggesting that they might benefit more from immunotherapy. TIDE has proven to be a useful tool for evaluating immunotherapy efficacy. In our study, low NRRS patients had significantly lower TIDE scores, which indicated that they may be more sensitive to immunotherapy (Fig. 8A-D). Additionally, Submap analysis also confirmed that low NRRS patients had higher response rates to immunotherapy (Fig. S9C and D).

We next examined whether NRRS could predict immunotherapy response based on two independent datasets. In the PRJEB25780 dataset, 45 GC patients treated with PD-L1 inhibitors were included. The results demonstrated that the CR/PR group exhibited a lower NRRS than the SD/PD group (Fig. 8F), and 66.7% of patients in the CR/PR group had low NRRS (Fig. 8G). ROC curve illuminated that the predictive accuracy of NRRS was superior to MSI, EBV status, and combined positive score (Fig. 8H). In the IMvigor210 dataset, the two NRRS subtypes differ in the expression levels of 8 nerve-related genes (Fig. 8E). Furthermore, patients with low NRRS exhibited better response to immunotherapy (Fig. 8I and J). Survival curves indicated that low NRRS patients had statistically significant longer OS than patients with high NRRS (Fig. 8K).

Single-cell profiling of the NRRS signature in GC

We obtained single-cell transcriptomes for 55, 052 cells after filtering. UMAP cluster analysis revealed that the TME encompasses 7 cell clusters including T/NK, epithelial, myeloid, B, fibroblast, plasma, and endothelial cell (Fig. 9A). Cell clusters can be distinguished based on their similar expression patterns, while unique marker genes and functional characteristics can reflect the status of different cell subpopulations (Fig. 9B). The NRRS signature in GC tissues was significantly higher than that in normal tissues (Fig. 9C). Gene-expression analysis of 8 key nerve-related genes demonstrated variable expression patterns across different cell subpopulations (Fig. 9D and E). EPHB3 and LPAR2 were highly expressed in epithelial cells, while NRP1, GNAI1, and SEMA6A were highly expressed in endothelial cells. Additionally, the expression levels of NPY1R, AGT, and LRRC4C were relatively low in gastric cancer microenvironment. Cell-cell communication analysis showed that the signaling network responsible for intercellular communication includes the immune and nervous communication (Fig. S10). The results of enrichment analysis also showed an enrichment of signaling pathways related to neurotransmission (Fig. S11). We then validated our conclusions in four public scRNA-seq datasets and found similar results (Fig. S12).

Discussion

The tumor microenvironment has recently emerged as a critical component, contributing to tumor growth, proliferation and metastasis in human malignancies [40, 41]. Endothelial cells, immune cells and fibroblasts in the TME can facilitate tumor growth and evade immune responses through autocrine and paracrine mechanisms [42]. Therefore, many targeted drugs targeting the TME achieved this goal in cancer treatment with some success [43]. Recently, an increasing number of studies have found that neural activity plays a key part in the TME. GC has extensive innervation, and the role of nerves and nerve-related intracellular signaling pathways in GC has long been emphasized [44]. Therefore, we designed this study to investigate the role of NRGs in the occurrence and development of GC.

In this study, 441 NRGs were included, and the NRRS was developed on the basis of 8 selected NRGs. Our results showed that low NRRS was correlated with longer OS in GC patients. To test the stability of NRRS, the predictive power of NRRS was verified in independent datasets, and survival curves indicated that high NRRS patients had significantly shorter OS. As expected, the performance of NRRS was stable in independent datasets. Additionally, a nomogram based on NRRS and clinical indicators was created to predict survival of GC patients. Previously, several NRGs have been demonstrated to be correlated with progression of malignant tumors. Mu et al. investigated the expression of DRD2 in 84 paired GC and normal tissues, and analyzed its correlation with the prognosis of GC patients [45]. Survival analyses showed that increased DRD2 expression contributes to tumor metastasis and is associated with shorter survival time. Deng et al. found that high NRP1 expression is related to shorter OS of GC patients [46]. Wu et al. revealed that AGT is a component of the renin-angiotensin system, and patients with low AGT was correlated with longer OS in GC [47]. Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis indicated that the NRRS signature was significantly higher in GC tissues. EPHB3 and LPAR2 were highly expressed in epithelial cells, while NRP1, GNAI1, and SEMA6A were highly expressed in endothelial cells. Together, the studies above indicated that NRGs were closely correlated with the progression and survival of GC patients.

We also conducted a DEG analysis to study the biological characteristics and signaling pathways in different NRRS subtypes. Among the DEGs, the top 20 genes with largest differences were selected, and the interactions between genes were revealed in the expanded gene network. Subsequently, DEG analysis implied that high-risk group mainly acted on immune and neural pathways, while low-risk group primarily enriched in the metabolism-related pathways. Previous studies have reached similar conclusions, suggesting that NRGs have profound effects on the TME [48, 49]. Together, our results revealed that the two NRRS subtypes differ in the immune- and neural-related pathways. More detailed studies are needed to examine the correlation between nerve and immunity.

Since the distribution of NRRS is highly correlated with immune-related pathways, we assumed that there are differences in immune cell subtypes between different NRRS subtypes. It was found that high NRRS patients displayed a significantly higher infiltration abundance of immune cell types. Furthermore, high-risk group tended to express higher levels of costimulatory and coinhibitory molecules than the low-risk group. Additionally, we examined whether NRRS could predict immunotherapy response based on two independent datasets. Our results demonstrated that low-risk cases had longer OS, which suggests that NRRS may be a powerful indicator for response to immunotherapy. Multiple research have showed that neural activity plays a vital part in the TME. Recent in vitro studies have indicated that norepinephrine can activate tumor cells to secrete IL-6 and IL-8 through β2-adrenergic receptor signaling pathway [50, 51]. Similarly, Wong et al. found that increased peripheral adrenergic signaling can induce a decrease in proinflammatory TH1 cytokines and an increase in immunosuppressive TH2 cytokines [52]. Partecke et al. found that vagus nerve dissection significantly increases TNF levels, leading to increased TAM recruitment [53]. These studies suggest that neuro-immune interactions may help tumor cells modify the immune microenvironment to evade the immune response.

However, there are also limitations in this study. First, since this is a retrospective observational study using public datasets, we are limited as to how much information can we get. Second, the molecular mechanism of NRRS has not been completely determined in this study. Future experiments will be needed to validate the functional role of these NRGs. Third, the optimal cut-off for NRRS remains uncertain and needs to be further investigated. Thus, additional prospective multi-center studies are needed to confirm these findings. Despite these limitations, our study indicated that NRRS is a valuable prognostic marker for GC patients.

Conclusions

In summary, we developed a stable and robust NRRS model to predict survival in GC patients based on the expression of nerve-related genes. This study will contribute to an enhanced understanding of the distribution of gene mutation landscape, enrichment annotation and immune infiltration in different NRRS subtypes. The NRRS can help to identify GC patients who might benefit from chemotherapy and immunotherapy.
