Application of anterior sciatic nerve block with liposomal bupivacaine in lower leg fracture surgery: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Yue Li, Libin Zhao, Xinyi Shen, Bin Chai, Li Zhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative acute pain management remains a critical challenge in orthopedic surgery. This study compared the analgesic efficacy of liposomal bupivacaine versus conventional ropivacaine when administered via an ultrasound-guided anterior sciatic nerve block in patients undergoing lower leg fracture surgery.MethodsIn a randomized, double-blinded trial, eighty adult patients scheduled for elective open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) of lower leg fractures. Background

Lower leg fractures, particularly distal tibiofibular fractures (OTA/AO 43 type), frequently necessitate surgical intervention via open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) to achieve anatomical alignment and stability [1–5]. Despite surgical success, postoperative acute pain management remains a critical challenge, prompting widespread adoption of multimodal analgesia protocols combining patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) with peripheral nerve blocks [6]. The sciatic nerve’s anatomical trajectory [7] supports its blockade as a routine intervention for knee and lower limb surgeries [8]. While the traditional posterior approach under ultrasound guidance remains effective, it necessitates lateral positioning, which can aggravate discomfort in fracture patients during preparation. To resolve these challenges, we devised an optimized anterior technique employing a distinct sonographic landmark. Originally introduced by Beck in 1963 as a landmark-based technique [9], the anterior approach has evolved with the advent of ultrasound guidance. A comparative trial by Ota et al. demonstrated that the ultrasound-guided anterior approach—using the lesser trochanter as a key bony landmark for nerve localization—was as efficacious as the posterior subgluteal approach for knee surgery [10]. Our technique employs a distinct sonographic landmark: the interface between the semitendinosus muscle and the sciatic nerve in the proximal thigh. Under real-time ultrasound guidance, we target this specific muscle-nerve relationship rather than relying on deeper osseous structures for initial orientation. This direct visualization enhances accuracy across varied patient anatomies. Moreover, despite ropivacaine’s established safety [11], its limited duration (2–8 h) inadequately addresses prolonged postoperative pain in fracture cases. Liposomal bupivacaine circumvents this temporal gap via controlled-release mechanisms, where phospholipid bilayers encapsulate the drug to sustain therapeutic levels for up to 72 h [12]. Although liposomal bupivacaine has been extensively investigated in clinical practice [13–19], no studies have investigated its application in anterior sciatic nerve block for patients undergoing lower leg fracture surgery. This prospective randomized controlled trial aims to: Systematically assess whether the application of liposomal bupivacaine, administered via a standardized ultrasound-guided anterior approach to sciatic nerve block, can prolong analgesia and improve perioperative pain management compared to ropivacaine. We hypothesized that liposomal bupivacaine would provide superior prolonged analgesia compared to ropivacaine, as evidenced by a significantly lower longitudinal pain profile over the 72-hour postoperative period, which will be analyzed as the primary endpoint using Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE).

Methods

Ethical approval and study design

This trial was approved by the Ethics Committee of Lu’an Hospital (No. 2024LL003) and registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (http://www.chictr.org.cn, ChiCTR2400084397, date of registration: May 15, 2024). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

A total of 88 patients undergoing open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) for lower leg fractures at Lu’an People’s Hospital were enrolled and randomized into two groups between June 1, 2024, and December 31, 2024: Group LB (n = 44): Received ultrasound-guided anterior approach sciatic nerve block (SNB) with liposomal bupivacaine 20 mL (266 mg), Group R (n = 44): Received identical SNB technique with 0.5% ropivacaine 20 ml. Inclusion Criteria: (1) ASA physical status I-III ; (2) Age 18–65 years ; (3) BMI 18–30 kg/m² ; (4) Scheduled for elective lower leg fracture surgery ; (5) Voluntarily signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) Allergy to amide local anesthetics; (2) BMI > 30 kg/m²; (3) Severe cardiac/hepatic dysfunction (NYHA class > III, Child-Pugh B/C); (4) Chronic renal insufficiency or uncontrolled comorbidities; (5) History of substance abuse or chronic opioid use; (6) Uncontrolled hypertension (BP > 180/110 mmHg) or diabetes (HbA1c > 8%); (7) Pregnancy/lactation; (8) Neurological/psychiatric disorders impairing communication.

Patients were randomly assigned using a computer-generated permuted block randomization sequence (block size = 4) created with SPSS software (Version 27, IBM). Patients were randomized into two groups: Group LB and Group R. To ensure allocation concealment and blinding, the group assignment for each patient was placed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. An independent anesthesiologist uninvolved in the study opened the envelope and prepared the identical-appearing 20 mL formulation used for the block. The anesthesiologist performing the nerve blocks did not participate in study drug preparation or postoperative follow-up. The syringes were concealed by an opaque label during drug administration. The patient was placed in a supine position and could not view the syringe. The operating surgeon and the post‑anesthesia care unit (PACU) nursing staff were not involved in the pre‑operative block procedure. Therefore, the performing anesthesiologist, operating surgeon, PACU nursing team, patients, and outcome assessors were all blinded to group allocation.

Prior to the nerve block, patients received light sedation with an intravenous loading dose of dexmedetomidine (0.8 µg/kg) administered over 10 min, followed by a continuous infusion of 0.5 µg/kg/h, and a single bolus of sufentanil (5 µg) to alleviate anxiety and procedural discomfort. The level of sedation was titrated to achieve and maintain a Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) score of 0 (alert and calm) to -1 (drowsy, maintains eye contact). the ultrasound-guided anterior SNB was performed in supine position with hip abduction. Anterior sciatic nerve block was used in all patients preoperatively, group LB received 20 mL liposomal bupivacaine (266 mg); Group R received 20 mL 0.5% ropivacaine. No posterior approach blocks were performed in either group.

Patients were positioned supine with the affected hip slightly abducted. A low-frequency curvilinear ultrasound probe was placed on the proximal thigh of the affected limb, 10 cm distal to the inguinal crease (Fig. 1A). The probe orientation was adjusted to visualize the following anatomical structures in the transverse plane: femur, deep femoral artery and vein, quadriceps femoris muscle, biceps femoris muscle, adductor longus muscle, adductor brevis muscle, adductor magnus muscle, and semitendinosus muscles (Figs. 1B and 2A). Under real-time ultrasound guidance, the sciatic nerve was identified at the level of the proximal thigh, lying lateral to the semitendinosus muscle. The characteristic spatial relationship, where the oval semitendinosus muscle is positioned immediately medial to the hyperechoic sciatic nerve bundle, served as the primary landmark for needle advancement, its depth from the skin surface was typically approximately 8 cm in our study cohort, a 10-cm short-bevel nerve block needle was advanced from lateral to medial via a short-axis in-plane approach, with meticulous attention to vascular avoidance. For Group LB, 20 mL of liposomal bupivacaine (266 mg) was injected perineurally, whereas Group R received 20 mL of 0.5% ropivacaine (Fig. 2B). Sensory block was assessed after target sedation (RASS 0 to -1) was achieved and with the patient responsive to verbal commands. Sensory blockade was assessed via pinprick testing (22-gauge blunt needle) at 1-min intervals post-block until surgical incision. Testing ceased when analgesia (0/10 VAS) was confirmed in all four dermatomes: plantar surface (tibial nerve), dorsal foot (superficial peroneal nerve), posterolateral leg (sural nerve), and posterior thigh (posterior femoral cutaneous nerve). Patients failing to achieve complete blockade within 30 min would be excluded (none encountered).

Following confirmation of an adequate sensory block, patients were transferred to the operating room, where standard monitoring was applied, including electrocardiography (ECG), non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP), pulse oximetry (SpO₂), and bispectral index (BIS) monitoring. general anesthesia was induced. general anesthesia was induced with intravenous midazolam (0.02–0.05 mg·kg⁻¹), sufentanil (0.5–0.7 µg·kg⁻¹), etomidate (0.3–0.5 mg·kg⁻¹), and cisatracurium (0.2–0.25 mg·kg⁻¹). Following tracheal intubation, anesthesia was maintained with a continuous infusion of propofol (280–400 mg·h⁻¹), remifentanil (0.2–0.8 mg·h⁻¹), and cisatracurium (3–6 mg·h⁻¹), and by maintaining BIS values between 40 and 60. Supplemental sufentanil boluses (5–10 µg IV) were administered based on predefined hemodynamic triggers (Systolic blood pressure fluctuations exceeding 20% from baseline) or at the anesthesiologist’s discretion for clinical signs of nociception (e.g., tachycardia > 100 bpm). All perioperative medications and interventions, apart from the study drug (liposomal bupivacaine or ropivacaine), were standardized and administered identically to both groups according to the study protocol. Following surgical completion, patients were transferred to the PACU under continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring, where tracheal extubation was performed after meeting strict neurological recovery criteria. In the PACU, patients were connected to a standardized PCIA pump. The PCIA solution contained sufentanil 150 µg and flurbiprofen axetil 100 mg, diluted with 0.9% saline to a total volume of 150 mL. The pump was programmed with a basal infusion of 3 mL/h, a bolus dose of 2 mL, and a lockout interval of 20 min. Supplemental rescue analgesia with intravenous sufentanil 5 µg boluses was available in the PACU for breakthrough pain (VAS ≥ 4) before the PCIA became fully effective. Sufentanil was administered intravenously in 5-µg boluses every 30 min until VAS < 4. Total sufentanil consumption during the initial 2-hour PACU phase was recorded. Following transfer from the PACU, the PCIA pump remained the primary mode of analgesia on the ward for a standardized duration of 48 h. The PCIA protocol was consistent with that used in the PACU (basal infusion of 3 mL/h, bolus dose of 2 mL, lockout interval of 20 min). For the management of breakthrough pain on the ward, defined as a patient-reported VAS score ≥ 4 despite the basal infusion, the first-line intervention was for the patient to self-administer a PCIA bolus dose. All patients received standardized pre-operative education on the use of the PCIA device. Pain scores and the cumulative number of PCIA bolus demands were recorded by the blinded outcome assessors during their scheduled follow-up visits at 72 h postoperatively.

Measurement of outcomes

Outcome measures were analyzed for participants who received the allocated intervention and completed the surgical procedure. Outcome measures were systematically evaluated by a blinded independent researcher using standardized protocols. The primary outcome measure was the longitudinal profile of postoperative pain scores assessed using the VAS ( 0–10 cm) at predetermined intervals: 6 h (T1), 24 h (T2), 48 h (T3), and 72 h (T4) following surgery, with pain severity categorized as mild (0–3), moderate (4–7), or severe (8–10) based on functional impact criteria [20]. Secondary outcomes included perioperative opioid use, assessed as intraoperative sufentanil consumption (µg), PACU rescue(the number of patients requiring rescue sufentanil boluses during their PACU stay), PCIA demands(the number of demand attempts during PCIA). Postoperative motor function of the sciatic nerve was assessed by testing active ankle dorsiflexion and plantarflexion, recorded as ‘present’ or ‘absent’. Postoperative Hospital Stay, sensory block onset time, functional recovery measured by the Barthel Index [21], which assesses 10 domains of basic ADL (e.g., feeding, bathing, grooming, dressing, bowel/bladder control, toileting, chair/bed transfers, ambulation, stair climbing) with a total score ranging from 0 (completely dependent) to 100 (completely independent); and incidence of postoperative complications including nausea/vomiting, shivering, and respiratory depression. All outcome data were collected by an independent researcher blinded to group allocation.

Samples size calculation

Based on the preliminary pilot study, with a two-sided significance level (α) of 0.05, a statistical power of 80%, and accounting for a potential 10% dropout rate, the calculated sample size was 46 patients per group. To account for enrollment logistics, we ultimately aimed to recruit 48 patients per group, resulting in a total planned sample size of N = 96. This target aligns precisely with our final enrollment population.

Statistical analysis

The analysis was performed in the intention-to-treat (ITT) population, with missing data handled using multiple imputation. Baseline characteristics including demographic variables, ASA classification, and preoperative functional status were analyzed descriptively using means ± SD for normally distributed continuous variables (verified by Shapiro-Wilk test), frequencies/percentages for categorical data. and non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). The primary outcome was the longitudinal profile of postoperative pain scores assessed by the VAS at 6, 24, 48, and 72 h. For the primary analysis of this outcome, a GEE model with an independent working correlation structure was employed to analyze the effects of time, group, and their interaction. Between-group comparisons utilized independent samples t-test, GEE for non-normal data, and χ²/Fisher’s exact tests for categorical outcomes. All analyses were two-sided, with p-values < 0.05 deemed statistically significant. Statistical analysis was done using the SPSS version 27 (IBM Software).

Results

After screening 96 eligible candidates, 88 patients met the inclusion criteria and were randomized. All randomized patients (n = 88) were included in the final analysis according to the intention-to-treat principle. Among them, 80 patients received the allocated intervention and completed the study (Fig. 3). Baseline demographic characteristics, including age, sex distribution, ASA physical status, and body mass index, demonstrated comparable profiles between groups (p > 0.05; Table 1). A statistically significant difference was observed in operative duration (Group LB: 94.98 ± 35.55 min vs. Group R: 110.61 ± 21.68 min; p = 0.015), Subgroup analysis of fracture patterns using the OTA/AO classification system revealed comparable distribution between groups (p = 0.815), indicating fracture complexity did not account for the operative time discrepancy.

No significant intergroup differences were observed in total perioperative opioid utilization, including intraoperative sufentanil consumption, postoperative rescue doses in PACU, and the number of PCIA demand attempts. To control for potential confounding factors, we performed a multiple linear regression analysis that included operative duration, fracture type (with type C as the reference), and treatment group in the model. The results demonstrated that after adjusting for these variables, the group LB remained a significant independent predictor of both the 24-hour VAS score (β = -0.467, p < 0.001) and the Barthel Index (β = 0.361, p = 0.008). Neither operative duration nor fracture type were significantly associated with the outcomes(p > 0.05), indicating that the treatment effect is an independent factor influencing postoperative pain and recovery.

No loss of active ankle dorsiflexion or plantarflexion was observed in any patient in either group during the 72-hour postoperative follow-up. Complication analysis showed transient sensory abnormalities in 17 participants (Group LB: 22.73% vs. Group R: 15.91%), all resolving within 24 h. PONV rates were 11.36% (Group LB) and 6.82% (Group R). Serious adverse events included one delayed union requiring revision (Group LB) and one persistent thermohypesthesia with 50% symptom resolution by day 7 (Group R). No motor blockade or group differences in overall complication rates were observed. (Table 4).

Discussion

For patients with lower leg fractures, postoperative acute pain management remains a critical challenge. Although the ultrasound-guided anterior approach to sciatic nerve block offers practical advantages [10] and liposomal bupivacaine is increasingly used clinically, [22, 23] high-quality evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of liposomal bupivacaine administered via this route remains limited. Therefore, this randomized controlled trial aimed to evaluate LB in ultrasound-guided anterior approach sciatic nerve block for postoperative pain management following lower-leg fracture surgery.

This randomized controlled trial demonstrated that, in patients undergoing lower leg fracture surgery, an ultrasound-guided anterior sciatic nerve block with liposomal bupivacaine provided prolonged postoperative analgesia, associated with improved functional recovery at discharge and shorter hospital stay compared with ropivacaine. This is the first RCT to investigate the combination of the ultrasound-guided anterior sciatic nerve block with liposomal bupivacaine specifically for lower leg fracture surgery, addressing an evidence gap regarding the efficacy and safety of this long-acting formulation administered via this particular anatomical approach.

The longitudinal profile of postoperative VAS scores exhibited a distinct biphasic pattern. Although Group LB demonstrated higher pain scores at 6 h postoperatively, it achieved significantly lower scores at 24, 48, and 72 h. This pattern aligns with the known pharmacokinetics of liposomal bupivacaine, which is characterized by a delayed peak effect due to its sustained-release mechanism while maintaining extended plasma concentrations. [24] These observations are consistent with those reported by Hamilton et al., [25] who noted comparable or even inferior early analgesia with liposomal bupivacaine compared with conventional agents, likely attributable to its biphasic release properties. Therefore, the early analgesic gap highlights a clinically relevant consideration: to fully exploit the prolonged analgesic benefits of liposomal bupivacaine, a proactive supplemental strategy during the initial postoperative hours is warranted. This may include bridging with oral analgesics (acetaminophen or NSAIDs). For more comprehensive coverage, particularly in surgeries involving the lower limb, combining the sciatic nerve block with a femoral nerve block could be considered.

The observed reduction in postoperative hospital duration and the superior Barthel Index at discharge in Group LB are clinically meaningful secondary outcomes that likely stem from the improved quality of sustained analgesia. Better pain control during the postoperative period may facilitate earlier mobilization and participation in rehabilitation, thereby accelerating functional recovery. These findings align with previous studies by Cherian et al. [23] and Chughtai et al. [26], which highlighted LB’s efficacy in reducing opioid consumption and enhancing recovery in joint arthroplasty, suggesting its broad applicability in diverse surgical settings. Notably, these benefits were achieved without an increase in total perioperative opioid consumption or in the incidence of opioid-related adverse events, suggesting that liposomal bupivacaine can enhance recovery without exacerbating opioid-related risks.

The anterior approach utilized in this study simplified patient positioning and landmark identification, avoiding the need for lateral decubitus positioning required in traditional posterior approaches—a practical advantage in fracture patients. No motor blockade or serious nerve-related complications were observed, aligning with previous safety reports of sciatic nerve blocks in lower extremity surgery. [27] The transient sensory abnormalities reported were self-limiting and comparable between groups, reinforcing the procedural safety when performed under ultrasound guidance.

It is noteworthy that, despite comparable fracture complexity, a statistically significant difference in operative duration was observed between groups. However, multiple linear regression analysis indicated that the superior analgesia and functional recovery in the group LB were primarily attributable to the intervention itself.

The present findings have several limitations that warrant consideration. First, this was a single-center study, which may affect the generalizability of findings. Second, the sciatic nerve block was not combined with a femoral nerve block; given the dual innervation of the lower leg, a combined approach might have provided more comprehensive analgesia, particularly in the early postoperative period. Notably, a meta-analysis [28] demonstrates that combined nerve blocks significantly reduce postoperative resting pain scores, suggesting that a combined femoral-sciatic nerve block approach may achieve more comprehensive analgesia for patients with lower leg fractures. Third, this study did not include a formal cost-effectiveness analysis. While the observed reduction in hospital stay with liposomal bupivacaine suggests potential economic benefits, future studies incorporating detailed cost data are needed to fully evaluate its economic value. [29] Fourth, the follow-up period was limited to 72 h, precluding assessment of potential late complications or chronic neuropathic outcomes. Fifth, the exclusion of patients with a BMI > 30 kg/m² limits the generalizability of our findings, particularly as obesity represents a growing proportion of the surgical population. Further studies are needed to evaluate the feasibility of this anterior approach in obese patients. Additionally, the continuous administration of a potent opioid (sufentanil) via PCIA in the postoperative period may have attenuated the observable difference in pain scores attributable solely to the nerve block, thereby potentially obscuring the independent analgesic effect of the intervention.

The clinical utility of liposomal bupivacaine also remains a point of discussion. While its prolonged efficacy in major joint arthroplasty is established [13–15, 23, 26] our findings confirm that it provides superior prolonged analgesia compared to ropivacaine. However, several limitations of the present study must be acknowledged. Future high-quality randomized controlled trials focusing on lower leg fracture populations should consider conducting multicenter studies with enlarged sample sizes. These trials should incorporate broader patient populations, including those with higher BMI, and extend the observation period to assess long-term complications and safety. Regarding clinical application, further investigation is warranted to evaluate the efficacy and cost-effectiveness profile of combined nerve block regimens that incorporate liposomal bupivacaine.

Conclusions

Compared to ropivacaine, liposomal bupivacaine administered via the ultrasound-guided anterior sciatic nerve block approach provided superior postoperative analgesia with a prolonged duration, which was associated with improved functional recovery and reduced hospital stay in patients undergoing lower leg fracture surgery.
