YAP contributes to liver injury through mediating capillarization of liver sinusoidal endothelial cells via GATA6/eNOS signaling
Authors: Junjun Wang, Zhenyang Shen, Guangwen Chen, Weiming Dai, Zhu Mei, Bo Shen, Yuecheng Guo, Jianxiang Wang, Hanjing Zhangdi, Qingqing Zhang, Jiaqi Gao, Qichao Ge, Hui Zhou, Hui Dong, Lungen Lu, Xiaobo Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCapillarization of liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) is a central event in response to liver injury. In this study, we investigated the role of Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1, also known YAP) in LSEC capillarization and liver injury.MethodsYAP expression was assessed in liver samples from mice injured by CCl4 injection and DDC diet. EC-specific Yap1 conditional knockout mice (Yap1∆end) were generated by breeding Yap1fl/fl mice with Cdh5-CreERT2 mice. HA-PEI/siYap1 nanoparticles were applied to specifically inhibit YAP expression in LSECs.ResultsYAP was primarily expressed in LSECs, and its expression was elevated during liver fibrosis. EC-specific Yap1 deficiency significantly increased the fenestrae in LSECs and mitigated hepatocyte death and liver fibrosis. Overexpression of YAP in EC aggravated capillarization, hepatocyte death, and liver fibrosis. Mechanistically, YAP inhibited Gata6 transcription via binding to its promoter and thus resulted in LSEC capillarization. Overexpression of GATA6 in EC alleviated capillarization and liver fibrosis by activating Nos3 transcription. Moreover, specific delivery of HA-PEI-siYap1 nanoparticles to LSEC alleviated liver injury in mice.ConclusionsYAP-GATA6/eNOS signaling is essential in LSEC capillarization and subsequent hepatocyte death. Interventions targeting YAP in LSECs offer a promising strategy for the treatment of liver fibrosis.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11658-025-00798-4.

Background

Hepatocellular damage triggers the transformation of a variety of parenchymal and nonparenchymal cells into a pro-inflammatory state, leading to hepatic fibrosis ultimately in persistent chronic liver diseases [1]. Despite tremendous research efforts around the world, there is currently no approved drug for specific intervention in liver fibrosis, which is characterized by excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) in the liver [2]. In-depth understanding of the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying liver fibrosis is an urgent prerequisite for the development of new therapeutic strategies.

The key event in liver fibrosis is the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs). Other cell types in the liver, including liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs), macrophages, hepatocytes, and cholangiocytes, are also involved in the progression of fibrogenesis. LSEC account for ~90% of all endothelial cells in the liver [3]. As gatekeepers of the fibrotic process, fenestrated LSEC in healthy livers are responsible for maintaining the quiescent state of HSC [4]. LSEC capillarization, characterized by the formation of an organized basement membrane and the loss of fenestration, plays a critical role in the pathophysiology of various liver diseases, such as CCl4-induced liver fibrosis and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) [5–7]. LSEC dysfunction alters their physicochemical properties and contributes to the activation of HSC and Kupffer cells (KCs), which together promote the progression of liver fibrosis [6]. Besides, capillarization of LSEC impedes the exchange of oxygen and other molecules between hepatocytes and hepatic sinusoids, which ultimately results in the apoptosis and necrosis of hepatocytes [8, 9]. The release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and other factors by apoptotic or necroptotic hepatocytes would activate HSC subsequently [10, 11]. Capillarization of LSEC precedes the activation of HSC and triggers liver fibrosis [11–13]. Nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) is a critical enzyme that catalyzes the formation of nitric oxide (NO). The nitric oxide (NO)-dependent pathway is impaired during capillarization [14, 15], and therapeutic approaches targeting LSEC have been proposed to increase NO bioavailability, upregulate endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) levels, and/or ameliorate capillarization [10]. However, the role of LSECs in liver diseases, in particular in the molecular mechanisms underlying capillarization, remains largely unknown.

The Hippo pathway plays a central role in tissue and organ regeneration and wound healing [16]. The transcriptional regulators YAP and TAZ are essential components of the Hippo pathway and share conserved structural domains. The implementation of YAP/TAZ-mediated biological functions mostly relies on their binding to their cognate transcription factors, TEADs [17]. Dephosphorylated YAP/TAZ enters the nucleus and interacts with TEADs when the Hippo pathway is inactive, resulting in the activation of YAP/TAZ/TEAD target genes such as factors of the cellular communication network 1 (Ccn1, also called Cyr61) and 2 (Ccn2, also called Ctgf) [18]. In the process of liver fibrosis, YAP is regarded a key driver of HSC activation, and selective depletion of YAP in HSC can induce senescence and mitigate liver fibrosis [19, 20]. Moreover, YAP deficiency in hepatocytes alleviates CCl4-induced liver injury and fibrosis [21]. The role of YAP in KCs has also been investigated, suggesting that YAP upregulates the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and promotes the development of NASH [22]. In endothelial cells, YAP regulates angiogenesis during the maturation and maintenance of vascular barrier [23]. A recent study demonstrated that endothelial TAZ improves NO bioavailability by upregulating eNOS expression and acts as an inhibitor of LSEC capillarization and liver fibrosis [24]. However, it remains unclear whether YAP is involved in the regulation of fenestration and NO bioavailability in LSEC.

In the present study, we investigated the involvement of YAP in capillarization of LSEC and liver injury to unveil its potential molecular mechanisms and translational applications. Our findings suggest that YAP promotes capillarization of LSEC and liver injury through negative regulation of the GATA6/eNOS pathway. Therefore, interventions targeting YAP in LSECs may be a promising strategy for the treatment of liver fibrosis.

Materials and methods

Generation of EC-specific Yap1 conditional knockout mice

Yap1fl/fl mice on a C57BL/6 background [25], kindly provided by the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences, were crossed with Cdh5-CreERT2 mice to obtain Yap1fl/fl Cdh5-CreERT2 mice. To generate EC-specific Yap1 conditional knockout mice, tamoxifen (20 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich) was injected intraperitoneally once every 24 h for six consecutive days in 8-week-old male mice. All animals were kept in a specific pathogen-free environment, and all experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Shanghai General Hospital.

Mouse models and treatment

To induce liver fibrosis, 8-week-old male C57BL/J mice were injected intraperitoneally with 10% carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) diluted in corn oil at 5 μL/g or corn oil (n = 8 per group), three times per week, for 6 weeks. The animals were sacrificed 24 h after the last injection of CCl4. For 3,5-diethoxycarbony l-1,4-dihydrocollidine (DDC) (Trophic, China) diet-induced cholestatic liver injury, 8-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice were fed a diet containing 0.1% DDC or chow diet (n = 5 per group) for 3 weeks. To induce lipotoxic-associated liver injury, 8-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice were fed the choline-deficient, l-amino acid defined, high-fat diet (CDAHFD, A06071302, Research Diets Inc.) or chow diet (n = 5 per group) for 8 weeks.

To produce specific Yap1 overexpression in LSEC, mice were pretreated with 100 μL of adeno-associated virus 9 (AAV9)-Tie1-Yap1 or AAV9-Tie1-Control (1 × 1011 vg/mouse, Hanbio Tech, China) via tail vein. Similarly, LSEC-specific Gata6 overexpression was induced by injection of 100 μL of AAV9-Tie1-Gata6 or AAV9-Tie1-Control (1 × 1011 vg/mouse, Hanbio Tech, China) via the tail vein. CCl4 or DDC treatment was administered 3 weeks after transfection.

HA-PEI/siYap1 or negative control (siNC), produced by SunLipo NanoTech (Shanghai, China), was injected at a dose of 0.6 mg/kg via the tail vein every 3 days during the DDC diet period (n = 5 per group). All mice were euthanized under deep anesthesia, and liver tissues and serum were collected for further examination.

Isolation and culture of primary liver cells

Hepatocytes, KCs, HSCs, and LSECs were isolated according to previously described protocols, with minor changes [26, 27]. The liver was removed, minced, and digested using a Liver Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The dissociated tissue was passed through a 100-μm mesh to obtain a single-cell suspension. The hepatocytes were then removed by low-speed centrifugation at 50 × g for 2 min. The supernatant was centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min to collect nonparenchymal cells (NPCs). The NPC pellet was resuspended in Dulbecco's modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Gibco, China) and gently added to Percoll (Yeasen, China) gradient buffer (50% and 25% Percoll). After centrifugation at 900 × g for 20 min, LSECs and KCs were collected at the interface between 50% and 25% Percoll layers. The HSCs were collected from the layer between 25% Percoll and DMEM. LSEC were purified by magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) with the CD146 antibody (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) [5, 28]. MACS-enriched LSECs were stained for PE-CD146 and AmCyan antibodies listed in Supplementary Table S1. Dead cells were excluded by AmCyan staining. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S1A, the viability of isolated primary LSECs was determined by the percentage of AmCyan+ cells, and the purity was determined by the percentage of CD146+ cells using flow cytometry analysis. Subsequently, LSECs were transferred to a new collagen-coated plate and cultured in an incubator for 2 h until further treatment or examination.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied in the comparison between or among groups as appropriate. The least significant difference (LSD) test was used for multiple comparisons following significant one-way ANOVA results. Normality and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene’s test respectively. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Elevated expression of YAP in the fibrotic liver

By analyzing our own scRNA-seq data published previously [29], we find that Yap1 is primarily expressed in LSECs, while it is also expressed in HSCs and macrophage (Fig. 1A, B). To validate these findings, we then isolated liver primary hepatocytes, HSCs, KCs, and LSECs and examined the expression level of YAP using Western blotting. As shown in Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. S1C–G, the expression level of YAP in LSECs is significantly higher than in hepatocytes, cholangiocytes, HSCs, and KCs. These results indicate that YAP is primarily expressed in LSECs in the liver. Western blotting showed that YAP expression was increased significantly in primary LSECs isolated from CCl4-treated mice, while eNOS expression was decreased (Fig. 1D). Then, the hepatic expression of YAP was examined in two different mouse models of liver fibrosis induced by CCl4 and DDC. Compared with the control group, the expression of YAP in LSEC was elevated along with that of the LSEC capillarization marker CD31 in the fibrotic livers of CCl4-/DDC-treated mice (Fig. 1E, G). Next, murine primary LSEC were isolated from both the control group and CCl4-/DDC-treated mice. EC-specific Yap1 deficiency ameliorated capillarization in mice

To further investigate the causal relationship between the expression of Yap1 and LSEC capillarization, LSEC-specific Yap1 conditional knockout mice (Yap1∆end) were treated by CCl4 or DDC (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. S2A). The efficiency of Yap1 knockout was verified in isolated primary LSECs by western blotting (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. S2B). Compared with Yap1fl/fl mice, the expression of YAP and its target genes Ctgf and Cyr61 were reduced in the primary LSEC of Yap1∆end mice treated with CCl4/DDC (Fig. 2C, D, Supplementary Fig. S2C, D). Importantly, liver fibrosis was significantly alleviated in CCl4-/DDC-treated Yap1∆end mice, as indicated by Masson and Sirius red staining of murine liver tissue (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Fig. S2E). Notably, compared with Yap1fl/fl mice, the number of fenestrae and porosity increased significantly in the liver of CCl4-treated Yap1∆end mice (Fig. 2F). Furthermore, the expression of lymphatic vessel endothelial receptor 1 (LYVE1), a marker for fenestrated LSEC, also increased, while capillarized LSEC markers, such as CD31 and CD34 decreased significantly in CCl/DDC-treated Yap1Δend mice (Fig. 2G, H, Supplementary Fig. S2F, G). Additionally, hepatic expression of capillarized LSEC markers genes, including platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule (Pecam1) and von Willebrand factor (vWF), were lower, while the expression of fenestrated LSEC marker genes, including nitric oxide synthase 3 (Nos3), stabilin-1 (Stab1), Stab2, and lymphatic vessel endothelial hyaluronan receptor 1 (Lyve1), were higher in CCl-treated Yap1Δend mice (Fig. 2I). In addition, we established another liver injury model that was induced by the CDAHFD diet in Yap1∆end mice (Supplementary Fig. S3A). As expected, the expression of Yap1 and its target genes Ctgf and Cyr61 decreased in primary LSECs isolated from Yap1∆end mice (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Histopathological evaluation of liver tissues indicated that NASH activity scores (NAS), lipid accumulation, and collagen deposition were reduced in Yap1∆end mice, suggesting that both steatosis and liver injury were alleviated (Supplementary Fig. S3C). Furthermore, serum ALT and total cholesterol also decreased significantly in Yap1∆end mice (Supplementary Fig. S3D). Besides, genes related to fibrogenesis (Col1a1 and Acta2) and fatty acid synthesis and transport (Srebp1c, Acc1, Fasn, Scd1, and Cd36) were significantly downregulated in Yap1∆end mice (SupplementaryFig. S3E). Taken together, these data demonstrate that LSEC-specific Yap1 deficiency ameliorate LSEC capillarization in mice.

Overexpression of YAP in LSECs aggravated capillarization in mice

To explore whether elevated expression of YAP in LSECs contributes to the aggravation of LSEC capillarization, mice were injected with AAV9-Tie1-Yap1 at 3 weeks prior to CCl4 treatment (Fig. 3A). As expected, YAP was upregulated in primary LSEC isolated from CCl4-treated mice injected with AAV9-Tie1-Yap1 (Fig. 3B). Consequently, the expression of Ctgf and Cyr61 also increased (Fig. 3C). In particular, liver fibrosis was significantly aggravated in mice overexpressing YAP, as indicated by Masson's and Sirius Red staining of murine liver tissue (Fig. 3D). Note that, compared with control group, the number of fenestrae and porosity decreased significantly in the liver of AAV9-Tie1-Yap1 mice (Fig. 3E). Furthermore, hepatic expression of vWF, Cd34/CD34 and Pecam1/CD31 were higher, while the expression of Nos3, Plvap, Stab1, Stab2, and Lyve1/LYVE1 were lower in AAV9-Tie1-Yap1 mice (Fig. 3F–H). YAP regulated LSEC capillarization through the GATA6/eNOS signaling pathway

Primary LSECs would undergo spontaneous capillarization, characterized by defenestration, after 2 days of culture in vitro [30]. Consistently, we observed that the expression of fenestrae LSEC markers such as Plvap, Stab1, Stab2, Lyve1, and Nos3 and the level of p-eNOS were downregulated, accompanied with a decrease in the level of phosphorylated YAP (p-YAP) and an increase in the levels of TEADs, Ctgf, and Cyr61 in isolated mouse primary LSECs cultured for 2 days, implying that the nuclear entry of YAP increased and the NO-dependent pathway was inhibited in capillarized LSECs (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. S4A–C). Accordingly, when Yap1 expression was knocked down by siYap1, the expression of CD31, Ctgf, and Cyr61 decreased, and that of Nos3, Plvap and the level of p-eNOS increased significantly (Fig. 4B, C, Supplementary Fig. S4D). Importantly, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) showed that the number of fenestrae and porosity in mouse primary LSEC were significantly reduced after 2 days of culture, which could be partially rescued by downregulating Yap1 expression, suggesting that Yap1 might play a role in the promotion of LSEC capillarization (Fig. 4D). Previous studies showed that tubular formation and migration of LSECs would be altered upon capillarization [31, 32]. The capillarization marker CD31 and CD34 were downregulated when YAP1 was knocked down, whereas the overexpression of YAP1 increased the expression of CD31 (Fig. 4E, F). The number of hepatic CD34+ cells was positively correlated with liver fibrosis [33]. Besides, as shown in Fig. 4G–J, overexpression of YAP1 in the human liver endothelial cell line TMNK-1 promoted tube formation and migration, whereas YAP1 knockdown led to reduced tube formation and migration capacity. In addition, we also observed that Nos3 expression decreased when Gata6 was removed from TMNK-1 cells, accompanied by enhanced tube formation and migration (Fig. 5H, Supplementary Fig. S5A, C, D). When Gata6 was overexpressed in TMNK-1 cells, the expression of Nos3 increased, while tube formation and migration were reduced (Fig. 5I, Supplementary Fig. S5B, E, F). Next, reconstructed plasmids including p-WT, p-∆P1, p-∆P2, and p-∆P3 were generated to identify the GATA6 binding motif in the Nos3 promoter region using dual-luciferase reporter assay (Supplementary Fig. S5G). The activity of luciferase was determined after co-transfecting siGATA6 with p-WT, p-∆P1, p-∆P2, or p-∆P3, respectively, into HEK293T. However, in the cases of p-∆P1 and p-∆P3, the controls (siNC) exhibited similar luciferase activities to those of siGATA6, implying that deletion of the P1 or P3 motif was sufficient to prevent binding of GATA6 to the Nos3 promoter regardless of the expression of GATA6 (Fig. 5K). Targeted delivery of siYap1 to LSECs mediated by HA-PEI nanoparticles alleviated liver fibrosis in mice

Previous studies showed that HA-PEI nanoparticles could efficiently and precisely deliver nucleic acids to LSECs [35, 36]. In this study, we first injected Cy7-labeled HA-PEI/FITC nanoparticles into healthy mice through the tail vein and observed their time-dependent distribution in major organs. HA-PEI nanoparticles were mostly enriched in the lung at 3 h post-injection because they entered the lung first through the pulmonary circulation before being sent to other organs (Supplementary Fig. S9A–C). More fluorescence retention was observed in the liver than in any of the other major organs at 12, 24, or 36 h after administration, indicating that the HA-PEI nanoparticles were distributed mainly in the liver.

Next, HA-PEI/siYap1 nanoparticles were injected into DDC-treated mice at a dose of 0.6 mg/kg to specifically inhibit YAP expression in LSEC in fibrotic livers (Supplementary Fig. S9D). As shown in Supplementary Fig. S9E, YAP expression was significantly downregulated by the administration of HA-PEI/siYap1 nanoparticles. Liver injury and LSEC capillarization induced by DDC were alleviated, as indicated by the shrinkage of Sirius Red- and Masson-positive areas, decreased levels of AST and ALT, lower expression of F4/80, α-SMA, CD34, and CD31, and higher expression of LYVE1 in murine liver tissues (Fig. 8A–D). The expression of Pecam1, Cd34, vWF, Acta2, and Col1a1 was downregulated while that of Nos3, Plvap, and Stab2 was upregulated in the liver injected with HA-PEI/siYap1 nanoparticles (Fig. 8E, F). Discussion

The role of LSECs in liver diseases has drawn much attention in recent years, as dysregulation of LSECs promotes liver inflammation and fibrosis, microvascular thrombosis, and portal hypertension [37]. Capillarized LSECs, which lack the functions of LSECs with normal fenestrae, drive HSC activation and liver fibrosis. Pharmacological therapy that restores the fenestration of LSECs promotes the reversion of activated HSCs to quiescence and prevents the progression of fibrosis in rat models [13]. Our results indicated that YAP was primarily expressed in LSECs and its expression was elevated in injured mice. In the present study, we investigated the critical role of YAP in LSEC capillarization and liver fibrosis at different stages in mouse models treated with CCl₄, DDC, and CDAHFD, respectively. Our findings indicated that EC-specific Yap1 deficiency ameliorated LSEC capillarization and liver fibrosis in all the three mouse models, suggesting that YAP might be involved in liver fibrosis regardless of its severity. Thus, the application of targeted delivery of siYap1 to LSECs would be promising in the treatment of liver fibrosis.

YAP/TAZ is closely involved in fibrogenesis in organs, including the heart, lungs, liver, kidneys, and skin, mainly by promoting myofibroblast differentiation [38]. YAP is also known to play a profibrotic role in hepatocytes, KCs, and HSCs in the liver [19–22]. YAP in LSECs was also reported to be involved in liver fibrosis, either through activating transforming growth factor-beta signaling or promote the secretion of inflammatory factors [39, 40]. Our data indicated that liver fibrosis was significantly improved in LSEC-specific Yap1 conditional knockout mice after treatment with CCl4, DDC, or CDAHFD, whereas it was aggravated when Yap1 was overexpressed in LSECs, suggesting that YAP in LSECs also participates in the promotion of liver fibrosis. Therefore, YAP may be essential for hepatic fibrosis, regardless of its cellular origin. The biological effects of YAP and TAZ generally overlap with each other [41]; however, distinct functions of the two paralogs have been revealed in recent studies [42–44]. Similarly, our findings indicate that YAP promotes capillarization of LSECs and liver fibrosis, whereas endothelial TAZ functions as an inhibitor of capillarization and liver fibrosis of LSECs through NO production, implying that there could be a division of labor between YAP and TAZ in LSECs [24].

eNOS plays a protective role in maintaining vascular tone and regulating blood pressure by producing NO [14, 45]. Activation of endothelial Notch leads to decreased fenestration and increased liver fibrosis in an eNOS-dependent manner [46]. It has been reported that YAP induce the gene expression of Notch receptors and/or ligands and cooperate with the Notch intracellular domain (NICD) to regulate target genes concomitantly [47]. Consistently, we found that the expression of Notch receptors (Notch 4), Notch ligands (Jag1, Jag2, and Dll4), and Notch target genes (Hes1 and Hey1) was downregulated when Yap1 was silenced in primary LSECs (Fig. 5B). Notably, our data showed that the expression of YAP in LSECs was negatively correlated with that of eNOS both in vitro and in vivo, suggesting that YAP promotes LSEC capillarization and liver fibrogenesis by downregulating eNOS. Hence, our results suggest that, in addition to Notch signaling, the GATA6/eNOS pathway is also regulated by YAP.

In this study, GATA6, a transcription factor that binds directly to the promoter of Nos3 [34], was identified as the target of YAP. We find that Gata6 expression is upregulated when Yap1 was knocked out, which is consistent with the results of a recent study on the relationship between GATA6 and YAP in the human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cell line [48]. Importantly, our results showed that YAP negatively regulates Gata6 expression at the transcriptional level by directly binding to its promoter region, while GATA6 positively regulate eNOS mRNA expression by binding to the motif that lies in the promoter region (from −213 to −200 bp) of the Nos3 gene. GATA6 is critical for embryonic development of the liver, as well as the function and survival of endothelial cells [49, 50]. Endothelial GATA6 deficiency results in pulmonary arterial endothelial cell dysfunction and disease progression in pulmonary arterial hypertension [34]. Genetic deletion of GATA4 in LSECs, another member of the GATA family of transcription factors, is sufficient to cause liver fibrosis [51]. Our results indicate that overexpression of GATA6 in endothelial cells could attenuate capillarization of LSECs and liver fibrosis. Therefore, both GATA4 and GATA6 are important for vascular homeostasis in the liver and for protection against liver fibrosis.

In recent years, several groups have reported that chemicals that suppress YAP activity would improve liver fibrosis, but neither targeted YAP directly [52–54]. In the present study, an siRNA, which selectively silences Yap1 via sequence-specific binding, was used to treat liver fibrosis. A previous study showed that hyaluronic acid (HA)–polyethyleneimine (PEI) nanoparticles delivered siRNA or miRNA to LSECs efficiently [35, 36]. This efficiency was attributed to the high expression of the HA receptor stabilin-2 in LSECs and the effectiveness of PEI in gene delivery [35, 55]. To improve the stability of the siRNA delivery system and its specificity for LSECs, HA-PEI nanocarriers were used to deliver siYap1. Our results indicated that the fluorescence signal of FITC-labeled HA-PEI/siYap1 nanoparticles was predominantly located in the perisinusoidal space, and Yap1 expression was significantly reduced together with the mitigation of LSEC capillarization and liver fibrosis in DDC-treated mice, suggesting that HA-PEI-delivered siYap1 is an effective approach to improve liver fibrosis. Note that HA-PEI/siYap1 nanoparticles could also be ingested by KCs or other cells expressing hyaluronan receptors CD44 [56]. As discussed above, YAP contributes to the progression of hepatic fibrosis in various cell types including LSECs, KCs, HSCs, etc. Thus, capture of nanoparticles by hepatic cells other than LSECs is likely to enhance the antifibrotic effect of siYap1.

In conclusion, our study indicated that YAP promotes capillarization of LSECs and liver injury by inhibiting the GATA6/eNOS pathway. Downregulation of Yap1 expression by HA-PEI/siYap1 nanoparticles offers a new antifibrotic treatment for liver fibrosis. Considering that siRNA is a highly promising therapeutic approach [57], it is worth expecting that siYap1 nanoparticles would be used for the treatment of liver fibrosis in the near future.
