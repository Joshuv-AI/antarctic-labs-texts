Bacterial nanocellulose-based hydrogel from Bacillus subtilis W7 for a sustainable adsorption of tetracycline from wastewater
Authors: Talema Tesfaw Deresse, Gizachew Haile Gidamo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The production of nanocellulose using microbes has been gained interest across wide range of industries due to its flexible characteristics and advantages as compared to plant cellulose. The most active producers of bacterial nanocellulose were acetobacteraceae. The present study was conducted with the aim to isolate and characterize bacterial nanocellulose for the production of nanocellulose based hydrogels for removal of tetracycline from wastewater. The bacterial nanocellulose producing bacterias were isolated and screened from Awash winery waste (Ethiopia) using Hestrin-Schramm media. Analysis of the 16S rRNA gene sequence revealed that the bacterial isolate W7 shows 98.12% similarity with Bacillus subtilis strain T13 and isolate W4 shows 99.93% similarity with Bacillus subtilis strain 9-1-2.The highest bacterial nanocellulose yield of 5.15 ± 0.13 g/L was obtained from bacterial isolate W7 in Hestrin-Schramm culture media and the yield was further improved to 5.88 ± 0.25 g/L by using molasses as a main carbon source. The bacterial nanocellulose hydrogel was synthesised from bacterial nanocellulose by using glutaraldehyde as cross-linking agent. The synthesised bacterial nanocellulose based-hydrogel was tested for its tetracycline removal efficiency using a batch adsorption method at different experimental conditions. The maximum adsorption capacity (qe) and removal efficiency (R%) was observed in the experimental conditions of pH 7 (59.52 ± 1.3 mg/g and 59.52 ± 1.3%), 60min contact time(54.97 ± 0.57 mg/g and 54.97 ± 1.5%), 50 mg adsorbent dose (128.39 ± 1.3 mg/g and 64.19 ± 1.30%), and 50 mg/L adsorption concentration (75.24 ± 1.09 mg/g and 56.69 ± 0.9%). Pore-filling effect, hydrogen bonding, hydrophilic, electrostatic and π–π interactions attributed to the adsorption of tetracycline. The result indicated that cellulose from Bacillus subtilis strain W7 is a promising adsorbent for the removal of tetracycline from waste water.

Introduction

Pharmaceuticals such as antibiotic were widely used in medicine and farming industries. These chemicals were frequently detected in soils, surface water, ground water, and drinking water. The primary sources of antibiotic contaminants into the water bodies have been determined to be wastewater treatment plant discharges with a lower contribution from secondary sources like hospital effluent and pharmaceutical company wastewater discharges1. Literatures reports indicated that some of the drugs that are consumed are not fully metabolized by humans or animals. They are mostly eliminated through the urine and feces as metabolites and unaltered parent compounds2 and are low biodegradable. These antibiotic residues can potentially cause variety of adverse effects such as toxicity, disruption of aquatic photosynthesis, impact on indigenous microbial communities and increases in the antibiotic resistance gene evolution3. Therefore, the presence of antibiotic residues in the environment pose a series risk to human and the environment, and is a major concern.

Antibiotic tetracycline (TC) removal from wastewater is currently being pursued with great effort through a variety of techniques including ozonation, biodegradation, advanced oxidation processes (AOP), adsorption, and coagulation-sedimentation. Different adsorbents like Nano-nickel (II) oxide to remove amoxicillin, coffee husk to remove norfloxacin, and microalgal processes3 were employed. According to Cui et al.4, UiO-66/PDA/BC was employed to treat pharmaceutical wastes that contained the antibiotics tetracycline and aspirin5. However, the majority of conventional treatment techniques have a lot of drawbacks including high costs, insufficient removal, the creation of sludge and hazardous byproducts. Currently, there is an increasing interest in substituting the conventional wastewater treatment technologies with nanosorbents. A wide range of nanoadsorbents are being developed to improve the adsorption removal of emerging pollutants. These includes carbon nanomaterials, magnetic nanoparticles, polymeric compounds, and siliceous compounds6,7.

The bacterial nanocellulose-based hydrogels are promising adsorbent because of their high mechanical strength, biodegradability, non-toxicity, ultrafine nanofibrous network structure, high purity, high degree of polymerization, remarkable water absorption and retention capabilities, high crystallinity (70–80%), and flexibility compared with plant cellulose7,8. In addition, they can be reused. Bacterial nanocellulose (BNC) differs from plant cellulose in macromolecular structure, physical and chemical characteristics. The β-1, 4-glucosidic bonds that links the glucose monomers in BNC makes insoluble in water and other common solvents, which is also responsible for differing in their cellulose crystallinity. Another advantage of BNC is its ease of production and modification processes to obtain desired characteristics. This can be achieved by using chemical, biological, physical modifications and fermentation conditions. The BNC chemical modification processes that affect physico-chemical properties of the resultant BNC including cross-linking using cross-linking agents such as glutaraldehyde and BNC composite production using cellulose Imidazolium9, nanomaterials such as magnetite (Fe3O4)10 and copperoxide11, and plant extracts12. Through such modifications, their physicochemical properties were tuned to make suitable for diverse applications.

The modified BNC have an immense application potential, particularly in biomedical industries. They are used as wound dressing13, drug delivery14, tissue engineering and components of implants4,7,15–17. This is due to their unique self-assembly into nanostructured biomaterial, biocompatibility, high purity, and tunable mechanical properties. The uses of modified BNC were also reported in the removal of heavy metals like lead and nickel12, dye10,18, and antibiotics19 from wastewater. The potential BNC application was also reported as packing, thickening, stabilizing and gelling agent in foods industries8. Recently, BNC application as a substitute for gelling agent in plant tissue culture was tested and reported as cheap, reusable and reliable alternative for gelling agent20. Biological BNC modification also includes coating with nanomaterial’s which can be used for wastewater purification21,22.

Hydrogel is a polymeric material that can be produced by a reaction between one or more monomers. BNC- based hydrogels combine the chemical properties of BNC with hierarchical microporous structures. It has been applied in many biomaterials applications due to their exceptional qualities23. The high water absorption and inexpensiveness of hydrogels have attracted much focuses as efficient adsorbents. The swelling ability of hydrogels is due to the cross-linking network in their three-dimensional structure24. The diverse functional groups within the hydrogel network make them highly effective for adsorbing and extracting pollutants from water through electrostatic interactions. Additionally, the straightforward synthesis along with their ability to be regenerated and efficiently recycled, are significant advantages that contribute to their suitability as materials for the adsorption process25.Hydrogels have also demonstrated a remarkable efficiency in the removal of various organic and inorganic water pollutants including metal ions, hazardous dyes, and pharmaceuticals in wastewater treatment applications25.

Several bacterial isolates have been known to produce BNC through either static or agitated fermentation processes. During fermentation process, microorganisms metabolize glucose and form a linear β-1, 4-glucan chains. The linear chains accumulated extracellularly and form a crystalline structure to form cellulose. Then after, certain amount of monofilaments form an aggregate at the surface of liquid media to form a filamentous fiber or ribbon-like structures and finally form a three dimensional and gelatinous structure26.

The Gram-negative strain of acetic acid producing bacteria such as Komagataeibacter xylinus is the most studied BNC producers. Other acetic acid bacterial species such as K. medellinensis, K. hansenii, K. nataicola, K. oboediens, K. rhaeticus, K. saccharivorans, and K. pomaceti have also been shown to be potent producers of cellulose26,27. In addition to acetic acid bacteria, BNC production by Bacillus velezensis and other Bacillus species were reported28. However, BNC production from Bacillus subtilis has never been reported. For the first time, the production of BNC from B. subtilis W7 strain was described in this report. In addition, the physical and chemical properties of the BNC and its hydrogel for tetracycline removal from waste water was investigated.

Molecular identification of nanocellulose producing bacteria

For molecular characterization, the partial 16S rRNA gene was sequenced, and compared for similarity with the bacterial sequences deposited in GenBank. The bacterial isolates W7 and isolate W4 exhibited 98.12% and 99.98% similarity with Bacillus subtilis strain T13 and B. subtilis strain 9-1-2, respectively. The nucleotide sequence of the bacterial isolates W7 and isolate W4 was deposited in a GenBank with accession number PQ182605 and PQ182591, respectively. Their evolutionary relationship was investigated based on the phylogenetic tree constructed by neighbour-joining method using MEGA7 software with a bootstrap value of 1000 replicates as shown in Fig. 5. The isolates were exhibited a close phylogenetic association with B. subtilis species.Fig. 5Phylogenetic tree the B. subtilis strain W7 and W4 (bold dot) and their related B. subtilis strains.

Characterization of BNC

The BNC produced by the isolate W7 was investigated using FTIR. The presence of different functional groups in the dried BNC was confirmed by the peak point value of the FT-IR spectra (Fig. 8). The absorption peak at 3334 cm−1 is assigned to the (–OH) stretching of dried BNC. The peak at the region 2828 cm−1 and 2939 cm−1 reveals the presence of (–CH) stretching of CH2 and CH3 aliphatic groups. Furthermore, the peak 1660 cm−1 (H–O–H) bending of adsorbed water and the absorption band at 1462 cm−1 assigned to the CH3 asymmetric deformation vibration. The peak values at 1104 cm−1 were assigned to (C–O–C) group of cellulose. The FTIR spectrum of absorption band at 1016 cm-1 represents (C–O) stretching. The last absorption peak with the very weak intensity band assigned at 617 cm−1 in the spectra BNC is corresponding to OH out-of- phase bending.Fig. 8Fourier transforms infrared (FTIR) spectra of BNC from isolate W7 and the BNC hydrogel after the tetracycline adsorption.

Hydrogel synthesis and characterization

The BNC based hydrogel was successfully synthesized via chemical cross-linking method using 1% glutaraldehyde as a cross-linking agent. The wet and dried BNC hydrogel pellicles were shown in Fig. 10. The initial weight of BNC hydrogel increased from 5 g to the final dry weight 8.75 g after immersed in 100 mL distilled water. The swelling percentage of hydrogel was calculated and found to be 75%. The FT-IR spectra of the TC adsorbed BNC hydrogel showed a shift in peaks compared with the BNC (Fig. 8). The SEM image analysis of the BNC hydrogel also indicated a layered structure with evenly distributed pores on the surface of BNC hydrogel (Fig. 9C). Tetracycline adsorption experiment

Effect of initial concentration of adsorbate on TC adsorption

The adsorption experiments were performed in a serious of batch adsorption experiments in a flask for a predetermined time and other adsorption parameter using the synthesised hydrogel. The effect of initial concentration of tetracycline on the adsorption capacity and removal percentage was investigated at the initial TC concentration which varied from 10 mg/L to 80 mg/L while keeping other parameters constant. On the other hands, the removal percentage BNC hydrogel increased when the adsorbate concentration increased from 10 mg/L to 50 mg/L and reached its maximum value (56.69 ± 0.90%) at 50 mg/L tetracycline and then, decreased with further increase in tetracycline concentration.

Effect of adsorbent dosage on TC adsorption

In order to understand the effect of adsorbent amount on the adsorption of TC, a batch adsorption was performed by varying the amount of adsorbent BNC hydrogel from 30 to 70mg while keeping other parameters constant. As it can be seen in Fig. 11, the maximum adsorption capacity (qe) and removal percentage (R%) raised-up linearly in corresponding to the increase in the adsorbent dose from 30 to 50 mg and goes down with further increasing of adsorbent dosage. The maximum adsorption capacity and removal percentage (128.39 ± 1.3 mg/g and 64.19 ± 1.30%), respectively, were reached at the adsorbent concentration of 50 mg as shown in Fig. 11.Fig. 11Effect of adsorbent dose on TC adsorption (BNC hydrogel dosage of 30 mg-70 mg, C0 = 50 mg/L, pH = 7.0, volume = 100 mL and 25 °C, qe is the adsorption capacity; R (%) is the removal percentage).

Regeneration and reuse of BNC hydrogel

The regeneration and reuse of BNC hydrogel was investigated after several adsorptions-desorption experiments using desorbing agents such as 0.1M HCl and 0.25 MNaOH. The BNC hydrogel can be reused up to 3 times without significant loss of adsorbing capability (Fig. 14).

Adsorption isotherm and kinetic studies

The adsorption kinetics of tetracycline was studied to determine the possible adsorption mechanisms and rate limiting steps. The adsorption behaviour of the tetracycline was studied by fitting the Langmuir and Freundlich linear and non-linear models to the equilibrium adsorption data (Fig. 15). The two models well described the experimental data. The calculated qmax (mg/g), KL (L/mg), and R2 values for the Langmuir model were 61.86, 0.097, and 0.937, respectively. The calculated KF (L/mg), n and R2 values for the Freundlich model were 13.84, 3.07, and 0.972, respectively. From the Ce/qe versus Ce plot for the langmuir isotherm model, relatively less R2 value (0.9374) was observed compared with Freundlich model (Fig. 15C,D). Therefore, comparison of the R2 values demonstrated that the Freundlich model was able to better describe the adsorption behaviour of the tetracycline in the BNC hydrogel.Fig. 15The adsorption isothermal curve of TC using bacterial nanocellulose at 25C and pH 7: (A) Langmuir isotherm model parameters fitting, (B) Freundlich isotherm model parameters fitting; (C) Ce/qe versus Ce plot for langmuir isotherm model; (D) ln(qe) versus ln(Ce) plot for Freundlich isotherm model.

The pseudo-first-order (PFO) and pseudo-second-order (PSO) models were fitted to the experimental data to determine the possible adsorption mechanisms and rate limiting steps for tetracycline adsorption kinetics. The TC adsorption indicated a slowly increasing time dependent pattern which reached its adsorption equilibrium within 60 min. After 60 min, no further increase in TC adsorption was observed (Fig. 16). The calculated qe (mgg−1), k (gmg−1 min-1), and R2 values for the PFO kinetic model were 52, 0.038, and 0.804, respectively. The calculated qe (mgg−1), k (gmg−1 min−1), and R2 values for the PSO kinetic model were 117.2, 0.000205, and 0.9946, respectively. From the qt versus t plot of PFO kinetics model, relatively less R2 value (0.804) was observed compared with qt versus t plot for PSO kinetics model (Fig. 16C,D). Therefore, comparison of the R2 values demonstrated that the PSO kinetics model was able to better describe the adsorption mechanisms of the TC in the BNC hydrogel. This suggests that the chemical process exist in the hydrogel system is the main adsorption mechanism. In addition, diffusion from main solution to the surface, piercing surface to distribute to active site, and the adsorption equilibrium processes on the adsorbent were the main steps involved in the adsorption mechanism. Discussion

The potential BNC producing bacterial isolates were successfully isolated from Awash winery wastes, Ethiopia, using spread and streak plate methods. From 40 isolates, a total of 5 bacterial isolates designated as W7, W4, W16, W13, and W2 were identified as the BNC producers. The formation of white pellicle at the air–liquid interphase indicates the potential of the isolates to produce BNC on Hestrin-Schramm (HS)-media29. The result of this work was in agreement with the previous literatures in which the potential BNC producing bacterial isolates were identified from organic waste. For instance, a facultative anaerobic and gram-negative bacteria, Enterobacter sp. M003 was isolated from tropical fruit mangosteen30. Besides, the potent BNC producing strain of G. xylinus BCMZ sp. was successfully isolated from rotten fruit29. The other gram negative bacteria K. xylinus TELE8 was also isolated from spoiled lemon juice31. In addition to gram negative bacteria, gram-positive, rod-shaped BNC producing bacterial strain Bacillus sp. strain T15was isolated from fermented tea32. Therefore, sugar rich winery waste is also a good source for isolation of BNC producing gram positive bacteria33.

The 16S rRNA gene sequence analysis of isolate W7 (PQ182605) and W4 (PQ18259) revealed similarity with B. subtilis species. Isolate W7 showed 98.12% similarity with B. subtilis strain T13 and isolate W4 shared 99.93% similarity with B. subtilis strain 9-1-2. Hence, our isolates are closest relative to the species of gram positive B. subtilis species. In the previous reports, BNC producing bacteria have been extensively reported as gram-negative bacteria such as K. xylinus, and K. sucrofermentans H110, and G. xylinus BCMZ sp.34. However, many gram-positive bacterial strains such as Bacillus strain T151,47, B. velezensis strainSMR28, and B. licheniformis ZBT235 were reported as potential BNC producers from the Bacillus genus. Therefore, the bacterial isolate W7 and W4 were identified as the potential BNC producer gram positive bacteria of Bacillus subtilis species kind.

The Bacillus subtilis species were a well known bacteria that produces hydrolytic enzymes such as carboxymethylcellulase (CMCase) that degrades cellulose. On the other hand, endogenous over expression or exogenous addition of the CMCase enzyme to the culture medium was also known to increase the amount of nanocellulose production. Cellulose degradation by CMCase enzyme helps to reduce the stress created on the glucan chain during the formation of crystalline cellulose microfibril36. In addition, capabilities of Bacillus subtilis species for biofilm formation has also been documented37.The structural component of the biofilm was provided by the exopolysaccharides such as BNC which containing glucose monomers37. BNC based biofilm formations were also reported for Bacillus subtilis species from various carbon rich sources such as fermented coconut water38. Like the fermented coconut water, winery waste water contains high level of organic matter such as sugars, acids, and alcohols39. This carbon rich environment can be a good source to isolate biopolymer producing bacteria. BNC production by Bacillus subtilis strain W7 have an adaptation role to the liquid environment by forming a biofilm in air–liquid interface. This helps to maintain their position at air-liquid interface for optimal oxygen access, facilitated nutrient uptake, prevent dehydration of the bacterial cells, and resist physical disturbance.

The dried BNC yield of the isolate W7 was 5.15 ± 0.13g/L and found to be highest compared with other isolates (Table 1). The isolate was also showed better BNC yield as compared to the previous studies in a similar carbon sources such as glucose. BNC yield of 2.75 g/L40, 1.84g/L and 1.55 g/L, were reported from K. saccharivorans strain MD123, KX and K975, respectively41. In addition, G. hansenii P2A strain produced 1.89 ± 0.08 g/L BNC in HS media under a shaking condition42. Furthermore, 2.80 g/L BNC was reported from K. Medellinensis which grown on glucose supplemented media as a main carbon source43. Therefore, isolate W7 showed better BNC production compared to the previous literature reports.

The pH of the culture medium significantly influences the yield of BNC produced by bacterial isolates. The pH in the fermentation culture media is altered independently by the production of secondary metabolites like acetic acid, gluconic acid, and lactic acid during the BNC fermentation process44. BNC yield of isolate W7 was high in acidic media and showed a gradual increment as the pH value raised from 4.5 to 6, and decreased while pH declined from pH 6 to 7 (Fig. 6). The highest BNC yield in dry weight was obtained from pH6 (5.1 ± 0.36 g/L) followed by pH 5.5 (4.94 ± 0.15 g/L). At pH7.0, BNC production by isolate W7 declined (1.71 ± 0.19 g/L). This indicated that pH6 was the optimum pH for the production of BNC. The production of BNC was reported in pH ranging from acidic to neutral17,29. However, the effect of pH on the BNC yield could be altered depending on the nature of the strain. Optimal pH of 9, 10, and 6 were also reported for K. maltaceti P285, K. pomaceti O277, and K. nataicola TISTR 975 strains, respectively30.

Since the publication of Hestrin and Schramm research on the production of bacterial cellulose, glucose has been the most commonly used carbon source for BNC production. However, subsequent studies have focused on finding new carbon sources to take its place in order to reduce expenses and achieve successful large scale production. This is due to the fact that, glucose leads to low yield of BNC as a result of gluconic acid production and reduction in pH levels of the culture media that impacts the viability of bacterial cells31. A number of studies have demonstrated that the production of NBC was improved by using alternative carbon sources such as Cantaloupe juice32, corn steep liquor33, and acid whey40. In this study, the highest yield of BNC was obtained by using molasses (5.88 ± 0.25 g/L) as carbon source (Fig. 7). Abol-Fotouh et al., reported the maximum BNC yield of 3.2 g/L by K. saccharivorans MD1 using treated molasses medium45. Similarly, 2.2 g/L BNC was obtained by using K. medellinensis strain on fique juice medium46. Thus, our isolate W7 showed highest BNC yield on low cost medium compared to other studies. A comparable BNC production was reported for the bacterial strain K. xylinus BPR 2001(7.5 ± 0.54 g/L) using molasses as a cheap carbon source46 and for Bacillus sp. strain T15 (7.84 ± 19 g/L) in molasses medium47.This is due to the presence of abundant sugars such as sucrose, fructose, and glucose in molasses48. Thus, molasses could be used as a preferable carbon source for the production bacterial cellulose for large scale production.

The FT-IR based characterization of the TC adsorbed BNC hydrogel indicated a peak shifting and peak appearance/disappearance (Fig. 8) which indicates the type of interaction between the surface functional groups and TC. Similar FTIR results were reported for BNC obtained by the isolate BCMZ49. In additions, SEM result also revealed the presence of ultrafine thread-like non-directionally arranged microfibers of cellulosic materials, which confirmed the formation of fiber-shaped particles of BNC. A similar microfiber patterns were reported in literatures29,30.

The maximum adsorption capacity (qe) and removal percentage(R%) were significantly affected by the initial concentration of tetracycline in the solution. As the result in the graph of Fig. 10 shows, the adsorption efficiency of BNC hydrogel was raised corresponding to increase in the initial concentration of the adsorbate(i.e. TC) and reached its maximum adsorption (75.24 ± 1.09 mg/g) while the initial concentration of TC was 80mg/L. This trend is similar with the previous findings reported by Banaei et al50. Therefore, based on this result our adsorbent BNC hydrogel showed better adsorption capacity as compared to the previous study on TC adsorption using GO/CA51. Accordingly, the removal percentage (%R) of BNC hydrogel was increased (28.04 ± 0.25% to 56.69 ± 0.90%) as the initial concentration of adsorbate increased from 10mg/L to 50mg/L. This is due to the fact that, the initial concentration gradient creates a driving force which allows the mass transfer of the adsorbate to the adsorbent, which creates readily availableadsorption site42, thereby increase adsorption efficiency. A comparable results were reported in 2020 using chitosan based magnetic adsorbent51,52.However, the removal percentage gradually decreased when TC concentration exceeds 50 mg/L. The decrease in TC removal percentage of BNC hydrogel was due to the fact that, the adsorption sites for the adsorbate molecules are saturated and there is a competition for adsorption sites53. Furthermore, increase in the repulsive force between TC molecules due to its higher concentration eliminates the adsorption of other TC molecules to the adsorbent sites54.

The effect of adsorbent amount on the maximum adsorption capacity (qe) and removal percentage (R%) of BNC hydrogel for TC were evaluated out to understand how the adsorbent dosage affects its adsorption capacity. The maximum adsorption capacity(qe) and removal percentage (R%) raised-up linearly in response to the increase in the adsorbent dose from 30 to 50 mg due to the availability of adsorption sites for the adorbate molecules as a result of larger surface area of the adsorbent55–57. The maximum adsorption and removal percentage was recorded as 128.39 ± 1.3 mg/g and 64.19 ± 1.30%, respectively, with the BNC hydrogel dose of 50mg. This result is comparable with the previous findings on tricaprylmethylammonium chloride conjugated chitosan hydrogel beads52 and magnetic nano-biocomposite57. Although, the adsorption capacity and removal percentage were initially increased proportionally with adsorbent dose, the adsorption process reached its equilibrium at 50mg adsorbent dose and a significant decrement of both adsorption capacity (qe) and removal percentage(R%) of BNC hydrogel on TC were observed while the adsorbent dose was beyond 50mg. The decrease in adsorption capacity (qe) and percentage removal(R%) of the BNC hydrogel adsorbent for TC while adsorbent dosage further increased above 50mg is due to the decrease in adsorption sites which leads to the reduction in adsorbent-adsorbate interaction58. In addition, at higher concentrations the adsorbent may form an aggregate and prevents the movement of TC in the solution, thereby decrease in adhering to the adsorbent and lower the adsorption rate54.

To understand the absorption process, the influence of initial pH on sorption capacity of the BNC hydrogel was investigated in the range 4–12. The protonated states and the amount of TC adsorption can be easily affected by pH. TC has three functional groups such as dimethylamino, phenol deketone group and tricarbonyl methane that can undergo protonation- deprotonation reactions depending on the solution pH59. At solution pH < 4, 3.5–7.5, and > 7.5, it forms cationic species, zweitterionic, and anoinic species, respectively. The maximum adsorption capacity (qe) and removal percentage(R%) were boosted in a similar trends as pH values increased in an acidic conditions and reached the maximum value(59.52 ± 1.3 mg/g and 59.52 ± 1.3%), respectively at neutral pH(pH 7) (Fig. 12), which relates to the zwitterionic form of the TC. The adsorption capacity decrease when the pH was 7 and above. At pH > 7, TC exist as anionic due to the loss of proton from tri-carbonyl system and phenol di-ketone moiety. At low pH, cation-exchange and H-bond may be involved; its influence gradually decreases when TC changes from cation to zwitterionic and anionic form60. The H-bond may contribute to TC interaction with the bacterial nanocellulose hydrogel at acidic pH. Decrease in TC adsorption at pH above 7 is due to high hydrophobicity and low hydrophobicity interactions. As a result, water clusters and H-bond formation was enhanced. Therefore, cation-exchange and hydrophobic interactions are the dominant TC adsorption mechanisms. On the other hand, the phenol ring of TC can serve as an electron donor, where as the protonated amino group can serve as an electron acceptor, which may result in the interaction of the π-orbitals of TC and BNC hydrogel for increase adsorption. Some literatures reported cation-exchange as a primary mechanism for TC adsorption59–61.

The contact time of adsorbent to the adsorbate was found to be the determinant factor to evaluate the adsorption efficiency of the adsorbent. The change due to contact time was varied from 30 to 80min and its removal percentage (R%) was shown in Fig. 13. Initial fast removal rate of TC by BNC hydrogel was recorded when contact time increased initially from 30 to 60 min, followed by a second slower removal rate from 60 to 80 min. This is due to the gradual occupation of surface binding site, until reaching equilibrium62. In the initial adsorption time, more unoccupied adsorption sites were available on the adsorbent and the adsorbate can easy adhered. When the exposure time further increased, the adsorption site decreased and which results in lowering the adsorption capacity. Our result is in agreement with the previous findings50,57,63.

BNC hydrogel was re-used for five times with its consistence adsorption performance in the first three cycles and slightly decreased at round 4 and 5 in a similar trend with the result reported for the regeneration and reuse of tricaprylmethyl-ammonium chloride conjugated chitosan hydrogel54. The slight reduction in adsorption capacity and percentage removal after several adsorption–desorption experiment could be due to incomplete desorption and irreversible adsorption of TC on the adsorption sites64.

Adsorption isotherm was studied to understand the interaction between the amount of adsorbate and the adsorbent material65. The regression analysis (R2) for Langmuir (R2 = 937) and Frendlich (R2 = 972) models indicates better fit of the experimental data for the TC adsorption. Comparatively, low R2 value was observed for Langmuir model (R2 = 937) which indicates that TC adsorption was not purely mono-layer type. The R2 value of the Langmuir isotherm model is slightly less than the Frendlich model which indicates that the experimental isotherm data fits Frendlich model satisfactorily. The model describes the occurrence of adsorption on the heterogeneous adsorbents surface for both chemisorption and physisorption in which the adsorption affinity of the adsorbate varies over the adsorbent surface66,67. The SEM micrograph also confirmed the presence of layers structures on the BNC hydrogel (Fig. 9). This indicates the presence of heterogeneous adsorbent surface that provides high surface area and porosity for TC molecules to be adsorbed. Similar results were reported for TC adsorption using nanocrystalline cellulose from seaweed63,66,67.

Adsorption kinetic studies were conducted in order to determine the rate of adsorbate removal with respect to contact time which is important to optimized contact time in the adsorption process at a certain temperature67.The two kinetic models namely PFO and PSO models were applied to study adsorption kinetics of tetracycline on the BNC hydrogel. The PFO model assumed the dominance of physiosorption, which describes solid–liquid adsorption mechanisms due to the sorption ability of solids68. Whereas, PSO model describes the occurrence of chemisorption in the adsorption process62. According to the result, TC adsorption by BNC hydrogel is well fitted by both models, but PSO non-linear model R2 value (R2 = 0.9983) which is relatively higher compared to PFO non-linear model (R2 = 0.804). Therefore, the Pseudo-second-order kinetic model better describe the slowly increasing time dependent adsorption in the experimental data.

Peak and intensity change in our FT-IR result indicates change in the bond strength and the BNC hydrogel environment which surrounds TC molecules. Layered structures in SEM image also indicate its potential to provide large surface areas to facilitate TC adsorption. Therefore, tetracycline adsorption processes in this case involved multiple interactions such as pore filling effect, hydrophobic, π-π electron donor–acceptor, electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding interactions 49. Combinations of these physical and chemical interactions are the main tetracycline adsorption mechanism in BNC hydrogel.

Conclusions

In this study, BNC producing gram positive bacteria was successfully isolated from Awash winery waste, Ethiopia and was identified as B. subtilis. The 16S rRNA gene partial sequence analysis indicated that isolate W7 was closest relative to B.subtilis strainT13 with an identity percentage of 98.12%. The bacterial isolate W7 displayed a significant nanocellulose producing capacity of 5.88 ± 0.25 g/L using molasses as a substrate under static condition. BNC hydrogel was synthesized using glutaraldehyde as a cross-linking agent. The synthesized polymer was successfully employed as an effective adsorbent for tetracycline with 64.19% removal potential at 50 mg adsorbent concentration. The adsorption process conformed to PSO and Freundlich adsorption models. The TC adsorption was promoted by pore filling effect, hydrophobic, π–π electron donor–acceptor, electrostatic and hydrogen bonding interactions. Therefore, the BNC produced by B. subtilis strain W7 can be used in the treatment of antibiotics contaminated wastewater.

Materials and methods

Isolation of BNC producing bacteria

A100mL sewage from Awash winery industry fermentation residues was collected for the isolation of nanocellulose producing bacteria. BNC producers were isolated by using standard dilution and streak plate methods on petri plates containing HS medium. The HS medium contains 20 g/L glucose, 5 g/L peptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, 1.15 g/L citric acid, and 2.7 g/L Na2HPO4 supplemented with 0.01%cycloheximide(w/v) to avoid the contamination of fungi and yeast, the pH of the medium was adjusted in acidic condition (pH6) and the medium was autoclaved for 20min at 121 °C. The petri plates containing the culture media were inoculated loop-fully with the sample suspension and incubated at 30 °C for five days in static condition49. Then after, the formation of bacterial colonies on the inoculated plates was investigated after five days. The white gelatinous material formed on the surface of medium was sub-cultured and purified on HS agar plates. The purified isolates were then screened for their ability to produce nanocellulose. After 5 days incubation at 30 °C, each grown colony isolated in a Petri plates containing HS agar media was maintained at 4 °C on HS agar for further investigation69–73.

Preliminary screening of bacterial Isolates for BNC production

BNC producing isolates were preliminarily screened by inoculating fresh culture in 10 mL HS fermentation broth containing (g/L): glucose (20.0), yeast extract (5.0), peptone (5.0), Na2HPO4 (2.7), and citric acid (1.15), and pH adjusted to 6.0, in test tubes and incubated for 10 days at 30 °C in static condition. The static culture conditions were preferred to promote polymerization and formation of highly crystalline bacterial nanocellulose with high mechanical strength. The isolates with pellicles formation at the air–liquid interface, which was an indication for BNC production, were selected for further study17,70.

Biochemical characterization of the bacterial isolates

The morphology of BNC producing bacteria was tested microscopically by gram-staining to differentiate gram- negative and gram-positive strains71. The shape, colour, size and elevation of BNC producing isolates were characterized. Furthermore, the acid production test of the strain was examined by streaking the purified culture on CaCO3-agar plate that contained 2 g/L glucose, 5 g/L yeast extract, 15 g/L calcium carbonate, and 4% ethanol, and the formation of clear zone around the colony was investigated72.

Molecular characterization of the bacterial isolates

Genomic DNA extraction from bacterial isolates (W4 and W7) was performed using phenol chloroform protocols73,74. The isolates were cultured in LB broth and incubated at 30 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the overnight culture was transferred to Eppendorf tubes and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. The bacterial pellet was suspended in 1X TE buffer, 20 mg/mL lysozyme, 100 mg/mL SDS, 25 mg/mL proteinase K, phenol–chloroform-isoamyl alcohol, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. The upper aqueous layer was transferred to a new Eppendorf tube, and the extraction step was repeated with phenol–chloroform. The supernatant was collected in a fresh tube, and the DNA was precipitated by adding 5 M sodium acetate and 60 µl of isopropanol. The DNA pellet was air-dried for 5 min, then 70% ethanol was added, and the sample was centrifuged again at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The DNA pellet was air-dried for another 5 min before being suspended in 1X TE buffer.

To amplify the 16S rRNA gene, a polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed using 2 μL of purified DNA as the template. The PCR setup included a set of primers: 1 μL of each primer, with the forward primer 27-F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3′) and the reverse primer 1492-R (5′-TACGGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′). The PCR reaction mixture contained 10 μL of master mix (which included 10 × Taq buffer, 10 mMdNTPs, 25 mM MgCl2, and 1 μL of Taq DNA polymerase. The amplification process was carried out in a thermo-cycler under the following conditions: an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min; 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, annealing at 55 °C for 40 s, extension at 72 °C for 1 min, followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. After amplification, the PCR products were resolved by gel electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel and analysed using a gel documentation system. The 16S rRNA gene PCR product was then sequenced using an Sanger sequencing following the manufacturer’s guidelines74.

The raw 16S rRNA gene sequence data were reviewed and assembled to produce consensus using CotigExpress and then converted to FASTA format. Sequences of low quality were trimmed from the 3′ to 5′ ends, and the cleaned sequences were assembled with CotigExpress before being submitted to GenBank to obtain accession numbers. The sequences were compared with the NCBI database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) using BLASTn search tools. Finally, a phylogenetic tree was created using the neighbor-joining method in MEGA7 software, with bootstrap values from 1000 replicates and calculations performed using the Kimura 2-parameter model30.

BNC production

BNC was synthesized in a static fermentation conducted in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks in the 10% (v/v) volume of the inoculum with OD600 value of 0.08 at 30 °C for 14 days, the pH value of the broth adjusted to 6 using 5M NaOH. The purified bacterial nanocellulose was then gently washed with distilled water until the filtrates reached a neutral pH. After purification the BNC film was dried in a vacuum drier at 60 °C. Effect of pH on BNC production

In order to investigate the effect of pH on BNC production, bacterial isolate with the highest BNC production was chosen and cultured in HS broth at 30 °C for 24h. Then, in six different flasks, 100 mL of HS broth were prepared with pH value of 4.5, 5, 5.5, 6, 6.5, and 7. Then, each flask was inoculated with the same cell concentration, and the BNC yield was determined as previously mentioned after two weeks of incubation76,77.

Effect of carbon source

In order to investigate the effect of carbon source on the production of bacterial nanocellulose, fresh bacterial culture was inoculated in 250 mL flasks containing 100 mL fermentation media with different carbon sources 20 g/L glucose, 20 g/l glycerol, 20 g/L mannitol, and 20 g/L molasses and incubated for two weeks in a static condition at 30 °C at pH of 678.Accordingly, BNC yield of bacterial isolate on these carbon sources was analysed based on the dried weight of purified BNC.

Characterization of BNC

The BNC film that was synthesized by the potent isolate was recovered from the two weeks fermentation culture, purified in alkaline solution (0.1 M NaOH), and dried at 60 °C in a vacuum drier. Then the dried BNC was characterized using Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy at wave numbers ranging from 4000 to 400 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−179 The BNC was dried in vacuum at 60 °C and further characterized using scanning electron microscope (SEM), by using high vacuum mode operating at 15 kV79. The pore diameter of the BNC was analysed from the SEM image using ImageJ software.

Hydrogel synthesis and characterization

For BNC based hydrogel synthesis, previously published protocols with minor modifications was used80. A 5 g of the BNC was added into a beaker containing 20 mL of H3PO4 to breakdown cross-linked and the long chained cellulose. The mixture was stirred continuously for 24 h. To achieve complete homogeneity of the cellulose, 1 g of polyethylene glycol (w/w) was added to the BNC solution and stirred continuously for 30 min. Then, 15 mL of a 10% citric acid solution was added and stirred using magnetic stirrer at room temperature to obtain a homogenous solution. Chemical cross-linking of the BNC was done using 1% gluteraldehyde at a ratio of 10:1. Then the solution was heated at 55 °C for 4 h with continuous stirring, poured into a petridish and allowed to solidify at room temperature. The synthesised hydrogel was washed with distilled water to remove any unreacted materials and store at 4 °C until use for further study80.

The Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was used to characterize the synthesised hydrogel80. Tetracycline adsorption experiment

The adsorption efficiency of BNC hydrogel towards tetracycline contaminant was tested in a serious of batch adsorption experiments using 1000 mL tetracycline stock solution (100 mg/L) and further diluted into different concentrations while performing adsorption experiments for different parameters. In this study all the adsorption experiments were conducted using tetracycline solution without BNC hydrogel as a control. The pH effect on adsorption

Effect of pH on the adsorption tetracycline by the bacterial nanocellulose hydrogel was studied by adjusting the pH ranges of tetracycline from 2 to 12 using NaOH or HCl at 25 °C. For this experiment, 10 mg of the bacterial nanocellulose based hydrogel was added to 10 mL of 140 ppm of tetracycline solution having different pH values (pHinitial) at contact time of 10h. Then, the samples were filtered using 0.22 μm syringe filter and the final pH value (pHfinal) was recorded. The point of zero charge (PZC) was calculated from the change in pH values (ΔpH = pHinitial–pHfinal)79.

Regeneration and reuse of BNC hydrogel

After the completion of the first adsorption, the adsorbent was separated from the solution and its first round adsorption capacity was analysed using 20 mL of 0.1MHCl to desorb these tetracycline molecules at 30 °C under shaking condition at 120 rpm for 120 min. Then the adsorbent was immersed in 100 mL of 0.25 MNaOH at room temperature for10 min to neutralize the acidity. The recovered adsorbent was washed with distilled water and second round adsorption capacity was determined. This adsorption desorption experiment was repeated five times to investigate the reusability of the adsorbent81.

Statistical data analysis

All the experiments were performed in triplicates and the data was presented as mean and standard deviation of triplicate data. One way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using R version 4.3 along with Tukey post hoc test at p < 0.05. The isothermal and kinetic models were fitted using the Excel Solver.
