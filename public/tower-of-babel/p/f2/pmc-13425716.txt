Endurance exercise elicits a hepatic memory associated with improved metabolic function and protein secretion
Authors: Yi-Heng Huang, Clay J Weidenhamer, Nasrin Dabirian, Mikaela Kasperek, Donald Molina, Samuel Deyhle, Subhashis Natua, Jaume Amengual, Jacob Matthew Allen, Auinash Kalsotra, Diego Hernández-Saavedra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndurance exercise protects against metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), yet whether these effects persist following cessation of training remains unclear. Here, we employed endurance training cycles in mice to isolate the hepatic memory of exercise. Our results indicate that endurance retraining potentiates systemic and hepatic glucoregulatory benefits. Exercise retraining persistently reduced hepatic steatosis, hallmarked by decreases in diacylglycerols and increased phosphatidylcholines (PC). Liver transcriptomic analysis identified lipid and protein secretory pathways induced by endurance retraining. Importantly, retraining enhanced hepatic expression of carboxylesterases (CES), including Ces2b, Ces3a, Ces3b, and Ces4a, and increased circulating carboxylesterase activity and CES4A protein levels. Exercise retraining reduced serum LDL-c and increased HDL-c, while enhancing the delivery of lysoPC and PC, predicted targets of CES, to the working muscle. Similarly, mice fed an obesogenic diet demonstrate that this hepatic memory of exercise persists under an obesogenic challenge. In humans, we show that a 6-week training period increases serum CES activity primarily in individuals with prior training. Lastly, our studies identify the PPAR-RXR-clock axis as a potential trigger that may engage the synchronized lipid delivery to skeletal muscle and support fatty acid oxidation. Together, these findings suggest that endurance retraining elicits a hepatic exercise memory characterized by persistent transcriptional reprogramming and lipid remodeling that restore metabolic benefits after inactivity and confer resilience against MASLD.Keywords: Liver memory, Carboxylesterases, Glucose metabolism, MASLD, PPARα, Phosphatidylcholines

Introduction

Metabolic-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has emerged as one of the most prevalent liver disorders worldwide, affecting nearly one-third of the global population [1]. It encompasses a spectrum of progressive abnormalities, ranging from simple liver steatosis to Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatohepatitis (MASH), fibrosis, cirrhosis, and liver failure [2]. As a central metabolic hub, the liver governs key processes including glucose homeostasis, lipid metabolism, detoxification, and protein synthesis [[3], [4], [5]]. Chronic overconsumption of dietary sugars and fats promotes hepatic lipid overload mediated by heightened de novo lipogenesis (DNL), leads to ectopic fat deposition, triggering lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, and inflammation within hepatocytes [[6], [7], [8]]. These insults progressively impair mitochondrial function and compromise hepatic glucose and lipid handling [[9], [10], [11]]. Steatotic hepatocytes release free fatty acids and cytokines that compromise insulin signaling in peripheral tissues, thereby promoting systemic insulin resistance [10,12]. The resulting disturbances extend beyond the liver, promoting endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and a pro-thrombotic milieu, collectively accelerating atherosclerosis and cardiovascular risk [13]. Despite growing awareness of MASLD's global burden, safe and efficacious pharmacological treatments remain limited, underscoring the urgent need for accessible lifestyle interventions, such as aerobic exercise.

Aerobic exercise is a powerful lifestyle intervention that can prevent—and even reverse—key features of MASLD [[14], [15], [16]]. Epidemiological studies consistently demonstrate that higher levels of physical activity and cardiorespiratory fitness are inversely associated with MASLD prevalence, independent of body mass index and waist circumference [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. Clinical evidence shows that individuals with higher baseline cardiorespiratory fitness are more likely to reduce liver fat during lifestyle interventions, lowering their risk of MASLD [21]. Moreover, aerobic endurance exercise mitigates aberrant hepatic DNL induced by excessive nutrient intake and physical inactivity through direct downregulation of lipid synthetic enzymes [22,23]. In addition, endurance exercise enhances hepatic fatty acid oxidation [24,25] and supports gluconeogenic flux [3], collectively reducing intrahepatic lipid accumulation and improving liver function. These hepatic improvements are not isolated but interact with other organs, establishing crosstalk—including substrate flux between the liver, adipose tissue, and skeletal muscle—that further amplifies the systemic metabolic benefits of exercise [14]. While the benefits of aerobic exercise training on liver health are well established, whether these adaptations can persist even after exercise cessation remains unexplored.

Improved glucose control is a hallmark of both pharmacological therapies and lifestyle interventions, including exercise, and has been consistently linked to long-lasting reductions in the risk for liver, cardiovascular, and kidney disease [[26], [27], [28]]. The concept of "metabolic memory" emerged from the Diabetes Control and Complications Trial (DCCT), which demonstrated that 6.5 years of intensive insulin therapy conferred durable protection against microvascular complications and reduced cardiovascular disease burden by 42% including myocardial infarction, stroke, and death [29]. The subsequent Epidemiology of Diabetes Interventions and Complications (EDIC) follow-up study further showed that these benefits persisted for more than 30 years after treatment cessation [30,31]. Collectively, these findings established the principle of metabolic memory: early glycemic control exerts enduring protective effects against diabetic complications [32]. Beyond systemic glycemia, effective glucose regulation directly impacts hepatic metabolism and MASLD progression. Insulin resistance amplifies hepatic DNL and very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) overproduction, creating a cycle of hypertriglyceridemia, atherogenic dyslipidemia, and chronic low-grade inflammation [33,34]. Conversely, tight glucose control reduces hepatic lipid deposition, dampens inflammatory signaling, and improves insulin resistance, thereby slowing or even reversing disease progression [35,36]. These observations position glucose control as a central determinant of liver health and provide a mechanistic basis for linking metabolic memory to long-term hepatic protection. Analogous to this systemic memory, recent evidence from our group and others indicates that skeletal muscle retains a form of “muscle memory” that enhances adaptations during endurance exercise training [[37], [38], [39]]. However, whether a similar memory exists in the liver—particularly under metabolic stress caused by obesogenic diets—remains unexplored. Defining how therapeutic interventions can elicit a hepatic memory that sustains glucose control and preserves liver function is of paramount importance.

Here, we aimed to characterize the hepatic memory of exercise and its role in sustaining metabolic health. Using endurance exercise training cycles, we examined how training, detraining, and retraining shape hepatic and metabolic health. Our study demonstrates that endurance retraining persistently improves metabolic and hepatic health. This memory is characterized by improved glucose tolerance, enhanced hepatic gluconeogenic control, and distinct lipid remodeling, including reductions in insulin-desensitizing diacylglycerides (DG) and enrichment of phosphatidylcholines (PC) and lysophosphatidylcholines (LPC) linked to favorable metabolic adaptations. Transcriptomic profiling revealed the upregulation of hepatic secretory programs, notably carboxylesterases (CES), that may coordinate systemic improvements in glucose and lipid metabolism. Lastly, we propose that the PPARα-RXR-clock axis may serve as a trigger that engages the synchronized lipid delivery to skeletal muscle and supports fatty acid oxidation. Understanding this hepatic exercise memory may provide novel insights into non-pharmacological strategies for managing MASLD and related metabolic disorders.

Methods

Mice and training paradigm

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (Charles River Laboratories) were randomized to sedentary (static cages) or endurance training (Voluntary Wheel Running, VWR) according to baseline body composition, and were fed ad libitum either a control diet (CD; 10% kcal from fat; Research Diets Inc.; Cat# D12450H), or a high-fat diet (HFD; 45% kcal from fat; Research Diets Inc.; Cat# D12451). The study was conducted across three independent cohorts: in the TRAIN cohort, mice were sedentary (SED) or given free access to 9.5-inch stainless steel running wheels (VWR) throughout 4 weeks; for the DETRAIN cohort mice were sedentary for 8 weeks (SED) or underwent 4-week endurance training and switched to static cages (unloading) for 4 weeks (VWR); as for the RETRAIN cohort, one group was sedentary for 12 weeks (SED), and one group that was endurance-trained for 4 weeks, followed by 4 weeks sedentary, and an additional 4-week endurance training (VWR). A timed-exercise control was introduced to eliminate the effects of prior training, receiving only 4-week endurance training starting at week 9 (nVWR) fed CD (Figure 1A). Mice were provided food and water ad libitum and were housed at 25 °C with standard 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycles. Body weight was recorded weekly, and body composition (fat and lean mass) was assessed at baseline and monthly using an EchoMRI-700. To distinguish sustained adaptations from acute exercise responses, two independent cohorts were sacrificed following different washout periods. A 2-day washout cohort (collected at weeks 4, 8, and 12) was used to capture robust exercise-induced adaptations while minimizing the transient effects of the final exercise bout. A separate 1-week washout cohort (collected at weeks 5, 9, and 13) was used to determine the persistence of exercise-induced adaptations one week after the final exercise bout. Mice were euthanized via CO2 inhalation following the respective washout period prior to tissue collection. Tissue collection was performed in overnight-fasted (12-hr) or fed conditions as specified for each analysis (see figure legends). Male low-density lipoprotein receptor-deficient (Ldlr−/−) mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (#002207, Bar Harbor, ME). Serum, liver, muscle (gastrocnemius), and interscapular brown adipose tissue (iBAT), perigonadal white adipose (pgWAT), and subcutaneous white adipose (scWAT) were collected and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen–cooled isopentane or embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound and stored at −80 °C until processing. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Illinois Urbana–Champaign.

In vivo glucose metabolism

Glucose, insulin, and pyruvate tolerance tests were conducted after each experimental phase (4, 8, 12 weeks) to evaluate systemic glucose metabolism in three independent cohorts. For the in vivo glucose tolerance test (GTT), dextrose (MilliporeSigma; Cat# D9434) was diluted in PBS to a final concentration of 20%. Following an overnight fast (12 h), mice received an intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of glucose solution at 10% of their body weight (e.g., a 25.0 g mouse received 0.25 mL). Blood glucose levels were measured at 0-, 15-, 30-, 45-, 60-, and 120-minutes post-injection using a glucometer (ARKRAY USA Assure™ Platinum; Cat# 22-355-169). For the in vivo insulin tolerance test (ITT), insulin (Humulin-R, Med Vet International; Cat# 50-218-8599) was diluted 1:1000 in saline and, following a 2-hr fast, it was administered via i.p. injection at 10% of body weight. Blood glucose was measured at 0-, 10-, 15-, 30-, 45-, and 60-minutes post-injection. For the pyruvate tolerance test (PTT), sodium pyruvate (MilliporeSigma; Cat# P5280) was diluted in PBS and administered at a dose of 2 g/kg body weight. Overnight-fasted mice were injected i.p. with 10% of their body weight in solution, and blood glucose levels were measured at 0-, 15-, 30-, 45-, 60-, 90-, and 120-minutes post-injection.

Fasting glucose, insulin, and HOMA-IR

Fasting blood samples were collected following a 12-hour overnight fast. Serum insulin levels were quantified using an ultra-sensitive mouse insulin ELISA kit (Crystal Chem; Cat# 90080), and fasting glucose concentrations were measured using a colorimetric glucose assay kit (Cell Biolabs; Cat# STA-680) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Insulin resistance was estimated using the Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) index, calculated as follows: HOMA-IR = [fasting insulin (μU/mL) × fasting glucose (mmol/L)]/22.5.

Liver histology

Liver cryosections (10 μm thick) were mounted onto microscope slides, followed by lipid staining using an Oil Red O kit (Abcam; Cat#ab150678). Briefly, slides were air-dried, fixed in 10% formalin, rinsed in tap water, and incubated in propylene glycol, stained overnight with ORO solution at room temperature. Stained sections were differentiated in 85% propylene glycol, rinsed with distilled water, and counterstained with hematoxylin to visualize nuclei. Coverslips were mounted using ClearMount and scanned using a NanoZoomer 2.0-HT (Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan). Hepatic lipid droplets were quantified using ImageJ by selecting ten fields per slide (n = 3 mice/group), and data were expressed as the percentage of lipid-positive area relative to the total tissue area.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and qPCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol™ reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 15596018) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, tissue samples were homogenized using a bead beater in 400 μL of TRIzol, followed by the addition of 80 μL of chloroform. After vigorous mixing and centrifugation at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, the aqueous phase was collected, and RNA was precipitated with 500 μL of isopropanol. The RNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol, dried, and resuspended in 100 μL of DEPC-treated water. cDNA was synthesized from purified RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Cat. No. 43-688-14). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using Power SYBR™ Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Cat. No. 4368708) on a StepOnePlus™ system. Expression levels of target genes were normalized to the geometric mean of Rpl7a, Tbp, and Actb, and relative fold changes were calculated using the ΔΔCt method. Primer information is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), mapping, and analysis

Total RNA was extracted from liver tissue, purified with the Direct-zol kit (Zymo Research, Cat# R2072), and processed for polyadenylated mRNA library preparation, quality control, and sequencing on an Illumina Novaseq platform to a depth of at least 25 million 100-bp reads per sample. Transcript quantification was performed using Salmon (v1.10.0) in quasi-mapping mode to the GENCODE vM33 transcriptome (GRCm39), followed by gene-level aggregation with tximport and normalization and filtering in DESeq2. Downstream analyses included differential expression, pathway enrichment (KEGG), and dimensionality reduction (PCA), with results visualized using ExpressAnalyst. Genes with low expression were excluded prior to statistical testing. The entire workflow and parameter settings have been previously described in detail [38]. Transcription factor prediction was performed using Epigenetic Landscape In Silico Analysis (LISA) with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) [40].

Carboxylesterase enzymatic activity and CES4A protein quantification

CES activity was assessed according to previously described [41,42] using a colorimetric assay based on the hydrolysis of p-nitrophenyl acetate (pNPA) to p-nitrophenol, which exhibits a strong absorbance at 405 nm. For tissue CES activity, liver protein lysates (digestion buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl, 250 mM Mannitol, 50 mM NaCl, 50 mM NaF, 1.3 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 5 mM NaPPi decahydrate, 1 mM β-glycerophosphate, 10% w/v glycerol, 1% w/v Tween 20and Pierce protease inhibitor; Themo-Fisher, Cat# A32953), were diluted 1:100 in 50 mM Tris-Cl buffer (pH 7.4). For blood CES activity, serum samples were diluted 1:100. A fresh working solution of 1 mM 4-nitrophenyl acetate (Sigma–Aldrich; Cat# N8130-5G) was prepared in 50 mM Tris-Cl buffer (pH 7.4). For each assay, 150 μL of the substrate solution was dispensed into a 96-well plate, pre-incubated for 5 min at 37 °C, and subsequently an equal volume of diluted liver lysate or serum sample was added to initiate the reaction (final volume 300 μL). Absorbance at 405 nm was recorded every 43 s for 6 min using a microplate reader. Background absorbance (buffer + substrate) was subtracted, and reaction rates were calculated using the slope of the linear portion of the kinetic curve. Relative CES activity was expressed by comparing slopes across experimental groups. Serum CES4A concentrations were measured in 12-h fasted mice using a Mouse CES4A ELISA Kit (Abbexa, Cat# abx522399) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Serum lipid quantification and lipoprotein fractionation

Retrained serum lipids (week 12) were analyzed using the Abaxis Piccolo Xpress Lipid Panel Disc (Abbott Laboratories) for the measurement of total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), very low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (VLDL-c), and triglycerides (TG). Plasma lipoproteins from Ldlr−/− mice were fractionated as previously described [43,44] using fast-performance liquid chromatography with two Superose 6 10/300 GL columns (G.E. Healthcare, Boston, MA) using a Shimadzu HPLC system (Columbia, MD).

Untargeted lipidomic analysis

Liver, serum, and muscle (gastrocnemius) lipidomics analysis was conducted using ultra performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) with drift tube ion mobility mass spectrometry (DTIMS-MS). Following lipid extraction, including labeled internal standards, samples were analyzed using an Agilent 1290 UPLC system coupled with an Agilent 6560 IM-QTOF platform (Santa Clara, CA). Both positive and negative mode ESI data were collected between a mass range of m/z 50–1700. Lipids were then separated by reversed-phase liquid chromatography by using a Thermo Accucore C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 2.6 μm) with mobile phase A (60% acetonitrile: 40% water with 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid) and mobile phase B (90% isopropanol: 10% acetonitrile with 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid) with a flow rate of 0.4 ml/min. Standards purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, AL) were used for quality control and data normalization. Lipids were filtered to exclude >80% missing values and normalized before analysis. Significant lipid processes were analyzed using the lipid ontology enrichment tool (LION) [45].

Human subject recruitment

All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Illinois Urbana–Champaign (IRB protocol #21730). Participants were 20 currently sedentary adults (n = 20; 11 female, 9 male) aged 21–37 years (mean age 25.95 ± 1.08 years) with a mean BMI of 26.10 ± 1.02 kg/m2 and body fat percentage of 37.48 ± 1.33%. Inclusion criteria required participants to be male or female, aged ≥18 years, with a BMI between 19 and 45 kg/m2. Participants were categorized by BMI into healthy weight (HW; BMI <25 kg/m2), overweight (OW; BMI 25–30 kg/m2), and obese (OB; BMI >30 kg/m2). Exclusion criteria included antibiotic use within the preceding three months, a history of gastrointestinal disease (including irritable bowel syndrome, ulcerative colitis, or Crohn's disease), and chronic use of over-the-counter medications known to affect bowel function.

Clinical assessments in human subjects

All participants completed a 6-week supervised, progressive multi-component exercise intervention (3 sessions/week) consisting of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic training (2 sessions/week; Monday/Friday) and resistance training (1 session/week; Wednesday). Body composition, including body fat distribution and visceral adipose tissue, was assessed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) before and after the intervention. Baseline physical activity level was quantified using the Godin Leisure–Time Activity Questionnaire, and cardiorespiratory fitness was assessed via maximal oxygen uptake (VO2Max) using a standardized Bruce treadmill protocol [46].

Statistical analysis

All assumptions for parametric testing—including normality, homogeneity of variance, and linearity—were verified prior to analysis. For comparisons between SED and VWR groups at each time point, statistical significance was assessed using a two-tailed Student's t-test, with p < 0.05 considered significant, and one-way ANOVA with Dunnett's post-hoc comparing VWR and 9-wk-VWR, vs. SED controls. Throughout the text, statistical significance is denoted. All pairwise comparisons are described in detail in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

Endurance exercise training cycles potentiate fat loss and improve glucose homeostasis in a diet-dependent manner

To assess the long-term metabolic effects of endurance exercise, we compared the outcomes of TRAINing, DETRAINing, and RETRAINing phases in both control diet- (SED/CD and VWR/CD) and high-fat diet-fed mice (SED/HFD and VWR/HFD) using a mixed-effects model incorporating diet, exercise, and time as main factors (Figure 1A; Suppl. Fig. 1A–H). VWR Endurance training consistently prevented weight gain and fat accumulation across diets (Figure 1B,C). Mixed-effects analysis of body weight revealed significant main effects of diet, exercise, and time, as well as significant interactions between all factors (Diet ∗ Exercise ∗ Time, p < 0.0001), indicating that the magnitude of weight and fat mass changes differed by both diet and training history (Figure 1B,C; Suppl. Fig. 1I). In the TRAIN cohort, exercised mice displayed lower body weight and reduced fat mass, but not lean mass, compared to SED controls regardless of diet (Suppl. Fig. 1A–D). The DETRAIN cohort reproduced these early reductions during the initial training weeks (Suppl. Fig. 1E–H), but these effects were largely lost after exercise cessation in both CD- and HFD-fed mice (Suppl. Fig. 1F–H). In the RETRAIN cohort, exercised mice again exhibited lower body weight (Fig. 1B), with greater reductions in both body weight and fat mass across training phases in both diets (Fig. 1C). Lean mass increased during detraining, accounting for the transient rise in body weight (Fig. 1C). Overall, endurance retraining produced marked reductions in body weight and fat mass with a modest increase in lean mass, and these effects were amplified under HFD feeding, as indicated by the significant Diet ∗ Exercise interaction (Fig. 1C). Exercise retrained mice had comparable running volumes, albeit higher distances were recorded during the first training period for both CD- and HFD-fed trained mice (Suppl. Fig. 1I).

We next evaluated how prior exercise influences whole-body glucose homeostasis across diets (Fig. 1D–L; Suppl. Fig. 2A–I). After 4 weeks of training (TRAIN cohort), VWR/CD mice showed improved glucose tolerance (GTT) (Fig. 1D), without significant changes in fasting glucose, insulin tolerance (ITT) or insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) (Figure 1E,F; Suppl. Fig. 2A). HFD feeding is known to impair hepatic glucoregulation by inducing insulin resistance [47,48]. VWR/HFD mice showed improved glucose tolerance (GTT AUC, p < 0.07) and significantly enhanced insulin sensitivity (Figure 1D,E), but only marginal improvement in fasting insulin and HOMA-IR (Figure 1F; Suppl. Fig. 2A). Although initial gains in glucose and insulin tolerance were evident across diets (Suppl. Fig. 2B and C), these benefits were largely lost following detraining in CD-fed mice (DETRAIN cohort), with only mild improvements in glucose (Fig. 1G) and insulin tolerance (Fig. 1H), but not HOMA-IR (Figure 1I; Suppl. Fig. 2D). In HFD-fed mice, glucoregulatory benefits persisted during detraining, showing marked improvements in glucose tolerance (Figure 1G; Suppl. Fig. 2B), but not insulin sensitivity (Figure 1H–I; Suppl. Fig. 2C). However, after 4-week retraining (RETRAIN cohort), both VWR/CD and VWR/HFD mice showed pronounced enhancement in glucose tolerance and HOMA-IR (Figure 1J, L; Suppl. Figs. 2E, F, I), though insulin tolerance remained more variable (Figure 1K; Suppl. Fig. 2G and H). Collectively, these results indicate that endurance retraining enhances glucose homeostasis beyond the effects of initial training, with HFD-fed mice showing evidence of persistent glucoprotection during detraining, consistent with the establishment of a diet-resilient metabolic memory.

Endurance exercise retraining potentiates hepatic gluconeogenic control and persistently reduces liver lipid accumulation

To further investigate the mechanisms underlying improved glucose homeostasis, we assessed the effects of endurance training on hepatic function across diets. We evaluated hepatic gluconeogenic control using a pyruvate tolerance test (PTT) to measure the capacity to convert pyruvate into glucose in vivo. A significant main effect of diet and exercise was detected (Diet p < 0.001, exercise p < 0.01), with VWR mice showing lower glucose excursion across all timepoints (Figure 2A–C; Suppl. Fig. 3A–C). In CD-fed mice, mild reductions in hepatic gluconeogenesis were observed after 4 weeks of endurance training (Figure 2A; Suppl. Fig. 3A and B), indicating improved gluconeogenic control; however, this effect was lost following 4-week detraining (Figure 2B; Suppl. Fig. 3C). Endurance retraining led to a trend toward improved hepatic gluconeogenesis in CD-fed mice (Fig. 2C). In HFD-fed mice, endurance training reduced glucose excursion during training (Figure 2A; Suppl. Fig. 3A and B) and was marginally preserved after detraining (Figure 2B; Suppl. Fig. 3C). Endurance retraining potentiated hepatic gluconeogenic control, significantly reducing glucose production in both dietary conditions (Fig. 2C). These findings suggest that repeated training induces latent hepatic adaptations that are further amplified under an obesogenic diet challenge.

The liver plays a major role in whole-body lipid homeostasis and HFD increases hepatic lipid deposition [[49], [50], [51]]. Thus, we evaluated how endurance exercise shapes hepatic lipid accumulation via Oil Red O (ORO) staining across the three cohorts and both diets (Figure 2D,E). After the initial 4-week training phase, hepatic lipid content was markedly reduced in VWR mice compared to sedentary controls in both CD and HFD conditions (Figure 2D,E). This effect was lost after 4-week detraining in CD-fed mice, while HFD-fed mice maintained reduced lipid accumulation throughout detraining (Figure 2D,E). Endurance retraining led to robust reductions in hepatic lipid accumulation in CD- and HFD-fed mice, surpassing those observed after the first training bout (Figure 2D,E). Comparable liver steatosis was observed in sedentary controls regardless of diet, consistent with previous reports [52]. Importantly, hepatic lipid accumulation was consistently reduced throughout training, detraining, and retraining in HFD-fed mice compared to age- and diet-matched sedentary controls, suggesting that prior training confers a durable hepatic memory against diet-induced steatosis.

Untargeted lipidomic analysis of livers revealed distinct lipid signatures and processes using lipid ontology (LION) across training phases and diets (Figure 2F–K; Suppl. Fig. 4A–H). In CD-fed mice, 4-week training increased plasmalogens (O-PC, O-PG) and phosphatidylcholines (LysoPC, PC) associated with endoplasmic reticulum membranes (Figure 2F; Suppl. Fig. 4A). After detraining, these effects disappeared with reductions in LPC and PC lipids and increasing triglyceride (TG) accumulation (Figure 2F; Suppl. Fig. 4B). Endurance retraining resulted in a unique lipid pattern that reduced diacylglycerides (DG) while increasing ether TG (O-TG) and ether PC (O-PC), with a consistent enrichment in PC species across cohorts (Figure 2F–H; Suppl. Fig. 4C and D). In HFD-fed mice, lipidomic profiles showed robust alterations in ER-residing and fat storage lipids (Figure 2F,I, J), resulting in reductions of TG and DG during training and detraining (Suppl. Fig. 4E and F), and consistent enrichment in PC species across all cohorts (Figure 2J; Suppl. Fig. 4E–H). Soft clustering of lipids across timepoints identified phosphatidylcholines (PC) as the major lipid species and processes increased by retraining in VWR/CD mice, compared to sedentary controls (Figure 2G; Suppl. Fig. 4D). These effects were also observed in VWR/HFD mice (Figure 2I; Suppl. Fig. 4H) and identified PC as a shared feature of the retrain liver lipidome (Fig. 2K).

Endurance retraining induces a shared hepatic transcriptional program of protein and lipid secretion independent of diet

To understand how endurance training cycles impact hepatic gene regulation, we performed bulk RNA-seq from livers of CD- and HFD-fed mice across all three cohorts and compared them to age- and diet-matched sedentary controls (Figure 3A–D; Suppl. Fig. 5A and B). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in CD-fed livers identified distinct transcriptomic signatures during the initial TRAINing that disappear after DETRAINing, and are dramatically amplified after RETRAINing (Figure 3A,B; Suppl. Fig. 5A). While only 18 genes were differentially regulated after initial training in VWR/CD mice, a robust response was observed after retraining, with ∼2,000 downregulated and >4,000 upregulated genes compared to age-matched sedentary controls (Fig. 3B). Endurance retraining persistently enhances hepatic carboxylesterase expression and secretion across diets

Regardless of diet, both endurance-retrained groups (VWR/CD and VWR/HFD) increased several secreted proteins including Major urinary proteins (Mup) and Carboxylesterase (Ces) genes, pointing to a robust retraining transcriptional effect independent of dietary background (Figure 4A). These findings indicate that endurance retraining reactivates a conserved hepatic program of protein and lipid secretion that persists under metabolic stress. Moreover, we show that carboxylesterase genes Ces2b, Ces3a, Ces3b, and Ces4a are not increased after training or detraining, but were robustly induced after retraining in both dietary conditions (Figure 4B,C). These liver gene expression patterns were not observed in the fed state (Suppl. Fig. 6A). To assess functional changes, we measured carboxylesterase enzymatic activity (CES) in serum using the p-NPA substrate. Only retraining led to a significant increase in serum CES activity in both CD-fed (Fig. 4D) and HFD-fed mice (Fig. 4E). Hepatic tissue fasting CES activity (Suppl. Fig. 6B) and serum and liver activity in the fed state remained largely unchanged in both groups (Suppl. Fig. 6C and D). Among the known secreted CES enzymes, serum CES4A protein measured by ELISA was marginally elevated at week 12 in VWR/CD mice (Fig. 4F), whereas the increase in VWR/HFD mice did not reach significance, consistent with more rapid CES turnover in the context of HFD-induced hypertriglyceridemia.

To determine the persistence of these adaptations, we measured Ces mRNA expression and CES4A protein one-week after exercise cessation (Figure 4G–I). In CD-fed mice, Ces2b, Ces3b and Ces4a remained elevated at week 13, while Ces3a returned toward baseline (Fig. 4G). Taken together, these data establish CES4A as a secreted hepatokine whose induction by exercise retraining is durable, diet-independent at the transcriptional level, and associated with persistent changes in circulating CES activity.

Circulating carboxylesterases associate with lipoproteins and retraining enhanced lipid delivery to skeletal muscle

Carboxylesterases are members of the serum hydrolase superfamily that can cleave exogenous (xenobiotic) and endogenous ester bonds, including cholesteryl esters, triacylglycerols, phospholipids, among others, thereby playing a significant role in the regulation of lipid metabolism [[53], [54], [55]]. However, whether they are secreted through the classical pathway or are packaged into lipoproteins has not been determined. To determine if CES proteins associate with circulating lipoproteins, we profiled CES enzymatic activity in serum lipoprotein fractions from Ldlr−/− mice, which exhibit blunted lipoprotein turnover [56]. LDL particles showed the highest CES activity among all lipoprotein fractions (Figure 5A,B), and CES activity was inversely correlated with LDL cholesterol (r2 = −0.32, p = 0.002) and positively correlated with HDL cholesterol (r2 = 0.28, p = 0.005) (Fig. 5C). These data indicate that CES proteins may associate with circulating lipoproteins to act on circulating lipid cargo.

Circulating carboxylesterase activity is enhanced by exercise in humans and is associated with cardiorespiratory fitness

To explore the translational relevance of the hepatic CES secretory axis identified in mice, we measured serum CES activity in a cohort of individuals with healthy-weight (HW), overweight (OW), and obesity (OB) (N = 20) before and after a 6-week supervised exercise intervention comprising aerobic (60–75% HRR, 2 sessions/week, 30–60 min each) and resistance training components (1 session/week of full body resistance 65–80% predicted 1RM) (Figure 6A). The intervention effectively reduced fat mass (Figure 6B; p < 0.001), confirming adequate exercise stimulus. Serum CES activity increased significantly from pre-to post-intervention across all participants (Figure 6C; p = 0.01), indicating that a short-term exercise program is sufficient to upregulate circulating CES activity in humans.

To determine whether prior physical activity history modulated the CES response, we stratified participants by habitual physical activity (PA) level using the Godin Leisure–Time questionnaire. Only participants classified as high PA (Leisure Score Index (LSI) ≥ 24) showed a significant pre-to-post increase in CES activity (Figure 6D; p < 0.05), whereas no significant change was observed in Low PA and Mid PA groups, with no significant correlation (Fig. 6E). A similar pattern was observed when participants were stratified by baseline VO2 Max; only those with prior good VO2 Max (≥32 Female, 36 Male ml/kg/min) showed a significant increase in CES activity following the intervention (Figure 6F; p < 0.05). Furthermore, baseline VO2 Max was positively correlated with post-intervention serum CES activity (r = 0.51, p = 0.021; Fig. 6G), with VO2 explaining ∼26% of the variance in CES activity (r2 = 0.26). Together, these preliminary human data support the concept that circulating CES activity is responsive to exercise and that prior fitness level, analogous to prior training history in the mouse model, amplifies this response.

Prior training history is required to activate the hepatic exercise memory

Improvements in metabolic and hepatic function observed after endurance retraining could simply reflect a cumulative effect of 4 weeks of exercise rather than a true memory of prior training. Thus, we compared retrained mice (4-week train, 4-week detrain, and 4-week retrain) to exercise-naïve mice that received only a single 4-week bout of exercise beginning at week 9 (nVWR), as well as CD-fed, sedentary controls (SED) (Figure 7A). Both retrained VWR and nVWR mice showed significant improvements in body composition and reductions in fat mass, but not lean mass, compared to SED controls (Fig. 7B). Despite similar cumulative running distances and training volumes during weeks 9–12 (Suppl. Fig. 8A), running distance analysis confirmed that retrained VWR mice restrict their running activity during the dark phase (active phase), and reduce their running activity during the light phase (inactive phase), compared to nVWR mice (Fig. 7C). This suggests prior exercise entrains temporal organization of voluntary physical activity. At week 12, retrained VWR mice showed significantly improved glucose tolerance and HOMA-IR compared to SED controls, whereas nVWR mice showed no significant glucoregulatory improvements despite equivalent training volumes (Figure 7D,E). Retrained VWR mice also showed a significantly higher HDL-c/LDL-c ratio compared to both SED and nVWR/CD groups (Fig. 7F).

To identify key features of the retraining transcriptional memory, we compared hepatic bulk transcriptomes from retrained VWR and nVWR mice (Figure 7G; Suppl. Fig. 8B). Reactome pathway analysis identified PPARα-activated gene expression, regulation of lipid metabolism by PPARα, fatty acid and ketone body metabolism, and circadian clock pathways as selectively enriched in retrained livers (Fig. 7G). Thus, we propose a model for the reactivation of the hepatic memory: among the genes activated by retraining (vs. nVWR), the Pparα and Pparδ were increased, which are known to be regulated by the clock machinery Bmal1 (Arntl), Clock, Npas2, while reducing expression of the negative feedback circadian regulators Per1, Per2, Cry1, Cry2, and Rev-erbα. We propose that this axis can target the expression of Ces genes and lipid secretion to support muscle fatty acid oxidation [38] (Fig. 7H). Predictive transcription factor analysis (LISA) [40] confirmed that PPARα and the clock machinery (ARNTL, CLOCK) were the most enriched regulators in retrained VWR/CD and VWR/HFD livers compared to diet-matched controls, with progressive enrichment across training phases (Fig. 7I).

Hepatic expression of Ces genes assessed by qPCR was increased in both nVWR and retrained VWR for Ces3b, but only retrained livers showed increased Ces2b and Ces4a expression, compared to SED (Fig. 7J). Further, neither serum or liver CES activity (Figure 7K; Suppl. Fig. 8C) nor CES4A protein (Fig. 7L) were increased in nVWR mice, indicating that prior training is required for the secretion. Adipose tissue is known to express Ces4a, but we did not identify significant training (nVWR) or retraining effects in white or brown adipose tissue in either dietary group (Suppl. Fig. 8D and E), confirming the fasted liver as the primary source of CES induction.

Lastly, hepatic Ces gene expression one week after training was only increased in retrained VWR mice (Fig. 7M), consistent with persistent serum CES4A levels (Fig. 7N). Thus, we show that prior training in mice and humans is linked to the establishment of a latent, memory-like transcriptional program in the liver, which is associated with improved glucoregulation and hepatic secretion.

Discussion

The hepatic benefits associated with endurance training are well documented [5,14], including enhanced glucose control [4] and fatty acid oxidation [22,24,25], but whether these adaptations persist after training cessation remains unclear. Evidence from the DCCT/EDIC studies revealed that intensive glycemic control confers long-lasting cardiovascular protection [29,31], suggesting that exercise could similarly establish a form of metabolic memory to improve glucose metabolism. Here, we demonstrate for the first time that endurance exercise induces a glucoregulatory memory that enhances glucose homeostasis and hepatic gluconeogenesis. Training attenuates body weight gain and reduces adiposity, and while the glucoregulatory effects might be diminished following detraining, a second training bout not only restores but amplifies these improvements. Notably, retraining increases hepatic transcription of protein and lipid secretion genes, an effect maintained under an obesogenic diet challenge. We propose that these adaptations may implicate the lipid-sensing nuclear receptor PPARα, acting with the clock machinery, can potentially coordinate lipid supply to the muscle. Collectively, our findings reveal that endurance retraining establishes latent yet durable biological adaptations that enhance responsiveness upon re-exposure to exercise and may be leveraged to reduce metabolic disease burden.

A central finding of this study is that endurance retraining elicits greater improvements in glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity than the initial training alone. While detraining abolished early benefits in CD-fed mice, a subsequent training bout restored and even amplified the glucoregulatory effects, as reflected by marked improvements in glucose tolerance and HOMA-IR despite lower training volumes. In HFD–fed mice, prior training provided partial protection, with glucose and insulin tolerance remaining superior to sedentary controls even during detraining. These results parallel the concept of “metabolic memory” originally described in diabetes; wherein intensive glycemic control confers lasting vascular protection long after treatment cessation. Candidate mechanisms underlying the metabolic memory have been proposed, including fluctuations in metabolites—acetyl-CoA, methyl donors, NAD+, and lactate—that feed epigenome modifiers (histone modifiers including acetylation and methylation, and DNA methyltransferases and demethylases), thereby imprinting DNA methylation and histone marks at promoters or enhancers of glucose-regulatory genes [57]. Analogous long-lasting memories have been defined for inflammation persistence [58,59]. In the context of diabetes, marks linked to hyperglycemia and nutrient excess persist even after normoglycemia is achieved, sustaining transcriptional programs that affect insulin signaling, hepatic gluconeogenesis (e.g., Pck1, G6pc), glycolysis, and DNL, which can reinforce the chronic inflammatory state, thus worsening insulin resistance. Evidence from human cohorts and animal models shows HbA1c-linked methylation signatures and hyperglycemia-induced shifts in histone acetylation and methylation that persist despite glucose control [[60], [61], [62], [63], [64]], and in turn can be reshaped by exercise. Our findings extend this principle to exercise, showing that prior training primes whole-body and hepatic metabolic adaptations that persist under metabolic stress and are potentiated with retraining. The absence of comparable benefits in exercise-naive mice undergoing the same training duration and volume further reinforces that retraining effects are not simply cumulative but rather reflect memory-like processes established during prior exercise exposure.

The liver emerged as a central mediator of long-term exercise adaptations. As the primary regulator of glucose and lipid metabolism, the liver balances glycogen storage and gluconeogenesis from substrates such as lactate, pyruvate, and glycerol [3,65]. Under fasting or exercise, glycogenolysis initially maintains blood glucose, but gluconeogenesis becomes the dominant source of glucose once glycogen stores are depleted [3,66,67]. In metabolic disorders, steatosis and insulin resistance promote excessive gluconeogenesis, contributing to persistent hyperglycemia [68,69]. In our study, endurance training improved hepatic gluconeogenic control and reduced lipid accumulation—effects that were lost after detraining but robustly reinforced upon retraining. Remarkably, in HFD–fed mice, prior training conferred sustained hepatic protection, with pyruvate tolerance preserved and hepatic steatosis reduced even during detraining, consistent with a hepatic memory of prior exercise. Hepatic lipid droplets are dynamic organelles composed of TG and cholesterol esters (CE) that accumulate excessively with MASLD, driving steatohepatitis (MASH) under chronic lipid overload and inflammation [[70], [71], [72]]. Our results show that exercise consistently attenuated lipid accumulation in both mice with and without prior training (VWR/CD or 9-wkVWR/CD vs. SED/CD, respectively), but improvements to insulin sensitivity and gluconeogenic control were unique to retrained mice. Further, liver lipidomics revealed a distinct lipid profile of endurance retraining, decreasing insulin-desensitizing DG and enriching ether-TG, LPC, and PC, which might help stabilize cellular membranes and support endoplasmic reticulum processes [[73], [74], [75], [76]]. Circulating levels of LPC and PC species have been linked to aerobic exercise [77] and are markers of metabolically favorable steatotic liver disease [78]. Prior studies have also linked liver-derived lipid species like PC (36:1) [79] as promoters of muscle fatty acid oxidation (through PPARα) observed with exercise [77], while several LPC species, including LPC 16:0—previously shown to enhance glucose uptake and lower glucose in mice [80]—also increased, consistent with beneficial roles of LPC in insulin sensitivity and MASH outcomes [78,81]. These findings support a model in which endurance retraining reprograms hepatic lipid metabolism, which in turn might coordinate whole-body metabolic adaptations.

Transcriptomic profiling further implicated hepatic secretory pathways in this exercise memory. Endurance retraining selectively upregulated multiple carboxylesterases (Ces2b, Ces2c, Ces3a, Ces3b, and Ces4a), expanding beyond CES2 isoforms previously linked to protection against steatosis and insulin resistance [41,[82], [83], [84]]. Importantly, only retrained mice exhibited increased serum CES activity while hepatic CES activity remained unchanged, suggesting enhanced export. CES4A, uniquely lacking the endoplasmic reticulum retention motif (KDEL) found in other isoforms [54,55,85,86], was detectable in circulation and remained elevated one-week after retraining, supporting its role as a secreted hepatokine that mediates persistent lipid remodeling. In addition, the lack of serum CES4A increase in HFD-fed exercise retrained mice might be due to rapid turnover in agreement with the reduced fasting triglycerides. Retraining did not alter Ces4a expression in adipose depots, nor did the fed status, confirming that the fasted or exercised liver is the primary source. Exercise retraining was associated with greater hepatic Ces expression and serum CES activity, which might be linked to serum reductions in DG and an increase in LPC, which in turn might improve the turnover of PC and PE species in skeletal muscle—both critical for muscle force production and fatty acid oxidation [[87], [88], [89], [90], [91]]. Further, other secreted proteins were identified with retraining regardless of diet including several Major urinary proteins or MUPs (Mup22, 20, 17, 16, 15, 14, 12, and 1) associated with improved hepatic function and coordinated of glucose improvements across the body [92,93]. Given the central role of the liver in glucose, lipid, and secretory homeostasis, the ability of exercise retraining to preserve hepatic function, enhance protein and lipid secretion, and remodel the lipid quality may help explain the long-term cardiometabolic benefits of physical activity observed in epidemiological studies. Importantly, our data suggest that even if individuals experience lapses in exercise adherence—a common barrier in real-world settings—resuming endurance exercise can rapidly restore and potentiate metabolic benefits.

Our findings in human subjects further strengthen the translational relevance of the persistent effects of exercise on liver function, which aligns with epidemiological evidence indicating that higher level of physical activity and cardiorespiratory fitness inversely associates with MASLD prevalence [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. We find that baseline aerobic fitness (VO2Max) and prior self-reported physical activity levels predict the magnitude of the circulating CES activity following a 6-week exercise training period. Although our human study did not directly incorporate the training–detraining–retraining paradigm, our findings parallel our murine data showing that prior high exercise exposure potentiates CES induction during retraining. These observations also align with the concept of metabolic memory originally described in patients with diabetes [29], whereby prior metabolic conditioning confers durable protective effects long after the initial intervention. In this regard, we posit that prior aerobic conditioning may similarly imprint a persistent hepatic program that facilitates enhanced lipid handling and systemic metabolic regulation.

At the cellular level, our data highlight a potential exercise-PPARα axis in the liver. Compared to mice without prior training, endurance retraining enhances PPARα-activated genes and, aided by the circadian BMAL1/CLOCK/NPAS2 regulators, it coordinates a response to amplify protein and lipid secretion. Further, predictive transcription factor analysis based on retraining transcription identified the enrichment of PPARα-RXR and BMAL1/CLOCK/NPAS2 regardless of diet, pointing to a shared hepatic memory. This regulatory liver axis may be triggered by adipose-derived lipids such as PC (18:0/18:1) (also called PC (36:1)) [79], which show a diurnal rhythm and coordinate fuel utilization. Moreover, the increase in hepatic PC (18:0/18:1) production is driven by PPARδ activation, and the release of this lipid stimulates muscle fatty acid uptake and fatty acid oxidation [79]. In addition to Pparα increases, endurance retraining increased hepatic Pparδ expression regarless of diet (VWR/CD adj. p = 0.003 and VWR/HFD adj. p = 0.077). In the context of tumor progression, interorgan lipid transfer from the liver into circulation acts as a funnel of excess sugars like fructose towards LPC synthesis, which are then used by the growing tumor for membrane growth, and disruption of hepatic fructose flux reduces tumor growth [94]. We have demonstrated that exercise retraining enhances muscle growth and that this is supported by improvements in fatty acid oxidation [38]. Thus, we propose that recurrent exercise entrains the liver to respond rapidly to supply and synchronize energy metabolism during and after exercise.

Conclusion

Endurance retraining induces hepatic exercise memory that restores and amplifies the glucose- and lipid-regulatory benefits lost after detraining. This memory is marked by persistent transcriptomic reprogramming, distinct lipid remodeling, and enhanced secretory function which we propose might be coordinated by a PPARα-RXR-CLOCK axis. These findings reveal the liver as a central hub of exercise memory and highlight retraining as a powerful strategy to sustain protection against MASLD and cardiometabolic disease, even after lapses in activity.
