“My eating disorder story”: an interpretative phenomenological analysis of social media, narrative identity, and patient influencers in eating disorder recovery
Authors: Lola Xie, Xiaoxu Ding, Rachel Patterson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs recovery stories about eating disorders proliferate on social media, many individuals with lived experience are emerging as informal health influencers. While these online spaces can offer community and information, they also shape how individuals understand and perform their identities in recovery. This study examines how young women with clinically diagnosed eating disorders, at different stages of recovery, actively and intentionally document and share their recovery journeys on social media, and how they use these platforms not only to seek support, but also to construct, maintain, and sometimes step away from patient influencer identities.MethodsWe conducted in-depth, semi-structured interviews with 19 women (mean age = 21.47) in the United States who actively documented their eating disorder recovery journeys on platforms like Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube. Using interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA), we explored how participants made sense of social media’s role across different stages of their recovery.ResultsFindings reveal a U-shaped pattern of social media engagement. Participants initially used social media in ways that reinforced disordered behaviors, immersing themselves in content that emphasized dieting, body comparison, and perfectionism. During early recovery, many chose to withdraw from these platforms to protect their mental health and reduce triggers. Later, they re-engaged with social media more intentionally, seeking out pro-recovery communities, reliable health information, and opportunities to tell their stories. Through public storytelling, participants reconstructed their identities, found accountability, and connected deeply with peers. However, some eventually distanced themselves from eating disorder-centered content to grow beyond the patient influencer identity, reflecting the fluid, evolving nature of recovery and online self-presentation.ConclusionsBy centering the voices of patient influencers, this study highlights how social media functions both as a risk and a resource in eating disorder recovery, simultaneously shaping psychological well-being, social support, and identity work. These insights underscore the need to view patients as active agents navigating digital spaces and call for treatment approaches that address the online environments where recovery, support, and stigma are continually negotiated.

Introduction

Mental health management in the digital age

Young people today increasingly turn to the internet as a primary source of health information, particularly for mental health concerns [40]. This shift accelerated during the COVID-19 pandemic, which limited in-person services and normalized digital interaction for support-seeking [41]. Prior to their first engagement with formal mental health services, most young users have already conducted substantial online searches about their condition [46]. Notably, there are expectations now for mental health providers to be social media literate, and users value therapist recommendations for trustworthy digital resources [4, 44].

Although social media has long been scrutinized for its role in promoting unrealistic beauty standards and poor mental health outcomes [14, 45], growing evidence positions it as a more complex ecosystem. Platforms such as TikTok, Instagram, and YouTube are not only sources of entertainment, but also sites where young users actively seek mental health-related content, including coping strategies, peer support, and self-help interventions [20, 37]. While algorithms can intensify harmful content through echo chambers, they can also enable the creation of curated, peer-led recovery communities [3]. This duality presents a paradox: social media can be both a risk factor for psychological distress and a valuable tool for managing mental health.

The rise of patient influencers

At the center of this paradox emerges a hybrid figure: the patient influencer. Often categorized within the broader category of social media microcelebrities, patient influencers are individuals with lived experience of a particular health condition who share personal narratives, medical information, and health-related opinions through digital and social media platforms to educate, support, and influence peers, caregivers, and broader public audiences [56]. In existing literature, patient influencers are typically distinguished from formally credentialed medical professionals in terms of their source of authority; rather than professional training, they derive credibility from lived experience and emotional transparency [15, 57]. Importantly, this distinction is contextual rather than categorical, as individuals with lived experience may move between patient, advocate, and professional roles over time. Patient influencers publicly share their health journeys, often blending personal storytelling, advocacy, and peer support in ways that engage and resonate with large online audiences.

Functioning as multidimensional agents of informal health communication, Lee and Lee [26] identified several roles for patient influencer. First, they serve as peer educators, translating medical knowledge and lived experience into digestible and relatable content for audiences with diverse personal and literacy background. Second, they offer emotional support, fostering community and solidarity through public vulnerability. Third, they act as narrative framers, constructing coherent, often hopeful, narratives of illness and recovery that shape both personal meaning-making and public understanding of health.

Their content often serves a dual purpose: personal catharsis and public education. This blend of affective labor and informal communication positions them as both powerful and precarious figures in the digital health ecosystem [1, 28]. While many audiences view influencers with lived experience as more authentic and relatable than clinical experts [36], their perceptions of influencers’ tone, posting frequency, and whether the content is being used for monetization can substantially moderate these perceptions. Sponsored content or perceived self-promotion can undermine their perceived authenticity, even when the content remains informative [7]. These tensions highlight the need for deeper inquiry into how patient influencers construct, negotiate, and communicate health narratives in participatory digital spaces.

Becoming patient influencers: a missing perspective

Recent scholarship has increasingly examined the role of “patient influencers” as informal health communicators, particularly in how they disseminate information, engage followers, and shape public understanding of illness through personal storytelling [57]. Much of this work focuses on the communicative functions of patient influencers, such as trust-building, health promotion, and destigmatization, and on how their audiences interpret or are affected by their content [16, 27]. However, considerably less attention has been paid to the experiences of the influencers themselves: how they negotiate the emotional labor of disclosure, manage the pressures of visibility, and balance recovery with public performance.

Mental health self-disclosure is increasingly framed as both therapeutic and strategic. Self-presentation as “raw and relatable but still aspirational” [16] is now a valued currency in influencer economies, particularly for young women navigating platformed femininity [52]. While disclosures may reduce stigma [11], they also expose influencers to audience judgment, potential backlash, and internal pressure to sustain an identity of transparency, even amid ongoing struggles [12, 31]. For some, narratives of failure, relapse, or emotional breakdown are equally commodified as content, raising ethical tensions around vulnerability-as-performance.

In addition to external pressures, patient influencers face internal psychological challenges. The demands of visibility, consistency, and parasocial intimacy can contribute to burnout, emotional exhaustion, and identity diffusion [30]. Research has shown that high levels of platform engagement correlate with elevated stress, anxiety, and strained relationships, particularly among influencers with large followings [39]. Ironically, while these influencers often support others in navigating mental health issues, their own well-being is often jeopardized by the very visibility that underpins their influence [57].

Eating disorders patient influencer

Within the specific context of eating disorders, social media’s dual roles of providing harmful or beneficial content are magnified. Eating disorders are serious mental health conditions characterized by persistent disturbances in eating behaviors and distorted thoughts about food and body image. While they can affect individuals across lifespans and gender identities, eating disorders disproportionately impact young women and rank among the leading causes of disability and mortality in this demographic [6, 13, 58]. Despite growing public awareness, eating disorders are still widely misunderstood and often mischaracterized as lifestyle choices rather than chronic psychological disorders.

Online communities for people with eating disorders have been consistently scrutinized due to the misconceptions around the mechanisms of these illnesses and the high risk of dangerous and life-threatening medical complications. Whitehead [55] notes that journalistic examinations of the community have emphasized the “dangerous,” “deadly,” and “destructive” quality of such online communities, thereby “leading to the stigmatization of the community as whole.” However, individuals with eating disorders continue to create these online communities, in part due to the loneliness and isolation that often accompany the condition.

Decades of research have shown that traditional and new media reinforce narrow body ideals, foster social comparison, and contribute to internalized body dissatisfaction and disordered eating behaviors [24, 50, 51].

Social media, in particular, intensifies this dynamic through algorithmic amplification of appearance-focused content and 24/7 exposure to curated self-presentations, emerging evidence suggesting that platform recommendation systems may adapt to users’ engagement histories in ways that increase exposure to eating-disorder-related material among already at-risk users [21]. Within these algorithmically structured environments, thematic analyses of eating disorder–related social media content further suggest that recovery-oriented, pro-recovery, and potentially triggering content often coexist within the same digital environments, exposing users to contradictory messages that can both support and undermine recovery efforts [19].

At the same time, however, social media offers unique affordances that complicate its reputation as purely harmful. Despite a “threefold increase in residential eating disorder treatment programs from 2011 to 2021” that reflects the increase in prevalence and severity of these conditions, professional treatment can still be inaccessible due to location, insufficient insurance coverage, and lack of screening for eating disorders in minority populations [53]. While professional treatments for eating disorders remain limited and stigmatized [42], online communities can provide peer-to-peer guidance, narrative healing, and opportunities for identity reformation grounded in shared lived experience [18, 29]. Au and Cosh [2] further demonstrate the value of peer-led recovery communities in this context, suggesting that shared lived experience can foster validation and hope in ways that differ from, but complement, formal clinical support.

For individuals in eating disorder recovery, platforms like YouTube, TikTok, and Instagram can therefore function as tools for emotional expression, community support, peer accountability, and health information exchange. However, recent qualitative research highlights that benefiting from these environments is rarely passive. Nikolova and LaMarre [38] show that individuals in eating disorder recovery engage in strategic and ongoing efforts to navigate platforms shaped by competing biopedagogies of health, body norms, and recovery. Rather than simply consuming content, participants actively manage their social media experiences by unfollowing, muting, blocking, reinterpreting, or disengaging from content in ways intended to protect recovery and maintain psychological safety. Hockin-Boyers et al. [17] conceptualize such practices as digital pruning, highlighting both the agency involved in curating safer online environments and the laborious, ongoing effort required to maintain them. Through this cautious curation, some users repurpose social media as a site of self-management, self-reflection, and narrative healing.

This paradox that social media as both a risk and a resource raises important questions about the digital behaviors of eating disorder patients, particularly those who move beyond content consumption to content creation. Yet few studies systematically explore these “celebrity” recovery storytellers: how their self-disclosure shapes their own recovery and influences others.

While many individuals in eating disorder recovery use social media to seek connection or document their experiences, not all such engagement constitutes influencer activity. In this study, we distinguish patient influencers from individuals who use social media more quietly or primarily for community-oriented support. Patient influencer practices are characterized less by follower counts than by intention, visibility, and communicative labor: participants described consciously addressing an audience beyond their immediate social network, curating and planning content, engaging with followers, and orienting their posts toward helping, educating, or motivating others. This public-facing orientation often brings heightened visibility and, in some cases, forms of microcelebrity, though influencer status in this context is not reducible to numerical reach alone. Rather, influencerhood emerges through sustained audience engagement, perceived responsibility to others, and the positioning of one’s recovery narrative as a shared resource rather than a private record. This distinction helps clarify why not all recovery-related content creators experience the same pressures, rewards, or identity negotiations associated with patient influencer roles.

Addressing the gap

Despite growing attention to patient influencers and recovery-oriented social media use, little is known about how individuals with eating disorders come to occupy these public-facing roles, how such identities evolve across recovery trajectories, or how the labor of recovery storytelling shapes their own well-being. Existing research has focused primarily on content characteristics or audience effects, leaving the lived experiences of patient influencers themselves underexamined, particularly within eating disorder recovery contexts.

This study addresses these gaps by examining the lived experiences of young women in eating disorder recovery who actively document and share their recovery experiences on social media, with particular attention to how patient influencers navigate self-disclosure, content creation, and peer engagement across different stages of recovery. By centering their lived experiences, this study contributes to theoretical efforts to conceptualize eating disorder recovery as a relational and evolving process shaped through publicly visible and digitally mediated interactions. It further informs clinical practice by highlighting the emotional labor, self-regulation, and risks involved in public recovery storytelling, and offers insights relevant to digital health policy discussions concerning platform responsibility, algorithmic exposure, and the need for ethically guided support structures for recovery-oriented content creators.

To guide this inquiry, the following research questions are proposed:

RQ1: How do individuals in eating disorder recovery use social media to construct and share personal narratives as patient influencers?

RQ2: What types of support and health information do patient influencers seek and provide through their social media platforms?

RQ3: What challenges do eating disorder patient influencers encounter in balancing authenticity, recovery, and audience expectations on social media?

Methods

Study design

To examine how young women with eating disorders engage with social media during recovery, the lead author conducted in-depth, semi-structured one-on-one interviews. The study focused on how participants used social media to navigate recovery, identity, and support, with particular attention to the challenges and opportunities that emerge at the intersection of patienthood and influencerhood. Specifically, the study examined how perceptions of agency and confidence in recovery shaped participants’ engagement with digital health support over time.

Given the study’s emphasis on lived experience and meaning-making across recovery trajectories, we employed in-depth qualitative interviews and interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA). In-depth interviews were chosen to facilitate an in-depth exploration of the lived experiences of young women in eating disorder recovery who publicly document their recovery online. Recovery from an eating disorder is an emotionally sensitive, deeply personal, and heterogeneous process, with meanings that vary substantially across individuals and over time. Qualitative interviews are therefore particularly appropriate for capturing participants’ own interpretations of recovery, self-presentation, and social media engagement, allowing for analytic attention to nuance, complexity, and subjectivity that would be difficult to access through standardized quantitative measures alone.

IPA was chosen as the analytic approach because it enables an idiographic, in-depth examination of how individuals interpret and make sense of significant life experiences within their specific social contexts. Epistemologically, the study is grounded in interpretative phenomenology, which assumes that participants actively make sense of their lived experiences and that researchers, in turn, engage in a second-order interpretative process to understand participants’ meaning-making.

Ethics approval and consent

This study received ethics approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee at Hong Kong Baptist University (Approval No. REC/23–24/0210). All participants provided informed consent prior to participation and were informed of the voluntary nature of the study, their right to withdraw at any time, and the measures taken to protect confidentiality and anonymity.

Recruitment

Participants were recruited based on the following inclusion criteria: aged 18 and above, self-identified women, with a current or past experience of a clinically diagnosed eating disorder, including anorexia, bulimia, binge eating disorder, or other specified eating disorder (OSFED), self-identified as being “in recovery” or “fully recovered,” and active in documenting their recovery journey on social media.

Participants were not recruited based on a predefined “influencer” label. Rather, recruitment involved self-identification as women in eating disorder recovery who publicly documented their recovery on social media. All participants engaged in intentional, public-facing recovery content creation (e.g., maintaining recovery-focused accounts, regularly posting recovery-related content, and interacting with audiences beyond their immediate offline networks). The term patient influencer is therefore used as an analytic construct to describe participants’ practices and roles, rather than as a category of self-identification required at recruitment.

Purposive sampling was used to identify potential participants. Invitations were sent to public Instagram, YouTube, and TikTok accounts featuring eating disorder recovery narratives. Additional participants were recruited through a local eating disorder support group in Northeastern United States. Snowball sampling was employed to expand the pool, with existing participants referring others who met the criteria. A total of 19 participants took part in the study.

Participants

All participants identified as women and were based in the United States. Of the 19 participants, 14 identified as White, 3 as Asian, and 2 as non-white Hispanic. The average age was 21.47 years, SD = 2.17. In terms of eating disorder history, 10 reported a diagnosis of bulimia, 3 reported anorexia, and 6 reported binge eating disorder. At the time of the interview, 16 participants identified as “in recovery,” and 3 as “fully recovered.” Table 1 summarizes participants’ clinical characteristics and platform use.

Participants’ social media followings varied substantially, ranging from several hundred to over 500,000 followers, reflecting diverse levels of visibility and audience reach among those documenting recovery online. Participants also differed in the intensity and duration of their engagement with recovery-oriented social media communities. At the time of interview, most reported sustained involvement over several months to multiple years, with patterns ranging from frequent daily engagement (e.g., posting, commenting, and direct messaging) to more intermittent or observational participation. These variations provided important contextual depth for examining how recovery-related social media use was experienced, interpreted, and renegotiated across different stages of recovery. Given the niche and publicly visible nature of recovery-focused social media content, care was taken to limit the disclosure of highly specific contextual details that could increase the risk of participant re-identification. To further protect participant confidentiality in this niche and publicly visible population, detailed demographic characteristics such as age and ethnicity are not reported at the individual level in Table 1.

Procedure

Semi-structured interviews were conducted by the lead researcher on Zoom with all participants. All interviews lasted 45 min to one hour, and the interview guide was split into four sections asking participants about (1) their eating disorder and recovery history, (2) their social media use in general in eating disorder recovery, (3) their content creation motivation, routine, and challenges, and (4) their role as patient influencers and the health, emotional and social consequences associated with being an influencer. While the interview guide included 8–10 questions per section, the format was flexible, allowing participants to reflect and move across topics organically.

Consistent with an interpretative phenomenological stance, interview questions were designed to elicit participants’ subjective interpretations and reflections on their experiences, rather than to test predefined theoretical constructs. Thus, the interviews followed a flexible guide designed to elicit participants’ lived experiences, interpretations, and meaning-making around recovery and social media use. Questions were open-ended and adaptive, encouraging participants to articulate how they understood their experiences rather than responding to predefined constructs. Example prompts included: “Can you tell me about the moment you decided to document your recovery publicly?” “How did sharing your recovery online influence how you understood yourself during that time?” “In what ways did engaging with others online shape your sense of identity or confidence in recovery?” and “How has your relationship with sharing changed as your recovery progressed?”

Given their experience with both eating disorder recovery and social media communication, participants were articulate and reflective, often drawing connections across different phases of their journey. We conducted member-checking by inviting two participants, who expressed interest in staying engaged with the project during their interviews, to review a summary of the preliminary findings. Both participants provided feedback in follow-up sessions, verified, and confirmed that the identified findings resonated with their understanding and experiences.

Due to the sensitivity of the topic, participants were provided with a written consent form in advance. They were informed that interviews would be recorded for research purposes only and could opt out of any questions they found distressing. At the beginning of each session, the lead author reminded participants of their right to withdraw or skip any part of the discussion.

Consistent with the concept of information power [33], sample adequacy was guided by the specificity of the study aim, the relevance and homogeneity of the sample, and the depth of participants’ accounts. We aimed for a sufficient number of cases to allow both detailed idiographic analysis and meaningful cross-case interpretation.

Analytical tool

All interviews were transcribed verbatim by the researchers. Pseudonyms were assigned to protect participant identities. The authors employed Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) to examine the data, a method well-suited for exploring lived experiences in health research involving small, specialized samples [5]. IPA is grounded in interpretative phenomenology and characterized by a double hermeneutic process, in which participants seek to make sense of their experiences while the researcher engages in interpretative meaning-making [48]. In this study, analysis remained closely anchored in participants’ language and idiographic accounts, while acknowledging the researcher’s active interpretative role. The method has been effectively applied to health research on topics such as addiction [47], autism [22], and eating disorders [17].

Analysis followed a three-step process. First, the lead author repeatedly read each transcript to gain familiarity with the data, highlighting significant statements and preliminary reflections. Second, the statements were coded into emergent themes. In the third stage, relationships between these themes were examined, in order to group them into broader conceptual categories while maintaining close alignment with participants’ language and perspectives. Throughout the analysis, two authors coded and reviewed the data collected independently and collaboratively. Regular peer debriefing sessions with co-authors were conducted throughout the duration of analysis to reflect on emerging interpretations and assumptions. During data analysis, we prioritized depth of qualitative data. Sample size was determined based on the richness and complexity of the data rather than numerical saturation alone.

Consistent with IPA’s epistemological orientation, meaning was understood as co-constructed through participants’ sense-making and the researchers’ interpretative engagement. The lead author is a health communication researcher with prior lived experience of anorexia, which informed sensitivity to the emotional, relational, and identity-based dimensions of eating disorder recovery. At the same time, the lead author has never publicly shared her recovery or occupied an influencer or patient influencer role, positioning her as an analytic outsider to the specific practices of public recovery storytelling examined in this study.

Reflexive practices, including memo writing, peer debriefing with co-authors, and iterative discussion of analytic decisions, were employed throughout analysis to critically examine how the researcher’s positionality shaped interpretation and to ensure that findings remained grounded in participants’ accounts. While interpretation is inherent to IPA, care was taken to distinguish between participant accounts and the lead author’s own analytical insights. In the findings section, we present participants’ narratives and then offer interpretative commentary grounded in their lived experiences. This reflexive positioning aligns with IPA’s epistemological commitment to interpretative phenomenology, in which analytic insight emerges through sustained engagement with participants’ accounts rather than claims of objective neutrality.

Findings

In keeping with interpretative phenomenological analysis, excerpts are presented prior to interpretative commentary, which remains grounded in participants’ language and contexts. While participants did not explicitly use terms such as “patient influencer” or “digital self-manager,” these labels are introduced analytically to capture recurring patterns of practice, responsibility, and meaning-making observed across accounts.

Social media use: from consumption to creation

Participants in this study described a complex and evolving relationship with social media throughout their eating disorder journey, reflecting how meanings attached to social media shifted over time and recovery stages. As digital natives, all participants reported heavy social media use both prior to and during recovery, but they also described substantial changes in engagement over time. Collectively, their usage patterns followed a U-shaped curve: intense activity before recovery, a steep decline during the early phase of healing, and then a gradual, more intentional return to digital platforms as recovery progressed. Across accounts, this pattern functioned less as a uniform behavioral sequence than as an interpretative trajectory participants used to make sense of shifting recovery needs and perceived risk.

Pre-recovery: algorithmic immersion and comparative harm

Pre-recovery, participants reported using social media heavily, both to consume and create content. Social platforms were not just places for entertainment or staying in touch with friends; they became deeply entangled with diet culture, body ideals, and performance of perfection. Many described algorithmically curated feeds saturated with “before and after” transformations, fitness influencer routines, calorie-tracking posts, and glamorized health narratives."I found a lot of diet and food-related content on my ‘for you’ page across platforms and back then, I would always click on them to read more and there would be more showing up… I ended up having an Instagram feed that was completely about trendy diets, workout routines of celebrities or influencers, and worst of all, ‘before and after’ photos".(Participant #13)

Taken together, these accounts illustrate how algorithmically curated social media environments functioned not merely as passive backdrops, but as active contexts that intensified comparison, reinforced disordered meanings, and shaped participants’ early sense-making around bodies, health, and self-worth prior to recovery.

Early recovery: strategic withdrawal and emotional protection

The turning point often came when participants formally recognized their eating disorder, either through medical diagnosis or a personal breaking point. At this stage, nearly all participants either voluntarily stepped away from social media or were advised by professionals to do so."It was extremely difficult to keep my perfect life going on social media… Being completely offline was scary, but it turned out to be one of the best decisions I made".(Participant #9)

During this withdrawal phase, participants reported significant mental relief. Fourteen participants also began professional treatment (therapy, outpatient program, or hospitalization) around this time, reinforcing the idea that early recovery often requires stepping back from digital spaces to avoid triggers and regain clarity.

Later recovery: intentional re-engagement and meaningful use

However, social media reentered participants’ lives during later stages of recovery, this time in a very different role. Rather than using it for comparison or perfectionism, participants sought it out for information, connection, and support. Some reentered gradually, cautiously curating their feeds and setting boundaries; others returned with a mission to document their journey, connect with like-minded individuals, or create spaces that didn’t exist when they were first struggling."I started using social media again, but this time it was different. I followed recovery hashtags, joined a few recovery Facebook groups, and it just felt more purposeful… like I was building something for myself".(Participant #11)

"I started using social media again, but this time it was different. I followed recovery hashtags, joined a few recovery Facebook groups, and it just felt more purposeful… like I was building something for myself".

(Participant #11)

By the end of this curve, participants were no longer just passive users. They had begun to act as patient influencers, actively shaping their recovery narratives, offering peer guidance, and co-constructing new communities of care. Analytically, we conceptualize this U-shaped trajectory as a temporal pattern of meaning-making, in which participants repeatedly reinterpreted social media in relation to shifting emotional capacity, embodied recovery, and perceived risk across recovery stages. This interpretation resonates with qualitative recovery scholarship that conceptualizes eating disorder recovery as cyclical, contingent, and repeatedly reworked over time, rather than as a fixed endpoint [8, 32].

Social media as a source of health information

In the later stages of their recovery, participants re-engaged with social media not as a source of comparison or pressure, but as a place to actively search for meaningful, pro-recovery health information. Social media became a tool for learning, about their conditions, about how others were coping, and about practical tips for navigating the ups and downs of recovery. This shift marked a clear redefinition of how participants used digital spaces, moving away from passive exposure and toward intentional, selective information gathering.

Several participants shared how, after distancing themselves from toxic content, they began to discover what many called “the other side” of social media, a side filled with recovery-oriented content, medical advice from professionals, and patient-shared experiences that felt grounded, hopeful, and honest."YouTube saved my life. My friend sent me a video about intuitive eating… I was hesitant, but eventually I opened it. It was a registered dietitian talking about traps in a so-called ‘healthy’ lifestyle and it was exactly what I needed to hear".(Participant #17)

"YouTube saved my life. My friend sent me a video about intuitive eating… I was hesitant, but eventually I opened it. It was a registered dietitian talking about traps in a so-called ‘healthy’ lifestyle and it was exactly what I needed to hear".

(Participant #17)

What made this content particularly valuable was that it filled gaps participants often experienced in traditional treatment. Many reported that medical appointments were limited in time and scope, and social media became a space to supplement those resources, especially when emotional or practical concerns arose between therapy sessions."I learned so much there, things rarely addressed by my doctors, things I can’t really talk about with my friends, and just things only we [people with EDs] know".(Participant #8)

The types of information participants sought out ranged from concrete (nutrition facts, recovery timelines, treatment options) to abstract (coping with weight gain, understanding relapse, self-compassion practices). This diversity of content was delivered through a range of formats: Instagram posts, Reddit threads, recovery YouTube videos, TikToks, and blog posts.

A common thread across accounts was the realization that social media itself was neutral. Its value or danger depended on how it was used. Early in their journey, participants found themselves trapped in algorithm-driven spirals of diet culture and body idealization. But once their online behaviors changed, many discovered how those same algorithms could begin to push recovery-centered, positive, and affirming content."When I was deep in it, my feed was full of diets, workouts, before-and-afters. But when I started engaging with recovery content…liking, following, commenting…everything changed. It felt like I had entered a new part of the internet".(Participant #13)

"When I was deep in it, my feed was full of diets, workouts, before-and-afters. But when I started engaging with recovery content…liking, following, commenting…everything changed. It felt like I had entered a new part of the internet".

(Participant #13)

That shift wasn’t automatic, though. Participants described needing to critically evaluate the content they encountered, actively filter out triggering posts, and sometimes create new accounts altogether to separate recovery from their pre-recovery digital identities. This practice, defined as digital pruning by Hockin-Boyers and colleagues [17], allowed participants to reconfigure their feeds, removing harmful content and cultivating a safer, more affirming digital environment."I created a new account for recovery. My old one was full of stuff that would make me spiral. Now I only follow accounts that uplift me or help me learn something. I don’t engage with anything that makes me feel bad".(Participant #11)

Over time, participants described gaining confidence, not only in their ability to find the right information online, but in their ability to navigate social media thoughtfully. For many, this included learning to block or unfollow harmful content, question misleading posts, and intentionally interact with content that supported their well-being."At first, I’d still get triggered. Across participants’ narratives, evolving engagement with social media was described as a reorientation in how recovery was understood and enacted, which we interpret as a key transformation: from vulnerable content consumers to empowered digital self-managers, capable of curating their own information environments in ways that supported their healing.

Social media as a peer support system

As participants progressed in their recovery, social media became more than just a space to gather health information. It transformed into a site of emotional connection, community building, and mutual support. Through digital communities formed around shared experiences, participants found a level of understanding, validation, and solidarity that many reported lacking in their offline lives.

Many participants described encountering recovery-focused online spaces, via Reddit, Instagram hashtags, or YouTube comments, as providing emotional relief and a sense of belonging."It was a group of eating disorder patients sharing, basically anything, with one another… You finally found a group of people that knows exactly what you were going through, and all of a sudden, you felt safe to talk about your story, confusion, concerns, and frustrations with them".(Participant #4)

"It was a group of eating disorder patients sharing, basically anything, with one another… You finally found a group of people that knows exactly what you were going through, and all of a sudden, you felt safe to talk about your story, confusion, concerns, and frustrations with them".

(Participant #4)

This digital peer support was not one-sided. Participants frequently described themselves both as support seekers and support providers, offering tips, sharing personal experiences, and responding to others’ struggles. In this way, they transitioned from isolated patients to active members of recovery communities, and in many cases, to informal mental health communicators in their own right."When I first posted about my eating disorder struggles… I tagged a bunch of pro-recovery hashtags. To my surprise, the post gained a lot of attention. People commented, some DMed me, and we ended up talking for hours. It made me feel so much better about myself". (Participant #8)

The sense of being understood without explanation, of speaking a common emotional language, was a key theme across interviews. Several participants emphasized that even the most well-meaning friends and family couldn’t always grasp the complexity of eating disorders. Participants also reflected on the therapeutic benefits of helping others. Giving advice or offering encouragement was not only helpful for those they supported, but it also helped strengthen their own recovery. The act of sharing was not framed as performative or superficial. Instead, participants described it as a kind of reciprocal caregiving, a cycle of emotional support, reflective growth, and identity reinforcement. Over time, they no longer saw themselves only as “in recovery,” but as part of a broader network of recovery influencers, whose collective knowledge, empathy, and resilience helped others feel less alone."It just made me realize there’s a community out there… If it wasn’t for this community, I wouldn’t be where I am today".(Participant #10)

Interpretatively, these accounts illustrate how meaning was co-constructed relationally, as participants interpreted their recovery through ongoing exchanges with others who shared similar embodied experiences. Digital communities gave participants a sense of belonging, a space for emotional expression, and a new role, as both recipients and sources of recovery support. Through these interactions, they developed confidence, built meaningful relationships, and took an active role in shaping supportive spaces for others, thus deepening their own recovery in the process.

Constructing recovery narratives: identity, reflection, and influence

Narrative identity work and meaning-making

Beyond offering information and community, social media provided participants with something deeper: a platform to make meaning of their eating disorder experiences and actively shape their evolving identities through storytelling, as participants reflected on past, present, and anticipated future selves. For more than half of the participants (n = 11), creating content became not only a form of expression but also a method of narrative reconstruction: a way to move from silence or shame into self-understanding and self-ownership."When I started to post about my bulimia online, for the first time, I accepted that I’m bulimic. I never thought of it as part of who I was… But telling my story on Reddit helped me recognize it, own it, and deal with the underlying issues. It was surprisingly helpful for my recovery".(Participant #7)

"When I started to post about my bulimia online, for the first time, I accepted that I’m bulimic. I never thought of it as part of who I was… But telling my story on Reddit helped me recognize it, own it, and deal with the underlying issues. It was surprisingly helpful for my recovery".

(Participant #7)

Through this act of public storytelling, participants engaged in what McAdams [34] calls narrative identity work. The use of personal stories to make sense of a difficult past and imagine a more coherent self-moving forward. Telling their story allowed them to reflect, contextualize, and often reframe their condition from something shameful into something survivable and even meaningful. This pattern aligns with recovery scholarship that conceptualizes eating disorder recovery as narrative reconstruction, in which individuals actively rework identity, meaning, and agency through storytelling rather than simply achieving symptom remission [8, 32]. From this perspective, recovery narratives function as ongoing practices of sense-making rather than retrospective accounts of illness.

Audience accountability and reflective self-monitoring

Several participants noted that documenting their recovery on social media held them accountable to their audience. This form of social presence, where others were watching, cheering, or relying on them, gave participants a powerful incentive to stick with their recovery plan.

“I wasn’t in a stable stage yet, but I started posting my meals. And that helped me stay on track. I felt like someone was watching, and I needed to keep going, not just for me, but also because people were learning from me.”(Participant #2)

(Participant #2)

Importantly, participants were not simply performing wellness; they were narrating transformation, in real time, with real complexity. Through their content, they documented setbacks, uncertainties, breakthroughs, and small everyday acts of care. The process of telling their recovery story online became a form of ongoing reflection, helping participants sort through emotional triggers, assess coping strategies, and notice patterns in their behaviors."Every time I wrote about my experience, I felt like I understood myself better. What was working, what wasn’t. I could look back at posts and think: ‘oh, I see what triggered that, or what helped me get through that day".(Participant #19)

"Every time I wrote about my experience, I felt like I understood myself better. What was working, what wasn’t. I could look back at posts and think: ‘oh, I see what triggered that, or what helped me get through that day".

(Participant #19)

Some participants went even further, describing how content creation helped them distinguish between their “eating disorder voice” and their own voice, a cognitive shift frequently mentioned in therapeutic contexts but now occurring through digital narration."When I was writing about my recovery on Instagram, it became clear to me what my own voice is and what my eating disorder’s voice is. That voice could be sneaky and make you do things you’d regret later. But reflecting in my posts helped me recognize it and take a step back before acting".(Participant #12)

These accounts illustrate how recovery was experienced not only behaviorally but as an ongoing reflective process through which participants actively made sense of their illness, progress, and identity over time. For many, this reflection was not just about personal insight. It was a means of rebuilding trust in themselves. By organizing their thoughts, naming their triggers, and seeing their growth over time, participants developed a more stable sense of identity, one rooted not in disorder, but in agency and resilience.

Distancing from eating disorder identity and letting go of the influencer role

While many participants used social media to publicly narrate and make sense of their recovery, a smaller but notable group described a different trajectory: the deliberate distancing from their eating disorder identity once they reached a more stable point in recovery. For these participants, recovery also meant letting go, not just of disordered behaviors, but of the online persona built around their conditions."Once I started to feel better, I didn’t want to be ‘the girl with an eating disorder’ online anymore. I archived all my old posts. It felt like shedding a skin".(Participant #5)

"Once I started to feel better, I didn’t want to be ‘the girl with an eating disorder’ online anymore. I archived all my old posts. It felt like shedding a skin".

(Participant #5)

This decision was often framed as an act of closure, a way to signal personal growth and move on to a new chapter. Some participants described it as reclaiming their social media presence for other purposes, such as posting travel content, fitness videos, or lifestyle vlogs that had little or nothing to do with recovery."I still use my account, but I don’t talk about eating disorder stuff anymore. I focus more on wellness and just my daily life. That part of me is over".(Participant #10)

This pivot reflects a complex identity shift. While early-stage patient influencers often found empowerment in disclosure, later-stage participants sometimes felt confined by their eating disorder narrative, especially when they felt their audience expected them to continue producing recovery content."People still DM me asking for advice, and sometimes I feel guilty not replying. But I’m not in that headspace anymore. I’m done being that version of me online".(Participant #15)

These accounts underscore an important insight: the patient influencer identity is not static. For some, it served a crucial role in healing. But over time, it could also become limiting, emotionally taxing, or misaligned with how participants saw themselves. Choosing to step away from that identity was, for these individuals, an extension of their recovery. It’s not a rejection of the community nor a disengagement from recovery itself, but a way to reclaim narrative agency on their own terms, reflecting a later-stage renegotiation of identity.

Taken together, these findings illustrate that narrative construction through social media is not a one-time act but an evolving process, one that reflects not only how participants made sense of their past but also how they envisioned their future. For many, documenting recovery publicly helped build coherence, agency, and accountability; it allowed them to externalize inner conflict and reshape a fragmented identity. Yet for others, that very narrative, once empowering, became something to outgrow. As their relationship with their eating disorder changed, so too did their relationship with the online identity built around it. In choosing to step away from eating disorder content or pivot to new topics, participants demonstrated a different kind of agency: the power to edit or even retire their public health narrative when it no longer served them.

Whether continuing or letting go, each decision reflected a deliberate form of identity work, shaped by introspection, audience dynamics, and the unique pressures of narrating illness in a digital age. Rather than progressing toward a singular “recovered” identity, participants’ narratives revealed ongoing revision, ambivalence, and recalibration, with meaning-making unfolding unevenly across time and platforms. Consistent with Conti’s [8] work, recovery narratives functioned less as closure and more as sites of continual identity negotiation, shaped by changing relationships to the self, audiences, and the illness history itself.

Challenges and opportunities of influencing while healing

While participants ultimately described social media as a valuable tool in their recovery, their journey toward reclaiming digital space was not linear or without complexity, reflecting ongoing, lived negotiations between bodily vulnerability, emotional regulation, and public visibility. In fact, much of their experience as patient influencers was marked by a constant balancing act, between vulnerability and self-protection, exposure and control, helping others and safeguarding themselves.

The initial challenge came from reentering social media after their break. Participants were keenly aware of the risks: algorithmic traps, residual comparisons, and emotionally charged content that could trigger relapse. Many described their return to platforms as cautious and intentional, often starting with new accounts, restricted follow lists, and heavy content filtering."It was not an easy decision… I still had urges to binge when I saw fitness content. But at the same time, it was nearly impossible to cut social media out of my life completely".(Participant #15)

(Participant #15)

These strategies reflect what scholars have termed digital pruning, a self-care practice where users unfollow, block, or mute content that triggers negative emotions [17]. Over time, participants not only managed their feeds more effectively but began to build digital environments explicitly designed to support recovery."I created a new Instagram account and a new YouTube channel for recovery. It was nice to have a space full of inspiration, empowerment, and body positivity. I rarely logged into my old accounts".(Participant #11)

Through this process, participants developed greater self-efficacy in navigating digital spaces. They learned to identify harmful content, recognize subtle emotional cues, and take proactive steps to curate healthier digital experiences. However, the role of patient influencer came with emotional and cognitive demands. Several participants discussed the pressure to appear “stable” or “in control” in front of their followers, even when they themselves were struggling. While participants did not frame these experiences in theoretical terms, we interpret these moments as lived tensions between embodied recovery realities and externally imposed expectations of what recovery “should” look like, revealing how meaning was continually negotiated under conditions of visibility.

Despite these challenges, participants framed their journey as deeply empowering. They described becoming more reflective, more critical of media, and more in tune with their values and needs. Social media, once a source of harm, had become a space where they could support others, express themselves, and most importantly reclaim ownership over their stories."I went from being someone who spiraled every time I opened Instagram to someone who helps others find recovery accounts and builds their own. In summary, social media posed both threats and therapeutic possibilities for participants in this study, not as opposing forces but as coexisting conditions that required continual negotiation. While participants developed greater digital literacy, boundary-setting skills, and confidence in using online spaces to support recovery, these capacities did not eliminate vulnerability, emotional labor, or conflict. Instead, participants’ experiences revealed recovery-oriented influencing as an unstable and relational practice, which could be shaped by relapse, audience expectations, visibility, and shifting personal needs. Exercising agency often involved not only speaking, but withholding; not only engaging, but stepping back; and not only narrating recovery, but resisting demands to perform it in prescribed ways. Through these ongoing negotiations, participants did not arrive at a fixed endpoint of empowerment but rather enacted a dynamic form of digital self-management in which recovery, identity, and influence were continually recalibrated.

Discussion

This study explored how young women in eating disorder recovery use social media not only as a source of information and support, but as a space for identity construction and self-management. By focusing on individuals who publicly document their recovery journeys, the study identifies the emergence of the patient influencer, an innovative role that transcends the boundaries across storyteller, informal health communicator, and digital peer mentor. While prior research has largely examined the effects of influencer content on audiences, this study centers the perspective of the influencers themselves, offering new insight into how recovery narratives are shaped, performed, and sometimes reconfigured as individuals move beyond the patient influencer identity.

Narrative identity as recovery work

One central finding of this study is that social media functioned as a powerful site for narrative identity construction during recovery, reflecting the idiographic ways participants made sense of their lived experiences within their own recovery trajectories. For many participants, publicly sharing their eating disorder experiences online marked a pivotal moment in their recovery, a transition from silence or shame to self-recognition and self-ownership. Through writing, filming, and reflecting via posts, participants began to reframe their illness not only as a period of suffering, but as one chapter within a broader life narrative. This process aligns with narrative identity theory, which suggests that individuals construct coherent self-understandings by organizing their experiences into stories that confer meaning, growth, and continuity (McAdams, 1997).

Participants’ posts served multiple functions: they externalized complex emotional experiences, tracked day-to-day struggles and progress, and rendered abstract therapeutic concepts (e.g., recognizing the “eating disorder voice”) more tangible. For some, social media documentation also functioned as a self-imposed accountability system. In this way, content creation was not merely a performance but a form of cognitive and emotional processing, helping participants gain insight into their triggers, patterns, and coping strategies. While many participants found healing through public storytelling, others reached a point where the identity of “eating disorder recovery influencer” no longer felt representative of who they were. Several participants described a deliberate choice to pivot away from recovery content and archive old posts, or shift toward new topics such as travel, lifestyle, or general wellness. This transition was framed as an act of closure or personal growth, letting go of an identity that had once been central to their healing but later became constraining. This finding highlights an important nuance: for some, the narrative identity built during recovery becomes a springboard, not a destination. It reflects the fluid nature of identity in digital spaces, where individuals engage in ongoing meaning-making by curating, revising, and sometimes discarding past selves. For patient influencers, recovery is not only about reclaiming control over the body and mind, but also about developing the capacity to redefine or relinquish recovery-based identities as they move across recovery stages and life contexts [8, 25].

Social media use as a u-shaped curve: from harm to healing

A further contribution of this study is the identification of a U-shaped trajectory in participants’ social media use throughout their recovery journey. Prior to acknowledging their eating disorders, participants reported high levels of engagement with social media, which often centered around diet culture, fitness trends, and curated performances of perfection. This early stage was marked by heightened social comparison, algorithmic reinforcement of disordered behaviors, and an uncritical consumption of relevant content, which collectively exacerbated ED symptoms. Participants described these digital environments as persistently “triggering,” “obsessive,” and “addictive,” reinforcing their illness-related beliefs and behaviors .

Entry into early recovery represented a critical turning point in the curve. Whether prompted by medical diagnosis, emotional burnout, or intervention from loved ones or clinicians, most participants withdrew from social media during this phase. Whether voluntary or clinician-recommended, this break was widely described as necessary and protective for recovery. It created space for offline reflection, reduced exposure to harmful content, and gave participants distance from the hyper-performance of digital life. Notably, this withdrawal was often supported by treatment programs, many of which discouraged or restricted media use as part of their approach.

The final phase of the curve began when participants re-engaged with social media, but this time with greater caution, purpose, and control. Rather than returning to their previous behaviors, participants began seeking out pro-recovery content, connecting with others experiencing similar challenges, and curating social media feeds that aligned with their values and progress. This process of re-engagement wasn’t immediate nor uniform; some participants returned slowly, others started brand new accounts, and nearly all reported a learning curve in how to filter, evaluate, and selectively engage with content.

This U-shaped pattern suggests that social media use in eating disorder recovery is not inherently harmful or beneficial, but fluid and contingent on how it is engaged over time. This temporal pattern aligns with recovery scholarship that conceptualizes eating disorder recovery as cyclical, uneven, and characterized by ongoing negotiation rather than linear progression toward a stable endpoint [23, 32]. From a media studies perspective, early engagement may reinforce disordered behaviors, whereas later engagement, marked by increased awareness and skill, can support recovery. This insight expands on earlier research that tends to categorize social media as either helpful or harmful [9], by offering a more temporal, developmental perspective. It also illustrates how participants’ media literacy and self-efficacy evolved in tandem with their recovery.

By the time participants re-entered social media spaces, many had developed “digital resilience,” the ability to navigate algorithmic environments, recognize triggering content, and actively create safer digital spaces. This progression highlights that an important part of recovery includes learning to navigate digital spaces, using boundaries that expand and contract as needed, in order to manage an omnipresent technological environment, with tools that are ingrained into many parts of everyday life. It involves learning how to engage with digital systems in ways that protect, support, and strengthen mental health.

Social media as health management tools

Consistent with prior research, participants described social media as a valuable supplement to formal treatment, particularly during periods when access to professional care was limited or unavailable. Participants actively sought out both expert-led content, such as videos by registered dietitians, and experiential insights from peers with lived experience. This mirrors findings by Naslund et al. [37] and Goodyear and Armour [20], who highlight the role of social media in expanding mental health literacy. However, this study extends those insights by showing how participants developed informational discernment over time as they learn to navigate misinformation, evaluate credibility, and select content that aligned with their recovery values through trial and error, as shown in the U-shaped engagement curve.

Echoing earlier findings on the therapeutic value of online peer support [49, 54], participants described online recovery communities as deeply affirming, nonjudgmental, and emotionally resonant. What emerged in this study, however, was the bidirectional nature of this support. Participants were not only supported but also became supporters, offering advice, encouragement, and shared experience to others. This aligns with Riessman’s [43] helper therapy principle, which suggests that providing support can be as therapeutic as receiving it. In the context of eating disorder recovery, this reciprocal exchange helped participants build confidence, feel useful, and strengthen their own coping strategies. In many ways, this process marked their transition from “patient” to “influencer,” not in the commercial sense, but as informal advocates and educators within their digital communities.

Emotional and ethical tensions of patient influencing

Finally, while becoming a patient influencer offered participants meaning, connection, and motivation, it also introduced a set of emotional and ethical tensions. Several participants described the pressure to appear recovered or stable, even even when their embodied recovery experiences remained uncertain, effortful, or fragile. Others associated emotional labor with being “always available” to followers, including responding to messages, offering advice, or maintaining a consistent presence despite burnout or relapse risk. These demands were further intensified by the implicit social contract of authenticity, when participants felt accountable not only for their own recovery but also for the perceived influence of their narratives on audience members who modeled their recovery efforts.

This tension between healing and helping reflects what some scholars describe as “vulnerability-as-performance” [28], where openness is both a tool for connection and a potential burden. Participants also noted moments of guilt or self-doubt about whether they were “qualified” to share advice, particularly during their own ongoing recovery. These findings suggest that while social media offers patient influencers powerful platforms for impact, it can also complicates boundaries between recovery, responsibility, and public identity. The development of ethical frameworks and support systems for non-professional health communicators remains limited and requires further scholarly attention.

Additionally, public recovery storytelling can unintentionally produce normative pressure by promoting a narrow ideal of “successful recovery” characterized by linear progress, aesthetic coherence, and positive emotions. Such representation of a “correct answer” may marginalize individuals with nonlinear or less visible recovery trajectories, reinforcing feelings of inadequacy or impostor syndrome. This finding highlights the importance of fostering space for diverse, imperfect, and evolving representations of recovery, and suggests that digital recovery storytelling should be accompanied by critical literacy and community care practices to mitigate unintended harms.

Practical implications

Clinically, these findings suggest that assessment of eating disorder recovery should extend beyond symptom presentation to include patients’ digital environments, particularly patterns of social media use, content exposure, and perceived emotional impact across recovery stages. Given the important role of social media in younger generations [40], clinical approaches that rely on long-term or permanent avoidance of digital platforms may be difficult to sustain in practice. Reframing social media avoidance as a form of strategic withdrawal may better support recovery by allowing individuals to develop skills, agency, and resilience before re-engaging with digital spaces in more intentional ways. Clinical treatment may benefit from explicitly incorporating conversations about social media use across recovery stages, including collaboratively identifying when withdrawal is protective, when re-engagement may be appropriate, and how patients can recognize early digital warning signs of emotional destabilization.

Incorporating digital literacy into treatment may support patients’ ability to re-engage online without compromising recovery. Importantly, participants’ difficulties did not stem from a lack of awareness about how social media operates [38]. Rather, their accounts point to the absence of guided and safeguarded navigation of digital spaces, through which individuals in recovery can learn how to engage, pause, or disengage when content becomes emotionally destabilizing. This suggests a policy gap not in digital literacy per se, but in the provision of structured support for in-the-moment self-regulation and recovery-oriented digital coping. For example, clinicians might support patients in developing individualized “digital coping plans” that outline preferred content boundaries, early warning signs of digital overload, and pre-agreed strategies for disengagement during periods of vulnerability.

At the platform level, study findings suggest a need to reconsider how eating disorder recovery content is categorized and algorithmically distributed. Participants’ accounts indicate that recovery-oriented posts are often treated as generic wellness content, exposing creators to triggering material and heightened audience scrutiny. Platform policies that recognize recovery content as health-adjacent and context-sensitive may help mitigate unintended harms. Moreover, participants’ reliance on self-taught strategies such as digital pruning highlights a policy gap in platform design. Greater transparency around algorithmic content resurfacing and expanded user control over recovery-relevant content categories may reduce emotional strain for individuals navigating recovery in highly personalized digital environments [35].

Beyond platform governance, these findings point to important public health policy implications. Patient influencers are already functioning as informal health communicators, often developing recovery narratives and support practices outside formal organizational communication structures. This underscores the need not only for stronger support systems and ethical guidance for patient influencers, but also for the integration of lived-experience perspectives into institutional recovery communication frameworks, ensuring that public guidance reflects the realities of recovery while minimizing unintended harm.

For example, the collaboration between patient influencer Chris Henrie and the Eating Recovery Center illustrates an ethically grounded partnership model in which experiential knowledge is paired with clinical oversight [10]. Recovery-relevant topics, such as navigating holiday-related triggers and body-related transitions, were communicated in ways that emphasized coping and care rather than prescriptive recovery ideals. This model demonstrates how experiential trust can be meaningfully incorporated within institutional contexts, highlighting the value of policy frameworks that support ethically guided collaboration between health organizations and lived-experience advocates.

Limitation and future research

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study’s sample was relatively homogenous: all participants identified as women, most were White, and all were based in the United States. The findings may not fully capture the experiences of individuals from different cultural, racial, gender, or geographic backgrounds. Second, consistent with interpretative phenomenological analysis, the findings reflect a co-constructed interpretative process shaped by participants’ accounts and the researcher’s reflexive engagement. While this hermeneutic approach allows for in-depth meaning-making, alternative interpretations are possible. Third, as a qualitative study based on self-reported experiences, the data reflects participants’ retrospective sense-making and may be influenced by recall bias or social desirability. Finally, the focus on public-facing influencers introduces a likely self-selection bias: individuals who openly document recovery online may differ in meaningful ways from those who recover privately or participate in closed or anonymous spaces, potentially limiting transferability.

This study opens several paths for future research. While the high mortality rates of eating disorders can pose a challenge for longitudinal studies, these types of studies could explore how patient influencer identities evolve over time, particularly in relation to shifting recovery status, platform migration (e.g., from eating disorders to wellness or fitness content), or burnout. More work is also needed on the emotional impact of audience engagement, both on influencers themselves and their followers. Additionally, examining non-Western and intersectional experiences of eating disorder recovery in digital spaces would provide a fuller picture of how sociocultural factors shape both narrative construction and digital health engagement. Finally, as more individuals turn to social media for health guidance, there is a growing need for interdisciplinary collaboration between researchers, clinicians, and patient influencers to co-create ethical standards and support structures for this emerging role.

Conclusion

This study highlights how young women in eating disorder recovery engage with social media not just as users, but as active agents in shaping their own healing. Through public storytelling, peer support, and intentional platform use, participants navigated the tensions of being both patients and influencers, constructing, revising, and sometimes retiring their digital identities in real time. The findings underscore the evolving nature of narrative identity in online spaces and offer a more nuanced understanding of how social media can function both as a risk factor and a recovery tool. As patient influencers increasingly shape the landscape of digital health communication, their experiences offer critical insights into the possibilities and complexities of healing in public.
