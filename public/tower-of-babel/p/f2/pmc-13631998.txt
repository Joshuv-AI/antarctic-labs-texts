Influence of ambient illumination on color difference (ΔE) and color adjustment potential of universal resin composites in 3D-printed teeth: an in vitro study
Authors: İkbal Esra Pehlivan, Merve Aksoy Yüksek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUniversal resin composites have been developed to simplify restorative procedures by improving color matching with surrounding tooth structures. However, the optical behavior of these materials may vary with environmental conditions, such as ambient illumination. The influence of different lighting conditions on the color-matching and color-adjustment potential (CAP) of universal composites remains insufficiently understood. This study aimed to evaluate the influence of different ambient light sources on the color difference (ΔE) and color adjustment potential (CAP) of universal resin composites restored in three-dimensional (3D) printed teeth.Materials and methodsThree universal resin composites were evaluated using standardized cavities prepared in 3D-printed tooth models. Color measurements were performed using a spectrophotometer under four ambient lighting conditions (3000 K, 4000 K, 5500 K, and 6500 K). Color differences (ΔE00) were calculated at two-time intervals (T1–T2 and T1–T3). The color adjustment potential-instrumental (CAP-I) values were calculated using the formula CAP-I = 1 − (ΔEdual/ΔEsingle). The effects of composite type, lighting conditions, and time interval on color outcomes were evaluated using nonparametric statistical tests with appropriate post hoc comparisons.ResultsAmbient lighting conditions significantly influenced ΔE values of the tested composites. Differences in color change were observed among light sources and composite materials across both time intervals. The combined pattern of composite type, illumination condition, and time interval indicated material-dependent changes in color-difference outcomes. CAP-I values also varied across composites and lighting conditions, indicating that instrumental color-adjustment outcomes were material- and illumination-dependent.ConclusionAmbient illumination influenced the instrumental color-difference outcomes and CAP-I values of universal resin composites. Standardization of lighting conditions during shade selection and color evaluation may therefore be important for reliable assessment of restorative color behavior.Keywords: Resin composites, Dental materials, Color perception, Spectrophotometry, Lighting, Dental restoration

Background

Ambient lighting conditions are critical external factors that shape the perceived color and blending behavior of universal resin composites in clinical dentistry. In both structurally colored (pigment-free) and pigment-based universal composites, lighting has been shown to affect color-matching accuracy, color-difference measurements (using various formulas, such as ΔE), and the blending or color-adjustment potential (CAP), which enables a restoration to visually merge with the surrounding tooth structure. The literature combines standardized instrumental color measurements under standardized lighting with analyses of blending behavior across background shades, thickness, translucency, and aging [1, 2]. However, data on the impact of variable light sources—reflecting clinical reality—on the color-matching performance of these materials remain limited [3]. To fill this gap, a more detailed assessment of the varying effects of different light sources on color deviations, calculated using CIE L*a*b* values and the CIEDE2000 formula, is necessary for evaluating the optical performance of materials [4, 5].

Lighting conditions critically shape the perceived color of resin composites and, consequently, their color-adjustment potential and color-matching performance. Synthesizing findings from recent in vitro and review studies, we summarize how light source spectra, color temperature, and brightness interact with the inherent optical properties of universal resin composites and how they affect (a) the color adjustment potential in terms of lightness, chroma, hue, and translucency (CAP00, ChrAP00, HAP00, LAP00, TAP00), and (b) practical color matching behavior in clinical scenarios. In this context, evaluating the optical response of restorative materials under different light spectra is of great importance for optimizing clinical esthetic success [6, 7]. Our study aims to quantitatively elucidate the impact of lighting conditions on the color adjustment potential of composites. This research focuses on the clinical implications of the sensitivity of the optical harmony between materials with varying levels of opacity and translucency and dental tissues to changes in lighting variables [8].

The color adjustment potential (CAP) and overall color harmony depend both on material properties and the lighting environment. In dental aesthetics, various studies have formalized color adjustment potentials within the CIEDE2000 framework and have categorized adjustments in lightness, chroma, hue, and translucency as CAP00, ChrAP00, HAP00, LAP00, and TAP00, respectively [9]. Lighting directly modulates perceived color through spectral power distribution (SPD), color temperature, and brightness, contributing to metamerism and to shading challenges in color harmony [10–12]. Additionally, the different wavelengths of the light sources used can alter the material’s translucency and scattering behavior, resulting in unpredictable variations in clinical aesthetic outcomes [13, 14]. This situation necessitates an in-depth investigation into how clinical light sources with different spectral properties affect the visual integration of restorations with the surrounding dental tissue [15].

Although the color-matching and color-adjustment potential of universal resin composites have been investigated under standardized experimental conditions, limited information is available regarding how different ambient illumination conditions affect ΔE00 and CAP-I outcomes in standardized 3D-printed Class IV restorations. Because clinical shade selection and esthetic evaluation are performed under variable lighting conditions, evaluating the influence of light sources with different color temperatures may provide a more clinically relevant understanding of these materials’ optical behavior. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the influence of different ambient light sources on the color difference (ΔE00) and instrumental color adjustment potential (CAP-I) of universal resin composites restored in standardized 3D-printed teeth. The study hypothesis was that ambient illumination, composite type, and time interval would influence ΔE00 and CAP-I values.

Material and method

Sample size determination

The sample size of the present study was determined a priori using G*Power software (version 3.1; Heinrich-Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). Because the primary comparisons were planned among composite groups under standardized experimental conditions, the initial calculation was based on a one-way ANOVA model with a moderate effect size (f = 0.25), 80% statistical power (1 − β = 0.80), and a 5% significance level (α = 0.05). This calculation indicated that at least 11 specimens per group would be required. Due to material and fabrication constraints, 10 specimens per group were included. This number was close to the calculated requirement and was consistent with specimen numbers commonly used in comparable in vitro color and CAP studies. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the multifactorial structure of the study, including the composite type, ambient light source, and time interval, represents a limitation for sample size estimation because the a priori calculation did not explicitly model all interaction terms. Therefore, the sample size calculation should be interpreted as an estimate for the primary group comparisons rather than as a fully powered multifactorial interaction analysis.

Specimen design and fabrication

Digitally designed tooth models were used in this study to ensure geometric standardization. Both intact tooth models and Black’s Class IV cavity preparations were generated using computer-aided design (CAD) software and subsequently manufactured using a three-dimensional (3D) printing system (Fig. 1).

Workflow of experimental design for CAP evaluation

The 3D-printed teeth were fabricated by EduDent Educational Materials (İstanbul, Türkiye) using HEX284 resin (Hexagon İleri Malzemeler ve Teknolojiler Tic. Ltd. Şti., İstanbul, Türkiye), a urethane acrylate-based, ultraviolet-light-curable model resin, in B1 shade. The material lacks biocompatibility certification and is classified as a model resin. No specific optical property data were available from the manufacturer.

During the digital design stage, a 1-mm-wide, 45 ° inclined bevel was incorporated along the enamel margins of the cavity preparation. Integrating the bevel directly into the digital model enabled consistent cavity geometry across all specimens and eliminated variability that might arise from manual bevel preparation. Furthermore, this design approach more closely simulated clinical cavity preparation and facilitated a more homogeneous optical transition at the tooth–restoration interface, thereby enabling a more reliable evaluation of the color-adjustment potential of the tested resin composite materials.

Preparation of single and dual specimens

Two specimen configurations were produced to evaluate the potential for color adjustment: single and dual specimens.

Single specimens were fabricated by producing resin composite replicas of intact 3D-printed teeth using a silicone index (Fig. 2). The composite replicas were polymerized using a light-emitting diode (LED) curing unit (Elipar S10, 3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA) with a light intensity of 1200 mW/cm² for 20 s from both the buccal and palatal surfaces.

Schematic representation of the specimens: (A) intact 3D-printed tooth (control specimen), (B) 3D-printed tooth restored with resin composite (dual specimen), and (C) tooth replicated using resin composite (single specimen)

Dual specimens were prepared by restoring 3D-printed teeth containing digitally designed Black’s Class IV cavities with resin composite. Silicone indices were obtained from intact 3D-printed tooth models to standardize the morphology and thickness of the restorations. All restorations were performed using these indices to ensure that the form and dimensions of the Class IV restorations were consistent across all composite materials (Fig. 2).

Adhesive procedure and restorative protocol

Before restoration, the cavity surfaces of the 3D-printed teeth were conditioned with 35% phosphoric acid etchant (GC Etchant, GC, Tokyo, Japan) for 20 s in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. The surfaces were then rinsed with water and gently air-dried for 10 s using compressed air at 0.25 MPa from approximately 2–5 mm.

Subsequently, a universal adhesive system (G-Premio Bond, GC, Tokyo, Japan) was applied to the cavity surfaces using a microbrush with an active scrubbing motion. The adhesive layer was air-thinned for 5 s under the same air-pressure conditions and then light-cured for 20 s using an LED curing unit.

Composite restorations were completed using a silicone index to standardize restoration morphology, followed by light polymerization for 20 s.

To ensure consistent curing conditions, the output intensity of the curing unit (1200 mW/cm²) was verified for every five specimens using a digital dental radiometer (Hilux Light Meter, Benlioğlu, Türkiye).

Color measurement

Color measurements were performed using a spectrophotometer (Spectroshade Micro; Medical High Technologies, Italy). Color measurements were performed in a darkened enclosure constructed to minimize uncontrolled external light exposure. The enclosure included an opening designed to accommodate the spectrophotometer tip. Type A silicone sockets were prepared for the roots of the specimens and fixed within the measurement apparatus to ensure standardized positioning during all measurements. The specimens were consistently placed in these sockets by their roots. Ambient lighting conditions were simulated using a light source capable of producing different color-temperature values. The light source was positioned within the apparatus to illuminate the specimens at a 45° angle. The tested illumination conditions were 3000 K, 4000 K, 5500 K, and 6500 K, representing commonly encountered ambient lighting environments. The 3000 K, 4000 K, 5500 K, and 6500 K conditions were intended to represent warm indoor illumination, neutral white clinical lighting, daylight-equivalent illumination, and cool daylight illumination, respectively. Although the spectrophotometer includes an internal illumination system, the external ambient light source was standardized to assess whether controlled surrounding illumination conditions could influence the recorded color coordinates under a fixed measurement geometry. Detailed spectral power distributions, illuminance, and color rendering index values for the light sources were not available; therefore, the illumination conditions were characterized primarily by their color temperatures.

To maintain measurement accuracy, the device was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions before every set of five measurements.

Measurements were conducted at three evaluation periods: 24 h, 1 week, and 1 month after specimen preparation. The selected evaluation periods were intended to represent early and short-term optical changes after restoration placement. The 24-hour measurement was used as the initial post-polymerization reference point, allowing early polymerization-related stabilization and initial water interaction to occur. The 1-week period was selected to evaluate early short-term changes in color behavior, whereas the 1-month period was included to determine whether the color difference and color adjustment potential remained stable or changed over a longer short-term interval. For each measurement, the L*, a*, and b* color coordinates were recorded, and these values were subsequently used to calculate the color difference (ΔE).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Because the data did not follow a normal distribution (p < 0.05), nonparametric statistical tests were applied.

Groupwise comparisons of ΔE values were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test according to predefined composite, ambient light source, and time-interval categories. When significant differences were detected, pairwise comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction. These analyses were interpreted as groupwise nonparametric comparisons rather than as formal multifactorial interaction or repeated-measures models.

Comparisons of CAP-I values among composite groups for each light source were also performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Mann–Whitney U tests for pairwise comparisons when significant differences were detected.

Descriptive statistics were expressed as mean ± standard error (SE) for ΔE values and mean ± standard deviation (SD) for CAP-I values. The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Discussion

Ambient light and color harmony

In the literature, it has been shown that shade matching of universal resin composites depends not only on material composition but also on the lighting conditions under which measurements or observations are performed. Various studies have reported that background color, restoration thickness, translucency, and structural coloration mechanisms affect the shade-matching performance of composites, and that these factors interact with the lighting used during measurement [16–19]. Especially in universal composites based on structural color, the reflection and scattering properties of light within the material combine with optical signals from the surrounding tooth structure to determine the perceived color. Therefore, the spectrum and intensity of the lighting used during measurement can alter the spectrophotometrically obtained L*, a*, b* values and, consequently, the ΔE results [19, 20].

The findings of this study also showed that ambient lighting has a significant effect on color matching performance. The variability in ΔE values obtained under different light sources supports that the optical behavior of restorative materials is influenced not only by the properties of the materials themselves but also by the light spectrum present in the measurement environment. This highlights the importance of considering ambient lighting when selecting clinical colors.

Optical properties and color harmony in universal composites

The color-matching behavior of universal composites is largely related to the optical properties of the material. Universal shade composites rely on complex optical interactions between filler particles and the resin matrix, in which light scattering and interference phenomena contribute to the perceived color-blending effect rather than on conventional pigment-based absorption mechanisms. In some universal composites with structural coloration, color formation occurs through light scattering and interference between particles, rather than through pigment absorption. This mechanism allows light from the surrounding dental tissue to pass through the restoration, thereby altering the perceived color [19, 20]. Nevertheless, in pigment-based universal composites, the degree of translucency and the optical path length within the resin matrix also affect how light reflected from the dental tissue is dispersed within the restoration, which may cause the color matching to vary under different lighting conditions [6, 21, 22].

In this study, the differences observed in ΔE and CAP values among different composite materials can also be related to these optical properties. The fact that the composites evaluated in the study have different optical structures and translucency may have caused them to exhibit different color-matching performance under varying lighting conditions. This indicates that the color matching of universal composites cannot be explained by a single factor alone and that material composition and optical properties should be evaluated together.

The material-dependent variation observed under different ambient illumination conditions may be explained by differences in the optical behavior of restorative materials. Universal composites differ in filler size distribution, refractive-index matching between the filler and the resin matrix, and overall translucency, all of which influence light scattering and internal reflection within the material. When exposed to light sources with different spectral power distributions and color temperatures, these optical properties may alter the balance between transmitted, absorbed, and scattered light. As a result, the perceived color and spectrophotometric color difference values may vary depending on the interaction between the illumination spectrum and the material’s optical structure [23, 24]. This mechanism may explain why certain composites exhibited different color-matching behavior under different lighting conditions in the present study.

The color-matching ability and optical integration of universal resin composites with the surrounding tooth structure largely depend on the material’s optical design. Filler particle size, filler distribution, refractive-index matching between the resin matrix and fillers, and the translucency of the material are key factors that determine light scattering and transmission within the restoration [25–27]. These optical properties influence how the restoration interacts with light reflected from the surrounding tooth structure, thereby affecting the perceived color match and the material’s color-adjustment potential [15, 27].

Vittra APS Unique is a single-shade composite that achieves optical blending primarily through light-scattering mechanisms. The presence of submicron (~ 200 nm) zirconium silicate fillers promotes multidirectional light scattering within the restoration, allowing optical signals originating from the surrounding tooth structure to propagate more extensively throughout the material. This optical behavior represents one of the fundamental mechanisms underlying the “chameleon effect” observed in single-shade composites [27–29]. However, color formation mechanisms that rely primarily on light scattering may also be more sensitive to variations in ambient illumination. This is because the spectral composition of scattered light is influenced by the spectral power distribution of the light source [15]. Consequently, variations in color-matching behavior across different color-temperature conditions may be expected. In the present study, Vittra APS Unique exhibited moderate CAP values and variable ΔE values across different illumination conditions, suggesting that the optical blending mechanism of single-shade composites interacts with ambient lighting.

Filtek Easy Match, on the other hand, is a nanocomposite based on 3 M’s True Nanotechnology system, containing nanoscale silica and zirconia particles as well as nanoclusters in its inorganic phase. The nanocluster filler architecture may enable more controlled light scattering within the restoration and contribute to improved optical stability [30, 31]. This system combines pigment-based color control with controlled translucency, allowing the material to exhibit relatively stable optical behavior across different clinical conditions [29, 32]. As a result, nanocluster-based composites may exhibit more stable color behavior under varying illumination conditions than single-shade systems that rely primarily on light scattering. In the present study, the comparatively lower CAP values observed for Filtek Easy Match may indicate that the nanocluster filler architecture limits the influence of the surrounding tooth color on the restoration’s perceived shade, thereby reducing the overall magnitude of color adjustment.

OptiShade Universal incorporates Adaptive Response Technology (ART), which uses a filler network that generates color via structural color mechanisms. In this approach, color formation is not solely dependent on pigment absorption; instead, the size distribution of nanoparticles and glass fillers influences light scattering and interference within the material, ultimately determining the perceived color [33, 34]. In particular, the presence of barium glass particles with an approximate size of 400 nm—close to the wavelength range of visible light—may enable them to interact with incident light and contribute to structural color generation [35, 36]. Such structural color mechanisms may theoretically influence optical adaptation between the restorative material and the surrounding tooth structure. However, in the present study, OptiShade Universal exhibited negative CAP-I values under all tested illumination conditions. Therefore, although its structural color-based formulation may theoretically contribute to optical adaptation, the present findings do not support a consistently enhanced blending performance for this material under the tested dual-specimen conditions.

The differences observed among the tested composites may therefore be explained by their distinct optical design strategies. Vittra APS Unique relies primarily on light scattering within a single-shade system, Filtek Easy Match utilizes nanocluster filler technology with controlled translucency, and OptiShade Universal incorporates a structural color approach that combines light scattering and interference mechanisms. These different optical mechanisms may affect the materials’ behavior under different illumination spectra, which could explain the variations in ΔE and CAP-I values observed in the present study. However, because CAP-I values were mostly negative, these mechanisms should not be interpreted as producing uniformly enhanced blending performance. Rather, the color-matching behavior of the tested universal composites appears to be material- and illumination-dependent under the present experimental conditions.

Interestingly, the lowest ΔE values were observed under illumination conditions with spectral characteristics closer to natural daylight. Light sources with a color temperature around 5500 K provide a relatively balanced spectral power distribution like natural daylight and are therefore commonly considered the reference illumination condition for color evaluation in dentistry [37–39]. Under such illumination conditions, the optical interaction between the restorative material and the surrounding tooth structure may be more accurately represented, resulting in lower color difference values.

Overall, the present findings indicate that the optical behavior of universal resin composites is influenced by their material-specific design strategies. Materials incorporating structural color mechanisms may exhibit different light-scattering behavior; however, in the present study, this did not translate into consistently positive CAP-I values under all illumination conditions. Therefore, the color-matching behavior of universal composites should be interpreted as material- and illumination-dependent rather than uniformly enhanced. These findings highlight that universal composites do not exhibit identical optical behavior and that material architecture should be considered when evaluating color outcomes under different illumination conditions.

Standardization of specimen geometry and optical background

Restoration thickness and background shade were not independent variables in the present study; however, both factors are known to influence L*, a*, and b* coordinates, ΔE values, and color-adjustment potential measurements. Previous studies have shown that restoration thickness, translucency, and the optical characteristics of the underlying substrate can affect the shade-matching behavior of universal resin composites [6, 17, 19, 22, 40, 41]. Therefore, these parameters were standardized rather than experimentally varied in the present study.

The use of digitally designed 3D-printed teeth enabled standardization of specimen geometry, Class IV cavity design, bevel configuration, and optical background conditions. In addition, silicone indices were used to standardize restoration morphology and thickness across all specimens. By controlling these factors, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of composite type, ambient illumination, and time interval under reproducible experimental conditions. Nevertheless, because the 3D-printed B1 shade resin teeth may not fully reproduce the optical properties of natural enamel and dentin, including translucency, fluorescence, and subsurface light propagation, the findings should be interpreted within the limitations of an in vitro model [42, 43].

Color adjustment potential (CAP)

One important concept used to describe the clinical performance of universal composites is the color adjustment potential (CAP), which refers to the ability to adjust color. The concept of color adjustment potential has been introduced to quantify the ability of restorative materials to visually integrate with surrounding tooth structures, providing a useful metric for evaluating the blending performance of universal composites. CAP refers to the ability of a composite restoration to optically adapt to the surrounding dental tissue and is typically calculated by comparing the color differences between restored and unrestored samples [6]. CAP measurements can be influenced by both the material’s translucency and the measurement conditions. Measurement geometry, background color, and the spectrum of the light used can alter the interpretation of CAP values.

In this study, the calculated CAP-I values varied among composite materials and under different lighting conditions. This indicates that the color-adjustment behavior of universal composites depends not only on the material’s optical properties but also on the measurement conditions.

In the present study, most CAP-I values remained within the negative range. According to the CAP-I formula, negative values indicate that the color difference between the intact and restored teeth was not lower than the color difference between the intact teeth and the corresponding composite replicas. Therefore, the tested universal composites did not consistently demonstrate improved color adjustment under dual-specimen conditions. This finding should be interpreted cautiously, particularly because the Class IV cavity configuration and the use of 3D-printed B1 shade substrates may have limited the optical contribution of the surrounding tooth structure. Thus, the CAP-I findings suggest that the blending performance of these materials was condition-dependent rather than uniformly enhanced.

Furthermore, this variability in CAP values suggests that universal composites may exhibit different color-matching performance across various clinical lighting conditions.

Metamerism and the effect of lighting

It is well known that different lighting sources significantly affect perceived color during color assessment in dentistry. Several studies have demonstrated that illumination conditions can significantly alter spectrophotometric color measurements and visual shade perception, highlighting the importance of standardized lighting environments during both experimental measurements and clinical shade matching. The phenomenon in which the same material can appear in different colors under light sources with different spectral power distributions and color temperatures is defined as metamerism. Studies have shown that clinically perceptible color shifts can occur in ceramic and resin-based restorative materials under different lighting sources [10–12]. This situation may be especially pronounced in universal composites with high translucency, as these materials exhibit stronger optical interactions with background dentin reflections and ambient light [40].

In this study, the variability in ΔE and CAP values obtained under different light temperatures suggests that the metameric effect may play a significant role in restorative materials. This finding indicates that color matching can vary under different clinical lighting conditions and highlights the importance of standardizing the light source used during color evaluation.

Clinical implications

In the present study, ΔE00 values were also interpreted in relation to clinically relevant perceptibility and acceptability thresholds. Commonly used thresholds for CIEDE2000 indicate that a ΔE00 value of approximately 0.8 represents the 50:50 perceptibility threshold, whereas a ΔE00 value of approximately 1.8 represents the 50:50 acceptability threshold. In this study, the observed ΔE00 values generally remained below the acceptability threshold and were mostly close to or slightly above the perceptibility threshold. Therefore, although the measured color differences may be perceptible under certain illumination conditions, they generally remained within clinically acceptable limits. Nevertheless, the variations observed among composite materials, ambient light sources, and time intervals indicate that illumination conditions can influence instrumental color-difference outcomes and should be standardized during color evaluation.

When the literature data are evaluated together, it appears that color-matching performance in restorative dentistry depends not only on the material’s optical properties but also on the lighting conditions used during evaluation. Studies have shown that ΔE values and CAP measurements can vary depending on environmental factors such as lighting conditions, background color, and sample geometry [6, 9, 14, 22, 40, 44, 45].

The findings of this study also show that the color harmony of universal composites can vary under different lighting conditions. Therefore, it is recommended that lighting conditions be considered when selecting shade in clinical practice and that standardized lighting conditions be used whenever possible. In addition, using spectrophotometric measurements alongside visual color assessment can help interpret perceptual differences that may arise under various lighting conditions more accurately [46–48].

Study limitations

Despite providing valuable insights into the influence of ambient illumination on color matching and the color adjustment potential of universal resin composites, this study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results.

First, the study was conducted in vitro, which cannot fully replicate the complex optical environment of the oral cavity. In clinical situations, factors such as saliva, surrounding soft tissues, enamel translucency, and dynamic lighting conditions may influence color perception and the behavior of restoration blending.

Second, three-dimensional printed teeth were used as substrates to standardize specimen geometry and optical background. Although this approach improves experimental reproducibility, the optical behavior of printed materials may not fully replicate the light-scattering and transmission properties of natural enamel and dentin.

Third, the study evaluated color behavior under specific controlled lighting conditions, whereas clinical environments may involve mixed illumination sources with varying spectral distributions. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted within the context of controlled laboratory illumination settings.

In addition, detailed spectral power distribution, illuminance, and color rendering index data for the ambient light sources were not available, which should be considered when interpreting the influence of illumination conditions.

Finally, only a limited number of universal resin composite materials were evaluated. Different materials may exhibit distinct optical properties depending on filler composition, refractive index matching, and translucency characteristics.

In addition, although the sample size was close to the a priori calculated requirement, the power analysis was based on the primary group comparison model and did not specifically account for all multifactorial interaction terms. Therefore, the findings related to the combined effects of composite type, ambient illumination, and time interval should be interpreted with caution.

Future research directions

Future studies should further investigate the influence of ambient illumination on the color adjustment behavior of restorative materials under conditions that more closely simulate clinical environments. In particular, in vivo or clinically simulated studies evaluating universal composites in natural teeth may provide more comprehensive information regarding their blending performance.

In addition, further research should explore the relationship between material optical properties such as translucency, filler morphology, and refractive index and their impact on color adjustment potential under different lighting conditions.

Another important research direction involves the simultaneous evaluation of visual and instrumental color assessments, since clinical shade matching relies heavily on human perception, which may differ from spectrophotometric measurements.

Finally, future investigations may examine the effects of restoration thickness, background shade, and aging conditions on color-matching behavior across various lighting environments to better understand the clinical performance of universal composite systems.

Conclusion

In conclusion, ambient lighting conditions influenced the instrumental color-difference outcomes, and CAP-I values of universal resin composites restored in standardized 3D-printed teeth. The observed ΔE00 values generally remained below the acceptability threshold, although they were close to or slightly above the perceptibility threshold under some conditions. CAP-I values were mostly negative, indicating that the tested materials did not consistently demonstrate improved color adjustment under dual-specimen conditions. Therefore, the color-matching behavior of universal resin composites should be interpreted as material- and illumination-dependent rather than uniformly enhanced. Within the limitations of this in vitro model, standardization of lighting conditions during shade selection and color evaluation appears important for obtaining reproducible color assessments.
