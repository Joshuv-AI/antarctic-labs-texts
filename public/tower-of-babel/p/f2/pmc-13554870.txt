Surgical arteriovenous (AV) fistulas are commonly used to obtain vascular access in patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) who are undergoing hemodialysis (HD). However, this low-resistance pathway can create a high-flow circulatory state, eventually leading to cardiac decompensation.1,2 We present our approach to a patient who developed high-output heart failure (HF) and pulmonary hypertension (PH) after a long-standing AV graft.

History of Presentation

We present a 42-year-old woman who was referred to our clinic for renal transplantation risk assessment. Her principal complaints included dyspnea on exertion, orthopnea, and fatigue. Her body mass index was 19.5 kg/m2, and she had elevated blood pressure. Physical exam was remarkable for an elevated jugular venous pulse, a loud pulmonic component of the second heart sound, and a soft systolic murmur at the left lower sternal border.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of ESRD due to chronic glomerulonephritis status post deceased donor renal transplant 15 years prior. This was complicated by graft failure requiring re-initiation of HD via a 22-year-old left-arm brachiocephalic AV graft.

Differential Diagnosis

High-output HF due to an AV graft, acute systolic heart failure due to nonischemic cardiomyopathy, and hypertensive cardiomyopathy were considered in the differential diagnosis.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiogram showed a mid-range left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of ∼40% to 45% and a restrictive left ventricular (LV) diastolic filling pattern with elevated left atrial and LV filling pressures and moderate PH.

Cardiac MRI revealed an LVEF of 45% and biventricular dilatation with mild-to-moderate tricuspid regurgitation, with no evidence of infiltrative disease. Initial right heart catheterization (RHC) revealed elevated right- and left-sided filling pressures and moderate postcapillary PH with evidence of right ventricular (RV) dysfunction (Table 1). Her cardiac index was 2.52 L/min/m2 by thermodilution and 4.14 L/min/m2 by indirect Fick's method.

Management

Aggressive volume removal with HD was recommended. The patient developed progressively worsening symptoms, including marked exercise intolerance that ultimately left her dependent on a walker. The patient was aggressively dialyzed with a plan for RHC. A vascular surgery team temporarily occluded the AV graft using a balloon during RHC.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Baseline pre-occlusion RHC showed low filling pressures with mild PH and hyperdynamic circulation; postocclusion RHC showed normalization of pulmonary pressures (Table 1). These findings were consistent with mixed PH (groups 2 and 5) and RV overload secondary to high-output HF caused by AV graft. After a multidisciplinary team (cardiologists, nephrologists, and vascular surgeons) reviewed treatment options, the patient underwent ligation of the AV graft and placement of a peritoneal dialysis catheter. On follow-up, she reported significant symptom improvement and was able to ambulate independently. Repeat transthoracic echocardiogram revealed an LVEF of 45% to 50%, normal RV function, and RV systolic pressure of ∼45 mm Hg. Clinical improvement was documented 1 month postligation. RHC 18 months postligation showed normal filling pressures, normal pulmonary arterial pressures, and a cardiac index of 2.18 (by Indirect Fick) and 2.43 (by thermodilution). She was later relisted for a renal transplant.

Discussion

High-output HF is defined by an abnormally elevated cardiac output or cardiac index despite persistent HF symptoms and is an uncommon complication of hyperdynamic circulation due to an AV fistula or graft.3,4 Patients with ESRD have some initial degree of LV dysfunction, increasing their risk of developing symptomatic PH and HF with further changes.4 Studies have shown that up to 42% of patients on dialysis develop HF and PH with an increased risk of morbidity and mortality; however, the exact figures are unknown.5 Increased risk of complications may be associated with chronicity and more proximal AV fistulas.5

In a case series of 5 patients,5 manual compression performed in the catheterization laboratory showed submaximal improvement in hemodynamics, underscoring the hesitation in removing an existing source of access. However, the authors reported improvement in clinical, echocardiographic, and hemodynamic indices after revision or closure of AV dialysis access. In our case, with the assistance of vascular surgery, we occluded the graft and noted a dramatic response. Diagnosing high-output HF in this setting remains challenging and is largely a diagnosis of exclusion. Treatment of HF and PH due to a high-flow AV fistula or graft via permanent surgical ligation is complicated.6 Evidence suggests that surgical ligation of AV fistulas improves cardiac function, but these studies are not adequately powered to change practice.6,7 The risks and benefits of AV fistula ligation or removal must be reviewed carefully. The UK COBALT (Cardiorespiratory Optimisation By Arteriovenous fistula Ligation after renal Transplantation) trial aims to identify functional and laboratory parameters that correlate with improvement after AV fistula ligation.8

Another challenge to consider is the optimal modality for obtaining vascular access. Patients may require tunneled dialysis catheters, which carry the risk of exposure to multiple bloodstream infections. Peritoneal dialysis could be a better alternative for these high-risk patients. If an AV fistula is needed, a distal arm fistula should be considered over an upper arm fistula.9 These complex decisions should involve a multidisciplinary team, including a nephrologist, cardiologist, and vascular surgeon, to decide the best course of action for each patient. In addition, patients with underlying LV systolic dysfunction also require multidisciplinary discussion before AV fistula placement, as extra flow may worsen preexisting HF.

Conclusions

High-output HF from long-standing AV access is an important and potentially reversible cause of cardiopulmonary dysfunction in patients with ESRD. Temporary AV graft occlusion can aid in diagnosis and guide management. Definitive ligation, when appropriate, may result in significant clinical and hemodynamic improvement.
