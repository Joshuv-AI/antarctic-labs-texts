Might modulation of the endocannabinoid system provide a potential dual therapeutic strategy for osteoporosis and depression?
Authors: Peng Wei, Pianpian Zhao, Zenghui Gu, Mengyuan Yang, David Karasik, Ching‐Lung Cheung, Houfeng Zheng
Published: 2026

Over the past several years, the association between major depressive disorder (MDD) and bone loss has attracted growing attention from the scientific community. 1 A recent large‐scale cohort study in women aged ≥ 50 years revealed that MDD is significantly associated with the risk of osteoporosis/osteopenia. 2 Hormonal imbalance, elevated inflammatory cytokine levels and reduced physical activity in patients with MDD are all major risk factors for osteoporosis. 3 On the other hand, a 10‐year nationwide study of the US population showed that osteoporosis is an independent risk factor for depression among older adults. 4 The pain, limited mobility and reduced quality of life in patients with osteoporosis or bone fractures predispose them to depressive symptoms. 5 These findings indicate a bidirectional relationship between MDD and osteoporosis or fractures in older adults.

GENETIC EVIDENCE LINKING MDD AND OSTEOPOROSIS/FRACTURE

As early as 2000, Swantek et al. proposed that MDD and osteoporosis might share a genetic link. 6 However, another study did not find the genetic causal role of depression in osteoporosis or fracture. 7 Recently, the study by Zhao et al. not only identified a statistical correlation but also uncovered a shared genetic architecture between MDD and fracture (genetic correlation coefficient = 0.23, standard error = 0.02). 8 The most significant shared genetic signal is located near the SGIP1 gene; over‐expression of SGIP1 in neuronal tissue is associated with increased MDD risk (odds ratio [OR] = 1.02, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.02–1.03, p < 0.001) and increased risk of fracture (OR = 1.02, 95% CI = 1.01–1.04, p < 0.001). 8 The SGIP1 gene encodes a protein involved in cannabinoid receptor 1 (CB1R) signalling. 9 Notably, CB1R plays a crucial role in mood regulation and bone metabolism. In fact, components of the endocannabinoid system (ECS), including endocannabinoids (eCBs) and cannabinoid receptors (CBRs), actively participate in both processes.

THE ENDOCANNABINOID SYSTEM

The ECS is a complex signalling network composed of CBRs, eCBs, and enzymes responsible for eCB synthesis and degradation. It plays a vital role in maintaining physiological homoeostasis. 10 CBRs include cannabinoid receptor type 1 (CB1R) and type 2 (CB2R), both of which are Gαi/o‐protein‐coupled receptors that inhibit adenylyl cyclase activity (figure 1A), leading to decreased intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate and protein kinase A levels. The Cnr1 and Cnr2 genes encode CB1R and CB2R, respectively. As illustrated in figure 1A, the main endogenous ligands, anandamide (AEA) and 2‐arachidonoylglycerol (2‐AG), are synthesised on demand, released locally to activate CBRs and then rapidly degraded by specific enzymes. AEA is derived from N‐arachidonoyl phosphatidylethanolamine (NAPE) via NAPE‐phospholipase D and degraded by fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH) (figure 1A). Meanwhile, diacylglycerol is hydrolysed by diacylglycerol lipase to produce 2‐AG, which is subsequently degraded by monoacylglycerol lipase (figure 1A).

ENDOCANNABINOID SIGNALLING AS A MODULATOR OF EMOTIONAL HOMOEOSTASIS

As a major modulator of the central nervous system, the ECS exerts broad regulatory effects on emotion. Through retrograde signalling (figure 1A), eCBs modulate neurotransmitter release by activating CB1R and CB2R. 10 Notably, CB1R activation exhibits dose‐dependent antidepressant effects. Both CB1R agonists and FAAH inhibitors produce antidepressant‐like outcomes, with low‐dose activation improving depressive symptoms. However, excessive activation may induce adverse effects, following a U‐shaped dose‐response curve. 11 Experimental evidence shows that CB1R deficiency leads to mood deterioration; CB1R knockout mice display increased anxiety‐ and depression‐like behaviours. 12 CB2R activation also exerts antidepressant effects, primarily by inhibiting microglial activation, reducing neuroinflammation and alleviating oxidative stress. CB2R agonists have been shown to significantly reduce lipopolysaccharide‐induced depressive‐like behaviours. 13 Collectively, activation of CB1R and CB2R enhances mood stability, whereas their antagonism or deletion induces depressive phenotypes.

ENDOCANNABINOID CONTROL AND BONE REMODELLING

A fully functional ECS is also present in the skeletal system. CB1R and CB2R are expressed in osteoblasts and osteoclasts (figure 1B), where they interact with locally produced AEA and 2‐AG to regulate bone metabolism. 14 In fact, osteoblasts and osteoclasts not only express CB1R and CB2R but also locally produce eCBs (AEA and 2‐AG). Upon activation of CB1R and CB2R, eCBs regulate intracellular signalling in osteoblasts and osteoclasts, thereby modulating bone resorption and bone remodelling (figure 1B). Knockout models reveal that ECS signalling is critical for bone integrity: CB1R−/− mice initially exhibit increased bone mass but develop age‐related osteoporosis, whereas CB2R−/− mice show age‐dependent alterations in bone resorption and formation. 15 CB1R expressed in sympathetic nerve terminals inhibits norepinephrine (NE) release, thereby promoting bone formation (figure 1C). 16 Pharmacological activation of CB2R‐using agonists, such as HU‐433 or HU‐308, stimulates osteoblast proliferation, suppresses osteoclast activity and prevents ovariectomy‐induced bone loss. 15 Short‐term cannabinoid administration has been shown to promote bone formation and inhibit bone resorption, supporting the therapeutic potential of ECS modulation in osteoporosis. 17 However, chronic ECS overactivation may disrupt bone homoeostasis and contribute to osteoporosis development. 18

THE POTENTIAL OF ECS REGULATION AS A CONCURRENT THERAPEUTIC STRATEGY

Experimental data indicate that elevated eCB levels or CBR activation can promote osteoblast differentiation and function while alleviating depressive symptoms. 10 Conversely, CB1R or CB2R knockout animals display heightened anxiety‐ and depression‐like behaviours and develop severe osteoporosis with ageing. 15 Cannabinoids such as cannabidiol (CBD) have been proposed for the treatment of MDD and other psychiatric disorders. 19 A randomised, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled pilot study evaluating the efficacy of adjunctive cannabidiol in bipolar depression found that early administration of CBD resulted in a significant reduction in Montgomery–Asberg Depression Rating Scale scores. 20 Another multicenter, double‐blind, randomised clinical trial demonstrated that a nanodispersible cannabidiol oral solution was effective in patients with mild to moderate anxiety disorders, as well as in improving comorbid depressive symptoms and sleep quality. 21 Despite these studies, cannabinoids remain controversial in psychiatry, and their long‐term efficacy and safety require further evaluation. For the skeletal system, short‐term, low‐dose administration of tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) analogues 22 or CBD 23 enhances bone strength and health, whereas long‐term or high‐dose cannabinoid use may excessively activate the ECS, disturb bone metabolism and increase fracture risk. 24 Kulpa et al. reviewed two clinical trials showing that short‐term treatment with CBD‐ or THC‐based medical cannabis products reduced bone resorption markers. 25 However, these studies involved relatively small sample sizes and short treatment durations; the conclusions should be interpreted with caution. Overall, ECS activity exhibits bidirectional regulatory potential—moderate activation produces beneficial effects, whereas overactivation causes harm. Balanced ECS modulation may therefore represent a potential therapeutic avenue for the treatment of both MDD and osteoporosis. Furthermore, Kadriu et al. reported that patients with MDD treated with ketamine, an agent interacting with CB1R signalling, showed significantly increased osteoprotegerin/receptor activator of nuclear factor‐κB ligand (RANKL) ratios and plasma osteopontin levels along with reduced RANKL concentrations, whereas bone marker levels in healthy controls remained stable. 1

These preclinical and clinical findings provide support for interactions between mood‐related pathways and bone metabolism, but their mechanistic and clinical implications remain to be clarified. One potential mechanism of the ‘neuro‐bone axis’ via ECS modulation is the regulation of the hypothalamic‐pituitary‐adrenal (HPA) axis. Patients with depression often exhibit persistent HPA axis hyperactivity, characterised by elevated levels of cortisol. Such sustained neuroendocrine disturbances may compromise bone metabolism by inhibiting osteoblast proliferation and impairing bone formation. 26 Both acute and chronic stress exposure are associated with reduced AEA levels and elevated serum cortisol levels. 27 Chronic stress induces dysregulation of the HPA axis and impairs glucocorticoid receptor nuclear translocation in both the central nervous system and bone tissue, which, in turn, promotes osteoblast apoptosis and accelerates bone loss. 28 The ECS interacts closely with neuroendocrine systems and functions as a homoeostatic regulator of HPA axis activity under both basal and stress‐related conditions. 27 Activation of CB1R reduces glutamate release from glutamatergic synapses and is a key mechanism underlying the glucocorticoid‐mediated feedback regulation of the HPA axis. 29 The traditional Chinese medicine formula Chaihu‐Shugan‐San not only ameliorated chronic stress‐induced depressive‐like behaviours but also reversed osteoporosis through the suppression of neuroinflammation and correction of HPA axis hyperactivation. 30

Another potential mechanism is the rapid sympathetic‐adreno‐medullar axis. CB1R has been reported to be expressed on sympathetic nerve terminals in bone, where its activation suppresses the release of NE, thereby relieving the inhibitory effect of sympathetic signalling on bone formation and ultimately promoting osteogenesis. 31 Tam et al. showed that traumatic brain injury (TBI) stimulated bone formation at a distant skeletal site and that this response was preceded by an increase in local 2‐AG levels together with a reduction in NE levels. 16 Notably, this TBI‐induced osteogenic effect was absent in CB1‐null mice, highlighting the essential role of CB1R in neuro‐bone communication. 16 Moreover, exogenous administration of 2‐AG reproduced the effects of TBI, whereas treatment with the beta‐adrenergic receptor agonist isoproterenol blunted this response, further supporting the notion that ECS‐mediated regulation of bone formation is closely linked to sympathetic modulation. 16

In summary, the bidirectional association between major depressive disorder and osteoporosis suggests that patients with MDD, particularly those with chronic illness, reduced physical activity and poor dietary intake, may warrant greater attention to bone health and bone mineral density screening. More importantly, the ECS may represent a key hub among the multiple pathways that coordinately regulate emotional homoeostasis and bone metabolism. Although further large‐scale and well‐designed clinical studies are needed to clarify its therapeutic relevance, CBR signalling could represent a potential mechanistic link between emotional regulation and osteoporosis. Modulation of the ECS may have antidepressant‐like effects and also contribute to the regulation of bone formation, indicating a potential therapeutic strategy for the co‐management of both diseases.
