Multistage Bowel Salvage Through Surgical Resection and Superior Mesenteric Artery Revascularization in Acute-on-Chronic Mesenteric Ischemia: A Case Report
Authors: Kyoung Moo Im
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report a case of acute-on-chronic mesenteric ischemia (AOCMI) in an 85-year-old woman presenting with progressive postprandial abdominal pain, signs of panperitonitis, and free intraperitoneal air. Emergency laparotomy revealed diffuse small bowel ischemia without clear demarcation, reflecting acute ischemia superimposed on chronic hypoperfusion. Intraoperative indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence angiography enabled selective resection of nonviable bowel, preserving potentially salvageable intestine. Delayed recognition of a nine-month history of chronic mesenteric ischemia (CMI) prompted staged endovascular revascularization of the superior mesenteric artery (SMA). Despite transient short bowel syndrome (SBS) requiring parenteral nutrition, bowel continuity was ultimately restored.This case highlights the diagnostic challenge of AOCMI and demonstrates that selective bowel salvage combined with staged multidisciplinary intervention can preserve bowel function and quality of life even in elderly high-risk patients.

Introduction

Mesenteric ischemia is a rare but highly lethal gastrointestinal vascular disorder associated with an overall mortality rate often ranging from 60% to 80% [1-4]. It is broadly classified into acute and chronic subtypes [3]. Acute mesenteric ischemia (AMI) is a fatal vascular emergency resulting from a sudden interruption of intestinal blood flow, typically due to arterial embolism or thrombosis [4]. It has a very poor clinical prognosis and is rare, accounting for only 0.09% to 0.2% of all hospital admissions [5].

Conversely, chronic mesenteric ischemia (CMI) is defined as a progressive syndrome resulting from long-standing atherosclerotic stenosis, which ultimately leads to an insufficient blood supply to meet the metabolic demands of the gastrointestinal tract [1,6]. The clinical presentation of CMI is characterized by insufficient blood flow during the period of increased demand following a meal, leading to the classic triad of postprandial abdominal pain, sitophobia, and weight loss [7]. CMI is a rare clinical entity, accounting for fewer than 1 in 1,000 hospital admissions for abdominal pain, with an estimated prevalence of 0.03% [5,6,8]. Once symptomatic CMI develops, the prognosis is poor, with the five-year mortality rate of untreated CMI approaching 100% [6,7,9].

Acute-on-chronic mesenteric ischemia (AOCMI) represents a distinct clinical phenotype in which an acute thrombotic event occurs on a background of long-standing atherosclerotic stenosis and collateral-dependent mesenteric circulation [8]. This failure of compensatory perfusion results in rapid progression to extensive, irreversible bowel necrosis. Consequently, patients require major bowel resection, placing them at significant risk for short bowel syndrome (SBS) and prolonged parenteral nutrition dependence [2,5,6]. Because conditions such as AOCMI are exceptionally rare, research remains far more limited, and there are no well-established or evidence-based guidelines for optimal diagnosis and management [1,2,5,10].

We report a case of AOCMI in an elderly, high-risk patient who was successfully managed through a staged approach integrating surgical and vascular intervention with postoperative nutritional care. Beyond the favorable outcome, this case may serve as a practical reference for treatment strategy and clinical decision-making in AOCMI.

Case presentation

Patient information

An 85-year-old woman presented to the Emergency Department with a two-week history of progressively worsening diffuse abdominal pain that aggravated after meals. The pain was accompanied by intermittent diarrhea without any other symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, hematemesis, or hematochezia. Her past medical history included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, angina, and prior open abdominal surgery for uterine myoma. She had no history of smoking.

Further history revealed that she had experienced recurrent chronic abdominal pain for the preceding nine months. During this period, she had undergone abdominal ultrasonography and endoscopic evaluation at a local hospital. A pancreatic lesion was suspected at that time, prompting further evaluation with magnetic resonance imaging. However, it was considered benign, and she had been managed conservatively with digestive medications.

Clinical and diagnostic findings

On arrival at the Emergency Department, her initial vital signs were stable (blood pressure 119/77 mmHg, heart rate 62/min, respiratory rate 20/min, temperature 36.0°C). Physical examination showed diffuse tenderness with marked rebound tenderness in the left upper quadrant. Laboratory tests showed elevated levels of C-reactive protein (30.66 mg/dL), lactate (3.4 mmol/L), and creatinine (2.14 mg/dL) (Table 1). Due to impaired renal function, a non-contrast computed tomography (CT) scan was performed, revealing pneumoperitoneum, diffuse small bowel dilatation, and a very narrowed superior mesenteric artery (SMA) with extensive atherosclerotic change (Figure 1). Initial findings were suggestive of extensive small bowel ischemia. Therefore, the patient was prepared for emergency exploration.

Therapeutic intervention

Laparoscopic exploration revealed that the entire small intestine appeared pale and devascularized, with no identifiable site of perforation. After initial laparoscopic assessment, the procedure was converted to laparotomy for direct bowel inspection, indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence assessment, bowel resection, and stoma formation. Palpable air crepitus was noted along the mesenteric side of the bowel, corresponding to the preoperative CT finding of suspected free air and suggestive of pneumatosis intestinalis. Under white-light inspection, the bowel extending from approximately 90 cm distal to the ligament of Treitz to 1 m proximal to the ileocecal valve showed diffuse whitening and loss of peristalsis (Figure 2A). ICG fluorescence angiography demonstrated sharply demarcated areas of absent perfusion (Figure 2B), with additional borderline segments showing delayed or patchy enhancement (Figure 2C). Based on these findings, approximately two-thirds of the ischemic small bowel was resected. Primary anastomosis was avoided due to the high risk of anastomotic complications, and an end jejunostomy was created. The length of the remaining small bowel was 90 cm from the ligament of Treitz to the jejunostomy. Final pathology revealed mucosal hemorrhagic necrosis with multifocal ulceration, vasculitis, and serositis, consistent with transmural infarction.

Prophylactic-dose enoxaparin was initiated on postoperative day 2 at a dose of 40 mg subcutaneously once daily after confirming the absence of postoperative bleeding. Although the patient remained hemodynamically stable and asymptomatic after advancement to a soft diet, endovascular revascularization was planned to address the underlying SMA stenosis and prevent recurrent ischemia. Ten days after the initial operation, SMA angiography was done via left brachial artery access using a 6-Fr Flexor® Shuttle® Guiding Sheath (Cook Incorporated, Bloomington, IN, USA). Angiography revealed ostial stenosis with diminished antegrade flow (Figure 3A). After pre-balloon angioplasty, a 5 × 18-mm balloon-expandable stent was deployed, followed by post-balloon dilatation. Final angiography confirmed achieving successful restoration of luminal patency and improved perfusion of mesenteric branches (Figures 3B-3C).

Follow-up and outcomes

Despite intermittent oral intake trials, persistent high-output stoma losses and malabsorption limited nutritional autonomy, consistent with SBS. The patient received three months of nutritional optimization with continuous total parenteral nutrition. After multidisciplinary evaluation and informed discussions with the patient and family, jejunostomy reversal was performed four months after the initial surgery (Figure 4). The total remnant small bowel length was approximately 190 cm. In addition, because one bowel segment showed chronic fibrotic changes with impaired passage, an additional entero-enterostomy bypass was created. Postoperatively, the patient tolerated a regular diet and was discharged without complications.

Postoperatively, the patient did not experience persistent diarrhea and tolerated oral intake without major difficulty. Her baseline weight and BMI were 57 kg and 22.8 kg/m², respectively, at a height of 158 cm. During the postoperative period, her weight decreased to 52 kg, and larger meals provoked abdominal discomfort. Therefore, she received supplemental home parenteral nutrition three times weekly, which was later reduced to twice weekly as her oral intake improved to approximately 80% of her usual intake. After nutritional optimization, her weight increased to 54 kg, corresponding to a BMI of 21.6 kg/m². At 12 months of follow-up, she remained clinically stable without recurrent mesenteric ischemia or stent-related complications.

Discussion

AOCMI represents a severe form of mesenteric ischemia with high mortality, resulting from an acute reduction in mesenteric blood flow superimposed on preexisting chronic arterial insufficiency [3]. In patients with CMI, chronic atherosclerotic disease and collateral circulation may partially compensate for reduced perfusion [8,11]. However, an acute insult such as thrombosis, embolization, or systemic hypoperfusion can abruptly overwhelm these compensatory mechanisms, leading to rapid progression to transmural bowel infarction [12]. In such cases, it may result in extensive intestinal necrosis and substantially higher morbidity and mortality compared with isolated AMI or CMI. The risk is further amplified in elderly patients with diffuse atherosclerosis, multivessel mesenteric involvement, and cardiovascular comorbidities, in whom physiologic reserve is limited [11,13].

In this case, the patient had exhibited typical symptoms of CMI for over nine months prior to the acute event. CMI classically presents with postprandial abdominal pain, often accompanied by reduced oral intake and weight loss. Despite these characteristic features, the diagnosis was not established until progression to an acute event. This highlights the diagnostic difficulty of CMI as symptoms are often nonspecific and overlap with common gastrointestinal disorders such as peptic ulcers, cholecystitis, or cancer [13,14]. In this case, before presentation to the emergency department, diagnostic evaluation of postprandial pain was limited to abdominal ultrasonography and endoscopic examinations, without any vascular imaging or contrast-enhanced CT. As demonstrated in this case, CMI remains underrecognized and is often diagnosed only after catastrophic progression to AMI.

During surgical exploration, the entire bowel should be inspected, and only infarcted bowel should be resected [15]. However, determining bowel viability in AOCMI is particularly challenging. In typical AMI with transmural necrosis, the ischemic segment is often demarcated from adjacent viable bowel. In contrast, in patients with AOCMI, chronically impaired mesenteric perfusion obscures the distinction between reversible ischemia and irreversible necrosis. Moreover, the availability of reliable intraoperative tools for assessing viability remains limited [16]. Although evidence remains limited, experimental and early clinical data suggest that ICG fluorescence provides a more accurate bowel viability assessment than visual judgment alone and can alter operative decision-making in emergency cases [16,17]. In our case, ICG fluorescence imaging provided real-time assessment of bowel perfusion, enabled accurate determination of the resection margins, and may have helped avoid second-look surgery in a high-risk, elderly patient.

Nevertheless, even when ischemic bowel is appropriately resected, surgical treatment alone is often insufficient in patients with AOCMI, as it does not correct the underlying chronic mesenteric hypoperfusion. Without restoration of mesenteric blood flow, the remaining intestine remains vulnerable to recurrent ischemia. In the present case, definitive treatment of CMI was achieved through subsequent endovascular revascularization of the SMA.

Conclusions

This case highlights the importance of combining damage control surgery with timely vascular intervention to achieve recovery. Although treatment strategies for AOCMI are not yet well established, this case suggests that a staged approach may be feasible even in an elderly patient. Through selective bowel resection during the acute phase and subsequent revascularization for CMI, excessive bowel loss was avoided, and intestinal continuity was ultimately restored.
