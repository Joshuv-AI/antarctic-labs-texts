A multi-omics prognostic model integrating disulfidptosis-related molecular features in colorectal cancer
Authors: Huabin Zhou, Songrui Xiang, Yongjun Wu, Shipeng Li, Yuanchi Zheng, Yulan Liu, Chao Zheng, Min Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDisulfidptosis is a recently described cell-death-related process associated with intracellular disulfide stress and may be relevant to tumor progression and immune regulation. However, its prognostic significance and broader biological relevance in colorectal cancer (CRC) remain unclear.MethodsTranscriptomic and clinical data from TCGA and GEO cohorts were systematically integrated to characterize disulfidptosis-related molecular patterns in CRC. A four-gene prognostic signature was developed in a randomly assigned training cohort and evaluated in the corresponding internal test cohort. Public datasets were further used for gene-level expression analysis, and survival associations were assessed in the GSE39582 cohort. Immune infiltration, tumor mutation burden, microsatellite instability, and estimated drug-sensitivity analyses were performed to explore the biological correlates and therapeutic-response-related patterns associated with the model. The four model genes were further examined using single-cell RNA-seq data and public immunohistochemical images from the Human Protein Atlas, and were preliminarily assessed in 11 paired CRC tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues by RT-qPCR and western blotting.ResultsA four-gene signature consisting of SPINK1, GPRASP1, TIMP1, and KRT6A stratified patients into prognostically distinct risk groups. The high-risk group showed poorer survival, higher tumor mutation burden, higher TIDE scores, and elevated stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores, suggesting a more immune-evasive tumor microenvironment. Enrichment analyses indicated that high-risk tumors were associated with extracellular matrix remodeling and PI3K-Akt-related pathways, whereas low-risk tumors were enriched in metabolic processes. Estimated drug-sensitivity analyses indicated agent-specific differences between the two risk groups. The high-risk group showed lower estimated IC50 values for several cytotoxic and targeted agents, including cisplatin, docetaxel, doxorubicin, etoposide, vinorelbine, nilotinib, and gefitinib, whereas the low-risk group showed lower estimated IC50 values for AKT inhibitor VIII, sorafenib, lapatinib, erlotinib, and BIBW2992.ConclusionsThis study identified a four-gene prognostic signature associated with disulfidptosis-related molecular heterogeneity in CRC. The signature was associated with survival differences, distinct tumor microenvironmental features, and divergent biological programs across risk groups, while KRT6A showed the most consistent pattern across public-dataset analyses and tissue-level analyses. These findings support the potential relevance of this subtype-guided signature to prognostic stratification in CRC and identify related genes for further biological investigation and independent validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12935-026-04417-w.Keywords: Disulfidptosis, Colorectal cancer, Prognostic model, Tumor immune microenvironment, KRT6A

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is among the most prevalent and lethal malignancies worldwide and remains a major public health burden [1]. Although advances in surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy have improved outcomes, the five-year overall survival rate of patients with CRC remains unsatisfactory [2, 3]. Prognostic assessment still relies primarily on the TNM staging system, which is limited by its dependence on clinicopathological features and its lack of molecular information, and may therefore constrain precise treatment stratification [4, 5]. More reliable molecular markers are needed to refine prognostic assessment and support individualized treatment in CRC.

Programmed cell death plays a central role in tissue development, homeostasis, and diverse pathological processes [6]. Accumulating evidence indicates that dysregulated cell death contributes to tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, therapeutic resistance, and prognosis [7]. Disulfidptosis has recently been described as a cell-death-related process associated with intracellular disulfide stress under metabolically constrained conditions [8]. Emerging studies suggest that disulfidptosis-related molecular features may be linked to tumor biology, immune-cell infiltration, and tumor microenvironmental regulation [9–11].

Despite growing interest in disulfidptosis-related molecular features, their prognostic and biological significance in CRC remains incompletely understood. In particular, it remains unclear whether disulfidptosis-related molecular heterogeneity can contribute to prognostic stratification in CRC. In this study, we first characterized the expression patterns, genomic alterations, and prognostic associations of 15 previously reported disulfidptosis-related genes in CRC and used them to define molecular subtypes. Rather than building a prognostic model directly from the initial DRG set, we identified subtype-associated differentially expressed genes to capture broader transcriptional programs linked to these molecular states and derived a four-gene prognostic signature. We then examined the associations of this signature with the tumor microenvironment, genomic features, immunotherapy-related indicators, and computationally inferred drug sensitivity, and further evaluated the model genes using public databases, single-cell RNA sequencing data, and paired clinical tissues. We therefore sought to determine whether disulfidptosis-associated molecular heterogeneity could inform risk stratification in CRC and identify candidate biomarkers for further biological investigation.

Materials and methods

Data collection and preprocessing

Transcriptomic data and clinical information for colorectal cancer (CRC) patients were obtained from TCGA-COAD and the GEO cohorts GSE17538 and GSE39582, whereas somatic mutation and copy number variation (CNV) data were obtained from TCGA-COAD only [12–14]. A total of 15 disulfidptosis-related genes (DRGs) were curated from previously published studies [8]. Raw gene-expression data were normalized and batch-corrected using the limma and sva packages in R [15, 16]. After preprocessing, 689 CRC expression profiles were retained for downstream transcriptomic analyses; among these, 604 patients with complete overall survival data and relevant clinical information were included in survival analyses.

In addition, 11 paired primary CRC tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues were collected from newly diagnosed, treatment-naïve patients undergoing surgery at the Department of General Surgery, Dongguan Songshan Lake Central Hospital, in 2024. All patients provided written informed consent. The same 11 paired specimens were used for both RT-qPCR and western blot analyses, and the corresponding clinicopathological characteristics are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Dongguan Songshan Lake Central Hospital (Approval No. Dong Song Yi Lun Shen 2023 No. 5).

Differential expression analysis, TMB, CNV, and prognostic evaluation

Differential expression of DRGs between tumor and normal tissues was assessed using the limma package, with Wilcoxon tests applied for pairwise comparisons where appropriate [16]. Tumor mutation burden (TMB) was defined as the number of somatic non-synonymous mutations per megabase [17]. Somatic mutation data for TCGA-COAD were downloaded from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) portal, and DRG mutation frequencies were analyzed in 448 cases with available mutation data using the maftools package [18]. CNV profiles were examined to identify copy number gains and losses. Univariate survival analysis was performed using the survival and survminer packages to compare overall survival between high- and low-expression DRG groups [19].

Consensus clustering based on DRGs

Unsupervised consensus clustering was performed using the ConsensusClusterPlus package based on the expression profiles of the 15 DRGs [20]. The optimal cluster number was determined by assessing clustering stability across k = 2–9. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to compare overall survival among molecular subtypes, and survival curves were generated using the survival and survminer packages. Dimensionality reduction and visualization were performed using principal component analysis (PCA), t-SNE, and UMAP with standard R workflows and the ggplot2 package [21–23]. Associations between molecular subtypes and clinical characteristics were visualized using pheatmap [24]. Pathway activity across subtypes was assessed using the GSVA and GSEABase packages [25, 26], and immune-cell infiltration levels were estimated using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA).

Consensus clustering based on differentially expressed genes

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between DRG-defined molecular subtypes were identified using the limma package, and GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler package. The resulting DEG expression matrix was then used for a second round of consensus clustering, with k ranging from 2 to 9 and the optimal k selected according to clustering stability. Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to compare overall survival between the resulting subtypes, and subtype separation was visualized using PCA, t-SNE, and UMAP with ggplot2.

Construction and internal validation of a subtype-guided prognostic model

Univariate Cox regression was performed to assess the association between the 936 DEGs identified from DRG-defined subtype comparisons and overall survival, yielding 361 prognostically significant genes. Among the 604 patients with complete survival and clinical data, 302 were randomly assigned to the training cohort and 302 to the internal test cohort. LASSO-Cox regression was performed using the glmnet package to reduce dimensionality and limit overfitting, followed by multivariate Cox modeling to derive a risk score for each patient. Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score within each cohort. Associations between risk scores, DRG expression levels, and molecular subtypes were visualized as box plots using ggpubr. Kaplan–Meier survival curves and time-dependent ROC curves for 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival were generated using the timeROC package, and gene-expression heatmaps were produced using pheatmap.

Nomogram construction and validation

A nomogram integrating the risk score with available clinical variables was constructed using the rms package to estimate overall survival probabilities at 1, 3, and 5 years. Model performance was assessed using calibration curves, ROC analysis, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were also performed to evaluate whether the risk score served as an independent prognostic factor.

Immune cell infiltration, functional enrichment, somatic mutation, TMB, and cancer stemness

Immune-cell subset proportions in CRC samples were estimated using the CIBERSORT algorithm [27], and differences between high- and low-risk groups were compared. Pathway activity was assessed using GSEA and GSVA. Somatic mutation profiles were visualized using waterfall plots generated with maftools [28]. TMB levels were compared between risk groups, and correlations with the risk score were evaluated. The association between the RNA stemness score (RNAss) and the risk score was also examined.

Immune checkpoints, tumor microenvironment, MSI, and drug-sensitivity analysis

Expression levels of selected immune checkpoint-, DNA-repair-, and stromal-related genes were compared between risk groups. The ESTIMATE algorithm was applied to derive immune, stromal, and ESTIMATE scores, as well as tumor purity estimates. Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) scores were computed to characterize potential immune-evasion patterns [29]. The distribution of risk scores across microsatellite instability (MSI) subgroups was examined. Drug sensitivity was assessed by estimating IC50 values for selected chemotherapeutic and targeted agents using the pRRophetic package [30]; these estimates were computationally inferred and were interpreted accordingly.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis and public database validation

Single-cell RNA sequencing data from 10 CRC samples in GSE146771 were downloaded from GEO and analyzed using a standard single-cell RNA-seq workflow including quality control, dimensionality reduction, clustering, and cell-type annotation to examine the distribution of model-gene expression across cell populations. Expression patterns of the four model genes were examined in the TCGA-COAD and GSE39582 cohorts, whereas survival associations were evaluated in the GSE39582 cohort. Representative immunohistochemical images were retrieved from the Human Protein Atlas and were analyzed as public protein-level reference data.

RT-qPCR and western blot validation

Differential expression of SPINK1, GPRASP1, TIMP1, and KRT6A was examined in 11 paired CRC tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues by RT-qPCR and western blotting. Total RNA was extracted, reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and amplified using a three-step qPCR program consisting of 50 °C for 2 min and 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 55–60 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 1 min. Relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the reference gene.

For western blotting, total protein was extracted using a total protein extraction kit (SAB, Cat. No. PE001) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails were added to ice-cold lysis buffer; lysates were shaken at 4 °C for 20 min and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (biosharp, Cat. No. BL521S). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred onto PVDF membranes, and incubated with primary antibodies followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Primary antibodies were used at the following dilutions: GPRASP1, 1:1500; TIMP1, 1:2500; SPINK1, 1:20,000; KRT6A, 1:1000; GAPDH, 1:10,000; and β-actin, 1:1000. GAPDH served as the loading control for GPRASP1, SPINK1, and KRT6A, whereas β-actin was used as the loading control for TIMP1. All secondary antibodies were used at a dilution of 1:10,000. Protein bands were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence and quantified using ImageJ. Statistical analyses and figure preparation were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Detailed information on primers, antibodies, and reagent catalog numbers is provided in the Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software and GraphPad Prism 10, as appropriate. Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test unless otherwise specified, whereas paired comparisons were used for matched CRC and adjacent normal tissues where appropriate. Survival differences were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier analysis and the log-rank test. Prognostic associations were assessed using univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. Correlations between continuous variables were evaluated using correlation tests as indicated in the corresponding analyses. For all statistical tests, p ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In figure annotations, ns indicates no statistical significance, and *, **, ***, and **** indicate p < 0.05, p < 0.01, p < 0.001, and p < 0.0001, respectively.

Results

Expression, genetic alterations, and prognostic significance of disulfidptosis-related genes in colorectal cancer

The expression, genomic alterations, and prognostic associations of 15 previously reported DRGs were first evaluated in the TCGA-COAD cohort. Eleven of the 15 DRGs showed significant differential expression between tumor and normal tissues (p < 0.05). MYH10 and SLC7A11 were significantly upregulated, whereas FLNA, FLNB, MYH9, TLN1, MYL6, CAPZB, IQGAP1, PDLIM1, and CD2AP were significantly downregulated in tumor tissues. ACTB, DSTN, ACTN4, and INF2 did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 1A). Among 448 cases with available somatic mutation data, the overall DRG mutation frequency was 30.36% (altered in 136 of 448 samples). MYH9 had the highest mutation frequency (12%), followed by FLNB and FLNA (each 10%), MYH10 (7%), TLN1 and INF2 (each 6%), and IQGAP1 (5%); the remaining DRGs showed mutation frequencies ranging from 1 to 3% (Fig. 1B). CNV analysis showed both copy number gains and losses across the DRG set. Copy number gains were most frequent in IQGAP1 and ACTB, whereas copy number losses were most prominent in CAPZB, with relatively high loss frequencies also observed in PDLIM1, DSTN, SLC7A11, and FLNB (Fig. 1C–D). Kaplan–Meier analysis identified eight DRGs that were significantly associated with overall survival and were therefore included in the survival panel; higher expression of ACTB, TLN1, INF2, FLNA, and MYH10 was associated with worse outcomes, whereas higher expression of CD2AP, ACTN4, and PDLIM1 was associated with more favorable prognosis (Fig. 1E). Co-expression analysis revealed substantial pairwise correlations among the DRGs (Fig. 1F).

Identification of DRG-defined molecular subtypes, immune infiltration, and pathway enrichment

Unsupervised consensus clustering of 689 CRC samples based on DRG expression profiles identified two molecular subtypes—DRG cluster A (n = 383) and DRG cluster B (n = 306)—with optimal stability at k = 2 (Fig. 2A–B). Among the 604 patients with complete overall survival data, Kaplan–Meier analysis showed significantly better overall survival in DRG cluster A than in cluster B (Fig. 2C). PCA, t-SNE, and UMAP analyses supported the separation between the two subtypes (Fig. 2D). Heatmap visualization showed that most DRGs were expressed at higher levels in cluster B, with FLNA displaying a particularly marked difference; clinicopathological annotations were also displayed across the two subtypes (Fig. 2E).

ssGSEA indicated broad differences in immune-cell signatures between the two DRG clusters, with DRG cluster B showing higher enrichment scores for multiple immune-cell populations, including activated B cells, activated CD8 + T cells, activated dendritic cells, macrophages, mast cells, regulatory T cells, and several T-helper cell subsets (Fig. 2F). GSVA further indicated that DRG cluster B was associated with stronger enrichment of extracellular matrix organization, cell adhesion and migration, integrin-related processes, actin-cytoskeleton regulation, TGF-β/MAPK-related signaling, focal adhesion, and ECM–receptor interaction pathways (Fig. 2G–H).

Differentially expressed genes between DRG Clusters and DEG-Derived Subtyping

A total of 936 DEGs were identified between DRG cluster A and cluster B. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses indicated that these genes were mainly associated with extracellular matrix organization, collagen-containing extracellular matrix, cell-substrate and cell–matrix adhesion, focal adhesion, ECM–receptor interaction, PI3K–Akt signaling, proteoglycans in cancer, and regulation of the actin cytoskeleton (Fig. 3A–D). A second round of consensus clustering applied to the DEG expression profiles in 689 CRC samples identified two subtypes—gene cluster A (n = 359) and gene cluster B (n = 330) (Fig. 3E). Among the 604 patients with complete overall survival data, Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that gene cluster B had significantly worse overall survival than gene cluster A (Fig. 3E). PCA, t-SNE, and UMAP analyses supported the separation between these subtypes (Fig. 3F), and most DRGs showed higher expression in gene cluster B than in gene cluster A (Fig. 3G). These findings indicate that the heterogeneity captured by the 15 DRGs was accompanied by broader subtype-associated transcriptional differences.

Construction and internal validation of the prognostic model

Univariate Cox regression applied to the 936 DEGs identified 361 genes significantly associated with overall survival. Subsequent LASSO-Cox regression yielded a four-gene signature comprising GPRASP1, TIMP1, KRT6A, and SPINK1 (Fig. 4A–C). The risk score was calculated as follows: Risk Score = 0.33660 × GPRASP1 + 0.28270 × TIMP1 + 0.15647 × KRT6A − 0.15510 × SPINK1. Risk scores were significantly higher in DRG cluster B and gene cluster B than in their corresponding cluster A groups (Fig. 4D–E). Expression patterns of multiple DRGs also differed between the high- and low-risk groups (Fig. 4F).

(C) Regression coefficients of the four genes included in the final signature, yellow bars indicate positive regression coefficients, whereas the blue bar indicates a negative regression coefficient. (F) Expression of the 15 DRGs in the high- and low-risk groups; statistical significance: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001

In the full cohort, time-dependent ROC analysis showed AUCs of 0.651, 0.685, and 0.675 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival, respectively (Fig. 5A). Kaplan–Meier analysis showed significantly worse overall survival in the high-risk group in the full cohort, training cohort, and internal test cohort (Fig. 5A–C). Risk-score distribution, survival-status plots, and model-gene expression heatmaps showed the expected risk stratification pattern across the three cohorts, with higher GPRASP1, TIMP1, and KRT6A expression and lower SPINK1 expression generally observed in the high-risk group. A nomogram integrating the risk score and clinicopathological variables was subsequently constructed (Fig. 6A). In the nomogram, pathological stage showed the widest point range, whereas age and risk group also contributed to the total points. The illustrated example had a total score of approximately 110, corresponding to predicted 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival probabilities of approximately 0.959, 0.895, and 0.834, respectively (Fig. 6A). Calibration curves showed good agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities in both cohorts, with C-index values of 0.771 (95% CI: 0.711–0.832) in the training cohort and 0.776 (95% CI: 0.716–0.836) in the internal test cohort (Fig. 6B–C). DCA showed a net clinical benefit for the nomogram across selected threshold probability ranges in both cohorts (Fig. 6D–E). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses showed that the risk score remained an independent prognostic factor in multivariate analysis (Fig. 6F–G).

Immune cell infiltration, pathway enrichment, somatic mutation, and cancer stemness

CIBERSORT-based analysis showed that the risk score was positively correlated with estimated macrophage and neutrophil infiltration and negatively correlated with plasma cells and resting CD4^ + memory T cells (Fig. 7A–B). Consistent with these correlations, the high-risk group had significantly higher estimated fractions of M0, M1, and M2 macrophages and neutrophils, whereas plasma cells, resting CD4^ + memory T cells, and resting dendritic cells were more abundant in the low-risk group (Fig. 7C).

GSEA indicated that the high-risk group was enriched in pathways related to extracellular matrix remodeling and collagen biology, whereas the low-risk group showed greater enrichment of mitochondrial and metabolic pathways (Fig. 8A–D). Somatic mutation analysis identified TP53, APC, TTN, and KRAS as the most frequently mutated genes in both risk groups; the high-risk group showed a modestly higher overall mutation frequency (98.03% vs 94.33%) (Fig. 8E–F). TMB was significantly higher in the high-risk group (p = 0.0024) and showed a modest positive correlation with the risk score (R = 0.18, p = 0.00041) (Fig. 8G–H). RNAss showed a significant negative correlation with the risk score (R = − 0.48, p < 2.2 × 10^ − 16) (Fig. 8I).

Immune checkpoints, tumor microenvironment, MSI, and estimated drug sensitivity

Among the selected immune checkpoint-, DNA-repair-, and stromal-related genes, PDCD1, CD274, CTLA4, FAP, TAGLN, and LOXL2 showed significantly higher expression in the high-risk group, whereas MSH2 was slightly but significantly lower in the high-risk group. In contrast, POLE2, FEN1, MCM6, POLD3, and MSH6 did not differ significantly between the two risk groups. Pairwise correlation analysis also showed correlations among several of these genes and the risk score (Fig. 9A–B). ESTIMATE analysis indicated that the high-risk group had significantly higher stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores (Fig. 9C). MSI-H tumors had significantly higher risk scores than both MSS and MSI-L tumors (p < 0.001), whereas no significant difference was observed between MSS and MSI-L groups (p = 0.6) (Fig. 9D). TIDE analysis showed higher TIDE scores in the high-risk group, suggesting greater predicted immune evasion (Fig. 9E).

Predicted drug sensitivity differed significantly between the two risk groups. Compared with the low-risk group, the high-risk group showed lower predicted IC50 values for bexarotene, bleomycin, bryostatin 1, camptothecin, cisplatin, nilotinib, dasatinib, docetaxel, doxorubicin, etoposide, temsirolimus, vinorelbine, imatinib, sunitinib, vinblastine, gefitinib, pazopanib, and AICAR. In contrast, lower predicted IC50 values for AKT inhibitor VIII, pyrimethamine, sorafenib, lapatinib, erlotinib, and BIBW2992 were observed in the low-risk group (Fig. 10A–D). These IC50 values were computationally estimated and require experimental validation.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing data from 10 CRC samples in GSE146771, comprising 10,468 cells, were processed through dimensionality reduction and clustering, yielding 20 clusters that were subsequently annotated into major cell types, including B cells, CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, malignant cells, mast cells, monocytes/macrophages, NK cells, plasma cells, and regulatory T cells (Fig. 11A). TIMP1 expression was relatively enriched in fibroblast-associated populations but was also detected in monocytes/macrophages, endothelial cells, plasma cells, mast cells, and to a certain extent in malignant cells. GPRASP1 showed low and scattered expression across several stromal and immune-cell populations. SPINK1 and KRT6A were predominantly expressed in malignant cells (Fig. 11B–C).

Public database validation of model gene expression and survival associations

Expression patterns of the four model genes were examined in the TCGA-COAD and GSE39582 cohorts, and survival associations were evaluated in the GSE39582 cohort. In TCGA-COAD, TIMP1 and KRT6A were upregulated in tumor tissues, whereas GPRASP1 showed lower transcript-level expression in tumor tissues; SPINK1 did not differ significantly between tumor and normal tissues (Fig. 12A). Similar expression patterns were observed in GSE39582, with tumor upregulation of TIMP1 and KRT6A and transcript-level downregulation of GPRASP1, whereas SPINK1 did not show a stable tumor-specific increase (Fig. 12B). Survival analysis in GSE39582 showed that higher GPRASP1, TIMP1, and KRT6A expression was associated with poorer overall survival, whereas higher SPINK1 expression was associated with more favorable overall survival (Fig. 12C).

Representative immunohistochemical images from the Human Protein Atlas were examined as public protein-level evidence. In the representative images, GPRASP1 was annotated as high in both normal colorectal tissue and colorectal cancer tissue, which was not concordant with the tumor-specific transcript-level decrease observed in public datasets (Fig. 13A). TIMP1 and KRT6A were annotated as not detected in normal tissue and medium in tumor tissue, consistent with their transcript-level upregulation (Fig. 13B and D). SPINK1 was annotated as not detected in normal tissue and low in tumor tissue (Fig. 13C). Overall, TIMP1 and KRT6A showed the most reproducible expression patterns across public transcriptomic datasets and public protein-level images, whereas SPINK1 showed less stable transcript-level behavior and GPRASP1 showed discordance between public transcriptomic data and representative Human Protein Atlas images.

Discussion

Using a stepwise analytical framework, we characterized disulfidptosis-related molecular heterogeneity in CRC and derived a four-gene prognostic signature from subtype-associated transcriptional alterations. Starting from 15 previously reported DRGs, we identified molecular subtypes with distinct survival, immune-infiltration, and pathway-enrichment profiles and found that these subtype differences were accompanied by broader downstream transcriptional changes. The resulting four-gene risk model was associated with survival outcome, tumor microenvironmental features, genomic instability-related indicators, and computationally inferred drug sensitivity. These findings suggest that disulfidptosis-associated molecular heterogeneity may contribute to prognostically relevant risk stratification in CRC and may reflect tumor microenvironmental differences across risk groups.

The four-gene signature was derived from subtype-associated DEGs identified after DRG-based molecular subtyping. This model-development strategy indicates that SPINK1, GPRASP1, TIMP1, and KRT6A represent transcriptional programs associated with DRG-defined molecular states and their downstream biological features. In this context, the signature captured a phenotype characterized by extracellular matrix remodeling, stromal activity, immune contexture, and survival risk. At the single-gene level, several DRGs showed prognostic associations, whereas at the subtype level, DRG cluster B and DEG-derived gene cluster B consistently showed worse outcomes and stronger enrichment of immune-cell-infiltration, extracellular-matrix-, and PI3K-Akt-related programs. CNV and mutation analyses did not show a simple correspondence between copy number events and mutation frequency, suggesting that these represent parallel genomic layers contributing to DRG dysregulation. Extending the analysis from the initial 15-gene set to subtype-associated DEGs therefore helped identify broader transcriptional programs with greater prognostic relevance.

KRT6A, an epithelial-specific intermediate filament protein, is frequently upregulated in multiple solid tumors and has been associated with poor differentiation, invasiveness, and unfavorable prognosis. In lung cancer, KRT6A has been linked to radioresistance and metastasis through p53 signaling [31] and to MYC-mediated activation of the pentose phosphate pathway [32]. In bladder cancer, it is regulated by miR-31-5p and has been associated with increased adhesion and resistance to apoptosis [33]. In CRC, Harada-Kagitani et al. reported high KRT6A expression at the invasive front and in tumor-budding areas, implicating it in cytoskeletal remodeling and local invasion [34]. In the present study, KRT6A was consistently upregulated at both the mRNA and protein levels, was enriched in malignant epithelial cells in the single-cell dataset, and was associated with extracellular matrix-related pathway enrichment. Among the four model genes, KRT6A showed the most reproducible validation signal, supporting its close association with epithelial remodeling and invasive behavior in CRC.

The other three genes showed more variable validation patterns across datasets and platforms. TIMP1, which remained enriched in the high-risk group, has been linked to extracellular matrix regulation, FAK/Akt signaling, and macrophage-associated immune remodeling [35, 36]. GPRASP1 showed low and scattered expression across several stromal and immune-cell populations in the single-cell dataset and may reflect a context-dependent stromal or immune-associated component of the risk phenotype [37–39]. SPINK1 showed a protective association in the present model, consistent with the context-dependent behavior of prognostic genes across CRC cohorts [40–43].

Immune and microenvironmental analyses further suggested that the high-risk group was characterized by a stromal-rich, immune-evasive state. This group showed increased macrophage and neutrophil infiltration, elevated stromal and ESTIMATE scores, higher TIDE scores, and enrichment of extracellular matrix remodeling and PI3K-Akt-related pathways, whereas the low-risk group showed greater enrichment of metabolic programs. These observations indicate that the risk model reflects variation in stromal activation and immune exclusion in addition to survival differences. Estimated drug-sensitivity patterns also differed between the two groups, adding pharmacogenomic information to the characterization of the risk model.

Single-cell analysis showed preferential expression of KRT6A and SPINK1 in malignant cells, whereas TIMP1 was detected across fibroblast-associated, monocyte/macrophage, endothelial, plasma-cell, mast-cell, and malignant-cell populations. GPRASP1 showed low and scattered expression across several stromal and immune-cell populations. These localization patterns suggest that the four-gene signature may reflect both tumor-cell-intrinsic and microenvironmental components of the disulfidptosis-associated molecular state.

Tissue-level analyses further showed that KRT6A had the most consistent upregulation across transcript-level and protein-level assessments. TIMP1 and SPINK1 showed more variable patterns across platforms, and GPRASP1 showed downregulation in public transcriptomic datasets but non-concordant findings in Human Protein Atlas images and paired tissue assays. These findings require confirmation in larger independent cohorts.

Several recent studies have developed disulfidptosis-related prognostic models in CRC or colon adenocarcinoma, although their gene sets, analytical strategies, and validation depth have differed substantially. Yang et al. analyzed 24 DRGs in TCGA-COAD and GSE39582 and constructed a broader prognostic framework centered on DRG subtyping and immune characterization [44]. Song et al. focused on T-cell-related immune regulation and highlighted DIAPH1 and NDUFB10 through external validation, single-cell profiling, and functional experiments in T cells [45]. Shi et al. followed a workflow more similar to ours, using a 15-DRG-based subtype-guided strategy, deriving DEGs between DRG-defined subtypes, and constructing an eight-gene prognostic model through multi-omics analysis [46]. However, the exact composition of the initial 15-DRG set differed between the two studies. Shi et al. included a mixed DRG set containing metabolism-, mitochondrial-function-, and cytoskeleton-related genes, whereas the present study used a more cytoskeleton- and actin-associated DRG set. In addition, differences in cohort composition, preprocessing, DEG selection, survival screening, and model-building procedures may also have contributed to the distinct final prognostic genes. Therefore, the present four-gene model should be interpreted as a complementary subtype-guided signature rather than evidence of methodological superiority over previous models. Together with these studies, our results add a compact subtype-guided prognostic signature to the broader disulfidptosis-related molecular landscape in CRC.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the model showed statistically significant prognostic discrimination, its predictive performance remained moderate, and its incremental value beyond standard clinicopathological variables was not formally assessed. Second, validation was based on an internal random split rather than an independent external cohort. Third, tissue-level validation included only 11 paired samples, and concordance between transcript-level and protein-level findings was incomplete for several genes. Fourth, the mechanistic relationship between the four model genes and disulfidptosis remains inferential because no perturbation experiments were performed. Finally, the public cohorts analyzed here were predominantly colon/COAD-based, and the applicability of these findings to rectal cancer and the broader CRC spectrum requires further investigation.

Conclusion

This study identified a four-gene prognostic signature associated with disulfidptosis-related molecular heterogeneity in CRC. The model was linked to survival differences and distinct tumor microenvironmental features, and KRT6A showed the most consistent validation pattern across public-dataset and tissue-level analyses. These findings provide a basis for further evaluation of subtype-guided prognostic stratification in CRC and identify candidate biomarkers for further biological investigation and independent validation.
