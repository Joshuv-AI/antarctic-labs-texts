Linking the reversal of gestational insulin resistance to postpartum depression
Authors: Minoli Vinoda Abeysekera, Duan Ni, Leah Gilbert, Emily Hibbert, Ralph Nanan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostpartum depression (PPD) constitutes a significant mental health disorder affecting almost one fifth of pregnancies globally. Despite extensive research, the precise etiological mechanisms underlying PPD remain elusive. However, several risk factors like genetic predisposition, hormonal fluctuations, and stress-related environmental and psychosocial triggers have been found to be implicated in its development.MainRecently, an increased risk of PPD has been reported to be associated with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), which is characterized by the disruption of glucose metabolism, primarily attributed to the emergence of insulin resistance (IR). Background

Postpartum depression (PPD) is characterized as the emergence of major depressive symptoms within 4 weeks following childbirth, as identified by the peripartum-onset specifier defined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition, text revision [1]. However, this definition of PPD is increasingly viewed as excessively limited [2–4]. Consequently, the concept of PPD has been broadened to encompass depressive episodes occurring within a timeframe ranging from 6 to 12 months post-delivery [5].

PPD affects 17% of pregnancies worldwide, as recently reported [6]. It results in significant burdens of morbidity and mortality, including the risk of suicide, impaired daily functioning, compromised maternal-infant bonding, and adverse outcomes for the infant [5, 7].

Multiple risk factors contribute to PPD, including both genetic and environmental factors, primarily rooted in the heightened maternal stress [8]. From an endocrinological perspective, one of the pivotal elements linked to PPD is the rapid and substantial hormonal shifts that transpire during pregnancy and the postpartum phase. Notably, hormones such as estrogen and progesterone experience a pronounced surge during pregnancy, followed by a precipitous decline after childbirth. These dynamic hormonal fluctuations exert an influence on mood regulation and can contribute to PPD [9]. Other hormones like thyroid hormones, cortisol, and oxytocin are believed to be implicated as well [10], though their detailed contribution to the pathophysiology of PPD remains elusive. There are other endocrinological risk factors for PPD including conditions like gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), accompanied with aggravated insulin resistance (IR) [11].

With accumulating evidence suggesting the role of insulin and glucose metabolism in mood control in the brain, we here propose a novel concept, positing that the abrupt reversal of gestational IR following childbirth and the concomitant alterations in central nervous system (CNS) insulin and glucose metabolism play a role in the development of PPD. This hypothesis is supported by the fact that GDM patients, who undergo more pronounced metabolic changes than those without, exhibit a higher risk of PPD. It represents a novel possibility, and if further validated, it will provide guidance for future PPD prevention, diagnosis, and treatment.

Critical roles of glucose metabolism and insulin in the brain

The human brain is an incredibly energy-demanding organ, accounting for roughly 20% of the body’s total energy consumption, despite its relatively small size, constituting only about 2% of the total body weight [12]. To meet this substantial energy requirement, the brain heavily relies on glucose metabolism as its primary energy source [13, 14]. Historically, it was believed that the brain was primarily insulin-insensitive and leveraged insulin-independent glucose transporters for glucose uptake. However, recent research has revealed that the brain also expresses insulin-dependent transporters, including GLUT4 and GLUT8 [15–17], indicating the involvement of insulin in brain glucose metabolism.

Insulin in the brain comes from various sources. A portion of brain insulin is obtained through uptake from the peripheral circulation, via the transport across the blood–brain barrier and the blood-cerebrospinal fluid barrier, which mainly occurs at the choroid plexus located in the brain ventricles [18–20]. Furthermore, emerging evidence now suggests that insulin can be synthesized within the brain as well, by neurons [21] or the epithelium of the choroid plexus [22]. Collectively, these mechanisms contribute to brain insulin levels that are approximately 10–100 times higher than those found in the bloodstream, facilitating the precise and intricate fine-tuning of brain glucose metabolism.

Involvement of insulin and glucose metabolism in mood modulation

Insulin, in conjunction with its associated glucose metabolism, plays a pivotal role in modulating mood. Research in rodent models has highlighted the significant positive impact on brain functions from glucose administration directly into the cerebrospinal fluid, which significantly enhanced memory [12, 23]. In some clinical studies, individuals receiving intranasal insulin treatment demonstrated improved memory and mood [24]. These observations suggest the direct positive modulatory effects of glucose and insulin signaling on brain functions. Structurally, targets of insulin in the brain mainly distribute within regions such as the nucleus accumbens, ventral tegmental area, amygdala, and raphe nuclei [25, 26]. These are all brain regions responsible for mood control, suggesting the critical mood-regulatory role of insulin and glucose metabolism. Disruption of insulin homeostasis like IR and the downstream glucose metabolism can alter the brain structures directly, leading to mood disorders [27]. This has been found in major depressive disorders where there are reduced hippocampal volume and abnormal cortico-hippocampal circuits, accompanied with IR [27]. Supporting this, rapid alterations in IR or glycemic regulation have also been found to be linked to elevated risk of depression symptoms, as observed in patients undergoing gastric bypass surgery [28, 29], insulin therapy [30], or GLP1 receptor agonist treatment [31, 32].

Mechanistically, insulin exerts a positive influence on dopamine action. It enhances dopamine activity by promoting the expression of dopamine reuptake transporters, inhibiting dopamine-degrading enzymes, and modulating the firing frequency of dopaminergic neurons [26]. Disruption of insulin signaling through ablating insulin receptors in the hypothalamus and astrocytes has been shown to lead to increased anxiety and depression-like behaviors in mice. These effects are likely attributed to reduced dopamine release, diminished glial ATP release within the dopaminergic system, and alterations in purinergic signaling [25, 26, 33]. In addition to dopamine, insulin can also finetune another neurotransmitter serotonin. For example, an increased insulin level, triggered by carbohydrate intake, was found to increase the circulating level of tryptophan, the serotonin precursor, which further contributed to mood enhancement [34]. Similar effects were observed in humans after carbohydrate ingestion, which promotes serotonin synthesis in the brain and helps to improve moods directly [35].

On the other hand, insulin-sensitizing agents like thiazolidinediones have been shown to improve depression-like symptoms in rodent models [26]. Collectively, these findings all indicate the regulatory roles of insulin and the downstream glucose metabolism on mood and the brain.

Gestational IR and the brain

IR is commonly found during pregnancy. It appears to be a well-preserved physiological phenomenon throughout evolution, with GDM representing an exaggerated variant of this normal physiological alteration [36–38]. Pregnancy imposes unique challenges on maternal energy metabolism as it must efficiently allocate energy resources between both the mother and the developing fetus. In this context, the progressive IR observed during pregnancy serves to augment the availability of glucose, thereby enhancing the energy supply to both the fetus and the mother, with particular significance for the maternal brain [39, 40].

The heightened energy supply to the maternal brain is of central importance during pregnancy. It is particularly increased in the second and third trimesters of gestation. This expanding energy reservoir plays a crucial role in supporting pregnancy-induced adaptive changes within the brain. These adaptations lead to prominent reshaping of neural networks, including the default mode network, and finely tune maternal behaviors [41, 42]. Consequently, these brain changes further enhance maternal–fetal bonding, nesting behavior, and the physiological responsiveness to fetal cues, ultimately contributing to the overall well-being of both the mother and the developing fetus [43].

Gestational IR and PPD

Gestational IR improves rapidly postpartum [44, 45], regardless of the presence or absence of GDM [46]. Although most of these findings were made in the periphery, it can be speculated that similar changes are likely to occur in parallel in the maternal brain. Given the critical role of gestational IR in providing essential energy support for the maternal brain [39, 40], it is reasonable to infer that its reversal post-delivery would lead to changes in maternal brain glucose metabolism, affecting maternal mood, and possibly, as we hypothesize, result in PPD. Indeed, patients with GDM, who typically experience a more pronounced reversal or improvement of IR after delivery, face a 1.59 times higher risk of PPD [11].

Mechanistically, reversal of IR post-delivery rewires the glucose metabolism in the periphery [44–46]; these changes are likely to extend towards the brain as well. This would inevitably alter the brain insulin homeostasis and suppress glucose metabolism. Since the insulin and glucose metabolic signals influence various neurotransmitter as discussed above [25, 26, 33–35], their postpartum modifications are likely to profoundly disrupt the equilibrium of brain dopamine and serotonin levels, compromising the delicate balance of neurotransmitters and their actions.

These alterations are critical to maternal mood control, and when they surpass a defined threshold, they can profoundly interrupt maternal brain functions, ultimately detrimentally affecting mood regulation and leading to PPD (Fig. 1).

Proposed model linking the development of postpartum depression (PPD) to gestational insulin resistance (IR). During pregnancy, the mother develops IR both in the periphery and the brain, which increases glucose influx into the brain and promotes insulin-mediated glucose metabolism. This supports enhanced serotonin and dopamine signaling, contributing positively to mood control. After delivery, the IR rapidly reverses and glucose influx and metabolism in the brain are reduced, thereby disrupting the serotonin and dopamine signaling of pregnancy. When such changes exceed a critical threshold, as in gestational diabetes mellitus, the significant postpartum alteration of brain glucose metabolism and downstream neural signal pathways may lead to abrupt mood changes, resulting in PPD

These changes might be more evident in the case of GDM, where, following delivery, more substantial acute changes in brain IR and marked relative reductions in both brain insulin and glucose levels and their metabolism are expected. Consequently, this might cause abrupt shifts in brain functions related to mood control, which may partly explain the heightened risk of PPD in GDM patients [8, 36].

Conclusions

While various potential risk factors for PPD have been identified, many of them have not been comprehensively interrogated to date and might only account for part of the full etiology of PPD. Our concept, drawing upon the state-of-the-art advances in metabolism and neuroscience, proposes a novel mechanism for the development of PPD. We suggest that PPD may arise as an exacerbated consequence following the reversal of gestational IR.

It is important to acknowledge that the exact etiology of PPD is likely to be multifactorial. There are various aspects at play, such as the weight change [47–49] and sleep deprivation [50] during postpartum period, breastfeeding behaviors [51, 52], and, more mechanistically, hormonal changes [2] and metabolic alterations like in ketosis [53] during pregnancy. However, many previously proposed mechanisms struggle to explain the association between PPD and GDM. These observations could instead be explained to some extent by our hypothesis, despite arguments that receiving the diagnosis of GDM itself might lead to PPD, which still remains controversial [54–57]. In future, more in-depth and comprehensive research is imperative to delve deeper into changes in gestational IR, PPD, and their associated symptoms, shedding more light onto their plausible mechanistic links.

In summary, our hypothesis introduces an additional mechanism contributing to the understanding of PPD. If validated, our model could offer valuable insights for future clinical diagnosis of PPD and may inform strategies for its prevention and treatment. This could involve monitoring insulin and glucose metabolism or implementing interventions aimed at modulating brain glucose supply and metabolism for individuals at risk.
