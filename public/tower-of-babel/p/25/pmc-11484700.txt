Investors reward countries for participating in climate agreements
Authors: Konark Saxena, Mandeep Singh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
International climate agreements are one of the best-known approaches to coordinating climate actions among governments but face a free-rider problem, where individual governments lack sufficient incentives to reduce emissions. This study examines the role of investors in providing rewards to governments through sovereign bond yields to encourage climate cooperation and reduce emission intensity. Using a difference-in-differences method, this study examines how sovereign bond yields change around the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement. The findings show that investors reward governments committed to reducing greenhouse gas emissions with a 4 to 5 basis point decrease in sovereign bond yields. This suggests that investors better compensate countries making significant commitments to climate agreements, helping mitigate the free-rider problem and encouraging effective climate action.

Introduction

Describing his Nobel prize-winning contributions, William Nordhaus refers to climate change as the ultimate challenge for economics1. He explains why individual investors and consumers are unable to solve the problem of climate change via utility maximization and trading: markets fail to address climate change because our planet’s climate is a public good. This feature creates, perhaps, the most consequential global negative externality where market prices do not include information on how countries, investors, and consumers should optimally share the Earth’s global budget for greenhouse gases. In the absence of informative market prices, international climate agreements are one of the best ways to coordinate actions by various governments to address global climate externalities1. For example, the Montreal Protocol is credited with successfully limiting the use of ozone-killing chlorofluorocarbons2,3.

Climate agreements help governments coordinate on various fronts, including regulation, emissions, carbon prices, and green technology. Participating in a well-designed climate agreement is typically a decision made by governments concerned about the impact of climate change on the welfare of current and future generations. However, various political and economic concerns can influence a government’s decision not to participate. Therefore, despite the well-known benefits of climate agreements, they often encounter a free-rider problem. This problem arises when individual countries have little incentive to contribute to reducing greenhouse gas emissions if they can benefit from the investments made by other countries1. This scenario leads to a perverse equilibrium where individual countries do not have their incentives aligned to solve a problem that they all want to solve. This issue of free-riding lies at the heart of the challenge of grappling with climate change.

Financial incentives can be employed to overcome the free-rider problem by encouraging governments to act responsibly. Climate agreements serve to assist investors in coordinating and integrating climate considerations into financial incentives. They can achieve this by establishing a pool of capital dedicated to supporting climate-friendly actions of governments and firms. For instance, the United Nations established the Net Zero Asset Managers Initiative4, which aims to invest trillions of dollars in alignment with the goals of the Paris Agreement.

Climate-conscious investor alliances are consistent with recent theoretical developments in financial economics suggesting that companies should maximize an investor’s overall welfare, including climate concerns, instead of focusing solely on profits5,6. Nevertheless, the use of financial incentives to drive environmentally friendly behavior has only recently been documented for firms7–13. This evidence suggests that green firms that employ climate-friendly sustainable practices (such as renewable energy companies) are rewarded, while brown firms that have significant negative climate impact (such as fossil fuel companies) face penalties from investors with tastes for low emissions. Additionally, research has demonstrated that two bonds with nearly identical risk profiles, issued by the same government of a country, can trade at different prices due to one being considered climate-friendly or green9. Climate-conscious investors are willing to pay a premium (referred to as the greenium) for such bonds.

When it comes to countries, the taste channel suggests penalizing those with high emissions intensity and rewarding those with low emissions intensity. However, this approach may not be wise, let alone fair, if the aim is to encourage climate cooperation. An alternative approach is to provide incentives for climate cooperation, especially to high-emission countries. In this approach, governments that face larger costs and higher risks due to participation in climate agreements would receive larger financial incentives. That is, the increases in transition and regulatory risk faced by countries signing a climate agreement can be offset by a corresponding increase in financial incentives. Following this approach, investors would use incentives to encourage countries with varying capabilities and willingness to undertake climate-friendly actions to join a climate agreement and offset their participation costs. The underlying conjecture is that investors believe they can enhance aggregate financial and climate outcomes by facilitating cost-sharing and risk-sharing among countries participating in climate agreements, promoting cooperation, and benefiting all parties involved.

To incentivize governments, investors can use sovereign bonds, the pricing of which depends on the underlying yield. A decrease (increase) in the underlying yield increases (decreases) the price of the bond and allows the government to raise more (less) capital for the same bond. A change in sovereign bond yield upon joining a climate agreement directly affects governments’ borrowing capacity and ability to fund their country’s future (potentially greener) growth. Lower borrowing costs for governments can also be passed on to households through reduced interest rates on mortgages, enhancing the political appeal of this incentive. Therefore, for governments engaging in a cost–benefit analysis of participation in climate agreements, understanding the impact on their sovereign yields of commitments made under a climate agreement is crucial. If investors’ demand for sovereign bonds for countries committing to climate action under a climate agreement increases, we will observe a decrease in sovereign yield for those countries.

While such financial incentives for climate cooperation are a theoretical possibility, there is scant empirical evidence of whether investors nudge governments to join a climate agreement to cooperate on global climate action and consequently help address the free-rider problem. This study explores whether sovereign yields change when countries join a climate agreement that is, do they act as incentives for participation.

According to the incentive hypothesis, investors’ demand for the sovereign bonds of countries that bear higher costs or enjoy relatively fewer benefits from a climate agreement increases, implying a decline in sovereign bond yields for such countries. This could encourage more countries to participate and enhance the agreement’s likelihood of success. We proceed by categorizing countries into groups based on their relative rankings in four key areas: 1) exposures to climate regulatory and transition risks; 2) emission intensities; 3) physical climate risks; and 4) population. These groupings are useful in identifying countries that face higher costs to participate. Climate transition and regulatory risks stem from moving to low-carbon technologies and introducing new climate-related regulations. Existing literature supports this view, showing that transition and regulatory climate risks have already affected asset prices for high-emissions firms, including their stock returns, options, and corporate bonds14–19.

Climate agreements may exacerbate transition risks in countries with strict legal commitments stipulated in and imposed by a climate agreement and in countries with large populations contributing to high total emissions despite low per-capita levels, and in emissions-driven economies. They may also result in poorly designed laws or regulations that increase environmental risks such as those identified in some studies20,21. If investors perceive the risk channel as dominant, sovereign yields for such countries may increase. Conversely, suppose investors are optimistic about the benefits of climate cooperation and believe those benefits outweigh the additional risks. Then, we may observe a decline in the sovereign yields of such countries. A more substantial decline in sovereign yields for countries with lower incentives (exacerbated transition and regulatory risks or more emissions-intensive economic growth) to participate in a climate agreement aligns with investors encouraging countries to participate in climate agreements rather than with the risk channel.

The Kyoto Protocol offers a unique setting for studying the differential impacts of countries’ participation in climate agreements, particularly those facing varying degrees of regulatory and transition risks. Adopted in 1997, the Kyoto Protocol is an international treaty that places a higher burden on a predefined set of countries, called Annex-I countries, mandated under the protocol to reduce greenhouse gas emissions. The protocol justifies this higher burden on the basis that these countries are principally responsible for the current levels of greenhouse gas emissions due to more than 150 years of unabated industrial activity. Annex-I countries that ratified the Kyoto Protocol were legally required to reduce their emissions. The regulatory and transition costs and risks are likely higher for Annex-I countries that ratify the protocol than those that do not ratify. According to the risk channel, sovereign yields of ratifying Annex-I countries should increase upon ratification to compensate investors for bearing these additional risks. However, if investors want to incentivize climate cooperation because they consider the overall benefits of a stronger commitment to the protocol to be higher than the additional regulatory and transition risks it imposes, then sovereign yields of ratifying Annex-I countries should decrease. We analyze how investors perceive participation in the Kyoto Protocol as an Annex-I ratifying party by evaluating the difference in sovereign bond yields between Annex-I ratifying and Annex-I non-ratifying countries, before and after ratifying the Kyoto Protocol.

We also study differences between participating countries with relatively high and low emission intensities, where emissions intensity is defined as the sensitivity of total Gross Domestic Product (GDP) to total CO2 emissions. While we study these differences for both the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement; this approach has particular importance for the Paris Agreement, which has a more advanced institutional framework for technology transfer, offering a potential pathway for emission-intensive countries to reduce their emissions.

Furthermore, we explore how emission intensities interact with a country’s population to account for the additional costs associated with emissions reduction, especially in those countries with large populations. Countries with high emission intensities and large populations may warrant more substantial financial incentives to offset their specific challenges in addressing global environmental issues.

Additionally, we consider how emission intensities interact with a country’s physical risk exposure. We classify groups of countries as having high or low exposure to physical risk. Countries facing high physical climate risks are poised to benefit more from the success of a climate agreement, given the expected reduction in global physical climate risks with its implementation. Thus, in contrast to transition and regulatory risks, countries with greater exposure to physical climate risks should experience a more pronounced decline in sovereign yields from a risk perspective22. Alternatively, from an incentive perspective, these countries are already grappling with the effects of climate change and have a natural motivation to participate in climate agreements, potentially rendering investor incentives to reduce emissions unnecessary. Therefore, investors should offer weaker incentives to countries with higher exposure to physical climate risks. In contrast, countries with lower exposure to physical climate risks require more substantial incentives to reduce emissions, suggesting the possibility of lower sovereign yields for this group of countries.

We employ the difference-in-differences method with extensive fixed effects to identify how sovereign yields respond across various groups of countries, such as those with high and low emission intensities, indicating their intentions to participate in a climate agreement. For the Kyoto Protocol, we estimate the difference in yield differentials across groups before and after countries ratify the protocol. In the case of the Paris Agreement, we study the difference in yield differentials across groups before and after countries submit their Intended Nationally Determined Contributions (INDC). This difference-in-difference approach helps us understand the effects of participation and sheds light on how sovereign yields of governments react to their decision to join the climate agreement while controlling for various other sources of heterogeneity across countries and time periods.

We find evidence consistent with the hypothesis that investors reward governments participating in the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement. In the case of the Kyoto Protocol, our analysis reveals that sovereign yields rewarded governments that ratified the protocol, resulting in a notable decrease of approximately 4 basis points in the yields of Annex-I ratifying countries’ 10-year government bonds post-ratification. This decline, equivalent to 1 percentage point of the sample mean yield, bears significant economic importance and the potential for substantial cost savings for governments. Moreover, our investigation into both agreements finds that countries with higher emission intensities received compensation in the form of reduced sovereign bond yields. We further explore the interplay between emission intensities and other factors, including population and physical risk exposure. In both agreements, nations characterized by higher total emissions intensity and larger populations were incentivized more by a reduction in sovereign yields, aligning with the idea that these countries face more substantial challenges in addressing climate change and, therefore, need stronger incentives to participate. Additionally, lower physical risk exposure corresponded to lower sovereign yields in both cases, underscoring that investors offer stronger incentives to countries with lower physical risks. Our study tested the robustness of these findings through various analyzes, all of which consistently support our main results. Overall, our research suggests that climate-conscious investors are strategically designing incentives to encourage governments to engage in climate agreements to address the climate crisis.

We partition countries into two groups based on the cross-sectional median value of these measures. Discussion

This study examines the impact of joining a climate agreement on a country’s sovereign bond yield, a crucial financial instrument for government funding. The primary finding of this research is that sovereign bond yields tend to decrease when a country commits to climate-friendly actions within a climate agreement. This effect is particularly pronounced for countries requiring stronger incentives to address climate change, including those with higher emission intensity, higher populations, and lower exposure to physical climate risks.

From a policy perspective, our findings provide insights into the economic benefits for countries derived from joining climate agreements. While most policy arguments in support of joining such agreements focus on long-term planetary benefits for future generations and are rooted in ethical and environmental concerns, the financial and economic implications of participation have received less attention. One reason for this is that the tangible economic benefits of climate agreements often manifest over decades after the signing and are difficult to disentangle from other events that have transpired since.

We attempt to address this issue by leveraging the fundamental premise of finance—current market prices reflect risk-adjusted expectations of future outcomes. By analyzing the outlook of sophisticated market participants, as reflected in sovereign bond yields, we can gauge the immediate market perception of the benefits associated with joining a climate agreement, after accounting for any associated increase in regulatory and transitional risks. Of course, market expectations change rapidly, and optimism at the time of signing an agreement can subside over time. Nevertheless, our analysis offers valuable insights into how investors responded to countries joining the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement.

Our results suggest that, after estimating the long-run costs, benefits, and environmental value of climate agreements, investors view countries willing to participate in them favorably, especially if it is more costly for them to participate. From a cost perspective, investors should discount sovereign bonds given the additional transition and regulatory risks countries face after joining a climate agreement. From a benefits perspective, investors should pay a premium to countries cooperating to address global climate externalities if the value of climate cooperation exceeds the additional transition and regulatory risks the climate agreement imposes. We find that the net effect is a premium that investors pay to cooperating governments, which serves as a potential incentive for countries to engage in climate agreements and as investor testimony that such cooperation creates economic value.

The evidence in this study aligns with investor preferences for positive climate outcomes7,8,10. However, in contrast to earlier studies linking climate preferences to historical or current emissions, our paper associates climate preferences with the choice to cooperate on climate agreements aimed at reducing future emissions levels while being forgiving of higher historical emissions.

For instance, within the framework of tastes for low historical emissions, countries like China might potentially face penalties for their higher emissions, often stemming from other nations outsourcing emissions to China. However, this approach may be neither fair nor efficient, as the source of these emissions can be traced back to the consumption patterns of other countries. In contrast, our research reveals a different scenario in which investors opt to reward countries, including China in this example, for their willingness to participate in climate agreements and commit to reducing emissions, despite their higher emissions intensities. In fact, if multiple countries outsource emissions to China, and China becomes more efficient in reducing emissions following the climate agreement, it can lead to an overall reduction in global emissions.

We consider this rational investor behavior that aligns with incentivizing countries that shoulder the responsibility of reducing emissions not only for themselves but also for the global community.

Overall, our findings highlight the key role of investors’ preferences in rewarding countries’ climate action, especially when the risks associated with cooperation are high. The evidence indicates that a preference for climate action is becoming a positive and encouraging force in financial markets: it nudges governments to enact climate regulations. These regulations, in turn, nudge firms to reduce emissions17–19,23.

We note that our findings are focused on climate agreements, and we do not analyze the broader question of which risks influence the pricing of sovereign bonds. For example, it is plausible that physical climate risks, as documented in the pricing of municipal bonds, also affect the pricing of sovereign bonds22. Our primary interest lies in examining how sovereign bond yields react to countries’ decisions to join climate agreements. We find that yields do not decrease more markedly for countries with higher physical climate risks at the time of these events. However, this finding does not imply that physical climate risks do not influence bond pricing in general.

Given our results, a natural question arises: who are the climate-friendly investors integrating long-term climate incentives into sovereign yields? Anecdotal evidence points to central banks, sovereign wealth funds, and pension funds as investors who engage in detailed climate scenario analysis, and we believe they represent an important subset of climate-aware investors shaping the observed sovereign yield patterns. Further insight could be gained by analyzing portfolio holdings to identify categories of investors engaging in buying and selling sovereign bonds in response to government actions related to climate change. However, we defer this analysis to future research.

When designing future climate agreements, comparing the incentives provided by the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement is crucial. The relevant comparison in our analysis is between Tables 2–3 for the Kyoto Protocol and Tables 4–6 for the Paris Agreement. The implied yield changes are similar directionally but differ slightly in magnitudes. This difference might stem from variations in the structural features of these agreements, the time elapsed between them, and the macroeconomic conditions surrounding the two agreements. However, investor attitudes towards climate change have materially evolved, suggesting stronger effects for the Paris Agreement. Since we cannot control for changes in the macroeconomic environments across the two agreements, we are unable to determine definitively whether the rewards have strengthened over time and are more robust for the Paris Agreement.

We leave a more careful analysis of the relative magnitudes of these effects and the role of changes in climate tastes over time for future research. We also do not address the conflicts of interest problem, where governments unwilling to participate in climate agreements might also be important clients of investment managers involved in climate-conscious investor alliances. Additionally, we leave to future research the questions of how much declines in secondary market bond yields effectively reduce sovereigns’ primary market cost of capital and the threshold magnitude of reduction in yields needed to meaningfully address the free-rider problem. Our focus in this study is to document that, despite such frictions, sovereign yields react to participation in climate agreements in a way consistent with the incentive mechanism.

The fundamental premise of this study, that investors incentivize governments to collaborate on climate action, also carries prescriptive potential. Collectively, investors can play a role in nudging reluctant governments toward enacting climate regulations, often the primary lever for reducing firms’ carbon emissions. Our evidence suggests a critical role for financial markets in motivating governments to implement such regulations in the future. We propose a strategy that investor alliances can employ to enhance government incentives for addressing climate change, especially in the context of future climate agreements: rewarding countries for their climate cooperation, with stronger incentives for those facing greater economic challenges in climate cooperation and having lower exposure to physical climate risks.

For countries where governments or voting citizens do not treat climate as a top priority, our evidence suggests that they should carefully consider cooperating in climate agreements or face rising costs of capital on sovereign debt.

Overall, the evidence suggests that governments and markets are beginning to act as checks and balances for each other in global efforts to address climate change. This study highlights how, in the context of climate agreements, sovereign yields behave as though investors are collectively incentivizing governments to address the climate crisis and mitigate a consequential global negative externality.

Methods

Data sources and description

The data on 10-year monthly sovereign bond yields are from the Refinitiv Datastream database. The ultimate source of these data is countries’ central banks, which compile these data from information on the observed quotes and prices provided by bond dealers. This information is a critical indicator of prevailing market conditions for issuance in the primary market. The underlying bonds are denominated in domestic currency. We have chosen a tenor of 10 years for our analysis as it provides the most extensive cross-section of countries. We extracted all data relating to the Kyoto Protocol, including reduction targets and ratification dates, from the official website of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC). Data for the Paris Agreement, including INDC submission dates and emissions reduction targets, are from the Carbon Brief24, a website dedicated to tracking countries’ efforts to reduce emissions.

The country-level coastal population data are from the Socioeconomic Data and Applications Center (SEDAC) of the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA)25. These data are available at decadal frequency, and we use data for the decades ending 2000 and 2010. The historical data on the annual total gross domestic product (1950–2012) and annual total CO2 emissions data (1950–2012) are acquired from Our World in Data26, a scientific data publication produced as a collaborative effort between researchers at the University of Oxford and the Global Change Data Lab. These emissions data are fossil fuel emissions, measuring the country level quantity of carbon dioxide emissions from burning fossil fuels and directly from industrial processes. These emissions data include emissions from coal, oil, gas, flaring, cement, steel, and other industrial processes, but do not include land use change, deforestation, soils, or vegetation. These are production-based emissions, which are akin to scope 1 emissions. We use these data to form country groups, as described below. We source country-level annual macroeconomic and population data from the World Bank Group’s World Development Indicators (WDI) database, which are utilized as control variables in our regression analysis. Summary statistics of relevant variables are provided in Supplementary Table 5.

Description of country groups for various analyzes

Our empirical approach is designed to evaluate how the relative sovereign yields of two groups of countries change after they participate in a climate agreement. Below, we explain the construction of various country groups for analyzing participation in the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement.

Supplementary Table 6 lists Annex-I countries and their corresponding ratification dates in our sample. This categorization of countries is exogenous to our analysis and is provided by the Kyoto Protocol, which places a higher burden on Annex-I countries to reduce emissions. In this study, we compare Annex-I ratifiers with Annex-I non-ratifiers (Australia and the USA).

We also categorize countries into low and high total emissions intensity groups based on their sensitivity of total GDP to total CO2 emissions. For the Kyoto Protocol, these sensitivities are estimated using time-series regressions for each country with yearly data spanning 50 years, from 1950 to 1999. In Supplementary Table 7, we provide estimated sensitivities and grouping of countries into high and low sensitivity groups for the analysis of the Kyoto Protocol. For the Paris Agreement, we estimate these sensitivities using time-series regressions for each country with yearly data spanning 63 years, from 1950 to 2012. In Supplementary Table 8, we provide estimated sensitivities and grouping of countries into high and low sensitivity groups.

In our analysis of climate physical risk, we categorize countries into high and low groups based on the fraction of a country’s total population residing within 100 kilometers of the coast. The rationale behind this measure is that countries with a considerable portion of their population living near the coast are more vulnerable to the adverse effects of sea-level rise. Using this measure for the decades ending in 2000 and 2010, we classify countries into high and low groups for the Kyoto Protocol (Supplementary Table 9) and the Paris Agreement (Supplementary Table 10), respectively.

Finally, we establish country groups based on total population. For the Kyoto Protocol (Paris Agreement), we employ the cross-sectional median value for 1999 (2012) to divide the 30 (43) countries into large and small population groups. We present country groups based on population in Supplementary Table 11 and Supplementary Table 12 for analysis focusing on the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement, respectively.

Sovereign yield differential across country groups

Our econometric approach to analyzing sovereign yields across groups of countries resembles the creation of a long-short portfolio of sovereign bonds. In such portfolios, investments are made in the sovereign bonds of one group of countries while funding these investments by shorting the sovereign bonds of another group of countries. This interpretation of our method aligns with a well-established tradition in financial research and practical investment management, where empirical tests using such long-short portfolios are a common practice. For example, common stocks are often grouped into large and small stocks or high and low valuation stocks to analyze their risk and return properties27,28.

The advantage of grouping the sovereign bonds of countries is that country-specific or idiosyncratic drivers that change the yield of a country get diversified away and have a mitigated effect on the average sovereign yield of the group of countries29. For instance, changes in the economic outlook or political instability in a small country can meaningfully influence its yield, but within a sufficiently large group of countries, the influence of a single country’s political instability on the average sovereign yield of the group diminishes. With a substantial number of countries in each group, the focus shifts to common factors affecting the group, which systematically influence the sovereign yields of the group. For example, the average sovereign yield of a group of countries with high emissions intensity is more likely to experience substantial changes due to factors affecting most high emission-intensity countries, such as the adoption of a global climate agreement, rather than factors specific to individual countries, like country-specific credit risk or liquidity.

To minimize the effect of pre-existing differences in sovereign yield levels between the two groups, we compare a similar mix of countries. For instance, for the Kyoto Protocol, most of the Annex-I (ratifiers and non-ratifiers) are advanced economies. Under the Paris Agreement, both the high and low emissions intensity groups include countries at different stages of economic development. Our empirical strategy to address any issues when measuring the impact of participating in climate agreements relies on a combination of the Difference-in-Differences (DiD) procedure, fixed effects, and macroeconomic control variables to account for country-specific heterogeneity.

Discussion of the Parallel Trends Assumption

The validity of the DiD approach relies on the parallel trends assumption, which we expect our data to meet based on established financial economics insights on sovereign yields and asset prices. This subsection outlines why we anticipate the parallel trends assumption to hold in our analysis.

Sovereign yield models assert that yield levels differ across countries due to factors like default risk, renegotiation potential, regime changes, and liquidity risks30. Developing countries with higher default risks typically have higher yields than developed nations. Asset pricing models predict that current sovereign yields reflect all known fundamental risk factors, including default and liquidity risks, as well as risks resulting from policy changes such as climate agreements. This supports using the first difference in the DiD approach, which accounts for level factors in sovereign yields.

According to the Efficient Market Hypothesis31, the expected price of a sovereign bond should immediately adjust to anticipated trends in all determinants of their yields, thus making future yield changes unpredictable. Thus, there should be no violation of the parallel trends assumption in sovereign yield data under the Efficient Market Hypothesis.

The no-arbitrage principle further justifies the parallel trends assumption. According to the Fundamental Theorems of Asset Pricing32,33, in an arbitrage-free market, discounted sovereign bond prices are martingales, meaning their expected future value, given past information, equals the most recent observation.

Given the well-established financial economics theory, we expect the parallel trends assumption to hold, supporting our DiD approach with sovereign bond yields. A violation of the parallel trends assumption for sovereign yields would mean that investors can make unreasonably large profits without taking on additional risks, which would contradict reasonable market behavior.

While no existing theory predicts a violation of parallel trends in sovereign bond yields, it remains an empirical possibility. Therefore, we empirically check for violations of the parallel trends assumption in our sample. Supplemental Fig. 5 shows no statistically significant decline in sovereign yield differentials before the treatment date, supporting the parallel trends assumption.

The next subsection describes our specific DiD approach.

Fixed effects analysis in sovereign bond yield determination

In our primary specification, we incorporate country and time fixed effects, allowing us to account for factors that are unique to each country over time or to a particular month across all countries.

We use country fixed effects, represented by γc in equation (1), to control for omitted variables due to unobserved heterogeneity between countries that remains constant over time. This helps control for the average level of liquidity, credit risk, and economic environment in countries during our sample period. However, it does not account for changes in such variables that may occur during the sample period. If these changes are systematically correlated with the timing of joining a climate agreement and have a differential impact on the various groups of countries considered in our analysis, then such omitted variables can influence our results.

We also use time fixed effects, represented by γt in equation (1), to control for omitted variables that affect all sovereign yields in our sample in a given month. For example, an unexpected increase in global output can impact the sovereign yields of all countries, inadvertently affecting our measurement of how sovereign yields change when a country participates in a climate agreement for that month. These fixed effects account for such common effects across all countries in any given time period. Additionally, these fixed effects control for time trends that may be common across all countries, such as trends related to decreasing or increasing global inflation or general climate awareness worldwide.

Including time fixed effects in our regression specification impacts how we interpret our estimated coefficients. For example, the specification in equation (1), with the dependent variable as the 10-year government bond yield, becomes effectively equivalent to a specification where the dependent variable is the interest rate spread between the sovereign yield of a country and any proxy for the global riskless rate. These approaches are equivalent because time-fixed effects ensure that the analysis will produce identical point estimates and standard errors for both the 10-year yield and the interest rate spread, provided that the riskless rate remains constant across the cross-section in a given time period.

Note that fixed effects will not control for variables that vary across countries and time periods. For example, suppose we wanted the dependent variable to be the interest rate spread of the sovereign yield in US dollars terms minus the US Treasury Bill rate. Fixed effects are not sufficient for this interpretation, and we need to add controls for exchange rate changes. However, as we explicitly include changes in the foreign exchange rate for each country and time period as a control variable in our specification, we could also interpret our estimated coefficients as if the regression was estimated with all investments in government bonds made from the perspective of a US investor.

In summary, the estimated coefficients in our regression specification, which includes time fixed effects, country fixed effects, and various controls, should be interpreted as the change in the country-specific 10-year government bond yield after joining a climate agreement. In this context, country-specific refers to changes in sovereign yields after accounting for global factors, such as the risk-free rate, which influence the average yield across countries. It also considers country-specific factors, such as long-term physical risk exposures, which affect the average yield of a country over the sample period, and changes in yields due to factors captured by control variables, such as foreign exchange rate and macroeconomic factors, including GDP per capita, inflation rate, and reserves as a percentage of GDP.

Implementation of the difference in differences regression specification

In this section, we outline our approach to implementing the DiD model. A staggered DiD model is characterized by staggered treatment adoption across units. This feature introduces additional complexities due to the inherent heterogeneity among units and the challenges in making comparisons between different groups (such as no treatment, early treatment, and late treatment)34. We build upon recent research that acknowledges these issues as potential threats to the parallel trends assumptions necessary for valid DiD analysis35–38. Specifically, our regression model is shown in equation (2).2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${y}_{ctd}={\beta }_{1}({Treated}_{cd}\times {Post}_{td})+{{{\mathbf{\Gamma }}}}^{{\prime} }{{\bf{X}}}+{\gamma }_{cd}+{\gamma }_{td}+{u}_{ctd}$$\end{document}yctd=β1(Treatedcd×Posttd)+Γ′X+γcd+γtd+uctd

In equation (2), subscript d denotes a specific sub-experiment. Here, y represents yield on a 10-year government bond, Treatedcd is 1 (0 otherwise) when country c is a treated unit in sub-experiment d, and Posttd is 1 (0 otherwise) for periods t post-ratification or post-INDC submission in d. The equation contrasts non-treated with treated units within each sub-experiment. This approach averages time-specific effects into a singular, averaged effect, sidestepping biases from dynamic inter-group comparisons. The set of control units remains the same for each treated unit, akin to matching with a fixed group of non-treated units, while controlling for both observable and unobservable heterogeneity through macroeconomic variables and fixed effects. Consequently, our model robustly addresses biases both in post-participation comparisons between high and low commitment groups, stemming from inherent group differences, and biases from temporal comparisons potentially swayed by evolving trends affecting these groups. u represents the error term. Γ and X represent a vector of coefficients and associated control variables. Specifically, the control variables include the lagged dependent variable to control for persistence in yield, natural logarithm of GDP per capita and log growth in GDP per capita, foreign exchange rate (relative to the US dollar), reserves as a percentage of GDP, and inflation rate.

Our approach has three features that enhance the likelihood of maintaining the parallel trends assumption in our setup. First, in equation (2), each treatment instance is treated as a separate sub-experiment. This empirical choice allows us to carefully construct each comparison group to mirror the treatment group before the intervention, better maintaining the parallel trends. We achieve this by ensuring that the control units remain consistent across different treatment periods and sub-experiments, effectively creating a fixed baseline against which we can measure any changes due to treatment.

Second, including country-sub-experiment (γcd) and time-sub-experiment (γtd) fixed effects implies that each sub-experiment in our model is isolated from others, meaning that the dynamic inter-group comparisons, which can introduce bias in traditional staggered DiD analyses, are avoided. The control groups selected to match the treated units in terms of the pre-treatment characteristics and unaffected by the treatment at any point in the sub-experiment, help in maintaining parallel trends within each sub-experiment. This ensures that any observed differences post-treatment are attributable to the treatment itself and not to other external changes or different evolution patterns in the comparison group.

Third, our regression model includes controls for observable and unobservable heterogeneity, such as macroeconomic variables and fixed effects. These controls help adjust for time-varying factors that might otherwise violate the parallel trends assumption. By including these control variables and fixed effects, we account for differences that could lead to divergent paths between the treated and control groups unrelated to the treatment, thus reinforcing the validity of the parallel trends assumption. In summary, our approach not only addresses the typical biases encountered in staggered treatment settings but also upholds the parallel trends assumption, ensuring that the changes we attribute to the treatment are not confounded by other factors. This rigorous approach allows us to deliver more reliable and accurate estimates of the treatment’s effects.
